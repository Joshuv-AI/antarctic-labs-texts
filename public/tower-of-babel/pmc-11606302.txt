Validation and adaptation of the moral outrage scale in the Peruvian adult population
Authors: Cristian Ramos-Vera, Marcelo Casa-Chicata, Zafiro Portilla-Yacolca, Carlos Puma-Maque, Yaquelin E Calizaya-Milla, Jacksaint Saintila, Elmer Lopez-Lopez, Sandra P Carranza-Cubas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMoral outrage (MO) is an emotional experience that arises in situations where a moral standard is violated. Despite its relevance, there are few psychometric studies in the Latin American context, characterized by inequality and social problems. This highlights the need for specific assessment instruments for this construct. The purpose of this study was to analyze the psychometric properties of an MO scale in the Peruvian population.MethodsThe sample consisted of 881 Peruvians (57.2% women, Mage = 24.5 years, SD = 9.07 years). The moral outrage scale was administered to participants. To analyze the data, two models were tested: a structural equation model (SEM) and an exploratory structural equation model (ESEM).ResultsThe CFA confirmed a two-factor model (emotional and cognitive), with satisfactory fit indices and standardized factor loadings greater than 0.70. The ESEM model also showed improved fit indices. Measurement invariance across sex was established, and convergent validity was demonstrated between the dimensions of MO and authoritarianism, as well as other sociodemographic variables such as sex, age, and socioeconomic level.ConclusionsThe MO scale tested in this study is a reliable, valid, and invariant instrument, suitable for assessing moral outrage in both men and women within the Peruvian context.Keywords: Moral outrage, Psychometric properties, Moral emotions, Validity, Reliability, Measurement invariance

Introduction

In 2022, the poverty rate experienced an increase, a situation worsened by a series of additional factors such as political and/or armed conflicts, effects of climate change, job layoffs, among others [1]. Additionally, the study by the Oxford Committee for Famine Relief details how the cost-of-living crisis has exacerbated economic and social inequality [2]. These social phenomena have the potential to trigger emotional responses in individuals, even when they are not directly affected. Such responses can motivate us to act for the benefit of others or society at large and are referred to as moral emotions. Characterized by their selflessness, these emotions are particularly notable for their ability to inspire prosocial actions [3].

According to the theory of moral foundations, morality is defined as the set of values that guide an individual in his or her reasoning process, particularly during the confrontation of challenging social situations [4, 5]. Under this perspective, a moral standard relates to those values that promote respect for the rights and welfare of others, while restricting certain freedoms and establishing roles for members of a group, to meet that moral standard [6]. However, it is the individual themselves who assesses and determines the validity of the moral standard, as well as whether it has been transgressed or not in a specific situation [7]. In contrast, another individual might embrace a different ethical perspective regarding food, based on their own philosophical or ethical principles. For example, they might believe in the importance of a diet that includes meat to meet their nutritional needs or follow a philosophy that does not assign the same ethical significance to the consumption of animal products.

Moral outrage (MO) is a prominent moral emotion that arises when a moral standard is violated, evoking feelings of anger or annoyance toward the violator [8]. It is characterized by being experienced even without having a direct relationship with the victims of injustice, separating it from other forms of anger or rage [9]. For example, in Peru, daily news about femicides or abuse, distributed through television or social networks, can generate anger in the population, even in those who are not directly related to the victims or the situation.

The study of MO traces back to the Existential Guilt Inventory, developed by Montada et al. [10, 11] and Montada & Schneider [8] in which this variable is measured by means of individuals’ ratings of instances of injustice. The concept of MO includes two key dimensions: cognitive and emotional. Montada and Schneider [8], building on cognitive models of emotions, proposed that both the emotional and cognitive aspects of moral outrage play a crucial role. These models provide a theoretical framework for understanding the connection between emotions and mental and cognitive processes, such as thinking, perception, and evaluation [12–14]. These theoretical approaches suggest that to experience an emotion, one must first witness an event that triggers it, and it is the result of the individual’s own evaluations that one emotion or another is experienced. These specific evaluations play a crucial role in the experience and expression of the emotion, which may or may not elicit a response from the individual [8, 12–14]. In the specific context of MO, its cognitive dimension includes the process of making a rational judgment and critically assessing the unjust situation, leading to the development of cognitions related to inequity or assigning blame to a third party [15]. In contrast, the emotional dimension is the individual’s affective and visceral response [13, 14]. Specifically, in the case of moral outrage, it refers to the manifestation of anger or indignation in response to the critical evaluation of the situation [8]. For example, these emotions can arise from accidental situations or negligence that affect the environment and human health. A recent and significant event in Peru was the oil spill caused by the Repsol company in the Ventanilla sea, a clear example of such circumstances [16]. Following this negligence, efforts to rectify their mistake were inadequate and lacked evident planning. This lack of interest and commitment to addressing the disaster resulted in a response from the company that was deemed irresponsible. This generated moral outrage not only among direct victims, such as fishermen and local residents, but also among the vast majority of people who became aware of this lackluster attitude. This feeling of moral outrage was reflected in negative comments directed at the company and those responsible, as well as in messages expressing concern for the damage caused, both on social media and television. These messages called for action to be taken against the company [16].

As mentioned above, this emotion can serve as a motivator for altruistic actions, such as offering support or showing solidarity with victims. These prosocial actions often stem from a committed and responsible disposition to address injustice and contribute to collective solutions [17, 18]. An example of this would be cases of femicide, which generate deep MO in many people globally. These acts of sex-based violence, resulting in the murder of women due to their sex, represent a violation of human rights and sex equality. In this particular case, MO stems from the blatant injustice of these crimes and concerns about insufficient efforts to prevent and punish the perpetrators. Society’s reactions have been reflected in social movements, both on social media with movements like #MeToo, and in public marches with unified chants [19].

A construct inversely related to moral outrage is authoritarianism, which is defined as a predisposition to support established authorities and traditional values, expressing disagreement with groups opposing these ideals [20]. Therefore, as described by Celik [21] individuals with higher levels of authoritarianism tend to perceive moral outrage to a lesser degree. In other words, as authoritarianism increases, moral outrage tends to decrease and vice versa. This is because individuals with higher levels of authoritarianism tend to validate and accept the system they support. This allows them to justify situations where moral standards are violated as their political regime permits. As a result, they exhibit a lack of response or concern in such situations [22, 23].

Based on research by Montada et al. [8], Wakslak et al. [24] used a scale composed of 10 items to measure moral outrage. Subsequently, this test has been used in various contexts, including China [25], Switzerland and the Netherlands [26], France [23], and the United States [27]. However, to date, there are only two psychometric studies have analyzed the factorial structure of this instrument. Firstly, Celik [21] assessed 251 Turkish university students aged 17 to 27, confirming a two-factor structure with nine items. These elements were grouped into two dimensions: emotional and cognitive, based on these characteristics. To determine this model, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was initially performed, grouping five items (1, 3, 4, 5, and 6) into the emotional dimension and items (2, 7, 8, and 6) into the cognitive dimension. However, due to the low factorial loading of item 2 (0.31), it was removed from the scale. The model was then retested, giving satisfactory results. Subsequently, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed with a final model that included four error covariances (8–9, 4–6, 7–8, and 7–9), which resulted in optimal fit indices (χ2 = 63.10, df = 23, GFI = 0.95, AGFI = 0.90, CFI = 0.93 and RMSEA = 0.08). Subsequently, Bastias and Cañadas [17] established the unidimensionality of the moral outrage scale in a sample of Argentine adults. Initially, they conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with the 10 items in 368 participants. The results showed low reliability in factor 2 (α = 0.58), prompting a detailed review of each item. Items 10 (belonging to factor 1) and 6 (belonging to factor 2) were eliminated due to their low correlation with the factor and low communality. Furthermore, items belonging to factor 2 were not included due to low item-total correlation. Based on this, the internal structure was reanalyzed, considering only five elements (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5). This model was evaluated through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with the five mentioned elements in a second sample of 374 participants, which confirms a unidimensional model with satisfactory fit indices (X2 = 11.46, df = 5, p < 0.001; CFI = 0.97; RMSEA = 0.059).

One aspect to mention is the absence of an assessment of the factorial invariance of the instrument. Invariance refers to the consistency in measuring the construct analyzed among different groups. To achieve this, different constraints known as configural invariance, metric invariance, scalar invariance, and strict invariance must be applied. It is important to note that achieving scalar invariance will allow for comparisons between the evaluated groups without measurement biases.

[28, 29]. Even in other recent instrumental studies on moral emotions, this measurement equivalence property has not been evaluated [30–35], while in other validations of measures related to moral emotions, a lack of factorial invariance has been determined based on sex and age [36, 37].

Age

As people age, their cognitive and emotional development profoundly influences their social and political beliefs [38]. Cognitive maturity enables individuals to examine social injustices with greater nuance, frequently eliciting profound moral outrage in response to perceived injustices [9]. However, this same capacity can lead them to justify and perpetuate social hierarchies, especially in contexts that reinforce these structures. As individuals age, emotional development fosters a deeper internalization of social norms and a stronger focus on maintaining community cohesion. Having spent more time in an environment that prioritizes order and stability, older individuals tend to experience heightened moral outrage when they perceive traditional norms are being challenged [39]. This tendency is further reinforced by the perception of the world as a dangerous place, where any challenge to established norms is viewed as a threat to social order. As a result, older individuals may be more inclined to punish those who violate these norms, justifying their actions as necessary to protect social and moral stability [40, 41].

Income

On the other hand, socioeconomic differences can impact cognitive and emotional responses when individuals confront social issues such as poverty, inequality, or unemployment [42]. Specifically, differences can be observed in attributions, prejudices, and attitudes among individuals with higher economic income [43], as well as in prosocial behaviors within societies of varying socioeconomic status [44]. For example, exclusionary or inclusive attitudes towards minority groups may differ based on these factors [45, 46]. In this context, societies with greater economic inequality tend to experience moral emotions based on negative sanctions—such as anger, contempt, disgust, or shame—more frequently and with greater intensity. Conversely, societies with lower levels of inequality are more likely to exhibit positive moral emotions [47, 48]. Despite the significance of these differences, moral outrage has not been a central focus in studies evaluating them.

Sex

Women tend to experience higher levels of moral emotions, particularly feelings of shame and guilt [49]. Moral indignation often arises when individuals witness situations of injustice, particularly those related to sex inequality. These differences may be attributed to greater emotional involvement on the part of women, who tend to experience dysphoric emotions in situations where the well-being of others or justice is compromised [50], and to judge immoral actions more harshly than men [50]. However, sex differences in moral outrage outside the context of sex inequality have not yet been investigated.

Therefore, in response to the limited prior psychometric research on the instrument and with the aim of addressing this practical limitation, this study seeks to analyze the psychometric properties of the Moral Outrage Scale in Peruvian adults. This includes assessing internal validity, convergent validity with dimensions of authoritarianism, reliability, and measurement invariance by sex. This analysis will provide insight into how moral outrage can play a crucial role in responding to social injustices and will contribute significantly to the theoretical and methodological understanding of this construct in the Peruvian context.

Materials and methods

Design

For the present study, an instrumental design was used, as psychometric properties were analyzed to determine the factorial structure of the instrument. This design allowed for a comprehensive examination of the instrument’s properties and structure [51].

Participants

In the present study, the total sample consisted of 881 participants (M = 24.5 years, SD = 9.07 years). Concerning sex, 57.2% (504) were women. Of the sample, 51.8% (465) were individuals who were not employed, while the remaining 48.2% (425) were employed. As for socioeconomic status (SES), most of the sample belongs to sector E with 43.6% (384), followed by D with 26.9% (237) [52]. In terms of marital status, most participants are single (83.1%). Regarding educational level, most of the sample had completed higher education (67%), followed by technical education (13.8%) and secondary education (13.2%). A lower percentage had completed postgraduate studies (4.2%), while a minority had only completed primary education (1%) or had no formal education (0.8%). Regarding the participants who were enrolled in an educational institution at the time of the assessment, 62% reported attending a public institution, 21.6% attended a private institution, and 16.5% mentioned that they were not currently studying.

The sample size was estimated using an a priori calculator for structural equation models [53], which considered an effect size of 0.30, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80, along with two latent variables and ten observable variables.

A non-proportional quota sampling approach was used, in which the sample selection was based on specific criteria determined by the researchers, taking into consideration the ease of access to participants [54].

Instruments

Moral outrage scale (MO)

The Moral Outrage Scale used in this study is the version validated by Bastias & Cañadas [17]. This scale in Spanish consists of 10 items that are divided into two dimensions: the emotional dimension, which includes items (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 10); and the cognitive dimension, which comprises items (6, 7, 8, and 9), which are reverse-coded. Participants rated their responses on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (‘Completely disagree’) to 6 (‘Completely agree’). Consequently, as the score obtained on this scale increases, so does the level of moral outrage experienced by the individual being assessed. Regarding their psychometric properties, the alpha coefficients found in this study were significantly high, with values of α = 0.913 (emotional dimension) and α = 0.876 (cognitive dimension).

Right-Wing Authoritarianism scale (RWA)

This scale was developed by Zakrisson [55] and adapted into Spanish by De la Rubia [55]. The instrument consists of 15 items, organized into three dimensions: authoritarian aggression (5 items), authoritarian submission (5 items), and conventionalism (5 items). It utilizes a Likert-type rating scale with six response options (1 = Completely disagree to 6 = Completely agree). In this study, the alpha coefficients obtained for the dimensions were α = 0.913 for the emotional dimension and α = 0.876 for the cognitive dimension.

Data analysis

First, the sociodemographic data provided by the participants were analyzed by calculating frequencies, allowing for the description and summary of these characteristics. Subsequently, an initial analysis of the items was conducted using univariate statistics such as skewness and kurtosis. The purpose was to determine whether the data followed a normal distribution, and this was done using a cutoff point of ± 3 [56].

Subsequently, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to examine the scale structure. Given the ordinal nature of the items, the weighted least squares estimator with adjusted mean and variance (WLSMV) was employed [57]. Furthermore, to assess the fit of the model, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and the root mean square standardized residual root (SRMR) indices were used, considering values below.05 as optimal and values between 0.05 and 0.08 as acceptable [56]. Furthermore, the comparative fit index (CFI) and the Turker-Lewis index (TLI) were used, with values above 0.95 indicating satisfactory results and values above 0.90 considered acceptable [58]. For the reliability analysis, the omega coefficient was used to estimate the internal consistency of the scale, with values above 0.70 deemed acceptable [59]. Since it has been demonstrated that Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling (ESEM) produces a better model fit compared to the CFA approach in various applications, we chose to employ this model to compare the fit indices results [60]. This analysis is preferable when there is uncertainty about the factor structure or when items load on multiple factors. In this case, previous studies have indicated cross-loadings and disparity of results on structure, so its use is recommended in similar instruments. In particular, ESEM integrates elements of CFA, SEM, and EFA. In contrast, ESEM is less biased and produces more accurate factor correlation estimates [60]. All statistical analyses were performed using the Rstudio software, version 4 [61, 62].

Regarding the second phase, the measurement invariance based on the sex of the participants was examined. To achieve this, we first examined configurational invariance, which presents the reference model. Then, metric invariance was assessed, which involves the equality of factor loadings. Subsequently, scalar invariance was performed, which includes the equality of factor loadings and intercepts. Finally, strict invariance was performed, which implies the equality of factor loadings, intercepts, and residuals. To ensure the proper functioning of this process, differences in the fit indices ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA were examined. In the case of ΔCFI, values below 0.010 were recorded, indicating the presence of model invariance. Similarly, ΔRMSEA was analyzed, where values below 0.015 suggest invariance between groups [58, 63].

To assess convergent validity, network analysis was employed, using various statistical packages, including qgraph, bootnet, mgm, and networktools [64]. The network analysis was based on partial correlation, which measures the degree of association between two variables while controlling for the influence of other variables. This method is preferred over Pearson’s r or Spearman’s rho in this context because it provides a clearer picture of direct relationships between variables. The nodes are connected by lines, called “edges,” which can be understood as relationships between elements. For this type of network analysis, we used the Spearman estimator for the GGM, as is commonly recommended for ordinal and nonparametric variables. Network visualization was achieved using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm, using the qgraph package. Furthermore, the inclusion of variables of sex, age, and socioeconomic level was considered, using the mgm R package to evaluate the graphical model with different types of variables (continuous, categorical, among others). To ensure the reliability of the results, the precision of edge weights was estimated using Bootstrapping with 3000 samples around each edge of the network, at a 95% confidence level. Finally, centrality indices, such as expected influence, were explored, thus identifying the most relevant variables by connecting all elements in the network.

Procedure

First, permission was requested from the authors who translated the Moral Indignation Scale into Spanish for use and administration of the instrument via email [17]. The linguistic adaptation of the scale to the Peruvian context was then carried out using the Delphi methodology, a process that involves consulting experts and obtaining their consensus [65]. In this case, the translation and linguistic adaptation previously conducted in Argentina was considered to assess whether the items needed modification for better comprehension in the Peruvian sample. For this purpose, three experts in psychology, social work, and sociology were selected to thoroughly review the items of the MO Scale. Each expert conducted their review independently. Subsequently, a meeting was held to discuss the revisions. No changes were made to the items, as they were deemed appropriate for the Peruvian context.

Data collection was carried out during the months of May and August 2023. Instead of employing conventional methods to obtain data in person, the online survey approach was chosen [66]. These surveys were collected using a Google Form, a widely recognized tool in psychological research that follows the guidelines of Internet-Based Methodology [67]. Permission was obtained from the directors of each university faculty through a formal request outlining the objectives of the research.

Results

Descriptive analysis of sociodemographic data

To avoid discriminatory controversies and biased assessments, Ipsos Peru has developed a new categorization for socioeconomic levels, which includes five options: A, B, C, D, and E [52]. In this system, level A represents the highest socioeconomic category, while level E corresponds to the lowest socioeconomic category [52]. Consequently, most of the participants in this study were classified into groups C and D. Additionally, it could be noted that 83.1% of the participants are single, 67.0% have a university education, and 62.0% attend public universities.

This system is primarily based on the analysis of average monthly household income, expressed in Peruvian soles, as the main economic indicator. To determine the specific characteristics of the different socioeconomic levels in Peru, data from the National Household Survey (ENAHO) were used. ENAHO, conducted periodically by the National Institute of Statistics and Informatics (INEI), provides detailed information on various aspects of living conditions, including income, consumption, employment, education, and access to basic services. These data allow for the classification of households into different socioeconomic levels, facilitating the analysis of economic inequality, access to opportunities, and resource distribution across the country. The socioeconomic levels were converted to U.S. dollars to enhance understanding of the established averages. According to IPSOS Peru (2022), the averages for each socioeconomic level are as follows: Level A ($3,370.84), Level B ($1,869.14), Level C ($1,057.05), Level D ($660.32), and Level E ($346.14). Discussion

In Latin America, the region’s cultural diversity, history of turmoil and conflict, along with challenges such as poverty, limited access to healthcare, and unemployment, have shaped both the individual and collective morale of its population [69]. As a result, moral emotions like compassion and gratitude are prevalent in Latin America and influence social behaviors aimed at combating violence and inequality [70]. Additionally, the cultural attributes of each country may shape different beliefs about moral dilemmas, such as bribery and child labor [71]. Moreover, significant economic disparity leads to varied interpretations of morality in the labor context, where informality, tax evasion, and corruption are common practices [72]. Therefore, it is important to consider the unique cultural particularities of this context, as they may influence the interpretation and applicability of the MO scale.

MO occurs when an individual perceives a violation of a moral norm and experiences feelings of discomfort or anger directed toward the individual responsible for that transgression. This emotion manifests itself even when there is no direct connection to the victims of injustice. This study focused on conducting a rigorous evaluation of the psychometric validity of the Spanish version of the Moral Indignation Scale adapted by Bastias and Cañadas [17], in a sample of Peruvian adults. To achieve this, we evaluated the psychometric properties of the scale, including reliability and scale invariance. Convergent validity was specifically tested through network analysis.

CFA was used to assess the scale’s structure, but the results did not meet established standards. This finding underscores the difficulties encountered in trying to confirm the proposed model. In other studies with the same objective, as in the case of Celik [21], a two-factor instrumental model with nine items was confirmed after making adjustments, such as including covariances between errors and eliminating a specific item (item 2). In addition, Bastias and Cañadas [17] conducted evaluations with the original 10 items of the scale, but after an analysis, they decided to exclude the cognitive dimension of the scale and one of the items of the emotional dimension (item 10). This led to the confirmation of a unidimensional model composed of 5 elements. Both results were due to limitations such as covariances between errors, so in the present study the decision was made to analyze the scale using the ESEM approach. ESEM represents an integration of CFA, SEM, and EFA and provides a way to use the advantages of exploratory factor analysis for confirmatory and predictive purposes, resolving some limitations of CFA models on multidimensional scales that produce biased parameter estimates [61]. This finding is relevant given that, initially, an evaluation had been performed with the CFA, but the model built with ESEM proved to be superior. Specifically, this model managed to maintain the inclusion of all items in contrast to previous versions, indicating an improvement and consistency in the representation of the scale in question.

So far, no psychometric studies have been carried out to analyze sex invariance. This specific study has shown that the factorial structure in its different levels (configurational, metric, strict, and scalar) has remained stable. These findings support the assertion that the elements evaluated consistently measure the underlying variable in both men and women as a function of factor structure [57]. It should be noted that after a review of psychometric articles on moral emotions, two studies included factorial equivalence as a function of sex; however, the results show that such properties were inconclusive [36, 37].

The findings of the present research indicate that the emotional dimension of MO maintains a negative relationship with the “submission” dimension of authoritarianism. Other studies present similar results, reporting a negative relationship between authoritarianism and emotional indignation [21]. This finding suggests that individuals with a high level of submission tend to accept the actions, values, and norms represented by legitimate figures in society who have authority in legal and moral aspects over other individuals. This could influence their behavior and attitudes, as they are more likely to accept and follow the guidelines of these authorities on legal and moral issues [20]. According to Schwartz’s moral value theory, these individuals modify the value of moral principles in their hierarchy of principles, due to various factors such as individual psychological needs or the influence of an authority Fig. [73]. When there is a biased valuation of the consequences of an authority’s actions, an individual may justify his or her behavior based on the value of loyalty and obedience to that authority, even above social moral standards. This means that they might be willing to follow the directives of authority, even if those actions go against what is generally considered morally correct in society.

The attitude of submission to authority may be related to the experience of anger or indignation when they witness unjust situations or grievances towards other individuals when caused by those authorities. This is because they value loyalty and membership in a social group overseen or directed by that authority more highly, so they may be willing to overlook injustices in order not to react in some way to their group or authority [25]. For example, in the incident of the Dalai Lama, the leader of Tibetan Buddhism and Nobel Peace Prize winner, who during a ceremony engaged in lewd behavior toward a child by kissing him on the mouth and then asking him to “suck” on his tongue [74]. This provoked anger in various parts of the world at the perceived breach of an established moral standard, specifically the right and dignity of a minor. However, the Tibetan leader’s followers justified their actions and showed their support for Dalai Lama despite the questionable fact, which may be due to the high loyalty and emotional influence he has over his followers, as well as a higher degree of social interdependence towards Tibetan Buddhism.

As for the manifestation of aggressive behaviors, in the case of the Dalai Lama, hostile behaviors and indignation with offensive comments through social networks, since they also identify their values as offended, as indicated by psychosocial theory [20, 75]. In the case of individuals who present an identification with authority figures who employ hostile attitudes in their actions to preserve social order, they may present a higher level of attitudes of authoritarian aggression. This can cause an individual to learn to relate to these attitudes as a way of coping with external threats and thus feel protected [76]. A greater affiliation and orientation towards such authoritarian behaviors is related to the desire for a stable and certain reality, since these individuals with a higher level of authoritarianism consider that in the world there must be a stable system shaped by their norms, values, and beliefs, given that they are very intolerant of uncertainty [77]. Individuals with this trait exhibit low emotional control, which can lead them to react impulsively to possible threats to the certainty of their reality, reactions such as emotional outrage, as reported in Turkish adults [78]. Therefore, the presence of moral-emotional indignation may reinforce these attitudes directed at groups that are perceived as threatening.

Sex

Network analysis showed that being female is positively related to the emotional dimension of MO, while being male is positively related to the cognitive dimension of MO. This may be largely because sex roles exert considerable influence on individuals’ emotional experience and expression by establishing cultural and social norms about how they should behave and express themselves according to their sex [79]. As pointed out by the Cognitive Sex Schema Theory, which emphasizes the impact of cultural and social expectations about sex behavior on the development and behavior of individuals [80]. Such expectations influence cultural norms that dictate acceptable emotional expression for men and women, associating emotional control with the male sex and emotional sensitivity with the female sex [81].

The positive relationship of the male group with the cognitive dimension of MO can be explained from Belief and Attitude Theory, which highlights two fundamental aspects in the formation of attitudes and decision making: rational beliefs and affective evaluations [81]. Rational beliefs originate in logic, experience, and education. In the context of MO, rational beliefs are linked to the logical understanding of what is morally right or wrong. Within these cognitive aspects, several dimensions can be identified, including thinking about inequalities, which involves reflecting on the disparities observed in society. Injustice-related ruminations are an intensified cognitive response in which the mind repeatedly dives into the discrepancy between what is considered morally right and perceived reality. This leads to more critical judgments when evaluating a perceived social problem, which reinforces the belief that the world is unfair and that moral transgressions are overlooked [82].

Economy

Regarding the results, it is reported that having low income is related to the cognitive dimension of MO. This can be interpreted because low-income individuals tend to internalize passive or docile roles, which are related to limitations in educational access and opportunities to develop skills that foster self-assertion and decision-making [83]. Low-income individuals who have always lived in poverty tend to have a sense of learned helplessness, where they internalize the belief of the impossibility of changing circumstances and may feel less empowered to take action in the face of events that are often of concern in their environment or even that they consider unjust. It is likely that these ingrained ideas can lead the individual to constantly reflect on the injustices he or she faces, which can cause considerable distress, as the individual is immersed in a continuous preoccupation with the arbitrary circumstances of his or her environment [78].

In the network, a positive relationship was observed between the high economic level and the emotional dimension. In relation to this, it is important to mention the just world theory, which holds that individuals tend to believe in a world in which actions and outcomes are distributed equally [82]. The affluent position that comes with a better economic situation may lead to less exposure to experiences that highlight inequalities or social problems. Therefore, these individuals can perceive the distribution of resources as fair and that the world is an inherently fair place, as evidenced in a study by Kerry [84]. Therefore, when they face situations of inequality that challenge this belief, it can break with their expectations and generate in them a greater impression, which leads to a more intense emotional reaction. Witnessing a situation of injustice may make them feel more bewildered and they may even experience deep feelings of anger and annoyance. Witnessing arbitrary actions in scenarios that are more uncertain than the social reality of these individuals often generates surprise at such events and can lead to an emotional reaction with a high degree of indignation [42].

Age

Regarding age, the younger an individual is, the greater the relationship with the emotional dimension of MO. This can be interpreted to mean that lack of good or bad experiences may limit the understanding of injustices or social problems. Therefore, there would be a decrease in the emotional impact, which may be due to a lower understanding of the emotional impression in the presence of an arbitrary adverse situation, which may result in a lower emotional connection, making it more. As Bandura’s social-emotional theory points out, which suggests that experience and direct exposure influence how they perceive their environment, they are perceived to be unable to manage their emotions due to their immaturity and lack of emotional skills [76]. Due to limited emotional resources and limited experience, young people may face a lower intensity of emotional burden that arises from witnessing unfair situations. This can lead to a reduced understanding of these situations and avoid dealing with them due to overwhelming emotions that arise, such as outrage in the face of discriminatory acts [85].

In relation to being older, it is positively related to the cognitive dimension of MO. This can be interpreted as due to accumulated experience and knowledge; adults have a better understanding of social injustices and their implications. As Bandura’s theory points out, in adulthood, individuals have a greater cognitive capacity to process observed information, due to the behaviors they perceive from their environment, which would lead them to evaluate unfair situations rationally [76]. Therefore, being older is associated with a greater capacity for analysis and deep reflection on social problem [86]. This ability often triggers opinion judgments due to a higher accumulation of experiences. These experiences include being a spectator and listener of arbitrary and abusive situations, which can lead to a heightened sense of social concern and cognitive indignation [87].

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, a cross-sectional convenience sample was used, which may introduce biases that limit the generalizability of the results. Future research could benefit from using stratified sampling to obtain more representative samples. Although the sample size in this study is small, it is considered reliable as the factor loadings are above 0.60 [88]. The participants were primarily young women from socioeconomic level E, highlighting the need to validate the scale in more diverse samples, including participants from different regions of Peru, to gain a more comprehensive understanding. Additionally, the questionnaire was self-administered, which may have introduced social desirability bias. As the study is cross-sectional, longitudinal studies are recommended to gain a deeper understanding of the scale.

Despite these limitations, the results provide empirical support for expanding the body of research on MO. In fact, previous studies primarily relied on simple factor analysis and correlations to establish convergent validity. Similarly, future research could explore constructs such as Moral Disconnection, Social Dominance Orientation, and System Justification, as well as other factors related to the political or social environment. The robustness of the methodological approach used, which enables a more complex and detailed evaluation of the scale’s psychometric properties, further supports its validity and reliability. Additionally, the inclusion of invariance analysis ensures that the scale can be applied consistently across different groups, enhancing its usefulness in comparative studies. Finally, the use of network analysis provides an innovative and detailed perspective on convergent relationships in a systematic way for psychometric [89–91] and correlational studies [92, 93]. This perspective provides an additional layer of understanding not previously explored, highlighting the added value of this study in the fields of psychometrics and MO research. Moreover, there are no other studies on MO in the Peruvian context, making this a valuable precedent for future research. Additionally, the parsimonious nature of the scale ensures it is both simple and accessible, facilitating its use in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the MO scale applied to a Peruvian sample is a two-factor instrument with satisfactory psychometric results. This scale can be a valuable tool for research analyzing the presence and magnitude of this phenomenon within the Peruvian population. However, further research is needed to explore how the MO scale can be utilized in relation to sociodemographic characteristics, as assessed in this study. Future studies are crucial, given the clear need for instruments with robust psychometric properties to assess MO. Additionally, the scarcity of psychometric research on this phenomenon in various contexts highlights the importance of continuing such studies to better understand its scope and implications in different social settings in Peru.
