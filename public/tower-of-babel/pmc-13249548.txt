Biological activity of 2-methylbenzimidazole derivatives with heterocyclic rings on development of Bombyx mori
Authors: Udawaththa Kankanamge Don Sahan Suganda Gunasekara, Konatsu Inoue, Wataru Tsuchiya, Keisuke Kutsuwada, Shuhei Henmi, Takahiro Shiotsuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Derivatives of 1-heterocyclic 2-methylbenzimidazoles (HMBIs), prepared from previously reported 1-benzyl-2-methylbenzimidazoles, were synthesized to assess their larvicidal activity against the silkworm, Bombyx mori. Among the series, 2-methyl-1-(thiophen-2-yl)-methylbenzimidazole (SGH2) produces a distinctive biological phenotype characterized by growth retardation arising from epidermal abnormalities, together with defects in molting and pupation. To explore the basis of these effects, we examined the potential disruption of endocrine signaling as a causal mechanism. Specifically, we evaluated the binding of SGH2 to lepidopteran hemolymph juvenile hormone (JH)-binding protein (JHBP), an important modulator of insect hormone function, using a fluorescence-based ligand-sensor system that couples JHBP to two reporter fluorophores. SGH2 exhibited binding to JHBP with an EC50 of 2.13±0.34 µM, a potency comparable to that of JH. These findings indicate that HMBI derivatives, such as SGH2, can interfere with JH-mediated developmental processes in Lepidoptera and identify a promising scaffold for the design of novel insect growth regulators aimed at controlling lepidopteran pests.

Introduction

Widespread insecticide use in modern crop protection has produced two principal challenges: the selection of resistant populations and the collateral harm to non-target species.1,2) Insect growth regulators (IGRs) represent an attractive alternative because they disrupt developmental pathways specific to insects. Registered IGRs are classified into three main groups according to their mode of action: juvenile hormone (JH) analogs (IRAC code 7), ecdysone antagonists (code 18), and chitin synthesis inhibitors (codes 16 and 17). These compounds have been used to control key lepidopteran pests, such as Plutella xylostella,3) Spodoptera litura,4) Mamestra brassicae,5) and Manduca sexta,6) while minimizing their adverse effects on pollinators.7–9) By interfering with endocrine-regulated processes, IGRs block progression through life stages from embryo to adult, thereby suppressing pest populations through developmental arrest.10,11)

In insects, development and growth are principally orchestrated by two low-molecular-weight hormones, JHs and 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E).12) Several imidazole-containing IGRs have been reported to perturb these endocrine pathways in Lepidoptera, producing diverse phenotypes such as 1-benzyl-5-[(E)-2,6-dimethyl 1,5-heptadienyl]imidazole (KK-42)–induced precocious metamorphosis,13,14) 1-[3-(4-phenoxyphenoxy)propyl] imidazole (KS-175)–mediated inhibition of molting and pupation,15) and the acute toxicity associated with compound 1-(3,7-dimethyl-7-ethoxy-2-octenyl)-2-methylbenzimidazole (B-1).16) Recent work from our laboratory has shown that 1-benzyl-2-methylbenzimidazole (BMBI) derivatives possess both insecticidal and IGR activities against the silkworm Bombyx mori.11) More broadly, heterocyclic scaffolds are important pharmacophores in the design of pharmaceutical17–19) and agrochemical agents.20)

Building on previous results, we designed a new series of targeted derivatives according to the strategy outlined in Fig. 1 and discussed below. A set of heterocyclic substituents (1-heterocyclic 2-methylbenzimidazoles (HMBIs)), replacing the benzyl group of the parent BMBI scaffold, were synthesized (Fig. 2). HMBIs incorporate five- or six-membered heterocyclic rings bearing oxygen, sulfur, and/or nitrogen atoms linked to the benzimidazole at the position of N1 via a methylene spacer. The resulting HMBIs were evaluated for in vivo activity against the lepidopteran Bombyx mori. In lepidopteran insects, juvenile hormone binding proteins (JHBPs) play a crucial role in regulating metamorphosis. These proteins are responsible for transporting JH through the hemolymph, protecting it from degradation by enzymes. By stabilizing JH levels, JHBPs help maintain the larval state and prevent premature development into pupal or adult stages. As JH levels decline and JHBP-mediated protection decreases, the insect becomes competent to initiate metamorphosis. Thus, JHBPs are essential for the precise timing of developmental transitions in holometabolous insects like moths and butterflies. HMBIs triggered precocious metamorphosis in B. mori by prematurely reducing JH levels. To probe molecular mode of action of HMBIs, the JHBP was chosen as a one of the target candidates, and the interaction of the JHBP and HMBIs was assessed with JHBP coupled with fluorescent chromophores as a JH-sensor by Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) efficiency served as the quantitative readout of ligand affinity (Kikuta et al.).21)

Materials and methods

1. Chemicals and instruments

All chemicals were purchased and used as received without purification. Thin layer chromatography (TLC silica gel 60 F254 Aluminum sheets 20×20 cm (EMD Millipore 1.5554.0001)) was used to separate components of mixture and visualized using combinations of UV visualization and potassium permanganate. Purification was performed by Silica gel gravity column chromatography using wakogel® C-200 (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). Proton NMR spectra of compounds were recorded on a JEOL JNM-ECX 500 FT NMR spectrophotometer using TMS as an internal reference (Chemical shift represented in δ ppm). All mass spectroscopy data were recorded using Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Velos Pro Hybrid Mass Spectrometer Ultimate Confidence. Melting points were measured with an uncorrected Yanaco MP-500D. The FRET activity assay was performed by described method with JH sensor, FREJIA using a fluorescence plate reader (SH-9000, Corona Denki Co., Ltd.) and 96-well plate. JH sensor developed by Kikuta et al. was used.21)

2. Syntheses of HMBIs

The syntheses were performed with described procedure for 1-benzyl-2-methyl-1H-1,3-benzimidazole (BMBIs) (Scheme 1), that is, after a reduction of the heterocyclic carboxylic acid or aldehyde and conversion to halides from the alcohol, coupled with 2-methylbenzimidazole via SN2 reactions, subsequently. As an example of the synthesis, 2-methyl-1-[(thiophen-2-yl)methyl]-1H-1,3-benzimidazole (SGH2) is as follows. 2-Thiophenecarboxylic acid (1.0 g, 7.8 mmol) was reduced with LiAlH4 (0.9 g, 23.7 mmol) in THF by refluxing at 70°C for 4 hr to obtain corresponding alcohol. To a solution of the alcohol (2.6 mmol) in CH2Cl2 (15 mL) was added pyridine (7.8 mmol) and thionyl chloride (4.0 mmol) slowly at 0°C to convert to corresponding chloride. NaH (0.07 g, 2.9 mmol; 60% oil suspension) was added gradually to the 2-methylbenzimidazole (0.2 g, 1.5 mmol) dissolved in DMF, followed by the addition of crude 2-(chloromethyl)thiophene (0.18 g, 1.3 mmol). The reaction mixture was purified by silica gel chromatography (n-hexane: EtOAc) to obtain SGH2 as white amorphous solid (0.24 g, 91%); m.p. 88.5–91.8°C; 1H-NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3) δ 7.71–7.69 (m, 1H), 7.36–7.33 (m, 1H), 7.26–7.21 (m, 3H), 6.93 (dd, J=5.2, 3.6 Hz, 1H), 6.90 (t, J=2.3 Hz, 1H), 5.46 (s, 2H), 2.66 (s, 3H); HRMS m/z ([M+H]+): Calcd for C13H13N2S: 229.0790; Found, 229.0794. The syntheses of other HBMIs are described in Supplement file.

3. Evaluation of biological activity of HMBIs

The biological activity against Bombyx mori (Shunrei×Shougetsu) were evaluated using larvae reared on Silkmate 2S (Nippon-nosan Kogyo, Japan) under a photoperiodic regime of 12 hr of light and 12 hr of darkness at 25°C. HMBIs (1 µL) dissolved in acetone were applied topically on the dorsal thorax of one day old 3rd and zero-day 4th instar larvae. The dose dependent activity was evaluated against 3rd and 4th instar larvae at the dosage of 0.1–10 µg. Acetone was applied to larvae as the control. The timing of mortality, molting, spinning, and other phenotypic changes were observed. The effect was recorded separately for acute toxicity, early pupation, molting inhibition, abnormal skin and pupal inhibition. The mortality rates were corrected by applying Abbott’s formula and evaluated on a percentage scale of 0–100. Three replicates were conducted for each assay to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

4. FRET activity assay

The interaction of tested compounds with JHBP was evaluated by Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) activity with JH sensor, FREJIA as a recombinant Bombyx JHBP coupled with two different fluorescent chromophores.21) FREJIA (at a final concentration of 1 µM, 297 µL) in 10 mM Tris-HCl containing 150 mM NaCl buffer solution (pH 7.5), and HMBIs or JH III (10–1000 µM, 3 µL) in ethanol, were sequentially added to each well of a 96-well plate (Nunc™ MicroWell™ 96-Well, Nunclon Delta-Treated, Flat-Bottom Microplate) for fluorescent analysis. In the ligand-absent state, initial state, the sensor mainly fluoresces at 494 nm by an excitation of 450 nm. The fluorescence at 530 nm by the same wavelength of excitation after was measured using a fluorescence plate reader (SH-9000, Corona). Ethanol and 10 µM of JH III in ethanol were used as negative control and positive control respectively. Results

1. Biological activities and structure-activity relationship of HMBIs

Previously described BMBIs exhibit pronounced IGR–type effects in Bombyx mori, producing distinct phenotypic outcomes depending on the substitutions present on the benzyl ring. In the present study, we designed and synthesized a series of HMBI analogs (SGH1–SGH7) in which heterocyclic rings replaced the benzyl substituent at the benzimidazole 1-position. These compounds display a range of biological activities that correlate with their structural variations and developmental stage at which they are applied.

1.1. Effect of HMBIs on 3rd instar larvae

As summarized in Table 1, topical application of SGH1–SGH7 (10 µg per larva) to first-day third-instar larvae produced a range of phenotypes, including acute toxicity, molting failure accompanied by cuticular abnormalities, precocious pupation, and pupation arrest. The predominant insecticidal outcomes were acute lethality, molting inhibition with epidermal defects, and pupation blockade.

1.2. Effect of HMBIs on 4th instar larvae

Topical application of SGH1–SGH7 (10 µg per larva) to newly molted fourth-instar larvae produced phenotypes comparable to those observed in third-instar assays, but overall mortality was lower in the older instars (Table 3). SGH1 induced minimal effects, yielding 4.0% pupation inhibition (Fig. 3L). SGH2 showed the highest IGR activity in fourth-instar larvae, with an overall effect rate of 61.67%, comprising molting failure with cuticular deformities (35.0%; Figs. 3F–H) and pupation inhibition (26.67%; Figs. 3L), and some individuals underwent precocious pupation (Figs. 3K). SGH3 produced a similar overall mortality (58.8%), with molting defects in 35.3% of larvae (Figs. 3F–H) and 23.5% pupation inhibition (Figs. 3L) and additionally induced early pupation in a subset of subjects. SGH4, SGH5, and SGH6 elicited only low levels of mortality (8.0%, 4.0%, and 5.0%, respectively; Fig. 3L). SGH7 caused 42.4% mortality, mainly from acute toxicity (36.4%; Fig. 3C), with a smaller contribution from pupation inhibition (6.0%; Fig. 3L), markedly less pronounced than its effect on third-instar larvae. Survival curves for fourth-instar larvae treated on day 0 with 10 µg per larva mirrored the patterns seen in third-instar treatments for SGH2 and SGH3, although the decline in survival for SGH2 was attenuated relative to third instars (Fig. 7). The 48-hour survival reduction following SGH7 exposure was significantly smaller in the fourth instar than in the third instar, despite the presence of both acute toxicity and pupation blockade. Dose–response experiments with SGH7 (0.1–10 µg per larva) in fourth instars showed that doses ≥3 µg produced both acute lethality and growth inhibition, whereas doses <3 µg primarily induced growth-inhibitory effects, with overall activity lower than that observed in third instars (Fig. 4B). The survival curve across the dose range with replicates is displayed in the Supporting Information (Fig. S1).

2. Interaction with JH binding protein

One proposed mode of action of HMBIs is interference with JHBP, a key chaperone that stabilizes and transports JH in lepidopteran larval hemolymph. To assess HMBI interactions with JHBP, we employed a FRET-based JH sensor and report the results in Table 4. SGH2 exhibited the strongest JHBP binding, reaching 112.74±5.06% relative activity with an EC50 of 2.13±0.34 µM, a potency approaching that of JH III (100.0±1.94%, EC50=0.50±0.08 µM). By comparison, the previously described BMBI showed modest binding (59.0±6.8%), while SGH3 and SGH7 displayed intermediate affinities (46.99±1.15% and 49.99±1.93%, respectively) relative to SGH2 (Table 4, Fig. 8). A strong correlation was observed between mortality rates and FRET signals among the SGH1–SGH6 derivatives (Fig. 9), supporting the relationship between JHBP binding and biological outcomes.

Discussion

Based on previous reports of imidazole-containing IGRs, we designed a new series of target molecules through deliberate structural modifications (Fig. 1). Earlier work showed that KK-42, which carries a benzyl substituent at the 1-position of the imidazole ring, induces precocious metamorphosis in B. mori, whereas replacement of the benzyl group with a citronellyl moiety, 1-citronellyl-5-[(E)-2,6-dimethyl 1,5-heptadienyl] imidazole (KK 22), markedly reduced this activity.13) Similarly, compound B-1 demonstrated larvicidal effects attributable to a 2-methylbenzimidazole core bearing a terpene side chain, with potency enhanced by a methyl substituent at the benzimidazole 2-position.16) Therefore, benzimidazole is recognized as a privileged scaffold for medicinal and agrochemical discovery.22) In our previous studies, 2-methylbenzimidazole bearing a benzyl group at N1 showed substantial IGR activity against B. mori.11) Recently, heterocycles containing oxygen, sulfur, or nitrogen have shown pronounced bioactivity across the pharmaceutical and agricultural contexts.18,23) Motivated by these observations, we synthesized a series of 2-methylbenzimidazole derivatives in which the N1 benzyl substituent was replaced by five- and six-membered heterocycles (SGH1–SGH7) to evaluate their potential as IGRs.

HMBIs produced two principal IGR outcomes: (i) acute lethality, defined as death within 48 hr, and (ii) developmental disruption manifested as epidermal abnormalities, molting failure, and/or inhibition of pupation. Additionally, administration of SGH2 and SGH3 at 10 µg per larva induced precocious metamorphosis in a subset of animals. The diversity of the observed phenotypes suggests that HMBIs may act on multiple molecular targets or pathways.

The biological effects of the compounds varied with both the larval stage and dose. In third-instar larvae at 10 µg per larva, SGH7 produced pronounced acute toxicity, whereas SGH1–SGH6 primarily elicited developmental disruptions without rapid lethality. At doses below 3 µg per larva, SGH7 induced minimal mortality but retained the ability to inhibit pupation and doses below 10 µg per larva prolongated the duration of the third-instar stage of larvae.

Across SGH1–SGH6, the dominant phenotypes were growth and pupation inhibition; these effects were observed for all of these analogs but not for SGH7 at 10 µg. SGH2 displayed a distinctive profile, with epidermal abnormalities as the major symptom, and both SGH2 and SGH3 additionally produced molting failure and, less frequently, precocious pupation. In fourth-instar larvae, the inhibitory effects were generally attenuated compared to third-instar larvae, although SGH2 and SGH3 still induced molting defects similar to those seen in younger larvae. By contrast, a previously reported BMBI at 10 µg per larva showed substantially lower pupation inhibition in fourth instars than SGH2, SGH3, or SGH7.

The comparison of heterocyclic substituents revealed clear trends. The saturated six-membered oxygen ring in SGH7 elicited higher mortality than the saturated five-membered oxygen ring in SGH4. The unsaturated five-membered oxygen ring of SGH3 exhibits intermediate activity, whereas the saturated five-membered oxygen rings are largely inactive. Unsaturated five-membered rings tended to inhibit development of larvae rather than cause acute toxicity. Among the five-membered heterocycles, the thiophene derivative SGH2 showed the strongest growth-inhibitory activity compared to the furan ring, consistent with the known utility of thiophene as a bioactive pharmacophore.24) Overall, while these patterns indicate the influences of ring size, saturation, and heteroatom identity on activity, detailed structure–activity relationships—particularly the roles of saturation and specific heteroatom placement—remain to be fully elucidated.

Previous studies have attributed the acute lethality of 2-methylbenzimidazole derivatives, such as B-1, to respiratory inhibition,16) suggesting that a similar disruption of respiration may underlie the rapid mortality induced by SGH7. However, at sublethal doses (≤7 µg per larva), SGH7 produced slowed development and growth inhibition, consistent with interference in JH–dependent pathways; this interpretation is supported by its measurable JHBP binding (FRET activity 49.99±1.93%).

SGH2 and SGH3 produced distinct phenotypes characterized by molting failure and cuticular abnormalities that have not been previously reported for BMBI derivatives, implying additional or alternative targets, such as hormone regulatory genes involved in JH gene network. One another plausible mechanism is the perturbation of chitin metabolism, which accounts for the exoskeletal defects and lethality.25) Genes involved in chitin turnover, such as chitinase, chitin synthase, and chitin deacetylase, are under endocrine control and positively regulated by 20E. For example, 20E administration elevates chitinase expression in Bombyx mori and Manduca sexta larvae, whereas JH analogs suppress it.26) Thus, chitinase inhibition causes the upregulation of ecdysone-related genes as a feedback mechanism, and a high ecdysone titer may activate the expression of late development genes, such as Titin and Yellow protein.26) Disruption of chitinase activity or its regulated expression impairs the degradation of the old cuticle during ecdysis, such as RNAi knockdown of Cht5 in Bombyx mori causes failure of larval-to-larval molting. Temporal regulation of Cht5 by 20E and miRNAs ensures cuticle degradation during early molting and restoration after ecdysis.27) Other cuticle-related genes (e.g., CPAP family members) are similarly regulated by ecdysteroids and JHs and are essential for proper cuticle formation.28)

Yellowing followed by darkening of the head cuticle observed in SGH2 and SGH3 could therefore reflect altered 20E titers or disrupted expression of ecdysone-responsive and cuticle-forming genes. Taken together, the data are consistent with HMBIs acting at multiple sites, including respiratory targets, JH-related chaperones, and components of the ecdysteroid–chitin regulatory axis; definitive assignment of each phenotype to a molecular target will require targeted biochemical and genetic follow-up, and the effects of these compounds on hormone concentrations are to be analyzed. However, consideration of JH agonistic or antagonistic activity has become essential for elucidating the mode of action in insecticide development.29) Early reported several JH analogues, such as methoprene,30) fenoxycarb31) and pyriproxyfen,32) have been proven to be effective IGRs and exhibit JH-agonistic activity. Subsequently, imidazole-type IGRs, such as KK-4213,14) and KS-17515) were reported to exhibit inhibitory activity in lepidopteran larvae, potentially attributable to their JH-antagonistic effects.11,33) Anti-JH activity induces characteristic phenotypes, including precocious metamorphosis, abnormal or incomplete metamorphosis, morphological abnormalities, and cuticular defects.34,35) HMBIs, particularly SGH2 and SGH3, exhibited biological effects like anti-JH activity, as described above.

Further, KK-42 has been reported to induce precocious metamorphosis in B. mori by lowering JH levels early, thereby suppressing ecdysteroid biosynthesis in the prothoracic gland.11) A comparable precocious-metamorphosis phenotype was observed in a subset of larvae treated with SGH2 and SGH3. Moreover, reduction of JH levels alters the 20E titers, thereby inducing morphological abnormalities,26) as observed for SGH2 and SGH3. Therefore, the disruption of JH signaling is a plausible mechanism underlying these growth effects. In Lepidoptera, a ∼30 kDa JHBP is essential for stabilizing and delivering JH during development.12,29,36) High-affinity, low-molecular-weight JHBPs appear to be restricted to Lepidoptera and Diptera, and only a few species (e.g., M. sexta, B. mori, and G. mellonella) have been characterized in detail.37) To determine whether SGH2 perturbs JH transport, we measured its interaction with JHBP using a FRET-based JH sensor that reports ligand-dependent conformational changes as wavelength shifts.21,38) The FRET response is concentration-dependent, enabling the quantitative estimation of ligand affinity relative to JH III. The affinity for JHBP was correlated with IGR activity in our panel, supporting the hypothesis that HMBIs can perturb developmental JH homeostasis via JHBP binding. However, the exact mode of action of HMBIs remains unclear, as reductions in JH titers can arise from multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of JH biosynthetic enzymes and induction of JH-metabolizing enzymes.34) Further investigation is necessary to comprehensively determine the mode of action of HMBIs.

In conclusion, we identified novel 2-methylbenzimidazole derivatives bearing heterocyclic rings at the N1 position as candidate IGRs against B. mori. SGH2 produces a distinct suite of developmental and pupation defects, whereas SGH7 causes acute lethality. The bioactivity of SGH2 is supported by its measurable affinity for hemolymph JHBP, which is comparable to that of endogenous hormones. Ongoing studies will aim to define the SGH2–JHBP interaction at atomic resolution by crystallography and in silico docking. On the other hand, multiple phenotypes induced by SGHs may be attributed to two or more distinct modes of action in addition to interference with JHBP, and their effects on the gene expression of hormone-related factors are currently under investigation.
