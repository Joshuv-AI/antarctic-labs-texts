Introduction

Given a representation
\Pi
of a Lie group
G
, and a representation
\pi
of a Lie subgroup
G^{\prime}\subset G
, one can think about the
G^{\prime}
-intertwining operators going from the restriction
\Pi\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
to
\pi
. These operators are called
symmetry breaking operators
, and their study may help us understand the nature behind the branching problems that appear in representation theory. In fact, the
problem of describing the space of these operators
\text{{Hom}}_{G^{\prime}}(\Pi\rvert_{G^{\prime}},\pi)
corresponds to Stage C of the ABC program that T. Kobayashi proposed to address branching problems for real reductive Lie groups (
[
11
]
):

\bullet

Stage A
: Abstract features of the restriction.

\bullet

Stage B
: Branching laws.

\bullet

Stage C
: Construction of symmetry breaking operators.

In this paper, we focus on the task of understanding these symmetry breaking operators. Concretely, we focus on the construction and classification of symmetry breaking operators that can be written as
differential
operators for a concrete pair of reductive Lie groups
(G,G^{\prime})
. One classical example following this line is given by the Rankin–Cohen bidifferential operators (
[
2
]
,
[
22
]
), which are a special case of symmetry breaking operators for the tensor product of two holomorphic discrete series representations of
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. Another example is given by the conformally covariant differential symmetry breaking operators for spherical principal series representations of the Lorentz group constructed by A. Juhl in the frame of conformal geometry (
[
7
]
).

One reason to think about differential symmetry breaking operators is that the space of such operators is isomorphic to the space of homomorphisms between certain generalized Verma modules, thanks to the duality theorem (see
[
14
, Thm. 2.9]
). Thus, the problem of determining all differential symmetry breaking operators can be approached from an algebraic point of view.

Another reason is that depending on the setting, the differential operators may be a quite large class inside all symmetry breaking operators, as it can occur that no other symmetry breaking operators exist. In other words, it may happen that any symmetry breaking operator is a differential operator. This type of phenomenon is called the localess theorem (cf.
[
14
, Thm. 5.3]
,
[
17
, Thm. 3.6]
).

In the following, we give a specific geometric setting that we consider throughout the paper. This setting allows us to consider Stage C in a significantly wide class of cases.

Given two smooth manifolds
X
and
Y
, two vector bundles over them
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow X
and
\mathcal{W}\rightarrow Y
and a smooth map
p:Y\rightarrow X
, we can talk about the notion of a
differential operator
between the spaces of smooth sections
T:C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
, following the line of the famous result of J. Peetre (
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
) about the characterization of differential operators in terms of their support (see Definition
2.1
). In addition, suppose that we have a pair of Lie groups
G^{\prime}\subset G
acting equivariantly on
\mathcal{W}\rightarrow Y
and
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow X
respectively, and that
p
is a
G^{\prime}
-equivariant map between
Y
and
X
. In this setting, we can consider the following problem:

Problem 1.1
.

Give a description of the space of
G^{\prime}
-intertwining differential operators (differential symmetry breaking operators)

D:C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W}).

The setting for Problem
1.1
is very general and differential symmetry breaking operators may or may not exist. For instance, if
\mathcal{W}
is isomorphic to the pullback
p^{*}\mathcal{V}
, the restriction map
f\mapsto f\lvert_{Y}
is clearly a
G^{\prime}
-intertwining differential operator from
C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V})
to
C^{\infty}(Y,\mathcal{W})
.

In the general setting where there is no morphism between
p^{*}\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
, there are cases where non-zero differential symmetry breaking operators exist, but determining when this occurs is a considerably hard task, and there are many unsolved problems. As for the ones that have been solved during the past years, for example, T. Kobayashi and B. Speh constructed and classified not only the ones that can be written as differential operators, but all the symmetry breaking operators for the tuple
(X,Y,G,G^{\prime})=(S^{n},S^{n-1},O(n+1,1),O(n,1))
and for a concrete pair of vector bundles
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
(
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
).

In the case where
X=G/P\supset Y=G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}
are flag varieties and the vector bundles
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}
are those associated to representations of the parabolic subgroups
P
and
P^{\prime}
respectively, T. Kobayashi proposed a method (
the F-method

[
9
]
) to construct and classify all differential symmetry breaking operators

D:C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(G^{\prime}/P^{\prime},\mathcal{W}).

This F-method allows us to reduce the problem of constructing differential symmetry breaking operators to the problem of finding polynomial solutions of a system of PDE’s by applying the “algebraic Fourier transform” to certain generalized Verma modules, and
to use invariant theory to solve the latter. It is known (
[
14
, Prop. 3.10]
) that if the nilradical of the parabolic subgroup
P
is abelian, then the principal term of the equations of the system of PDE’s is of order two.

In this setting, T. Kobayashi and M. Pevzner used the F-method to construct differential symmetry breaking operators for some concrete pairs of hermitian symmetric spaces
X\supset Y
(
[
15
]
). In a slightly different context, T. Kobayashi together with B. Ørsted, P. Somberg and V. Souček used the F-method to solve a similar problem in the setting of conformal geometry. In addition, in
[
12
]
all conformal symmetry breaking operators for differential forms on spheres were constructed and classified by T. Kobayashi, T. Kubo and M. Pevzner.

In the present paper, we consider the case
(X,Y,G,G^{\prime})=(S^{3},S^{2},SO_{0}(4,1),SO_{0}(3,1))
and study Problem
1.1
for a concrete pair of vector bundles
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})=(\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})
(see (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) for the definition). We consider these specific bundles in order to think of differential symmetry breaking operators between
any
pair of principal series representations of
SO_{0}(4,1)
and
SO_{0}(3,1)
.

In particular, we divide Problem
1.1
into the following two problems:

Problem A
.

Give necessary and sufficient conditions on the parameters
\lambda,\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
such that the space

\text{{Diff}}_{SO_{0}(3,1)}\left(C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1}),C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})\right)

(1.1)

of differential symmetry breaking operators

\mathbb{D}_{\lambda,\nu}^{N,m}:C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})

is non-zero. In particular, determine

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\text{{Diff}}_{SO_{0}(3,1)}\left(C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1}),C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})\right).

Problem B
.

Construct explicitly the generators

\mathbb{D}_{\lambda,\nu}^{N,m}\in\text{{Diff}}_{SO_{0}(3,1)}\left(C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1}),C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})\right).

In the case
N=m=0
, Problems A and B above are known to be completely solved (see, for instance
[
7
,
13
]
). When
N=1
, a solution to Problems A and B is given in
[
12
]
for
m=0
(see
[
21
, Rem 1.5]
) and in
[
21
]
for
|m|\geq 1
. Therefore, the problems above have been completely solved when
N=0,1
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In the present paper, we consider the general case
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and solve Problems A and B when the parameter
m
takes the special value
|m|=N
(see Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
respectively). Moreover, although for a general
(N,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, Problems A and B are still unsolved, by using the F-method we prove that, when
|m|\geq N
, they are equivalent to the problem of solving a certain system of ordinary differential equations (see Theorem
5.2
and Remark
5.4
). Since the nature of the appearing system is quite different when
|m|=N
and when
|m|>N
, we solve this system when
|m|=N
in this paper (which allows us to solve Problems A, B), and consider the case
|m|>N
in another paper we expect to publish soon.

At the end, we show the existence of a duality between the cases
m
and
-m
when
|m|\geq N
(see Proposition
8.2
), proving that the solution to Problems A and B for
m\leq-N
is equivalent to that for
m>N
; therefore, it suffices to solve one of the cases to obtain the whole solution.

Throughout the text, we use the following notation:
{\mathbb{N}}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
,

.

Let

We recall the definition of a

Definition 2.1

(
[

)

A continuous linear map

p\left(\text{{supp }}Tf\right)\subset\text{{supp }}f,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }f\in C^{\infty}(X,\mathcal{V}).

G
is a reductive Lie group acting transitively on

of

The proof is a bit long and uses several technical results, including properties of renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials and imaginary Gegenbauer operators which are listed in the appendix (Section

In order to solve this system, we follow a strategy divided in three phases. Below we give a brief explanation of what does each phase consist of:

Phase 1
:

Solve equations

and obtain

up to two constants, say

.

Phase 2
:

Inductively, for

and by using the expressions of

obtained in Phase 1. After that, check that these

satisfy equations

we obtain two different expressions (one coming from the

side and another coming from the

side).

Phase 3

Check when the two expressions for

After completing Phases 1 and 2, we will have that the space of solutions

Remark 7.6
.

An important consequence from the lemma above is that
f_{0},\ldots,f_{N}
solve
(A_{j}^{+}),(B_{j}^{+})
(for all
j=0,\ldots,N)
if and only if (
7.12
) holds for any
j=0,1,\ldots,N
whether
\Gamma_{0}^{+}
vanishes or not.

Next, we consider the case
\Gamma_{0}^{-}=0
. In this case, we can still apply Lemma
7.1
(2) recursively to obtain all

. However, we cannot assure that the constants
c_{j}^{-}
appearing in this process must vanish in order to solve

. That is why in this case we have to carry forward all these constants until the step

. It will be after that, in Phase 3, when we will be able to assure that they all must vanish.

In order to state the main lemma of this part, let us fix some notation.

Given
j=0,1,\ldots,N
,
s=1,\ldots,N
and
n=0,1,\ldots,N+s
, and given constants
c_{N-s}^{-}
,
c_{N-s-2}^{-}
,
\ldots
,
c_{N-s-2\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}^{-}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we define a polynomial
P_{-j}(t)\in{\mathbb{C}}[t]
as follows:

•

For
j=N-s+1,N-s+2,\ldots,N
we set
P_{-j}\equiv 0
.

•

For
j=N-s-n=0,1,\ldots,N-s
we set

P_{n+s-N}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}c_{N-s-2k}^{-}\left(\sum_{d=k}^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}M(n,k,d)(it)^{n-2d}\right).

Here, the coefficients

are defined as follows for

and

where
(a)_{\ell}
denotes Pochhammer’s falling factorial defined as

(a)_{0}=1\kern 4.62497pt\text{ and }(a)_{\ell}=a(a-1)\cdots(a-\ell+1).

When

is even, we define
M(n,k,\frac{n}{2})
as

\displaystyle M(n,k,\frac{n}{2})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ for }0\leq k\leq\frac{n-2}{2},\\
1,&\text{ for }k=\frac{n}{2}.\end{cases}

The definition of the polynomials

may seem arbitrary. However, it is the key to solve the system when
\Gamma_{0}^{-}=0
, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 7.7
.

Suppose that

is given by (

, or equivalently, that

and

\displaystyle+M(n,k,\ell)(n-2\ell)(n-2\ell-1)-2M(n-1,k,\ell)(n+s)(n-2\ell-1)\Big)

Remark 7.8

Proof.

9
Appendix: About Gegenbauer Polynomials

In this section, we define the
renormalized
Gegenbauer polynomials
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}
and discuss some of their properties. These Gegenbauer polynomials are just a renormalized version of the well-known classical Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{\ell}^{\mu}
, named after the Austrian mathematician Leopold H. Gegenbauer
[
4
]
. The key property of these polynomials is that they solve the Gegenbauer differential equation.

9.1
Renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the ultraspherical polynomial (also known as Gegenbauer polynomial), is defined as follows (
[
1
, Sec. 6.4]
,
[
3
, Sec. 3.15.1]
):

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{\ell}{2}]}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\ell-k+\mu)}{\Gamma(\mu)k!(\ell-2k)!}(2z)^{\ell-2k}.

This polynomial satisfies the Gegenbauer differential equation
G_{\ell}^{\mu}f(z)=0
, where
G_{\ell}^{\mu}
is the Gegenbauer differential operator:

G_{\ell}^{\mu}:=(1-z^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-(2\mu+1)z\frac{d}{dz}+\ell(\ell+2\mu).

Note that
C_{\ell}^{\mu}\equiv 0
if
\mu=0,-1,-2,\cdots,-[\frac{\ell-1}{2}]
. In order to obtain a non-zero polynomial for any value of the parameters, we renormalize the Gegenbauer polynomial as follows:

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{\ell}(z):=\frac{\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+[\frac{\ell+1}{2}]\right)}C^{\mu}_{\ell}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{\ell}{2}]}\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\ell-k)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+[\frac{\ell+1}{2}]\right)}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!(\ell-2k)!}(2z)^{\ell-2k}.

(9.1)

We write below the first five Gegenbauer polynomials.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{0}(z)=1
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{1}(z)=2z
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{2}(z)=2(\mu+1)z^{2}-1
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{3}(z)=\frac{4}{3}(\mu+2)z^{3}-2z
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{4}(z)=\frac{2}{3}(\mu+2)(\mu+3)z^{4}-2(\mu+2)z^{2}+\frac{1}{2}
.

Observe that from the definition, all terms of
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
have the same parity, namely, the parity of
\ell
. Moreover, the number of terms is at most
\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]+1
. Depending on the value of
\mu
, some terms may vanish, but the term of degree
\ell-2\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]\in\{0,1\}
is non-zero for any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
By a direct computation one can show that this term is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]}}{\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]!}(2z)^{\ell-2\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]}.

Note that if
\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]=0
, then
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
is constant for any
\ell\geq 0
. For
\ell<0
we just define the renormalized Gegenbauer polynomial as
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}\equiv 0
.

The next result shows that the solutions of the Gegenbauer differential equation on
\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}
are precisely the renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials, where

\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{even}}=\text{{span}}_{\mathbb{C}}\{z^{\ell-2b}:b=0,1,\ldots,\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]\}.

Theorem 9.1

(
[
15
, Thm 11.4]
)
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
the following holds.

\{f(z)\in\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}:G_{\ell}^{\mu}f(z)=0\}={\mathbb{C}}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z).

In
{\mathbb{C}}[z]\setminus\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}
, the Gegenbauer differential equation may have other solutions depending on the parameters
\mu,\ell
. For a concrete statement of this fact see
[
15
, Thm. 11.4]
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
imaginary
Gegenbauer differential operator
S_{\ell}^{\mu}
is defined as follows:

S_{\ell}^{\mu}=-(1+t^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}-(1+2\mu)t\frac{d}{dt}+\ell(\ell+2\mu).

(9.2)

This operator and
G_{\ell}^{\mu}
are in the following natural relation.

Lemma 9.2

(
[
12
, Lem. 14.2]
)
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[z]
define
g(t)=f(z)
, where
z=it
. Then,

\left(S_{\ell}^{\mu}g\right)(t)=\left(G_{\ell}^{\mu}f\right)(z).

9.2
Algebraic properties of renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials and imaginary Gegenbauer operators

In this section we collect some useful properties of (imaginary) Gegenbauer operators and (renormalized) Gegenbauer polynomials. We start by showing some algebraic identities involving derivatives and follow by recalling one three-term relation among Gegenbauer polynomials.

Given
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the following gamma factor:

\gamma(\mu,\ell):=\displaystyle{\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+2}{2}\right])}{\Gamma(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right])}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\ell\text{ is odd},\\
\mu+\frac{\ell}{2},&\text{if }\ell\text{ is even}.\end{cases}}

(9.3)

Note that this definition can be extended to
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. For
\ell<0
it suffices to take
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ell+2k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and define
\gamma(\mu,\ell):=\gamma(\mu-k,\ell+2k)
.

Another remarkable property of
\gamma(\mu,\ell)
is that it satisfies the following identity, which is straightforward from the definition:
