Multi-omics analysis reveals the pathobiome-host interactions in the bleaching disease of the seaweed Saccharina japonica
Authors: Mengxin Wang, Cangming Zhao, Weifeng Gong, Qin Lin, Xiaoyu Zhang, Fei Sun, Mingyu Ma, Teng Guo, Gaoge Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emerging concept of the pathobiome has revolutionized our understanding of disease etiology by emphasizing the complex interactions between multiple pathogens and their hosts during disease progression. Although significant advancements have been made in characterizing the pathobiome in human, animal, and plant diseases, the pathobiome associated with seaweed diseases remains unexplored. Saccharina japonica, a commercially important farmed seaweed, has increasingly suffered from bleaching disease during its nursery stage, severely compromising the supply of healthy sporelings and sea field cultivation. Despite its significant economic consequences, the pathobiome associated with this bleaching disease and its interactions with the host remain unclear, posing a major challenge for disease control.ResultsThrough multi-omics and meta-omics analyses, we identified the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica and elucidated its interactions with the host. The pathobiome is dominated by the core taxa Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.), which employ virulence mechanisms such as chemotaxis, motility, and toxin secretion to initiate infection. In response, the host S. japonica activates a multifaceted defense, including mechanisms like cell wall strengthening, reactive oxygen species bursts, and antibiotic production to combat the invading pathobiome. To counteract these host defenses, the core pathobiome taxa upregulate genes associated with antioxidant enzymes and antibiotic resistance, enabling their establishment and persistence within the host.ConclusionsThis study provides the first analysis of the pathobiome in seaweed diseases. By identifying the core taxa of the pathobiome, their virulence mechanisms, and the host defense responses, we elucidate the pathobiome-host interactions underlying S. japonica bleaching disease. These findings significantly advance our understanding of the pathobiome in seaweed diseases and lay the groundwork for developing targeted strategies to control the bleaching disease in seaweed aquaculture. Introduction

The traditional pathological paradigm of “one pathogen-one disease” based on Koch’s postulates has been increasingly challenged by the complexity of infectious diseases [1, 2]. Meta-omics studies have shown that disease onset and progression are frequently driven by dynamic interactions among multiple pathogens and their hosts [3]. This shift in understanding culminated in the conceptualization of the “pathobiome,” initially introduced by Ryan in 2013 to describe the community of enteric pathogens associated with infantile diarrhea [4]. Nowadays, the pathobiome is broadly defined as a collection of organisms that interact with the host and contribute to its health decline, encompassing prokaryotes, eukaryotes, and viruses [2]. The pathobiome concept has been widely applied in research on human diseases [5, 6], complex tree diseases [7], and aquaculture-related diseases, such as monodon slow growth syndrome in shrimp [8].

The evolution of meta-omics technologies, particularly through advancements in high-throughput sequencing, has been instrumental in propelling pathobiome research [3, 9]. These approaches facilitate comprehensive detection of disease-associated microbiomes, providing new insights into disease etiology. 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and metagenomic analyses have demonstrated that shifts from a healthy microbiome to a pathobiome can trigger disease, with enriched pathogens emerging as core taxa that drive disease progression through the expression of virulence genes [10, 11]. Such microbial shifts have been implicated in various human diseases, such as irritable bowel syndrome [12] and cutaneous leishmaniasis [13]. In plants, similar shifts in microbial communities have also been observed. Integrated metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses have identified Brenneria goodwinii and Gibbsiella quercinecans as core taxa of the pathobiome associated with acute oak decline [14, 15]. These core taxa accumulate in diseased tissues and contribute to tissue lesions by expressing virulence genes associated with plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (PCWDEs), type II secretion systems (T2SS), and flagellar assembly [14, 15].

In response to pathogen infections, hosts have evolved sophisticated defense mechanisms. In plants, the cell wall constitutes the primary physical barrier against pathogens [16]. Upon infection, the cell wall undergoes dynamic remodeling, encompassing both chemical and structural modifications that enhance host resistance and restrict pathogen spread [17, 18]. Additionally, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and pathogen effectors can be recognized by plant receptors, triggering robust innate immune responses [19]. Notably, the rapid production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) represents one of the earliest and most critical immune defense mechanisms [19, 20]. ROS not only directly damage the pathogens but also function as signaling molecules to activate the expression of defense‐related genes, thereby playing a pivotal role in host immunity [21]. Furthermore, plants can also reinforce their defenses through metabolic reprogramming, as exemplified by the production of antibiotics via tryptophan metabolism in rice, which effectively suppresses the rice blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae [22]. Correspondingly, pathogens have evolved various strategies to escape host immunity, including enzymatic ROS detoxification and antibiotic resistance [23]. Thus, the pathogen-host interaction is a dynamic and ongoing battle, with each side continually adapting to the other, shaping the progression and outcome of disease.

To date, the pathobiome concept has not been applied to the study of seaweed disease. However, emerging studies have linked seaweed diseases to multiple pathogenic bacteria, some of which are likely opportunistic [24, 25]. Opportunistic pathogenic bacteria are those present on healthy seaweeds but can become pathogenic when the environment deteriorates or the seaweed defenses are compromised. For instance, under conditions of reduced salinity and light intensity, Vibrio sp. P11 and Cytophaga sp. P25, which are normally present on healthy Kappaphycus alvarezii, proliferate and dominate the epibacterial community, triggering ice-ice disease [25]. Similarly, bleaching of the red macroalga Delisea pulchra has been associated with a variety of opportunistic bacteria [26, 27]. 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and metagenomic analyses have revealed that bacteria of the Rhodobacteraceae, Saprospiraceae, and Flavobacteriaceae families, which are present on healthy D. pulchra, become key causative agents of bleaching disease when the algae’s defenses are weakened by high temperatures [27, 28]. Interestingly, no single bacterial lineage possesses all the virulence genes associated with bleaching disease, suggesting that the disease results from synergistic interactions among multiple bacteria species [27]. Infection experiments have further confirmed that taxonomically distinct bacteria can induce pigment loss in D. pulchra tissues [26]. Despite the identification of several pathogens involved in seaweed diseases, the mechanisms of their virulence and their interactions with seaweed hosts remain poorly understood.

Saccharina japonica is one of the most economically important farmed seaweeds globally [29]. Like terrestrial crops, S. japonica is susceptible to various diseases during its nursery and field cultivation stages, with severe cases resulting in yield losses of 20–50% [30, 31]. In 2022, a severe outbreak of bleaching disease occurred at a nursery in Guanwu, Fujian Province, China. Bleaching diseased S. japonica sporelings exhibited significant bleaching and tip decay, severely compromising the availability of healthy sporelings for local farms. To date, the causative agents of this bleaching disease and their interactions with the seaweed remain unclear. Here, by combining microscopic observations with multi- and meta-omics analyses, we (a) characterized the symptoms of bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings, (b) explored the pathobiome and its virulence mechanisms behind the disease, (c) investigated the host defense responses, and (d) elucidated the pathobiome-host interactions. The findings of this study will enhance our understanding of pathobiome-host interactions in seaweeds and provide a theoretical foundation for disease prevention and control during the nursery stage, contributing to the health and sustainability of the seaweed farming industry.

Materials and methods

Sample source and experimental design

Healthy and bleached sporeling samples used in this study were collected from the S. japonica nursery farm located in Guanwu, Fujian Province, China (26°19′27″N, 119°48′33″E) on November 29, 2022.

The experimental design was illustrated in Fig. 1.

Schematic diagram of the study design. All images of the instruments were sourced from the manufacturer’s official website. HS, healthy sporelings; DS, bleached sporelings. Scale bars, culture photographs: 5 cm; microscopic images: 25 μm

Observation of bleaching symptoms in S. japonica sporelings

Substrata containing healthy and bleached S. japonica sporelings were separately collected with sterile scissors and placed in sterile plastic bags. These samples were then transported to the laboratory within 24 h, using ice packs to maintain a low temperature. Upon arrival, the substrata were carefully disassembled in sterile seawater with tweezers. The healthy and bleached sporelings were subsequently examined under a dissecting microscope (ZSA6745, COIC, Chongqing, China) and a light microscope (Eclipse Ni, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to observe their macroscopic and microscopic characteristics, respectively. Images were captured using a microscopic imaging system (DS-Ri2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Omics sample collection

Healthy and bleached S. japonica sporelings were collected for omics analyses. A five-point sampling method was used, with one point at each corner and the center of each substrate (40 × 115 cm2), covering 10 × 10 cm2 per point. At each point, 1 g of sporelings was collected with sterile scissors. Samples from the five points on each substrate were pooled into a single 50-mL sterile Eppendorf tube as one biological replicate. The number of biological replicates for DNA sequencing, RNA sequencing, and untargeted metabolomic profiling was three, four, and six, respectively. After collection, samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and transported in dry ice to the laboratory, where they were stored at −80°C.

DNA extraction and metagenomic analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The DNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (ND-2000, Thermo Fisher, Wilmington, DE, USA), and its integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. For sequencing, 1 μg of DNA from each sample was used to construct sequencing libraries, which were then sequenced using the paired-end approach on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing reads were quality controlled with Trimmomatic (v0.36) to eliminate adapter sequences and low-quality reads [32]. The quality-controlled reads were subsequently aligned to the S. japonica genome (https://db.cngb.org/search/project/CNP0000364/) using the BWA-MEM algorithm (v.0.7.17) to remove host sequences. The resulting clean reads were assembled into contigs with MegaHit (v1.1.1-2-g02102e1) [33]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using META Prodigal (v2.6.3) [34], and a non-redundant gene set was generated by clustering with CD-HIT (v4.8.1) [35]. Clean reads from each sample were aligned against this gene set, and gene abundance was quantified based on the number of mapped reads, normalized using the transcripts per million (TPM) formula: TPM = 106 × (mapped reads/gene length)/sum(mapped reads/gene length). Taxonomic classifications of genes were assigned based on the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) using GTDB-Tk (v2.1.1) [36, 37].

Metagenomic binning analysis

Metagenomic binning was performed on the assembled contigs using metaBAT2 (v2.11.1) [38]. The completeness and contamination of the resulting metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were assessed with CheckM (v1.1.1) [39]. MAGs with completeness >50% and contamination <10% were classified as medium- to high-quality and retained for further analyses. MAGs from different samples were then dereplicated using dRep (v2.3.2) [40]. MAG abundance was quantified using the MetaWRAP quant bins module [41]. Taxonomic classifications of the MAGs were conducted with GTDB-Tk (v2.1.1) based on the GTDB database [36, 37]. Gene function annotation for each MAG was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [42] and carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) [43] databases. Virulence genes were identified based on the specific KEGG pathways (Table S1), and those involved in ROS detoxification, including K03782 (katG), K03781 (katE), K04564 (SOD2), K03386 (ahpC), K03387 (ahpF), K00425 (cydA), K02259 (ctaA), and K04565 (SOD1).

RNA extraction and metatranscriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and treated with DNase I (TaKara, Kyoto, Japan) to eliminate genomic DNA. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and a NanoDrop microspectrophotometer (ND-2000, Thermo Fisher, Wilmington, DE, USA), respectively. The rRNA was removed with Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and sequencing libraries were prepared using a TruSeqTM RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing reads were quality controlled with Trimmomatic (v0.36) to remove adaptor contaminants and low-quality reads [32]. The quality-controlled reads were subsequently aligned to the S. japonica genome using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.17) to filter out host sequences. The rRNA-related reads were removed by aligning the clean reads to the SILVA SSU (16S/18S) and SILVA LSU (23S/28S) databases with SortMeRNA (v2.1b) [44]. Remaining reads were assembled with MegaHit (v1.1.1-2-g02102e1) [33], followed by RNA-based gene prediction and construction of a non-redundant gene set. RNA-based gene abundance was estimated by mapping non-rRNA clean reads to this gene set with BWA-MEM (v.0.7.17), and TPM was calculated. The RNA-based taxonomic profiling of each sample was conducted using GTDB-Tk (v2.1.1) based on GTDB database [36, 37].

Metatranscriptomics analysis of MAGs

Reference indexes of MAGs nucleotide sequences were generated using Kallisto with a default k-mer length of 31 [45]. Paired-end clean reads from metatranscriptomic sequencing were mapped to these indexes using the quant subroutine in Kallisto with default settings [45]. TPM value was calculated to quantify the expression level of each transcript. The resulting data were then organized using Perl scripts to generate expression abundance for the MAGs [46].

Transcriptomics analysis of S. japonica

The quality-controlled RNA-Seq reads mapped to the S. japonica genome were assembled using StringTie (v. 1.3.1) [47]. Gene expression levels were calculated using the TPM method. Gene function annotation was performed based on the KEGG database [42].

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted to validate the RNA-seq data. RNA extracted during sequencing was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript® II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The qRT-PCR assays were performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 system with THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO, Japan) in four biological replicates. The gene tuba2 (Gene ID: SJ07828) was used as the reference for normalization. The qRT-PCR program was set as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Relative expression of each gene was calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. Primer sequences for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S2.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis based on liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

For metabolite extraction, 100 mg aliquots of each sample were cryogenically grounded into fine powder using liquid nitrogen. The powder was then resuspended in 500 µL of ice-cold 80% methanol (v/v) and vortex-mixed vigorously for 60 s. After 5 min ice incubation, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g, 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted with LC-MS grade water to a final methanol concentration of 53% (v/v). Following transfer to new microtubes, samples were re-centrifuged at 15,000 × g, 4 °C for 5 min. The final supernatant was separated using a Vanquish ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany) and analyzed with the Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany). For background subtraction, 53% methanol-water solutions (v/v) served as process blanks and were processed in parallel with experimental samples. Additionally, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 10 µL aliquots from each experimental sample and were analyzed intermittently throughout the sequence to monitor instrumental stability and reproducibility.

The metabolome raw data were processed in Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany) for peak alignment (retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; m/z tolerance, 5 ppm) and peak extraction (m/z tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity deviation, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100,000 counts). Missing values (<30% missingness) were imputed via k-nearest neighbors (k=5), followed by log₂-transformation and total spectral intensity normalization. Molecular formulas were predicted based on adduct ions, molecular ion, and MS/MS fragments. Metabolite annotations were primarily assigned to level 2 confidence by matching experimental MS/MS spectra against public databases mzCloud (MS/MS similarity ≥40) and mzVault (match score ≥50). Metabolites unidentifiable at level 2 were annotated at level 3 confidence based on exact mass matches using the MassList database (mass error <5 ppm). Functional annotation of metabolites was performed via KEGG [42], HMDB [48], and LIPID MAPS databases [49]. Prior to differential analysis, Pareto scaling was applied to equilibrate metabolite contributions.

Statistical analyses

Shannon index calculation and Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) were performed online with the platform http://www.cloud.biomicroclass.com/CloudPlatform/home. Differences in the bacterial relative abundance and transcriptional activity across various taxa were determined using Welch’s t test with a significance level of p < 0.05. Virulence genes with transcripts relative abundances > 0.01% in at least five samples were analyzed for differences in transcriptional activity using Welch’s t test, with statistical significance determined by p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. The R statistical package edgeR (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) was used for differential expression analysis of S. japonica genes, with thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) based on metabolite abundance was performed using metaX software [50]. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed with the adonis function of the R vegan package, and the adonis p value was generated based on 1000 permutations. Metabolites with OPLS-DA VIP ≥ 1, p value (t test) ≤ 0.05, and |log2FC| ≥ 2 were considered as differential metabolites. For correlation analysis, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated and visualized using the OmicShare tools (http://www.omicshare.com/tools).

Results

Symptoms of bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings

Compared with healthy sporelings (Fig. 2A), bleached sporelings exhibited bleaching symptoms at the tips of the thalli (Fig. 2B, indicated by the black arrows). Microscopic observation showed that chloroplasts in healthy cells were brown and evenly distributed (Fig. 2C). In contrast, chloroplasts in bleached cells appeared green or white and gathered around the cell walls (Fig. 2D, indicated by the black arrows).

Differences in bacterial communities associated with healthy and bleached S. japonica sporelings

To investigate the microbial dynamics associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings, we conducted metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses to characterize the composition and transcriptional activity of bacterial communities in both healthy and bleached sporelings (Tables S3 and S4). Sequencing yielded a total of 679,577 non-redundant bacterial genes and 143,327 mRNA transcripts, providing a robust dataset for downstream analyses (Tables S5 and S6).

We first assessed whether the structure and composition of the bacterial communities of S. japonica sporelings altered during the bleaching disease. Alpha diversity, measured using the Shannon index, and beta diversity, evaluated through Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), showed no significant differences in either diversity or community structure of the bacterial communities between healthy and bleached sporelings (p > 0.05; Fig. S1). Despite this, taxonomic profiling uncovered notable shifts in community composition. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria dominated both healthy and bleached sporelings, while Verrucomicrobiota and Bacteroidota were more abundant in healthy and bleached sporelings, respectively (Fig. 3A). At the genus level, Bradyrhizobium, Litorimonas, and Sulfitobacter were dominant in healthy sporelings, whereas Arenicella, Polaribacter, SCGC-AAA160-P02, and NORP294 were enriched in bleached sporelings (Fig. 3B).

C, D Transcriptional activity of bacterial communities at the phylum (C) and genus (D) levels. P values were from Welch’s t test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001) in B and D. Error bars represented mean ± SD. HS, healthy sporelings; DS, bleached sporelings

Transcriptional activity of the bacterial communities also showed significant differences between healthy and bleached sporelings. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota exhibited significantly higher transcriptional activity in bleached sporelings compared with the healthy counterparts (Fig. 3C). At the genus level, Arenicella, Zoogloea, Glaciecola, and Polaribacter showed increased transcriptional activity in the bleached sporelings (Fig. 3D).

Collectively, both the composition and transcriptional activity of the bacterial community in bleached sporelings undergo significant changes compared to the healthy ones. These shifts may reflect the establishment of a pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings.

Core pathobiome taxa and their virulence mechanisms in bleaching-diseased S. japonica sporelings

To identify the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings, we constructed MAGs by binning shotgun metagenome contigs. A total of 37 medium- to high-quality MAGs were obtained, all of which were present in both healthy and bleached sporelings (Table S7 and Fig. S2). Of these 37 MAGs, 21 and 23 MAGs exhibited significant differences in relative abundance and transcriptional activity between healthy and bleached sporelings, respectively (Fig. 4A and Fig. S3). Notably, Bin_4 (Endobugula sp. 000209515), Bin_26 (Litorimonas sp.), Bin_27 (Akkermaniaceae), Bin_31 (Sulfitobacter sp.), and Bin_33 (Endobugula sp.) were significantly more abundant and transcriptionally active in healthy sporelings (Figs. 4A and S3). In contrast, Bin_2 (TMED27 sp.), Bin_6 (NORP294 sp.), Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_10 (Hyphomonadaceae), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), Bin_18 (JACESI01 sp.), Bin_21 (GCA-2708415 sp.), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) were markedly enriched and more transcriptionally active in bleached sporelings, suggesting their potential role as components of the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease (Fig. 4A and Fig. S3).

To further identify the core taxa of the pathobiome, we functionally annotated genes within the MAGs that were enriched and transcriptionally active in bleached sporelings. Virulence genes (VGs) were identified in all these MAGs (Fig. 4B). Strikingly, more than 20% of the VGs in Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) were upregulated in bleached sporelings compared with the healthy counterparts (Fig. 4B). Therefore, Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) were identified as the core taxa of the pathobiome, given their significant enrichment in bleached sporelings and the upregulation of the majority of their VGs.

To elucidate the virulence mechanisms of these core pathobiome taxa, we functionally categorized their VGs. Distinct virulence functions were observed across different core taxa, indicating their unique virulence mechanisms (Fig. 4C). Specifically, VGs involved in flagellar assembly (3 genes encoding the flagellin FilC), pilus assembly (1 gene encoding the type IV pilus assembly protein PilA), and cytotoxins (1 gene encoding a putative hemolysin TlyC) were particularly active in Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.), respectively (Fig. 4D). Additionally, genes encoding the heat shock protein HtpG were upregulated in Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.) and Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), whereas VGs involved in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis were active in Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775) and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, genes related to antioxidant enzymes, including katG, SOD2, ahpC, and ahpF, were significantly upregulated in Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.) and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) (Fig. 4D). VGs associated with PCWDEs, bacterial secretion systems, two-component system, global regulators of virulence, biofilm formation, quorum sensing, antibiotic resistance, and bacterial chemotaxis were upregulated across all the core taxa of the pathobiome (Fig. 4D).

Transcriptomic reprogramming mediated by the core pathobiome taxa in S. japonica sporelings

PCoA and PERMANOVA analyses revealed significant differences in gene expression profiles between healthy and bleached S. japonica sporelings (Fig. 5A). Specifically, we identified 983 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in bleached sporelings compared with the healthy ones, with 492 genes significantly upregulated and 491 genes significantly downregulated (Fig. 5B).

To elucidate the functional implications of the transcriptional changes, we annotated all the DEGs using the KEGG database. A total of 204 DEGs were successfully annotated to specific proteins (Table S8). Notably, DEGs involved in cell wall strengthening (e.g., genes encoding mannuronan C-5-epimerase and cellulose synthase), heat shock response (two genes encoding heat shock protein), ROS burst and protection (e.g., genes encoding putative respiratory burst oxidase proteins, superoxide dismutase, and peroxidase), cell signaling (e.g., two genes encoding LRR-GTPase of the ROCO family), antibiotic production (e.g., 23 genes encoding vanadium-dependent iodoperoxidase), and defense and stress response (21 genes) were significantly upregulated in bleached sporelings (Fig. 5C). Conversely, downregulated DEGs were partly associated with processes such as development regulation, photosynthesis, protein transport, energy metabolism, damage repair, and organelle organization (Fig. S4). The reliability of the RNA-seq results was validated by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), which confirmed that the expression patterns of seven randomly selected DEGs from various pathways were consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. S5).

To explore whether S. japonica transcriptional changes were a response to the pathobiome infection, we performed correlation analysis between the top 30 DEGs (by relative abundance) and the core pathobiome taxa (Fig. S6). Significant correlations were observed between most DGEs and the core taxa, particularly for DGEs involved in antibiotic production, defense and stress response, and cell wall strengthening (Fig. S6).

Metabolic reprogramming mediated by the core pathobiome taxa in S. japonica sporeling holobionts

Using an untargeted metabolomic approach, we compared the metabolic profiles of healthy and bleached S. japonica sporeling holobionts (Table S9). Orthogonal Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) and PERMUTATION test revealed significantly separation between the metabolic profiles of healthy and bleached sporeling holobionts, indicating substantial metabolic reprogramming in the bleached holobionts (Figs. 6A and S7). In total, we identified 120 differential metabolites (DMs) in the bleached holobionts compared to the healthy counterparts, with 114 metabolites significantly upregulated and six metabolites downregulated (Fig. 6B and Table S10). Analysis of the KEGG pathways indicated that DMs were only enriched in the pathway of galactose metabolism (Fig. 6C). Further categorization of DMs based on database annotations revealed significant upregulation of metabolites belonging to terpenoids (13 metabolites), glycerophospholipids (14 metabolites), eicosanoids (nine metabolites), and flavonoids (six metabolites) in bleached holobionts (Fig. 6D).

To investigate whether the metabolic reprogramming in the S. japonica sporeling holobionts was linked to the pathobiome infection, we conducted correlation analyses between the top 30 DMs (by relative abundance) and the core pathobiome taxa (Fig. S8). The analysis indicated that the majority of DMs were significantly correlated with the core taxa, suggesting a potential link between metabolic reprogramming and the pathobiome (Fig. S8).

Discussion

The “pathobiome” concept has revolutionized our understanding of infectious diseases, shifting the focus from single-pathogen models to a more holistic view that encompasses the complex interactions between multiple pathogens and their hosts [2, 9]. Although the pathobiome has been implicated in various diseases affecting humans [5, 6], animals [8, 51], and plants [7, 15], its role in seaweed diseases remains unexplored. In this study, we employed multi- and meta-omics approaches to identify the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in commercially farmed S. japonica sporelings, elucidating its virulence mechanisms and dynamic interactions with the host.

Pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings

Disease progression is often accompanied by a shift from a healthy microbiome to a pathobiome dominated by multiple pathogenic taxa [2, 3]. In this study, we observed significant alterations in the bacterial communities of bleached S. japonica sporelings, with increased abundance and/or transcriptional activity of Glaciecola, Polaribacter, and Arenicella genera. Glaciecola is widely distributed in marine environments and on macroalgae [52–54]. Its ability to degrade algal polysaccharides, particularly agar, carrageenan, and alginates [54], suggests that Glaciecola may contribute to bleaching disease by breaking down the seaweed cell wall. Similarly, Polaribacter is also known for its capacity to degrade macroalgal polysaccharides [55, 56]. Although Arenicella, a genus commonly found in seaweeds, has been implicated in necrotic diseases in benthic marine animals [57, 58] and wasting disease in the seagrass Zostera muelleri [59]. However, no direct association between Arenicella and seaweed disease has been reported to date. The significant increase in both abundance and transcriptional activity of Arenicella in bleached sporelings suggests a potential involvement of this genus in the development of bleaching disease. Overall, the associations of Glaciecola, Polaribacter, and Arenicella with seaweed diseases indicate that the bacterial community in bleached sporelings has shifted toward a pathobiome.

Within this pathobiome, Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) emerged as core taxa, demonstrating significant upregulation of virulence genes in bleached sporelings. These core taxa likely function as opportunistic pathogens, becoming virulent under deteriorating environmental conditions, as evidenced by their presence at lower levels in healthy sporelings. However, the specific environmental triggers (e.g., elevated temperature, increased light intensity, or altered pH) activating their virulence remain to be elucidated.

Notably, the pathogens associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings differ markedly from those responsible for bleaching disease in the red algae D. pulchra, where Rhodobacteraceae, Saprospiraceae, and Flavobacteriaceae have been identified as the causative agents [27, 28]. This divergence suggests that similar disease phenotypes in different seaweed species may result from distinct pathogenic communities, underscoring the complexity of marine disease.

Virulence mechanisms of the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings

The successful infection of pathogens is mediated by an integrated suite of virulence mechanisms, primarily encompassing host adhesion, directed motility, and toxin production. Our analyses revealed significant upregulation of genes associated with chemotaxis and motility in the pathobiome core taxa, including those encoding methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins (MCPs), chemotaxis proteins (CheW and CheA), and flagellar and pilus assembly proteins (FilC and PilA). MCPs, a family of chemoreceptors, detect environmental signals and transmit them to the flagellum via chemotactic proteins such as CheW, CheA, and CheY, enabling pathogens to modulate their movement and direction toward host surfaces [60]. Mutations in these genes encoding MCPs, chemotaxis proteins, or flagella assembly proteins have been demonstrated to significantly attenuate pathogen virulence [61–63]. Additionally, type IV pili, dynamic filamentous appendages on the bacterial surface, also play crucial roles in motility and adhesion, as demonstrated in pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa [64]. Consequently, these findings suggest that the pathobiome employs chemotaxis and motility mechanisms to facilitate host colonization.

Following initial host attachment, pathogenic bacteria typically enhance their infectivity through coordinated toxin production and secretion. Consistent with this, we observed significant upregulation of genes encoding both endotoxins and exotoxins in the pathobiome core taxa. In particular, genes involved in LPS biosynthesis were upregulated in Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775) and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.). As a potent non-protein endotoxin, LPS serves as a critical virulence factor in pathogenic bacteria by eliciting robust host immune responses [65]. Within the exotoxin category, Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) exhibited significant upregulation of the gene encoding membrane-disrupting toxin TlyC. TlyC homologs have been demonstrated to mediate phagosomal vacuoles lysis, thereby facilitating pathogenic bacteria spread [66]. Furthermore, the expression of genes encoding alginate lyase, a type of PCWDEs, was significantly upregulated in the pathobiome core taxa. These enzymes specifically target alginate, a major structural polysaccharide in S. japonica cell walls, compromising host tissue integrity and facilitating pathogen infection [67]. The coordinated upregulation of genes encoding T2SS, which mediate alginate lyase secretion [68], in these core taxa suggests an active extracellular enzyme secretion mechanism during infection. Collectively, the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease may infect S. japonica sporelings by employing a multifaceted virulence strategy involving chemotaxis, motility, toxin production, and cell wall degradation.

Defense strategies of S. japonica sporelings against the pathobiome

To defend against pathogen infections, host organisms typically initiate resource reallocation and activate comprehensive defense mechanisms. In line with this, S. japonica sporelings undergo significant metabolic reprogramming during the pathobiome infection, redirecting energy resources from photosynthesis, energy metabolism, and organelle organization toward defense-related processes, including cell wall strengthening, ROS bursts, and antibiotic production. Specifically, genes encoding mannuronan C-5-epimerase were significantly upregulated in bleached sporelings. Mannuronan C-5-epimerase plays a pivotal role in catalyzing the reshaping of alginate, resulting in cell wall strengthening [69]. As the primary physical barrier in plants, cell wall strengthening represents a crucial first line of defense against pathogen infection [16, 17]. Concurrently, S. japonica sporelings activated innate immune responses. The significant upregulation of genes encoding transmembrane receptor kinases and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) proteins indicated the activation of sophisticated immune defense mechanisms. As pattern recognition receptors, transmembrane receptor kinases can initiate pattern-triggered immunity by recognizing PAMPs, whereas LRR proteins mediate effector-triggered immunity by recognizing pathogen-derived effectors [19]. These two immune pathways synergistically trigger stream signaling cascades, including ROS bursts and defense-related gene expression, thereby enhancing host resistance to pathogens [19]. The upregulation of genes encoding respiratory burst oxidase homolog proteins in bleached sporelings further underscores the central role of ROS in host defense mechanisms. These oxidases catalyze the transfer of electrons to molecular oxygen, generating ROS that directly target and kill the pathogens [70]. Furthermore, bleached sporelings exhibited significant upregulation of genes encoding vanadium-dependent haloperoxidases, including vanadium-dependent bromoperoxidase and vanadium-dependent iodoperoxidase. These enzymes catalyze the oxidation of halides to produce antimicrobial halogenated compounds, analogous to the halogenated furanones in D. pulchra that effectively inhibit bacterial virulence [71, 72]. Additionally, elevated levels of some metabolites with antibiotic properties, particularly terpenoids and flavonoids among the plant secondary metabolites [73], were observed in the holobiont of bleached sporelings, potentially indicating the activation of defense mechanisms associated with antibiotic production. Collectively, S. japonica sporelings appear to employ an integrated, multi-layered defense strategy against pathobiome infection, combining structural reinforcement through cell wall strengthening, ROS-mediated oxidative defense, and chemical defense via antibiotic production.

Pathobiome strategies to overcome host defenses

To establish sustained infections, pathogenic organisms must develop effective countermeasures against the host defense mechanisms. Our findings reveal that the pathobiome core taxa employ multiple strategies to circumvent host immune responses. A primary defense mechanism involves the upregulation of biofilm formation-related genes. Biofilms are polymeric structures produced by bacteria on host surfaces, which not only facilitate bacterial attachment but also create a protective microenvironment that shields bacteria from host immune attacks [74]. This biofilm-mediated protection appears to be a crucial strategy for immune evasion in the pathobiome. The core taxa also significantly upregulated genes encoding antioxidant enzymes, including catalases-peroxidases, superoxide dismutase, and alkyl hydroperoxide reductase. These antioxidant enzymes play a critical role in detoxifying ROS generated by the host, converting them into water and molecular oxygen to mitigate oxidative stress. This antioxidant defense mechanism was also observed in various well-characterized plant pathogens, such as Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris, and Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 [75, 76]. In addition to overcoming oxidative stress, the pathobiome core taxa have developed robust antibiotic resistance mechanisms. Notably, the mexE gene, encoding a component of the MexEF-OprN efflux pump, was upregulated in the pathobiome core taxon Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.). MexEF-OprN efflux pump, a major multidrug resistance system in Pseudomonas species, actively expels various antibiotics from bacterial cells, thereby reducing intracellular antibiotic concentrations and enhancing resistance [77]. Furthermore, genes encoding penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), such as mrdA, mrcA, and ftsI, as well as ATP-binding cassette transporters like amiABC were also upregulated in the pathobiome core taxa. PBPs are essential peptidoglycan transpeptidases and are primary targets for β-lactam antibiotics [78]. Previous studies have shown that the production of PBPs with lower affinity for specific β-lactams can confer significant antibiotic resistance [79], suggesting that the pathobiome core taxa may utilize a similar resistance mechanism. In parallel, ATP-binding cassette transporters are involved in the efflux of antimicrobial peptides [80], providing an additional layer of antibiotic resistance. Collectively, the pathobiome employs a multifaceted strategy to overcome host defenses, incorporating biofilm formation for physical protection, enzymatic ROS detoxification to mitigate oxidative stress, and multiple antibiotic resistance mechanisms. The coordinated expression of these defense-countering strategies appears essential for successful infection establishment and maintenance in S. japonica sporelings.

In summary, the pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings is primarily composed of the core taxa Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.). Disease onset is driven by complex interactions between the pathobiome and the host, involving multiple virulence mechanisms and host defense responses (Fig. 7). Although our findings contribute to expanding the baseline knowledge of pathobiome-host interactions in S. japonica bleaching disease, it is important to note that not all bacterial strains within the pathobiome could be successfully isolated, purified, and cultured for experimental validation. Using traditional culture-dependent techniques, only Glaciecola sp. nov. 3-19, a constituent of the pathobiome, was successfully isolated and shown to induce bleaching symptoms in S. japonica sporelings under laboratory conditions. This highlights the need for innovative methods to isolate and study seaweed-associated pathogenic bacteria, which will be crucial for validating pathobiome-host interactions and unraveling the mechanisms of disease progression.

Conclusions

Collectively, our study identified a pathobiome associated with bleaching disease in the commercially farmed seaweed S. japonica at nursery stage and elucidated its complex interactions with the host. This is the first study to introduce the pathobiome concept into the etiology of seaweed disease. Through multi- and meta-omics analyses, we identified Bin_7 (Glaciecola sp.), Bin_12 (Arenicella sp. 017854775), and Bin_22 (Arenicella sp.) as the core taxa of the pathobiome. These core taxa infect the host by upregulating virulence genes involved in chemotaxis, motility, and toxin secretion. Simultaneously, gene expression of the core taxa related to antioxidant enzymes and antibiotic resistance enable them to counteract the host defense mechanisms, including cell wall strengthening, ROS bursts, and antibiotic production, enabling sustained infection. Our findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the complex interactions between the pathobiome of bleaching disease and its host S. japonica sporelings. These insights advance our knowledge of the underlying pathogenesis of bleaching disease in S. japonica sporelings and offer a foundation for targeted disease management strategies.
