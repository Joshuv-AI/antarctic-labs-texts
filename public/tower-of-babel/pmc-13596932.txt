Exploratory multivariate adaptive regression splines modeling of myopic progression in children receiving atropine
Authors: Jun-Wei Chen, Chieh-Han Yu, Tzu-Chi Liu, Chi-Jie Lu, Tzu-En Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The increasing global prevalence of pediatric myopia has led to the widespread use of atropine for myopia control. Despite its proven efficacy, variable treatment responses and complex interactions among refractive error, treatment duration, and dosage make individualized treatment challenging. Therefore, clinicians require decision support tools for personalized treatment management.Methods:This retrospective study analyzed 1545 pediatric myopic eyes treated with topical atropine at Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (Taipei, Taiwan) between 2005 and 2008. The multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS) approach was used to examine associations between baseline clinical characteristics, atropine exposure, changes in the spherical equivalent (SE), and myopic progression. Eight clinical predictors, including age, sex, Baseline SE, intraocular pressure (IOP), IOP changes, treatment duration, and cumulative and average monthly atropine dosages, were used. The model performance was evaluated via repeated cross-validation and benchmarked against standard linear regression.Results:Baseline SE was the strongest predictor of myopic progression, followed by cumulative atropine exposure and average monthly dosage. The MARS model demonstrated robust predictive performance (root mean square error = 0.540) and identified the baseline SE, cumulative atropine exposure, and average monthly dosage as the principal predictors of myopic progression. The average monthly dosage showed a nonlinear V-shaped relationship with a transition point near 11.66 mg/mo.Conclusion:The baseline refractive status and atropine exposure were the principal factors associated with treatment response and may support individualized myopia management. The observed V-shaped pattern may reflect the treatment transition characteristics of the historical high-concentration atropine era (0.1%-1.0%), during which the atropine dosage was frequently adjusted according to the clinical response. This nonlinear pattern suggests that the treatment response varied across different levels of atropine exposure, warranting further clinical interpretation. Therefore, children receiving intermediate monthly dosages may represent a heterogeneous group undergoing physician-directed dose escalation, resulting in an apparent nonlinear relationship. Accordingly, this pattern most likely reflects real-world treatment strategies, rather than the intrinsic pharmacological effects of intermediate atropine dosages. These findings should be used for hypothesis generation and require prospective validation before influencing clinical dosing decisions.

1. INTRODUCTION

Myopia has emerged as a major global public health concern. Projections suggest that by 2050, nearly half of the world’s population will be affected by this condition, and up to 10% will develop high myopia. Children who develop myopia early have a higher risk of progressing to high myopia. Early interventions are needed to slow disease progression and enable tailored treatment.1–3 Topical atropine eye drops are widely recognized as safe and effective in managing myopic progression. The ATOM2 study demonstrated that low-dose atropine reduces refractive changes with minimal side effects, a result confirmed by large trials such as Low-Concentration Atropine for Myopia Progression and Atropine Treatment Long-Term Assessment Study.4–6 Despite the proven efficacy of atropine, interindividual variability in treatment response remains a significant challenge. Regression models typically use predictors such as age, sex, baseline spherical equivalent (SE), and parental myopia. However, they often miss complex variable interactions and rely on assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity.7,8

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) offer compelling alternatives. ML models have demonstrated superior performance in handling nonlinear high-dimensional datasets. Several studies have begun applying ML to predict myopic treatment strategies.9–13 Wu et al.9 used ML models such as XGBoost to identify key predictors for changes in intraocular pressure (IOP) under atropine therapy. Peng et al.11 used an XGBoost model to predict the onset of myopia based on individual daily information. Chen et al.12 incorporated Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to explain the interindividual variability and contributors in IOP responses to atropine treatment. The International Myopia Institute (IMI) emphasizes early intervention for children at risk of developing myopia. With myopia now included in the ICD-11 and WHO action plans, efforts are being made to standardize its diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment.7,14,15 ML can uncover hidden patterns that inform precise treatment strategies, offering real-time support for clinical decision-making.

This study used ML models and real-world data from a medical center to explore the changes in myopic SE in children treated with topical atropine. The baseline SE (Base SE), IOP, treatment duration, cumulative atropine dosage, and demographics were examined to identify risk factors and support personalized atropine therapy for myopia progression.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study design and population

This retrospective observational study utilized de-identified clinical records from the Department of Ophthalmology at the Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (Taipei, Taiwan).

Data were collected under routine clinical practice rather than using a standardized research protocol, which reflects real-world variability in treatment and follow-up schedules. Data were collected from pediatric patients who underwent topical atropine treatment for myopia control between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2008. Initially, data from 2706 eyes were screened using predefined eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). First, to ensure that all participants had completed the follow-up, 367 patients with incomplete data due to loss to follow-up were excluded. Second, as this study focused on SE, 276 patients with other ocular comorbidities were excluded. Third, 324 participants aged outside the 3 to 18-year range at baseline were excluded, as this study focused on children. Fourth, 168 patients who concomitantly used other cycloplegic agents were excluded. Finally, participants who were prescribed corticosteroids or anti-glaucoma medications were excluded (n = 26). After applying the predefined eligibility criteria, 1545 eyes were included in the final analysis. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (IRB no. 20220706R). All patient data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality, in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines.

2.2. Clinical measurements and variables

The ocular examinations of children with myopia included slit lamp evaluations and refraction assessments. The refractive error was measured using an autorefractor (RK5; Canon, Tokyo, Japan). When myopia was suspected, cycloplegia was induced with three drops of 1% cyclopentolate at 10-minute intervals, and cycloplegic autorefraction was performed at least 30 minutes after the final drop. Autorefraction was repeated three times before and after cycloplegia at each visit. The IOP was measured using non-contact tonometry (Xpert NCT Plus; Reichert, Leica Inc., Depew, New York, USA), with patients in a seated position and without topical anesthesia. The primary outcome variable was the change in SE (SE difference; denoted as Y, diopters), which was calculated as the myopic SE at the final follow-up visit minus the Base SE (X1). The following predictor variables were also included: Base IOP (X2; mmHg), IOP difference (X3; change in IOP over the treatment period), sex (X4), age at baseline (X5), total duration of atropine treatment (X6; months), cumulative atropine dosage (X7; mg), and average monthly atropine dosage (X8; mg/mo). The IOP difference (X3) was calculated as the IOP at the final visit minus the IOP at baseline. The treatment duration (X6) was defined as the interval between the initial atropine prescription and final clinic visit and was restricted to the study period of 2005 to 2008. Atropine was prescribed at concentrations of 1.0%, 0.5%, 0.25%, or 0.1% (Oasis Chemical Ind. Co., Yilan, Taiwan (R.O.C.)), according to the clinical severity of myopia. Each bottle contained 50, 25, 12.5, or 5 mg of atropine. The prescribed dosage at each visit was calculated as the number of bottles multiplied by the atropine content per bottle. The total cumulative dosage (X7) was determined by summing the doses prescribed throughout the study period. To normalize the treatment intensity, the average monthly dosage (X8) was calculated by dividing the cumulative dosage by the total number of treatment months. Notably, X8 was a derived, normalized variable constructed for modeling purposes to enable comparison across patients with different follow-up durations. X8 did not represent an exact, real-world measure of the actual dosing frequency, prescribed concentration changes, or patient adherence, which could not be captured retrospectively.

2.3. ML modeling scheme

To explore SE differences and obtain interpretable feature information supporting the diagnosis, multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS) was used. MARS is a nonparametric extension of the regression method capable of capturing nonlinear relationships among features.16 This is achieved using splines with a piecewise linear structure. For each predictor feature, MARS identifies the most suitable combinations of splines and then joins them together. These joints, known as knots, indicate the corresponding cut points of the feature. In summary, the combination of splines and knots forms the basis function (BF) of a corresponding feature, and the MARS equation is constructed from the BFs of all features.

Fig. 2 shows the modeling scheme used in this study to construct effective models and identify the most impactful features. The data were randomly split into training and testing sets, with the model built on the former and evaluated on the latter. A five-fold cross-validation was performed to tune the hyperparameters of the MARS model. This construction process was repeated 100 times, with feature importance rankings generated in each run and aggregated by averaging. To identify the most influential features affecting the SE difference, a stepwise approach was applied based on the aggregated rankings, rebuilding MARS models by sequentially adding features in order of importance. Finally, after selecting the optimal feature set, the corresponding MARS equation was generated.

The performance was evaluated using three error metrics: the root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and relative absolute error (RAE). Furthermore, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO)17 was built following the same process as MARS for performance comparison, to ensure that using MARS was reasonable.

3.2. Model and feature performance

Table 2 summarizes the predictive performance of the LASSO and MARS models across 100 repeated runs, with the corresponding SD. The performance of both models was similar across the three metrics. Specifically, LASSO achieved an RMSE of 0.535, MAE of 0.392, and RAE of 0.917, whereas MARS achieved an RMSE of 0.540, MAE of 0.393, and RAE of 0.918. To further confirm that MARS and LASSO had similar performances, a Student’s t test at a significance level of 5% was performed. The p values shown in the table demonstrated no significant differences in the three metrics between MARS and LASSO. While MARS and LASSO showed similar accuracies, MARS was chosen because of its ability to detect nonlinear patterns and thresholds via spline-based functions. This is valuable in pharmacology, such as in atropine therapy, where dose–response relationships may be nonlinear.

This study identified a nonlinear association between the average monthly atropine dosage and myopia progression, as demonstrated by the combined effects of BF3 and BF4 for the total average dosage (X8) in Fig. 6. Although atropine treatment was associated with reduced myopia progression across the studied dosage range, the observed relationship was not linear. An apparent transition point was observed at approximately 11.66 mg/mo. Rather than indicating reduced treatment efficacy at this dosage, this pattern more likely reflected differences in disease severity, dose adjustment during follow-up, and individualized prescription strategies in routine clinical practice. Patients with slower disease progression were often maintained at lower atropine dosages, whereas those with faster disease progression were more likely to receive dose escalations during follow-up. Therefore, the observed nonlinear pattern should be interpreted as reflecting the real-world treatment selection rather than supporting a specific optimal or suboptimal atropine dosage.

4. DISCUSSION

This study utilized MARS, an exploratory ML model, to analyze the factors influencing myopic progression (SE differences) in children receiving atropine treatment. Our MARS analysis identified the Base SE, total cumulative dosage, and total average dosage as the main predictors of myopic progression. Base SE (X1) was the strongest predictor, which aligns with findings from other studies showing that higher baseline myopia leads to faster progression.7,8,14 The ATOM2 and ATLAS trials confirm these findings, and the IMI notes that an early onset is linked to a risk of high myopia.4,6,15 Our findings, derived from a data-driven ML approach, robustly reaffirm the importance of early intervention and personalized patient care based on the initial refractive status. This observation supports stratified treatment approaches for children with more negative SE at baseline.18

However, our analysis demonstrated a negative correlation between the total cumulative dosage (X7) and SE difference, showing that higher cumulative doses were associated with greater myopic progression (Fig. 5). This association may be explained by confounding indications, as children who experience faster progression tend to receive more intensive treatment. Other factors may contribute, including longer exposure, varying adherence, or unmeasured risks linked to both higher dosing and greater progression. Given these potential confounding indications, a causal inference regarding the relationship between the cumulative dosage and treatment outcome is not warranted in the present retrospective, non-randomized design. This hypothesis-generating association requires prospective randomized studies to separate drug effects from prescription bias and other confounders.

Our analysis identified a nonlinear association between the average monthly atropine dosage (X8) and myopia progression, with a transition point at approximately 11.66 mg/mo (Fig. 6). This dosage does not represent an optimal or suboptimal treatment threshold but is more likely to reflect differences in disease severity, treatment adjustments during follow-up, and real-world prescription practices. This hypothesis-generating finding requires prospective validation before application to individualized treatment decisions. For illustrative purposes only and not as a direct clinical dosing recommendation, this dosage is equivalent to approximately one bottle of 0.25% atropine (12.5 mg) or two bottles of 0.1% atropine (5 mg each) per month. Clinicians may use this reference as a conceptual benchmark, pending prospective validation, when control is inadequate. Similarly, this nonlinear, threshold-based pattern is broadly consistent with the finding by Chuang et al.19 that stepwise escalation of atropine concentrations benefits children with rapid progression. However, the results contrast with the generally linear dose-dependent efficacy reported in some controlled trials,20,21 which is further supported by the long-term ATLAS finding that early sustained atropine therapy is associated with greater choroidal thickness into adulthood.22

Interestingly, the total treatment duration (X6) ranked differently between LASSO and MARS, suggesting that prolonged therapy engagement, irrespective of the dose, may exert a protective effect reflecting both pharmacologic efficacy and behavioral compliance, consistent with several cohort studies.5,6,19 IOP differences (X3) were important in LASSO but less so in MARS. This divergence likely reflects methodological differences; LASSO may overweight correlated variables, whereas MARS attenuates the effect of the IOP once nonlinear relationships are modeled. Furthermore, this finding aligns with those of prior pediatric studies showing no notable IOP change with the administration of topical atropine,13,23 although monitoring IOP remains important for long-term safety. Age (X5) and sex (X4) showed relatively low importance despite the emphasis the IMI places on an early age of onset as a risk factor for high myopia,7,14 suggesting that biometric and pharmacologic factors may outweigh demographic variables in predicting SE progression.

This exploratory study had several limitations. The MARS model showed moderate predictive performance, indicating the capability to identify key determinants and nonlinear dose-response patterns but the need for improved accuracy for clinical use. Most data involved higher atropine concentrations (0.1%-1%), while lower, more commonly prescribed doses (0.01%, 0.025%, and 0.05%) were absent, limiting the generalizability to current practices. Because the dataset (2005-2008) reflected this earlier, higher-concentration prescribing era, the identified 11.66 mg/mo breakpoint is an exploratory finding specific to this dose range and should not be directly extrapolated to contemporary low-dose (0.01%-0.05%) atropine regimens without further validation. Future studies should include lower concentrations to improve the relevance of these results. Additionally, the actual treatment adherence was not measured because of missing compliance data, which could have affected the outcomes; future studies should address this gap.

To further enrich the predictive framework, future research could incorporate advanced ML methodologies.24 Using interpretable AI methods such as SHAP can improve the clinical trust and usability of ML models for real-world myopia prediction. SHAP quantifies the impact of each predictor on an individual’s risk, helping clinicians understand model decisions and facilitating future research in this area.

Our study aligns with current public health goals, as organizations such as the WHO and IMI support the early detection and management of myopia, particularly in children with premyopia.7,14,15 Recent research highlights the importance of considering environmental and behavioral factors, such as outdoor activities and near work. With advances in wearable sensors, integrating real-time behavioral data into our ML model could enhance myopia prediction and serve as a digital screening tool in schools and primary care.

Our current study lacked certain biometric parameters and baseline characteristics, such as axial length, choroidal thickness, and parental myopia, which influence the progression of myopia. The inclusion of biometric parameters, particularly the axial length and choroidal thickness, has been shown to substantially improve predictive accuracy.21,25 Given the interplay between environmental and genetic factors in myopia progression, enriching the feature set would provide a more holistic and precise risk profile. Multicenter validation studies are critical to ensure the generalizability across diverse ethnic and geographical populations. Our study was based on Asian cohorts, necessitating external validation before broader clinical implementation. Finally, this study provides research ideas and a foundation for future studies integrating ocular biometrics, genetic profiles, and environmental exposure. The interpretability of the proposed model ensures that such complex systems are clinically applicable. This can enable scalable personalized interventions at the population level.

In conclusion, the baseline refractive status and atropine exposure were the principal factors associated with treatment response in pediatric myopia. The observed nonlinear relationship between the atropine dosage and treatment response suggests that increasing the dosage may not always result in greater therapeutic benefit, supporting individualized treatment rather than routine dose escalation. Because these findings were derived from retrospective data collected during the historical higher-concentration atropine era (0.1%-1.0%), they should be used for hypothesis generation and require prospective validation before application in clinical practice.
