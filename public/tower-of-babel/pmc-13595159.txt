First human infection caused by Nocardia gipuzkoensis in Japan
Authors: Shiori Kitaya, Hideaki Ohyagi, Takashi Yaguchi, Yoshiko Muramoto, Saori Takemura, Misa Muraoka, Sadaya Matano, Hajime Kanamori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 80-year-old man with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma developed pulmonary nocardiosis caused by Nocardia gipuzkoensis, with a concomitant central nervous system lesion. The isolate was identified as N. gipuzkoensis by secA1 gene sequencing. To our knowledge, this is the first reported human infection caused by N. gipuzkoensis in Japan.

Nocardia gipuzkoensis is a newly recognized Nocardia species first described in 2020, and only a few human infections have been reported worldwide [[1], [2], [3]]. We report a case of pulmonary nocardiosis caused by N. gipuzkoensis accompanied by a central nervous system (CNS) lesion in an 80-year-old man with a history of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL).

The patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and gastric cancer. Five years before presentation, a Nocardia species was isolated from sputum obtained during the evaluation of chronic airway inflammation; however, antimicrobial therapy was not initiated, and the patient was followed without treatment. He was subsequently diagnosed with DLBCL at the age of 76 years and achieved first remission after treatment. During routine follow-up after achieving remission of DLBCL, progression of chronic airway inflammation was observed, and repeat sputum culture again yielded a Nocardia species (Supplementary Fig. 1). Because 16S rRNA gene sequencing failed to achieve species-level identification, additional secA1 gene sequencing was performed, which identified the isolate as N. gipuzkoensis (Supplementary Method 1), and treatment with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT) monotherapy was initiated.

The patient subsequently developed fever, gait disturbance, and cognitive impairment. Chest computed tomography demonstrated bronchiectasis and a right lower lobe nodular opacity with surrounding patchy consolidation, while brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed an enhancing lesion in the splenium of the corpus callosum (Fig. 1). Disseminated nocardiosis involving the lungs and CNS was suspected. Cerebrospinal fluid culture, performed eight days after the initiation of antimicrobial therapy, was negative, and blood cultures were not performed. Based on the antimicrobial susceptibility testing results of the N. gipuzkoensis isolate recovered from sputum, the antimicrobial regimen was subsequently changed from SXT monotherapy to combination therapy with meropenem, amikacin, and SXT (Supplementary Table 1). Because CNS relapse of DLBCL could not be completely excluded, localized radiotherapy (32.5 Gy in 5 fractions) was delivered to the lesion in the splenium of the corpus callosum. Tirabrutinib was subsequently initiated but discontinued after 7 days because of poor tolerability. High-dose methotrexate was not administered because of concerns regarding infection exacerbation. After approximately 9 weeks of intravenous therapy, treatment was switched to oral agents. Owing to intolerance to tetracyclines, the oral antimicrobial regimen was subsequently modified several times and ultimately changed to combination therapy with SXT, amoxicillin-clavulanate, and amoxicillin. Follow-up imaging demonstrated marked improvement of both the CNS lesion and the pulmonary infiltrate. Nocardiosis primarily affects immunocompromised hosts and may disseminate to the CNS [4]. In the present case, advanced age, diabetes mellitus, and DLBCL with its associated treatment likely contributed to susceptibility to infection. Previously reported N. gipuzkoensis infections have mainly involved pulmonary disease in patients with structural lung abnormalities such as bronchiectasis [1,2]. In the present case, a CNS lesion was observed in addition to the pulmonary lesions; however, it was difficult to clearly determine whether the CNS lesion represented disseminated N. gipuzkoensis infection or involvement by DLBCL.

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns vary among isolates, including reports of SXT resistance [3], emphasizing the importance of susceptibility-guided therapy. In addition, accurate identification may require molecular targets beyond 16S rRNA because of the close relationship between N. gipuzkoensis and the N. abscessus complex. In this case, secA1 sequencing enabled definitive species-level identification, highlighting its diagnostic utility [5].

To our knowledge, this is the first reported human infection caused by N. gipuzkoensis in Japan. This case describes pulmonary nocardiosis caused by N. gipuzkoensis accompanied by a CNS lesion and underscores the value of secA1 gene sequencing for accurate species-level identification.
