Conception-calibrated male pediatric tumor mitotic clocks
Authors: Jeremiah V. John, Darryl Shibata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular clocks can reconstruct tumorigenesis, but their calibration is limited by uncertainty in cancer ages. Pediatric cancers simplify this problem because age ranges are narrowly bounded by conception and a minimum of ~30 divisions. We developed mitotic clocks from rapidly fluctuating CpG (fCpG) DNA methylation on the X chromosome in male cancers, applying a two-state Markov model to estimate mitotic age and epimutation rates. Across acute lymphoblastic leukemia, acute myeloid leukemia, neuroblastoma, and embryonal brain tumors, modeled and observed methylation data were highly concordant, yielding epimutation rates of ~10-3 per division and tumor mitotic ages between 30 and several hundred divisions. The clocks also resolved remission dynamics, inferring relapses seeded by small numbers of variably dormant residual cancer cells. By exploiting the unique age constraints of male pediatric tumors, calibrated fCpG clocks provide a quantitative framework to reveal otherwise hidden features of human tumor evolution.

Introduction

Many aspects of human tumorigenesis cannot be directly observed, but past events can be inferred by analyzing somatic alterations that accumulate in the final tumor1,2. A challenge for molecular clock methods is that their conclusions are inferential, and few cancers have natural histories that allow for robust validation. Relative to adult cancers, pediatric cancers provide a simpler modeling framework because their development timelines are more tightly constrained, particularly for tumors resected in infancy. In such cases, maximum tumor age is limited by conception, and a minimum of ~30 cell divisions to reach detectable size.

A limitation of pediatric clocks is that few somatic alterations are detected in these tumors³. To overcome this, we measured rapidly fluctuating methylation at CpG sites (fCpGs), which undergo balanced cycles of methylation and demethylation3. This approach is orders of magnitude more informative than mutation-based clocks, with epimutation rates of ~10⁻³ per division compared to ~10⁻⁹ for mutations. fCpG sites can resolve even short intervals, as their methylation is polymorphic in tumors resected during the first months of life. The clock is further simplified with monoallelic X chromosome fCpGs in male cancers. We developed fCpG molecular clocks for pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), neuroblastoma (NB), and embryonal brain tumors (ETMR). These clocks reconstructed tumorigenesis, remission, and relapse, with simulations that closely matched experimental data. More broadly, pediatric cancer datasets provide a bounded temporal framework to train and validate clock methods that may extend to older children and adults.

Results

fCpG mitotic clock development

fCpG clocks were developed using pediatric ALL, AML, ETMR and NB methylation array data4–8. Methylation in male progenitor cells starts at either 0 or 100% on X-chromosome fCpG sites. With rapid and balanced fCpG methylation and demethylation, average methylation will drift towards 50% as the tumor grows (Fig. 1A). Conceptually, fCpG sites in mitotically younger cancers are synchronized by the pattern in their progenitor cells, with more sites near 0 or 100% methylation. Older cancer populations are more polymorphic with average fCpG methylation near 50%. fCpG sites, with average methylation between 40 and 60% in their cancers, had little overlap between the cancer specific clocks, and no fCpG site was shared by all four clocks (Fig. 1B).

Mitotic age was quantified using a “drift” metric—the mean deviation of all fCpG sites from 50% methylation—where low drift values indicate mitotically older cancers. Variation in drift across tumors was modeled using a two-state (binary) Markov process to infer relative ages, which were then converted to numerical mitotic age estimates. No tumor was allowed to begin growth before conception or to be younger than 30 cell divisions. To reflect the rarity of each diagnosis before birth, onset boundaries were set at −2 months for ALL, −4 months for AML, -4 months for NB, and 4 months of age for ETMR (see Methods).

Average fCpG methylation distributions differed across individual cancers (Fig. 1C). Mitotically younger cancers had bimodal “U” shaped distributions, likely reflecting synchronization between cells by the binary states (methylated or unmethylated) in their progenitor cells (Fig. 1A). Older cancers had more unimodal distributions, likely reflecting more divisions and stochastic fluctuating methylation and demethylation between cells. Cancers removed from the youngest infants generally had more unimodal distributions, indicating that cancers detected shortly after birth are not necessarily more rapidly growing.

Mitotic ages (divisions from growth onset and resection) spanned ~30 to several hundred divisions (Fig. 1D). As an internal check, per-site epimutation rates were estimated from single tumor values. Consistent with an overall binary error process, epimutation rates varied between fCpG sites but, on average, closely matched estimates from pooled tumor values (Fig. 1E). Using cancer-derived mitotic ages, these epimutation rates were converted to per-division methylation flip rates for each fCpG, ranging from 0.0016 to 0.011 per division per site.

Patient ages at the start of tumor growth were inferred from cancer mitotic ages, assuming one division per day (Fig. 2). No cancers were inferred to start growth before conception, albeit inferred starts would be earlier or later if tumor cells respectively divide slower or faster than once per day. Average mitotic ages were respectively 103, 101,158 and 57 divisions for ALL, AML, ETMR and NB. The NB clock is testable with orthogonal data because population screening and surgical resection of NBs during the first year of life did not reduce mortality9. Consistent with this failure, all high-risk NBs were inferred to begin growth after one year of age and thus could not have been intercepted by infancy screening (Fig. 2B). The NB clock indicates that tumors growing during the first year of life were those that tend to regress spontaneously10, with many initiating growth before birth. Taken together, the clocks infer when pediatric cancers begin to grow, show that growth dynamics are similar across ages, and align with the findings from large-scale NB infancy screening studies.

Reproducibility and progression of the AML clock were assessed using serial adult AML samples14 collected during treatment (Fig. 3C). Although age estimates over the few weeks varied, there were significant increases in mitotic age over time. Within patient fCpG methylation patterns were more similar across time points than between patients. The serial data support a molecular clock model in which fCpG methylation is typically copied between divisions while stochastic epimutations accumulate through time.

Pediatric AML diagnosis-relapse pairs6 exhibited more divergent trajectories over time, with many showing discordant fCpG methylation, consistent with continued epimutations during remission (Fig. 3D). In most cases, relapse mitotic ages were much lower than their remission intervals, suggesting dormancy during remission rather than the monotonic mitotic age increases seen in cancers without remission (Fig. 3C). Remission dynamics can be potentially reconstructed by comparing fCpG methylation between diagnosis and relapse (see below).

Cancer simulations with fCpG clocks

Calibration of hundreds of fCpG sites with empirically derived error rates enables a novel test: whether simulations initiated from a single progenitor cell can reproduce the methylation patterns observed in hundreds of real cancers. For each cancer type, simulations used the same fCpG sites and error rates, with mitotic age as the cancer-specific variable. Simulated final-stage methylation for ALL, AML, ETMR, and NB cancers closely matched experimental data, both in overall drift (Fig. 4A) and in Pearson correlations across fCpG sites (Fig. 4B). Inference of progression between diagnosis and relapse

fCpG clocks were used to reconstruct remission (Fig. 5A). Differences between diagnosis and relapse patterns reflect the numbers and dormancy of residual cancer cells responsible for relapse. To simulate remission, we used approximate Bayesian computation (ABC15) and methylation profiles at diagnosis and relapse, along with the number of intervening days, to infer three parameters: (i) number of relapse-initiating cells (1–32), (ii) latency fraction (0–0.995; proportion of divisions without net growth), and (iii) mitotic quiescence (0.001–1; proportion of mitotic activity per calendar day). Simulations were accepted if both drift and Pearson correlation between simulated and observed relapse methylation approximated experimental values. Acceptable parameter combinations were recovered for all ALL16, ETMR7 and NB17 pairs, but not for 8 of 73 AML6 pairs. The AML pairs that failed ABC had shorter remissions (117 days versus 402 days for successful ABC), suggesting scenarios outside the small-residual-cell-latency remission model. There was a diversity of possible residual cancer cell, latency, and quiescent combinations that reproduced the experimental data (Fig. 5). Relapse occurred with one or more residual cancer cells (Fig. 5B), and all cancers showed evidence of dormancy with different degrees of latency and mitotic quiescence (Fig. 5 C,D). Across cancer types, ALL and NB generally exhibited higher latency and quiescence, with fewer residual relapsing cells (often one), and fewer divisions during latency. AML and ETMR showed heterogeneity with examples with less latency and quiescence, and more residual relapsing cells.

fCpG clock remission inferences are orthogonally testable with remission dynamics inferred by mutation differences between the same paired diagnosis and relapse samples. ETMR relapse samples harbor many more mutations than diagnostic samples, with clear chemotherapy-associated mutational signatures, consistent with substantial mitotic activity during remission7. By contrast, NB relapse samples also contain more mutations than at diagnosis, but many of these variants are detectable at low frequency in the diagnostic sample, indicating that relatively few new mutations are acquired during remission17. Supporting this dormancy, NB cells sampled during remission often display non-cycling persister cell signatures18. Consistent with these findings, the fCpG clocks inferred very few cell divisions during NB remission compared with ETMR (Fig. 5E). ETMR mutations gained during remission scale with both remission duration and the numbers of inferred latency divisions (Fig. 5F), whereas in NB, mutations gained do not increase with remission time or with the few inferred remission divisions. Overall, fCpG clocks parameterized on primary cancers can also resolve remission dynamics, capturing features of latency and mitotic quiescence.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that pediatric cancers can be natural calibrators for tumor molecular clocks. Because the maximum possible age of any pediatric tumor is bounded by conception, these cancers provide a rare setting where molecular time can be anchored to narrow calendar intervals. We exploited this setting to calibrate mitotic clocks based on fCpG sites on the X chromosome in male cancers. A key strength of this approach is that the clock records epimutations that oscillate over short time scales. Many existing cancer molecular clocks are based on cumulative, essentially irreversible mutational processes (e.g., base substitutions), which lack high temporal resolution in pediatric cancers with low mutation burdens19. Other epigenetic clocks can infer aspects of human tumorigenesis20, but our clock3 resets to the pattern of the progenitor cell at start of growth, and balanced methylation and demethylation at fCpG sites provide a high-frequency ( ~ 10-3 per division) “tick” suitable for measuring divisions over months to years. Consistent with high epimutation rates, cancers detected in the first few months of life had polymorphic fCpG methylation (Fig. 1C). Calibrating this epimutation rate in cancers with narrowly bounded ages enables inferences about tumor initiation and progression that have been difficult to obtain with other molecular markers.

The Markov-model calibration revealed that site-specific epimutation rates were consistent with rates inferred from tumor-averaged data, supporting the assumption that these CpG sites function as largely independent two-state systems governed by similar per-division flip processes. Using these rates and the estimated mitotic age for each cancer, simulations reproduced both the drift distributions and site–site correlation structures observed in hundreds of tumors across ALL, AML, NB, and ETMR. This concordance, together with orthogonal data7,9,17,18, strongly supports the validity of the inferred parameters and the binary-drift model.

Applying this clock to paired diagnosis–relapse samples extends its utility to one of the least accessible phases of cancer biology: remission and dormancy, when growth is limited and cells divide more slowly for multiple possible reasons21–24. Small numbers of leukemic stem cells are thought to persist during remission25, leading to relapses that often carry new secondary genetic alterations and frequently originate from minor subclones at diagnosis26,27. Direct observation of residual cancer cell dynamics is rarely possible, yet these dynamics likely affect relapse risk. Using ABC, we inferred three biologically interpretable parameters for each diagnosis-relapse pair: the number of relapse-initiating cells, latency fraction, and the degree of mitotic quiescence. Relapses were consistently inferred to arise from small numbers of residual cancer cells that exhibited at least some dormancy, recapitulating the non-cycling persister phenotype observed in NB remission17,18 and the higher mitotic activity characteristic of ETMR remissions7. Concordance with orthogonal sequencing data strengthens clock validation, while higher clock epimutation rates enable more granular insights into the numbers and dormancy of remission cells that eventually drive relapse.

Reconstructing remission dynamics may help clarify mechanisms of relapse. In ALL, higher latency and quiescence with typically a single residual leukemic stem cell may reflect selection for dormancy under maintenance therapy28,29. The greater heterogeneity inferred for AML and ETMR, with larger numbers of residual cancer cells, may underlie their higher relapse frequencies and the limited efficacy of AML maintenance30.

This analysis has limitations inherent to molecular clocks. It assumes that epimutation rates remain constant during tumor growth and across ages, although age-related stabilization of lineage-tracing methylation has been reported31, and rates during progression, therapy, and aging remain to be better defined. In particular, epimutation rates inferred from primary tumors may be variable during treatment, dormancy or after relapse. The ground-truth boundary anchored to birth or conception was based on relatively few tumors resected in infancy, and ETMR ages were recorded only in years. Additional cases sampled shortly after birth could more precisely define how many epimutations or other alterations accumulate during the earliest months of tumor growth. Restricting the analysis to X-linked fCpGs in male cancers simplifies interpretation and allows application of the relatively simple two-state Markov model, but limits general applicability. Analogous, tissue-specific fCpG clocks have been developed for autosomal sites with different methods in normal tissues3, clonal hematopoiesis in twins32, breast cancers33, and chronic lymphoid leukemia34.

Pediatric cancers, with their more defined timelines, provide unique opportunities to validate and calibrate somatic evolutionary reconstruction methods applicable across all ages. Fast-ticking clocks better match the pace of tumorigenesis, enabling reconstructions over clinically relevant weeks-to-months timescales. Understanding how individual tumors respond to therapy could lead to improved treatments specifically directed towards their resistance mechanisms.

Methods

Cohorts and samples

Methylation array data (Illumina 450 K/EPIC) were obtained from X-chromosome CpG sites and male individuals (Table S1). Methylation beta values were used as provided or obtained from available idat files using minfi35 at standard setting. For the TARGET NB data, purity estimates were provided in the dataset, and samples with less than 40% tumor cells were excluded. Empirically, tumor purity did not confound NB mitotic age estimates, as there was no correlation (R = 0.005) between estimated mitotic ages and tumor purity (Fig. S2).

fCpG clock site identification

For each cancer type, we selected X-chromosome fCpGs using three criteria: 1) Mean methylation between 0.40–0.60 (0.37-0.63 for NB) across tumors of the same type, 2) High variability between individual cancers with CpG site variances between 0.03 and 0.1, and 3) Lack of age-related changes in methylation (Pearson between methylation and age at diagnosis between 0.1 and -0.1). This process produced cancer-specific panels of [416] (ALL, from 137 samples), [754] (AML, from 344 samples), [183] (NB from 76 samples), and [242] (ETMR from 61 samples) sites (Fig. 1B). Overlap across panels was minimal (see Fig. 1B) and no site was shared by all four clocks.

Estimation of mitotic ages

We estimated tumor mitotic ages using a symmetric two-state (“flip”) Markov model of fCpG methylation, chosen because it matches the biology of male X-linked CpGs and requires minimal assumptions. At each fCpG in a single cell the state is either methylated (1) or unmethylated (0); with each cell division, the state can flip with a per-division epimutation rate (ε). In bulk, array β values are the population average of these binary states and, under the model, relax toward 0.50 at a rate determined by ε. The model therefore has only two unknowns—the flip rate ε and the sample’s mitotic age t—without needing to assume a specific growth curve or site-specific selection. The approach works directly on standard methylation array data. We summarized distance from equilibrium with a “drift” metric defined per tumor as the mean absolute deviation of β from 0.50 across the fCpG panel (i.e., average of |β − 0.50 | ).

We implemented a Python workflow (Program 1) of a symmetric two-state Markov process that takes as input the age at diagnosis of each cancer and its average drift value. The program evaluates 500 linearly spaced candidate epimutation (flip) rates ε on [0.001, 0.01] (step ≈ 1.8×10⁻⁵) and selects the ε that minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) between the observed drift and the model-predicted drift computed with t = diagnosis age (assuming one division per day). Estimates are constrained so that no tumor has t < 30 divisions ( < 35 divisions for NB, which typically present as large tumors), and no start occurs earlier than disease-specific bounds (ALL: −2 months; AML: −4 months; NB: - 4 months; ETMR: +4 months relative to birth). The selected ε, the MSE, and the equations used to compute mitotic ages for each cancer type are provided in Table S2.

To obtain epimutation rates for individual fCpG sites, we used a second Python workflow (Program 2) that takes as input (i) tumor-specific mitotic ages and (ii) per-site drift values across tumors. For each fCpG site, the program fits a symmetric two-state Markov (“flip”) model by grid-searching epimutation rates over 0.0001–0.01 (step 0.0001) and minimizing the mean squared error between observed drift and the model expectation across tumors (sites with <3 evaluable tumors are skipped). The best-fitting epimutation rate and its MSE are written to a results file and summarized in Supplemental Data 2.

The two methods for estimating epimutation rates—using pooled drift (Program 1) and per-site data (Program 2)—provide an internal cross-check of the two-state Markov model assumptions, as they are expected to yield similar rates. Although epimutation rates varied across individual fCpG sites (Fig. 1E), the averages were similar to the values estimated from pooled data (Fig. 1D).

Tumor simulations

Tumors were simulated from single founder cells carrying the cancer-specific fCpG panel (Program 3). Founder states were initialized from the observed target tumor methylation: 0% if <40%, 100% if >60%, and random if 40–60%. Cells divided forward under a symmetric two-state “flip” model, copying the parental pattern and applying site specific epimutation rates from Program 2 at each division. Population growth followed a simple branching scheme (one or two surviving daughters per division) and continued until ~105 cells were reached, with total divisions tuned to the inferred mitotic age of the target tumor. We then computed bulk per-site methylation values and sample-level summaries (drift; cross-site Pearson correlation) and compared simulations with data (Fig. 4). Five replicates were run per tumor.

Approximate Bayesian computation

For each diagnosis–relapse pair, we initialized the simulation (Program 4) with the case-specific primary-tumor fCpG profile (from Program 3) and evaluated a discrete parameter grid spanning: N (number of relapse-initiating cells; integers 1–32), L (latency fraction; 0–1, which partitions the remission interval R days into a latency phase L·R with no net growth and a regrowth phase (1 − L)·R), and M (quiescence; 0.005–1, the relative division rate during latency so that lower M indicates greater dormancy). During latency, cell numbers were held constant while fCpGs flipped at the per-site rates inferred in Program 2; during regrowth, we simulated net expansion at 1 division/day until the population reached ~3×10^4 cells. For each grid point (N,L,M)(N,L,M)(N,L,M), we generated a simulated relapse methylation profile and computed two summaries—drift and the cross-site Pearson correlation with the observed relapse profile. Parameter settings were accepted if both summaries met preset tolerances; N, L, M sets with greatest acceptances are reported in Supplemental Data 3 and Fig. 5. Across synthetic datasets (remission intervals 100, 200, 400, 600 and 1000 days and grids of N, L, M), ABC more readily recovered L and latency-division counts than M or N (Fig. S1).

The robustness of our ABC inference was evaluated by calculating 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each diagnosis-relapse pair (Fig. S3, and Supplemental Data 4). In accordance with synthetic data simulations, parameters associated with the timing of regrowth (Latency (L)) exhibited higher precision than those describing relapsing cell number (N) or mitotic quiescence (M) during dormancy. ALL and NB samples generally yielded the most constrained parameter estimates, while AML and ETMR displayed higher inter-patient variability in both point estimates and uncertainty ranges.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses and modeling were performed using custom Python workflows as described in the Methods. Comparisons of paired measurements in serial AML samples (Fig. 3C) were conducted using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; p-values are reported as indicated.

Methylation-based comparisons throughout the study relied primarily on summary metrics rather than formal hypothesis testing. These included the “drift” metric, defined as the mean absolute deviation of CpG methylation (β values) from 0.50 across selected fCpG sites, and Pearson correlation coefficients, which were used as descriptive measures of similarity between methylation profiles (e.g., within-patient vs. between-patient comparisons, and simulated vs. observed data). Pearson correlation was also used as part of the acceptance criteria in ABC analyses.

Model parameters, including epimutation (flip) rates (ε), were estimated by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) between observed and model-predicted drift values using grid search over predefined parameter ranges. Per-site epimutation rates were similarly inferred using MSE minimization across tumors. For ABC inference of remission dynamics, parameter combinations (number of relapse-initiating cells, latency fraction, and mitotic quiescence) were evaluated over discrete grids and accepted if simulated data matched observed drift and Pearson correlation within predefined tolerances (see Methods).

Tumor simulations were performed using a stochastic two-state Markov model with site-specific epimutation rates. For each tumor, five independent simulation replicates were generated to assess reproducibility of inferred methylation patterns. Input data were filtered using predefined criteria, including restriction to X-chromosome CpG sites in male samples, selection of fCpGs based on methylation range and variance thresholds, exclusion of samples with tumor purity below 40%, and omission of CpG sites with fewer than three evaluable tumors.

Uncertainty in ABC parameter estimates was assessed using 95% confidence intervals derived from accepted parameter distributions. All parameter ranges, constraints, and implementation details are specified in the corresponding Methods sections to ensure reproducibility.
