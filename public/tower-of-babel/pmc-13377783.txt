Digital technology use for social cohesion among older adults in rural Myanmar during the civil war: barriers and coping strategies
Authors: Ruth Hkawn Awng, Truc Ngoc Hoang Dang, Kanokwan Tharawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation ageing in Myanmar is occurring amid prolonged political instability and civil war, exacerbating social isolation and vulnerabilities among older adults, particularly in rural areas. While digital technologies have the potential to support social cohesion, little is known about how older adults in conflict-affected rural Myanmar access and use these tools, the barriers they face, and the strategies they adopt to cope with digital exclusion.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in rural communities in Kayin State, southeastern Myanmar. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 30 older adults aged 60 years and above in rural Kayin State, Myanmar. Due to security concerns in the conflict setting, all interviews were conducted online via Zoom. Guided by the Socioecological Model and Stress and Coping Theory, data were thematically analyzed to explore patterns of digital technology use, multi-level barriers, and coping strategies related to social connection during the ongoing civil war.ResultsOlder adults primarily used mobile phones and social media platforms especially Facebook to maintain contact with family members, access information, and support emotional well-being during periods of conflict. These technologies facilitated intergenerational communication, reduced feelings of isolation, and enabled access to timely information related to security and daily living. However, their contribution to social cohesion was often limited by significant barriers, including low digital literacy, financial constraints, unstable electricity and internet connectivity, age-related physical limitations, and concerns about surveillance and phone signal and internet restrictions following the 2021 military coup. Despite these challenges, participants adopted adaptive coping strategies such as relying on family support, sharing devices, and selectively using familiar or low-cost platforms, relocating to access the signals, and adjusting communication practices to avoid surveillance risks.ConclusionDigital technologies provide a limited but meaningful contribution to sustaining social cohesion among older adults in rural, conflict-affected Myanmar by supporting communication, emotional connection, and access to information. However, their contribution remains uneven, as structural inequalities, political constraints, and infrastructural limitations continue to restrict equitable access. Addressing these challenges requires context-sensitive, age-appropriate digital inclusion strategies, alongside sustained investments in rural connectivity and community-based support, to strengthen social connectedness and well-being among older adults in protracted crisis settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07796-x.

Background

Population ageing is accelerating globally, with the number of people aged 60 years and above projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050 [47]. This demographic transition is particularly rapid in East and Southeast Asia, where countries are facing increasing pressures to address the health, social, and economic needs of older populations [12]. Myanmar is undergoing a similar shift, with the proportion of older adults expected to double by mid-century, marking its transition toward an aged society [43]. However, this demographic change is unfolding amid prolonged political instability, armed conflict, and economic crisis, intensifying vulnerabilities among older adults.

In Myanmar, approximately two-thirds of older adults reside in rural areas, where access to healthcare, social protection, and formal income support remains limited [46]. Many older people continue working in agriculture or informal livelihoods due to minimal pension coverage and financial insecurity [17]. Age-related disabilities are common, particularly among women and those living in rural and impoverished settings [44]. These structural disadvantages have been exacerbated by Myanmar’s ongoing civil war, which has disrupted livelihoods, weakened service provision, and fractured social and family networks.

Kayin State in southeastern Myanmar, which has the second-highest migration rate in the country, illustrates these challenges acutely. Decades of armed conflict, displacement, and out-migration—especially among younger adults—have eroded traditional intergenerational support systems [31, 45]. As younger family members migrate for safety, employment, or conscription avoidance, many older adults are left behind with reduced social support, heightened isolation, and limited access to information and services. In such contexts, maintaining social cohesion—the sense of belonging, trust, and connectedness within families and communities—is critical for older adults’ mental well-being and resilience [11].

The 2021 military coup in Myanmar has profoundly disrupted the social and economic lives of older adults, particularly in rural areas. Armed conflict, economic collapse, and widespread displacement have weakened community ties and eroded traditional support systems, leading to increased social isolation, anxiety, and depression among older populations [2, 3, 18]. These challenges have been intensified by the breakdown of healthcare services, compounded by internet shutdowns and ongoing regional insecurity, which have further undermined both physical and mental well-being [23, 44].

Conflict-driven migration and forced conscription have resulted in the large-scale departure of younger family members, leaving many older adults without primary caregivers or household support [37, 41]. Younger generations have migrated internally to urban or relatively safer areas, or internationally to neighboring countries such as Thailand, Malaysia, and India in search of security and livelihoods [6, 27]. Prior to the coup, multi-generational living arrangements were the norm, with 86% of older adults residing in such households according to the 2014 Myanmar Census [24]. The disruption of these family structures has heightened food insecurity, restricted access to healthcare, and exacerbated poverty and psychological distress among older adults [18, 23]. Rising inflation and the collapse of local markets have further deepened economic vulnerability and social exclusion [44].

Over the past decade, Myanmar has undergone rapid digital transformation following the liberalization of its telecommunications sector in 2013–2014. Expanded access to affordable SIM cards and smartphones led to a sharp increase in internet use, rising from fewer than one million users in 2013 to over 27 million by 2021 [22]. However, digital expansion has been uneven, with rural populations, lower-income groups, and women facing persistent barriers to access. Women aged 15–65 in Myanmar are estimated to be 29% less likely than men to own mobile phones, largely due to financial dependence, restrictive social norms, and lower levels of digital literacy [16]. In rural areas, poor infrastructure, unreliable electricity, and high device costs further limit technology adoption, reinforcing the urban–rural digital divide [41]. Despite these constraints, digital technologies have been increasingly integrated into key sectors, including agriculture, education, and healthcare. In agriculture, farmers use social media platforms such as Facebook to exchange market information, share best practices, and access agricultural knowledge [38]. In the health sector, digital initiatives have expanded access to preventive care and remote consultations, although progress remains limited by connectivity challenges and insufficient digital training among healthcare workers [20].

Digital technologies have the potential to strengthen social cohesion among older adults by facilitating communication, sustaining social relationships, and enabling access to information, healthcare, and humanitarian assistance [5, 42]. Evidence from diverse settings suggests that the use of messaging applications, voice and video calls, and social media platforms can reduce loneliness, alleviate depressive symptoms, and enhance social participation in later life [30]. Strong social cohesion is consistently associated with better self-rated health, emotional well-being, and collective resilience, particularly in times of crisis [9, 48].

In this context of fragmentation and insecurity in Myanmar, digital platforms have emerged as a critical means of maintaining social cohesion and intergenerational connection. Despite infrastructural constraints and surveillance risks, many older adults use platforms such as Facebook, Messenger, Viber, and mobile phone calls to sustain communication and emotional support with family members. Digital tools also support everyday survival, enabling access to real-time information on local security conditions, food distribution, power outages, and facilitating the receipt of remittances through mobile money services [40]. Facebook, in particular, remains a primary source of news, as local media outlets and citizen journalists provide timely updates during armed conflict and curfews [34]. Some older adults also engage with YouTube and TikTok for religious, cultural, or commentary content, though usage varies according to literacy and digital familiarity. Telegram is increasingly used for accessing uncensored news, especially in urban settings, but remains less common among older populations [32].

Despite these potential benefits, older adults face substantial barriers to digital technology use. At the individual level, limited digital literacy, negative attitudes toward technology, age-related sensory and cognitive decline, and financial constraints restrict engagement [26, 42, 49]. At the community and structural levels, inadequate telecommunications infrastructure, high internet costs relative to income, and the lack of age-friendly training programs further widen the digital divide, particularly in rural areas [14, 36]. In conflict-affected contexts such as Myanmar, these challenges are compounded by infrastructure damage, displacement, fear of surveillance, and cybersecurity risks, all of which discourage older adults from engaging online [33] and reinforcing digital exclusion among older adults [8, 23].

At the same time, older adults actively develop coping strategies to navigate digital barriers. Studies highlight the importance of intergenerational support, peer learning, community-based digital initiatives, and the use of simplified or assistive technologies in enabling older adults to engage digitally and sustain social connections [4, 28, 35]. However, most existing research on digital technology and ageing has been conducted in stable, high-income settings. Empirical evidence remains limited on how older adults in active conflict zones adopt digital technologies, negotiate barriers, and employ coping strategies to maintain social cohesion under conditions of violence, displacement, and social fragmentation.

To address this gap, this study examines digital technology use for social cohesion among older adults in rural Myanmar during the ongoing civil war, with a focus on perceived barriers and coping strategies. Guided by the Socioecological Model and Stress and Coping Theory, the study adopts a qualitative design to capture older adults’ lived experiences, feelings, perceptions, and coping strategies related to digital technology use in a conflict-affected rural setting. The sample size of 30 participants was selected to ensure diversity across 20 villages with differing geographic and socio-economic contexts to support the identification of patterns and themes by incorporating a broader range of perspectives and increases the likelihood of achieving data saturation [19]. The research was conducted in rural communities in Hpa-An Township, Kayin State, and involved in-depth, semi-structured online interviews with 30 adults aged 60 years and above. Due to security concerns and political instability, all interviews were conducted online. Thematic analysis was used to identify patterns across individual, interpersonal, community, and structural levels. By centering older adults’ voices in a fragile and under-researched context, this study contributes to scholarship on digital inclusion, ageing, and social cohesion in conflict-affected settings and offers insights for age-inclusive digital and social policies.

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by the Socioecological Model (SEM) [7] and Stress and Coping Theory [25] to examine older adults’ use of digital technology for social cohesion in rural Myanmar amid ongoing political instability and armed conflict. Together, these frameworks provide a multidimensional lens to understand both the structural conditions shaping digital engagement and the adaptive responses older adults employ in crisis contexts.

The SEM conceptualizes digital technology use as embedded within multiple, interacting levels of influence. At the individual level, factors such as physical decline, digital literacy, and financial constraints affect older adults’ capacity to engage with digital tools. Interpersonal influences include family support, particularly intergenerational assistance, while community-level factors encompass access to training opportunities, local social support networks, and share resources. At the societal level, infrastructure disruptions, governance challenges, and prolonged conflict critically shape access to connectivity and information. These structural constraints interact dynamically with community-level responses, whereby local networks and collective practices often adapt to, mitigate, or, in some cases, reinforce broader systemic limitations. By situating barriers across these levels, the SEM highlights how opportunities for social cohesion are shaped not only by individual capabilities but also by weakened family structures, limited community resources, and fragile national systems in conflict-affected rural settings.

Stress and Coping Theory complements the SEM by focusing on how older adults respond to these multilevel barriers. Problem-focused coping refers to efforts aimed at overcoming practical constraints, such as self-directed learning, participation in community-based training, seeking assistance from younger family members, investing financial resources in digital devices, or relocating to areas with better connectivity. Emotion-focused coping, in contrast, addresses the psychological strain associated with digital exclusion and instability, including accepting technological limitations, relying on non-digital means to maintain social ties, and employing emotional regulation strategies to preserve well-being.

Key concepts guide the analytical framework of this study. Older adults are defined as individuals aged 60 years and above [39]. Digital technology encompasses communication devices and platforms—such as mobile phones, smartphones, messaging applications, and internet-based services—that enable information access and social interaction [5, 42]. Social cohesion refers to the strength of relationships, trust, and solidarity within families and communities, reflected in older adults’ ability to sustain social connections, participate in community life, and access essential information and resources [13, 21]. Barriers are understood as obstacles operating at individual, interpersonal, community, or societal levels that constrain digital engagement. By integrating the SEM and Stress and Coping Theory, this framework captures both the contextual constraints and adaptive strategies shaping older adults’ digital technology use for social cohesion in rural Myanmar during periods of prolonged crisis. Methods

Study design

This qualitative study investigated older adults’ use of digital technologies for social cohesion, focusing on the barriers they encounter and the strategies they employ to cope with these challenges. Drawing on the Socioecological Model [7] and Stress and Coping Theory [25], the analysis considered how obstacles and adaptive responses unfold across multiple levels of influence. The study was based on 30 semi-structured online interviews via Zoom with individuals aged 60 years and older from 20 villages, in Hpa-an Township, Kayin State—a rural area near the Thailand–Myanmar border that has been deeply affected by ongoing conflict, social isolation, and family fragmentation resulting from the prolonged civil war following Myanmar’s 2021 military coup. The principal investigator, who conducted all interviews, has prior research and professional experience with elderly in Myanmar, which supported rapport-building and contextual understanding. To minimize potential bias, a semi-structured interview guide was used, transcripts were reviewed iteratively, and interpretations were discussed with co-authors to ensure consistency with participants’ perspectives.

Recruitment process

Purposive quota sampling combined with snowball sampling was used to achieve variation in key socio-demographic characteristics, including gender, household composition, and socioeconomic status, thereby capturing a wide range of perspectives from rural contexts. A local research assistant, trained by the principal investigator from 28 April to 2 May 2025, supported both participant recruitment and the interview process. Being a member of the community, the assistant recruited older adults in Hpa-an Township using in-person engagement and according to the study criteria. Under the supervision of the principal investigator, the assistant distributed participant information sheets, explained the study objectives, confidentiality and privacy safeguards, participant rights, and the voluntary nature of participation, and obtained informed consent. Interview appointments were then scheduled at times convenient for participants. For participants without access to digital devices or internet connectivity, suitable locations were arranged to enable their participation in online interviews. The assistant also facilitated access to secure internet locations, supported relocation when connectivity was disrupted, arranged transportation when necessary, and provided technical assistance to address connectivity challenges. In addition, he supported communication, ensured participant comfort, and monitored potential security risks. Figure 2 illustrates the recruitment process. Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted to explore older adults’ experiences with digital technologies, the barriers they faced, and the coping strategies they adopted. Guided by the conceptual framework, a semi-structured interview guide was developed to examine digital technology use, socioecological barriers across multiple levels, and coping responses. Due to ongoing political instability in Kayin State, all interviews were carried out online via Zoom by the principal investigator with the research assistant serving as a note-taker and observer, between 16 May and 6 June 2025. For participants without access to digital devices or internet connectivity, the assistant arranged a suitable location for them to join the online interviews. The assistant also facilitated access to secure internet locations, supported relocation when connectivity was disrupted, arranged transportation when necessary, and provided technical assistance to address connectivity challenges. He also supported communication, ensured participant comfort, and monitored potential security risks. Interviews lasted 30–45 min, were video-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim by the principal investigator. Established relationships with Older People Self-help Groups facilitated rapport-building prior to data collection.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts and field notes capturing participants’ verbal and non-verbal expressions were analyzed using a theory-informed thematic analysis. All interviews were conducted in Burmese, transcribed verbatim shortly after data collection, and subsequently translated into English for analysis. The principal investigator, who conducted all interviews, was responsible for both transcription and translation, allowing careful verification of meaning and context. Although back-translation was not undertaken, transcripts were repeatedly reviewed alongside audio recordings and field notes to ensure accuracy and consistency. To maintain confidentiality, participants were assigned identification codes.

The analysis was guided by the Socioecological Model and Stress and Coping Theory. Transcripts were coded and organized in Microsoft Excel, and codes were iteratively compared across cases to identify patterns and variations in participants’ experiences. A primarily deductive approach was used, with initial codes derived from the theoretical framework; however, the process remained iterative, allowing refinement and incorporation of emerging insights from the data. Codes were grouped into broader categories and further developed into themes reflecting different socioecological levels and coping processes.

The coding and analysis were conducted by the principal investigator. To enhance analytical rigor, the coding was developed on the theoretical framework collaboratively among the authors including themes and codes, and regular discussions were held to review and refine themes and interpretations. The analysis resulted in a systematic understanding of the barriers and coping strategies shaping older adults’ digital engagement in rural Myanmar.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included 30 older adults, comprising 17 women and 13 men, aged 60 to 80 years (mean age = 67.6). Health-related challenges were common, including musculoskeletal pain, sensory impairments, and chronic conditions such as hypertension; however, most participants reported maintaining functional independence. Twenty-four participants were married, while five were widowed and one was single. 28 participants had children, with 2 reporting no children. Living arrangements among those with children varied, with some co-residing with children or extended family members and others reporting adult children living elsewhere in Myanmar or overseas, and household size ranged from 1 to 7 individuals, reflecting differences in family structure and migration patterns. Notably, 13 participants had children working in Thailand and 1 in Malaysia, highlighting the influence of transnational labor migration on family support structures. Overall educational attainment was low; 13 participants had completed only primary or lower secondary education, while 13 had finished upper secondary school and 4 had attained tertiary-level education. 17 participants remained economically active, primarily engaged in agriculture or informal work, whereas 13 were no longer working and often relied on family remittances or pensions. Monthly income levels were generally modest, with 5 reporting with no income, 9 earnings below 100,000 MMK (25 USD) and only a small number indicating incomes between 200,000 to 300,000 MMK (50 to 75 USD).

Ethnically, 19 participants were Kayin, 5 were Mon, 1 was Pa-O, 1 was Shan, and 4 were Bamar groups. 28 participants identified as Buddhist, while two participants identified as Christian. All participants reported using digital technologies—primarily mobile phones—for social connection. Smartphones were widely used, although some participants continued to rely on keypad phones. Communication through phone calls and text messaging was the primary form of digital engagement, supplemented messaging applications such as text messenger, or VoIP service like Viber, and social media platforms such as Facebook, YouTube. Digital technology use among older adults in conflict-affected areas

Older adults in rural Myanmar consider digital technologies as essential tools for maintaining social connections amid ongoing political instability. This perception is shaped by family separation due to the migration of children abroad, age-related mobility limitations, and restricted travel resulting from conflict and insecurity, which make in-person interaction more difficult than in the past. All participants reported using some form of digital technology to sustain social connections, most commonly through mobile phones. While many continued to use basic keypad phones, the gradual uptake of smartphones reflected shifting communication practices in response to prolonged displacement and family separation. Even with limited functions, basic mobile phones enabled regular calls and messaging that helped older adults maintain contact with children and grandchildren who had migrated to urban areas or abroad following the 2021 crisis, providing emotional reassurance and continuity of intergenerational ties.“My daughter from Bangkok contacts me via video call every Saturday…” ID04, married man aged 67, Kaw La Pala Village“I have been using a phone for around three to five years, mainly to stay in contact with my family and employees ….” ID 10, widow aged 75, Kaw La Pala Village“I use my phone to stay connected with my friends and family...” ID 26, married woman aged 66, Mu Kawa Village

Participants with higher literacy levels or support from younger family members used platforms such as Facebook, Messenger, and Viber for both private interaction and broader social participation. Digital technologies also served a protective and adaptive function in conflict-affected settings. Participants described using online networks to monitor local security conditions, access timely news, and obtain market-related information, which supported informed decision-making and daily risk management. Group calls, messaging groups, and informal online chats further fostered peer support and a sense of belonging, encouraging continued digital engagement despite initial hesitation or limited digital skills. Barriers to digital technology use for social cohesion

Personal barriers

At the individual level, age-related physical and cognitive changes significantly constrained older adults’ engagement with digital technologies. Declining eyesight, hearing impairments, and memory difficulties limited their ability to learn, practice, and retain new digital skills. Even when training or assistance was provided, challenges in remembering instructions often led to frustration and reduced motivation to continue using digital tools.“I have difficulty seeing when I use my phone, so I need to wear glasses. My grandchildren patiently taught me how to use it, but because I am forgetful, I need to review the steps often…” ID 14, married women aged 61, Win Kyan Village

Limited literacy and minimal prior exposure to digital technologies further impeded participation. Many older adults had little formal education and few opportunities to interact with digital devices earlier in life, undermining their confidence in using smartphones or navigating applications. Concerns about accidentally damaging devices, pressing incorrect buttons, or depleting phone credit discouraged experimentation with unfamiliar features. Coping strategies

The barriers described above not only constrained older adults’ access to and use of digital technologies but also generated significant emotional and psychological strain. Many participants expressed feelings of frustration, anxiety, and reduced self-confidence when attempting to use digital devices, particularly when they encountered repeated difficulties, limited guidance, or unfamiliar interfaces. Age-related challenges such as declining vision, memory limitations, and low literacy further intensified these experiences, often leading to fear of making mistakes or damaging devices. In addition, financial constraints and unreliable infrastructure contributed to a sense of uncertainty and helplessness, especially when participants were unable to maintain consistent communication with family members.

These emotional responses were further heightened by the broader context of political instability and conflict. Concerns about surveillance, misinformation, and potential repercussions of online activities created an environment of fear and caution, discouraging more active engagement with digital platforms. Mobility restrictions, travel limitations, and family separation due to migration also increased reliance on digital communication while simultaneously amplifying the stress associated with its use. In line with Stress and Coping Theory, these accumulated stressors prompted older adults to adopt a range of coping strategies to manage both the practical challenges of technology use and the associated emotional burden.

Despite facing substantial constraints, older adults exhibited considerable resilience by adopting problem-focused coping strategies to enable digital technology use. Despite these constraints, digital technologies enabled older adults to maintain regular contact with geographically distant family members, particularly migrant children, providing emotional reassurance and continuity of relationships. For some participants, access to news and community information through digital platforms contributed to a sense of awareness, belonging, and connection to wider social networks, even in contexts of displacement and insecurity.

While coping strategies were broadly categorized as problem-focused or emotion-focused, some responses—particularly risk-avoidant behaviors such as avoiding political content or removing VPN applications—may overlap between these categories. Although these actions were often motivated by emotional responses such as fear and anxiety, they also represent practical efforts to reduce exposure to perceived risks. This overlap reflects the fluid and context-dependent nature of coping strategies, particularly in conflict-affected settings where individuals simultaneously manage both emotional and practical challenges.

Discussion

This study suggests that digital technologies can support social cohesion among older adults in rural Myanmar, particularly by enabling emotional and informational connections across generations, although their contribution remains uneven and constrained [5, 15]. In Hpa-an Township, where prolonged political instability, civil conflict, and economic marginalization have severely disrupted traditional family and community networks, mobile phones and platforms such as Viber, Messenger, and Facebook served as important means of maintaining social connections. These technologies enabled older adults to maintain contact with family members abroad, access up-to-date information, and preserve a sense of belonging, echoing findings that underscore the importance of digital connectivity during periods of crisis and uncertainty [10, 51].

Guided by the socioecological model, this study identified barriers operating at personal, interpersonal, community, and societal levels. At the personal level, age-related cognitive decline, visual impairments, and limited digital literacy constrained effective technology use, consistent with previous research on digital aging [28, 49]. These challenges were intensified by financial hardship, as the cost of devices, data, and electricity posed significant obstacles in a context marked by inflation and economic precarity. At the interpersonal level, digital engagement depended heavily on intergenerational support. Although children and grandchildren often served as primary sources of assistance, their limited availability and occasional impatience restricted sustained learning. Similar patterns have been documented in other Southeast Asian contexts, where family support plays a critical yet uneven role in facilitating older adults’ digital participation [4]. Peer-based learning among older adults was notably scarce, further limiting opportunities for shared skill development.

At the community level, digital exclusion was reinforced by infrastructural shortcomings. Participants frequently reported the absence of nearby phone shops, repair services, digital training centers, and age-appropriate learning opportunities. These findings reflect broader patterns of rural digital inequality observed in both low- and middle-income settings [14, 29]. At the societal level, Myanmar’s ongoing political crisis further deepened exclusion through unreliable electricity, internet shutdowns, surveillance, and fear of punitive consequences for online activity. Such conditions align with existing evidence on the impact of authoritarian governance and conflict on digital freedoms and access [1, 8, 23].

Across all levels, limited digital literacy and structural constraints emerged as the most substantial barriers to meaningful digital engagement. Although all participants owned mobile phones, most were restricted to basic voice calls and lacked the skills or conditions necessary to use messaging applications, video calls, or social media. Importantly, these findings demonstrate that device ownership alone does not ensure digital inclusion. Even when affordability was not the primary concern, weak mobile signals, intermittent internet access, and frequent power outages severely limited functionality. Addressing digital exclusion in conflict-affected rural areas such as Kayin State therefore requires integrated approaches that combine infrastructure investment with skills training and ongoing support. Unlike studies conducted in stable settings, this research highlights how political instability compounds existing vulnerabilities and intensifies digital marginalization.

Consistent with Lazarus’s Stress and Coping Theory, older adults employed both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping strategies to navigate these challenges. Problem-focused strategies included seeking assistance from family members or neighbors, engaging in trial-and-error learning, adjusting device settings, and investing in alternative energy sources such as solar panels or power banks. These adaptive behaviors mirror findings from other contexts where older adults actively modify their environments to maintain digital access [28, 30]. Emotion-focused coping was equally prominent. Many participants accepted their limitations by confining phone use to basic functions, while others derived comfort from routine digital activities such as listening to sermons or following news updates. Risk-avoidant behaviors—such as avoiding political content or removing VPNs during travel—further illustrate how emotional regulation strategies were used to manage fear and uncertainty. These patterns are consistent with prior research highlighting the role of psychological coping in older adults’ digital engagement [50].

Despite varying levels of digital literacy and infrastructural limitations, the widespread use of mobile phones underscored the perceived importance of digital communication in rural, conflict-affected settings. The findings also reveal notable resilience and adaptability among older adults. Participants demonstrated a strong desire to remain socially connected, along with a willingness to learn, adjust, and rely on informal support networks. Shared phone use, neighborly assistance, and peer encouragement functioned as compensatory mechanisms in the absence of formal programs. Such community-based coping strategies resonate with evidence from other crisis-affected settings, where collective responses have strengthened resilience amid instability [35]. Overall, these findings underscore the urgent need for interventions that are locally grounded, conflict-sensitive, and responsive to older adults’ lived experiences, particularly in digital technology use for social cohesion.

Strengths and limitation

This study has several strengths. It provides in-depth, context-specific insights into older adults’ use of digital technology for social cohesion in a conflict-affected rural setting, an area that remains under-researched. The qualitative design enabled the exploration of lived experiences, including perceptions, barriers, and coping strategies, which are not easily captured through quantitative approaches. In addition, the inclusion of participants from 20 villages with diverse geographic and socio-economic contexts enhanced the breadth of perspectives and supported the identification of recurring themes across different settings.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, while snowball sampling may have resulted in recruitment within existing social networks, potentially limiting diversity and the inclusion of more isolated individuals, efforts were made to mitigate this by conducting in-person recruitment who meet specific inclusion criteria across the wider community, beyond members of Older People Self-help Groups. This approach may still have favored individuals who are more socially connected, potentially leading to the underrepresentation of the most socially isolated older adults. Concerns about surveillance and unease with digital platforms in a sensitive political environment may have discouraged some older adults from participating or led participants to moderate their responses, potentially limiting the range of perspectives captured. To mitigate this, participants were recruited through trusted community networks, and interviews were conducted in a confidential and supportive environment to encourage open discussion.

Second, all interviews were conducted online due to security constraints, which may have excluded individuals without access to digital devices or stable internet connectivity. This limitation was partially addressed by providing logistical support through a local research assistant, including arranging access to secure internet locations, transportation, and technical assistance where needed. Given that all interviews were conducted online, older adults with the most limited digital access or connectivity may have been underrepresented, despite efforts to provide shared access and logistical support. As a result, the findings may primarily reflect the experiences of individuals with at least minimal or supported digital engagement.

Third, technical challenges—particularly unstable internet connectivity—occasionally disrupted interviews and may have influenced participants’ engagement and emotional responses; however, follow-up clarifications and careful transcription helped ensure the completeness and accuracy of the data. Although efforts were made to ensure accuracy through careful transcription and review, the absence of back-translation may have introduced minor translation bias. Finally, as the study was conducted in Hpa-an Township, Kayin State, the findings are context-specific and may not be fully transferable to regions with different socio-cultural or conflict dynamics.

Conclusion

This study suggests that digital technologies play a limited but meaningful role in supporting intergenerational connections, access to information, and community belonging among older adults in rural Myanmar in contexts of political instability and economic hardship. Guided by the Socioecological Model and Stress and Coping Theory, the findings demonstrate that digital engagement is shaped by multilevel barriers, while older adults adopt both problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies to navigate these challenges.

To enhance digital inclusion and social cohesion in similar fragile contexts, several practical implications emerge. First, age-friendly digital literacy programs should be developed and delivered through community-based platforms, such as Older People Self-help Groups, to support older adults with limited skills and confidence. Second, investments in rural connectivity and reliable electricity infrastructure are essential to reduce structural barriers and ensure consistent access to digital communication. Third, community-based support systems, including intergenerational assistance and shared access to devices or internet services, should be strengthened to help older adults engage with technology in safe and supportive environments.

Overall, while digital technologies offer important opportunities to maintain social connections, their benefits remain uneven in conflict-affected settings. Addressing these challenges requires context-sensitive, inclusive, and multi-level interventions to ensure that older adults are not left behind in an increasingly digital world.
