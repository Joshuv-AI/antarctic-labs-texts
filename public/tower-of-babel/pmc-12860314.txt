Innovative use of the hollow mill for broken pedicle screw extraction
Authors: Sivaraman Kumarasamy, Deepak Kumar Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Removal of fractured pedicle screws is technically demanding. Despite significant advances in transpedicular screw instrumentation and surgical techniques, screw breakage continues to account for 25% of complications and affecting 0.8–24.6% of patients. Here, we developed an innovative technique that employs the hollow mill set for broken pedicle screw retrieval.Methods: We describe our innovative technique for broken pedicle screw retrieval utilizing a hollow mill set. We also provide a comparison of its safety/efficacy with established extraction methodologies.Results: Our technique begins with tulip head removal followed by careful curettage of newly formed bone; this exposes the fracture surface without compromising pedicle structural integrity. Next, the hollow mill sleeve is advanced clockwise until achieving >50% engagement with the retained screw fragment. Subsequently, with counterclockwise rotation of the handle-mechanism, the broken screw fragment can be extracted within a protective sleeve. This approach requires minimal bone exposure and avoids aggressive pedicle bone removal, thereby preserving anatomical integrity essential for larger-diameter screw placement (i.e., without extending to additional fusion levels). Further, the hollow mill is part of standard orthopedic instrumentation readily available in most surgical suites.Conclusion: The hollow mill set for retrieving broken pedicle screw is readily available, cost-effective, technically simple, and effective for addressing fractured screws of varying diameters.

INTRODUCTION

There is still a high 0.8–24.6% incidence of screw breakage following transpedicular screw instrumentation.[2,4,5] Extraction of the broken pedicle screw is challenging, and risks damaging/weakening of the involved pedicles. Here, we utilized a hollow mill set to retrieve broken pedicle screws while preserving the integrity of the facet. Further, this set is readily available in most operating rooms, is cost-effective, technically simple, and safely/accurately removes screws of varying diameters.[1-3,5-10]

DESCRIPTION OF TECHNIQUE

Here are the four steps required to retrieve broken pedicle screws utilizing the hollow mill set. First, remove the tulip head of the broken pedicle screw and curette away any newly formed bone to expose and preserve pedicle integrity. Second, rotate the sleeve of the hollow mill clockwise to directly engage more than 50% of the broken screw [Figures 1a-f]. Third, rotate the handle of the hollow mill counterclockwise to retrieve the screw within the sleeve [Figures 2a and b]. Fourth, and finally, a larger diameter pedicle screw may be inserted. Notably, this procedure can be readily performed without adding adjacent levels in the instrumented construct.

DISCUSSION

Broken pedicle screw extraction has evolved from deep pilot-hole drilling with a reverse-thread extractor,[6] to superficial high-speed drill techniques, specialized friction based/slot drilling options, use of custom screw drivers, to standard plier-grip methods.[1,3,4,7-9] Utilizing the operating microscope and intraoperative fluoroscopy, the hollow mill technique for broken pedicle screw removal involves: no drilling around the pedicle, sufficiently exposes the upper surface of the broken screw to engage the sleeve of the hollow mill (i.e., thus preserving the integrity of the pedicle for repeat instrumentation), and avoids damaging adjacent neural tissues. This technique is a safe/effective alternative to Weng’s specialized retrieval instrumentation. Further, the hollow mill is universally available in standard orthopedic sets in most operating rooms, is thus immediately accessible, and simpler to use when compared to other broken pedicle/screw extraction methodologies [Table 1].[4,10]

CONCLUSION

The hollow mill set for removing broken pedicle screws is very practical for removing screws of different diameters. It is cost-effective, available/immediately accessible in the operating room, and of comparable safety/efficacy when compared to current technical alternatives.
