Epidemiology, risk factors and outcomes of Clostridioides difficile infection in a UK district general hospital: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pushpa Saajan, Shayi Shali, Kimberly Rose Cabiles, Anusha Karunasagar, Shikandhini Visuvanathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) remains a major healthcare-associated infection, causing substantial morbidity and mortality. Relapse and recurrence continue to pose significant management challenges.AimThe aim of this study was to evaluate risk factors, ribotype distribution and treatment outcomes, relapse, recurrence and 30-day mortality, among CDI patients in a UK district general hospital.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included all adult and paediatric inpatient and outpatient cases diagnosed with CDI between April 2023 and August 2024. Demographic characteristics, risk factors, CDI onset type, treatment regimens, ribotypes, relapse, recurrence and 30-day all-cause mortality were extracted from electronic records and analysed descriptively.ResultsIn total, 124 CDI episodes were analysed: 45.2% hospital-onset healthcare-associated, 16.1% community-onset healthcare-associated, 33.0% community-onset community-associated and 5.6% community-onset indeterminate. First episodes accounted for 84.7%, with relapse in 12.9% and recurrence in 2.4%. Key risk factors included prolonged or multiple hospitalisations (74%), broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure (71%), proton pump inhibitor therapy (49%), gastrointestinal disease (31.4%) and malignancy (24%). Patients aged >75 years had the highest relapse/recurrence rate (63.2%). Relapse (9%) and recurrence (4%) occurred among those treated with oral vancomycin, whereas no relapse or recurrence was observed among patients who received fidaxomicin monotherapy. Ribotyping was completed in 108 cases (87.1%). Four ribotypes, CE002, CE005, CE015 and CE020, were most frequent (each N = 11); CE001 accounted for seven cases, and 12.9% were untyped. The 30-day all-cause mortality rate was 7.3% (N = 9), predominantly in older patients with comorbidities and hospital-acquired infection.ConclusionsRelapse and recurrence were more frequently observed among older patients and those with repeated hospitalisation, prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, and gastrointestinal or malignant disease. No further episodes were observed among patients who received fidaxomicin monotherapy in this cohort. Ribotype patterns revealed predominance of non-hypervirulent strains, supporting strengthened stewardship and routine ribotype-informed surveillance.Keywords: Clostridioides difficile, Relapse, Recurrence

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) remains one of the most common healthcare-associated infections globally and a leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea in hospitalised patients [1,2]. Despite improvements in infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship, CDI continues to pose a substantial burden on morbidity and mortality, particularly among older, comorbid populations [3,4]. In England alone, more than 13,000 laboratory-confirmed CDI cases were reported in 2023, with recurrence contributing significantly to prolonged hospitalisation and increased mortality [5].

CDI results from disruption of the normal gut microbiota following exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, allowing toxin-producing Clostridioides difficile to proliferate in the colon [6,7]. Established risk factors include advanced age, prolonged hospitalisation, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use and underlying gastrointestinal or malignant disease [6,7]. These factors predispose to primary CDI and increase the likelihood of relapse and recurrence, which occur in up to 25% of patients after a first episode and in >50% of those with multiple prior episodes [3,4].

Although international treatment guidelines recommend fidaxomicin as first-line therapy [8,9], real-world data from UK district general hospitals remain limited. Most published evidence arises from tertiary centre cohorts, which may not reflect outcomes in general hospital populations [10].

Although several UK studies have described the epidemiology, recurrence, and clinical outcomes of CDI, these have largely focussed on national surveillance data, recurrent CDI, or specific clinical outcomes, rather than providing a comprehensive evaluation of patient risk factors, treatment pathways, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) ribotype distribution, and outcomes within a single contemporary cohort [11,12]. The present study complements the existing UK literature by offering an integrated assessment of these factors, including patient characteristics, treatment approaches, PCR ribotype distribution, relapse, recurrence and 30-day mortality, in a contemporary district general hospital cohort. As such, it provides a detailed real-world overview of CDI management and outcomes within routine National Health Services (NHS) clinical practice.

This study therefore aimed to determine the risk factors, ribotype distribution and treatment outcomes associated with CDI, including relapse, recurrence and 30-day mortality. By characterising local epidemiology and treatment response, we sought to identify high-risk patient groups and inform risk-based prescribing and infection-prevention strategies in everyday clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Princess Alexandra Hospital NHS Trust (PAHT), a 500-bed district general hospital in the East of England. The study included all adult and paediatric cases with laboratory-confirmed CDI diagnosed between April 2023 and August 2024.

Case definition and classification

A CDI case was defined as a stool specimen of Bristol Stool Chart types 5–7 that tested positive for both glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) antigen and C. difficile toxin by enzyme immunoassay, meeting UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) mandatory reporting criteria.

Episodes were categorised using UKHSA definitions (hospital-onset healthcare-associated [HOHA], community-onset healthcare-associated [COHA], community-onset indeterminate and community-onset community-associated [COCA]). Detailed definitions were applied as per national guidance [5].

Episodes not meeting stool criteria, lacking toxin positivity or occurring within 28 days of a previous episode were excluded.

Microbiological testing

Stool specimens conforming the Bristol Stool Chart type 5–7 criteria were tested using a two-step diagnostic algorithm. GDH detection was performed using the VIDAS® C. difficile GDH assay (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), followed by detection of toxins A and B using the TOX A/B QUIK CHEK® enzyme immunoassay (TechLab, USA). Only stool specimens positive for both GDH and toxin were classified as confirmed CDI and included in the study. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions with internal quality controls and the laboratory participates in national external quality assurance programmes.

PCR ribotyping

Toxin-positive specimens were referred to the UKHSA C. difficile Ribotyping Network (Midlands and East Region) for PCR ribotyping using standardised UKHSA protocols, with ribotype designation assigned according to the UKHSA reference database.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical and microbiological data were extracted from electronic hospital systems, including patient demographics, hospitalisation history, antibiotic exposure, PPI use, comorbid gastrointestinal or malignant disease, treatment regimens and laboratory results.

Outcome definitions

Relapse was defined as recurrence of CDI symptoms with a positive toxin test within 12 weeks of the previous episode. Recurrence was defined as a new CDI episode occurring >12 weeks after the previous infection. Thirty-day all-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause within 30 days of diagnosis. These definitions were based on the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline (NG199), which defines relapse as occurring within 12 weeks and recurrence as occurring more than 12 weeks after resolution of the previous episode [9]. Accordingly, relapse and recurrence were classified according to the interval between episodes rather than microbiological confirmation by strain typing.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed descriptively using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Variables were summarised as counts and percentages, with age presented as median and range. No inferential statistical analysis was performed as the study aimed to describe epidemiological patterns and outcomes.

Results

Patient and episode characteristics

A total of 124 CDI episodes were reviewed. Of these, 56 (45.2%) were HOHA, 20 (16.1%) COHA, 41 (33.0%) COCA and 7 (5.6%) community-onset indeterminate association. First CDI episodes accounted for 105 (84.7%) cases, while 18 (14.5%) represented second episodes and 1 (0.8%) was a third episode. Relapse occurred in 16 patients (12.9%) and recurrence in 3 (2.4%). The median age was 78 years (range: 2–99).

Treatment outcomes

Oral vancomycin was the most frequently prescribed treatment and was associated with relapse in 6 (9%) and recurrence in 3 (4%) patients. Few patients received metronidazole, and one relapse was observed. Among the four patients who received combination therapy with high-dose vancomycin and intravenous (IV) metronidazole, one experienced recurrence. No relapse or recurrence was observed among patients who received fidaxomicin monotherapy. In the sequential vancomycin–fidaxomicin group, the reported relapse and recurrence occurred before initiation of fidaxomicin and therefore were not attributed to fidaxomicin treatment (Table I). Twenty-three patients (18.5%) were excluded from treatment-specific analyses because CDI treatment was undocumented, treatment status was unknown, no CDI-directed treatment was recorded or treatment was not initiated before death. Most excluded patients were older adults (>75 years) and had COCA CDI. None died within 30 days of CDI. These patients were retained in the overall descriptive cohort but excluded from treatment-specific analyses because treatment exposure could not be reliably determined.

Risk factors

Exposure-related risk factors and comorbidities are summarised in Tables II and III. Most episodes occurred in older patients, with 61.2% aged >75 years and 24.1% aged 60–75 years. A history of multiple and/or prolonged hospitalisation was common (74.1%). Prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was recorded in 71.0%, while PPI use was documented in 49.1%. Underlying gastrointestinal disease (31.4%) and malignancy (24.1%) were common comorbidities (Table II, Table III).

Ribotyping distribution

Ribotyping was completed in 108 of 124 CDI episodes (87.1%). Four ribotypes – CE002, CE005, CE015 and CE020 – were the most prevalent, each detected in 11 cases. Several additional ribotypes were identified at lower frequency, reflecting a mixture of sporadic community strains and less dominant hospital lineages (Table V). Sixteen cases (12.9%) were not typed.

Mortality

Thirty-day all-cause mortality was 7.3% (9/124). The median age of patients who died was 79 years (range, 55–91 years), with five (55.6%) aged >75 years. Most deaths occurred in patients with HOHA CDI (6/9), followed by COHA CDI (2/9) and community-onset indeterminate healthcare-associated CDI (1/9). Seven patients received oral vancomycin, while two did not receive CDI-directed treatment, including one who was receiving end-of-life care.

Summary of key findings

Relapse and recurrence were predominantly observed in older, comorbid patients with repeated hospitalisations and prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure. No relapse or recurrence was observed among patients who received fidaxomicin monotherapy in this cohort, whereas oral vancomycin remained the most frequently prescribed treatment.

Discussion

This retrospective study provides real-world data on the epidemiology, risk factors and treatment outcomes of CDI in a district general hospital in the East of England. Our findings confirm that CDI predominantly affects older adults with multiple comorbidities and hospital exposures and that relapse and recurrence were more frequently observed among patients with identifiable risk factors. This combined clinical and microbiological approach provides practical insights to support infection prevention strategies and antimicrobial stewardship interventions in routine hospital practice.

The relapse rate in this cohort (12.9%) was lower than the 15–25% reported in international literature [3,4,8]. Similarly, the recurrence rate of 2.4% was lower than the 5–10% observed in other UK and European studies [5,10]. This may reflect early fidaxomicin use in high-risk patients; however, the modest sample size and retrospective design may have underestimated recurrence.

Our findings are consistent with national surveillance data reported by the UKHSA, demonstrating that CDI remains an important healthcare-associated infection. Relapse and recurrence were concentrated among patients aged >75 years (63.2%), consistent with prior reports identifying advanced age as an important risk factor for poor outcome [3,4,10]. Repeated hospitalisation, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, gastrointestinal disease and malignancy were the leading risk factors, in line with previous studies [3,4,6,7].

Treatment response varied by antimicrobial agent. Oral vancomycin, the most frequently prescribed drug, showed relapse and recurrence rates comparable to those reported internationally [6,7]. Few patients received metronidazole, and one relapse was observed. Therefore, no conclusions regarding comparative treatment outcomes can be drawn from this small subgroup. Combination therapy with vancomycin and metronidazole conferred no additional benefit and was associated with recurrence, consistent with prior findings [6].

In this cohort, no relapses or recurrences were observed among patients treated with fidaxomicin. However, this observation should be interpreted cautiously because treatment allocation was not randomised and comparative effectiveness was not evaluated. Our findings are consistent with randomised trials and real-world studies reporting lower recurrence rates with fidaxomicin than with vancomycin, although comparative effectiveness cannot be inferred from this descriptive study [[8], [9], [10]]. These findings support current NICE and European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) recommendations for the use of fidaxomicin in patients at high risk of recurrence [9,10].

Ribotype analysis demonstrated four predominant lineages (CE002, CE005, CE015 and CE020), consistent with recent UK surveillance showing a shift away from hypervirulent strains [5]. No hypervirulent ribotypes were detected, and the presence of multiple low-frequency ribotypes suggests ongoing introduction of community strains. Routine ribotyping may support infection control and epidemiological surveillance [10].

The 30-day all-cause mortality rate of 7.3% aligns with national data [5] and was highest among elderly, comorbid patients with hospital-acquired infection. As mortality was reported as all-cause 30-day mortality, deaths could not be directly attributed to CDI. Furthermore, this elderly population had substantial comorbidity, and all-cause mortality is likely to include deaths unrelated to CDI, limiting interpretation of these findings.

Broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure and PPI use were common, reinforcing the importance of antimicrobial stewardship and deprescribing strategies [6,7]. Early review, IV-to-oral switch and targeted prescribing remain key interventions to reduce CDI risk.

Strengths of this study include inclusion of both community- and hospital-onset CDI and real-world clinical data. Limitations include the retrospective design, single-centre setting and modest sample size, which may limit generalisability. Interpretation of laxative use as a potential risk factor was limited by the high proportion of missing data (52.4%), and therefore no conclusions could be drawn regarding its association with CDI or recurrence. Exclusion of 23 patients from the treatment-specific analyses because of incomplete or undocumented treatment information may have introduced some selection bias; however, these exclusions were based on missing treatment data rather than treatment response.

These findings are particularly relevant to similar healthcare settings, highlighting the importance of risk-based management, appropriate first-line therapy and targeted infection prevention strategies to reduce recurrence and improve patient outcomes.
