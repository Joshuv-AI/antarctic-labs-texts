A remark on weighted average multiplicities in prime factorisation
Viktor Mirjanić, Daattavya Aggarwal, Challenger Mishra

Abstract
. We study a generalisation of the quality of an ABC triple that we call the weighted average multiplicity (WAM), in which the logarithmic heights of prime factors are raised to a complex exponent s s . The WAM is connected to the standard ABC conjecture at s = 1 s=1 . We show that for real part of s s less than 1 1 , WAM is unbounded over ABC triples both for integers and polynomials. For real part greater than 1 1 , we characterise a boundary beyond which WAM is holomorphic and bounded. In this region, we show that WAM is related to the multiplicity of the largest prime factor of the triple, a quantity that we connect with the original ABC conjecture and whose distribution we explore computationally.

1.
Weighted Average Multiplities

Given an integer
n
with prime factorisation

n=\pm\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{k}^{e_{k}},

the
weighted average multiplicity (WAM)
of its set of prime factors is defined by

(1)

\wam(n):=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}\log p_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\log p_{k}}.

This definition is due to Minhyong Kim. Here as in the following, the indexing of primes in a factorisation like this will be in increasing order. We will use the standard terminology
height
of an integer
n
for
\log|n|
, which is roughly its information-theoretic complexity.

This note argues that this definition provides a natural way to formulate and investigate a number of properties of factorisation, especially the difficult ideas surrounding the
ABC conjecture
1
1

1

A proof of this conjecture was announced by Shinichi Mochizuki in 2012 in a series of four preprints, which were published in 2021
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In 2018, Peter Scholze and Jakob Stix visited Kyoto for discussions with Mochizuki and subsequently wrote a report
[
19
]
explaining what they viewed as a gap. Recently, Kirti Joshi released a series of preprints
[
5
,
8
,
7
,
6
,
4
,
3
,
9
]
claiming to have fixed the gap. The discussion in our paper is independent of the status of this long disagreement, other than the fact that we refer to the key inequality as a conjecture.
. The reader will notice right away that

(2)

\wam(n)=\frac{\log|n|}{\log\rad|n|},

where

\rad|n|:=\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_{k},

is the radical of
n
, or the number obtained from
n
by removing all multiplicities. This is the connection to ABC as usually formulated. We call an
ABC triple
a triple of coprime integers
a,b,c
such that
a+b=c
.
It is conjectured that for any
\epsilon>0
, there are only finitely many ABC triples such that

(3)

\wam(abc)>3+\epsilon.

We note that this is implied by the standard form of the ABC conjecture, which is sometimes expressed in terms of the ‘quality’
q
of the triple:

(4)

q(a,b,c):=\frac{\log\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)}{\log\rad|abc|},

conjectured to be less than
1+\epsilon
for all but finitely many triples.
Our statement might be strictly weaker than the usual conjecture and, in particular, it is not at all clear that the bound
3+\epsilon
is optimal. Nonetheless, since the precise exponent is of little conceptual importance, we will stick to this formulation and confine our investigation to the WAM rather than the quality in this paper.
In any case, it appears natural that multiplicities are weighted by the heights of the primes they contribute, and the ABC conjecture suggests that this weighting is what keeps the average multiplicities of ABC triples bounded: If small primes occur in
abc
with large multiplicities, compensation will come from large primes with small multiplicities. The meaning of such informal accounts of the conjecture are made precise by the expression
1
for the WAM, which is not as apparent with the simpler expression
2
.

Another advantage of making the weights explicit is that we can then vary them in a family to define the function

(5)

\wam(n,s):=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{s}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\log p_{k})^{s}},

giving the heights more or less prominence depending on the size of
s
. We allow
s
to take on complex values, whereby we get a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
. A feature of this family is that while the value at
s=1
is the original WAM, the value at zero

\wam(n,0)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}}{m}

is the normal average multiplicity, and

\lim_{\Re(s)\to+\infty}\wam(n,s)=e_{m},

the multiplicity of the largest prime factor.

Note the inequality

\wam(n,1)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}(\log p_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\log p_{k})}\geq\frac{e_{m}\log p_{m}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\log p_{k})}\geq\frac{e_{m}}{m}

giving us the crude bound

e_{m}\leq\wam(n,1)\omega(n).

on
e_{m}
. Here,
\omega(n)
is the usual notation for the number of distinct prime divisors of
n
.
A theorem of Erdos and Kac
[
1
]
implies that
e_{m}
will tend not to be much larger than
\wam(n,1)
. It is not clear how large
e_{m}
can be for ABC triples, but it is quite easy to see that the average multiplicity
\wam(abc,0)
is unbounded.

The definite mathematical results of this paper are quite modest and amount to the observation that the ABC inequalities appear to be quite finely balanced.

Theorem 1
.

If
\Re(s)<1
, then the quantity

\wam(abc,s)

is unbounded as we run over ABC triples of integers.

In short, we cannot hope for a bound if the weights are even slightly less than the heights of prime divisors. In fact, the ABC triples
(2^{n},1-2^{n},1)
for large
n
suffice to prove the theorem.

Recall the standard analogy between integers and polynomials over finite fields. That is, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field and let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
have factorisation

f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{k}^{e_{k}}

into irreducible polynomials
p_{k}
. We define

\wam(f,s):=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}(\deg p_{k})^{s}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\deg p_{k})^{s}}

An ABC triple in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
are coprime polynomials
a,b,c
such that
c=a+b
and at least one of
a,b,c
have a non-zero formal derivative. In this case, it is shown by
[
11
, Lemma 2]
and Stothers that

\wam(abc)=\wam(abc,1)\leq 3,

so that an
‘ABC inequality’
is not conjectural. Nonetheless, we show

Theorem 2
.

If
\Re(s)<1
, then the quantity

\wam(abc,s)

is unbounded as we run over ABC triples of polynomials.

The proof here is somewhat different from the integer case and utilises an interesting counting argument. In principle, it should be possible to use the explicit ABC triples
(x^{\ell^{n}},1-x^{\ell^{n}},1)
for a suitable prime
\ell
. However, for the irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials, we need to find an odd
\ell
such that
q
is a primitive root modulo
\ell^{2}
. Such an
\ell
almost certainly always exists. For example, for
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
, we can use
\ell=3
. However, it seems hard to prove the existence in general.

Having asserted the finely tuned nature of the conjecture (in
\mathbb{Z}
) and the theorem (in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
), we need to ask what happens when
\Re(s)>1
, that is, when the heights are given even more importance.

This question appears to depend delicately on the possible pole structures of the WAM coming from the denominators

\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\log p_{k})^{s}.

In general, numerical experiments indicate that the poles are quite chaotically distributed as we run over ABC triples. However, we can still make some remarks.
We tackle this by first noticing that for any
n
, we can find
a_{\text{crit}}=\Re(s)
such that
\wam(abc,s)
is bounded in the region
\Re(s)>a_{\text{crit}}
. Furthermore, we show that our choice of
a_{\text{crit}}
is optimal.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In
Sections

2
and
3
we prove the unboundedness of
\wam(n,s)
for both integer and polynomial triples when
\Re(s)<1
. Then, in
Section

4
we perform multiple numerical experiments with a dataset of
[
20
]
in order to explore the relationship between
\wam
and ABC triples in the region
\Re(s)>1
. We then show the boundedness of
\wam
for
\Re(s)>a_{\text{crit}}
, and present some plots to show the behaviour of the poles in the region
1<\Re(s)<a_{\text{crit}}
.

Looking back, we remark on the significance of computation for our understanding of
\wam
even in the region where
\Re(s)<1
. We believe that further computation will help with questions raised by our observations on the distribution of poles of
\wam
when
\Re(s)<a_{\text{crit}}
, or on the multiplicity of the largest prime factor
e_{m}
over ABC triples.

2.
Proof of
Theorem

1

In this section we will prove
Theorem

1
. We will show divergence of
\wam
for integers by computing
\wam
over
abc
triples
(1,2^{n}-1,2^{n})
and showing that it will be unbounded as
n
tends to infinity. Firstly, we will prove the simpler case for real
s
, and then we will generalise to complex
s
.

2.1.
Divergence for real
s<1

Behaviour of
\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen
depends on prime factorisation of
2^{n}-1
. While this factorisation is inaccessible, it will be sufficiently regular that we can place bounds on it. Let

2^{n}(2^{n}-1)=2^{n}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{e_{k}}

be the prime factorisation of
2^{n}(2^{n}-1)
, and
k=\omega(2^{n}-1)
be the number of prime factors of
2^{k}-1
. Then, we compute

\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen=\frac{n(\log 2)^{s}+\sum e_{i}(\log p_{i})^{s}}{(\log 2)^{s}+\sum(\log p_{i})^{s}}.

By placing suitable lower and upper bounds on the numerator and denominator respectively, we will show that the numerator of this expression asymptotically grows strictly faster than the denominator, which will make
\wam
diverge in the limit.

For the numerator we use the trivial bound

(6)

n(\log 2)^{s}+\sum e_{i}(\log p_{i})^{s}>n(\log 2)^{s}.

Next, we will show that the denominator grows strictly slower than that. We will consider cases
s\leq 0
and
0<s<1
separately.

Denominator bound for
s\leq 0
:

When
s\leq 0
we have that
(\log 2)^{s}>(\log p_{i})^{s}
for all primes
p_{i}
since
p_{i}>2
. Therefore, the denominator satisfies

(\log 2)^{s}+\sum(\log p_{i})^{s}<(k+1)(\log 2)^{s}

From this and
Eq.

6
we conclude

(7)

\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen>\frac{n}{k+1}

Now it suffices to show that
k
grows much slower than
n
. Recall that
k=\omega(2^{n}-1)
. From prime number theorem we have the asymptotic bound that for all
\epsilon>0
and
a>a_{0}

\omega(a)<(1+\epsilon)\frac{\log a}{\log\log a},

and even stronger bounds exist in
[
18
]
. Substituting
2^{n}-1
we conclude that asymptotically

k<c\frac{n}{\log n},

and applying this to
Eq.

7
shows that
\wam
grows at least as fast as
\log n
. Note that
\omega(a)=o(\log a)
is sufficient for the proof, but the stronger bound allows us to comment on the speed of the divergence.

Denominator bound for
0<s<1
:

When
s<0<1
, the function
x\mapsto x^{s}
is concave, and we will be using Jensen’s inequality

\sum\lambda_{i}f(x_{i})\leq f\left\lparen\sum\lambda_{i}x_{i}\right\rparen

for constants
\lambda_{i}
satisfying
\lambda_{i}>0
, and
\sum\lambda_{i}=1
. With this, we bound the denominator as follows

\displaystyle(\log 2)^{s}+\sum^{k}(\log p_{i})^{s}

\displaystyle=(\log 2)^{s}+k\sum\frac{1}{k}(\log p_{i})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq(\log 2)^{s}+k\left\lparen\sum\frac{1}{k}\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{s}

\displaystyle\text{Jensen's inequality for }(-)^{s}

\displaystyle=(\log 2)^{s}+k^{1-s}\left\lparen\sum\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{s}

\displaystyle\text{factor out }k

\displaystyle=(\log 2)^{s}+k^{1-s}\left\lparen\log\rad(2^{n}-1)\right\rparen^{s}

simplify sum

\displaystyle\leq(\log 2)^{s}+k^{1-s}\left\lparen\log(2^{n}-1)\right\rparen^{s}

\displaystyle\rad(x)\leq x

\displaystyle<(\log 2)^{s}+(1+\epsilon)\left\lparen\frac{n}{\log n}\right\rparen^{1-s}\left\lparen\log(2^{n}-1)\right\rparen^{s}

\displaystyle\text{bound }k

\displaystyle<(\log 2)^{s}+(1+\epsilon)\left\lparen\frac{n}{\log n}\right\rparen^{1-s}\left\lparen n\log 2\right\rparen^{s}

\displaystyle\text{by }\log(2^{n}-1)<\log(2^{n})

\displaystyle<(\log 2)^{s}+(1+\epsilon)\frac{n\lparen\log 2\rparen^{s}}{\lparen\log n\rparen^{1-s}}

simplify

\displaystyle\sim(\log 2)^{s}\frac{n}{\lparen\log n\rparen^{1-s}}

asymptotic bound

From this, we have that asymptotically
\wam
is lower bound by

\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen>\frac{n(\log 2)^{s}}{(\log 2)^{s}\frac{n}{\lparen\log n\rparen^{1-s}}}=(\log n)^{1-s},

which diverges since
s<1
.

This proof is formalized in Lean 4
[
16
]
, and can be found at
https://github.com/mirjanic/wam_lean
.

2.2.
Divergence for complex
s
with
\Re(s)<1

If
s
is complex then so is
\wam
, so we will prove that it diverges in magnitude. Specifically, we will show that

(8)

\left\lvert\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen\right\rvert>\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1-\left\lparen\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\right\rparen^{1-\Re(s)}\right\rparen\wam\Big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),\Re(s)\Big\rparen.

Note that the given constant factor is positive. Together with the result from the previous section, this implies that
\wam
does diverge in magnitude for
\Re(s)<1
. To show
Eq.

8
we begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The prime factorisation of
2^{n}-1
satisfies

\sum e_{i}\left\lvert\left\lparen\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{s}\right\rvert<n\lparen\log 3\rparen^{\Re(s)-1}\log 2

Proof.

We consider two cases depending on whether
\Re(s)>0
or not.

Case
\Re(s)\leq 0
:

Since
p_{i}\geq 3
then
\log p_{i}\geq\log 3
. But, because
\Re(s)<0
we have
(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}\leq(\log 3)^{\Re(s)}
. From this we conclude

\displaystyle\sum e_{i}\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum e_{i}(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\lvert(-)^{s}\rvert\mapsto(-)^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum e_{i}(\log 3)^{\Re(s)}

by above

\displaystyle=(\log 3)^{\Re(s)}\Omega(2^{n}-1)

\displaystyle\text{by }\sum e_{i}=\Omega(2^{n}-1)

\displaystyle\leq(\log 3)^{\Re(s)}\frac{\log(2^{n}-1)}{\log 3}

\displaystyle\text{by }\Omega(x)\leq\frac{\log x}{\log p_{\text{min}}(x)}

\displaystyle<(\log 3)^{\Re(s)}\frac{n\log 2}{\log 3}

\displaystyle\text{by }\log(2^{n}-1)<\log(2^{n})

\displaystyle=n\lparen\log 3\rparen^{\Re(s)-1}\log 2

Case
0<\Re(s)<1
:

In this case the function
x\mapsto x^{s}
is concave, and we will use Jensen’s inequality. Let
K=\Omega(2^{n}-1)
.

\displaystyle\sum e_{i}\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\rvert

\displaystyle=K\sum\frac{e_{i}}{K}(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\lvert(-)^{s}\rvert\mapsto(-)^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle<K\left\lparen\sum\frac{e_{i}}{K}\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\text{Jensen's inequality for }(-)^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle=K^{1-\Re(s)}\left\lparen\sum e_{i}\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\text{factor out }K

\displaystyle=K^{1-\Re(s)}\left\lparen\log(2^{n}-1)\right\rparen^{\Re(s)}

simplify sum

\displaystyle\leq\left\lparen\frac{\log(2^{n}-1)}{\log 3}\right\rparen^{1-\Re(s)}\left\lparen\log(2^{n}-1)\right\rparen^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle\text{by }\Omega(x)\leq\frac{\log x}{\log p_{\text{min}}(x)}

\displaystyle=(\log 3)^{\Re(s)-1}\log(2^{n}-1)

factor

\displaystyle<n(\log 3)^{\Re(s)-1}\log 2

\displaystyle\text{by }\log(2^{n}-1)<\log(2^{n})

∎

In particular, we note that this implies

(9)

\sum e_{i}\left\lvert\left\lparen\log p_{i}\right\rparen^{s}\right\rvert=\sum e_{i}(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}<n(\log 2)^{\Re(s)}=n\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert

Next, we apply triangle inequalities to the numerator and denominator of
\lvert\wam\rvert
:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\wam\big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),s\big\rparen\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{n(\log 2)^{s}+\sum e_{i}(\log p_{i})^{s}}{(\log 2)^{s}+\sum(\log p_{i})^{s}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert-\sum e_{i}\left\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\right\rvert}{\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert+\sum\left\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\right\rvert}

triangle inequalities

\displaystyle>\frac{n\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert-\lparen\log 3\rparen^{\Re(s)-1}\log(2^{n}-1)}{\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert+\sum\left\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\right\rvert}

by
Lemma

1

\displaystyle>\frac{n\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert-n(\log 3)^{\Re(s)-1}\log 2}{\lvert(\log 2)^{s}\rvert+\sum\left\lvert(\log p_{i})^{s}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\text{by }\log(2^{n}-1)<\log(2^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n(\log 2)^{\Re(s)}-n(\log 3)^{\Re(s)-1}\log 2}{(\log 2)^{\Re(s)}+\sum(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}}

\displaystyle\lvert(-)^{s}\rvert\mapsto(-)^{\Re(s)}

\displaystyle=\left\lparen 1-\left\lparen\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\right\rparen^{1-\Re(s)}\right\rparen\frac{n(\log 2)^{\Re(s)}}{(\log 2)^{\Re(s)}+\sum(\log p_{i})^{\Re(s)}}

simplify numerator

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1-\left\lparen\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\right\rparen^{1-\Re(s)}\right\rparen\wam\Big\lparen 2^{n}(2^{n}-1),\Re(s)\Big\rparen

by
Eq.

9

Since the latter diverges, we are done.

3.
Proof of
Theorem

2

In this section we will prove
Theorem

2
, which states that
\wam
diverges over
abc
triples of polynomials for
\Re(s)<1
. We will first prove the theorem for
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, and then for
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
. These proofs will be significantly less demanding compared to the integer case. In both cases, we will construct triples that have few irreducible factors but large multiplicities, and this will make
\wam
diverge in the appropriate limit.

3.1.
Proof for
\mathbb{Q}[x]

Consider the following polynomial triple:
(P,Q,R)=(1,x^{p}-1,x^{p})
, for some prime
p
. This is a valid
abc
triple since clearly
P+Q=R
, and they are not all constant. We will proceed to compute
\wam
for these triples and show that
\lim_{p\to+\infty}\wam(PQR,s)
diverges for
\Re(s)<1
.

To compute
\wam
we need to factorise
PQR
, and this amounts to factorising
Q
. We have

Q(x)=(x-1)(1+x+\dots+x^{p-1})=(x-1)\Phi_{p}(x),

where
\Phi_{p}
is the cyclotomic polynomial, which is known to be irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Thus, we have
PQR=(x-1)x^{p}\Phi_{p}(x)
, so

\wam(PQR,s)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}e_{k}(\deg p_{k})^{s}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\deg p_{k})^{s}}=\frac{\overbrace{1\times 1^{s}}^{\text{from $x-1$}}+\overbrace{1\times p^{s}}^{\text{from $\Phi_{p}(x)$}}+\overbrace{p\times 1^{s}}^{\text{from $x^{p}$}}}{1^{s}+p^{s}+1^{s}}=\frac{p+p^{s}+1}{p^{s}+2}.

When
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
s<1
, this expression diverges since
p\gg p^{s}
in the limit of
p\to+\infty
over primes
p
. For complex
s
with
\Re(s)<1
, the same argument holds except we consider the magnitude of
\wam
instead.

3.2.
Proof for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

The above construction can fail in a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
since, as already hinted, the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi
can become reducible. Finding an irreducible cyclotomic polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
will to our knowledge require Artin’s conjecture on primitive roots, which currently relies on the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis
[
2
]
. Thus we abandon this approach and instead provide a non-constructive proof based on a counting argument.

The number of monic polynomials of degree
n
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
is trivially
q^{n}
. On the other hand, the number of
irreducible
monic polynomials
N_{q}(n)
can be found in classical textbooks such as
[
10
, Theorem 3.25]
, and is given by the formula

N_{q}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}=\frac{1}{n}q^{n}+\mathcal{O}(q^{n/2})

where
\mu
is the Möbius function. For
n\gg q
, this expression can be very accurately estimated by its first order approximation.

Now, we pick some large
n
, and to ensure that formal derivatives will not vanish, we require that the characteristic
p
does not divide
n
. Next, we note that

\frac{1}{n}q^{n}=q^{n-\frac{\log n}{\log q}}

and we pick
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N_{q}(n)>q^{n-k}
. In practice,
k=\lceil\frac{\log n}{\log q}\rceil
works for sufficiently large
n
. We remark that
k
grows like
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
since
q
is fixed.

Now, consider the generic monic polynomial of degree
n
:

\underbrace{a_{0}+a_{1}x+\dots+a_{n-k-1}x^{n-k-1}}_{\text{Lower part}}+a_{n-k}x^{n-k}+\dots+x^{n}

There are
q^{n-k}
different lower parts among these polynomials. Meanwhile, the number of irreducible monic polynomials is strictly bigger than
q^{n-k}
by the definition of
k
. Therefore, by the pigeonhole principle, there must exist two irreducible polynomials that share the same lower part. Call them
P
and
Q
.

Since they share the same lower part,
P(x)-Q(x)=x^{n-k}R(x)
for some
R
with
\deg R=k
. Our triple will be
(Q,x^{n-k}R,P)
, which satisfies all conditions needed to be an
abc
triple. Finally, we calculate

\wam(PQR,s)=\frac{\overbrace{1\times n^{s}}^{\text{from $P(x)$}}+\overbrace{1\times n^{s}}^{\text{from $Q(x)$}}+\overbrace{(n-k)\times 1^{s}+\mathcal{O}(\log n)}^{\text{from $x^{n-k}R(x)$}}}{n^{s}+n^{s}+1^{s}+\mathcal{O}(\log n)}=\frac{n+2n^{s}+\mathcal{O}(\log n)}{2n^{s}+1+\mathcal{O}(\log n)}.

The
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
term comes from factorising
R(x)
, which we have no control over, but know it must be bounded by
\deg R=k=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Finally, just like earlier, we observe that
\wam
will diverge as
n\to+\infty
since the
n
term in the numerator dominates all other terms in the expression.

4.
Behaviour of
\wam
for
\Re(s)\geq 1

Having analysed the behaviour of
\wam(n,s)
in the region
\Re(s)<1
in
Sections

2
and
3
, we now turn to the region
\Re(s)\geq 1
. We provide numerical evidence based on the dataset of over
10^{7}
ABC triples collected by
[
20
]
.

(a)

Large section of the complex plane, where
\max
is taken over 50000 triples.

(b)

Zoomed in region with poles, where

4.2.
Behaviour for
\Re(s)\to+\infty

As

\lim_{\Re(s)\to+\infty}\wam(n,s)=e_{m},

where

abc

We observe that triples with large

is unbounded in the limit.

As mentioned in the introduction, we have
e_{m}<\wam(n,1)\omega(n)
. If the

Therefore, the asymptotic behaviour of

4.3.
Behaviour for
1<\Re(s)\ll+\infty

Consider the primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
appearing in factorisation of some integer
n
. Assume
n
is not a perfect power of a squarefree number so that
\wam
is not constant and let

(11)

f(s)=\sum(\log p_{k})^{s}

be the denominator of
\wam
. Further assume that
m>2
so that poles of
\wam
are nontrivial. We wish to characterise the region in which
\wam(n,s)
has no poles. A necessary condition is to find a region where
f(s)
has no zeros.

Let
s=a+ib
. If
\wam
has a pole at
s
, then
f(s)=0
. Expanding
f
one obtains

\displaystyle\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{s}

\displaystyle=-(\log p_{m})^{s}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{s}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert(\log p_{m})^{s}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}

\displaystyle\geq(\log p_{m})^{a}

Therefore, every pole of
\wam
satisfies this inequality. Both left and right hand sides are monotonically increasing, but the left side is greater for small
a
while the right side is greater for large
a
. Thus, there exists a unique
a_{\text{crit}}
at which the equality is attained:

(12)

\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a_{\text{crit}}}=(\log p_{m})^{a_{\text{crit}}}

and every pole
s=a+ib
satisfies
a\leq a_{\text{crit}}
. Thus, we have by construction the following.

Proposition 1
.

The function
f(a+ib)
has no zeros in the region
a>a_{\text{crit}}
.

Next, we will show that this bound is optimal and that there exist zeros arbitrarily close to the critical line
a=a_{\text{crit}}
. We begin by establishing some useful lemmas.

Lemma 2

(Equidistribution)
.

If
1,\alpha,\beta,...
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
then the set of fractional parts

\bigg\{\Big\lparen\{\alpha n\},\{\beta n\},\dots\Big\rparen\Big|~n\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\}

is dense in the unit hypercube.

Lemma 3
.

For two different primes
p
and
q
,
\log p
and
\log q
are linearly independent.

Proof.

Otherwise, one would have
p^{a}=q^{b}
for some integers
a,b
, clearly a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2
.

For any set of phases
(\theta_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
there is a sequence
(b_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
such that for all
k
:

\lim_{t\to\infty}(b_{t}\log p_{k}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)=\theta_{k}

Proof.

Notice that the vector
\theta/2\pi
is inside the unit hypercube. By linear independence of
\log p_{k}/2\pi
over
\mathbb{Q}
we can find a sequence of integers
m_{t}
such that for all
t
:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\{\frac{\log p_{k}}{2\pi}m_{t}\right\}=\frac{\theta_{k}}{2\pi}

Then, the sequence
(b_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
defined with
b_{t}=2\pi m_{t}
satisfies the desired condition.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The above sequence
(b_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
\lim_{t}f(a+ib_{t})=\sum(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}}
.

With these statements we can now focus on the zeros of
f
near
a_{\text{crit}}
.

Lemma 4
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the derivative of
f
has a positive lower bound
M>0
on the vertical strip
a\in(a_{\text{crit}}-\varepsilon,a_{\text{crit}}]
:

\lvert f^{\prime}(a+ib)\rvert\geq M

Proof.

Let

\delta=\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}\left\lparen 1-\frac{\log\log p_{k}}{\log\log p_{m}}\right\rparen>0.

Next, pick
a_{0}
such that

(\log p_{m})^{a_{0}}+\delta=\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a_{0}}

This way for
a\in(a_{0},a_{\text{crit}}]
one has

(\log p_{m})^{a}+\delta>\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}

Now, by computing the derivative we have

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(a+ib)|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum(\log p_{k})^{s}\log\log p_{k}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\left\lvert|(\log p_{m})^{s}|\log\log p_{m}-\sum^{m-1}|(\log p_{k})^{s}|\log\log p_{k}\right\rvert

triangle ineq.

\displaystyle=(\log p_{m})^{a}\log\log p_{m}-\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}\log\log p_{k}

\displaystyle>\left\lparen\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}-\delta\right\rparen\log\log p_{m}-\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}\log\log p_{k}

\displaystyle\text{ from }a>a_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}(\log\log p_{m}-\log\log p_{k})-\delta\log\log p_{m}

\displaystyle>\sum^{m-1}\big\lparen(\log p_{k})^{a}-(\log p_{k})^{a_{0}}\big\rparen(\log\log p_{m}-\log\log p_{k})

\displaystyle\text{ definition of }\delta

\displaystyle=M(a)>0

Finally, pick some
\varepsilon<a_{\text{crit}}-a_{0}
and
M=\min M(a)>0
.
∎

Theorem 3

(Rouché)
.

If two holomorphic functions
g
and
h
satisfy
|h(z)|<|g(z)|
on some contour
\partial K
, then
g
and
g+h
have the same number of zeros in
K
.

Proposition 3
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
a\in(a_{\text{crit}}-\varepsilon,a_{\text{crit}}]
and phases
(\theta_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
satisfying

\sum(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}}=0,

there is a sequence of pairs
(a_{t},b_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
with:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}a_{t}

\displaystyle=a

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}(b_{t}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}\mathrm{\ for\ all\ }k

\displaystyle f(a_{t}+ib_{t})

\displaystyle=0\mathrm{\ for\ all\ }t

Proof.

By the previous proposition we have a series
(b_{t})
so that
\lim f(a+ib_{t})=\sum(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}}=0
. However,
a+ib_{t}
are not zeros of
f
. To fix this, we will show that there are zeros of
f
near
a+ib_{t}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
z_{0}=a_{\text{crit}}+ib
for some
b
where
|f|<\varepsilon
and consider the two functions

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle=f(z_{0})+f^{\prime}(z_{0})(z-z_{0})

\displaystyle h(z)

\displaystyle=f(z)-g(z)

Since
g
is a first order Taylor approximation to
f
, we can expect to have
|h|<|g|
in a radius
r
around our point. Also,
g(z)
has a zero exactly at
z_{0}-f(z_{0})/f^{\prime}(z_{0})
. Since
|f(z_{0})|<\varepsilon
and
|f^{\prime}(z_{0})|>M
, we have that a zero of
g
is within
\varepsilon/M
of
z_{0}
(which we can make arbitrarily small by shrinking
\varepsilon
to fit inside
r
).

Therefore, by Rouché we have the
f=g+h
also has a zero near
z_{0}
.

Since these zeros will approach
a+ib_{t}
arbitrarily close by shrinking
\varepsilon
, taking the sequence of these zeros completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The above sequence
(a_{t},b_{t})
, satisfies
\lim(\log p_{k})^{a_{t}+ib_{t}}=(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}}
for all
k
.

Proposition 4
.

The function
f(a+ib)
has zeros in the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the line
x=a_{\text{crit}}
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Set
\theta_{m}
= 0 and
\theta_{k}=\pi
for
k<m
. By definition of
a_{\text{crit}}
one has

\sum(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}}=0
. By
3
there exists a sequence
(a_{t},b_{t})
such that
f(a_{t}+ib_{t})=0
and
\lim a_{t}=a_{\text{crit}}
, as required.
∎

So far we established that
f
has zeros arbitrarily close to
a=a_{\text{crit}}
. However, this is not sufficient by itself to conclude that
\wam
has poles here. For that, one has to show that at these zeroes the numerator of
\wam
is non-zero, as the singularities could be removable otherwise.

Proposition 5
.

The function
\wam(a+ib)
has poles in the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the line
x=a_{\text{crit}}
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
a_{\text{crit}}
is not the optimal bound for poles of
\wam
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
so that all poles with
a\in(a_{\text{crit}}-\varepsilon,a_{\text{crit}}]
are removable, meaning

(13)

f(s)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{s}=0

We will show that this implies all
e_{k}
are the same, making
\wam
constant and having no poles at all. To do this we will
‘realify’

Eq.

13
, just as previously we
‘realified’
the condition
f(s)=0
into a statement involving
a
only.

Let
a_{0}
be some value in range
(a_{\text{crit}}-\varepsilon,a_{\text{crit}}]
, and let
\theta_{k}
be any phases that exactly satisfy the following equation:

\sum(\log p_{k})^{a_{0}}e^{i\theta_{k}}=0.

By
3
we can find a sequence
(a_{t},b_{t})
so that
\lim a_{t}=a_{0}
,
f(a_{t}+ib_{t})=0
for all
t
, and each term in
f
approaches the chosen phase individually:

\lim(\log p_{k})^{a_{t}+ib_{t}}=(\log p_{k})^{a_{0}}e^{i\theta_{k}}\text{ for all }k

From
Eq.

13
we know that the the numerator is zero at these points, and since it also has a well defined limit we conclude that for our choice of
a_{0}
and
\theta_{k}
we also have

\sum e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{a_{0}}e^{i\theta_{k}}=0.

By varying
a_{0}
and choosing appropriate phases
\theta_{k}
(namely, dependent on
a
) at each point, we obtain the following key identity:

(14)

\sum(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}(a)}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{a}e^{i\theta_{k}(a)}=0

Recall that
\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}\geq(\log p_{m})^{a}
, for all
a
in this region, with equality holding at
a_{\text{crit}}
. Therefore, in the left
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
a_{\text{crit}}
we have that
(\log p_{1})^{a}
,
(\log p_{2})^{a}
, and
(\log p_{m})^{a}-\sum_{3}^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}
satisfy the triangle inequality.

Now, we can pick our phases
\theta_{k}
so that
\theta_{m}=\pi
,
\theta_{k}=0
for
3\leq k<m
, and
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are uniquely determined to satisfy the first part of
Eq.

14
. From this we obtain:

(15)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\log p_{1})^{a}e^{i\theta_{1}(a)}+(\log p_{2})^{a}e^{i\theta_{2}(a)}+\sum_{3}^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}-(\log p_{m})^{a}

(16)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=e_{1}(\log p_{1})^{a}e^{i\theta_{1}(a)}+e_{2}(\log p_{2})^{a}e^{i\theta_{2}(a)}+\sum_{3}^{m-1}e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{a}-e_{m}(\log p_{m})^{a}

Multiplying
Eq.

15
by
e_{1}
and subtracting from
Eq.

16
we obtain

0=(e_{2}-e_{1})\underset{\in\mathbb{C}}{\underbrace{(\log p_{2})^{a}e^{i\theta_{2}(a)}\vphantom{\sum_{3}^{m-1}(e_{k}-e_{1})(\log p_{k})^{a}-(e_{m}-e_{1})(\log p_{m})^{a})}}}+\underset{\in\mathbb{R}}{\underbrace{\sum_{3}^{m-1}(e_{k}-e_{1})(\log p_{k})^{a}-(e_{m}-e_{1})(\log p_{m})^{a}}}

but this is only possible if
e_{1}=e_{2}
since the complex term is not constant.

Finally, we conclude by symmetry that all
e_{k}
are pairwise equal, meaning that
n
is a
e
-th power of a squarefree number. This is a contradiction because we ruled this case out at the beginning of the section.
∎

a_{\text{crit}}
have been established, we can investigate the behaviour of
a_{\text{crit}}
for different
n
, and
\wam
for
a>a_{\text{crit}}
.

Firstly, we numerically observe from

a_{\text{crit}}\sim p_{m}

Then, we note that
\wam
is unbounded on line
a=a_{\text{crit}}
, even though it does not have poles on this line. Meanwhile, for any fixed
a>a_{\text{crit}}
there exists a bound on
\lvert\wam\rvert
and it is derived using the triangle inequality:

(17)

\lvert\wam(n,a+ib)\rvert\leq\frac{\sum e_{k}(\log p_{k})^{a}}{(\log p_{m})^{a}-\sum^{m-1}(\log p_{k})^{a}}=\wam^{upper}(a)

This bound is not necessarily optimal, but it is monotonically decreasing and converges to
e_{m}
in the limit, which is consistent with what was previously derived about
\wam
.

There are tools in complex analysis such as Phragmén-Lindelöf theorem
[
17
]
that can also produce bounds on
\wam
.

Theorem 4

(Phragmén-Lindelöf)
.

Let
a\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
G=\{z:|\text{arg z}|<\frac{\pi}{2a}\}
. Suppose
f
is holomorphic on
G
and there is a constant
M
such that
\limsup_{z\mapsto w}{|f(z)|\leq M}
for all
w
in
\partial G
. If there are positive constants
P
and
b<a
such that

|f(z)|\leq P\exp(|z|^{b})

for all
z
with
|z|
sufficiently large, then
|f(z)|\leq M
for all
z
in
G
.

However, this requires establishing a bound on a boundary of some region
G
first. By the requirement of holomorphicity,
\Re(z)
in the region G and on the boundary
\partial G
must be at least
a_{\text{crit}}
.

If the upper bound on
G
is established using
Eq.

17
, then the bound from Phragmén-Lindelöf will be strictly weaker than simply applying
Eq.

17
to the bulk of
G
.
