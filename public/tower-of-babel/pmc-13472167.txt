Introduction

Paragangliomas (PGLs) are tumours derived from extra-adrenal chromaffin cells located in the sympathetic paravertebral ganglia of the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis.1 The incidence of PGLs is between 1.5 and 9 cases per million people. Cardiac PGLs are rare, accounting for <0.3% of mediastinal tumours and 1–3% of all primary cardiac tumours.2 In the heart, the most common location is in the left atrium (55%), followed by the interatrial septum (16%), the anterior surface of the heart (10%), and the right atrium, aortic root, or left ventricle in about 5% of cases. These tumours are well vascularized and thus, should not be biopsied, as this may result in life threatening haemorrhage. Surgical resection is the primary treatment for PGLs; however, early diagnosis is critical as pre-operative pharmacological blockade is necessary to mitigate the risk of intra-operative complications caused by the unregulated release of catecholamines from hormonally active tumours.3

Case report

A 56-year-old male athlete presented to the office with complaints of persistent chest pressure, that he described as a constricting feeling across the chest; associated headaches, palpitations, and diaphoresis over the last few months. The chest pressure was relieved with running, was non-radiating and had no association with breathing. His past medical history was significant for hyperlipidaemia and coronary artery disease (CAD) for which he had undergone a percutaneous coronary intervention to the left anterior descending artery (LAD). He did not have a family history of cardiac disease or sudden cardiac death. He had never smoked, consumed alcohol rarely and denied use of any illicit substances in the past. Vital signs in the office revealed a blood pressure of 146/88 Hg in the right arm while seated, a heart rate of 96/min, a respiratory rate of 26/min and temperature of 99 F. A physical examination was suggestive of a lean and thin body habitus with clammy palms, a fine tremor of the upper extremities and normal pulmonary/cardiac examination.

For the evaluation of his persistent symptoms, the patient underwent a cardiac catheterization that revealed no significant residual obstructive CAD and a patent drug-eluting stent in the LAD. This also showed a vascular mass adjacent to the bifurcation of the LAD and left circumflex (LCx) artery (Figure 1). Further workup included a computed tomography (CT) angiogram (Figure 2) which showed a well circumscribed 2.7×1.9 cm enhancing mass in close proximity to the LAD and adjacent to the medial aspect of the left atrial appendage. A cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) was performed which showed a highly vascular, lobular mass in the atrioventricular groove, adjacent to LAD/LCx artery bifurcation (Figure 3). An echocardiogram revealed a normal ventricular function and moderate left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy (predominantly septal; 15–16 mm) with dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) and systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet on exercise echocardiography (Figure 4; C–E demonstrating stress images). Fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan was also performed which showed a well circumscribed mass with markedly increased PET activity adjacent to the LAD along the superior aspect of the base of the LV and an intensity at greater curvature of stomach (a subsequent biopsy of which was benign). Based on the clinical assessment and the results of the above diagnostic findings, our most likely differential diagnoses were a cardiac PGL vs. a haemangioma.

For definitive diagnosis and management of this suspicious cardiac mass, a surgical resection was planned after a shared decision-making process. Consultation with Endocrinology and further labs were obtained, revealing elevated 24-h urine normetanephrine, 958 mcg (normal: 24–96 mcg) and plasma normetanephrine, 1.48 nmol/L (normal: 0–0.89 nmol/L). The patient underwent pre-operative alpha blockade with doxazosin, however because of his left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, the team was extremely vigilant with the use of alpha-blockade. He underwent an excision of the suspected LV epicardial PGL with a septal myectomy. The mass was found to be located on the surface of the LV abutting the LAD. Post-operatively, the patient developed hypotension secondary to vasoplegia, not responsive to norepinephrine or phenylephrine. He was thus, started on vasopressin, and eventually weaned off the vasopressors over the following day. Surgical pathology confirmed the diagnosis of a cardiac PGL and additionally, revealed the presence of moderate myocyte hypertrophy and focal disarray, mild interstitial and replacement fibrosis.

The patient was counselled on the diagnosis and genetic testing was advised, which he decided to undergo. The genetic report showed the presence of SDHC c.86del (p.Pro29Leufs*18) mutation, consistent with the diagnosis of hereditary PGLs-pheochromocytoma syndrome. The patient did not recall having any family history of a PGLs or pheochromocytoma. Post-operative imaging revealed regression of LV hypertrophy without any recurrence of the mass on a 1- and 6-month follow-up visit.

Discussion

Cardiac PGLs, while rare, are a significant differential of vascular cardiac masses and may present with subtle symptoms, as exemplified in the case. The European Society of Hypertension offers comprehensive guidelines for the diagnosis and management of PGLs, which involves a combination of biochemical testing, imaging, and genetic assessment.4

Biochemical testing typically includes the measurement of plasma-free metanephrines or 24-h urinary fractionated metanephrines, both of which demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity for catecholamine-secreting tumours. Imaging modalities such as CT and cMRI are essential for both preoperative anatomical localization and tissue characterization of cardiac masses. In our case, cMRI was particularly critical in distinguishing a cardiac PGL from other intracardiac lesions. The mass demonstrated hyperintense signals on T2-weighted sequences and avid post-gadolinium enhancement, features highly suggestive of a vascular neuroendocrine tumor.5 Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging contributed important complementary information for tissue characterization and anatomic definition when interpreted alongside CTA and PET findings. On cMRI, the mass was well circumscribed, demonstrated heterogeneous signal intensity with T2 hyperintensity, and showed avid, heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement, consistent with a highly vascular lesion. While these features are not specific to paraganglioma, their combination helped refine the differential diagnosis. Thrombus was unlikely given the presence of robust contrast enhancement, as thrombi are typically avascular and non-enhancing. Cardiac haemangiomas, although vascular, more commonly demonstrate relatively uniform enhancement and a less lobulated morphology. Myxomas typically appear heterogeneous on imaging but are generally less intensely vascular, often show a characteristic site of attachment, and do not usually exhibit the marked hyperenhancement seen in this case. Metastatic cardiac deposits may demonstrate heterogeneous signal intensity and variable enhancement but more often present as multiple lesions or in the setting of known extracardiac malignancy, neither of which was present here. Importantly, cMRI was particularly valuable in defining the lesion’s precise location and its relationship to adjacent cardiac structures and coronary vasculature, findings that closely correlated with CTA, which demonstrated a sharply marginated, intensely enhancing mass. When integrated with PET imaging showing focal metabolic activity without evidence of multifocal disease, the overall multimodality imaging profile supported the diagnosis of a solitary, highly vascular cardiac tumour and illustrates the role of cMRI as part of a comprehensive, rather than standalone, diagnostic framework.5,6 Additionally, PET/CT has demonstrated high specificity and sensitivity in identifying PGLs, particularly in patients with SDHx mutations, consistent with recommendations from the European Association of Nuclear Medicine.7 The integration of these imaging modalities allowed precise surgical planning and informed the decision for safe resection, highlighting the indispensable role of cMRI in evaluating suspected cardiac PGLs

Genetic testing plays a crucial role, given the strong genetic predisposition associated with PGLs. Approximately one-third of cases are linked to germline mutations in susceptibility genes, such as those encoding succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) subunits.4 In our patient, identification of a germline SDHC variant highlights the role of hereditary PGLs–pheochromocytoma (PGL/PCC) syndromes in seemingly sporadic cardiac PGLs. Although SDHC mutations account for a small fraction (2%–8%) of hereditary PGL/PCC, they are disproportionately associated with thoracic and mediastinal locations, consistent with the epicardial mass observed in our case. Unlike SDHB/SDHD variants, SDHC mutations often demonstrate reduced penetrance and may present without a discernable family history.8,9 Loss of SDHC function leads to succinate accumulation and pseudohypoxic signalling via HIF stabilization, driving tumorigenesis.10 Clinically, most SDHC-related tumours are non-metastatic but may be functionally active, as in this case with elevated normetanephrines.8 Recognition of this genotype carries important implications for lifelong biochemical and imaging surveillance, as well as cascade genetic counselling for at-risk relatives, even in the absence of familial disease.9

Catecholamine-secreting PGLs can cause septal hypertrophy and dynamic LVOTO through catecholamine-induced myocardial remodelling and hypercontractility.11,12 Excess catecholamines promote hypertrophy and hyperdynamic flow, narrowing the LVOT and reproducing physiology similar to HCM. In our patient, echocardiography revealed moderate basal septal hypertrophy with stress-induced LVOTO and SAM of the anterior mitral leaflet, explained by flow acceleration rather than intrinsic mitral pathology. Histology showed moderate myocyte hypertrophy with focal disarray and mild fibrosis—changes consistent with catecholamine-mediated myocardial stress rather than sarcomeric HCM. Dynamic LVOTO has been reported in PGLs patients with adrenergic excess and often resolves after tumour resection and normalization of catecholamine levels.11–13 Because regression of LV hypertrophy was anticipated following removal of the hormonal trigger, additional HCM gene testing was not pursued.

The primary treatment for PGLs is surgical resection, which is the only curative option. Preoperative preparation includes initiation of alpha-adrenergic blockade to control the blood pressure and prevent intraoperative hypertensive crises. Beta-blockers may be cautiously added after adequate alpha blockade to manage tachycardia and arrhythmias. In select cases, especially when tumours are highly vascular or anatomically challenging, preoperative embolization can be considered to reduce intraoperative bleeding. Postoperative follow-up should include biochemical monitoring and imaging to detect recurrence. In cases of metastatic disease, treatment options are limited and may include systemic chemotherapy, radionuclide therapy, and targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, though evidence is largely from small case series.4,14

Conclusion

Cardiac PGLs present a unique diagnostic challenge and should be strongly suspected in patients presenting with suggestive signs/symptoms, characteristic imaging findings and elevated catecholamines suggesting hormonally active tumours. After definitive surgical resection, genetic testing is recommended for common associated mutations to guide further counselling for family members.
