Piperine suppresses M1 phenotype and induces indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase gene in LPS-stimulated murine macrophage cell line
Authors: Nasser Gholijani, Zeinab Dehghan, Samira Sadat Abolmaali, Atefe Ghamar Talepoor, Kurosh Kalantar, Gholamreza Daryabor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveActivated macrophages play important roles during immune responses by polarizing into M1 and M2 subsets and overproducing several mediators such as nitric oxide (NO), pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO). This study aims to investigate the possible effects of piperine, a major component of the pepper plant, on the expression of M1 and M2 macrophage markers.ResultsPiperine dose-dependently decreased iNOS gene expression and NO level in LPS-stimulated macrophages. Additionally, at concentrations of 1–20 µg/ml, it significantly suppressed the production and/or gene expression levels of proinflammatory M1-related cytokines. Its effects on cytokine genes were dose-dependent, and reduced IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ expression (17 ± 11.8, 1.33 ± 0.76, and 25.4 ± 5.2 fold, respectively, compared with LPS-stimulated cells). Piperine at 1 µg/ml didn’t affect the IL-10 gene but significantly increased IDO1 (from 8.4 ± 0.24-fold in LPS-treated cells to 28.3 ± 5.3-fold in piperine-treated cells), and decreased IL-4 gene expressions at the higher concentrations. Dexamethasone (a glucocorticoid anti-inflammatory drug) reduced most mediators but did not affect the IDO gene. Our data demonstrate that piperine dose-dependently inhibits M1-macrophages and upregulates IDO1 at low concentration, a unique mechanism of action, positioning it as a multifaceted therapeutic candidate for inflammatory conditions.

Introduction

Inflammation is a highly coordinated physiological process triggered by harmful stimuli, including infections, exposure to microbial products, and tissue injury [1]. Prolonged or exaggerated inflammatory responses contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetes, arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancers [2, 3]. Macrophages, derived from monocytes in virtually all body tissues, exhibit a highly plastic and heterogeneous phenotype and adapt to their surrounding microenvironment. They play a pivotal role in the progression of inflammatory diseases by infiltrating injured tissues and releasing pro-inflammatory mediators [4]. In response to environmental signals, macrophages polarize into M1 (classically activated or “killer”) and M2 (alternatively activated or “healer”) phenotypes [5]. Inflammatory stimuli such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) drive macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, whereas Interleukin (IL)−4, IL-10, and IL-13 promote differentiation into the M2 phenotype [5]. M1 macrophages produce pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, and TNF-α), mediating antimicrobial and antiviral responses [5, 6]. In contrast, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-4, IL-10, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)), playing a key role in tissue repair and remodeling [5, 6]. The M2 macrophage phenotype is not a single entity but encompasses several subsets crucial for resolving inflammation, promoting tissue repair, and restoring homeostasis [4–6]. The imbalance between pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization is a key pathophysiological feature of chronic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and sepsis [7, 8]. In these conditions, sustained M1 activity leads to tissue destruction and impaired healing. Therefore, identifying compounds that can reprogram macrophages from a damaging M1 to a reparative M2 phenotype has significant therapeutic implications. For thousands of years, several plant species have served as therapeutic agents for immune-mediated and inflammatory disorders [9, 10]. Black pepper (Piper nigrum Linn.) and long pepper (Piper longum Linn.) are medicinal plants used since ancient times to treat various diseases in many cultures, worldwide. Pipper plants contain numerous bioactive phytochemicals with health benefits. Among their key components, piperine, an alkaloid with C17H19NO3 chemical formula and 1-[5-(1,3-benzodioxol5-yl)−1-oxo-2,4-pentadienyl] IUPAC name, possesses a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities and is responsible for the pungent taste [11, 12]. Although piperine’s general anti-inflammatory and therapeutic efficacy is established, its specific influence on macrophage polarization remains unclear [12–15]. Piperine can also enhance the bioavailability of drugs and potentiate their pharmacological effects [16, 17]. Accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of macrophages in immune responses and the inhibitory effect of piperine on their inflammatory activity [18, 19]. This study investigated piperine’s modulatory effects on macrophage polarization, a key determinant of immune response, by quantifying critical M1 and M2 phenotypic markers in vitro.

Materials and methods

Reagents

RPMI-1640 medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were from Gibco (USA). Sigma-Aldrich (USA) supplied trypan blue, MTT, LPS (E. coli 0111:B4), and DMSO. L-glutamine, penicillin, and streptomycin were from Biorsera (USA). L-NAME was from Life Sciences (UK), the total RNA extraction kit from Pars Tous (Iran), the Real Q Plus 2x Master Mix Green from Amplicon (Denmark), and the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit from Applied Biosystems (USA).

Cell line culture

The J774.1 murine macrophage cell line (Pasteur Institute, Tehran) was cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U/ml penicillin/100 µg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Cells with ≥ 90% viability, as determined by trypan blue exclusion, were used for experiments.

Determining the cytotoxic effects of piperine

The MTT assay was used to determine the possible cytotoxic effect of piperine on J774.1 cells [12]. J774.1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (7.5 × 10³ cells/well) and treated for 24 h with piperine (0.1–100 µg/ml) or cisplatin (50 µg/ml; positive control), with untreated cells as a negative control. After treatment, 10 µl of MTT (5 mg/ml) was added and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The formazan crystals were then dissolved in 150 µl DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm (reference 630 nm) using a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell viability was calculated as a percentage of the untreated control.

Culture conditions

J774.1 cells were cultured in complete RPMI medium at a density of 106 cells/well in a 24-well plate and stimulated with LPS (1 µg/ml) to induce inflammatory responses. The stimulated cells were immediately treated with piperine (1, 10, 20 µg/ml), L-NAME (1 mM/ml), and dexamethasone (a steroid/glucocorticoid, anti-inflammatory drug, 0.1 µg/ml) and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity for 24 h. Untreated cells and LPS-treated cells served as the negative and positive controls, respectively. After treatment, supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis for NO and cytokine measurements, while the cells were used for gene expression analysis via real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Nitrite assay

Nitrite, a stable by-product of nitric oxide (NO), was measured in cell supernatants using the Griess colorimetric method [20]. Briefly, 80 µl of the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of Griess reagent and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Afterward, the optical density (OD) of the reaction product was measured at 570 nm. The OD values of test samples were compared to a sodium nitrite standard curve to calculate nitrite concentration.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the levels of TNF-ɑ, IFN-γ, and IL-1β cytokines in cell supernatants were measured using mouse ELISA kits (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) with a sensitivity of 8 pg/ml.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and real-time polymerase chain reaction

RNA was extracted from J774.1 cells using a total RNA extraction kit (Pars Tous, Iran) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a UV spectrophotometer (Picodrop, Hinxton, United Kingdom), while its integrity was evaluated by electrophoresis of RNA samples on a 1.2% agarose gel. cDNA synthesis was performed using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, ABI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression analysis was conducted using the 7500 Real-Time PCR System (ABI) in a total reaction volume of 20 µl. Each reaction consisted of 2 µl cDNA, 10 picomoles of forward (0.8 µl) and reverse (0.8 µl) primers, 10 µl of Real Q Plus 2x Master Mix Green, and 6.4 µl nuclease-free H2O. Primer sequences and their characteristics are shown in Table 1. The PCR was performed as follows: a cycle of 95 °C for 15 min to activate the hot-start DNA polymerase enzyme, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (5 s at 95 °C), annealing (15 s at temperatures mentioned in Table 1), and extension (30 s at 72 °C). Melt curves were analyzed to verify amplification specificity. Relative gene expression of the target genes (relative fold change or RFC) was determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Samples were normalized to the expression levels of β-actin and were compared to the results of unstimulated J774.1 cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0) software for Windows. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate. A comparison between groups was done using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and if the results were significant, a Tukey post-test was used. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

The possible cytotoxic effects of piperine on macrophages were evaluated using the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 1, piperine at concentrations ≥ 25 µg/ml significantly reduced J774 cell viability by %71. According to these results, three non-toxic concentrations of piperine (1, 10, and 20 µg/ml) were selected for subsequent experiments.

Effects of piperine on M1 macrophage markers

The potential effects of piperine on M1 macrophage polarization were assessed by measuring NO release and iNOS expression under various treatments. As shown in Fig. 2, LPS stimulationinduced a significant increase in NO release, from 9.96 micromole per milliliter (µmol/mL) in untreated cells to 47.7 ± 3.2 µmol/mL (Fig. 2A), and up-regulated iNOS gene expression by 5.7 ± 0.9-fold (Fig. 2B). L-NAME and dexamethasone treatment decreased NO levels to approximately 18 µmol/mL (0.37 fold) and iNOS gene level to 0.9 and 0.6 fold, respectively. Piperine reduced NO levels in a dose-dependent manner, from 36.5 ± 3.4 µmol/mL at 1 µg/ml to 19.8 ± 3.3 µmol/mL at 20 µg/ml (0.52-fold decrease), and decreased iNOS expression from 2.84 ± 0.5 relative fold change (RFC) to 0.62 ± 0.1 RFC (0.28-fold). Notably, 20 µg/ml piperine showed comparable inhibitory effects to dexamethasone on both NO production and iNOS expression.

The effects of piperine on the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) in various macrophage treatment conditions were evaluated. We observed increased expression of all cytokines after cells were exposed to LPS (Fig. 3). Dexamethasone treatment significantly suppressed cytokine production. It notably reduced IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ protein levels (0.5, 0.69, and 0.46 fold, respectively) and gene levels (0.0084, 0.24, and 0.18 fold, respectively). Piperine at a concentration of 20 µg/ml decreased IL-1β from 481.1 ± 1.3 pg/ml in LPS-treated cells to 234.2 ± 3.5 pg/ml (0.48 fold, p < 0.001, Fig. 3A), TNF-α from 2133 ± 14.4 pg/ml to 1148 ± 4.1 pg/ml (0.54 fold, p < 0.01, Fig. 3B), and IFN-γ from 43.1 ± 3.3 pg/ml to 27.2 ± 1.5 pg/ml (0.63 fold, p < 0.1, Fig. 3C). The same dose also significantly suppressed IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ gene expression levels to 17 ± 8.3 (0.1 fold, P < 0.001, Fig. 3D), 1.33 ± 0.53 (0.16 fold, p < 0.01, Fig. 3E), and 25.4 ± 3.7 RFCs (0.12 fold, p < 0.01, Fig. 3F), respectively. Suppressing these cytokines is critical, as their persistent production directly drives tissue damage and symptoms in diseases like septic shock, inflammatory bowel disease, and rheumatoid arthritis.

Effects of piperine on M2 macrophage markers

The potential effects of piperine on M2 macrophage markers were determined by measuring gene expression of IDO enzyme and the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-4. As shown in Fig. 4, dexamethasone did not alter IDO1 or IL-10 expression levels but significantly suppressed IL-4 from 79.4 ± 3.8 in LPS-treated cells to 4.8 ± 4.6 RFC. Piperine at 1 µg/ml increased IDO1 gene expression level from 8.4 ± 0.17 in LPS-treated cells to 28.3 ± 3.7 RFC (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A). The induction of IDO1, a critical enzyme for immune tolerance, suggests that piperine may not only suppress inflammation but also actively promote an immunoregulatory environment, which has implications for treating autoimmune conditions. IL-4 gene expression, however, was attenuated by piperine in a dose-related fashion; the 20 µg/ml concentration produced the strongest suppression (4 ± 3.1 RFC), an efficacy comparable to dexamethasone.

Discussion

Macrophages are essential components of innate immunity. They are generally polarized into two main subsets (M1 and M2) with distinct extreme phenotypes and opposing functions [5]. M1 cells produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and contribute to tissue damage, whereas M2 cells secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines and play a crucial role in tissue repair and wound healing [5]. The functional heterogeneity of M2 macrophages underscores their central role in coordinating the resolution of inflammation and tissue repair [4–6]. Harnessing the specific therapeutic potential of these subsets, while carefully avoiding their pathological dysregulation in fibrosis, allergy, and cancer, represents a promising frontier in immunomodulatory medicine. Macrophage polarization exerts profound effects on various physiological and pathological processes [5, 21]. The reversibility of macrophage polarization holds significant therapeutic value in several diseases [21]. Therefore, balancing the M1/M2 ratio using specific agents and modulating cytokines in the context of inflammation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy [22, 23].

In this study, the possible effects of piperine on the polarization of macrophages were evaluated in vitro, and compared with those of the standard anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid, dexamethasone. We found that piperine reduces M1 markers and increases IDO. We demonstrated that piperine dose-dependently inhibited iNOS gene expression and NO generation in activated macrophages. In this regard, piperine at 20 µg/ml exhibited effects similar to those observed with 0.1 µg/ml of dexamethasone (Fig. 2). The piperine concentrations employed in our experiments (≤ 20 µg/mL) were selected based on existing safety profiles, which confirm that such doses are non-cytotoxic to a range of cell lines, notably macrophages [24–26]. Piperine also suppressed the expression level of IL-1β and TNF-ɑ genes and their corresponding protein levels (Fig. 3A). These data align with previous studies in which piperine significantly decreased TNF-ɑ and NO levels in the serum of LPS- or Concanavalin-A (Con-A)- treated mice, as well as in the culture supernatant of LPS- or Con-A-treated peritoneal macrophages [27]. Bae et al. also demonstrated that piperine diminishes LPS-induced endotoxin shock, and reduces TNF-α generation in mice, as well as TNF-α synthesis in peritoneal macrophages [28]. Ying et al. showed that pre-incubation of murine RAW 264.7 macrophages with piperine at 10, 50, or 100 µg/ml before LPS stimulation suppressed the expression of TNF-ɑ and iNOS genes and inhibited NO production [29]. Another study by this group revealed that treating human osteoarthritis chondrocytes with piperine at the same concentrations before IL-1β stimulation decreased their ability to express matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)−3, MMP-13, and iNOS genes, as well as decreased NO levels [30]. These studies further revealed that piperine inhibits the degradation of inhibitor-κB (IκB), thereby preventing nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) [29, 30]. Additionally, we observed that piperine treatment of macrophages led to a significant reduction in IFN-γ protein levels and gene expression. This finding supports the inhibitory effects of piperine on M1 macrophages and aligns with Doucette et al.’s study, in which piperine dose-dependently suppressed the proliferation of murine T lymphocytes and IFN-γ production [31]. The inhibitory effects of piperine on the proliferation and IFN-γ induction in phytohemagglutinin-stimulated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells have also been demonstrated [32]. In contrast to the dose-dependent suppression observed for TNF-α and IFN-γ, the protein expression of IL-1β was not significantly reduced across the tested concentrations of piperine. The distinct two-step activation of IL-1β—requiring inflammasome-mediated cleavage—provides a plausible explanation. Our data indicate piperine suppresses the initial transcriptional priming but not the secondary inflammasome activation. Other contributing factors could include IL-1β protein stability, cell-type-specific effects, or alternative secretion pathways.

Concerning the effects of piperine on M2 macrophage markers, we showed that piperine at 1 µg/ml more effectively polarizes macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, as indicated by increased expression of the IDO1 gene (Fig. 4A). Catabolic products derived from IDO actively exert immunosuppressive functions, primarily by inducing regulatory T cell proliferation and regulating effector T cell anergy [33]. At the same concentration, piperine increased the expression of the IL-10 gene, another M2 macrophage-associated marker, although the change was not statistically significant. Our findings demonstrate a dose-dependent inhibition of IL-4, consistent with the effect observed in murine T lymphocytes [31]. Calculation of M1/M2 polarization ratios (e.g., TNF-α/IL-10) further supported a predominant suppression of the M1 phenotype, without indicating a significant shift towards a specific M2 subset. Our findings that piperine potently inhibits M1 cytokines while simultaneously inducing the immunoregulatory enzyme IDO have important pathophysiological implications. This dual action suggests that piperine could be more effective than simple anti-inflammatories in pathologies driven by a loss of immune tolerance, such as in autoimmune disease.

Limitations

This study is limited by the lack of IDO1 protein and activity measurements, leaving its functional upregulation unconfirmed. Caution is also needed in extrapolating the in vitro results, given the in vivo microenvironment and piperine’s pharmacokinetics. Future in vivo and clinical studies are warranted.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that piperine has biphasic, dose-dependent effects on LPS-stimulated macrophages. Lower concentrations induce an anti-inflammatory phenotype (e.g., reduced cytokines and increased IDO), while higher doses cause broad suppression of macrophage activation, including iNOS/NO and proinflammatory gene expression.
