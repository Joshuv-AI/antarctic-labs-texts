Abstract
. In our previous paper [ PPST3 ] , we gave a complete classification of the unitary highest weight modules for the universal covers of the Lie groups S ​ p ​ ( 2 ​ n , ℝ ) , S ​ O ∗ ​ ( 2 ​ n ) Sp(2n,\mathbb{R}),SO^{*}(2n) and S ​ U ​ ( p , q ) SU(p,q) , using the Dirac inequality and the so called PRV product. In this paper, we complete the classification of the unitary highest weight modules for the remaining cases; i.e., universal covers of the Lie groups S ​ O e ​ ( 2 , n ) SO_{e}(2,n) , E 6 ​ ( − 14 ) E_{6(-14)} and E 7 ​ ( − 25 ) E_{7(-25)} . We also describe unitary highest weight modules with given infinitesimal characters.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
denote the universal cover of one of the Lie groups
SO_{e}(2,n)
,
E_{6(-14)}
and
E_{7(-25)}
, and let
K
denote the subgroup of elements of
G
fixed by a fixed Cartan involution of
G
.
In all these cases
G
is of Hermitian type, so there exist unitary highest weight Harish-Chandra modules, i.e., unitary
Harish-Chandra modules generated by a highest weight vector that is annihilated by the action of all positive root spaces in the
complexified Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
(
[
HC1
]
,
[
HC2
]
). Let
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{k}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{0}
be the Cartan decomposition of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}

of
G
corresponding to the Cartan involution. As usual, we drop the subscript 0 to denote the complexifications. Since in our cases
(G,K)
is Hermitian, the
K
-module
\mathfrak{p}
decomposes into two irreducible submodules,
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{p}^{-}
, and each of
\mathfrak{p}^{\pm}
is an abelian subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{k}
have equal rank, and we choose a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathfrak{g}
contained in
\mathfrak{k}
. We fix compatible choices of positive roots for
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{t})
and
(\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{t})
,
\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}^{+}\supset\Delta_{\mathfrak{k}}^{+}
, such that
\mathfrak{p}^{+}
is contained in the Borel subalgebra defined by
\Delta_{\mathfrak{g}}^{+}
. Let
W
and
W_{\mathfrak{k}}
denote the Weyl groups of
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{k}
respectively.

If the highest weight module is in addition a
(\mathfrak{g},K)
–module, then its highest weight must be the highest weight of a
K
–type, hence it is dominant and integral for
\mathfrak{k}
. For each highest weight
\lambda
, there is a unique irreducible highest weight module with highest weight
\lambda
and it can be constructed as the unique irreducible quotient
L(\lambda)
of the generalized Verma module
N(\lambda)
with highest weight
\lambda
(see
[
PPST3
, Section 1]
for more details). We will consider only real weights (i.e., contained in the real span of roots) since otherwise, the associated highest-weight module can not be unitary. In
[
PPST3
]
we determined for which
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant integral and real weight
\lambda
the module
L(\lambda)
is unitary in cases when
G
is the universal cover of the Lie groups
Sp(2n,\mathbb{R}),SO^{*}(2n)
or
SU(p,q)
. This result was already proved in
[
EHW
]
and independently in
[
J
]
, but our approach was more elementary. In this paper, we will do the same in cases when
G
is the universal cover of the Lie groups
SO(2,n)
,
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
. In
[
PPST3
]
the essential tool was the Dirac inequality of
[
P2
]
. We will explain it briefly and refer the reader to a more detailed introduction of
[
PPST3
]
.

Let
B
be the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
and let
C(\mathfrak{p})
be the Clifford algebra of
\mathfrak{p}
with respect to
B
.
Let
b_{i}
be any basis of
\mathfrak{p}
and let
d_{i}
be the dual basis with respect to
B
. The Dirac operator (defined in
[
P1
]
) is defined as

D=\sum_{i}b_{i}\otimes d_{i}\qquad\in U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes C(\mathfrak{p}).

The square of
D
is the spin Laplacean (Parthasarathy
[
P1
]
):

(1.1)

D^{2}=-(\operatorname{Cas}_{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes 1+\|\rho\|^{2})+(\operatorname{Cas}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\Delta}}+\|\rho_{\mathfrak{k}}\|^{2}),

where
\operatorname{Cas}_{\mathfrak{g}}
,
\operatorname{Cas}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\Delta}}
are the Casimir elements of
U(\mathfrak{g})
,
U(\mathfrak{k}_{\Delta})
, where

\mathfrak{k}_{\Delta}
is the diagonal copy of
\mathfrak{k}
in
U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes C(\mathfrak{p})
defined by

\mathfrak{k}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}\hookrightarrow U(\mathfrak{g})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{k}\to\mathfrak{so}(\mathfrak{p})\hookrightarrow C(\mathfrak{p}),

and
\rho
and
\rho_{\mathfrak{k}}
are the half sums of positive roots for
\mathfrak{g}
respectively
\mathfrak{k}
.

If
M
is a
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module, then
D
and
D^{2}
act on
M\otimes S
, where
S
is the spin module for
C(\mathfrak{p})
.
If
M
is a unitary
(\mathfrak{g},K)
-module, we can tensor the inner product on
M
with the inner product on
S
(See
[
HP2
, 2.3.9]
for more details about the inner product on the spin module) to obtain an inner product on
M\otimes S
, with the property that
D
is self adjoint with respect to this inner product. It follows that
D^{2}\geq 0
; this is Parthasarathy’s Dirac inequality mentioned above.

Dirac inequality can be rewritten in more concrete terms using the formula (
1.1
) for
D^{2}
and the relation between Casimir actions and infinitesimal characters. As in the introduction of
[
PPST3
]
it can be easily shown that if
L(\lambda)
is unitary, then the inequality

(1.2)

\|\mu+\rho\|\geq\|\lambda+\rho\|,

must hold for any
K
-type
\mu
of
L(\lambda)
.

Proposition 1.3
.

[
EHW
, Proposition 3.9.]
With the notation as above,
L(\lambda)
is unitary if and only if the inequality (
1.2
) holds strictly for any
K
-type
\mu\neq\lambda
of
L(\lambda)
.

The problem with this approach is that it is difficult to determine all the
K
–types of
L(\lambda)
. Luckily, it is enough to consider the so-called PRV components of the tensor products of the irreducible finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{k}
-module
F_{\lambda}
with highest weight
\lambda
and each
K
–type of the module
S(\mathfrak{p}^{-})
, the so-called Schmid modules, which are very well known (see
[
S
]
,
[
PPST3
]
). More precisely, the following corollary holds (see
[
PPST3
, Corollary 2.5]
for the proof):

Corollary 1.4
.

(1) Let
s_{0}
be a Schmid module
such that for any Schmid module
s
of strictly lower level than
s_{0}
,
the strict Dirac inequality

(1.5)

\|(\lambda-s)^{+}+\rho\|^{2}>\|\lambda+\rho\|^{2}

holds. (Here
(\lambda-s)^{+}
denotes the unique
W_{\mathfrak{k}}
–conjugate of
\lambda-s
).
Furthermore, suppose that

\|(\lambda-s_{0})^{+}+\rho\|^{2}<\|\lambda+\rho\|^{2}.

Then
L(\lambda)
is not unitary.

(2) If (
1.5
)
holds for all Schmid modules
s
, then
N(\lambda)
is irreducible and unitary.

In
[
PPST3
]
, we constructed all unitary highest weight modules in the discrete part of the classification via the Segal-Shale-Weil representation and its decompositions, tensor products and PRV products (see
[
PPST3
, Definition 2.10.]
). Unfortunately, in exceptional cases, we cannot construct unitary highest-weight modules in the discrete part via the PRV products. However, we give a shorter proof of
[
EHW
, Proposition 12.6]
in Section
6
using the theta correspondence. For the proof that all highest weight modules in the discrete part of the classification for the case
E_{7}
are unitary see
[
EHW
, Pages 139 and 140]
). The situation is similar to the classical case in the sense that for
E_{7}
the proof of
[
EHW
]
uses the decomposition of the second tensor power of scalar unitarizable module from the discrete part.

In the second part of the paper, we describe unitary highest-weight modules with given infinitesimal characters.

2.
Classification for
\mathfrak{so}(2,2n-2),n\geq 3

In this case
\mathfrak{g}
is
\mathfrak{so}(2n,\mathbb{C})
and
\mathfrak{k}
is
\mathfrak{so}(2n-2,\mathbb{C})
plus a one-dimensional center. We use standard coordinates and the standard positive root system. The positive compact roots are

\epsilon_{i}\pm\epsilon_{j},\quad 2\leq i<j\leq n

and the positive noncompact roots are

\epsilon_{1}\pm\epsilon_{j},\quad 2\leq j\leq n.

So the half sum of all positive roots is

\rho=(n-1,\ldots,0)

and a weight
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
is
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant if and only if

\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-1}\geq|\lambda_{n}|.

Integrality with respect to
\mathfrak{k}
amounts to
\lambda_{i}\pm\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
2\leq i,j\leq n.

The highest (noncompact) root is
\beta=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}.
There are just two basic Schmid representations, one with highest weight
\beta
and the other one with highest weight
\beta_{2}=2\epsilon_{1}.

The basic Dirac inequality for a PRV component with a Schmid module

s

is

(2.1)

\|(\lambda-s)^{+}+\rho\|^{2}\geq\|\lambda+\rho\|^{2}

This is equivalent to

(2.2)

2\langle\gamma\,|\,\lambda+\rho\rangle\leq\|\gamma\|^{2}

where
\gamma
is defined by
(\lambda-s)^{+}=\lambda-\gamma.

Since every root has norm squared equal to 2, this is further equivalent to

(2.3)

\langle\gamma\,|\,\lambda+\rho\rangle\leq 1.

The Weyl group of
\mathfrak{k}
acts on weights by permuting coordinates
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and changing an even number of their signs. It is because of these sign changes and spinor representations that we have to distinguish several cases. The following lemma was proved in
[
PPST1
, Lemma 3.1.]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The basic Dirac inequalitiy for
s=\beta
is given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 0\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3/2-n\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},1/2,\ldots,\pm 1/2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2+p-2n\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle\text{ where }1\leq\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{p}>\lambda_{p+1}\text{ and }2\leq p\leq n.

We will refer to the first case as the
scalar
case, the second case is the
spinor
case and the remaining one is the
general
case.
The following theorem gives a complete classification of unitary highest weight modules for
SO(2,2n-2)
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Non-scalar modules are unitarizabile if and only if (
2.5
) holds. If the inequality is satisfied strictly, the modules are irreducible Verma modules. In the scalar case, unitarizable highest weight modules are the trivial module, the Wallach module
(2-n,0,\ldots,0)
and irreducible Verma modules
(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0),\lambda_{1}<2-n.

Proof.

In view of Corollary
1.4
, the theorem
follows from
[
PPST1
, Theorems 3.5, 3.6 and 3.7]
.
Namely, the only module which is not in the continuous part of its line, and is not at the end of the continuous part, is the trivial module, which is of course unitary.
∎

3.
Classification for
\mathfrak{so}(2,2n-1),\,n\geq 2

In this case
\mathfrak{g}
is
\mathfrak{so}(2n+1,\mathbb{C})
and
\mathfrak{k}
is
\mathfrak{so}(2n-1,\mathbb{C})
plus a one-dimensional center. We use standard coordinates and standard positive root system. The positive compact roots are

\{\epsilon_{i}\pm\epsilon_{j}\,|\,2\leq i<j\leq n\}\cup\{\epsilon_{i}\,|\,2\leq i\leq n\}

and the positive noncompact roots are

\{\epsilon_{1}\pm\epsilon_{j}\,|\,2\leq j\leq n\}\cup\{\epsilon_{1}\}

So the half sum of all positive roots is

\rho=(n-\frac{1}{2},n-\frac{3}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2})

and a weight
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
is
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant if and only if

\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\cdots\lambda_{n}\geq 0.

Integrality with respect to
\mathfrak{k}
amounts to
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and and
2\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
2\leq i,j\leq n.

The highest (noncompact) root is
\beta=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}.
There are just two basic Schmid representations and the second one has the highest weight
\beta_{2}=2\epsilon_{1}.

The basic Dirac inequality for a PRV component with a Schmid module is

(3.1)

\|(\lambda-s)^{+}+\rho\|^{2}\geq\|\lambda+\rho\|^{2}

This is equivalent to

(3.2)

2\langle\gamma\,|\,\lambda+\rho\rangle\leq\|\gamma\|^{2}

where
\gamma
is defined by
(\lambda-s)^{+}=\lambda-\gamma.

The Weyl group of
\mathfrak{k}
acts on weights by permuting coordinates
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and changing signs on them. The following lemma was proved in
[
PPST1
, Lemma 3.3.]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The basic Dirac inequalitiy for
s=\beta
is given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 1-n

\displaystyle\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},1/2,\ldots,1/2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+p-2n

\displaystyle\text{ for }\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle\text{ where }1\leq\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{p}>\lambda_{p+1}\text{ and }2\leq p\leq n.

The following theorem follows from
[
PPST1
, Theorems 3.8 and 3.9]
and from Corollary
1.4
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Non-scalar modules are unitarizabile if and only if (
3.4
) holds. If the inequality is satisfied strictly, the modules are irreducible Verma modules. In the scalar case, unitarizable highest weight modules are the trivial module, the Wallach module
(3/2-n,0,\ldots,0)
and irreducible Verma modules
(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0),\lambda_{1}<3/2-n.

The above theorem gives a complete classification of unitary highest weight modules for
SO(2,2n-1)
. Namely, the only module which is not in the continuous part of its line, and is not at the end of the continuous part, is the trivial module, which is of course unitary.

4.
Classification for
\mathfrak{e}_{6(-14)}

We consider roots as vectors in a six-dimensional subspace of an eight-dimensional space (see
[
Kn
]
), precisely we will consider them as
8
–tuples which have the same sixth and seventh coordinate, and the eight coordinate is equal to minus the sixth coordinate. The positive compact roots are

\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j},\quad 5\geq i>j.

and the positive noncompact roots are

\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{8}-\varepsilon_{7}-\varepsilon_{6}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}(-1)^{n(i)}\varepsilon_{i}\right)\text{with }\sum_{i=1}^{5}n(i)\text{ even}.

In this case

\rho=(0,1,2,3,4,-4,-4,4).

The set of simple roots is

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4},\alpha_{5},\alpha_{6}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{8}-\varepsilon_{7}-\varepsilon_{6}-\varepsilon_{5}-\varepsilon_{4}-\varepsilon_{3}-\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{1}\right),\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{3}-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{4}-\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{5}-\varepsilon_{4}\right\}.

The numbering of simple roots in the Dynkin diagram is given by

\binom{13456}{2}.

The strongly orthogonal non-compact positive roots are (see
[
EJ
]
)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\binom{12321}{2}=\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{3}+3\alpha_{4}+2\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}+2\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1,1,1,1,1,-1,-1,1\right),

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{11111}{0}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-1,-1,-1,-1,1,-1,-1,1\right).

The highest weight
(\mathfrak{g},K)
–modules have highest weights of the form

\displaystyle\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4},\lambda_{5},\lambda_{6},\lambda_{6},-\lambda_{6}),\quad

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}|\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\lambda_{4}\leq\lambda_{5},

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},\ 2\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad i,j\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}

The basic Schmid
\mathfrak{k}
–modules in
S({\mathfrak{p}}^{-})
have lowest weights
-s_{i}
,
i=1,2
, where

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1,1,1,1,1,-1,-1,1\right),

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=\left(0,0,0,0,1,-1,-1,1\right).

The basic necessary condition for unitarity is the Dirac inequality

||(\lambda-s_{1})^{+}+\rho||^{2}\geq||\lambda+\rho||^{2}.

The following theorem follows from
[
PPST2
, Theorems 2.1, 2.2, and 2.3]
and from Corollary
1.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\lambda=(0,0,0,0,0,\lambda_{6},\lambda_{6},-\lambda_{6})
, then
L(\lambda)
is unitarizable if and only if
\lambda_{6}\geq 2
or
\lambda_{6}=0
(in this case
L(\lambda)
is trivial). If
\lambda_{6}>2
, then
L(\lambda)=N(\lambda)
.

If
\lambda=(0,0,0,0,\lambda_{5},\lambda_{6},\lambda_{6},-\lambda_{6})
, where
\lambda_{5}\neq 0
, then
L(\lambda)
is unitarizable if
3\lambda_{6}-\lambda_{5}\geq 14
and
L(\lambda)
is not unitarizable if
3\lambda_{6}-\lambda_{5}\in\langle-\infty,14\rangle\setminus\{8\}
. If
3\lambda_{6}-\lambda_{5}>14
, then
L(\lambda)=N(\lambda)
.

If
\lambda
is such that
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})\neq(0,0,0,0)
, then
L(\lambda)
is unitarizable if and only if the basic Dirac inequality

||(\lambda-s_{1})^{+}+\rho||^{2}\geq||\lambda+\rho||^{2}

holds. If the strict basic Dirac inequality holds, then
L(\lambda)=N(\lambda)
.

The only modules that are not in the continuous part of their lines, and are not at the end of the continuous part, are the trivial module, which
is of course unitary, and a series of modules of the form
L(0,0,0,0,3\lambda_{6}-8,\lambda_{6},\lambda_{6},-\lambda_{6})
, such that
3\lambda_{6}-8
is a positive integer. These modules are also unitary.
This follows from
[
EHW
, Corollary 12.6.]
where the authors verified (
1.2
) directly on all
K
-types. We will give a shorter proof in Section
6
.

5.
Classification for
\mathfrak{e}_{7(-25)}

We consider roots as vectors in a seven-dimensional subspace of an eight-dimensional space (see
[
Kn
]
), precisely we will consider them as
8
–tuples which have the eighth coordinate equal to minus the seventh coordinate. The positive compact roots are

\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j},\quad 5\geq i>j,\quad\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{8}-\varepsilon_{7}-\varepsilon_{6}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}(-1)^{n(i)}\varepsilon_{i}\right)\text{ with }\sum_{i=1}^{5}n(i)\text{ even}.

\displaystyle\Pi

\binom{134567}{2}.

EJ

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

7.1.
The case of

Recall that
\rho=(n-1,n-2,\dots,0)

-dominant weights

\mathfrak{k}

\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}

By definition, the parameter of the irreducible highest weight
(\mathfrak{g},K)
–module

is
\Lambda=\lambda+\rho

. We will denote by

–dominant representative of the infinitesimal character

\mathfrak{k}

\Lambda_{2}>\Lambda_{3}>\ldots>\Lambda_{n-1}>|\Lambda_{n}|.

Recall that Theorem
2.6
implies
that

is unitary if and only if
\lambda
is of the form

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0),\quad

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=0\text{ or }\lambda_{1}\leq 2-n

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{1},\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\pm\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{3}{2}-n

In terms of the corresponding parameter

\Lambda

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{1},n-2,\ldots,1,0),\quad

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}=n-1\text{ or }\Lambda_{1}\leq 1

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}

or

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}),\quad

\displaystyle n-1\leq\Lambda_{2},\quad\Lambda_{i+1}=\Lambda_{i}-1,\quad i\in\{2,\ldots,p-1\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p+1}\leq\Lambda_{p}-2,\quad\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}\leq p-1.

We want to fix

and describe a criterion for a parameter

\displaystyle\text{either }\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{or}\quad\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z};

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{2}}>\dots>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}}>|\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}|.

Case 1.

(3)

Suppose that

, and the hat denotes that the coordinate is omitted. All of these

(2) The first two conjugates of

The next conjugates of

\displaystyle\Lambda=(n-i+\frac{1}{2}\,|\,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1},n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\widehat{n-i+\frac{1}{2}},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2})\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}=(-(n-i+\frac{1}{2})\,|\,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1},n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\widehat{n-i+\frac{1}{2}},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\mp\frac{1}{2}),

(3) Note first that the condition (

. If

, but since by (

Finally, suppose that

is as in (

). Then by the same argument as above, all parameters

7.2.
The case of
\mathfrak{so}(2,2n-1),\,n\geq 2
.

Recall that
\rho=(n-\frac{1}{2},n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2})
and that all
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant weights
\lambda
are of the form

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}),

where
\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
. Since we also assume that
\lambda
is
\mathfrak{k}
–integral, we have
\lambda_{i}\pm\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
2\leq i,j\leq n
, and
2\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, or equivalently
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
or
\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
.

By definition, the parameter of the irreducible highest weight
(\mathfrak{g},K)
–module
L(\lambda)
is
\Lambda=\lambda+\rho
. It is a particular representative of the infinitesimal character of
L(\lambda)
. We will denote by
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
the
\mathfrak{g}
–dominant representative of the infinitesimal character
\Lambda
, i.e., we have

\Lambda_{1}^{\operatorname{dom}}\geq\Lambda_{2}^{\operatorname{dom}}\geq\ldots\geq\Lambda_{n}^{\operatorname{dom}}\geq 0.

All parameters
\Lambda
corresponding to highest weight
(\mathfrak{g},K)
–modules must be dominant regular for
\mathfrak{k}
, i.e

\Lambda_{2}>\Lambda_{3}>\ldots>\Lambda_{n-1}>\Lambda_{n}>0.

Recall that Theorem
3.5
implies
that
L(\lambda)
is unitary if and only if
\lambda
is of the form

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},0,\ldots,0),\quad

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=0\text{ or }\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{3}{2}-n

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{1},\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq 1-n

or

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}),\quad 1\leq\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{p}>\lambda_{p+1},\quad\,\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\leq 1+p-2n,

for some
p\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
.

In terms of the corresponding parameter
\Lambda=\lambda+\rho
,
L(\lambda)
is unitary if and only if
\Lambda
is of the form

\displaystyle\left(\Lambda_{1},n-\frac{3}{2},\ldots,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}=n-\frac{1}{2}\text{ or }\Lambda_{1}\leq 1

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{1},n-1,\ldots,2,1),\quad

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}

or

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}),\quad

\displaystyle n-\frac{1}{2}\leq\Lambda_{2},\quad\Lambda_{i+1}=\Lambda_{i}-1,\quad i\in\{2,\ldots,p-1\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p+1}\leq\Lambda_{p}-2,\quad\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}\leq p-1.

for some
p\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
.

In order to have any
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant regular integral Weyl group conjugates,
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
must contain at least
n-1
coordinates,
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
, so that

(7.12)

\displaystyle\text{either }\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{or}\quad\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z};

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{2}}>\dots>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}}>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}>0.

There are now two cases:

Case 1.

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
contains
n-1
coordinates
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
satisfying (
7.12
), and another coordinate
x
(which must be
\geq 0
), so that
x
is either not congruent to
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
modulo
\mathbb{Z}
, or is equal to one of
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}},\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
or

.

Case 2.

The coordinates of
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
are either all in
\mathbb{Z}
or all in
\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
, and they satisfy

(7.13)

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1}>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{2}>\dots>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{n-1}>\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{n}>0.

We start by examining Case 1.
The assumption implies that the only
\mathfrak{k}
-dominant regular integral conjugates of
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
are

(7.14)

\Lambda=(x\,|\,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}},\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}})\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\Lambda}=(-x\,|\,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}},\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}).

The answer to the unitarity question for
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
is the following result.

Theorem 7.15
.

Assume
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
is in Case 1 and let
\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda}
be the two possible conjugates of
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
described in (
7.14
).
Then

(1)

Suppose that

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}=n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2},

so that
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
are both in the scalar case.
Then
\Lambda
is unitary if and only if
x\leq 1
, while
\tilde{\Lambda}
is always unitary.

(2)

Suppose that

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}=n-1,\dots,2,1,

so
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
are both in the spinor case. Then
\Lambda
is unitary if and only if
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, while
\tilde{\Lambda}
is always unitary.

(3)

Suppose that

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
are neither
n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2}
nor
n-1,\dots,2,1
, so that
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
are both in the general case.
Let

be the integers
corresponding to
\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda}
(or rather to
\lambda=\Lambda-\rho
,
\tilde{\lambda}=\tilde{\Lambda}-\rho
) as in Theorem
3.5
. Then
\Lambda
is never unitary.

If
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
then
\tilde{\Lambda}
is not unitary.

If
x>\frac{1}{2}
, then the unitarity of
\tilde{\Lambda}
is equivalent to
-x+\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq\tilde{p}-1
.

Proof.

(1)
By the unitarity criterion of Theorem
3.5
,
\Lambda
is unitary if and only
x\leq 1
, so that
0\leq x\leq 1
(
x
can not be
n-\frac{1}{2}
since we are assuming
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
is in Case 1). On the other hand, unitarity of
\tilde{\Lambda}
is equivalent to
-x\leq 1
, which is automatic since
x\geq 0
. This proves (1).

(2)
Note first that
x
must be
\geq 0
. By the unitarity criterion of Theorem
3.5
,
\Lambda
is unitary if and only if
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
; so
0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
On the other hand, unitarity of
\tilde{\Lambda}
is equivalent to
-x\leq\frac{1}{2}
, which is automatic since
x\geq 0
.

(3) The assumptions imply that

(7.16)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\geq n\quad\text{ if }\ \Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\geq n-\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{ if }\ \Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}.

Since
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
are both in the general case, the unitarity conditions are
x+\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq p-1
respectively
-x+\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq\tilde{p}-1
. This, together with
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\geq n-\frac{1}{2}
(which follows from (
7.16
)), and with

, implies that

\Lambda
is never unitary since
x\geq 0
, and
\tilde{\Lambda}
is not unitary if
x<\frac{1}{2}
.

If
x=\frac{1}{2}
, then the unitarity of

\tilde{\Lambda}
is equivalent to

-\frac{1}{2}+\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq\tilde{p}-1
by Theorem
3.5
, i.e.
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq\tilde{p}-\frac{1}{2}
.
If
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}},\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
are in
\mathbb{Z}
, then
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\geq n
, so
\tilde{\Lambda}
is not unitary.
If
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\dots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-2}},\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}
are in

, then it follows from (
7.16
) that
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\geq n-\frac{1}{2}
. So, if
\tilde{\Lambda}
is unitary, then
\tilde{p}=n
and
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}=n-\frac{1}{2}
, but in this case we have

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{n-1}}=n-\frac{1}{2},\ldots,\frac{5}{2},\frac{3}{2},\,x=\frac{1}{2}

and this is not Case 1. Therefore, if

, then
\tilde{\Lambda}
is not unitary.

If
x>\frac{1}{2}
, then the unitarity of
\tilde{\Lambda}
is equivalent to
-x+\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{i_{1}}\leq\tilde{p}-1
by Theorem
3.5
.

∎

We now handle the remaining case, Case 2. The result is

Theorem 7.17
.

Let
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}

(1)

Suppose that

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1}\geq n-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle(n-\frac{1}{2}\,|\,,n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2}),\quad\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle(-n+i-\frac{1}{2}\,|\,n-\frac{1}{2},\dots,\widehat{n-i+\frac{1}{2}},\dots,\frac{1}{2}),\quad i=1,\dots,n

(-\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1}\,|\,n-\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2}).

(2)

Suppose that

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}=(\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1},n-1,\dots,2,1),

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1}\geq n
. If

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}

(-\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}_{1}\,|\,n-1,\ldots,2,1).

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}=(n,n-1,\dots,2,1)

\Lambda^{\operatorname{dom}}
are

(-n+i\,|\,n,n-1,\dots,\widehat{n-i},\dots,2,1),\quad i=0,1,\dots,n-1.

(3)
