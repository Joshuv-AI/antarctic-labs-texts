Exploring healthcare professionals’ experiences and perceptions of self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity in breast cancer survivors: a qualitative study
Authors: Xianliang Liu, Mubei Yang, Yuhua Ma, Qiaohong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAdvancements in cancer treatments have significantly improved survival rates for breast cancer (BC) patients; however, these treatments are often accompanied by severe adverse effects, such as cardiotoxicity. Effective patient-centred self-management plays a critical role in the early detection of cardiotoxic symptoms and in enhancing BC survivors’ overall quality of life. To support this process, education and ongoing support from healthcare professionals is essential. This study aimed to explore healthcare professionals’ experiences and perspectives in facilitating BC survivors’ self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity.MethodsThis study employed a fundamental qualitative descriptive approach. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with healthcare professionals from a BC department in Guangzhou, China. Audio recordings were transcribed and analysed using NVivo 12 software. Thematic analysis was also conducted.FindingsThirteen participants – including doctors, nurses, and a case manager – were recruited. The participants’ working experience ranged from two to 30 years. Three themes and six subthemes were identified: (1) healthcare professionals’ understanding of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity and the importance of patients’ self-management of its symptoms; (2) strategies for supporting self-management; and (3) barriers and challenges to effective self-management, such as inefficient communication, low patient adherence, inadequate cross-departmental collaboration, and an outdated clinical system. Many of the participants relied more on pharmacological interventions than on empowering patients’ self-management skills.ConclusionsDespite healthcare professionals recognising the value of patients’ self-management of symptoms, multiple barriers hinder its implementation. Participants highlighted the need for more accessible, structured patient education approaches, as well as improvements to clinical information systems to better support ongoing monitoring and communication. Strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration and enhancing healthcare professionals’ training in self-management support were also identified as priorities for improving comprehensive cardio-oncology care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-026-00488-w.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide. In 2020, East Asian countries contributed approximately 24% of all newly identified cases and 20% of breast-cancer-related deaths, and relevant studies have indicated a further rise in both figures through 2040 [1]. Although advances in systemic chemotherapy have markedly prolonged overall survival, an expanding body of evidence has underscored chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity as an important, and potentially lethal, sequela in BC survivors [2]. In a study of 2,625 participants, including 51% with BC, the overall incidence of cardiotoxicity was 9% (226 cases) [3]. Among the BC patients, 9.7% experienced heart failure, with 98% of cardiotoxicity cases occurring within the first year [3]. Another study found that 18% of BC patients undergoing chemotherapy had cardiac dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] decline > 10%), with severe damage occurring after a median of seven months [4]. Cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity may interrupt cancer treatment and can evolve into overt congestive heart failure, thereby compromising both survival outcomes and patients’ quality of life (QoL) [5].

Cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity can manifest at various stages of cancer treatment and may present with atypical symptoms [6], complicating patients’ ability to discern whether the symptoms are associated with cardiotoxicity. Self-management strategies – regular home monitoring of vital signs, personalised exercise, a heart-healthy diet, and symptom tracking via mobile apps – enable earlier recognition of therapy-related cardiotoxicity and faster liaison with cardio-oncology specialists [7, 8]. When integrated into a formal survivorship plan that includes scheduled imaging, patient education, and decision aids for long-term cardiovascular pharmacotherapy, these strategies can reduce cardiac events and enhance cancer survivors’ QoL [9]. Consequently, it is imperative for healthcare professionals to provide patients with comprehensive education prior to the commencement of cancer therapy [10]. This education should encompass the risks of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity, strategies for symptom monitoring, preventive measures, and appropriate responses to potential cardiotoxic events [11]. Such proactive engagement is particularly critical for patients undergoing chemotherapy and for those transitioning into survivorship, as both groups are vulnerable to the short- and long-term effects of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity [12].

A critical challenge lies in determining how healthcare professionals can effectively support patients’ self-management through evidence-based and structured approaches, while integrating those processes into clinical practice as care models evolve towards a more person-centred framework [10]. Healthcare professionals are uniquely positioned to drive sustainable improvements in healthcare by facilitating self-management and delivering more personalised, patient-centred services [13]. Equally important is the necessity of exploring healthcare professionals’ perspectives and experiences in facilitating patients’ self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity. Their insights are invaluable for identifying existing gaps in care and formulating strategies to optimise the prevention, monitoring, and management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity [14].

In the context of interdisciplinary care, studying the views of healthcare professionals has become even more essential, as effective management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity requires collaboration among oncologists, cardiologists, nurses, and other healthcare specialists [15]. By integrating healthcare professionals’ expertise and experiences into the development of clinical guidelines and supportive interventions, healthcare systems can improve patient outcomes by addressing both oncological and cardiovascular health in tandem [8]. This interdisciplinary, patient-centred approach is particularly vital for BC survivors, who must navigate not only the complexities of cancer treatment but also the potential long-term cardiovascular sequelae associated with therapy [16]. Therefore, prioritising the perspectives of healthcare professionals is a critical step towards designing holistic care models that balance the dual goals of effective cancer management and the prevention of treatment-related cardiotoxicity.

This qualitative study aimed to enhance the understanding of healthcare professionals’ experiences and perceptions related to supporting BC survivors in managing cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity. By recognising their insights, the study sought to inform the development of targeted self-management interventions and guidelines, ultimately improving the surveillance of cardiotoxicity and overall care for BC survivors.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study used semi-structured interviews to gain an in-depth understanding of healthcare professionals’ experiences in and perceptions of supporting BC survivors in self-managing treatment-related cardiotoxicity and maintaining cardiovascular health [17]. As qualitative studies use “fundamental qualitative descriptions” [18], which are well-suited for addressing underexplored clinical questions, this method provided the research team with a rich, straightforward summary of the participants’ perspectives in the context of healthcare professionals [18, 19]. Consistent with the principles of “fundamental qualitative descriptions”, the aim of this study was to provide a comprehensive, low-inference summary of healthcare professionals’ accounts of clinical practice, perceived barriers, and supportive strategies related to cardiotoxicity self-management.

Participants and recruitment

A purposive sampling strategy was used to ensure that only those with at least two years of clinical experience were included [20]. The participants, including physicians, a case manager, and registered nurses, were recruited from the Department of Breast Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University in Guangzhou, China. Recruitment was facilitated by the department’s Head Nurse, who was also a member of the research team. Eligible participants were approached by a research assistant and given an information sheet outlining the study’s objectives, procedures, and participants’ rights. To minimize potential coercion and address hierarchical power dynamics, the Head Nurse’s role was strictly limited to circulating a general study invitation to eligible staff and did not include direct recruitment conversations, collection of consent, or knowledge of who agreed or declined to participate. All subsequent recruitment communication, eligibility confirmation, and written informed consent procedures were conducted by an independent research assistant who was not involved in departmental supervision or performance evaluation. Participants were explicitly informed that participation was entirely voluntary, that declining would have no impact on their employment evaluation or professional standing, and that interview data would be de-identified to protect confidentiality.

Data collection

The semi-structured interviews were conducted. The research assistant carried out face-to-face interviews until data saturation was reached after 11 interviews – when no new insights were generated from additional interviews [21]. To ensure no new information emerged, two additional interviews were conducted. The interview guide (see the Interview Schedule in Appendix 1) included both core questions and follow-up prompts to encourage more details and deeper insights [22]. Background information was collected during the interviews. The interview duration ranged from 9 min to 55 min. The first author [X-L L], who was experienced in qualitative research, provided training on both interviewing and data handling, as well as reviewed the recordings and transcripts for accuracy.

Data analysis

The digital audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Chinese, and thematic analysis was applied to the transcripts [23, 24]. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese by the bilingual research assistant, who is trained in qualitative interviewing. Open coding was conducted by the first author [X-L L], and all transcripts were organised and analysed using NVivo 12 software (QSR International ™) [25]. Although the analytic procedures were informed by the phases of thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke [23, 24], the purpose of analysis was not to generate interpretive theory but to produce a clear, practice-oriented summary of healthcare professionals’ reported experiences and clinical practices.

The analysis began with repeated readings of the transcripts and the creation of reflective notes. Key sentences were grouped into meaning units, which were then condensed and labelled with short codes, see Table 1 . Codes were kept as close as possible to participants’ own language and clinical terminology to ensure a low-inference, data-near representation of their accounts. By comparing these codes, the first author [X-L L] identified recurring patterns, which facilitated the development of major themes and subthemes. Coding was conducted inductively, with codes derived directly from participants’ narratives rather than from a pre-existing theoretical framework. Throughout the analytic process, developing summaries were repeatedly checked against the original transcripts to ensure that findings reflected what participants explicitly described, avoiding over-interpretation beyond the data.

To ensure analytical transparency, the research team documented all coding decisions, theme development, and revisions throughout the process. A bilingual research assistant independently reviewed the initial codes and themes, which were then refined by a bilingual expert in cardiovascular nursing and cardio-oncology. Once the themes were finalized in Chinese, the first author translated illustrative quotes into English. The research assistant cross-checked these translations, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Strategies for rigor

To ensure methodological rigor and trustworthiness, the criteria of credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability [26, 27] were applied. Credibility was enhanced through purposive sampling of healthcare professionals from multiple disciplines (physicians, nurses, and case managers), enabling triangulation of professional perspectives on cardiotoxicity self-management. Member checking was undertaken by returning verbatim interview transcripts to participants for accuracy verification. Participants were invited to clarify, correct, or elaborate on their statements; minor linguistic clarifications were incorporated, and no substantive changes to meaning were requested. Investigator triangulation was conducted through structured peer debriefing sessions held at three key stages of the study (after initial coding, during theme development, and prior to final theme confirmation). These sessions involved two additional researchers, who independently reviewed selected transcripts, coding decisions, and thematic interpretations. Confirmability was supported by offering the participants the opportunity to verify their responses. For instance, clarification questions were asked when necessary to ensure an accurate understanding of the participants’ responses. Reflexive memos were written immediately after each interview and during iterative data analysis to record emerging assumptions, disciplinary perspectives, and potential interpretive influences. Dependability was reinforced through systematic documentation of methodological decisions, iterative comparison of transcripts with codes, and independent verification of selected transcripts by members of the research team. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, recruitment procedures, and interdisciplinary composition of the sample. Thick description of clinical settings and professional roles was included to enable readers to evaluate the applicability of findings to other oncology or cardio-oncology contexts.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity was addressed through explicit consideration of the researcher’s positionality and experiences. The lead researcher (X‑L L) is an Associate Professor with expertise in cardio-oncology nursing and self-management research and has extensive experience in qualitative methodology. While this background facilitated a nuanced understanding of clinical terminology and contextual factors, it also introduced the potential to privilege biomedical interpretations of management barriers. To manage this, ongoing reflexive discussions were held to separate professional assumptions from participants’ expressed views. Furthermore, the research team included a head nurse with over 20 years of frontline experience in breast cancer and another Associate Professor with expertise in cardiovascular nursing. This multidisciplinary collaboration supported balanced interpretation and helped ensure that findings remained closely aligned with participants’ accounts of everyday clinical realities rather than reflecting a single professional lens. Throughout the analytic process, coding decisions and descriptive summaries were regularly examined during peer discussions. When differing interpretations arose, the team returned to the original transcripts to preserve participants’ intended meanings and maintain a data-near, low-inference analytic stance.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant university and hospital human research ethics committees (HE-FRSF/2024/01; KY-2025-097). This study followed the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Before each interview, each participant provided written consent. All participants were assured of anonymity, confidentiality, as well as their right to decline to answer any question or withdraw from the study at any point. The participants’ identities were anonymised using codes in all transcripts and stored data.

Findings

Participants’ characteristics

The study recruited 13 healthcare professionals from the BC department, the majority of whom were female (12 out of 13; 92.3%). The participants consisted of four physicians (one chief, two associate chiefs, and one resident), one case manager, and eight registered nurses. The participants had significant clinical experience, with an average of 14.2 years in practice (ranging from two to 30 years, see Table 2).

Themes and subthemes

Theme 1: awareness and understanding of cardiotoxicity

The participants demonstrated varying levels of awareness regarding the cardiotoxicity caused by cancer therapies. Most of the participants characterised cardiotoxicity as a rare but potentially severe consequence of anti-cancer therapy.

Subtheme 1.1: recognising cardiotoxicity risks

The participants recognised cardiotoxicity as a significant and potentially severe adverse effect of specific cancer therapies, especially chemotherapy and targeted therapies. They emphasised the role of pre-treatment evaluations and ongoing monitoring in identifying potential cardiotoxicity risks. Their proactive approach included echocardiograms, electrocardiograms, and the evaluation of cardiac biomarkers, such as LVEF, to assess baseline cardiac function:“For example, left ventricular ejection fraction. If it drops by 10 per cent. […] If it decreases five per cent or even more than 10 per cent, then it indicates a significant cardiac issue.” (D1).“Before chemotherapy, we recheck[ed] her [the patient’s] cardiac echocardiogram. If [the patient’s] cardiac function is normal – around 65 to 70 per cent – then during treatment, if her left ventricular ejection fraction drops below 60 per cent, say by about 10 to 15 per cent, we may consider intervening in her treatment plan, either by adjusting the dosage or changing the regimen.” (D4).

The participants also acknowledged the cumulative cardiotoxicity risks associated with anthracyclines. One physician noted the importance of reducing dosages or substituting them with liposomal formulations to minimise cardiotoxicity:“For example, in the past, when we used anthracyclines like doxorubicin in chemotherapy, the cardiotoxicity was quite pronounced. But now we’ve improved – we use liposomal doxorubicin, which has significantly reduced the toxicity.” (D1).

Although the nurses recognised the possibility of treatment-related cardiac events, several of the interviewees acknowledged that cardiotoxicity usually received less attention than other toxicities. For example, one nurse reported a narrow, reactive orientation – monitoring cardiac symptoms only when patients self-reported chest tightness, palpitations, or exertional dyspnoea, and then suggested that those patients needed to return to the hospital – rather than a proactive, educational strategy for cardiotoxicity prevention and early detection:“[I] haven’t given any talks specifically on heart-related issues, since I don’t work in the Cardiology or Cardiothoracic Surgery Department. […] If [a patient] is experiencing chest tightness or palpitations, these uncomfortable, then [the patient should] go to the hospital promptly. That’s what [I] can do. Or if she [the patient] feels shortness of breath doing light physical tasks, it might also suggest a heart problem, and [the patient] should get it checked. Nothing else.” (N2).

Or if she [the patient] feels shortness of breath doing light physical tasks, it might also suggest a heart problem, and [the patient] should get it checked. Nothing else.” (N2).

Subtheme 1.2: perceptions of self-management importance

Most of the participants recognised that self-management played an essential role in mitigating cardiotoxicity:“Every patient is their own best doctor – the best doctor for themselves. We really can’t intervene in [patients’] daily lifestyle. It all depends on them [the patients].” (CM1).

“Every patient is their own best doctor – the best doctor for themselves. We really can’t intervene in [patients’] daily lifestyle. It all depends on them [the patients].” (CM1).

However, the participants expressed concerns about patients’ ability to self-monitor effectively. They highlighted the importance of patient education in fostering an awareness of cardiotoxicity symptoms:“In terms of cardiac. […] Cardiovascular toxic side effects, there can be arrhythmias, chest tightness, and even angina – pain in the cardiac region. These are things she [the patient] may need to monitor on her own.” (D4).“Self-management can prolong survival for cancer patients – especially for those who adhere to medical advice, as they tend to live longer.” (D3).

When you ask if she [the patient] is feeling unwell, [she’ll] usually just say, ‘No, no, nothing.’ It’s like that – especially those who usually just want to leave right after finishing their treatment for the day.” (N8).“What concerns us most are patients who…, they don’t understand or pay attention to [symptoms of cardiotoxicity]… They just think ‘that’s how it is’.” (D4).

Theme 2: strategies for supporting self-management

The participants outlined diverse strategies for supporting the self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity among BC survivors, which focused on enhancing patient education and communication, as well as implementing robust monitoring and follow-up mechanisms. While the approaches varied based on the professional roles of the participants, the shared goal was to empower patients to proactively manage their health, mitigate cardiotoxic risks, and improve treatment adherence.

Subtheme 2.1: patient education and communication

Patient education was consistently highlighted as a cornerstone of supporting self-management. Oncology nurses and physicians were primarily responsible for providing patient education and communication regarding cardiotoxicity throughout the cancer treatment trajectory. The participants emphasised the importance of clear and tailored communication to ensure that the patients understood their treatment, potential cardiotoxic risks, and the necessary steps for self-monitoring and management. The participants noted that educating BC survivors began with preparing them for the potential side effects of cancer therapies. For example, one physician and one nurse, respectively, described how patients were informed about the potential cardiac effects of therapies such as anthracyclines and targeted treatments:“Before chemotherapy, we inform patients about potential side effects – such as cardiotoxicity, nausea, vomiting, and so on – and explain that [they] should report any such symptoms promptly.” (D2).“For every round of chemotherapy, we always tell her, the patient, to let us know immediately if [she] feels any discomfort. Someone. […] She may suddenly feel a bit of chest tightness during treatment – some sensation in the heart – we’ll slow down the infusion rate of the chemotherapy [drugs]. [We’ll also encourage the patients to] drink more water to promote metabolism and excretion, which helps relieve [the symptoms]. They usually feel much better after that, and the discomfort becomes much less.” (N4).

“Before chemotherapy, we inform patients about potential side effects – such as cardiotoxicity, nausea, vomiting, and so on – and explain that [they] should report any such symptoms promptly.” (D2).

The mode of education varied, incorporating both verbal instructions during clinical visits and written materials such as handouts and treatment plans. Additionally, many of the participants described their use of WeChat groups to facilitate ongoing communication and education. Within the clinical context, the WeChat groups were established and managed by the breast cancer unit. All patients admitted to the unit were invited to join the WeChat groups and to follow the unit’s WeChat Official Account. The WeChat platform allowed the patients to raise concerns, ask questions, and share experiences with peers and healthcare providers. The groups were moderated by the breast cancer unit, with oncology nurses responsible for follow-up, overseeing discussions and responding to patient inquiries. For example, one nurse mentioned:“We have a WeChat group where patients can report any discomfort, such as low white blood cell counts or heart-related symptoms. We respond promptly [to their messages in the group].” (N2).

In addition to group chats, the WeChat Official Account was also highlighted as a useful tool, as it allowed healthcare professionals to publish articles. WeChat groups enabled interactive communication, allowing patients to send messages and communicate directly with oncology nurses, whereas the WeChat Official Account functioned as a one-way platform through which patients received educational articles and health information published by the unit:“For example, we have WeChat groups and an Official Account. [We] send them some relevant information – like publishing some health education content.” (N1).

“For example, we have WeChat groups and an Official Account. [We] send them some relevant information – like publishing some health education content.” (N1).

Subtheme 2.2: monitoring and follow-up

The participants advocated for structured multidisciplinary pathways, joint cardiology clinics, and training in cardio-oncology to boost staff confidence and improve patient outcomes. The participants also stressed the importance of regular monitoring to detect early signs of cardiotoxicity, which included routine use of echocardiograms and electrocardiograms during and after cancer treatment:“With each round of chemotherapy, we monitor her, the patient’s electrocardiogram. Every three months [we] arrange a cardiac echocardiogram for the patient to assess the impact of our chemotherapy drugs on the patient’s heart.” (D2).“[…] Its advantage lies in the multidisciplinary discussion it enables. Before and after chemotherapy, we check cardiac enzymes and perform electrocardiograms to confirm whether cardiac function is normal. If any abnormalities are detected, we adjust the treatment plan accordingly.” (D1).

“With each round of chemotherapy, we monitor her, the patient’s electrocardiogram. Every three months [we] arrange a cardiac echocardiogram for the patient to assess the impact of our chemotherapy drugs on the patient’s heart.” (D2).

“[…] Its advantage lies in the multidisciplinary discussion it enables. Before and after chemotherapy, we check cardiac enzymes and perform electrocardiograms to confirm whether cardiac function is normal. If any abnormalities are detected, we adjust the treatment plan accordingly.” (D1).

To overcome self-management-related challenges, some of the participants suggested using wearable technologies, such as smartwatches, to enable real-time monitoring of heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation:“Nowadays, many smartwatches can measure blood oxygen saturation and your heart rate, and even detect changes in cardiac [function], including blood pressure.” (D1).

Theme 3: barriers and challenges to effective self-management

The participants described different significant barriers and challenges that healthcare professionals encountered while supporting BC survivors in their self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity.

Subtheme 3.1: patient-related challenges

One recurring challenge identified was patients’ limited understanding of their condition and the risks associated with cardiotoxicity. The participants frequently noted that some patients, particularly elderly individuals or those from rural areas, struggled to comprehend medical instructions. This was compounded by language barriers, as one nurse explained:“Right now, only patients who can use smartphones are able to use [apps]. […] And for many older [patients] – some of them are illiterate, and some of them don’t understand Mandarin very well. […] This [population]. […] There’s really not much [we can do]. This [population] was mainly [educated] through verbal communication. You know, she [the patient] doesn’t understand it, and she can’t read it to understand, so you can only explain it to her – but explaining doesn’t work. She can’t remember [what you’ve told her]. Just can’t remember well. Usually, [we end up] explaining [everything] to her family members.” (N3).“Sometimes patients just don’t understand – some [of them] may have [lower] levels of education, which can lead to such problems. [Everyone’s] level of understanding is different.” (N6).

Socioeconomic factors also played a significant role in limiting the patients’ self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity. In China, no cardiotoxicity-related services were provided free of charge; although most patients had basic government-run insurance, it required partial out-of-pocket payment for medications, diagnostic tests, and follow-up care. Financial difficulties were a common barrier, with some patients unable to afford cardioprotective medications or necessary follow-up tests. For example, the case manager noted:“Finances are a huge problem. Some patients indeed don’t have strong financial support. She [the patient] still has to return to society, go back to work, and her time isn’t enough, right? Finances are still quite an important factor.” (CM1).

“Finances are a huge problem. Some patients indeed don’t have strong financial support. She [the patient] still has to return to society, go back to work, and her time isn’t enough, right? Finances are still quite an important factor.” (CM1).

One nurse offered more details:“Our doctors and nurses will assess her [the patient’s] financial situation before chemotherapy – whether [the patient] can afford drugs with lower cardiotoxicity, such as liposomal chemotherapy agents. It depends on whether your [the patient’s] finances allow it, or whether [the patient] can use medications that are less harmful to the heart.” (N1).

“Our doctors and nurses will assess her [the patient’s] financial situation before chemotherapy – whether [the patient] can afford drugs with lower cardiotoxicity, such as liposomal chemotherapy agents. It depends on whether your [the patient’s] finances allow it, or whether [the patient] can use medications that are less harmful to the heart.” (N1).

Non-compliance with medical advice was another significant issue. The participants described that some patients failed to attend appointments, follow prescribed therapies, or monitor their symptoms. This was often attributed to a lack of motivation or fatalistic attitudes, particularly among older patients or those from traditional cultural backgrounds. One physician described this challenge:“Some patients […] she [the patient] feels that her illness can’t be cured, so she’d rather not spend her family’s money. She would say, ‘I’m suffering, and my family is spending money too. […]’” (D2).

Subtheme 3.2: systemic barriers

Systemic challenges, such as limited time and resources for follow-up, were frequently mentioned. The participants expressed concerns about the sustainability of intensive monitoring and education efforts, particularly given workforce constraints:“Some patients have low adherence. Moreover, our follow-up system takes quite a lot of time – it still relies on manual data entry. It’s time-consuming, and sometimes there just isn’t enough time. Right now, I’m also working part-time, not full-time.” (CM1).

The overburdened healthcare professionals also faced challenges in providing comprehensive support. Many of the participants described excessive workloads and insufficient staffing, which limited their ability to dedicate time to patient education and follow-up. Discussion

Main findings

This qualitative study included healthcare professionals with diverse backgrounds who provided insights into their experiences and perceptions regarding BC survivors’ self-management of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity. Generally, the participants were aware of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity and often responded by proactively adjusting treatment plans. For example, the healthcare professionals began with anthracycline-based regimens and later transitioned to taxane-based alternatives, which are considered safer from a cardiological standpoint [28]. They also monitored key indicators such as LVEF [29] and cardiac biomarkers [30]. However, despite recognising the importance of patient-centred self-management, most of the participants primarily relied on clinical interventions rather than actively supported patients’ self-management practices. Several reasons for this gap emerged during the interviews. First, poor communication between the healthcare professionals and their patients often stemmed from the patients’ limited education [31] and language barriers [32]. Second, the patients’ adherence was low, influenced by low health literacy [33], financial constraints [34], and a traditional cultural background [33]. Although basic government-run insurance was available, its limited coverage often left patients paying out of pocket for cardioprotective medications, cardiac monitoring, and long-term follow-up, creating financial barriers to sustained self-management. Third, heavy workloads limited the healthcare professionals’ time to provide patient care [35]. Fourth, the lack of advanced systems to track and manage patients’ overall health profiles added an additional burden to the staff.

Beyond these general challenges, the interviews revealed more nuanced issues. The nurses were often the first to detect patients’ symptoms and lifestyle [36], and thus they needed to be educated in assisting patients in reducing cardiotoxicity risks [37]. However, many of the nurse participants lacked relevant knowledge; for example, they were unsure which clinical indicators should be closely monitored. Moreover, the nurses had a low level of self-efficacy, which lowered their performance and motivation [38]; for instance, they viewed cardiotoxicity management as outside their scope of practice since they were not affiliated with cardiology departments. Additionally, the participants suggested that integrating departments and professionals and engaging in cross-departmental collaboration to share goals and mutual knowledge exchange, which were currently lacking, would offer patients well-rounded support across all stages of treatment – before, during, and after care [39, 40]. To bridge those gaps, the participants indicated that practical cardio-oncology self-management guidelines were needed, as existing literature failed to comprehensively address six key dimensions: physical, psychological, nutritional, educational, lifestyle-related, and caregiver support [41].

To address the findings of the interviews, the following are recommended. First, lifestyle interventions, including physical exercise, dietary modifications, and cardiovascular risk factor control, require further study [42]. For instance, while exercise is an effective non-pharmacological self-management strategy to mitigate cardiotoxicity in BC survivors, the optimal timing, type, and intensity of exercise remain uncertain [43]; nutritional interventions during chemotherapy have shown potential in reducing cardiotoxic risk [44]; and AI-based wearable devices should be evaluated for symptom monitoring and cardiotoxicity risk prediction [45]. Given participants’ concerns about patients’ limited symptom recognition and delayed help-seeking, structured, evidence-based lifestyle guidance could also serve as a practical framework for healthcare professionals to provide clearer, more actionable recommendations. Second, although psychological distress was not always explicitly recognised by participants, several described fatalistic attitudes, low motivation, and fear of reporting symptoms—factors that may reflect underlying psychological burden. Therefore, integrating routine psychological assessment and referral pathways into cardio-oncology care may help address these barriers and improve adherence to self-management [46]. Third, patient education should be made more accessible and engaging to serve a broader patient base [31]. This recommendation directly responds to participants’ reports of low health literacy, language barriers, and older patients’ difficulty using smartphone-based tools. For example, using platforms like TikTok instead of printed materials aligns with patients’ habits, as it enables the use of short, plain language that is easier to understand and allows information to spread widely among patients [47]. Fourth, a more advanced application is needed – one that combines chat features with profile management capabilities. Although the participants were currently using WeChat [48] to communicate, it was not systematic. Moreover, a well-designed platform can enhance patients’ self-management skills, including decision-making and collaboration with healthcare professionals [49]. Finally, to better support healthcare professionals, structured cardio-oncology training programs, clear role delineation, and mechanisms for interdisciplinary collaboration should be strengthened.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the participants came from diverse professional backgrounds, including doctors, nurses, and a case manager, which may have led to varied experiences and perceptions regarding patients’ self-management of alleviating cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity. However, the interdisciplinary representation was limited, as all participants were recruited from the Department of Breast Surgery, and no cardiology specialists were included. This imbalance may have constrained the depth of insight into cardiology-specific management practices and interdisciplinary collaboration. Second, although procedural safeguards were implemented to protect voluntary participation, the involvement of the department’s Head Nurse—who held an administrative leadership role and was a member of the research team—may still have introduced perceived power dynamics. Participants may have experienced implicit pressure to participate or may have provided socially desirable responses aligned with perceived departmental expectations. This potential recruitment and response bias cannot be fully excluded. Third, due to time constraints, some healthcare professionals were unable to participate, primarily oncology physicians. As the busiest frontline staff, their insights into the daily clinical challenges of managing cardiotoxicity would have significantly enriched our findings. Among those who did, several interviews were relatively brief. Those factors may have resulted in a limited depth of understanding regarding the topic. Additionally, because all participants were drawn from a single tertiary hospital, the transferability of findings to other institutional settings or healthcare systems may be limited.

Conclusions

Although healthcare professionals generally acknowledge the importance of patient-centred self-management in mitigating cancer treatment-induced cardiotoxicity, many challenges hinder its implementation, for example, limited communication, low patient adherence, isolated professional practices, heavy clinical workloads, and outdated information systems. Consequently, pharmacological interventions are the primary strategy. Addressing these structural and behavioural constraints will require co-produced, multimedia educational resources such as videos, interoperable electronic records with cardiotoxicity prompts, targeted cardio-oncology training for nurses, and formally integrated oncology-cardiology pathways.
