Modeling engineering and medical lifetime data using a flexible extension of the XShanker distribution under censoring
Authors: Amal S Hassan, Ghadah Alomani, Amer Ibrahim Al-Omari, Maisaa M Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complexity of today’s lifetime data necessitates more adaptable probability models. To address this need, we develop the Cosine Topp-Leone XShanker Distribution (CTL–XSD), a novel two-parameter extension of the XShanker distribution. We provide the mathematical properties of the new model, including its moments, incomplete moments, identifiability, quantiles, Lorenz curve, and Bonferroni curve. Using Type-II censoring, we employ both classical and Bayesian methods to estimate the parameters, survival function, and hazard function. Using Markov Chain Monte Carlo techniques, we derive Bayesian point and credible interval estimates under both symmetric and asymmetric loss functions. Additionally, we obtain the asymptotic confidence intervals based on the Fisher information matrix. We also provide a numerical study to evaluate the performance of the various estimators. We compare our findings with those from other models in the context of lifetime data analysis. The CTL–XSD outperformed the alternative models in terms of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test statistic, with values of 0.98886 and 0.98438 in the context of engineering and medical datasets, respectively, with the lowest values in terms of all information criteria.Keywords: Bayesian estimation, Cosine Topp-Leone–G, Identifiability, Approximate confidence intervals, Minimum expected loss function, Metropolis–Hastings algorithmSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Reliability engineering, medical survival analysis, risk assessment, and many other domains depend on accurate modeling of lifespan and time-to-event data for decision-making. The complicated nature of today’s systems, ranging from biological processes to mechanical components, produces failure data with a wide variety of behaviors, such as rising, falling, bathtub-shaped, and inverted bathtub-shaped hazard rates. With serious financial and safety effects, using an inadequately flexible probability model can result in skewed reliability predictions, incorrect maintenance schedules, incorrect warranty cost estimates, and inaccurate clinical prognoses. As a result, statistical distributions that are both tractable for inference and flexible to complicated real-world patterns are in consistently high demand, especially when data are incomplete due to censoring.

Literature review

Numerous generated families of distributions have been created as a result of notable advancements in the theory of probability distribution in recent years. Among the many well-known families are the Topp Leone-G (TL–G)1, the Marshall–Olkin–G2, the Kumaraswamy–G3, the Type II generalized TL–G4, the transmuted–G5, the power Lindley–G6, the alpha power transformed–G7, Lomax–G8, the Weibull–G9, the logistic–X10, the odd inverted TL–G11, the odd-Burr–G12. In the same vein, several probability models derived from generated families have been developed, including the Type II half–logistic Rayleigh Weibull distribution13 and a new two-parameter distribution introduced14. Other notable examples15–17.

The recent advancements in the field of distribution theory have been based on trigonometric transformation, which provides flexibility in data modeling. Following the development of the sine–G18 and sec–G19 families, several extensions have been introduced, including the sine alpha power–G20, weighted sine–G21, and so on. In the same direction, cosine-based models have also been introduced, such as the cosine Kumaraswamy–G22, cosine inverse Lomax–G23, and so on. Following this, Nanga et al.24 introduced the cosine TL–G (CTL–G) family, which extends the cosine–G and TL–G structures, thus addressing both monotonic and non-monotonic failure rate characteristics while maintaining the simplicity of the model through the addition of only one parameter.

Research gap and motivation

Several trigonometric distributions have recently emerged. The sine inverted exponentiated Weibull distribution29 was developed using the sine–G generator, with properties, Bayesian and ML estimation discussed. Similarly, cosine-Weibull30, sine power unit inverse Lindley31, log-cosine-power function32, alpha power cosine-Weibull33, sine power half-logistic34, sine Type II Topp-Leone Gompertz35, and sine inverted exponentiated Pareto36 distributions have been introduced, each featuring unique statistical properties and validated using ML or Bayesian estimation on real data. Additional contributions include a quantile regression framework based on the tangent exponentiated odd log-logistic Weibull distribution37 and a comparative analysis of estimation methods for the sine Topp-Leone Fréchet distribution38. A bivariate cosine Gaussian distribution was suggested in39.

However, with the exception of a single study, these works primarily focus on complete samples, leaving a significant methodological gap for censored data. In real-world reliability and survival studies, censoring is common due to time, cost, and operational constraints. Estimation methods that perform well on complete data can become unstable or biased under censoring, undermining their practical applicability. This gap highlights the need for reliable inferential techniques designed for censored lifetime data.

Novelty and contributions of the study

The primary novelty is the introduction of the CTL–XSD, which overcomes the XSD’s limitation to strictly increasing hazard rates by modeling increasing, decreasing, reversed J-shaped, and upside-down shaped failure patterns with a parsimonious two-parameter structure. However, its mathematical complexity necessitates numerical methods, and its behavior under censoring schemes remains unexplored. To address the inference gap for censored data, we adapt standard ML and Bayesian, along with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MC–MC) methodologies to provide a complete inferential framework under Type–II censoring (T–IIC). We use MC–MC for the calculation of Bayesian credible intervals under symmetric and asymmetric losses, and asymptotic intervals using the Fisher information matrix (FM), offering the flexibility of using different methodologies for estimating the model. Identifiability of model

A key characteristic of statistics and a requirement for accurate inference is identifiability. If it is theoretically feasible to ascertain the distinct true values of a model’s underlying parameters given an endless number of observations, the model is said to be identifiable. A model is said to be non-identifiable if several sets of parameter values yield the exact same probability distribution for the observed data. No amount of data can differentiate between those parameter groups in such case.

Parameter identifiability in the CTL–XSD ensures that its parameters can be uniquely and accurately estimated from observed data. This feature is crucial for accurate model fitting and inference because it keeps the model’s interpretability intact by preventing various parameter configurations from yielding identical distributions. Specifically, the mean obtained when s = 1, represents the average expected lifetime of a system or component and offers a basic measure of central tendency. In reliability engineering and survival analysis, this statistic is a crucial point of reference that provides a useful foundation for assessing long-term performance, scheduling maintenance, and calculating warranty durations. The variance quantifies the variability or dispersion of failure times around the mean, indicating the consistency of product performance. The skewness measure measures the asymmetry of the distribution; positive skewness is typical in lifetime data, reflecting that most failures occur early while a few units survive much longer. Kurtosis measures a distribution’s peakiness and tail heaviness. Failure times are more equally distributed and there are fewer outliers when the kurtosis value is low, indicating a flatter, more uniform distribution with lighter tails. A high kurtosis value, on the other hand, denotes a sharper center peak and heavier tails, which in terms of reliability may imply a concentration of failures around a certain time combined with a larger probability of extreme early or late failures. Knowing kurtosis is useful in survival and reliability analysis because it makes it easier to spot odd failure patterns, evaluate the risk of outlier events, and choose the right models for data with noticeable peak or tail behavior.

Bayesian estimation

The effectiveness of the Bayesian approach in the estimation of the parameters and the reliability characteristics under censoring is greatly dependent on the proper selection of the loss function and the informative prior distributions. Although the selection of the prior is one of the primary aspects of the Bayesian approach, there is no clear consensus in the literature with regard to the selection of the optimal prior for the unknown parameters. Instead, the selection of the prior is typically based on mathematical properties of the distribution. Gamma priors are considered a convenient choice because the gamma distribution has been shown to be flexible, meaning it takes on various shapes depending on its parameters, thus making it more effective for estimation compared to other complex distributions. Therefore, gamma priors are used in this study because of their flexibility compared to other distributions.

Computational complexity and comparative analysis

This sub-section addresses the computational aspects of the M–H algorithm and its comparison with the ML method. Although the Bayesian approach using the M–H algorithm is computationally more expensive than the deterministic Nelder-Mead optimization utilized for MLE, it offers significant practical benefits for survival analysis and reliability engineering, particularly with small sample sizes and under censoring schemes.

The increased runtime of the Bayesian approach is due to the necessity of constructing a large Markov chain (typically 10,000 iterations) to accurately approximate the entire posterior distribution. This stochastic sampling process is inherently more labor-intensive than resolving a single optimization problem to find point estimates. However, this computational expense results in a richer inferential output, including full posterior densities and credible intervals that do not rely on large-sample asymptotic approximations.

These advantages are especially critical in practical situations where data may be substantially restricted or sample sizes are small, such as in clinical trials with limited patient data or reliability testing of high-value components. In these contexts, the extra computational effort is frequently outweighed by the increased stability and accuracy of Bayesian results, particularly when using informative or weakly informative priors.

Figure 4 shows the MSE values for several estimate techniques under Set 1 (β = 0.5, ρ = 1.3). All sample sizes (105, 135, and 150) show that the MLE consistently yields a significantly higher MSE than the Bayesian-based approaches. The BEs (SELF_INP, LLF_INP, LLFP_INP, and MLF_INP), on the other hand, show similar and significantly lower MSE values, with only minor differences between them. This demonstrates that the Bayesian techniques are more resilient and efficient than the standard MLE, especially as the sample size increases.

Figure 5 shows the MSE performance of several estimators for Set 1 (β = 0.5, ρ = 1.3) under the N_INP scheme. All sample sizes (105, 135, and 150) show consistently higher MSE values when using the MLE approach, but the Bayesian-based estimates (SELF_N_INP, LLF_N_INP, LLFP_N_INP, and MLF_N_INP) yield significantly lower and more stable MSE results. As the sample size grows, the MSE decreases, highlighting the Bayesian techniques’ superior efficiency and dependability over MLE.

Figure 6 shows, with regard to β, the MSE of many different estimates under Set 1 (β = 0.5, ρ = 1.3). It is evident that for all sample sizes (35, 45, and 50), the MLE consistently produces larger MSE values than the Bayesian-based methods. On the other hand, even in small-sample situations, the BEs (SELF_INP, LLF_INP, LLFP_INP, and MLF_INP) produce significantly lower and more stable error levels, demonstrating their superior efficiency and robustness.

In Fig. 7, the MSE of the MLE consistently has the highest values, showing it is the least effective estimate for β among the tested methods. Its MSE decreases as the sample size increases from 35 to 50. The other four estimates (MSE_SELF_N_INP, MSE_LLF_N_INP, MSE_LLFP_N_INP, and MSE_MLF_N_INP) show significantly lower MSE values. The performance of these four superior methods is very similar to each other, with their MSE values clustered tightly together. Their MSE decreases as sample size increase from 35 to 50. For all five estimates, the MSE consistently decreases as the sample size increases, which is expected for consistent estimates.

Based on Fig. 8, the MSE of the INP case consistently provides a more accurate and efficient estimation of the parameter ρ compared to the N_INP case. The N_INP approach leads to a considerable increase in estimation error across all methods. This suggests that for a sample size of 135, the INP method is the preferred approach for estimating ρ with higher precision. Further investigation would be needed to understand the underlying reasons for the significant performance difference between the two cases.

Figure 9 illustrates the performance of the parameter estimates for Set 1 and Set 2 under both INP and N_INP frameworks. As the parameter ρ increases from 1.3 to 1.5 (with β held constant), the MSE increases across all estimation methods, suggesting that higher values of ρ are more challenging to estimate accurately. Notably, the LLFP_INP method consistently yields the lowest MSE in both datasets. Overall, the simulation results indicate that BE (both INP and N–INP) outperforms the MLE.

Based on Fig. 10, both parameters in the INP case appear to have converged to their stationary distributions, according to the diagnostic plots. Practical implications

The comparative advantage of the CTL–XSD is demonstrated through its performance evaluation against related models, including the XSD and other competing distributions. A key strength of the CTL–XSD lies in its enhanced flexibility in capturing a wide range of hazard-rate shapes, particularly inverted bathtub and reversed J-shaped patterns, which frequently arise in complex engineering and medical datasets. Unlike simpler models, the incorporation of the CTL–G component provides greater adaptability, especially in the tail regions of the distribution. This added flexibility enables a more accurate representation of the underlying failure mechanism, as reflected in the substantial improvements observed in goodness-of-fit statistics.

For the two datasets analyzed, the superior performance of the CTL–XSD further highlights its robustness in the presence of extreme and high-outlier observations. The flexible tail behavior, controlled by the model’s shape parameter, allows it to effectively accommodate extreme values without distorting parameter estimation. As a result, the presence of high outliers does not disproportionately influence the model fit, making the CTL–XSD particularly suitable for real-world applications characterized by heavy-tailed or extreme data. This robustness is visually supported by the PDF, CDF, and PP plots (Figs. 16 and 17), where the CTL–XSD closely follows the empirical distribution, including in the tail regions, while maintaining overall fit accuracy.

Although the datasets considered represent real-world extreme scenarios, the resilience of the CTL–XSD against high outliers is further confirmed by its superior goodness-of-fit measures, including AIC, BIC, and K–S statistics. We have added a brief clarification emphasizing that the model’s flexible tail structure enables it to accommodate extreme observations more effectively than traditional distributions.

The strong empirical performance of the CTL–XSD in both engineering and medical applications underscores its practical relevance. In engineering reliability analysis, the model facilitates accurate failure-time modeling, supporting improved maintenance planning and optimized system lifecycle management. In medical survival analysis, it assists in identifying high-risk periods, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making and resource allocation. Overall, the flexibility and robustness of the CTL–XSD establish it as a reliable and powerful statistical tool for data-driven decision-making in critical real-world contexts.

Conclusion

Lifetime distributions are fundamental tools for modeling reliability and survival data. This study introduces a new hybrid model, the CTL–XSD distribution, formed by combining the XSD with the CTL generator. Some key statistical properties are derived to demonstrate the model’s flexibility. Parameter estimation and reliability measures (survival and hazard functions) are developed under T–IIC using both ML and Bayesian approaches. Asy–CIs and BCIs under SELF, LLF, and MLF are constructed via MC–MC simulation. A comprehensive simulation study compares the estimators using MSE, AIL, and CP. Results show that Bayesian estimators based on N_INP priors generally outperform MLEs, producing smaller MSEs and shorter interval lengths while maintaining CPs near 95%. The model’s applicability is illustrated using engineering and COVID-19 survival datasets, where it successfully captures wear-out and inverted bathtub-shaped hazard behaviors and outperforms competing models. A limitation of this study is the focus on T–IIC only. Future work may consider progressive, hybrid, or adaptive censoring schemes, as well as E-Bayesian estimation under alternative loss functions.
