Insights Into Atomic Ordering and Morphological Control Integration Design for Next‐Generation Proton Exchange Membrane Fuel Cells
Authors: Eungjun Lee, Jieun Baik, Jin Soo Kang, Sung Jong Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are emerging as key energy conversion systems for heavy‐duty vehicles, stationary power plants, and high‐load infrastructures such as data centers. However, their long‐term durability remains critically constrained by transition metal dissolution and structural degradation in alloy catalysts, particularly under heavy‐duty target operation (90°C–120°C). Recent studies reveal that the fundamental origin of degradation lies not simply in alloy composition but in the instability of atomic ordering within each grain. Long‐range ordered (LRO) intermetallic phase enhances thermodynamic stability through strong atomic bonding, yet their high‐temperature synthesis often induces particle coarsening and structural defects. Conversely, short‐range ordered (SRO) phase arranges at lower energies, enabling morphology preservation while providing locally stabilized chemical environments that suppress dissolution. Advances in operando microscopy and three‐dimensional atomic electron tomography have clarified how atomic‐scale ordering, strain distribution, and site occupancy govern degradation pathways. Building on these insights, low‐temperature atomic rearrangement strategies coupled with morphology engineering offer a unified framework for achieving stable electronic structures, minimized defect density, and enhanced durability. This review consolidates the mechanistic understanding of ordering‐dependent degradation and proposes atomic ordering–morphology integration design for next‐generation PEMFC catalysts capable of sustaining high performance under demanding conditions.Keywords: atomic ordering, chemical short‐range order, morphology, oxygen reduction reaction, proton exchange membrane fuel cell

Introduction

The transition toward a hydrogen economy represents a pivotal step in achieving a carbon‐neutral society, and proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) has re‐emerged as one of the most promising high‐efficiency power conversion technologies to realize this goal [1, 2, 3]. PEMFC can deliver high power densities (>1 W/cm2) even at low operating temperatures, and their fast transient response within seconds and modular scalability make them an efficient and carbon‐free alternative to conventional fossil fuel‐based power generation systems [4]. With the rapid expansion of the hydrogen industry, research and development have shifted from small‐scale light‐duty mobility and residential fuel cells to large‐scale heavy‐duty vehicles (HDV), long‐distance transportation, drones, and stationary power generation [5, 6]. As the application scope expands, enhancing system efficiency and optimizing operating conditions have become critical for the commercialization of PEMFC (Figure 1a) [5].

In particular, in high‐load infrastructures such as rapidly growing artificial intelligence (AI) data centers, PEMFC is emerging as sustainable power sources alongside energy storage systems, small modular reactors, and solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) [8, 9]. In such large‐scale power‐demand environments, PEMFCs have achieved efficiencies exceeding 70% and can respond to real‐time load fluctuations within seconds. Their modular design enables flexible scalability from kilowatt‐ to megawatt‐level systems, offering stable power supply across mobile or stationary configurations. Above all, the intrinsic carbon‐neutral operation of PEMFCs distinguishes them from other power technologies. However, realizing their full potential requires simultaneous improvement in efficiency, cost, and durability with the latter remaining the most critical challenge [10].

A direct route to improving system efficiency involves raising the operating temperature from the current ∼80°C to the higher temperature range (90°C–120°C), thereby accelerating electrochemical kinetics. Elevated temperatures enhance oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) kinetics, proton conductivity, and gas diffusivity, while reducing thermal gradients between the reaction heat and coolant, leading to more efficient thermal management (Figure 1b) [5]. Because HDVs operated over long distances and under sustained high‐load conditions, PEMFC systems must achieve both high durability and high efficiency during prolonged services. For this reason, higher temperature operation is regarded as a promising strategy because it improves thermal management while helping to maximize system efficiency. To further support this discussion, we incorporated the recent NEDO roadmap for HDVs, which explicitly sets increasingly demanding thermal targets. As shown in Figure 1c [7], the roadmap assumes a maximum FC water temperature of 105°C around 2030 and 120°C around 2035/2040. NEDO has outlined performance targets through 2040. The 2030 target is based on a 25 ton heavy‐duty truck, whereas the targets from 2035 onward are expanded to include 44 ton heavy‐duty trucks. In the I–V curves, the square symbols represent the rated thermal operating points after accelerated durability test (ADT), while the triangles denote the equivalent rated thermal points at beginning of life. Each target is defined based on the rated power shown in the corresponding I–V curve. Although the required current density varies with the operating voltage, the power density remains constant across the conditions, which is clearly observed (Figure 1d) [7]. PEMFC development has placed less emphasis on maintaining high rated power density and has instead prioritized sustaining high kinetic activity. However, the current performance targets require not only high kinetics but also robust maintenance of the rated power density, which is strongly influenced by mass‐transport resistance. Therefore, future PEMFC catalyst development must deliver high performance across all aspects, including kinetic activity, mass‐transport properties, rated power density, and its retention (Figure 1e).

Nonetheless, these conditions exacerbate transition‐metal dissolution, weaken alloying effects, and alter surface compositions. Dissolved metal ions can migrate into the membrane or ionomer, causing contamination that simultaneously degrades performance and lifetime [11, 12, 13, 14]. Recent studies indicate that the fundamental cause of performance degradation is closely associated with the maintenance of the alloy structure [15]. Atomic ordering governs the uniformity of interatomic bonding strength and electronic density distribution, effectively suppressing transition metal dissolution while tuning the electronic structure and surface stability. Physical ordering describes whether atoms are regularly arranged at specific positions (lattice points) in three‐dimensional space to form structural periodicity, fundamentally regardless of the atomic species (e.g., whether it is Pt or a transition metal). At the nanoscale, this concept is further divided based on its spatial extent: long‐range order (LRO) refers to a state where this physical ordering is periodically extended over a range (>20 Å), whereas Warren–Cowley short‐range order (SRO) refers to a state where the physical ordering is locally present between different types of atoms within a range (2–5 Å). In the context of alloying, this local structural arrangement is fundamentally governed by the specific chemical interactions and mixing preferences between different types of atoms. Therefore, SRO is treated as practically synonymous with chemical short‐range order (CSRO), which is specifically defined by the conditional probability of certain elements being located around a central atom [16, 17, 18, 19]. LRO, characterized by well defined superlattice structures extending throughout the grains provides thermodynamic and chemical stability, whereas SRO involves local chemical ordering that stabilizes defects and interfaces. These two ordering strategies complementarily across different structural scales to enhance catalyst durability.

Consequently, recent advances have focused on forming atomic ordering through low temperature atomic rearrangement processes coupled with precise control of nanoparticle morphology. Low temperature synthesis minimizes grain growth and morphological loss typically induced by high temperature annealing while preserving internal atomic order. Furthermore, when the surface energy differences are small, it becomes possible to finely tune the exposure of active facets, achieving a balance between catalytic activity and stability [23, 24]. This emerging approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional high temperature alloying toward atomic rearrangement–morphology integrated strategy, where atomic ordering and particle morphology are concurrently engineered.

Building upon this state‐of‐the‐art research direction, this review article aims to systematically elucidate the correlation between atomic‐scale structural evolution and chemical ordering in alloy catalysts, starting from the inherent tradeoff between alloying and structural control. Furthermore, we propose a low‐temperature atomic rearrangement–morphology integration strategy that enables the concurrent enhancement of catalytic activity and durability, offering a new design paradigm for next‐generation alloy catalysts in PEMFCs.

Atomic Scale Strategies to Enhance Catalytic Activity and Durability

The overall performance of PEMFCs is governed by the ORR, which proceeds through a four‐electron pathway involving multiple reaction intermediates. Therefore, optimizing the binding energies of these intermediates is essential for maximizing catalytic activity [25, 26]. Such optimization strategies have evolved primarily along two directions: atomic arrangement engineering and morphological control.

As shown in Figure 2a [27], early studies focused on alloying Pt with transition metals such as Ni, Co, or Fe to tune the d‐band center, thereby weakening the OH* binding while strengthening the OOH* binding and approaching the optimal point predicted by the Sabatier relationship [31, 32]. Although these Pt‐based alloy catalysts exhibited higher activity than conventional Pt/C, rapid dissolution of the transition metals during operation led to the loss of alloy effects and severe degradation in durability [33, 34].

To overcome these limitations, intermetallic structures with well‐defined atomic ordering were introduced. Intermetallic phases possessing LRO strengthen Pt–TM bonding and significantly enhance structural and thermodynamic stability, ultimately improving long‐term durability [24]. Depending on the degree and type of ordering, various ordered phases such as A1 (fcc), L10, L12, B2 (bcc type), B11, and B35 (hcp type) can be formed [35] (Figure 2b) [28].

In addition to atomic arrangement optimization through intermetallic ordering, morphological controls have developed as a complementary strategy for developing highly active and durable ORR catalysts. Morphological control such as manipulating crystal facet exposure, particle shape, frame structures, and internal voids enables precise control over the extrinsic properties of active sites and improves charge and mass transport [36]. These nanostructures induce strain deformation and significantly enhance catalytic activity through structural and electronic changes of the catalysts (Figure 2c) [29]. Based on these principles, structurally engineered Pt nanocatalysts have been developed, including octahedral nanoparticles [37, 38], nanoframes [39], core–shell structures [40, 41], and nanowires [42], preferentially exposing specific facets (111), (110) known to be advantageous for the ORR [43, 44] (Figure 2d) [30]. In particular, three‐dimensional open frameworks and hollow structures maximize the density of accessible active sites and facilitate efficient electronic conduction and mass diffusion, thereby delivering superior ORR performance. In short, morphological strategies remove unstable regions and modulate the active surface, enhancing activity through the ligand and strain effects [29].

However, during long‐term operation, TM dissolution often occurs at defect sites such as grain boundaries, accelerating structural degradation. Potential cycling triggers repeated dissolution, reprecipitation, and Ostwald ripening [45, 46] (Figure 3a) [47], which drives the particles toward thermodynamically stable, low‐surface‐energy structures and eventually destroys the initial morphology (Figure 3b) [48]. This degradation becomes more severe for catalysts with higher surface area.

Among the various investigations on atomic arrangements aimed at improving PEMFC performance, intermetallic structures constitute a distinct subclass within the broader framework of long‐range ordering. Intermetallic arrangements capable of simultaneously controlling electronic bonding and chemical ordering have emerged as a promising alternative. The superior chemical durability of intermetallic alloys arises from orbital hybridization between Pt and TMs. Overlapping Pt 5d and TM 3d orbitals causes partial d‐electron delocalization from TM to Pt, redistributing charge density. This stabilizes TMs in less oxidizable states and shifts the Pt d‐band center below the Fermi level, enhancing resistance to dissolution. The reduction in TM–TM bonding also decreases vulnerable oxidation and leaching sites, further improving chemical stability [52, 53, 54]. Long range ordered intermetallics are reported to theoretically possess internal stability and high electrochemical durability because it has a sufficient strong Pt–TM metal bonding force [55].

More recently, it has been demonstrated that the local atomic configuration also plays a critical role in determining the durability of catalysts. Jeong et al. have reported that such degradation phenomena primarily originate from the instability of atomic arrangements and electronic structures within the alloy employed AET to investigate the three‐dimensional atomic‐scale dynamics of Pt–Ni and Ga‐doped PtNi alloy catalysts. Quantitative analysis of the CSRO for individual atoms revealed that Ga doping mitigated the CSRO difference between surface and subsurface regions after potential cycling [49], effectively suppressing Ni dissolution and improving chemical stability. Excessive surface segregation of Ni was largely suppressed, and dissolution occurred only in Ga‐deficient local regions. These results demonstrate, at the atomic level, that the preservation of CSRO is a key factor in improving the durability of alloy catalysts (Figure 3c) [49].

AET visualizations further confirmed that Ga doping homogenizes the compositional distribution between the surface and core of PtNi nanoparticles, alleviating Ni‐rich surface segregation. Before doping, the nanoparticles exhibited a distinct Ni‐rich shell and Pt‐rich core; after doping, the Pt–Ni arrangement became more uniform, maintaining stable CSRO during electrochemical operation. Distance‐dependent CSRO analysis (Figure 3d) revealed that Ga doping reduced the CSRO deviation between surface and interior regions, leading to a more uniform chemical ordering throughout the particle [49]. By reducing surface heterogeneity, vulnerable sites prone to metal dissolution are eliminated, thereby enabling the nanoparticles to maintain a more stable structural integrity.

Fundamentally, the degradation of an electrocatalyst is intricately tied to the loss of its localized structural integrity, a process that could be quantitatively reflected by a progressive positive shift in the SRO. Because this positive shift physically indicates elemental segregation, extending catalyst lifetime necessitates strategic structural designs that effectively maintain this localized chemical ordering under severe operational stresses. As illustrated in Figure 3e,f [50, 51], our evaluation of EXAFS‐derived SRO evolutions confirms that this fundamental dependence on SRO maintenance is universally applicable, regardless of the catalyst's initial phase. By tracking in‐situ gradual potential steps and across ADT cycles, it becomes evident that structural decomposition universally tracks with the positive shift of SRO. Consequently, whether the initial phase is ordered or disordered, the ultimate goal of rational catalyst design must be centered on "maintaining" the local atomic arrangement. Rigorously minimizing the variation of SRO across dynamic electrochemical environments to ensure superior structural stability and long‐term durability.

In short, durability improvement depends on how uniformly atoms maintain the degree and uniformity of atomic ordering within each grain.

Long Range‐Ordered Nanoalloys as Electrocatalysts

Intermetallic alloys generally exhibit a high energy barrier for the order–disorder transition and require sufficient thermal energy for atomic rearrangement (Figure 4a,b) [56, 57]. This phase transition proceeds through diffusion activated at elevated temperatures. Once an ordered configuration is established, the intermetallic phase becomes thermodynamically stable with a lower Gibbs free energy. Such stability results from a decrease in formation enthalpy (ΔH < 0) due to strong heteroatomic bonding, which competes with the entropic term (−TΔS) associated with disordering [60] (Figure 4b) [57]. Crystallographically, intermetallic represents a periodic alternation of constituent atoms throughout the crystal lattice, identifiable by characteristic superlattice reflections. Typical examples include the L12 (Pm‐3m) structure in Pt3M alloys and the L10 (P4/mmm) structure in PtM alloys, both formed under high‐temperature conditions that enable sufficient atomic diffusion and are thus regarded as thermodynamically stable intermetallic phases [57, 61].

However, nanoparticles as electrocatalysts used in electrochemical reactions are generally heterogeneous and polycrystalline. Multiple grains coexist within each particle, and achieving atomic ordering across grain boundaries and orientation mismatches require substantial energy. Because the particles are not composed of a single crystal facet, structural defects arising from lattice mismatch are inevitable. Moreover, mixed Pt and TM atoms undergo atomic migration and vacancy‐mediated rearrangement, which introduces crystallographic defects during ordering (Figure 4c) [58]. These defects act as initiation sites for metal dissolution, and prolonged electrochemical operation gradually weakens metallic bonds, leading to structural degradation of the catalyst.

Zeng et al. reported that an L12‐Pt3Co catalyst supported on Mn single‐atom sites underwent severe degradation during accelerated stress testing under light‐duty vehicle conditions (LDV, 80°C, 100% RH, 150 kPaabs) [59]. Dissolved Co ions contaminated the ionomer and membrane, resulting in a significant decline in membrane electrode assembly (MEA) performance, while the characteristic superlattice reflections nearly disappeared after testing (Figure 4d). Although intermetallic alloy catalysts show greater structural stability than disordered alloys, partial dissolution caused by intrinsic lattice defects still occurs under practical PEMFC operation. This observation indicates that intermetallic ordering alone cannot completely resolve the durability issue at the nanoscale. Therefore, new synthetic approaches are needed to maintain a high degree of ordering while minimizing dependence on high‐temperature processing.

Stabilization Role of Short‐Range Order in Nanoalloy Catalysts

More recent studies have highlighted SRO (a flexible and locally confined form of chemical ordering) as a new axis of atomic ordering. SRO refers to a statistically preferred bonding arrangement between specific atomic pairs within local regions, even in the absence of long‐range atomic arrangement. In particular, in nanoparticles with polycrystalline grains such as multiprincipal element alloys (MPEAs) or high‐entropy alloys (HEAs), if sufficient short‐range chemical ordering is present within grains, the density of intragranular precipitates and defects can be reduced, which greatly contributes to nanoparticle stability (Figure 5a) [17]. Unlike Long range ordered nanoalloys, SRO does not exhibit distinct superlattice peaks, making it difficult to confirm its atomic arrangement by bulk structural probes such as XRD. Therefore, its presence can be confirmed through local structural analysis such as EXAFS and pair distribution function (PDF) measurements. As the formation of SRO requires lower thermal energy than LRO, particle growth and surface‐area loss are minimized, providing a flexible design approach that maintains nanoscale structural stability while enabling morphology control [49, 64].

Li, Xiaoke et al. illustrates the introduction of localized tetragonal distortion within alloy catalysts by tuning the atomic thermal diffusion process (Figure 5b) [62]. Oxygen‐free Co3O4 was synthesized via rapid microwave heating and subsequently combined with Pt nanoparticles through thermal diffusion. Structural analysis revealed that the outer region exhibited a lattice spacing of 0.137 nm corresponding to the Pt(220) plane, while the interior consisted of domains with spacings of 0.265 nm (red region, fct structure) and 0.275 nm (blue region, fcc structure). PDF analysis showed a distinct multipeak feature near the second coordination shell, attributed to localized tetragonal lattice distortion. This result was interpreted as a partial transition from a disordered FCC to a partially ordered structure. Geometric phase analysis (GPA) of high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) images revealed a broad compressive strain field across the plane, with compression–tension strain dipoles forming near the particle edges, confirming the coexistence of both strain types. These “partially ordered” nanoparticles exhibit enhanced electrochemical stability, which is attributed to strain‐induced structural stabilization.

Recently, Xiong, Yin, et al. quantitatively investigated the effect of particle size on the onset of disorder as temperature increases according to SRO and LRO using Pt3Co particles, and performed Monte Carlo simulations to calculate the ordering parameter at thermodynamic equilibrium (Figure 5c) [63]. Although the inflection points of LRO and SRO curves occurred at nearly identical temperatures, the SRO‐containing particles were found to transition through an intermediate DO22 phase rather than directly into a disordered state. This observation suggests that partially retained ordering persists longer in SRO systems, favoring structural stability during phase transformation.

Moreover, it is roughly suggested that CSRO acts as a key factor that significantly changes the phase stability, lattice distortion, and defect energy in entropy alloys, and that the actual behavior of the alloy is not a completely random solid solution but rather exhibits persistent local compositional correlations [65]. Such behavior has been evidenced in several compositionally complex alloys, where SRO modifies cohesive energy and lattice distortion ultimately influencing phase stability and defect energetics [66, 67]. Extending this perspective to Pt–TM nanoalloy ORR catalysts, it can be expected that the chemical SRO formed inside the nanoparticles and near the grain boundaries acts as a variable that simultaneously controls the driving force of transition metal dissolution and the spatial distribution of active/inactive sites by readjusting the binding energy and local lattice distortion of Pt–TM heteroatom pairs. Similar SRO‐induced segregation and local structural rearrangements near extended defects have been reported in multicomponent alloys [68, 69], supporting the idea that nanoscale SRO may provide a structural engineering axis that alleviates the intrinsic tradeoff between ORR activity and durability by thermodynamically stabilizing the Pt‐rich surface and the Pt–TM‐mixed subsurface while tuning adsorption energies through controlled nearest‐ and next‐nearest‐neighbor coordination.

Atomic Ordering and Morphology Control Integration Strategies

LRO provides the chemical stability through its periodic superlattice and thus represents the core strategy for durable catalysts. However, forming LRO (such as intermetallic alloys) requires high‐temperature processing, which limits its practical application to nanoscale particles. To further enhance the long‐term durability of long range ordered nanoalloys, several recent studies have proposed two complementary stabilization strategies. The first is an internal stabilization approach, in which a third element is incorporated to modulate metal–metal bonding strength and increase the degree of atomic ordering, thereby reinforcing the structural stability of the intermetallic phase. The second is an external stabilization approach that suppresses metal dissolution by forming protective surface layers, such as carbon shells or Pt‐skin structures, which serve as physical and chemical barriers during operation.

Park et al. investigated the formation mechanism of a ternary intermetallic alloy by incorporating Ni into an ordered PtCo intermetallic seed (Figure 6a) [70]. Because direct migration of d‐PtNi onto the ordered PtCo core involves a high energy barrier, a directional atomic exchange at the interface is required to form a thermodynamically stable Pt–Co–Ni alloy. This interfacial exchange process is critical for maintaining internal ordering even under high‐temperature conditions. The resulting catalyst exhibited a rated power density of 1.013 W/cm2 and excellent durability under HDV operation (94°C, 250 kPaabs).

Enhancing the ordering degree typically requires high‐temperature annealing, which inevitably induces particle agglomeration. To overcome this limitation, Liang et al. proposed a low‐melting‐point–induced bond‐strength weakening (LMIBSW) strategy [53] (Figure 6b) [23]. The introduction of Sn weakened local metallic bonding, promoting atomic diffusion and enabling a high degree of ordering even at low temperatures. In situ high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF–STEM) analysis revealed the emergence of (110) superlattice peaks at 400°C–450°C, and an ordering degree of approximately 76% was achieved after 60 min of annealing. The particle size remained unchanged, confirming that structural coalescence was effectively suppressed. After accelerated stress testing under HDV conditions, the ordered structure retained over 80% of its power output at 0.7 V, demonstrating a robust and durable intermetallic catalyst design.

A critical challenge in synthesizing ordered intermetallic nanoparticles is the intrinsic tradeoff between atomic ordering and particle size. Conventional thermal annealing typically requires highly elevated temperatures to overcome the activation energy barrier for the order–disorder transition, which inevitably leads to severe nanoparticle agglomeration and a subsequent loss of electrochemical active surface area. However, advanced synthetic strategies can effectively lower this activation energy.

Consequently, the optimal parameter space for maximizing both activity and durability shifts toward significantly milder synthesis conditions. For instance, these advanced systems can achieve a substantial ordering degree (e.g., >70%–80%) at much lower annealing temperatures in the optimal range of 450°C–700°C. By securing robust intermetallic structures without triggering severe thermal agglomeration, catalysts synthesized within this optimal quantitative window exhibit high mass activities alongside exceptional long‐term stability. This quantitative mapping underscores that breaking the traditional thermal scaling relation of atomic ordering is a highly viable path for developing next‐generation PEMFC electrocatalysts.

External stabilization through protective surface layers has also proven effective. Various protective layer strategies (including carbon shells, Pt‐skin layers, inorganic barriers, and graphitic hollow spheres) have been reported. In particular, N‐doped carbon exhibits strong interfacial affinity with metal particles, preventing agglomeration and enhancing stability. Yoo et al. introduced bipyridine ligands into a Co–Pt complex to induce the spontaneous formation of an N‐doped carbon shell during thermal treatment (Figure 6c) [71]. Subsequent H2 annealing reconstructed a Pt‐skin layer, leading to superior durability. Likewise, Song et al. employed a small‐molecule‐assisted method to form a self‐generated carbon shell on Pt–Co intermetallic catalysts. During annealing, Pt–S bonds converted into Pt–Pt and Pt–Co bonds, simultaneously promote alloying and suppressing sintering. These protective‐layer approaches demonstrate an effective pathway to achieve intermetallic catalysts with both high activity and exceptional long‐term durability.

Our comparative analysis provides critical mechanistic insights. While a higher ordering degree generally correlates with performance exceeding DOE targets, several catalysts with relatively low ordering degree values still exhibit exceptional activity and durability. Evaluating these systems through the lens of EXAFS‐derived SRO reveals that it serves as a more precise and direct descriptor. Fundamentally, the sign and magnitude α of the value dictate the local atomic configuration: a positive α indicates a tendency toward elemental segregation (i.e., the localized clustering of identical atoms, such as Pt–Pt or TM–TM), whereas a decreasing, negative α signifies a strong thermodynamic preference for chemical ordering via alternating heteroatomic bonds. Consequently, catalysts exhibiting a lower (more negative) SRO value consistently demonstrate superior performance and stability. For Pt–TM alloy systems, the theoretical limit for maximum chemical ordering is an α value of −0.333, which corresponds to a maximized Pt–TM coordination number of 8 in a fully ordered L10 phase [22, 76]. Approaching this negative threshold signifies the robust establishment of Pt–TM bonds, highlighting that designing catalysts to maximize and maintain local Pt–TM coordination is highly advantageous for extreme activity and long‐term durability.

Furthermore, the compiled data indicates that TM dissolution (typically around 10 at% after end‐of‐life) does not exhibit a direct, linear correlation with either ordering degree or SRO. Because the evaluated order parameters are fundamentally Pt‐centric, ideal theoretical ordering implies that all TM atoms are stabilized by adjacent Pt atoms, which would heavily suppress dissolution. However, achieving such perfect homogeneity across entire polycrystalline nanoparticles is practically unfeasible. Consequently, the observed TM dissolution is primarily attributed to the degradation of localized TM–TM or TM–O bonds within segregated or less‐ordered domains, rather than the stable Pt–TM intermetallic anchors. This underscores that mitigating structural degradation requires not only enhancing Pt–TM SRO but also carefully minimizing the presence of isolated TM clusters within the nanoparticle.

Although LRO nanoalloys greatly enhance durability than random nanoalloys, their formation demands substantial thermal energy, restricting morphological control. By contrast, SRO can form at relatively low energies, it enables precise control over particle morphology, size, and structure. The work by Ma, Yanling et al. demonstrated that a disorder‐to‐order transition can be induced in 1D PtFe nanowires while preserving their morphology, and systematically analyzed the influence of annealing temperature and duration on structural deformation and atomic ordering (Figure 7) [77].

The emergence of surface atomic ordering was attributed to the reduced Gibbs free energy near the surface, arising from the enhanced thermal vibration of surface atoms, their lower coordination, and the weakened interatomic interactions (Figure 7a). Although such thermodynamic conditions enable phase transformation at the surface under low temperatures, the migration of the phase boundary between the newly formed ordered layer and the underlying random alloy is hindered by sluggish atomic diffusion within the nanowire interior. Consequently, extended annealing leads to the collapse of both ordering and morphology.

XRD patterns further indicate that the nanowires remain in a representative SRO state, as evidenced by the absence of superlattice reflections such as the (100) and (110) peaks, which would otherwise appear in a fully ordered intermetallic phase (Figure 7b). After annealing at 350°C for approximately 15 min, the surface of the PtFe nanowires transformed into an L12–Pt3Fe phase with a thickness of about three unit cells, forming a partially ordered surface layer confirmed by HAADF–STEM imaging (Figure 7c). In situ fast fourier transform (FFT) analysis also revealed that only fcc reflections were present below 350°C, whereas superlattice reflections ((100) and (010)) gradually emerged with prolonged annealing at 350°C.

Single‐cell measurements confirmed the structural advantages of the SRO configuration. The mass activity loss of Surf‐IM‐PtFe NWs after ADT was only ∼20.5%, compared to the 61.4% loss observed for commercial Pt/C. Post‐ADT analysis of the catalyst layers further showed that PtFe nanowires experienced severe Fe dissolution (up to 94.1%), while Surf‐IM‐PtFe NWs exhibited substantially suppressed Fe leaching, with less than half the loss of the PtFe NWs (Figure 7d).

Lee et al. reported the formation of dome–hollow NiPt–SP nanoalloys containing localized Ni3Pt5 intermetallic phases synthesized via an ultrasound‐assisted process, exhibiting relatively low energy compared to LRO structures (Figure 8) [64]. Ultrasonication‐induced cavitation and Kirkendall diffusion promoted atomic transposition, leading to the formation of this unique structure. Continuous ultrasonication during synthesis provided localized, short‐duration energy input, enabling partial ordering at low temperature and forming intragrain local chemical order. This structural feature was precisely verified using AET, extended EXAFS, and PDF analyses. AET results revealed a well‐defined Ni3Pt5 short range order phase located at the interface between the Ni‐rich surface and the Pt‐rich core. The short‐range order parameter (SROP) is introduced as a standardized metric based on the SRO, quantifying how closely a localized domain resembles a specific phase (e.g., Ni3Pt5 phase). Spatial mapping of the SROP visualized the atomic‐scale distribution of each phase, showing that Ni3Pt5 exhibited uniform SROP pattern. Strain tensor mapping confirmed the presence of tensile strain near Ni3Pt5 boundaries, demonstrating the direct correlation between local chemical ordering and strain distribution (Figure 8a). HR‐TEM and FFT analyses revealed multiple nanograins (∼2 nm) embedded within the alloy particles (Figure 8b). Radial compositional analysis showed that the Ni‐rich A1 phase was dominant near the surface and grain boundaries but decreased toward the interior, where Pt‐rich A1 and Ni3Pt5 phases became prevalent (Figure 8c). This locally ordered chemical distribution strongly influenced the electrochemical performance and durability. Under HDV operating conditions, the catalyst maintained its I–V curve shape across all voltages after 90,000 ADT cycles, with only ∼5% power loss and a Pt utilization efficiency of 3.4 kW/gPt, confirming outstanding long‐term stability (Figure 8d).

Perspective

The transition toward carbon neutrality, together with the rapid expansion of high‐power infrastructure, is creating unprecedented demand for reliable and efficient electricity. Among various technological options under consideration, hydrogen fuel cells remain a promising candidate for future power conversion. As discussed earlier, PEMFCs have already reached a level of technological majority that allows meaningful discussion on further performance enhancement, positioning them as one of the viable solutions for next‐generation energy systems.

To translate this potential into practical competitiveness, performance and durability targets far beyond the current state of the art must be achieved. Next‐generation PEMFCs are expected to operate stably at 94°C–120°C while meeting highly demanding properties, including a power efficiency above 72%, and delivering two or more fold increases in catalytic performance (e.g., 2 A/cm2 at 0.7 V, air fuel). In addition to these requirements, more than 50,000 h life time of structural and electrochemical stability under accelerated degradation protocols is needed, which corresponds to the level of “extreme catalysts”.

The most significant bottleneck in meeting these requirements lies in the catalyst layer, which remains the cost‐dominant component of the MEA. Reducing Pt loading while achieving high Pt utilization, and ensuring long‐term durability under operating conditions above 100°C, continues to be an essential yet unresolved challenge. These demands cannot be addressed through incremental adjustments to ordered alloy compositions or simple surface treatments. For this reason, more fundamental design strategies that integrate atomic arrangement and nanoscale morphology are required.

In this perspective, we propose atomic arrangement–morphology integration as a forward‐looking design principle for ORR catalysts. This approach simultaneously addresses active‐site configuration, which is influenced by local atomic arrangement, and the durability and mass‐transport characteristics governed by nanoparticle structure. MPEAs and HEAs offer notable advantages in this context. They can thermodynamically stabilize SRO within polycrystalline nanoparticles, maintain local order at relatively low annealing temperatures, and remain compatible with various morphological frameworks. At the same time, however, the compositional space of MPEAs and HEAs is extraordinarily large, making exhaustive experimental exploration impractical.

Recent developments in AI‐assisted materials exploration offer effective solutions. Importantly, current AI‐assisted HEA design does not yet generally predict the degree of short‐range or long‐range ordering directly. It mainly accelerates the screening of compositions and structure‐sensitive descriptors associated with local stability and catalytic function. For example, regression‐based AI models function as rapid predictors that estimate catalytic performance from descriptors such as cohesive energy, electronegativity, standard reduction potential, and interatomic distance as useful predictors of catalyst speciation, stability, and activity [81]. Similarly, machine‐learning methods that incorporate local site motifs and neighboring‐atom environments can capture characteristic elemental patterns relevant to adsorption energetics and catalytic behavior [82]. In addition, Bayesian optimization strategies help prioritize the most promising candidates, through representative models on DFT‐derived energetics, thereby greatly reducing the experimental load of catalyst discovery [83]. Although these descriptors do not directly quantify ordering degree, they reflect bond strength, reduction tendency, and local geometric/electronic effects that are closely related to ordering tendencies. Therefore, AI holds strong potential to evolve into an effective strategy for ordering‐aware MPEA/HEA catalyst design as more explicit ordering‐relevant descriptor sets become available.

In detail, machine‐learning‐accelerated study has demonstrated that strategically inducing topological disorder within the transition metal subsurface (e.g., Co–Cu/Ni disorder in ternary systems) provides critical thermodynamic stabilization [82]. This significantly lowers the activation energy required for the disorder‐to‐order transition, allowing AI to rationally design complex MPEA/HEA systems that achieve high degrees of chemical ordering at substantially reduced annealing temperatures. Crucially, this AI‐driven discovery unlocks the ultimate paradigm: the synergistic integration of atomic ordering with nanoscale shape control. By lowering the required annealing temperatures, delicate 3D morphologies can be protected from thermal degradation, successfully embedding robust chemical ordering within extrinsic‐efficient morphological structure.

To successfully realize these advanced designs, establishing practical compositional boundaries and standardized characterization workflows is imperative. Structurally, to maintain optimal compressive strain while preventing catastrophic TM dissolution in harsh acidic environments, the TM composition should be strictly limited to a maximum of 50% (i.e., adhering to the L10 stoichiometric boundary). Exceeding this limit inherently jeopardizes the integrity of the protective Pt‐skin. Analytically, we propose a multistep workflow: preemptively screening the bulk “degree of alloying” via XRD or wide‐angle X‐ray diffraction (WAXD) with actual elements composition using Vegard's law, followed by quantifying the precise SRO parameter via EXAFS or PDF to evaluate its proximity to the theoretical maximum.

Despite these conceptual advances, significant controversies and unresolved issues remain regarding the precise identification and dynamic evolution of MPEA/HEA SRO. Although recent pioneering studies have successfully demonstrated the power of AET for intuitive local atomic visualization, its highly demanding analytical procedures make it practically challenging to apply universally. Specifically, the fact that AET struggles to accurately differentiate between adjacent transition metals with similar atomic numbers presents a critical flaw for MPEA/HEA systems. Consequently, for these systems, 3D energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (3D EDS) depth tomography emerges as a much more suitable and practical alternative to map nanoscale elemental distributions.

More importantly, local chemical ordering is not a static property. Under severe electrochemical potentials and continuous intermediate adsorptions during fuel cell operation, the atomic ordering undergoes dynamic structural reconstruction. Consequently, bridging the gap between the synthesized atomic structure and its real operando configuration represents the ultimate frontier. Overcoming this challenge will require tracking the dynamic retention of the Pt–TM coordination or α value via in‐situ/operando EXAFS, offering a highly reliable, statistically significant predictor for evaluating the true efficacy of durability enhancement strategies in next‐generation extreme catalysts.

Conclusion

Research on PEMFC catalysts has developed through three main stages that include alloying, morphology control, and the formation of atomic ordering structures. However, the actual durability of nanoscale catalysts is not determined by the presence of an specific phase. It depends on how well atomic order is formed and maintained. Nanoparticles that do not possess sufficient atomic ordering cannot ensure structural stability. As low‐temperature atomic rearrangement methods continue to advance, the next generation of catalyst design will focus on creating morphological structures that maximize active sites while maintaining atomic order.

In addition to bimetallic catalysts, MPE alloys also rely on atomic ordering. Low‐temperature synthesis approaches will be essential for improving stability without sacrificing activity. This change represents a move from a phase‐centered approach to a site‐centered design concept. Such a shift will be important for achieving durable PEMFC catalysts that can operate reliably under higher temperature operation than current stauts and will help accelerate the commercialization of fuel cell technologies.
