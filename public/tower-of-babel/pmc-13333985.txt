Quantifying whiteness tolerance for fluorescent textiles using color-difference formulas
Authors: Mahdi Safi, Razieh Jafari, Fatemeh Taheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is currently no standardized method for evaluating color differences between white textile samples, and existing whiteness indices are not sufficient for accurately classifying fabrics treated with fluorescent whitening agents. This study presents a reliable approach for defining industrial tolerance limits by combining instrumental color-difference formulas (∆E*ab, ∆ECMC, ∆E94, ∆E2000) with visual assessments. Chemically bleached cotton fabrics were treated with two fluorescent whitening agents—Texbrite BYB and Ludox White 2B—at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.0% (o.w.f). Measurements included whiteness index, tint factor, and color differences. Pass/Fail thresholds were established using whiteness and color difference values, aligned with a 70% visual acceptance rate. Texbrite BYB showed wider tolerance limits (ΔE94 = 0.77, ΔWI = 4.82), but its whiteness exceeded the upper validity range of the CIE whiteness formula. Ludox White 2B had stricter limits (ΔE94 = 0.45, ΔWI = 1.57) and delivered more consistent visual results, with a 46% higher acceptance rate. A strong correlation (R2 > 0.75) between whiteness index differences and color difference values confirms that whiteness difference can serve as a practical indicator of perceived color variation. These results can be used as practical benchmarks for quality control in fluorescent-whitened textiles, particularly for maintaining consistent visual appearance in industrial applications.Keywords: Industrial tolerance limit, Whiteness index, Fluorescent whitening agent, Color difference formulae, Visual assessmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The visual environment we are surrounded by consists of chromatic and achromatic colors. Chromatic colors are those colors that display a variety of hues, contributing to the variety and vibrancy we experience in our colored world1. On the other hand, achromatic colors, such as those with black, white, and different shades of grey, do not display hues and are differentiated based on lightness levels, ideally2. However, achromatic colors, even though they do not have hues, have an important functional and aesthetic use in different industries. For instance, in the textile industry, the use of black and white colors is very common, especially in the production of clothing materials, due to their versatility and aesthetic value3,4. Moreover, in the automotive industry, achromatic colors are very common in the production of vehicles, especially for exterior use, due to their aesthetic value and acceptability by car manufacturers and users5. In the printing and paper industries, achromatic colors such as black, white, and grey play a vital functional and aesthetic role, especially in digital and physical printouts and papers6,7. These neutral colors serve not only practical purposes but also contribute to design balance and visual hierarchy in a wide array of applications1. Ideal white samples are those that exhibit maximum lightness while lacking any chroma or hue components. In the field of colorimetry, whiteness is defined as the extent to which a surface mimics the behavior of a perfect reflecting diffuser; an ideal surface that uniformly reflects all incident light without introducing any coloration8. However, in practical applications, white materials are often found to have tint characteristics. The slight color characteristics are classified as bluish white, reddish white, or yellowish white, depending on the tint present in the material9.

The creation of appropriate white raw materials plays a vital role in the textile industry, not only in enhancing the visual lightness of fabrics but also in establishing a suitable base for subsequent dyeing and printing, especially when dealing with natural fibers10. The effectiveness of this process is notably amplified through the use of fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs), which elevate the total radiance factor (TRF) of the treated fabric within the visible spectrum, thereby enhancing its whiteness perception11,12.

Optical brightening agents (OBAs), also referred to as fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs), are luminescent substances commonly used to enhance the visual whiteness and brightness of materials. They function by absorbing ultraviolet light, specifically within the UV-A range (340–370 nm), and subsequently emitting light in the blue region of the visible spectrum (400–450 nm), resulting in a cleaner, fresher, and more vibrant appearance13–16.

The psychological association between the color white and qualities like purity, cleanness, and hygiene has a significant impact on the quality and aesthetic value of the product and its value in the market. Moreover, the association of the color with the absence of impurities and the presence of neatness and sanitation is significant9. Considering the significance of the material and its use in various industries, the evaluation of the whiteness of the material is considered important17–19. Industries like the textile industry, paper production, printing, dentistry, detergents, and so on use the evaluation of the whiteness of the material significantly6,17,18,20–23. This is because the level of whiteness of the material has a significant impact on the quality of the product and the perception of cleanness and value of the product12. To get the results with the highest level of accuracy and reliability, the evaluation of the whiteness of the material is conducted by both visual and instrumental methods12. Whiteness assessment through visual means relies on the observer’s conventional comparison of a test sample against a reference under controlled lighting conditions and standardized viewing geometry within a designated light booth12,24,25. In contrast, instrumental techniques employ spectrophotometry to quantify the reflectance or total radiance factor of white materials, enabling the calculation of their colorimetric properties for use in various whiteness formulas26,27. In 1986, the International Commission on Illumination (CIE) formally introduced two quantitative measures: whiteness index (WI) and tinting factor (Tw), to standardize the evaluation of white samples. These metrics, defined by Eqs. 1 and 2, were designed to capture both the degree of whiteness and any subtle coloration (tint) present in materials. The WI value reflects how closely a sample approaches ideal whiteness, while the Tw value reflects subtle coloration in white samples, where positive values indicate a greenish tint and negative values correspond to a reddish hue. Together, these indices provide a more comprehensive framework for assessing whiteness in a range of industrial and scientific applications28–32.

In Eq. 1, Y represents the lightness level of the white sample in the equation. The coordinates for (x₀,y₀) are those of the chromaticity value of our illuminant, and for (x,y), our chromaticity value of the object is equal to the above standard. A Tw value of zero corresponds to a bluish-white appearance. Additionally, the CIE introduced two threshold conditions—outlined in criteria 3 and 4—that must be simultaneously satisfied for a sample to be classified as white based on its WI and Tw values19,28,32.

Although the CIE whiteness and tinting formulas are widely used and generally indicate a reasonable agreement with visual evaluation, there are, however, several important limitations to the use of these formulas for industrial quality control purposes. First, the range of validity for the CIE Whiteness Index (WI) is limited to a particular range of chromaticity coordinates and luminance values, beyond which the formula may give invalid or physically unreasonable results28. This is especially important for FWAs, since the R% values for the blue region may exceed 100%, owing to the emission properties of fluorescent whitening agents8,26. Secondly, the CIE system treats whiteness (WI) and tint (TW) as separate values, while the human eye perceives these properties holistically. Thus, while a sample may fall outside the prescribed limits for the CIE, such a sample may still be perceived as white12. Finally, the strict limits prescribed by the CIE system fail to take into account any application-specific tolerance requirements, an important consideration for industrial applications, where some factors may be either critical or irrelevant depending on the application33.

These limitations highlight the need for alternative or complementary approaches that integrate instrumental measurements with visual perception, particularly for fluorescent materials. For example, specimens with exceptionally high lightness (Y) may fail to satisfy the condition specified in threshold 3, while those exhibiting noticeable greenish or reddish tint may be disqualified under threshold 4, even if they are perceived as white by human observers.

While several whiteness and yellowness indices have been developed, including CIE Whiteness (WI), ASTM E313, Hunter Whiteness, and Berger Whiteness, their applicability varies depending on the optical characteristics of the material26,28,29,31,32,34. As presented in Table 1, the whiteness indices commonly reported in the literature differ substantially in both their mathematical formulation and their sensitivity to fluorescence-related spectral contributions. Among these, the CIE whiteness index (WI) stands apart as the only index that simultaneously incorporates luminance (Y) and chromaticity coordinates (x, y) within the CIE 1931 colorimetric framework, thereby enabling it to detect subtle but perceptually meaningful spectral shifts induced by fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs)28.

Indices such as Hunter Whiteness and Stensby Whiteness are fundamentally rooted in the Hunter Lab color space and rely primarily on the lightness–yellowness relationship34,35. While this approach is adequate for non-fluorescent substrates, it is inherently insensitive to the upward conversion of UV radiation into visible blue emission that characterizes FWA-treated materials17,18. Similarly, ASTM E313, despite its widespread industrial application, is derived from tristimulus weighting functions optimized for yellowness detection and does not account for the anomalous reflectance behavior observed in the blue spectral region (400–450 nm), where total radiance factor values may exceed 100% as a direct consequence of fluorescence emission17,18,36. The use of such indices in the context of optically brightened textiles therefore risks systematic underestimation or misrepresentation of true visual whiteness37,38.

The Berger and Ganz–Griesser indices offer partial improvements by incorporating blue-region reflectance weighting and perceptual calibration, respectively39–41, however, neither achieves the chromaticity-coordinate sensitivity required for rigorous characterization of fluorescent systems under standardized illumination conditions29,37.

The selection of WI in the present study was therefore made on both methodological and empirical grounds. From a methodological standpoint, the CIE index is the only formulation formally recommended by the International Commission on Illumination for the assessment of near-white fluorescent specimens, provided that measurements are performed under D65 illumination with a 10° standard observer28,42. From an empirical standpoint, the findings of the present study confirm a strong and statistically significant correlation between ΔWI values (calculated from WICIE) and perceptual color differences expressed as ΔE17,18,38,43, supporting its applicability to reflect visually perceptible whiteness variation with high fidelity.

In summary, the adoption of the CIE whiteness index in this work is not merely conventional practice but is scientifically justified by the fluorescent nature of the specimens examined, the chromaticity sensitivity of its formulation, and its alignment with international standardization frameworks28,36,42.

Objective

One of the biggest problems associated with quality control in white materials is the lack of specified tolerance values. Unlike colored materials, whose quality control and evaluation are carried out using specified formulas for determining differences in colors and corresponding tolerance values, white materials do not have specified values for quality control and evaluation. This has made it difficult to determine whether a white material meets specified visual requirements or not, especially when such differences are not pronounced but evident.

To address this issue, the present study aims to establish a Pass/Fail criterion by defining tolerance limits33,44,45 for white samples treated with fluorescent whitening agents. Drawing on the principles and tolerance structures used in color-difference evaluation, the study proposes quantitative acceptance criteria tailored to optical brightening agents. By comparing the treated samples with standardized references or control specimens, the investigation aims to identify parameters that are correlated with the results of the visual assessment. The establishment of these parameters will help in the standardization of optical whitening agents, and the manufacturer will be able to maintain the whiteness levels in the production of textile materials.

Experimental

Samples preparation

Chemically bleached woven cotton fabric served as the experimental substrate for this investigation. Two distinct fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs), Texbrite BYB Conce (Fluorescent Whitening Agent 86) and Ludox White 2B (Fluorescent Whitening Agent 24), were selected to represent two different classes of optical brighteners with distinct spectral and photophysical characteristics. Texbrite BYB, a stilbene-based triazine compound with a broad emission band (FWHM ≈ 85 nm) is known for its relatively higher fluorescence emission intensity and broader emission band in the blue region, which can result in higher whiteness values and greater chromatic variability at increased concentrations. In contrast, Ludox White 2B, is a diaminostilbene derivative with a narrower emission profile (FWHM ≈ 65 nm) that exhibits comparatively narrower emission spectra and more stable chromaticity behavior, leading to more controlled whiteness development. The selection of these two agents was therefore intentional, allowing the investigation of tolerance limits under two different whitening response profiles: a highly responsive system (Texbrite BYB) and a more stable and controlled system (Ludox White 2B). Such a difference offers a broader framework for assessing the relationship between instrumental differences in colors and visual perception in fluorescent materials. Additionally, the use of FWAs with different fluorescence efficiencies and spectral bandwidths allows for assessing the effect of fluorescence on whiteness index and color difference tolerances.

They were applied to the cotton fabrics at varying concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.0% on weight of fabric (o.w.f%) in incremental steps of 0.1%. The optical bleaching process was conducted under alkaline conditions utilizing a liquor-to-fabric ratio of 30:1, with pH maintained between 8.0 and 9.0. Treatment duration was standardized at 45 min under boiling temperature conditions.

Measurement

The total radiance factor (R%) spectra of the samples were measured using a GretagMacbeth Color-Eye 7000A spectrophotometer (X-Rite, USA) under di:8° (diffuse illumination, 8° observation) geometry, in accordance with CIE Publication 15 and ISO 13,655 standards with the specular component included (SCI). Measurements were performed using a large area view (LAV) aperture to ensure representative sampling of the textile surface. Given the presence of fluorescent whitening agents, all measurements were conducted under UV-included conditions to capture fluorescence emission effects accurately. The inclusion of UV energy is critical in evaluating optically brightened materials, as exclusion of UV radiation may lead to significant underestimation of whiteness and fluorescence effects. For fluorescent materials, the measured quantity is more accurately described as the total radiance factor (R%), as it includes both reflected and fluorescence-emitted components, which may exceed 100% in the blue region. Therefore, the spectral radiometric data for chemically and optically bleached white materials was measured and presented as a total radiance factor (R%) over a range of 360–750 nm at 10 nm intervals, with an effective spectral bandwidth of 10 nm. Before measurement, calibration was performed using a white and black standard, and a fluorescent calibration tile was used. To ensure the reliability and repeatability of the measurement, multiple measurements for some samples showed that there was no significant change.

Then, the colorimetric characteristics of white specimens over the CIExyY and CIELAB color spaces were computed using the measured total radiance factor (R%) space under the D65 standard illuminant and 10-degree standard observer. Next, the CIE whiteness index (WI) as well as the tint factor (Tw) of white samples were computed using Eqs. 1 and 2, respectively. Finally, the color difference values between the optically bleached samples and the reference one (chemically bleached white fabric) were calculated based on ∆E*ab, ∆ECMC, ∆E94, and ∆E2000 color difference formulae. Besides, the differences between the whiteness attribute (WI) of samples and the reference were computed.

Visual assessments

Visual assessment experiments were conducted using a standardized Verivide light booth to ensure controlled viewing conditions. All fabric specimens were precisely cut and prepared to uniform dimensions of 5 × 10 cm2. A panel of 25 observers from a textile manufacturing facility participated in the evaluation process, with a mean age of 36 years (range: 24–44 years). Before assessment, all observers underwent screening for normal color vision using the Ishihara color vision test46, a widely used diagnostic tool designed to detect red–green color vision deficiencies through pseudoisochromatic plates. Only observers who correctly identified all test plates were included in the study to ensure reliable visual discrimination performance.

Ethical approval and informed consent: This study involved low-risk perceptual assessments with no physical intervention or sensitive data collection. As no formal ethics committee is designated for this category of research at our institution, the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each observer prior to participation.

The evaluation protocol employed a paired comparison methodology, wherein observers assessed each optically bleached fabric specimen against a designated reference sample. Observers were instructed to select “pass” for sample pairs demonstrating acceptable similarity or “fail” for pairs exhibiting discernible differences. All visual assessments were performed under standardized D65 illumination with ultraviolet light conditions, utilizing a 0°/45° illumination/observation geometry configuration in accordance with international standards (Fig. 1). Because fluorescent compounds are highly sensitive to light, especially UV rays, we ensured that the light source intensity remained stable and consistent inside the light cabinet during all visual assessments. Following the paired comparison statistical framework, where n samples require n(n−1)/2 comparative evaluations, a total of 2250 individual visual assessments were completed across all sample combinations.

Results and discussion

Spectral analysis

The total radiance factor (TRFs) of the samples treated with Texbrite BYB and Ludox white 2B fluorescent whitening agents at various concentrations (0.1–1.0% o.w.f.) compared with a chemically bleached reference sample are plotted in Fig. 2. Both fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs) exhibit typical Stokes‑shifted behavior, with UV absorption maxima at 340–370 nm and blue emission peaks at 420–450 nm. Texbrite BYB also displays a broader emission spectrum (FWHM ≈ 85 nm) than Ludox (FWHM ≈ 65 nm), indicating differences in chromophore structure and excited‑state dynamics. As shown in the figure, Texbrite BYB presents a near-neutral to bluish base tone, which can be linked to the dip in spectral total radiance factor (R%) observed between 510 and 620 nm.

Spectral total radiance factor (R%) vs. wavelength for cotton samples treated with Texbrite BYB and Ludox White 2B (0.1–1.0% o.w.f.), compared to the bleached reference.

A comparison of the total radiance factor (R%) curves of Texbrite BYB, Ludox White 2B, and the untreated reference sample reveals significant differences in the blue region of the visible spectrum (approximately 400–450 nm). In this region, both FWAs exhibit total radiance factor (R%) values exceeding 100%, which is attributed to fluorescence emission. Notably, Texbrite BYB shows a broader and more intense emission band compared to Ludox White 2B. This broader emission leads to greater variability in spectral distribution and consequently increased sensitivity to concentration changes. As a result, Texbrite BYB exhibits wider tolerance limits in both ΔE and ΔWI metrics. In contrast, Ludox White 2B indicates a narrower and more stable emission profile, resulting in more consistent spectral behavior and stricter tolerance limits.

It must be noted, however, that in the presence of fluorescent whitening agents, the obtained total radiance factor (R%) values can be higher than 100% in the region of the visible spectrum corresponding to the color blue. This is not an effect of reflectance but rather an effect of fluorescence emission, in which absorbed ultraviolet radiation is re-emitted as visible blue light, thus adding additional radiative energy to the signal. In other words, the signal obtained is the total radiance factor (TRF). This behavior is a well-established characteristic of optically brightened materials and plays a critical role in the interpretation of whiteness measurements and visual perception, particularly in materials treated with FWAs where conventional reflectance assumptions are no longer valid.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, both fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs) are positioned within the blue–red quadrant of the color space, characterized by negative b* and positive a* values. In contrast, the reference sample remains near neutral, exhibiting only a slight yellow tint. Compared to Ludox White 2B, Texbrite BYB displays a more pronounced reddish–blueish tint, with similar a* shifts but a slightly greater negative b* displacement. Additionally, Texbrite BYB demonstrates higher chroma (C*) and lower lightness (L*) than Ludox White 2B, and both FWAs yield greater chroma than the chemically bleached reference. Notably, variations in Texbrite BYB concentration result in more significant changes in both L* and C* values than those observed with Ludox White 2B.

To better understand the origin of these differences, the variation in chromatic coordinates, particularly b*, was further analyzed. The relatively wide color differences observed in Fig. 3 can be primarily attributed to variations in the CIE b* coordinate. As is well established, the b* axis represents the blue–yellow chromatic balance, which is particularly sensitive in textiles treated with fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs). Even small shifts toward negative b* values (increased blueness) can lead to perceptually significant changes in whiteness, especially under UV-inclusive illumination conditions. In the present study, the FWAs induce non-linear changes in the spectral total radiance factor (R%) within the short-wavelength (blue) region, resulting in enhanced fluorescence emission and consequently more negative b* values. This effect amplifies the perceived whiteness but simultaneously increases the variability of color difference values (ΔE), particularly at higher FWA concentrations. Therefore, the wider tolerance limits reported here are strongly correlated with fluctuations in b*, rather than uniform shifts across all color coordinates. This finding highlights that, in FWA-treated systems, b* serves as the dominant contributor to color difference and should be carefully controlled in industrial quality assessment, especially when defining acceptable tolerance limits.

Whiteness–concentration correlation

Figure 4 illustrates the influence of concentration on the whiteness index for two optical brightening agents.

Whiteness index as a function of concentration (WIRef = 69.01).

As can be seen in Fig. 4, the whiteness index for Texbrite BYB increases from 126.93 to 155.86, while for Ludox White 2B, it increases from 111.40 to 137.69 over the range of concentrations from 1 to 10 g/L. At all points, i.e., minimum, midpoint, and maximum, it can be seen that for Texbrite BYB, the whiteness index is greater than for Ludox White 2B. The concentration–response curves, with zero concentration representing the chemically bleached control, confirm that both FWAs significantly enhance WI relative to the control, with Texbrite BYB consistently outperforming Ludox White 2B. The observed trends in whiteness values and spectral total radiance factor (R%) can be explained by the optical behavior of fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs). FWAs absorb ultraviolet radiation and re-emit it in the blue region of the visible spectrum, which enhances the perceived whiteness by shifting the color appearance toward a bluer hue. This fluorescence contribution increases the total radiance factor (TRF), particularly in the short-wavelength region, leading to total radiance factor (R%) values that may exceed 100%. As a result, variations in whiteness indices are not solely governed by the intrinsic reflectance of the substrate but are significantly influenced by the concentration and efficiency of FWAs, the spectral distribution of the illumination source, and the interaction between excitation and emission processes. Therefore, the differences observed in the results are primarily attributed to fluorescence intensity, UV content of the illumination, and the sensitivity of the whiteness metrics to blue enhancement effects. Both exhibit a slight red tint within the red–blue region of the CIELAB color space; however, Texbrite BYB shows greater chroma and higher total radiance factor (R%) across the visible spectrum. Based on combined criteria (WI and visual pass rate) in Fig. 4, an optimal application level for both agents is approximately 2 g/L (0.2%).

Visual assessment of whiteness

Twenty‑five trained assessors compared ten Texbrite BYB‑treated and ten Ludox White 2B‑treated fabrics in 45 paired combinations per treatment, using a paired‑comparison method with a > 70% agreement threshold for “Pass”. The results are reported in Tables 5 and 6. This psychophysical evaluation revealed that Ludox White 2B achieved a 46% higher pass rate than Texbrite BYB (35.6% vs. 24.4%), indicating greater whiteness uniformity and smoother concentration-dependent transitions. Both FWAs were visually distinct from the chemically bleached reference, confirming their effectiveness in producing perceptible whiteness enhancement. Texbrite BYB showed a sharp acceptability threshold near 0.3% o.w.f., while Ludox White 2B exhibited a more gradual, controlled response. Statistical analysis confirmed lower variability, more high‑agreement pairs, and broader stability for Ludox White 2B. Both agents maintained acceptable visual tolerance for concentration variations ≤ 0.1% owf within their optimal ranges, consistent with perceptual threshold behavior and just‑noticeable‑difference limits. The results demonstrate that visual acceptance did not always align with instrumental measurements: some pairs with small CIE color differences were rejected, while others with relatively large differences were accepted. To establish an objective tolerance threshold, the method introduced by Berns33,44,45 was applied. These observed differences must be explained taking into account the theoretical aspects of the color difference models being used. ∆E*ab calculates the Euclidean distance in CIELAB space and does not take into account the non-uniformities of human perception for color difference. In contrast, the ΔE94, ΔECMC and ΔE2000 color difference formulas incorporate weighting functions to help account for the lightness, chroma, and hue components when considering the perceptual characteristics of each of those components; thus, they will provide superior perceptually uniform results to the ∆E*ab formula, especially within near neutral areas. The current data suggest that ΔE94, ΔECMC and ΔE2000 provide a better fit between visual evaluation and the measurements than the ∆E*ab does. This is especially evident for Ludox White 2B, where narrower tolerance limits and more consistent Pass/Fail classifications were observed. The results indicate that advanced color-difference formulas are more suitable for evaluating subtle variations in fluorescent whitened textiles. Furthermore, in high-lightness and low-chroma regions typical of white samples, perceptual sensitivity is strongly influenced by small variations along the blue–yellow axis (b*). Since fluorescent whitening agents primarily enhance total radiance factor (R%) in the blue region, even minor shifts in b* can lead to noticeable visual differences. This explains the improved predictive performance of perceptually weighted formulas such as ΔE2000 in the present study.

The evaluation produced distinct pass-fail values for the two whiteness references. Acceptable samples of Texbrite BYB were measured at color differences less than ΔE94 = 0.77, ΔECMC = 0.91, ΔE2000 = 0.91, and ΔE*ab = 1.26. Ludox White 2B had stricter limits for acceptable samples of ΔE94 = 0.45, ΔECMC = 0.61, ΔE2000 = 0.62, and ΔE*ab = 0.61. Ludox White 2B had stricter visual acceptance criteria than Texbrite BYB, needing approximately 18–33% smaller ΔE values across all color difference formulas. This reduction demonstrates Ludox’s higher sensitivity to color discrimination.

Using the differences in whiteness index between the pairs, together with the counts of acceptable (‘Pass’) and unacceptable (‘Fail’) results, the cumulative percentage of pairs was determined. Following the method proposed by Berns33, cumulative percentage curves were plotted for the two fluorescent agents as a function of the whiteness index difference. According to his approach, instrumental colorimetric data are combined with visual assessments, where observers classify sample–standard pairs as ‘Pass’ or ‘Fail’ under controlled lighting using a near-gray anchor. These judgments are then aligned with CIE94 color differences, sorted, and expressed as cumulative percentages. The intersection of the Pass and Fail curves represents the optimized tolerance limit, minimizing the likelihood of erroneous decisions. The results are plotted in Fig. 6.

The observed differences in tolerance limits can also be interpreted in light of the characteristics of different whiteness indices. Since fluorescent whitening agents significantly enhance total radiance factor (R%) in the blue region (often exceeding 100%), indices with higher sensitivity to chromaticity shifts, such as WICIE, provide a more accurate representation of perceptual whiteness. This explains the strong correlation observed between ΔWI and ΔE values in the present study.

When ΔWI is plotted against the four ΔE values, both agents show strong positive linear relationships with correlation coefficients > 0.75. With regard to Texbrite BYB, it is noted that all four formulas give very similar regression slope and intercept values. In contrast, for Ludox White 2B, ΔE*ab and ΔECMC yield the steepest slopes and highest R2 values, suggesting these formulas best capture whiteness-related variation for this agent. Texbrite BYB’s regression lines are generally steeper than those of Ludox White 2B, reflecting greater perceptual variability per unit change in whiteness, whereas Ludox White 2B’s flatter slopes imply subtler visual changes. Conclusion

This study systematically investigated whiteness perception and color difference behavior in fluorescent-whitened textiles using combined instrumental and visual approaches. The results indicate that spectral total radiance factor (R%) values above 100% in the blue region significantly contribute to the enhancement of perceived whiteness, simultaneously causing non-linear fluctuations in colorimetric coordinates.

The CIE b* coordinate was found to be the most important factor that affected color differences, especially when UV light was present. Adding more fluorescent whitening agents (FWAs) made the b* values more negative, which made the whiteness better but also made the color difference tolerances wider. A comparative analysis of various FWAs demonstrated that their emission characteristics substantially influence both the perception of whiteness and tolerance thresholds, highlighting the necessity of meticulous FWA selection in industrial contexts.

A validated evaluation framework was proposed by integrating instrumental measurements with visual assessment, enabling the definition of practical pass/fail criteria based on ΔE and whiteness index difference (ΔWI). The results showed a strong relationship (R2 > 0.75) between ΔWI and different ΔE metrics, which confirmed that ΔWI is a good predictor of how people see color changes. Texbrite BYB had higher levels of whiteness and wider tolerance limits, but Ludox White 2B had more controlled performance, better compliance with standards, and a higher visual acceptance rate. This shows that people are more consistent in how they see whiteness.

The study shows that traditional whiteness indices have some problems and stresses the need for a more thorough evaluation method that takes into account spectral behavior and fluorescence effects. The suggested framework provides a useful and adaptable way to set strong quality control standards for making fluorescent-whitened textiles in factories.
