Green extraction and partial purification of roselle (Hibiscus sabdariffa L.) extracts with high amounts of phytochemicals and in vitro antioxidant and antibacterial effects
Authors: Namthip Chongwilaikasem, Patchima Sithisarn, Piyanuch Rojsanga, Pongtip Sithisarn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study is to develop roselle (Hibiscus sabdariffa L.) extracts with high amounts of phytochemicals and in vitro antioxidant and antibacterial effects using green extraction technique. Roselle extract prepared by ultrasonic extraction with 80% ethanol and 1% hydrochloric acid (RSUEH) promoted the highest yield and high total phenolic (5.21 ± 0.05 g% gallic acid equivalent of dried extract), total flavonoid (1.29 ± 0.06 g% quercetin equivalent of dried extract), and total anthocyanin contents (0.21 ± 0.00 and 0.44 ± 0.00 g% cyanidin 3‐glucoside equivalent of dried extract determined by pH‐differential method with standard calculation and pH‐differential method with standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside, respectively). From the validated high‐performance liquid chromatography analysis, delphinidin‐3‐sambubioside, delphinidin‐3‐glucoside, and cyanidin‐3‐glucoside were found to be the major anthocyanins in roselle extract. This extract exhibited moderate 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging effect (EC50 289.61 ± 0.16 µg/mL) and strong inhibitory effects against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus intermedius, Proteus mirabilis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa with the inhibitory concentration (IC50) values in the range of 0.15 ± 0.05 to 4.64 ± 0.07 mg/mL and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values ranged from 1.66 ± 0.06 to 7.11 ± 1.05 mg/mL. From partial purification, fraction 28 (F28) contained higher amounts of all analyzed phytochemicals than roselle extracts and promoted stronger DPPH scavenging effects (EC50 54.88 ± 2.23 µg/mL). F28 showed antibacterial activity with IC50 values against all tested bacteria in the range of 0.19 ± 0.07 to 4.69 ± 0.09 mg/mL, and the MIC values ranged from 2.46 ± 0.08 to 6.24 ± 0.09 mg/mL.

INTRODUCTION

Hibiscus sabdariffa L., commonly known as roselle, is a species belongs to the Malvaceae family. This species has originated in Africa, then it has been widely distributed to the tropical parts of the world, especially the countries in South America, Australia, and Asian countries, including India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand (Ilyas et al., 2021). The largest producing countries of roselle in Asia are China and Thailand (Mohamed, 2021).

The calyces of roselle have been used in traditional medicines and commonly consumed as food and drinks. Infusions of calyces of roselle have been widely used in traditional medicines of many countries (Islam et al., 2021). In India, Africa, and Mexico, they are traditionally used for diuretic, febrifugal, and hypotensive effects (Mohamed, 2021). In Egypt and Sudan, they have been used to lower the body temperature. For Thai traditional medicine, roselle has been used as antibacterial, hypocholesterolemia, diuretic, mild laxative, and antihypertensive agents (Chumsri et al., 2008). According to Thai Herbal Pharmacopoeia 2021, the calyces of roselle have been indicated as diuretic agent (Department of Medical Sciences, Ministry of Public Health, 2021). Moreover, roselle calyces have also been used as coloring and flavoring agents for food, cosmetics, and health care products (Chumsri et al., 2008).

Antioxidants protect human body by inhibition against free radicals, especially reactive oxygen species (ROSs). The excess amounts of ROSs could lead to the generation of oxidative stress, which could be the causes of various pathologies and diseases such as cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular, and neurological diseases (Pham‐Huy et al., 2008). Natural antioxidants from dietaries and plants have gained high attention. Roselle calyces contained polyphenolic acids, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, which are potent antioxidants (Wu et al., 2018). The aqueous extracts of roselle calyx showed in vitro antioxidant effects determined by various methods (Abebe et al., 2019; Hopkins et al., 2013).

Various infectious diseases result in health problems due to increasing strains of microorganisms and developing resistance to antibiotics. It has created serious global public health threats that exert a significant burden in terms of patient morbidity and financial crises (Kaper et al., 2004). Moreover, the prescription of antimicrobial drugs for bacterial infections is the standard treatments not only for human uses but also for animal therapies. Therefore, the new antimicrobial substances from plant sources for veterinary medicine have received a lot of attention in recent years. Roselle calyx methanol extracts have been reported to have antibacterial activities against various gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria (Abdallah, 2016).

There are several bioactive phytochemicals found in roselle, including phenolic acids (hydroxybenzoic and hydroxycinnamic acids), flavonoids (flavonols, flavanols, flavones, flavanones, and anthocyanins), and others (Riaz & Chopra, 2018). Anthocyanins are compounds responsible for the red color of roselle calyx (Pinela et al., 2019). Many factors have been reported to affect the stability of anthocyanins, such as pH, temperature, enzymes, light, and oxygen, especially in the extraction process for preparation of the red cabbage extract (Ghareaghajlou et al., 2021). Green extraction is related to the development of extraction processes using less energy and environment friendly solvents and ensure safe and high‐quality extract products (Chemat et al., 2012). Some processes including ultrasound‐assisted, homogenizer‐assisted, and microwave‐assisted extractions are classified as the green extraction methods (Gunes et al., 2020). In this study, the green extractions and partial purification were conducted to develop roselle extracts and fractions with high yield and high active constituent contents and biological activities.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of plant material

The dried roselle calyces were purchased from local shop in Bangkok, Thailand, in October 2021. Roselle samples were identified according to their botanical characteristics using the identification key described in Flora of Thailand volume 14, part 2, 2019 (Phuphathanaphong et al., 2019) (Voucher No. N. Chongwilaikasem 001). Species voucher specimen was kept in the Thai Traditional Medicine Herbarium, Department of Thai Traditional and Alternative Medicine, Thailand (Voucher No. TTM‐c No. 1000792). The species samples were cleaned and dried in a hot air oven at 60°C for 2 h and ground into a powder by an electric grinder (500 g multifunction disintegrator WF‐10B). Roselle powder was kept in the desiccator at room temperature until used.

Preparation of roselle extracts

Roselle calyx powders were firstly prepared by four different extraction methods—decoction, digestion, ultrasonic extraction, and maceration—with four green solvents: water, 1% hydrochloric acid (HCl) in water, 80% ethanol (EtOH), and 1% HCl in 80% EtOH. All extracts were phytochemically analyzed for total phenolic, total flavonoid, and total anthocyanin contents (TACs), and then the suitable extraction method was selected. After that, the suitable extraction solvent was evaluated by using the selected extraction methods with the variation of extraction solvents. Roselle extract from suitable extraction method and solvent was then selected for further partial purification and determination of in vitro biological activities. The processes for preparation of roselle extract were as follows:

Maceration

Roselle calyx powders were macerated with 80% EtOH (plant/solvent ratio 1:10 w/v) and shaken by a rotary shaker (GenLab) at room temperature, 150 rpm for 60 min. The extraction process was repeated three times. The supernatants were filtered through Whatman No. 1, then the filtrates were combined and evaporated to dryness by a rotary evaporator (BUCHI). The percentage of extract (%yield) was calculated. The extract was kept at −20°C until use.

Digestion

Roselle calyx powders were digested with deionized water at 60°C (plant/solvent ratio 1:10 w/v) for 60 min. The extraction process was repeated three times. The supernatants were filtered through Whatman No. 1, then the filtrates were combined and dried on a water bath (Memmert). The percentage of extract (%yield) was calculated. The extract was kept at −20°C until use.

Decoction

Roselle calyx powders were boiled with deionized water at 100°C (plant/solvent ratio 1:10 w/v) for 60 min. The extraction process was repeated three times. The supernatants were filtered through Whatman No. 1, then the filtrates were combined and dried on a water bath (Memmert). The percentage of extract (%yield) was calculated. The extract was kept at −20°C until use.

Ultrasound‐assisted extraction

Roselle calyx powders in 80% EtOH, 80% EtOH with 1% HCl, water, and water with 1% HCl were treated with the ultrasonic system under the following conditions: the extraction time (60 min), ultrasonic power (P 280 W; at a frequency of 37 kHz), the plant/solvent ratio (1:10w/v), and the temperature (40°C). The extraction process was repeated three times. The supernatants were filtered through Whatman No. 1, then the filtrates were combined and evaporated by a rotary evaporator (BUCHI) and lyophilized using a freeze‐dryer (SciQuip Ltd.). The percentage yields of all extracts (%yield) were calculated. All extracts were kept at −20°C until use.

Partial purification of the anthocyanin extract

The roselle extract by ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl (RSUEH) was selected for purification of anthocyanin‐enriched fraction. The purification was conducted from the method with some modifications, according to Maciel et al. (2018). A glass column 5 cm in diameter and 60 cm in length was used, with 180 g of Amberlite® XAD7HP resin. Then, 10 g of roselle extract was diluted with 32 mL of ultrapure water and acidified with 5 mL of formic acid. The solution was applied in the column and washed with ultrapure water to neutral pH. The anthocyanins were eluted with 1500 mL of 50% EtOH with 6.7 mL of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and 1500 mL of absolute EtOH with 12 mL TFA. All fractions were dried using a rotary evaporator. The percentage yields of all extracts (%yield) were calculated. All fractions were kept at −20°C until use.

Phytochemical analysis of roselle calyx extracts

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

TPCs of all roselle calyx extracts and fractions were analyzed using Folin–Ciocalteu assay (Sithisarn et al., 2015). Roselle extracts and fractions at the concentration of 1 mg/mL in distilled water were used. The TPC was calculated from the standard curve of gallic acid and was expressed as g gallic acid equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% GAE).

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC)

The TFCs of all roselle calyx extracts and fractions were analyzed by the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay (Sithisarn et al., 2015). Roselle extracts and fractions at the concentration of 5 mg/mL in distilled water were used. The TFC was calculated from the standard curve of quercetin and were expressed as g quercetin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% QE).

Determination of total anthocyanin content (TAC)

Determination of TAC using standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside

The TACs of all roselle calyx extracts and fractions were determined using the pH‐differential method with some modifications (Lee et al., 2005). Roselle extracts and fractions at the concentration of 25 mg/mL in distilled water were used. Each experiment was analyzed in triplicate. The total anthocyanin was calculated from the standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside and expressed as g cyanidin 3‐glucoside equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% C3GE).

Determination of TAC by standard calculation

The TACs of all roselle calyx extracts was determined using the pH‐differential method according to the guidelines of AOAC (Lee et al., 2005). Roselle extracts and fractions at the concentration of 25 mg/mL in distilled water were used. Each experiment was analyzed in triplicate. The total anthocyanin was calculated as g cyanidin‐3‐glucosise equivalent in 100 g of extract (g%C3GE) as follows:

High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of anthocyanins

A Shimadzu i‐Series LC‐2050C was used for HPLC simultaneous quantitative analysis of anthocyanins in roselle extracts and fractions. The system consists of a photodiode array detector, quaternary solvent and sample manager, cooling autosampler, and column oven. The data were processed using Lab solution software. Separation and quantitation of marker compounds were made on an YMC‐Pack Pro C18 RS column (250 × 4.0 mm, 3 µm) with YMC guard cartridge column (10 × 4.0 mm, 3 µm). A condition used in analysis was an injection volume of 10 µL and a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, a column temperature at 45°C. The mobile phase consists of 0.0375 w/v tartaric acid in water (solvent A) and EtOH (solvent B) at a ratio of 89:11 v/v using gradient elution. The detection wavelength was at 520 nm.

The validation method of the HPLC analysis was performed according to the ICH harmonised tripartite guideline: validation of analytical procedures: text and methodology Q2(R1) (international conference on harmonisation of technical requirements for registration of pharmaceuticals for human use, 2005). Linearity for standard delphinidin‐3‐sambubioside (D3S), delphinidin‐3‐glucoside (D3G), and C3G was determined by analysis of six different concentrations, each concentration in triplicate. Peak purity was investigated using a DAD for all peaks of interest. The precision of the method was evaluated by analyzing six independently prepared sample solutions on three different days. The peak area of each standard compound was determined, and the relative standard deviation percent (%RSD) was calculated. The accuracy of the method was confirmed by determination of the recovery, which was performed on samples spiked with three concentrations of each compound. Limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantitation (LOQ) were defined as the lowest concentration of chemical marker, which demonstrated the signal‐to‐noise ratio as 3:1 and 10:1, respectively. The validated HPLC analytical method was then applied for quantitative analysis of D3S, D3G, and C3G in roselle extracts and fractions.

Determination of in vitro biological activities

Determination of in vitro antioxidant activity

The DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) assay was used to determine antioxidant activity of all roselle calyx extracts and fractions. The EC50 value was calculated from the linear equation obtained from the plot between sample concentrations and percentage of inhibitions. Each experiment was analyzed in triplicate (Sunthudlakhar et al., 2019).

Determination of in vitro antibacterial activities

Disk diffusion method

The disk diffusion method was used for evaluation of antibacterial effects of selected roselle extract according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI, 2020). The assay was performed against five clinically pathogenic zoonotic bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus intermedius, Proteus mirabilis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The bacteria strains were obtained from the Microbiological Laboratory, Veterinary Diagnostic Center, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Kasetsart University, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand, and were isolated and characterized using differential bacterial culture and biochemical assays for clinical samples according to the standard method of Baron et al. (Pottumarthy et al., 2004). Standard antibiotic disc: amoxycillin 10 µg (AML10), gentamicin 10 µg (CN10), and doxycycline 30 µg (DO30) (Oxoid) were used as positive controls, while DMSO was used as the negative control. All tests were conducted in triplicate, and the average zone of inhibition was calculated with the standard deviation.

Broth microdilution method

The broth microdilution method was used to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the selected roselle extract (Sithisarn et al., 2016). The MIC is the lowest concentration of the extract that inhibiting the visible growth of microorganism under the defined conditions and time. Standard antibiotics: amoxycillin, gentamicin, doxycycline, and cefotaxime sodium at stock concentration of 5 mg/mL were used as positive controls. While bacterial suspension incubated without any extract was used as the negative control. All tests were conducted in triplicate, and the average value with standard deviation was then calculated.

Statistical analysis

The results from all experiments were expressed as mean ± standard deviation of triplicates analysis. Significant differences between samples were analyzed by analysis of variance and t‐test using SPSS version 18.0 (StatSoft). The value of p < 0.05 was considered significant. The Pearson correlation test (r) was used for correlation analysis using data analysis in Microsoft 365Apps for Windows 11 version 22H2 (Microsoft). The relationship between two variables is generally considered strong when the absolute value of r is >0.80 (Akoglu, 2018).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Yield of the extract

As shown in Table 1, the yields of all extracts were in the range of 26.46–57.69% w/w of dry roselle powders. Decoction and digestion with water and maceration with 80% EtOH provided the yields of the extract around 40%–50% w/w, while ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH or water provided extracts with lower yield. However, adding 1% HCl into the extraction solvents increased the yields of the extracts. Roselle extract prepared by ultrasonic extraction with 1% HCl in 80% EtOH (RSUEH) had the highest yield among all extracts. Among the obtained fractions from partial purification, fraction 1 (F1) provided the highest yield (54.94% w/w of dry extract). Phytochemical analysis of roselle calyx extracts

Determination of TPC

All roselle extracts and fractions were analyzed for TPCs using a Folin–Ciocalteu assay. TPCs of roselle extracts and fractions are shown in Table 1. Roselle extracts contained TPCs in the range of 2.02 ± 0.03 to 7.36 ± 0.04 g gallic acid equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% GAE). Extracts prepared from water or water with 1% HCl contained TPCs around 2–3 g% GAE, while extraction with 80% EtOH and 80% EtOH with 1% HCl provided the extracts (RSUE and RSUEH, respectively) with significantly higher TPCs (around 7 and 5 g% GAE, respectively). Roselle fractions contained various amounts of TPCs. It was found that fractions 28 and 34 contained the highest TPCs among all samples (12.57 ± 0.13 and 13.82 ± 0.03 g% GAE, respectively).

Determination of TFC

TFCs of roselle extracts and fractions are shown in Table 1. All extracts contained TFCs in the range of 0.17 ± 0.00 to 1.29 ± 0.06 g quercetin equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% QE). Extracts prepared from water or water with 1% HCl contained TFCs around 0.1–0.4 g% QE, while extraction with 80% EtOH and 80% EtOH with 1% HCl provided the extracts (RSUE and RSUEH, respectively) with significantly higher TFCs (around 0.6 and 1.3 g% QE, respectively). Roselle fractions contained TFCs around 0.3–3.0 g% QE. Fraction 28 (F28) contained the highest TFC (2.92 ± 0.01 g% QE).

Determination of TAC

TACs in roselle extracts and fractions were determined by pH‐differential with standard calculation method and pH‐differential with standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside method. TACs obtained from both methods ranged from 0.06 ± 0.00 to 0.50 ± 0.00 g cyanidin 3‐glucoside equivalent in 100 g of extract (g% C3GE). pH‐differential with standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside method provided the results of TAC about two times higher than the results from standard calculation method. RSUE and RSUEH contained the highest TACs analyzed by both methods (around 0.2 and 0.4–0.5 g% C3GE from standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside method and standard calculation, respectively). Roselle fractions contained different amounts of TACs. As shown in Table 1, F28 contained the highest TAC (0.64 ± 0.00 and 2.73 ± 0.00 g% C3GE from standard calculation and standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside method, respectively). From the results, partial purification process provided the fraction with the enriched amount of total anthocyanin and also high amounts of total phenolic and TFCs (F28).

HPLC analysis of anthocyanins

Linear correlations from 1 to 50 µg/mL were obtained D3S, D3G, and C3G, respectively. The r 2 values for all three compounds were >0.9990, confirming the linearity of the method. From the investigation of peak purity, there was no indication of co‐elution or impurities from all peaks of interest. The recoveries of all three concentrations of all compounds were close to theoretical amounts (% recoveries were in the range from 90 to 105%, %RSD < 5). Percentages of relative standard deviation (%RSD) of six sample solutions analyzed in three different days (intermediate precision) were <5%, while the %RSD values for repeatability were not >4%. Therefore, the method can be regarded as precise. The LOD of D3S, D3G, and C3G were 0.11 ± 0.01, 0.32 ± 0.01, and 0.14 ± 0.02 mg/g, respectively, while the LOQ of these three compounds were 0.19 ± 0.00, 0.57 ± 0.01, and 0.25 ± 0.02, respectively. The validation parameters obtained from the optimized HPLC methods were acceptable (Association of Official Analytical Chemists [AOAC], 2013); therefore, this analytical HPLC method could be used for quantitative analysis of the three anthocyanins in the roselle extracts and fractions. The validation parameters of the HPLC method are shown in Table 2. For system suitability parameters, tailing factors of all three standard compounds were <2, theoretical plate numbers were more than 1500, resolution >1.5, and %RSD values of the peak area were <2, suggesting the suitability of the analytical method.

HPLC chromatograms of roselle extracts and fractions showed three main peaks corresponding D3S, D3G, and C3G at retention time of 7.32, 8.03, and 11.27 min, respectively (Figure 1).

As shown in Table 1, roselle extracts and fractions were analyzed for the contents of D3S, D3G, and C3G using the validated HPLC method. Extractions using digestion, decoction, and maceration with solvents without acid provided the extracts, which could not detect any anthocyanin by HPLC method except extraction using ultrasonic method, which gave the extracts with some amounts of D3S. Using ultrasonic extraction, 80% EtOH was found to promote the extract with higher total amount of anthocyanins, especially the amount of D3G. Partial purification of RSUEH provided the fractions with different physical characteristics and chemical contents. Fractions 28 and 34 (F28 and F34) contained high amounts of all major anthocyanins, especially F28, which contained the highest anthocyanin contents among all tested samples (2.64 ± 0.02, 7.13 ± 0.02, and 3.24 ± 0.02mg/g of fraction for D3S, D3G, and C3G, respectively).

From the results, roselle was the good source of anthocyanin. Previous research reported TACs of some plants. Using pH‐differential method with standard calculation method, TACs of red cabbage (Brassica oleracea L. var. capitata f. rubra), sour cherry (Prunus cerasus), and blueberry (Vaccinium myrtillus) were in the range of 0.02–1.25 g% C3GE (Kurtulbaş et al., 2021; Paes et al., 2014; Yiğit et al., 2022). TAC in roselle extract prepared by microwave‐assisted extraction method analyzed by this method was 0.16 g% C3GE (Cassol et al., 2019). TACs analyzed by pH‐differential method with standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside were reported in fewer plants. Blackberry (Rubus spp.) and black rice (Oryza sativa) contained TAC of 0.21 and 0.02 g% C3GE, respectively (Pedro et al., 2016; Wen et al., 2015).

It was found that RSUEH, which was roselle extract prepared by green extraction of roselle using ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl, provided the extract with high total phenolic, total flavonoid, total anthocyanin, and anthocyanin contents, while F28 from partial purification of RSUEH was found to be an enrich fraction that contained the highest amounts of phytochemicals.

Determination of in vitro biological activities

Determination of in vitro antioxidant activity

All roselle extracts exhibited moderate to high DPPH scavenging effects, as shown in Table 1. The EC50 values of roselle extracts ranged from 94.95 ± 1.18 to 289.61 ± 0.16 µg/mL. The results showed that among the extracts, extract from ultrasonic extraction using 80% EtOH (RSUE) significantly promoted the highest DPPH scavenging activity (p < 0.05). Some fractions from partial purification of the roselle extract by ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl (RSUEH) were tested for DPPH scavenging effects. It was found that all tested fractions promoted a stronger DPPH scavenging effect than roselle extracts from various extraction method and solvent. The EC50 values of all tested fractions were <100 µg/mL. Fraction of F34 and F28, which contained the high TPCs (13.82 ± 0.03 and 12.57 ± 0.13 g% GAE, respectively), and high TFCs (2.52 ± 0.05 and 2.92 ± 0.01 g% QE, respectively), promoted the high DPPH scavenging activities (EC50 48.13 ± 0.99 and 54.88 ± 2.23 µg/mL, respectively). However, F28 and F34 showed no difference between their DPPH scavenging activity (p > 0.05). F28 also contained the highest amount of TACs analyzed by both standard calculation and standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside methods (0.64 ± 0.00 and 2.73 ± 0.00 g% C3GE, respectively).

From the results, partial purification of roselle extract by ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl (RSUEH) provided the fractions with higher amounts of total phenolic, total flavonoid, and TACs, leading to the promotions of the stronger antioxidant effects. According to TLC analysis, four standard anthocyanins, including D3S, C3S, D3G and C3G should be presented in roselle extracts. It was found that D3S, C3S, D3G and C3G exhibited strong DPPH scavenging effects with an EC50 values of 15.19 ± 0.18, 17.59 ± 0.04, 22.19 ± 0.14, and 25.13 ± 0.20 µg/mL, respectively. Therefore, these compounds could be used as active markers for quality control of raw materials and extracts from roselle for their antioxidant properties. Moreover, there were also previous reports that showed in vitro antioxidant effects determined by various methods. For the antioxidant properties were determined by using phosphomolybdenum method, the methanol and aqueous extracts of roselle calyx had total antioxidant capacity values of 38.822 ± 0.277 and 38.822 ± 0.250 mg ascorbic acid equivalent in g of extract (Chatepa et al., 2023). Furthermore, the ethanolic extracts of roselle calyx promoted the antioxidant capacity determined by 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity with an inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 4.06 ± 0.05 µg/mL and 2,2′‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline)‐6‐sulfonic acid (ABTS) radical scavenging activity with an IC50 values of 3.7 ± 0.07 µg/mL (Wu et al., 2018). In the ferric ion reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assays, the ethanolic extract from roselle calyx exhibited good FRAP (33.98 mg trolox equivalents in 100g of extract) (Wu et al., 2018). In this study, it was found that major anthocyanins in roselle, including D3S, C3S, D3G and C3G promoted strong antioxidant activities and could be responsible for the antioxidant effects of roselle extract. Moreover, some phenolics and flavonoids in roselle, including chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, quercetin, rutin, and myricetin, have been previously reported to promote antioxidant effects (Banwo et al., 2022; Borrás‐Linares et al., 2015; Hajimehdipoor et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2014). These compounds could also be responsible for the antioxidant activity of roselle.

To evaluate the correlation between phytochemical contents and DPPH scavenging effect, a Pearson correlation test was conducted. The correlation between TFC, TAC determined by standard calculation method, and TAC determined by standard curve of cyanidin 3‐glucoside method and DPPH scavenging activity was at moderate level with r values of 0.6965, 0.4591, and 0.5426, respectively, while TPC showed a strong correlation with DPPH scavenging effect with r value of 0.8231. RSUEH and F28, which were prepared by green extraction of roselle and partial purification, exhibited moderate and strong DPPH scavenging effects, respectively. The results of the strong antioxidant activities corresponded to the high amounts of phenolic, flavonoid, and anthocyanins that could be responding to the activities.

Determination of in vitro antibacterial activity

Disk diffusion method

The roselle extract by ultrasonic with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl (RSUEH) was selected for investigation of inhibitory effects against five clinically pathogenic bacteria, including E. coli, S. aureus, S. intermedius, P. aeruginosa, and P. mirabilis, using disk diffusion method. As shown in Table 3, the extract promoted strong inhibitory effects against all tested bacteria, with the inhibition zone ranging from 14 ± 0.00 to 18.5 ± 0.45 mm at a concentration of 12.5 mg/disc. Moreover, RSUEH showed stronger inhibitory effect against S. intermedius than gentamicin at the concentration of 10 µg/disc. RSUEH showed lower inhibitory effect against E. coli and S. aureus than doxycycline at the concentration of 30 µg/disc, RSUEH showed lower inhibitory effect against P. aeruginosa than gentamicin, and P. mirabilis than amoxycillin at the concentration of 10 µg/disc.

RSUEH showed the antibacterial activity with IC50 values against all tested bacteria in the range of 0.15 ± 0.05 mg/mL to 4.64 ± 0.07 mg/mL and the MIC values ranged from 1.66 ± 0.06 to 7.11 ± 1.05 mg/mL. F28 showed the antibacterial activity with IC50 values against all tested bacteria in the range of 0.19 ± 0.07 to 4.69 ± 0.09 mg/mL, and the MIC values ranged from 2.46 ± 0.08 to 6.24 ± 0.09 mg/mL. The results of IC50 and MIC values showed the antibacterial activity of RSUEH against all tested bacteria, particularly the gram‐positives (S. aureus and S. intermedius). While F28 showed effective inhibitory effects against some gram‐positive and negative bacteria. The best IC50 values of F28 was against P. aeruginosa. However, the MIC values showed the highest antibacterial activity of F28 against S. intermedius.

All standard anthocyanins (D3S, D3G, C3S, and C3G) showed the antibacterial activity against all tested bacteria, particularly the gram‐positives (S. intermedius). The IC50 values are in the range of 0.76 ± 0.56 to 60.96 ± 3.42 µg/mL, and the MIC values ranged from 48.84 ± 1.63 to 108.11 ± 5.13 µg/mL. There was no statistically significant difference between the MIC values of each standard and each bacterium (p > 0.05). Though the four standards showed the lowest antibacterial activity with IC50 and MIC values against P. mirabilis in the range of 58.72 ± 4.09 to 60.96 ± 3.42 and 104.76 ± 6.13 µg/mL to 108.11 ± 5.13 µg/mL, respectively. Therefore, these anthocyanins could be used as active compounds for antibacterial activities of roselle extracts.

From the results, antibacterial activity of RSUEH, F28, and four standards (D3S, D3G, C3S, and C3G) showed strong inhibitory effects against five clinically pathogenic bacteria. Antibacterial properties of RSUEH and four standards (D3S, D3G, C3S, and C3G) against gram‐positive bacteria were significantly higher compared with gram‐negative bacteria (p < 0.05). A statistical analysis showed no differences in the obtained IC50 and MIC values of F28 between gram‐positive and gram‐negative bacteria (p > 0.05). Although F28 has a higher amount of total phenolic, total flavonoid, TACs, and stronger antioxidant activity than the extract (RSUEH), F28 showed weaker antibacterial activity than RSUEH against most tested bacteria except P. aeruginosa. From the results, beside phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, it could be other phytochemicals that are responsible for the antibacterial activity. Moreover, even though the amounts of phytochemical could play an important role in antibacterial activity, the type of phytochemical could also have an effect.

There were some previous studies reported about antibacterial activities of some anthocyanin extracts from plants. Purple potato (Solanum tuberosum L. var. Vitelotte) aqueous extract, which contained approximately 0.16 g% malvidin equivalent, showed a high antibacterial activity against various gram‐negative and gram‐positive standard bacterial strains, especially S. aureus and Enterococcus faecalis from both standard and clinical sources with MIC = 15.6l and 31.3l g/mL for standard strains and clinical isolates, respectively (Bontempo et al., 2013). Chinese wild blueberries (Vaccinium uliginosum) extract with 0.42 g% cyanidin‐3‐galactoside equivalent promoted strong inhibitory effect with low MIC values against Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Listeria monocytogenes, S. aureus, and S. enteritidis (Sun et al., 2018). Morus alba L., Mae Hong Son cultivar fruit extract, which contained 0.18 g% C2GE showed high inhibitory activity against S. aureus with an MIC of 7.81 mg/mL (Suriyaprom et al., 2021). Crude extract butterfly pea (Clitoria ternatea) flower showed high inhibitory effects against gram‐positive bacteria (Bacillus cereus and B. subtilis) with an MIC value of 10 mg/mL. However, anthocyanin fraction showed more potent against these strains with an MIC value of 0.63 mg/mL. This fraction also showed inhibitory effect against gram‐negative bacteria (E. coli) with an MIC value of 10 mg/mL (Jeyaraj et al., 2022).

Previous studies reported that the antibacterial effects of roselle calyx aqueous and ethanol extracts against the tested bacteria (Salmonella typhimurium DT104, E. coli O157:H7, L. monocytogenes, S. aureus, and B. cereus) the MIC values ranged from in the range of 112–144 and 72–96 µg/mL, respectively (Chao & Yin, 2009). Moreover, the aqueous‐methanol extract of roselle calyces also showed in vitro antibacterial effects against S. aureus, Bacillus stearothermophilus, M. luteus, Serratia marscescens, Clostridium sporogenes, E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, B. cereus, and Pseudomonas fluorescence with the MIC in the range of 0.3–1.3 mg/mL (Olaleye, 2007). From the study, four major anthocyanins in roselle, including D3S, C3S, D3G, and C3G showed in vitro antibacterial activities against some clinically isolated bacteria. These compounds are responsible for the antibacterial effects of roselle extract and fraction. Moreover, roselle also contained other phytochemicals, including flavonoids, alcoholic compounds, triazine derivatives, and esters, which were reported to promote potent antibacterial activity (Abdel‐Shafi et al., 2019). Hibiscus acid exhibited an antibacterial effect against E. coli, S. typhimurium, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and V. cholerae with the suggested mechanism of action of disruption in bacterial cell membrane permeability (Portillo‐Torres et al., 2019; Prasetyoputri et al., 2021). Antibacterial activity of roselle extracts and fractions could be caused by multiple mechanisms and synergies of different phytochemicals. Therefore, further studies on the structure‐activity relationship and mechanism of actions should be performed in the future.

CONCLUSION

Green extraction by ultrasonic extraction with 80% EtOH and 1% HCl promoted roselle extract with the highest yield and high total phenolic, total flavonoid, total anthocyanin, and anthocyanin contents and showed in vitro antioxidant effect. Phytochemical studies by TLC analysis of roselle extracts showed that they had four main anthocyanins, including D3S, C3S, D3G, and C3G. Partial purification of roselle extract provide the enrich fraction with the highest amounts of phytochemicals and the strongest DPPH scavenging effect. In vitro determination of antibacterial activity against five clinically isolated bacteria, including E. coli, S. aureus, S. intermedius, P. aeruginosa, and P. mirabilis, showed that both roselle extract obtained from green extraction and the enrich fraction promoted strong inhibitory effects against all tested bacteria. Four major anthocyanins in roselle extracts, including D3S, C3S, D3G, and C3G promoted strong DPPH scavenging activities and antibacterial activities, which could be used as active markers for quality control of raw materials and extracts from roselle. Moreover, studies about the mechanism of action, structure‐activity relationship, stability, and the usage for pharmaceutical purposes should be conducted in the future.
