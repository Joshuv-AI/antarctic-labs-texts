Exponentially reduced circuit depths in Lindbladian simulation
Wenjun Yu, Xiaogang Li, Qi Zhao, Xiao Yuan

Abstract
Quantum computers can efficiently simulate Lindbladian dynamics, enabling powerful applications in open system simulation, thermal and ground-state preparation, autonomous quantum error correction, dissipative engineering, and more. Despite the abundance of well-established algorithms for closed-system dynamics, simulating open quantum systems on digital quantum computers remains challenging due to the intrinsic requirement for non-unitary operations. Existing methods face a critical trade-off: either relying on resource-intensive multi-qubit operations with experimentally challenging approaches or employing deep quantum circuits to suppress simulation errors using experimentally friendly methods. In this work, we challenge this perceived trade-off by proposing an efficient Lindbladian simulation framework that minimizes circuit depths while remaining experimentally accessible. Based on the incoherent linear combination of superoperators, our method achieves exponential reductions in circuit depth using at most two ancilla qubits and the straightforward Trotter decomposition of the process. Furthermore, our approach extends to simulate time-dependent Lindbladian dynamics, achieving logarithmic dependence on the inverse accuracy for the first time. Rigorous numerical simulations demonstrate clear advantages of our method over existing techniques. This work provides a practical and scalable solution for simulating open quantum systems on quantum devices.

Settings.—

The dynamics of open quantum systems, which interact with their environment, is described by the Lindblad master equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\underbrace{\vphantom{\left(D_{l}\rho D_{l}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{D^{\dagger}_{l}D_{l},\rho\}\right)}-\textup{i}[H,\rho]}_{\mathcal{H}[\rho]}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\underbrace{\left(D_{l}\rho D_{l}^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{2}\{D^{\dagger}_{l}D_{l},\rho\}\right)}_{\mathcal{D}_{l}[\rho]},

(1)

where
H
represents the system’s internal dynamics, and jump operators
\{D_{l}\}
characterize how the system interacts with its environment.
The evolution can be expressed through a superoperator
\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{l}
, which may exhibit continuous time dependence.

The challenge of Lindbladian simulation is to compute how an initial state
\rho(0)
evolves under this dynamics to reach
\rho(T)=\mathcal{T}\exp(\int_0^T \mc L dt)\rho(0)
.
For most practical applications, it suffices to extract measurement outcomes of the final state rather than explicitly constructing it—an approach known as
effective simulation
.
Our work presents an efficient way to effectively simulate the Lindbladian evolution.
For practical implementations, we focus on systems where both
H
and
\{D_{l}\}
are
q
-sparse, meaning they can be expressed using at most
q
non-zero Pauli operators.
To quantify the evolution’s strength, we define the Lindbladian norm as
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}\coloneqq 2(\|H\|_{1}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\|D_{l}\|_{1}^{2})
, where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
measures the
p
th order vector norm of the operator’s Pauli-decomposition coefficients.

LCS Formula.—

Before presenting the algorithm, we first introduce the linear combination of superoperators (LCS) formula, which provides a powerful framework for analyzing and implementing a broad class of superoperators beyond physical operations.
Specifically, a LCS formula of a superoperator
\mathcal{V}
is a convex combination of
\mathcal{V}_{i}
with positive coefficients
c_{i}
as

\mathcal{V}=\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}c_{i}\mathcal{V}_{i}=\mu\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}\Pr(i)\mathcal{V}_{i},

(2)

where
\mu=\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}c_{i}
, the normalization factor, is defined as the 1-norm and
\Pr(i)=c_{i}/\mu
.
The convex combination structure of the LCS formula in Eq. (
2
) naturally lends itself to implementation via Monte Carlo sampling.
This approach involves applying superoperator
\mathcal{V}_{i}
to the quantum state with probability
\Pr(i)
, followed by a renormalization
\mu
of the output state. The renormalization
\mu
introduces additional sample overhead by amplifying statistical fluctuations to
\epsilon_{s}=\order{\mu/\sqrt{N}}
in contrast
to
\order{1/\sqrt{N}}
with
N
rounds.
For small constant
\mu
, however, the average of the quantum states still rapidly converges to the result of applying the desired superoperator.
For tasks requiring the sequential application of multiple superoperators, we can concatenate their respective LCS formulae. This composition yields a new LCS formula that targets the overall superoperator.

While more powerful basis choices
{\mathcal{V}_{i}}
can enhance performance, we prioritize experimental feasibility by utilizing minimal resources.
To this end, we select
unitary-conjugate
operations as our basis, defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\phi_{\alpha\beta},\alpha,\beta}[\rho]\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{\textup{i}\phi_{\alpha\beta}}U_{\alpha}\rho U_{\beta}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{e}^{-\textup{i}\phi_{\alpha\beta}}U_{\beta}\rho U_{\alpha}^{\dagger}),

(3)

where
U_{\alpha},U_{\beta}
are unitary operators, and
\phi_{\alpha\beta}=-\phi_{\beta\alpha}
.
These operations can be efficiently implemented using the circuit structure depicted in Fig.
1
(c), requiring only a single ancilla qubit.
Moreover, we find this basis can express any Hermitian-preserving (HP) maps (Pauli-conjugate as a valid basis).
The HP assumption further implies
\Pr(\alpha,\beta)=\Pr(\beta,\alpha)
as in
[
30
,
31
]
.
When considering the concatenation of a series of LCS formulas, we show in the appendix that ancilla qubits can be reused without intermediate measurements.
This optimization reduces the quantum resource requirements while maintaining the desired operational sequence, enhancing the efficiency of our approach for complex quantum operations.

Algorithm Overview.—

We discretize the evolution time
T
into small intervals
\tau\ll 1
to control simulation errors.
For each time step, our approach to time-independent Lindblad adopts a hierarchical protocol: the coarse-grained simulation stage followed by the systematic error compensation stage, as shown in Fig.
1
.

The coarse-grained stage builds upon the conventional Lie-Trotter decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}\tau}=\overleftarrow{\prod_{l=1}^{m}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}\tau}\right)\circ\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{H}\tau}+\order{\tau^2},

(4)

where the left arrow denotes descending order.
However, the inherent non-unitarity of the dissipative terms
\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}\tau}\}
still poses challenges to the realization.
To overcome this, we introduce a single-qubit-assisted joint Hamiltonian evolution
\mathrm{e}^{-\textup{i}J_{l}\sqrt{\tau}}
, where

J_{l}\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&D_{l}^{\dagger}\\
D_{l}&0\end{array}\right)=\sigma_{-}\otimes D_{l}+\sigma_{+}\otimes D_{l}^{\dagger}.

(5)

This operation after tracing out the ancilla, denoted as
\mathcal{J}_{l}(\tau)
, approximates
\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}\tau}
to the first order:

\Tr_{a}(\mathrm{e}^{-\textup{i}J_{l}\sqrt{\tau}}[\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a}\otimes\rho]\mathrm{e}^{\textup{i}J_{l}\sqrt{\tau}})=\rho+\tau\cdot\mathcal{D}_{l}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}).

(6)

Both
J_{l}
and
H
evolutions are then approximated using the first-order Trotter-Suzuki formula, as shown in the blue blocks of Fig.
2
.

This Trotter-based approach, while experimentally feasible, involves three distinct sources of error including the approximation of
\mathcal{L}
evolution in Eq. (
4
), the approximation of
\mathcal{D}_{l}
evolution in Eq. (
6
), and the Trotter errors in the evolution of
J_{l}
and
H
.
Next, we show how to systematically compensate for these errors using the LCS of unitary-conjugate operations with minimal ancilla qubit overhead.
We summarize this process in yellow blocks in Fig.
2
.

Compensation Correction.—

While first-order Trotter formulas provide a feasible approach for our coarse-grained simulation, a systematic compensation stage is essential to achieve high accuracy.
Here, we illustrate this novel compensation stage using LCS formulas.

To address higher-order errors in Eq. (
4
), we desire a compensation
\mathcal{M}(\tau)
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}\tau}=\mathcal{M}(\tau)\circ\overleftarrow{\prod_{l=1}^{m}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}\tau}\right)\circ\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{H}\tau}.

(7)

Using exponential function’s properties, we can express
\mathcal{M}(\tau)
as
\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\circ\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{H}\tau}\circ\overrightarrow{\prod_{l=1}^{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{D}_{l}\tau}
.
When we expand each exponential term in its Taylor series, we obtain a power series
\mathcal{M}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{k}\tau^{k}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{s+\sum_{l=0}^{m}s_{l}=k}\frac{(\mathcal{H}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathcal{D}_{l})^{s}(-\mathcal{H})^{s_{0}}\overrightarrow{\prod_{l=1}^{m}}(-\mathcal{D}_{l})^{s_{l}}}{s!\prod_{l=0}^{m}(s_{l}!)}.

(8)

Although this compensation operation contains inverse dissipative terms that cannot be directly implemented physically, the LCS formula provides a practical solution.
By decomposing the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and dissipative terms
{\mathcal{D}_{l}}
in the Pauli-conjugate basis, we obtain an LCS formula for
\mathcal{M}(\tau)
.
We then truncate
\mathcal{M}(\tau)
to order
K
, yielding a finite-term approximation
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathcal{M}_{k}\tau^{k}
, as detailed in Appendix
.
The truncated formula
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
consists of Pauli conjugates with a quadratic 1-norm
\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau))=\order{1+\| \mc L\|_\p^2\tau^2}
(since
\mathcal{M}_{1}=0
) and an exponentially small bias

\displaystyle\epsilon_{M}\coloneqq\|\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)-\mathcal{M}(\tau)\|_{\diamond}\leq\left(\frac{4e\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}\tau}{K+1}\right)^{K+1}.

(9)

To implement this compensation, we employ the preceding Monte-Carlo sampling approach.
We first select an order
k_{0}\leq K
with probability
\mu(\mathcal{M}_{k_{0}})\tau^{k_{0}}/\mu(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau))
.
We then sample a Paul-conjugate operator according to its weight in
\mathcal{M}_{k_{0}}
, which is calculated through Eq. (
8
) and the decomposition of
\mathcal{H}
and
\{\mathcal{D}_{l}\}
.
As shown in Fig.
1
(c), the sampled operations can be implemented using two controlled Pauli gates, requiring one ancilla qubit and
\order{K}
elementary gates for Pauli locality
\order{K}
.
Overall, we effectively suppress the Lie-Trotter error in Eq. (
4
) by applying
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)
.

The method extends similarly to address higher-order discrepancies in both dissipative and Hamiltonian evolutions.
For each case, we derive an explicit compensation operation, truncate to the first
K
th-order terms, and apply the same sampling process to realize the LCS formula.
We delay the construct ideas in Appendix
and
for interested readers.
Each compensation similarly requires
\order{K}
elementary gates and one additional ancilla qubit and brings an exponentially small bias with
K
.
Note that compensation for Hamiltonian evolution of
J_{l}
in Eq. (
6
) requires two ancilla qubits since
J_{l}
is already a
n+1
-qubit Hamiltonian operator.

Simulation Construction and Overheads.—

We now construct a comprehensive high-accuracy simulation of Lindblad evolution by integrating previously described two-stage processes.
For each step, the simulation takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}\tau}\approx\tilde{\mathcal{M}}(\tau)\circ\overleftarrow{\prod_{l=1}^{m}}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{l}(\tau)\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{l}(\tau)\right)\circ\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tau),

(10)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{l}(\tau)
compensates for dissipative term errors, while
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{l}(\tau)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
represent compensations for Trotter evolutions of
J_{l}
and
H
, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

To achieve high accuracy while maintaining efficiency, we make several key design choices. First, we set the truncation order:

\displaystyle K=\Theta\left(\frac{\log(\varepsilon\tau/T)}{\log(\|\mc L\|_\p\tau)}\right)

(11)

to ensure each step’s bias remains below
\varepsilon\tau/T
.
Second, we choose
\tau=\order{1/(\|\mc L\|_\p^2 T)}
to keep the overall 1-norm
\mu=\exp[\order{\|\mc L\|_\p^2\tau T}]
at
\Theta(1)
when concatenating
T/\tau
steps.
Under these parameters, each step requires
\order{m(q+K)}
elementary gates due to the first-order Trotter’s
\order{q}
gate cost.
These choices lead to our main result (proved in Appendix
):

Theorem 1
.

For a
q
-sparse Lindblad operator
\mathcal{L}
, simulation time
T
, target error
\varepsilon
, the gate and sample complexities for our Lindbladian simulation algorithm are

\displaystyle N_{g}

\displaystyle=\order{(\|\mc L\|_\p T)^2m\left(q+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(\|\mc L\|_\p T)}\right)},

(12)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(1/\varepsilon^{2}\right)

Therefore, without the sophisticated or expensive constructions in Refs.
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, we also achieved logarithmic dependence on
1/\varepsilon
for open system simulation under the experimentally more friendly Trotter framework.

Time-dependent Lindblad Simulation.—

We can extend our approach with compensation to time-dependent Lindblad simulation.
For each time step
[t_{0},t_{0}+\tau)
, we construct a piecewise constant Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{M}(t)
by partitioning the step into
M
equal slices.
Within a slice
j
,
\mathcal{L}_{M}(t)
takes the value
\mathcal{L}_{j}
, evaluated at the slice’s midpoint.
When
\mathcal{L}(t)
has a bounded derivative,
\|\dot{\mathcal{L}}\|_{\diamond}\coloneqq\max_{t}\|\dot{\mathcal{L}}(t)\|_{\diamond}
, this new evolution approximates:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\mathcal{L}_{M}(t)dt}-\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\mathcal{L}(t)dt}\|_{\diamond}=\order{\frac{\tau^2\|\dot{\mc L}\|_\diamond}{M}}.

(13)

We implement the evolution under
\mathcal{L}_{M}(t)
through a time-independent simulation
\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{j}\tau/M}
followed by a compensation operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\tau)\coloneqq\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\mathcal{L}_{M}(t)dt}\circ\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{j}\tau/M}.

(14)

The time-independent part can be realized using our previous analysis, so we focus on
\mathcal{W}(\tau)
here.
Using Dyson and Taylor expansions along with the Pauli-conjugate basis

\mathcal{W}(\tau)
can be decomposed as a convergent power series when
\tau\ll 1/\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}
, where
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}\coloneqq\max_{t}\|\mathcal{L}(t)\|_{\textsf{pauli}}
.
We show the derivation in Appendix
.
Truncating the series at order
K
yields a finite Pauli-conjugate LCS formula
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)
with 1-norm
\exp[\order{(\|\mc L\|_\p\tau)^3}]
and bias:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{W}\coloneqq\|\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)-\mathcal{W}(\tau)\|_{\diamond}\leq\left(\frac{2\mathrm{e}\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}\tau}{K+1}\right)^{K+1}.

(15)

The single-step simulation thus becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\tau}\mathcal{L}(t)dt}\approx\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)\circ\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{j}\tau/M},

(16)

of which the gate count increases only by the cost of
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)
and still remains
\order{m(q+K)}
given the sparsity assumption.
To achieve the full simulation, we iterate the short-time evolution
T/\tau
times, where single-step error stays within
\order{\varepsilon\tau/T}
.
Three key parameters control the simulation accuracy and efficiency:
First, we set
\tau=\order{1/(\|\mc L\|_\p^2 T)}
which similarly results in
\mu=\order{1}
even accounting for the additional factor from
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\tau)
.
Second, we choose
M=\Theta\left(\|\dot{\mathcal{L}}\|_{\diamond}/(\varepsilon\|\mathcal{L}\|^{2})\right)
to bound the discretization error in Eq. (
13
).
Finally, by adopting the same order
K
as in Eq. (
11
), we suppress both bias in Eq. (
15
) and the time-independent error in Eq. (
16
).
These choices collectively guarantee the following analysis:

Theorem 2
.

For a
q
-sparse time-dependent Lindblad operator
\mathcal{L}
, simulation time
T
, target error
\varepsilon
, the gate and sample complexities for our Lindbladian simulation algorithm are

\displaystyle N_{g}

\displaystyle=\order{(\|\mc L\|_\p T)^2m\left(q+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(\|\mc L\|_\p T)}\right)},

(17)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(1/\varepsilon^{2}\right)

In summary, our compensation-based algorithm applies to time-dependent Lindblad simulation and achieves polylogarithmic depths for the first time.

Subplot (a) presents a comparison of the resource requirements between our method and the conventional first-order Trotter algorithm–the later essentially implementing only our coarse-grained stage.
We compile circuits into elementary gate sets comprising CNOT gates, single-qubit rotations, and single-qubit Clifford gates.
Focusing on the resource-intensive CNOT and rotation gates, our analysis demonstrates that our method achieves exponentially better gate efficiency as precision requirements become more stringent.

These strongly support the practicality of our theoretical framework for high-precision open system simulation.

Conclusion and Outlook.—

We have developed a Lindblad simulation method that achieves polylogarithmic circuit depths while requiring no more than two ancilla qubits. This breakthrough disproves the presumed trade-off between quantum resource usage and efficiency in solving the Lindblad simulation problem. Furthermore, our method can be extended to simulate time-dependent Lindblad dynamics, achieving for the first time a polylogarithmic dependence on the desired simulation error.
Despite these advancements, there is still room for further optimization. For instance, higher-order methods could be employed in the coarse-grained stages to reduce the complexity associated with extended simulation times. Additionally, an open question remains: can the current multiplicative polylogarithmic complexity be improved to an additive logarithmic complexity? Addressing these challenges could pave the way for even more efficient quantum simulations.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

A.1
Representation of Superoperators

In the scope of quantum information processing, we usually deal with states of quantum systems and operations therein.
There are two major families of representations for these ingredients.
For an isolated quantum system, which we refer to as a closed system, its state can be fully described by a set of basis and a corresponding vector in the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{n}
.
The operations summarized by Schrödinger equation and the intrinsic linearity are denoted by multiplying by unitary operators (matrices) in
\mathrm{L}(\mathscr{H}_{n})
.
In the remainder of this paper, we will use capital letters to denote these operators.
Nevertheless, it is ubiquitous to find the target quantum system correlating with some environmental systems, which we name an open system.
In this case, states of the system are generically described by density operators (matrices) in
\mathrm{L}(\mathscr{H}_{n})
, which are semi-definite Hermitian square matrices with unit traces.
The operations on these open-system states are, therefore, named as
superoperators
.
In the remainder of this paper, we will use calligraphic letters to denote these operators.

To represent the superoperator formally, we make use of the linearity of quantum operations and devise several methods.
For simplicity, we introduce the Pauli process-matrix representation for an arbitrary superoperator

Definition 1
.

For an arbitrary linear map
\mathcal{E}:\mathrm{L}(\mathscr{H}_{n})\rightarrow\mathrm{L}(\mathscr{H}_{n})
, we define the Pauli process-matrix representation of
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\rho]=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in{\sf P}^{n}}\chi_{\alpha\beta}P_{\alpha}\rho P_{\beta}^{\dagger},

(18)

where
{\sf P}^{n}
consists of
4^{n}
Pauli indices, and
P_{\alpha},P_{\beta}
are
n
-qubit Pauli operators. In this decomposition, each process matrix
\chi
element denotes a complex coefficient.
We further denote
\absolutevalue{\chi}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in{\sf P}^{n}}\absolutevalue{\chi_{\alpha\beta}}
.

A.2
Open-System Simulations with Lindblad Master Equation

In this work, we aim to simulate the dynamics of open-system evolution.
To specify the settings of the simulation problem, we start from the most general time-dependent Lindblad master equation.
When referring to the time-dependent Lindblad equation, we consider a bounded continuous Superoperator-valued function
\mathcal{L}(t)
defined on
t\in[0,T]
.
The corresponding Markovian open-system dynamics on a state
\rho
at time
t_{0}\in[0,T]
is depicted by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t_{0}}=\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\left(D_{l}\rho D_{l}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}D_{l}^{\dagger}D_{l}\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho D_{l}^{\dagger}D_{l}\right)}-i[H(t_{0}),\rho]}_{\mathcal{H}(t_{0})}+\underbrace{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\left(D_{l}(t_{0})\rho D_{l}(t_{0})^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}D_{l}(t_{0})^{\dagger}D_{l}(t_{0})\rho-\frac{1}{2}\rho D_{l}(t_{0})^{\dagger}D_{l}(t_{0})\right)}_{\mathcal{D}(t_{0})}=\mathcal{L}(t_{0})[\rho],

(19)

where
H(t_{0})
and
D_{l}(t_{0})
’s are continuous Hamiltonian and jump operators, respectively.

The Lindblad master equation leads to a clear formalism of the open-system simulation task: Given a Hamiltonian
H(t)
, jump operators
\{D_{l}(t)\}
, an input state
\rho(0)
, and a time
T
, we want to (approximately) output the final state:

\displaystyle\rho(T)\coloneqq\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}(t)dt}[\rho(0)]=\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{\mathcal{}}H(t)+\mathcal{D}(t))dt}[\rho(0)],

(20)

where
\mathcal{T}
represents time-ordered operator.
Nevertheless, extracting some properties of the final state is often more relevant than getting the state explicitly.
This generates a compromised version of the simulation task which we denote by the
effective simulation
: Given a Hamiltonian
H(t)
, jump operators
\{D_{l}(t)\}
, an input state
\rho(0)
, and a time
T
, we want to estimate some property
O
of the final state
\Tr(\mc T\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^T\mc L(t)dt}[\rho(0)]O)
.
In this work, we focus on the effective simulation of a continuous Lindbladian superoperator with sparse local Hamiltonian and jump operators.

To elucidate these constraints, we first consider the sparsity assumption.
To quantify the sparsity of the Hamiltonian and jump operator, we introduce the family of
Pauli-induced operator norms
.
For an arbitrary operator with Pauli decomposition:
A=\sum_{\alpha\in{\sf P}^{n}}c_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}
, we define the Pauli-induced operator norms based on the vector norm of the Pauli decomposition coefficients:
\|A\|_{p}\coloneqq\|\vec{c}\|_{p}
.
Based on this, the sparsity constraint requires that
\|H\|_{0}\leq q
and
\|D_{l}\|_{0}\leq q
for all
l\in[m]
, implying no more than
q
non-zero Pauli components.
Furthermore, the locality assumption imposes that those relevant Pauli components are local.

To further consider the superoperator induced by these Hamiltonian and jump operators, we adopt the Pauli process-matrix representation in Def.
1
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\chi_{\alpha\beta}^{(0)}(t)P_{\alpha}[\cdot]P_{\beta}^{\dagger},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathcal{D}_{l}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\chi_{\alpha\beta}^{(l)}(t)P_{\alpha}[\cdot]P_{\beta}^{\dagger},\ \forall\,l\in[m].

(21)

By the triangle inequality, we have the following conditions on these superoperators:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{H}(t)\|_{\diamond}\leq\absolutevalue{\chi^{(0)}(t)}\leq 2\|H(t)\|_{1},\hskip 20.00003pt\|\mathcal{D}_{l}(t)\|_{\diamond}\leq\absolutevalue{\chi^{(l)}(t)}\leq 2\|D_{l}(t)\|_{1}^{2},\ \forall\,l\in[m].

(22)

Our problem setting also imposes that the Lindbladian is continuous.
Therefore, we can use the following norm to quantify the Lindbladian’s intensity.

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}\|_{\textsf{pauli}}\coloneqq\max_{t}2\left(\|H(t)\|_{1}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\|D_{l}(t)\|_{1}^{2}\right).

(23)

A.3
Linear Combination of Superoperators Formula

As a power tool to analyze and realize a broader class of superoperators beyond CPTP maps, we introduce the following linear combination of super-operators (LCS) formula.

Definition 2
.

A
(\mu,\varepsilon)
-linear-combination-of-super-operator (LCS) formula of a super-operator
\mathcal{V}
is defined to be

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}c_{i}\mathcal{V}_{i}=\mu\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}\Pr(i)\mathcal{V}_{i},

(24)

such that the diamond norm distance
\|\mathcal{V}-\tilde{\mathcal{V}}\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon
, and
\max_{i}\|\mathcal{V}_{i}\|_{\diamond}=1
.
We require every
c_{i}
a positive real number and absorb the phase into
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.
Thus,
\mu\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\Gamma-1}c_{i}
, and
\Pr(i)\coloneqq c_{i}/\mu
is the probability of super-operators
\mathcal{V}_{i}
.
We call
\mu
the
1
-norm of this
(\mu,\varepsilon)
-LCS formula.

Given the definition of the formula in Eq. (
24
), the target map can be effectively implemented with a
\varepsilon
precision.
To achieve this, we employ a Monte-Carlo sampling over the decomposition
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}
, as summarized in Alg.
.
After applying the sampled superoperator, we measure the state on a desired observable
O
and re-normalize the outcome by the 1-norm
\mu
.
The results from the preceding post-processing are supposed to recover the map.

This Monte-Carlo routine to realize the LCS formula seems to require that all the basis superoperators
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}
must be physical to be applied
Nevertheless, we will show that every sampled
\mathcal{V}_{i}
can also be effectively implemented.
We validate this observation by introducing the
unitary-conjugate
basis:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\phi,\alpha,\beta}[\rho]\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{\textup{i}\phi}U_{\alpha}\rho U_{\beta}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{e}^{-\textup{i}\phi}U_{\beta}\rho U_{\alpha}^{\dagger}).

(25)

This family of maps is in general non-CPTP, which cannot be applied directly in a quantum system.
