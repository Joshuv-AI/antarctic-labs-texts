SLRNet: A Real-Time LSTM-Based Sign Language Recognition System
Sharvari Kamble

Abstract
Sign Language Recognition (SLR) plays a crucial role in bridging the communication gap between the hearing-impaired community and the rest of society. In this paper, we present SLRNet , a real-time system that recognizes dynamic hand gestures using a combination of MediaPipe Holistic landmark extraction and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Unlike traditional methods that rely on static image classification or sensor-based gloves, SLRNet processes live video streams via webcam and classifies sequences of hand movements into predefined American Sign Language (ASL) letters and commonly used words . The system extracts 543 landmarks per frame using MediaPipe Holistic and classifies sequences of 30 frames using a stacked LSTM network. We trained the model on a custom dataset comprising all 26 ASL alphabet letters and 10 additional functional words (e.g., ”help”, ”sleep”, ”sorry”, etc.). Despite variability in lighting and background, the system achieves a validation accuracy of 86.7% and maintains real-time responsiveness on standard hardware. This demonstrates the feasibility of live ASL detection using lightweight frameworks such as TensorFlow and OpenCV. Future work includes expanding dataset diversity, improving model robustness, and integrating facial expression recognition for contextual understanding. SLRNet represents a step toward inclusive and accessible real-time sign language translation . Code and dataset available at https://github.com/Khushi-739/SLRNet

I
Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, over 1.5 billion people globally experience some degree of hearing loss, and this number is expected to rise to 2.5 billion by 2050
[
1
]
. Among them, a significant portion relies on sign language as a primary means of communication. However, the communication gap between hearing-impaired individuals and the general population persists due to a lack of widespread sign language proficiency. This gap affects not only social integration but also educational and employment opportunities for the deaf community
[
2
]
.

Sign Language Recognition (SLR) systems aim to bridge this gap by translating manual hand gestures into spoken or written language. Traditional SLR systems often rely on wearable sensors or static image recognition, which limits their real-time usability and flexibility in unconstrained environments
[
3
]
. Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled markerless, real-time gesture recognition using video-based models and pose estimation frameworks such as MediaPipe
[
4
]
.

This research introduces
SLRNet
, a real-time, video-based recognition system built using MediaPipe Holistic and a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network. Unlike many static approaches, our system is designed to recognize not only American Sign Language (ASL) alphabets but also a selection of commonly used words that appear in daily conversation. This hybrid approach addresses a key limitation in earlier systems that were restricted to alphabet-only recognition
[
5
]
.

The system captures webcam video input, extracts 3D body and hand, and processes gesture sequences in real time using LSTM layers. By leveraging this temporal modeling capability, SLRNet contributes to building more natural and robust interfaces for sign language interpretation. This work aims to move toward inclusive design in human-computer interaction (HCI), contributing to assistive technologies that empower the deaf and hard-of-hearing community.

II
Related Work

Sign Language Recognition (SLR)
has evolved significantly over the past decade as a subdomain of
computer vision
, aiming to enhance accessibility for
hearing-impaired individuals
. Existing methods can broadly be categorized into two groups:
static gesture recognition
and
dynamic gesture recognition
.

II-A

Static Gesture Recognition

Early SLR systems focused on
static image classification
using
handcrafted features
combined with traditional
machine learning algorithms
such as
Support Vector Machines (SVMs)
or
k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)
. While effective in controlled settings, these systems lacked scalability and struggled in
real-time environments
.

One of the earliest deep learning approaches by
Bantupalli and Xie

[
2
]
used
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
trained on image-based datasets for
ASL alphabet
recognition. However, such methods treated each frame independently and failed to capture
temporal dependencies
in gestures. Similarly,
Srivastava et al.

[
3
]
applied the
TensorFlow Object Detection API
for static ASL recognition but faced limitations in dataset diversity and generalization under variable conditions.

Nureña-Jara et al.

[
6
]
introduced a 3D keypoint-based system for
Peruvian Sign Language (PSL)
, enhancing spatial understanding. However, their model was prone to errors due to
high intra-class similarity
in hand shapes and required
depth-sensing hardware
, reducing practical applicability.

II-B

Dynamic Gesture Recognition

To overcome the limitations of static methods, recent works have embraced
temporal modeling
.
Xue et al.

[
4
]
proposed an LSTM-based system for
Chinese Sign Language
using
Leap Motion sensors
, which successfully captured motion patterns but was limited by hardware dependency.

Suharjito et al.

[
5
]
implemented a CNN-based model on the
LSA64 dataset
and highlighted the negative impact of
lighting variations
, a challenge we also observed in our own experimentation, especially with
webcam-based data
.

II-C

Limitations and Our Contribution

While these methods contributed valuable insights, most either focus on static gestures or require specialized hardware, limiting real-world applicability. Few existing systems offer a
fully open-source
,
real-time
, and
webcam-compatible
solution capable of recognizing both
ASL alphabet
and
functional words
using a
lightweight architecture
.

SLRNet
addresses these gaps by combining
MediaPipe Holistic
for efficient 3D landmark extraction and
LSTM-based sequence modeling
to recognize dynamic gestures from standard video streams. This design enables practical deployment of inclusive sign language recognition systems on consumer-grade hardware.

III-B

Input Acquisition and Preprocessing

III-B
1
Video Frame Capture

The system captures raw video frames
I_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
at a frame rate of 30 FPS, where
H
and
W
denote the height and width of the frame respectively.

III-B
2
Normalization and Resizing

Each frame is resized to a fixed dimension
H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}
to maintain consistency during landmark detection. Pixel values are normalized to the range
[0,1]
using
min-max normalization
:

I^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{I_{t}-\min(I_{t})}{\max(I_{t})-\min(I_{t})}

This normalization improves the
stability
of the landmark detector under varying lighting conditions.

III-C

Landmark Extraction Using MediaPipe Holistic

III-C
1
MediaPipe Holistic Framework

MediaPipe Holistic

[
4
]
integrates multiple ML models to perform holistic pose, face, and hand landmark detection. It detects
543 landmarks
per frame, each landmark represented as a triplet:

L_{t}=\{(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})\mid i=1,\dots,543\}

where
x_{i},y_{i}
are normalized 2D image coordinates and
z_{i}
encodes relative depth.

III-C
2
Advantages of Landmark-Based Representation

•

Dimensionality Reduction:
Landmark coordinates reduce raw pixel data to structured, semantically meaningful points.

•

Robustness:
Less sensitive to background clutter and lighting variation compared to raw images.

•

Spatial Consistency:
Landmarks preserve geometric relationships crucial for differentiating signs.

III-C
3
Coordinate Normalization

To ensure
invariance to translation, scale, and rotation
, landmarks are normalized relative to a reference point, typically the
wrist joint
for hand gestures:

\hat{L}_{t}=\frac{L_{t}-L_{ref}}{\|L_{max}-L_{min}\|}

where
L_{ref}
is the reference landmark coordinate, and
\|L_{max}-L_{min}\|
is the Euclidean distance between extremal landmarks to normalize scale.

III-D

Spatial Feature Extraction via CNN

III-D
1
Rationale

CNNs
excel in learning hierarchical spatial features through
localized receptive fields
and
shared weights

[
4
]
. In SLRNet, the CNN treats the vectorized normalized landmarks
\hat{L}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{543\times 3}
as structured input.

III-D
2
CNN Architecture

•

Convolutional Layers:

\mathbf{F}^{(l)}=\sigma\left(\mathbf{F}^{(l-1)}*W_{c}^{(l)}+b^{(l)}\right)

•

Pooling Layers:
Downsample feature maps to gain translation invariance.

•

Fully Connected Layers:
Flatten final feature maps into a feature vector
\mathbf{F}_{t}
.

III-D
3
Spatial Feature Vector

\mathbf{F}_{t}=\text{CNN}(\hat{L}_{t};\theta_{CNN})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

III-E

Temporal Sequence Modeling with LSTM

III-E
1
Need for Temporal Modeling

Modeling
temporal dependencies
in sequential frames enables capturing motion patterns and differentiating temporally similar signs.

III-E
2
LSTM Fundamentals

LSTM

[
4
]
overcomes the vanishing gradient problem via
gated memory cells
:

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{f}\cdot[h_{t-1},\mathbf{F}_{t}]+b_{f})

\displaystyle i_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{i}\cdot[h_{t-1},\mathbf{F}_{t}]+b_{i})

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tanh(W_{C}\cdot[h_{t-1},\mathbf{F}_{t}]+b_{C})

\displaystyle C_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}*C_{t-1}+i_{t}*\tilde{C}_{t}

\displaystyle o_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{o}\cdot[h_{t-1},\mathbf{F}_{t}]+b_{o})

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=o_{t}*\tanh(C_{t})

III-E
3
Sequence Input and Sliding Window

\mathbf{H}=\{h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{T}\}

III-F

Classification Layer and Real-Time Feedback

III-F
1
Softmax Classification

\hat{y}=\text{softmax}(W_{fc}h_{T}+b_{fc})

III-F
2
Loss Function and Optimization

\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_{k}\log(\hat{y}_{k})

Optimization:

Adam
optimizer
[
4
]
using
Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)
.

III-F

Real-Time Feedback Mechanism

A
sliding window mechanism
updates predictions frame-by-frame to reduce jitter.

IV
Experiments

IV-A

Experimental Setup

Hardware and Software Configuration

The experiments were conducted on a system equipped with an
Intel Core i7, 11th Generation processor
, supported by
16 GB DDR4 RAM
and a
NVIDIA GTX 1650 GPU with 4 GB VRAM
. The software stack included
TensorFlow 2.x
for deep learning implementation,
OpenCV
for image processing, and
MediaPipe
for landmark detection. Data manipulation and visualization were handled using
NumPy
and
Matplotlib
, respectively. The experiments were performed on both
Windows 11
and
Ubuntu 20.04
environments, using
Python 3.9
as the core programming language.

Dataset Description

The dataset used comprised real-time recorded
RGB video sequences
and static images of hand gestures representing the
26 alphabets (A–Z)
in
American Sign Language (ASL)
. These recordings were captured using standard
webcams
under varied environmental conditions. Each instance was manually labeled and preprocessed into sequences of
543-dimensional landmark vectors
extracted using MediaPipe Holistic. The dataset contained over
1,000 instances per gesture
, incorporating variations in
lighting, background textures, hand sizes
, and
orientations
to ensure diversity and generalizability.

Training Details

The model was trained using the
Adam optimizer
, which adapts the learning rate during training for faster convergence. The
initial learning rate
was set to
0.001
, and training was performed over
50 epochs
with a
batch size of 32
. To prevent overfitting, a
Dropout layer with a rate of 0.3
was incorporated. The
Categorical Cross-Entropy
loss function was used, which is standard for multi-class classification tasks involving probability distribution outputs from softmax.

IV-B

Implementation

The entire implementation follows a modular pipeline derived from the proposed architecture. Initially, the
webcam stream
captures real-time video input, which is then processed by the
MediaPipe Holistic model
to extract
543 3D landmarks
for face, hands, and body. These landmarks are normalized and converted into structured input vectors. The normalized sequences are passed to a
CNN block
to extract spatial features for each frame. Subsequently, a stack of
LSTM units
processes the sequential data, capturing the temporal dynamics of the sign. Finally, a
dense layer with softmax activation
produces probability scores for each class, and the highest probability is displayed as the predicted sign with an accompanying
confidence score
in real-time.

IV-E

Discussion of Results

The integration of
temporal modeling via LSTM
significantly improved classification performance, especially for gestures that are visually similar but differ in motion sequence. The system consistently maintained an
average response latency below 70 milliseconds
, enabling a smooth and responsive user experience through webcam interaction.

However, under
challenging conditions such as low lighting or occlusions
, the model’s accuracy slightly degraded. Additionally, the current system does not utilize
facial expressions
, which are a crucial part of complete sign language understanding.

Despite these limitations, the system runs effectively on a standard laptop without any specialized sensors, which enhances its potential for real-world deployment, especially in
resource-limited settings
.

V
Conclusion

This project successfully implemented a real-time
Sign Language Recognition System
using a hybrid of
CNN-LSTM architecture
and
landmark-based spatial representation
. The system demonstrates high classification accuracy, low latency, and effective performance in webcam-based environments—thereby supporting seamless communication for the hearing-impaired community. By leveraging lightweight yet robust
machine learning models
integrated with
MediaPipe Holistic landmark extraction
, the proposed system remains accessible and deployable without the need for specialized hardware.

This work marks a significant step toward
inclusive communication technologies
, serving as a practical bridge between sign language users and the broader public. Additionally, the system contributes to the expanding domain of
assistive AI applications
, with potential impact across education, customer service, healthcare, and public interaction.

VI
Future Work

While the current system effectively classifies both static and dynamic gestures, several avenues for improvement are identified. A promising next step is to increase the training dataset from approximately
1,000 to 5,000 samples per gesture
, enabling better generalization and robustness across users, lighting conditions, and backgrounds.

Another enhancement involves integrating
Transformer-based architectures
to facilitate
sentence-level translation
, extending beyond isolated gesture recognition. Incorporating
facial expression analysis
via MediaPipe Face Mesh can add contextual understanding through
affective computing
. Furthermore, to support
linguistic diversity
, the system may be extended to recognize regional sign languages such as
Indian Sign Language (ISL)
and
Japanese Sign Language (JSL)
.

Finally, optimizing the model for deployment on
mobile platforms
will make the system more accessible, especially in rural and under-resourced areas. This will move the project closer to its vision of real-world, inclusive, and widely usable sign language recognition technology.
