Preresidency Fellowships in Neurosurgery: A Multi-Institutional, Cross-Sectional Survey of Utility and Outcomes
Authors: Tariq Parker, Odesanya Okikioluwa, Jolene Singh, Khalil St Brice, Felipe Ramirez-Velandia, Emmanuel Mensah, Theresa Williamson, Fedor Panov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Neurosurgery remains one of the most competitive residency specialties in the United States. For applicants, particularly international medical graduates (IMGs), preresidency fellowships (PRFs) have emerged to strengthen applications through research, clinical exposure, and mentorship. However, data on the effectiveness of PRFs in improving residency match outcomes remain limited. This study aims to assess the utility of neurosurgery PRFs in enhancing applicant preparedness, clinical competency, and match success.METHODS:A cross-sectional survey was distributed to current and former neurosurgery PRF participants in the United States between October 2024 and March 2025. Data collected included demographics, match outcomes, and perceptions of PRF utility. Likert scales and composite rankings were used to evaluate experiences and factors contributing to successful or unsuccessful match outcomes.RESULTS:Of 58 publicly identifiable fellows, 33 responded (57% response rate), most of whom (61%) were non-US IMGs. Among those who participated in the match (n = 30), 57% successfully matched into neurosurgery. PRFs were rated as extremely or very useful by 68% of respondents, with the most valued factors for success being PRF experience, letters of recommendation, and research productivity. Respondents who did not match cited low board scores and lack of neurosurgery mentorship as primary barriers. Most participants (88%) reported that they would recommend PRFs to future applicants.CONCLUSION:PRFs serve as valuable preparatory programs that improve match outcomes, especially for IMGs. They enhance clinical exposure, academic productivity, and access to mentorship. Further standardization and expansion of PRFs could increase accessibility and support equity in neurosurgical training pathways.

Neurosurgery, perhaps one of the most competitive residency specialties in the United States, requires applicants to demonstrate exceptional performance across a variety of disciplines.1,2 Applicants strive to distinguish themselves through their clerkships, subinternships, board examinations, and advanced degrees, with research productivity becoming increasingly important.3,4 Despite well-documented advantages in the research output domain, compared with US applicants,5 foreign graduates still face an even more arduous path, requiring stronger recommendations and, ideally, hands-on experience.6,7

Preresidency fellowships (PRFs) were initially established to assist medical graduates in bridging the gap between their medical education and residency training by enhancing their prospects of successfully matching into their desired programs. PRFs have emerged as an alternative, clinically focused opportunity to the traditional research fellowships and preliminary residency programs pursued by foreign medical graduates or unmatched applicants seeking to match into competitive residency programs.8-10 Over time, these programs have evolved to encompass diverse foci, including research opportunities, clinical exposure, and mentorship. Beyond serving as a preparatory stage for residency, PRFs have become a structured platform for academic development and professional training, equipping trainees with the essential skills required to excel in postgraduate medical education.

Despite their growing prevalence and decades of training fellows in various placements beyond neurosurgery, limited literature exists on the trajectory and effectiveness of PRFs in preparing applicants for neurosurgery residency. A data-driven assessment is needed to evaluate their impact on fellows' career paths and placement outcomes.

This study aims to evaluate the utility of PRFs in enhancing applicants' readiness for neurosurgery residency and evaluating the applicants' experience into their overall benefits strengthening their clinical skills, research experience, and overall competitiveness and readiness to transition to residency training.

METHODS

Study Design

The Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys guidelines were followed to design this survey.11 This cross-sectional online survey was created using Survey Monkey (SurveyMonkey Inc.) and used an anonymized open data collection strategy to ensure participant confidentiality.

Participant Recruitment

Eligible participants were past or present neurosurgery PRF participants in the United States. Those who had never completed a PRF were excluded. Recruitment used judgment-based sampling, targeting PRF alumni through institutional programs, online networks, and social media, supplemented by snowball sampling, which encouraged eligible participants to share the survey with their peers. Consent for participation was obtained at the survey outset, and no financial or material incentives were offered.

Survey Design

A structured, open online survey was developed to collect both quantitative and qualitative data regarding participants' experiences with PRFs in neurosurgery. The survey consisted of 14 questions with a mixture of free text, multiple-choice, and Likert-scale questions, along with optional demographic questions on age, sex, and race/ethnicity (Supplementary Appendix 1, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A126). The survey assessed the number and duration of PRFs completed, medical graduate status, PRF length, and residency match attempts and outcomes.

Participants were asked to rank factors influencing match success or failure, including preresidency work experience, research/publications, letters of recommendation (LoRs), US clinical experience (USCE), and board scores. Participants ranked factors contributing to a successful match on a 5 (most contributory) to 1 (least contributory) scale. A weighted average of these ranked factors contributing to a successful match was computed to generate a composite score. Participants rated factors influencing failure to match in a standardized fashion as “Very important,” “Important,” “Less important,” or “Not important.” Likert-scale questions evaluated the perceived utility of PRFs in securing a neurosurgery residency, ranging from “Extremely Useful” to “Not at All Useful,” and the likelihood of recommending PRFs to future applicants, ranging from “Definitely Would” to “Definitely Would Not.” A balanced, predetermined set of descriptors was also provided for participants to indicate their perspectives on the PRF experience. The survey was pretested with 1 current neurosurgery preresidency fellow to assess clarity, relevance, and ease of completion. Feedback from this initial review informed minor refinements to question wording and survey flow before broader distribution. The estimated time to complete the survey was under 3 minutes.

Data Collection

The survey was distributed through e-mail to PRF program coordinators, directly to PRF fellows with publicly available contact information, as well as through professional networks, social media, and alumni groups. Participants had a six-month period to respond to the survey (October 2024-March 2025). All participants completed the survey, and IP addresses were cross-referenced with survey responses to remove duplicate entries. Partial responses were retained and analyzed where appropriate. Graphs were generated using the SurveyMonkey platform (SurveyMonkey Inc), and descriptive statistics were performed on Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Excel Version 2605, Build 16.0.20026.20168; Microsoft Corporation). Reminder messages were distributed at regular intervals over the six-month collection period to encourage participation, including direct follow-up with program coordinators and periodic reposting on social media platforms. All responses were stored on securely using SurveyMonkey. No identifiable information was collected, and data access was restricted to the research team.

Data availability

All data tables are saved securely and can be made available upon request.

RESULTS

Thirteen programs which offered neurosurgery PRFs were identified in the United States, of which 1 program assisted with participant recruitment (Figure 1). Fifty-eight current and former preresidency fellows were identified based on a comprehensive search of publicly available institutional data, alumni resources/references, and social media. Thirty-four responses to the survey were received, and after cross-referencing IP addresses, 1 response was excluded as a duplicate. Thirty-three individual survey responses were included, which represented a 57% response rate (33/58). Of those who attempted the survey, we observed a 94% survey completion rate.

Of these respondents, 61% were non-US international medical graduates (IMGs) (20/33), 18% were US allopathic medical graduates (MDs) (6/33), 18% were US IMGs (6/33), and 3% were US osteopathic medical graduates (DOs) (1/33) (Figure 2A). A total of 72% of respondents were male (24/33), 22% were female (7/33), 3% preferred not to say (1/33), and 3% did not respond (1/33). A total of 85% of respondents pursued only 1 PRF (28/33), whereas the remainder pursued 2 (5/33; 15%). Most commonly, survey respondents had completed 1-2 years of PRF (16/33; 48.5%), followed by 6-12 months (9/33; 27%), >2 years (7/33; 21%), and <6 months (1/33; 3%) (Figure 2B).

A total of 90% (30/33) of survey respondents participated in the residency match process, of which 53% (16/30) successfully matched in neurosurgery, 13% (4/30) matched a different specialty (radiation oncology, pathology, anesthesia, and preliminary general surgery), and 30% (10/30) did not match. The adjusted success rate for matching into neurosurgery after PRF was 57% (17/30), accounting for 1 participant who matched after transitioning from a preliminary general surgery residency position (Table 1, Figure 2C).

Of the respondents who matched into other specialties (n = 4), 2 indicated that another specialty was a better fit and 1 reported that neurosurgery requirements were too difficult to obtain, whereas another respondent believed that despite completing more than 3 years of fellowship, low board scores were an insurmountable barrier for a neurosurgery match.

A majority of respondents reported a positive view of the utility of PRFs in matching into neurosurgery, with 68% (21/31) rating PRFs as either very useful or extremely useful. The remainder found it to be somewhat useful (7/31; 23%), not so useful (2/31; 6%), or not at all useful (1/31; 3%) (Figure 3A). Most of the respondents indicated that they definitely would (17/33; 52%) or probably would (12/33; 36%) recommend PRF to others interested in neurosurgery. The remainder probably would not (3/33; 9%) and definitely would not (1/33; 3%) recommend the program (Figure 3B). Every respondent who matched neurosurgery had positive feelings about recommending the fellowship (definitely recommend, n = 12 [71%]; probably would, n = 5 [29%]). While those who matched outside of neurosurgery had mixed responses (definitely recommend, n = 1 [33%]; probably would not, n = 2 [67%]), most of the respondents who did not match would recommend the fellowship (definitely recommend, n = 2 [20%]; probably would, n = 6 [60%]; probably would not, n = 1 [10%]; definitely would not, n = 1 [10%]).

The most highly ranked factors contributing to a successful match were preresidency work experience (composite score 4.2), followed by LoRs (composite score 3.7), research experience/publications (composite score 3.5), other USCE (composite score 1.9), and “other” (composite score 1.6) (Figure 3C). When asked to elaborate on these other factors, respondents wrote that the ability to perform neurosurgical procedures and take call at the level of a junior resident were factors that made it more likely to match, whereas lack of a neurosurgery department in medical school, lack of US mentors, and lack of advocates in the field were factors that made it difficult to match.

On average, those who matched neurosurgery ranked the factors contributing to their success in descending order, as follows: preresidency work experience, letters, research, and other/USCE. By contrast, those who matched outside of the specialty ranked the contributory factors as letters, preresidency work experience, other/US clinical experience, and research (Tables 2 and 3).

Among the respondents who did not match into neurosurgery, low board scores were cited as the most important factor which contributed to an unsuccessful match, followed by lack of USCE, weak LoRs, and lack of research/publications (Figure 3D).

For descriptors of the PRF experience, most of the responses reflected a positive description of their experience (86/136 unique responses; 63%). The most common descriptors used were educational (28/33; 85%), beneficial (26/33; 79%), and rewarding (23/33; 70%). Of the negative descriptors, challenging was the most commonly identified term (21/33; 64%), followed by stressful (20/33; 60%). DISCUSSION

This study presents the first comprehensive, multi-institutional survey-based assessment of neurosurgical PRFs in the United States and their impact on match outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that PRFs serve as a valuable bridge to neurosurgery residency, particularly for non-US IMGs, who comprised most (61%) of the respondents. On average, non-US IMGs who matched neurosurgery considered PRF work experience to be the highest contributory factor to their success.

The adjusted match rate into neurosurgery among PRF participants was 57%, with an additional 13% matching into other specialties. One PRF program had publicly available information on PRF outcomes, with 42.5% (17/40) matching into neurosurgery residency in the United States and an additional 50% (20/40) pursuing US residencies in other fields (92.5% total residency match rate).12 Of note, these publicly available outcomes may not be representative of all PRF outcomes who have pursued the fellowship. These results are in line with recent literature13 and highlights the utility of PRFs in enhancing the competitiveness and match success of applicants, when compared with the 30%-33% neurosurgery match rates observed among US and non-US IMGs based on the most recent data from the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP).14 More recent NRMP data further demonstrate widening disparities in neurosurgery match outcomes across applicant backgrounds. Non-US IMG neurosurgery match rates declined from approximately 31% in 202415 to 28% in 202516 and 23% in 2026,17 whereas US MD match rates remained comparatively stable between 68% and 70% during the same period. Over the past decade, approximately 18 of 170 IMGs successfully matching into neurosurgery were identifiable as previous PRF participants although equivalent longitudinal PRF participation data for US MD applicants remain unavailable. These findings support the notion that PRFs may represent 1 mechanism through which select applicants, particularly IMGs, seek to strengthen competitiveness within an increasingly selective residency landscape.

We also identified that PRFs with US MD degrees demonstrated a higher match success rate when compared with IMGs and may reflect a variety of barriers/biases that foreign medical graduates still face in applying to neurosurgery residency.18 In the 2026 match cycle, there were 491 neurosurgery applicants, with 349 US MDs, 19 US DOs, 61 non-US IMGs, and 12 US IMGs. Within this broader context, identified PRF-associated matches (4 US MDs and 2 IMGs) represented a relatively small but notable subset of successful applicants. These findings support the premise that the role and potential benefit of PRFs likely differ substantially between applicant cohorts. For IMGs, PRFs may function as a mechanism to establish US-based clinical credibility, mentorship, and advocacy in the absence of traditional domestic training pathways. Conversely, for US MD applicants, PRFs may serve more commonly as a supplemental research or academic strengthening opportunity.

The importance of PRFs in providing access to US-based clinical experience and mentorship is especially apparent for IMGs. PRFs help mitigate these barriers by providing immersive training environments where fellows gain hands-on experience, strengthen their networks, and receive targeted guidance on the US match process. In our cohort, matched candidates cited preresidency clinical work and strong LoRs as the most influential factors in their success. Importantly, many of the factors ranked independently by respondents—including LoRs, mentorship, and US clinical experience—are intrinsically interconnected within the PRF experience itself. As such, PRFs may best be conceptualized not as a single variable, but rather as a structured environment through which several highly valued application-enhancing opportunities are simultaneously obtained. Although statistical analysis is limited by small sample size, our data suggest that research was more contributory to successful neurosurgery match (3.5/5) compared with matching other specialties (2/5). Our study lacks objective data (ie, number of publications) to determine whether there were associated differences in research output between those who matched neurosurgery and those who did not. Nevertheless, these findings complement data from the NRMP, which has demonstrated a higher number of publications for those who successfully match into neurosurgery.14

We conclude from our data that low board scores were often an important factor in failure to match and, based on feedback provided in the open-response format, more years of PRF experience might not be able to mitigate this barrier. Thus, PRFs may help offset many, but not all, obstacles faced by applicants. While this has put additional stress on the candidates over the past few decades, the recent changes in STEP I score outputs (pass/fail) may mitigate this for most current and future PRFs.

The survey design adhered to Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys reporting guidelines and used judgment-based recruitment, a common strategy in neurosurgical survey research.19 Survey fatigue and lack of methodological transparency remain major barriers to affect in the field. Our multiplatform dissemination strategy with periodic reminders likely contributed to a strong response rate. Future surveys may benefit from similar standardized instruments to enhance reproducibility and cross-study comparisons.

Limitations and Future Directions

The major limitation of this study is the potential for selection bias, limiting its generalizability. For example, those candidates selected for a PRF program may represent stronger potential match applicants and hence higher overall match rates. In addition, those PRFs who responded to the survey may over-represent those with positive experiences/outcomes or, because of the need for strict anonymity of our survey, over-represent those from a specific PRF program, hampering our ability to accurately describe this inherently heterogenous experience. In the absence of a centralized national registry for neurosurgical PRFs, this study was limited to publicly identifiable programs and alumni. Consequently, some historical or institutionally internal PRF pathways might not have been captured, potentially leading to under-representation of the overall PRF landscape. Given the time, investment, and often personal sacrifice, required to pursue a PRF, institutions may also wish to explore avenues to increase public transparency of these programs to make them more accessible, especially for IMGs who may face additional hardship because of increasing visa and financial constraints. Finally, integrating PRFs within existing educational frameworks can generate longitudinal mentorship opportunities that extend into residency and beyond. These efforts will be critical for ensuring that PRFs continue to serve as an engine of equity and an effective mechanism to prepare highly motivated, capable applicants for the rigors of neurosurgical residency.

CONCLUSION

This study underscores the significant utility of PRFs as a strategic stepping stone for applicants interested in pursuing neurosurgery residency in the United States. Beyond simply improving match statistics, these programs offer immersive, mentored experiences that foster clinical maturity, academic engagement, and professional integration into the neurosurgical community. Fellows across diverse backgrounds seem to obtain meaningful professional growth, regardless of the eventual match outcome. As competitiveness within the neurosurgical specialty continues to grow, PRFs may play an increasing role in bolstering clinical preparedness for prospective residents, while also serving as a mechanism to nurture global talent and recruitment within neurosurgery. Broader institutional investment and longitudinal tracking of participant outcomes will be key to refining the structure of these important programs and ensuring their long-term value.
