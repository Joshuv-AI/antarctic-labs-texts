Analyzing bamboo-based enterprises in Injibara, Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Assayech Agidew Bogale, Dagim Fikir Abate, Jigar Yirsaw, Adamu Worku Kassa, Amsalu Nigatu Alamerew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bamboo has been recognized for its ecological, economic, and cultural importance in Ethiopia. However, bamboo-based enterprises remain underdeveloped and poorly understood, particularly in developing towns like Injibara. Limited research has been conducted on the characteristics, profitability, and growth prospects of bamboo-based enterprises. Such information is essential for evidence-based policy-making and sustainable development efforts. This study examines the structure, profitability, and growth potential of bamboo enterprises in Injibara town, which is selected for its rich bamboo resources and active small-scale industry. Data was collected in 2022/23 through key informant interviews, surveys, and field observations using a complete census of 60 bamboo-based enterprises. Accordingly, 44 processors, 7 producers, and 9 traders were contacted as data sources. Descriptive statistics and financial analysis techniques were used to evaluate enterprise performance. The results reveal substantial income variation across enterprise types, with processors showing the highest value addition. Profit margins range from 5.6% to 45.95%, showing significant disparities in value capture. Notably, industrial bamboo products—such as chopsticks, incense sticks, and charcoal—provide employment opportunities to a workforce that is approximately 50% women, emphasizing the sector’s potential for gender-inclusive development. Despite strong opportunities rooted in indigenous craftsmanship and resource availability, the sector faces critical constraints, including unreliable supply chains, weak market linkages, and insufficient policy support, all of which impede sustainable growth. The study underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions that enhance value addition and promote equitable development within the bamboo sector. Key recommendations include integrated stand management, comprehensive value chain analysis, improved product quality and durability, and environmental cost-benefit assessments. Additionally, strengthening the sector will require combining indigenous knowledge systems with modern processing technologies to improve productivity, competitiveness, and sustainability.

Introduction

In Ethiopia, bamboo is emerging as a potential alternative for environmentally impactful products. This is due to the biological and physiological properties of bamboo and the abundance of bamboo resources1]2, 3. National environmental initiatives further catalyze bamboo exploration4. It is becoming a sustainable potential raw material for the emerging industries of cottage, particleboard, fiberboard, and pulp and paper1]2. With its wider national distribution and cross-sectoral applicability, bamboo has become suitable for all levels of processing and industrial activities, ranging from small-scale hand-craft-based entities to modern and highly integrated industries5, 6]7, 8.

Bamboo-based enterprises are among business entities that play an important role in employment opportunities, and the generation of income mainly from bamboo culms6, 9, 10. These enterprises make a significant contribution to sustaining the economy through providing employment opportunities within the value chain, ranging from growing bamboo culms to processing and marketing for significant community groups, especially for rural and poor community groups5, 11, 12. In Ethiopia, the bamboo business, ranging from stand management to the processing and marketing of bamboo products, is emerging and characterized by few technological applications; weak, volatile, and mainly local demand-centered market dimensions; and networking13. Such bamboo-based micro and small-scale enterprises are practiced in both rural areas and peri-urban or urban areas, following the main roadside14.

Injibara (the northwestern part of the country) is among the areas endowed with abundant bamboo resource potential15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 12, where different levels of bamboo-based enterprises are operating. Based on the initial capital and number of employees, the bamboo processing enterprises in Injibara are categorized into three categories, namely, micro, small, and medium-scale processors. Microscale enterprises are businesses established with under 50,000 and hire a maximum of 5 employees. Small and medium-scale enterprises are enterprises established with initial capital of 50,001-500,000 and 1.5 million-20 million, respectively, and that hired 6–30 and 30–100 employees, respectively20, 10,40.

Historically, bamboo has contributed approximately USD 2,524,572 to Ethiopia’s GDP and supports the livelihoods of approximately 750,000 people, even though the volume of bamboo-based products exported is below its potential21. It placed third level above other sources of income22. Owing to the abundance of bamboo and forest resources, both naturally and in plantations, as well as the demand for bamboo products, households engaged in bamboo-related business activities in the Awi zone were found to generate 38%−60% of their annual cash income from bamboo-based enterprises12,41.

Some studies have been conducted on various aspects of bamboo, both at the national and local levels. Those studies focused on the distribution and potential resource status of bamboo forests, the value chain, socioeconomic contributions, indigenous management practices, and the productivity of bamboo 15, 16, 18, 19,42. The development trends, product characterizations, and economic feasibility of bamboo-based traditional handcrafters are underexplored15,23. This might be associated with limited technological applications, less attention to the sector, awareness among users. Previous studies have indicated that Ethiopian bamboo processing is limited to some areas and is characterized by poor processing and low-value addition, such as finishing5. Evaluating the performance and challenges of bamboo-based micro and small enterprises is among the important ways to draw strategic paths toward the optimization of such enterprises, particularly the bamboo sector in general24.

Although the resource status of bamboo has been well documented and understood, little attention has been paid to why bamboo-based enterprises struggle to scale, achieve sustained profitability, or transition beyond small-scale operations. Thus, this research was initiated to investigate the characteristics, growth, and profitability of bamboo-based enterprises. This investigation is crucial not only for enterprises and the bamboo sector but also for policymakers and practitioners to develop a sustainable management plan and make informed decisions. Such decisions support equitable benefit sharing within the value chain of bamboo and the wider bamboo-based industry.

Methodology

Study area description

The study was conducted in Injibara, an administrative town in the Awi Zone, Amhara Region, and northwestern Ethiopia (Fig. 1). Geographically located at 10°57′N latitude and 36°56′E longitude, it is 447 km northwest of Addis Ababa (the national capital) and 118 km south of Bahir Dar, the regional capital25.

Its average elevation is 2540 m above sea level26 with a range between 2300 and 3500 m above sea level. This elevation range is suitable for the production and management of highland bamboo27. Thus, the study area was selected due to the presence of indigenous craftsmen and the potential of highland bamboo plantations. According to the Awi Zone Office of Agricultural Development (OAD) and unpublished data, the bamboo area covers 2380 hectares of land in the urban and peri-urban areas of Injibara. The local economy is driven by agriculture, charcoal production, trade, and bamboo- and wood-based production systems.

Sampling

A reconnaissance survey was initially carried out, and a census was carried out in five kebeles (Kebele is the smallest governmental administrative unit in Ethiopian governance system) within the urban areas of Injibara from three categories of respondents (bamboo culm producers, bamboo processors, and bamboo culm traders). Accordingly, the survey included seven bamboo culm producers, nine traders, and 44 bamboo processors. A total of 60 bamboo enterprises were included in the census. The selection criterion for producers was the area of bamboo stand; therefore, owners that have bamboo in an area of 0.125 hectares and above were considered for the current study because farmers with less than 0.125 hectares mainly manage supplementary or household consumption, not as a primary source (farmers and local government). A total of 10 key informants were contacted. They were composed of elderly, senior experts (both from forest management and processing), and trade officers.

Methods of data collection and analysis

Data was collected through combinations of key informant interviews, surveys, and field observations from bamboo processors, traders, and culm producers. Both qualitative and quantitative data were analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively by descriptive statistical analysis (percentage, mean, and frequency) and financial model analysis. In the field observation notes, photos, and key informant survey data were analyzed qualitatively to identify themes and generate insights for comparison with the quantitative results. The results from the qualitative analysis are presented in narrative form and supported with tables and graphs when needed. For quantitative data, descriptive statistics such as percentages, means, and frequencies were calculated.

Furthermore, financial performance indicators, including growth, profitability, and profit margins, were estimated to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the performance of bamboo-based enterprises in the case study areas.

Analysis of Growth Most bamboo processors, especially microscale processors, have limited capital in cash but develop basic assets (such as building or buying a house) after they enter bamboo-based businesses. Then, to determine their value, their assets were converted to monetary value following the current market prices of each asset. The growth of small-scale processors was then estimated by the difference between the initial capital and the amount of capital from bamboo at the time of data collection.

A correlation analysis was performed between enterprise age and capital change. This was done only for bamboo processors, as the overall growth of bamboo growers is a combination of other income sources, and this study did not consider the relative value of bamboo for these groups.

Results and discussion

Demographic characteristics of bamboo-based enterprises

Bamboo-based enterprises are male-dominated. Only 14.3% of the bamboo culm producers are female, whereas all the processors and traders are male. Over half of the culm producers are illiterate, and approximately 85.7% are married. 60% of traders and 65% of processors completed secondary school. In the case of marriage, 88.9% of the traders are married, whereas most (75%) of the processors are unmarried. Therefore, bamboo-based enterprises are mostly practiced by young and unmarried male processors, while married adults and middle-aged individuals dominate trading and production activities. Bamboo-based enterprises cover from educated up to completed secondary school and those who are uneducated (Table 1).

Enterprises’ structure and product lines

According to the definition of the federal micro- and small-scale enterprise development agency20. Bamboo-based enterprises are categorized into three categories depending on scale or size. Specifically, micro, small, and medium-scale bamboo-based enterprises are considered. Fifty-eight enterprises are micro-scale, including bamboo culm producers, which have five or fewer than five employees. There is one small-scale processing enterprise with 10 employees and one medium-scale processing enterprise, which is privately owned and has 40 employees. However, all these bamboo-based enterprises are not formal30. Formal bamboo-based enterprises are registered and have a license, whereas informal enterprises are not registered and have no license except for medium- and small-scale bamboo enterprises. Most of the respondents are not interested in becoming formal because of the fear of taxation followinghand-craftede and value addition, which might reduce the number of customers looking for less value-added or handcrafted products. This sight discourages formal registration and acts as a significant barrier to business expansion, access to financial services, and participation in broader markets. Therefore, there should be subsidies or tax-free advantages to promote enterprises in this group. Moreover, value-added and formal bamboo products have received fewer premium payments than other timber products31, 32, 33, 23, implying the need for support or subsidies.

Bamboo-based processors produce a wide range of products. Most enterprises produce similar products. Qolemshesh, charcoal, tooth stick, sandalwood, and chopsticks are processed less frequently. The customer demand for Qolemshesh is not as high as that for others. This product is produced following preorders from customers, and it is mostly for roadside clothing sellers and shoeshine workers. Tooth sticks, incense sticks, and charcoal are the main products produced by the medium processor. This is because of relatively high initial investment or input costs and the demand for advanced technological equipment. Similarly34, 31, 32, 33, 23 reported that most of the micro and small-scale enterprises are handcrafted products under traditional informal enterprises. As shown in Table 2 above, the most produced products are three-seat chairs, stools, and triple shelves because of the demand for these products. Based on the demand, products are available in the case of Hula and Gumer districts, Ethiopia29. However, the similarity of products leads to increased competition, resulting in price discounts during sales23.

Different types of products are manufactured by three types of bamboo-based business enterprises. Most of the time, chairs, benches, tables, shelves, and mats are produced around towns, and grain granaries, baskets, and beehives are produced in peri-urban and rural areas14, 31, 32. However, in other countries such as Bangladesh, micro- and small-scale bamboo-based enterprises have been developed more widely throughout rural areas for socioeconomic development35. Products produced at different scales of processors are different because of the differences between their production systems. Because of their input costs and relatively advanced machinery, micro- and small-scale enterprises cannot produce products like those of medium-scale enterprises. The reverse is also true, as medium-scale enterprises are focusing on more value-added and nationally demanded products. Therefore, medium-scale processors need more value addition by investing more.

Market and input dynamics

According to the survey findings, there are five main marketing niches where bamboo-based products are sold: (i) at the production site or in the surrounding area; (ii) the regional city of Amhara; (iii) different woredas of the Awi zone; (iv) some zonal towns of the Amhara region; and (v) Addis Ababa. The niches varied by the products and to which they were sent. For example, market destinations for products such as chopsticks, toothpicks, and incense sticks are sent to Addis Ababa and Bahir Dar, whereas charcoal is sent only to the capital city of the country. The majority (50%) of micro- and small-scale processors sold their products at the site of production because they produce depending on the requests and demands of nearby customers. However, 40% of the processors sell their products to different districts within the zone. The remaining 10% sell their products out of the zone to the districts of nearby zones such as the Burie and Fenote Selam areas. Although bamboo-based processors in Injibara and around Injibara town generally produce a variety of products, these products are used only for domestic consumption. Similarly, bamboo-based products from the zone are not exported from the country29, 13.

This product is transported by different transportation systems, including human labor. Transportation to the working area is either by human labor or by animal-driven carts. Human labor, horse-driven carts, and hand carts are transportation systems for both products and inputs of bamboo culms. Vehicle transportation is common for transporting culms and products to other nearby zones and districts from Injibara.

All bamboo processors use bamboo culm sources from nearby farmers. There are no processors that have their own plantation sites. Small- and medium-scale bamboo processing enterprises use chemical treatments during preprocessing to enhance product value and extend shelf life by preventing culm decay caused by fungal and bacterial infestations36. Bamboo input is easily available and not reported as a major constraint for most enterprises, except for 4.4% of enterprises that reported a shortage of bamboo input as one of their challenges. Although medium-sized enterprises are encouraged by increasing demand for premium bamboo products, the lack of uniformity in the culm size of nearby bamboo supply sources is challenging for producing products that meet the standards of end users. As a result, they collect bamboo culms from distant areas that incur additional costs for them as well as the product users. Even though tools are mandatory for bamboo processors, approximately 90% of micro-scale bamboo processors operate the processing manually with simple and basic hand tools because of financial constraints in accessing the machinery they are looking for. This is in line with the case of the Dawuro Zone, the southern part of the country, reported by Kassahun17, which states the use of traditional and manually operated tools that are common bamboo processing tools for local market products. The introduction and adoption of modern machinery and technology could largely increase the productivity and creativity of bamboo products. Therefore, the highest-cost tools have been used by medium bamboo processors to add value to bamboo. Moreover, the current political instability is strongly affecting the productivity and profitability of the enterprises. It affects both in impeding transportation of inputs and products to and from processing spots to other locations and inputs from other locations to processing spots.

Enterprise growth and socioeconomic impacts

An enterprise’s growth is typically assessed by two basic indicators, namely, invested capital and profit. In the case of capital investment, the growth of bamboo-based enterprises is measured by comparing initial start-up capital with current capital investment. The minimum starting capital for micro-scale bamboo processors is 50 ETB, and for traders, it is 500 ETB. In contrast, a medium bamboo-based processor requires 12,000,000 ETB in starting capital and has a current turnover of 18,000,000 ETB within five years. Generally, the starting capital of all bamboo-based enterprises is lower than that of other wood-based enterprises, except for medium-sized bamboo processors.

Micro-scale enterprises have an average initial capital of 289.70 ETB, reaching an average capital of 163,164 ETB, with an average growth of 162,874 ETB over approximately 8 years. For a small-scale processor, it began with 1000 ETB and increased to its current capital of 1,000,000 ETB, with a growth of 999,000 ETB in 5 years. Medium-scale processors began their investment with an initial capital of 12,000,000 ETB and increased to 30,000,000 ETB, with a growth of 18,000,000 ETB. According to the results, medium-scale processors presented greater capital growth. Compared with small-scale and medium-scale processors, micro-scale processors have low capital growth. Therefore, there should be strong and coordinated actions by stakeholders, including the government, to support micro- and small-scale bamboo-based enterprises and medium-level enterprises to increase their socioeconomic benefits and optimize the national value of bamboo forest resources (Table 3).

Bamboo processing plays a vital role in a household’s livelihood. It is a major livelihood source not only for bamboo processors but also for employees. Notably, 37% of the respondents in this study were students who supported their education, even up to the completion of a first degree, hired by processors or by selling bamboo-based products, which they produced. Moreover, 55% of the processors depend only on bamboo processing and related activities for their income, whereas only 7.5% of the processors engage in supplementary work outside of their full-time job. Generally, more than 90% of processors have no alternative income source beyond bamboo processing. This is in line with37, which stated bamboo for sustainable economic development, poverty reduction, or combinations of provisional services (constructions, furniture, shoots for food, and medicinal values.

This business has supported most processors in fulfilling their basic needs, including their own housing, which underscores the economic significance of bamboo processing within the study areas and beyond. Bamboo has been a significant economic sector and among the top export commodities in countries such as China, demonstrating a strong contribution of bamboo resources to the national and international market and livelihood38.

Furthermore, small- and medium-scale bamboo processors are reliable employment avenues for the future because of their current potential, as indicated in their plans. Medium-scale processors are also potentially suited to a wider scope of academic backgrounds, ranging from secondary school competencies to professional diplomas and degree holders, enabling youths and employees to support their livelihoods. Bamboo is a potential economic resource capable of generating employment opportunities29, 39, 35. These enterprises have significantly increased their employment potential with the current employees of 40 workers, who were initially 20 over the past 5 years. On the other hand, small-scale processors have 10 employees hired on a monthly salary basis. More importantly, both scales of processing facilitate gender inclusion in the workforce. The medium-scale bamboo-based enterprise took great advantage in terms of gender inclusion, where up to half of the workers were females. Gender inclusion is also evident in the inclusive nature of the bamboo business43. Employment, including both males and female are advantageous in bamboo-based enterprises like other sectors29. The sector is a potential source of jobs not only for permanent firms but also for temporary work opportunities. A similar finding is documented in Bangladesh35. Historically, bamboo culms have been used mainly for the construction of traditional homes for dwelling, but now, culm producers use them financially, highlighting the significant economic importance of bamboo cultivation.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study mainly focuses on evidence-based evaluations of bamboo-based enterprises through examining their key characteristics, including enterprise categories, profitability, growth patterns, and socioeconomic importance. The enterprises assessed in this study fall into micro-scale, small-scale, and medium-scale categories. Despite its contributions, the study is geographically limited to urban areas and does not include rural sites where bamboo production and related activities may differ significantly. Additionally, although the study examines enterprise characteristics, it does not address the full bamboo value chain, which limits the depth of analysis on upstream and downstream linkages.

Conclusions and future implications

The study revealed that most bamboo-based enterprises are owned and operated by unmarried young males who attended a minimum of secondary school during their academic journeys. Bamboo is used to produce products ranging from traditional handicrafts to modernized products. In terms of value addition, bamboo culm producers which have profit margins and small-scale processors demonstrate greater economic advantages than microscale processors do. Over half of bamboo processors manage their lives entirely through bamboo-related economic activities. Although the growth levels of bamboo-processing enterprises vary, they all demonstrate income growth, which is positively correlated with the year of establishment. These enterprises possess strong indigenous knowledge of bamboo cultivation and processing, and they continue to operate even under challenging conditions, such as during the COVID-19 outbreak, weather extremes, and animal encroachment. The growing demand for bamboo products and significant resource potential both in natural areas and plantations are among the strong opportunities for future growth for bamboo-based enterprises and the sector in general.

The study revealed the significant role of women employed in the medium-scale sector, where they constitute 50% of the workforce. This is not just a reflection of the sector’s growth, but also an important step toward achieving gender-equitable development in the area.

Based on the findings of our study, the following implications are proposed for optimizing the value and services of bamboo in the study area and at the national level.
