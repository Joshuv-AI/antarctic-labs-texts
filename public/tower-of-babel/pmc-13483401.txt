Reflections by licensed practical nurses and nursing assistants on the violent behaviours of individuals receiving mental health care in an adult acute psychiatric ward: a qualitative study
Authors: Mphedziseni Esther Rangwaneni, Ndidzulafhi Selina Raliphaswa, Thingahangwi Cecilia Masutha, Mary Maluleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViolence in adult acute psychiatric wards remains a significant occupational hazard for nursing staff, particularly Enrolled Nurses (equivalent to Licensed Practical Nurses) and nursing assistants who often provide direct patient care without specialized psychiatric training. Exposure to patient-perpetrated violence has serious emotional and professional consequences. This study explored and described the reflections of Licensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants on the violent behaviours of individuals receiving mental health care in adult acute psychiatric wards.MethodsA qualitative, exploratory study informed by a phenomenological orientation was employed. Twenty Licensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants were purposively sampled from four adult acute psychiatric wards in Limpopo Province, South Africa. Twenty participants (7 Licensed Practical Nurses and 13 Nursing Assistants) participated. Semi-structured individual interviews were conducted, audio-recorded, and analyzed using Reflective Thematic Analysis as described by Braun and Clarke. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.ResultsTwo major themes and six subthemes were identified: (1) Experiences of patient-perpetrated violence, including physical violence, verbal abuse, and destruction of ward infrastructure during routine care, which compromised safety and disrupted service delivery; and (2) Emotional impact of patient violence, encompassing fear, anger, and frustration. Participants reported frequent exposure to aggression, which significantly affected emotional well-being and professional functioning. Various coping mechanisms were employed to manage the psychological impact of working in high-risk psychiatric environments.ConclusionLicensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants in adult acute psychiatric wards face significant physical and verbal violence, with profound emotional and professional consequences. These findings highlight the need for organisational interventions that prioritise trauma-informed training, structured debriefing, improved ward safety, and strengthened supervision for non-psychiatrically trained nursing staff. Addressing these systemic factors is essential for promoting staff well-being and sustaining safe therapeutic environments in psychiatric care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04775-w.

Introduction

Violence in adult acute psychiatric wards remains a significant occupational hazard for nursing staff worldwide. This study focuses on Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs) and Nursing Assistants (NAs). LPNs are referred to as Enrolled Nurses (ENs) in South Africa, Enrolled Nurses in Australia and New Zealand, and Registered Practical Nurses (RPNs) in Canada and parts of the United States. Those categories of nurses are recognized as second-level nurses who deliver essential front-line patient care under the supervision of Registered Nurses (RNs) or other healthcare professionals [1–3]. In the South African context, LPNs provide direct bedside care but often lack specialized mental health training, placing them at increased risk of patient-perpetrated violence due to their close and continuous contact with mental health care users [4].

The World Health Organization(WHO) defines violence as the intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or a group, which can result in injury, psychological harm, or deprivation [5]. In adult acute psychiatric wards, violent behaviours such as physical assault, verbal abuse, and property destruction are frequently reported and often occur during routine care activities, including medication administration, mealtimes, and patient supervision. These incidents disrupt therapeutic care and pose significant occupational risks for nursing staff, particularly those without specialised psychiatric training [6, 7].

Contemporary mental health care increasingly emphasizes patient-centred approaches, recognizing individuals as active partners in decision-making and risk management. Strategies such as joint crisis plans and co-produced safety tools enable patients to articulate their own risk indicators, preferences, and de-escalation strategies, a process shown to reduce conflict and strengthen therapeutic relationships [8, 9]. This growing body of language around co-production reflects a shift toward shared responsibility, in which patients co-develop risk plans with staff and actively reflect on their understanding of their own risks and those posed to others. Such collaborative planning is associated with improved safety outcomes and enhanced therapeutic engagement; however, its implementation in adult acute psychiatric wards remains challenging due to high patient acuity, involuntary admissions, staffing shortages, and limited resources, which often exacerbate aggression and staff distress [10].

Aggression and violence by mental health care users (MHCUs) in acute psychiatric wards have been critical issues for decades. The Mental Health Care Act (MHCA) No. 17 of 2002 of South Africa defines MHCUs as individuals admitted receiving care, treatment, and rehabilitation, who should be provided with appropriate care by mental health practitioners [11]. In this study, the terms “mental health care users” and “individuals receiving mental health care” are used interchangeably. Factors such as patient psychopathology, substance abuse, and involuntary admissions often contribute to the escalation of violence in acute psychiatric wards [12, 13]. Aggression encompasses physical violence, verbal abuse, property destruction, and self-harm. Physical and verbal aggression are categorized as overt forms typically involving hitting, pushing, or name-calling, and property destruction is often viewed as a manifestation of reactive or proactive aggression within clinical and forensic settings [14].

Historically, approaches to managing aggression in psychiatric settings have relied on restrictive interventions such as seclusion and restraint. However, current guidelines advocate less restrictive, patient-centered strategies, mandating that seclusion and restraint be used only when behavior poses an immediate risk to physical safety and after all other interventions have been exhausted [13, 15]. Emerging evidence suggests that systemic factors, including ward climate, institutional culture, and team dynamics, profoundly influence aggression and de-escalation processes. Supportive environments and collaborative teamwork have been shown to reduce the frequency and intensity of violent encounters, underscoring the need for organizational interventions beyond individual behavior [5].

An adult acute psychiatric ward is a hospital-protected emergency room for patients at risk of harm or suicide, aiming to ensure safety and provide comprehensive assessment and treatment, often mandated by mental health legislation such as South Africa’s MHCA No.17 of 2002 [11]. Violence in adult acute psychiatric wards has long-term psychological consequences for nurses, such as post-traumatic stress, increased anxiety, depression, and a sense of helplessness. These emotional experiences can affect their ability to provide compassionate care and, over time, may lead to professional disengagement and high turnover [16].

In South Africa, nurses are categorized into four groups: Nursing Assistants (trained for one year), Enrolled Nurses (trained for two years), registered nurses/midwives (trained for three to four years), and specialist registered nurses/midwives (who undergo additional training after registration) [17]. Collectively, these individuals are often the first responders to ward incidents, yet they frequently lack the advanced de-escalation training provided to specialized psychiatric nurses [18].

For this study, Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs) refer to Enrolled Nurses (ENs) who provide direct patient care in psychiatric inpatient settings without formal specialist education or qualifications in mental health or psychiatric nursing. Nursing assistants (NAs), who also lack psychiatric specialization, often share similar vulnerabilities when managing violent behaviours. Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs) and Enrolled Nurses (ENs) will be used interchangeably.

Aim and novelty

The aim of this study was to explore and describe the reflections of Licensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants on the violent behaviours of individuals receiving mental health care in adult acute psychiatric wards. While previous research has extensively examined patient outcomes and staff experiences [8], there is a lack of evidence focusing specifically on Licensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants, who provide front-line care yet remain underrepresented in the literature on workplace violence. This study addresses this gap by offering insights into their lived experiences, highlighting vulnerabilities linked to limited psychiatric training, and identifying opportunities for systemic and educational interventions.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative, exploratory design, informed by a phenomenological orientation, to understand how Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs/Enrolled Nurses) and Nursing Assistants (NAs) reflect on their experiences with patient-perpetrated violence in adult acute psychiatric wards. A phenomenological orientation was appropriate because the study sought to understand how participants interpreted and made meaning of their lived experiences of violence within their professional roles. While phenomenological thinking informed the focus on lived experience and subjective meaning, the study did not undertake formal phenomenological analysis. Instead, data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis as described by Braun and Clarke (2021), which enabled systematic identification of patterned meanings across participants while recognising the active interpretive role of the researcher [19].

This approach was chosen for its strength in capturing the lived experiences and subjective meanings that participants assign to their encounters with patient-perpetrated violence, thereby providing rich, in-depth insights into the emotional and professional impact of such incidents. The approach guided the study design, emphasizing participants’ personal reflections and meaning-making. Following an established qualitative methodology, we did not impose predetermined categories; instead, themes and subthemes were developed from the data. To enhance methodological rigor and ensure transparency, this study adhered to the 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist, which is provided as supplementary material and supports comprehensive, systematic reporting of qualitative research [20].

Research setting

The study was conducted in four adult acute psychiatric wards across three districts of Limpopo Province, South Africa. These wards provide short-term in-patient care for individuals experiencing acute mental health crises, with most patients admitted involuntarily under the Mental Health Care Act No. 17 of 2002 due to risks of harm to themselves or others.

The patient population was characterised by high clinical acuity, with frequent episodes of agitation and aggressive behaviour that disrupted routine care activities. Staffing comprised registered psychiatric nurses, Enrolled Nurses, and Nursing Assistants. The study involved Licensed Practical Nurses and Nursing Assistants delivering the most direct patient care. Due to staff shortages, non-specialist nurses were often required to manage high-risk situations with limited support. ENs and NAs are required to work under the supervision of RNs, as outlined by the SANC. This supervision can be either direct or indirect, depending on the specific task. However, due to staffing shortages, ENs and NAs often find themselves managing high-risk situations with limited support, which increases their vulnerability to burnout and emotional distress.

The wards operated in resource-constrained conditions, including ageing infrastructure and limited access to structured supervision and debriefing, which influenced staff safety and preparedness. This setting reflects broader challenges in South African public psychiatric services, where involuntary admissions are common, and nursing staff face significant emotional and professional strain related to patient-perpetrated violence.

Population and sampling procedure

A purposive sampling method was used to select participants. Participants included 7 ENs and 13 NAs; no registered nurses participated. This study specifically focused on these two nursing categories, comprising 16 females and 4 males who had worked in adult acute psychiatric care for more than 6 months. The inclusion criteria required participants to be licensed nurses by the South African Nursing Council (SANC), have worked for more than six months in adult acute psychiatric wards, and have experienced or witnessed some form of patient violence. Night-shift staff were excluded due to access constraints.

To ensure this, potential participants were identified through consultation with ward managers and gatekeepers, who were familiar with staff experience and could confirm exposure to patient-perpetrated violence. During recruitment, a relationship was established, and nurses were asked preliminary screening questions regarding their experiences with patient aggression or violence. Only those who confirmed such experiences were invited to participate in the study. Participants had access to limited organizational support through the Employee Wellness Program, which offers debriefing and psychological counselling after violent incidents. However, routine clinical supervision and formal post-incident reviews were infrequent.

The University of Venda’s Ethics Committee granted ethical approval (FHS/22/PDC/06/2104) prior to the commencement of the recruitment process. Participation in the study was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to the participant’s involvement. Confidentiality was assured throughout the study. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any point prior to data analysis. No participant withdrew from the study. Once the data were anonymised and aggregated, withdrawal was no longer possible. Permission was also obtained from the study setting’s authorities.

Data collection procedures

Recruitment

Participant recruitment was conducted in close collaboration with ward managers and institutional gatekeepers at the selected adult acute psychiatric wards. These individuals were instrumental in identifying participants who met the study’s inclusion criteria, specifically, Enrolled Nurses and Nursing Assistants who had worked in the ward for at least six months and had direct experience with patient-perpetrated violence. The research team consisted of female academics with doctoral qualifications and experience in mental health nursing and qualitative research. Reflexive discussions were conducted throughout data collection and analysis to acknowledge professional assumptions and minimise interpretive bias. An audit trail was maintained to enhance transparency. Prior to initiating the study, the first author, who held a PhD and had an established professional rapport with the ward, clearly communicated her role and the study’s aims to potential participants in a private room, face-to-face. The interviewer’s reasons for conducting the study and interest in the research topic were explicitly reported to ensure reflexivity and transparency, acknowledging potential assumptions and motivations that could influence data collection and interpretation.

Participants received detailed information sheets and provided written informed consent. To ensure relevance, participants were asked preliminary screening questions to verify their exposure to violent episodes before being formally invited to participate in the study. All invited individuals agreed to participate, resulting in no refusals or withdrawals.

Data collection

The first author collected data through in-depth, semi-structured individual interviews using an experience-focused interview style. Semi-structured interviews were selected because they enable participants to describe their experiences in their own words while allowing the researcher to explore specific topics relevant to the research question. A semi-structured interview guide (Annexure A) guided the interviews. The guide was developed from existing literature on workplace violence in psychiatric settings and refined in consultation with two senior psychiatric nursing experts and one qualitative research specialist.

The guide included one broad, open-ended reflective opening question followed by probing questions organized into three domains: experiences of violent behavior, emotional reactions, and perceptions of risk and vulnerability. This structure ensured that all interviews addressed comparable topics while allowing participants flexibility to narrate their experiences. The opening question encouraged reflective accounts of workplace violence. The interviews incorporated a narrative-style opening question that encouraged participants to provide extended, reflective accounts of their experiences, followed by semi-structured probing across predefined domains. A single broad, open-ended central question: “May you please explain your reflections on the violent behaviors of mental health care users in adult acute psychiatric wards? invited participants to provide extended reflective accounts of their experiences. Follow-up prompts were responsive and participant-led, allowing individuals to emphasize aspects of their experiences they considered most significant, while the predefined domains ensured that all interviews addressed comparable areas relevant to the study aim.

This interview approach, therefore, encouraged reflective, experience-based accounts that may include narrative elements, while remaining semi-structured and focused on the study’s substantive topics. Although participants often described events in detail, the purpose of the interviews was not to elicit complete personal narratives or life stories, but to generate rich experiential accounts suitable for cross-participant thematic analysis, consistent with the study’s phenomenological orientation and analytic aims.

All interviews were conducted by the first author, a female lecturer with a PhD and extensive experience in mental health nursing and qualitative research. Interviews were conversational and participant-led, focusing on how violence was experienced, interpreted, and managed by enrolled nurses/licensed practical nurses and nursing assistants in adult acute psychiatric wards. Interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred language; for those not fluent in English, Shangaan or Sepedi was used to ensure accurate and meaningful expression.

Each interview lasted 20–45 min and took place in a private room at the participants’ workplace during lunch breaks to minimise service disruption. Although interview durations were shorter than typically reported in qualitative research, this was attributed to participants’ workload and the emotional strain associated with recalling violent incidents. Interviews were conducted until sufficient depth and richness of patterned meaning were achieved to support robust thematic development. After analysing sixteen interviews, no substantially new patterns were identified, and four additional interviews were conducted to confirm the adequacy of the analysis, resulting in a final sample of twenty participants.

A single interview per participant was conducted, as the initial interviews generated sufficiently rich data to reach thematic saturation. Only the interviewer and participant were present during the sessions, and no formal field notes were taken. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, transcribed verbatim, and cross-checked against recordings for accuracy. The interview guide was pretested with two nurses (one enrolled nurse and one nursing assistant) who were not included in the main study to ensure clarity and appropriateness.

During some interviews, participants became distressed when recounting violent events. In such cases, the interviewer paused the interview, provided emotional support, and offered a referral to in-house psychological services or employee wellness programmes. A reflective thematic analysis was undertaken, and an audit trail was maintained to document analytic decisions and enhance transparency throughout the research process. Semi-structured interviews were selected because they enable participants to reflect on their experiences while allowing the researcher to explore patterned meanings across participants, which aligns with the interpretive aims of reflexive thematic analysis.

The use of a broad, reflective opening question led participants to respond in a narrative, experience-based manner, providing detailed accounts of violent incidents and their meanings. However, the purpose of the interviews was not to generate full narrative reconstructions of individual experiences, but to facilitate rich data generation that could support cross-case thematic analysis.

Data analysis

All audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim by the second author. Interviews conducted in local languages were translated into English by a professional translator to ensure consistency across the dataset. While the data contained narrative elements due to the reflective nature of the interviews, the analytic focus was on identifying patterns of shared meaning across participants rather than analysing individual stories as complete narratives. Reflexive thematic analysis was therefore appropriate, as it supports interpretation of patterned meaning across cases rather than narrative sequencing or structure.

Data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) following the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clarke (2021 [19]. RTA is an interpretive analytic method used to identify patterns of shared meaning across qualitative data, while recognizing the active role of the researcher in theme development. It was selected because the research question sought to identify patterned meanings and shared themes across Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs/ENs) and Nursing Assistants (NAs) regarding patient-perpetrated violence. The analytic focus was therefore on understanding commonalities and role-based interpretations across participants rather than constructing detailed individual life histories, which would have required a narrative analytic approach.

Reflexive Thematic Analysis is well-suited to interpretive qualitative research that aims to generate themes representing shared meaning while acknowledging the researcher’s active interpretive role. This approach enabled systematic cross-case analysis while remaining sensitive to contextual nuance and professional positioning.

While the interview questions focused on reflection of patient-perpetrated violence, the analysis moved beyond listing incidents. Analytic attention was directed toward how LPNs and NAs interpreted these experiences, how violence was embedded in everyday ward practices, and how their non-psychiatric training and role positioning shaped their emotional responses and perceived vulnerabilities.

Although RTA does not require intercoder reliability in the positivist sense, the authors held collaborative discussions to refine coding and theme development and enhance transparency of interpretation. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and the evolution of themes was maintained to enhance transparency. Although member checking was not conducted, reflexivity and triangulation across the research team ensured analytic rigor. Themes were therefore developed as interpretive patterns representing shared meanings across participants’ accounts rather than simple descriptive summaries.

Trustworthiness was ensured through strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was enhanced through analyst triangulation and prolonged engagement with the data. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and theme development was maintained to support dependability and confirmability. Reflexive notes were used to document researchers’ assumptions and analytic decisions. Member checking was not conducted, and transcripts were not returned to participants for comment, both of which are acknowledged as limitations. To mitigate bias, researchers reflected on and documented any assumptions during analysis. The consistent use of quotations from the data helps demonstrate alignment between the raw data and our interpretations. All data were securely stored on a password-protected laptop with cloud backup.

Theme 1: Experiences of patient-perpetrated violence among licensed practical nurses or nursing assistants

This theme captures the various forms of violence encountered by LPNs and NAs in adult acute psychiatric wards. Participants described how MHCUs exhibited violent behaviours that compromised staff safety and disrupted care delivery. The different forms of violence and their immediate practical consequences are described in three subthemes.

Subtheme 1.1: Physical Violence during routine care

These accounts illustrate how physical violence frequently occurs during routine care activities, reinforcing participants’ perceptions of vulnerability in the ward environment. These incidents often occurred during routine care activities, such as meal distribution or patient general supervision. Several reflections noted that male MHCUs were more commonly involved in these episodes, amplifying feelings of vulnerability and loss of control among staff.

I have seen a nurse being beaten by a patient, and I have also been beaten. A violent patient slapped me. I felt like I had been thrown at the deep end. I am not free here.— Participant 8, Female NA, 6 months of experience.

The participants’ accounts highlighted significant patient-perpetrated physical violence affecting ENs and NAs. In some instances, the violence occurred during routine care activities, such as serving meals, which not only compromised the nurses’ sense of safety but also disrupted care delivery and created a hostile work environment.

Participants reported frequent exposure to physical aggression from MHCUs, including being slapped, punched, and having objects like food and chairs thrown at them. These incidents often occurred during routine care activities, such as meal distribution and patient supervision. The violent behavior was perpetrated by male patients, contributing to a heightened sense of vulnerability and fear among ENs and NAs. Furthermore, participants described feeling unsafe and emotionally overwhelmed, with some expressing a lack of freedom and control in their work environment.

Physical assaults disrupt care delivery and reduce staff feelings of safety, particularly among early-career nurses (6–12 months in post). Participants described predictable high-risk situations, such as mealtimes and patient supervision, where staffing levels and patient acuity increase the likelihood of violence during routine care activities. These accounts directly address the research question by showing where, when, and how violence occurs in everyday practice.

Subtheme 1.2: Verbal violence and threats during routine care

Verbal aggression, such as swearing, insults, and direct threats, was a common experience, with MHCUs frequently. These behaviours often occurred during high-stress situations such as admissions mealtimes and personal care routines, further straining the nurse–patient relationship and contributing to emotional distress.

Participants described frequent experiences of verbal aggression from MHCUs, often involving vulgar language and unprovoked threats. These incidents typically occur during high-stress situations, such as admissions, mealtimes, and personal care routines. Female participants, in particular, reported being targeted by aggressive verbal outbursts, which contributed to a strained nurse–patient relationship and heightened emotional distress among staff. The unpredictability and intensity of verbal aggression were seen as significant challenges in maintaining a therapeutic environment. The study’s focus on Licensed Practical Nurses or Nursing Assistants, who frequently lack specialized psychiatric training but perform significant relational work, is sharpened by the reflections, which show how communication breakdowns, role limitations, and service pressures hinder de-escalation.

Subtheme 1.3: Destruction of ward infrastructure

Participants described incidents where MHCUs damaged ward property, including breaking doors, smashing windows, and dismantling locks from seclusion rooms. These acts of destruction were often linked to substance use or heightened aggression, and they posed significant safety risks for both staff and other patients.

Participants reported frequent incidents of property damage within psychiatric wards, including broken doors, smashed windows, and dismantled locks from seclusion rooms. These destructive behaviours were often associated with substance use and heightened aggression, particularly among male MHCUs. The damage not only compromised the security and functionality of critical spaces, such as seclusion rooms, but also posed serious safety risks to both staff and other patients. The facility expressed concern over the physical power of some patients and the inadequacy of infrastructure to contain violent episodes.

These reflections reframe violence as not only interpersonal but also environmental, with infrastructure damage weakening containment strategies, disrupting safety procedures, and escalating staff anxiety. The physical condition of the ward becomes a crucial component of LPNs’ and NAs’ risk assessment; this realization provides an alternative viewpoint on violence as a systemic and spatial phenomenon within everyday care.

Theme 2: Emotional impact of patient violence on licensed practical nurses and nursing assistants

This theme explores the psychological and emotional consequences of patient-perpetrated violence on ENs and NAs working in adult acute psychiatric wards. Participants shared how repeated exposure to aggression led to persistent emotional distress, affecting their sense of safety, professional confidence, and overall well-being. Three subthemes were developed under this theme through reflective thematic analysis:

Subtheme 2.1: Fear arising from unpredictability and perceived lack of protection

Many participants expressed a deep sense of fear when interacting with violent MHCUs. This fear stemmed from direct experiences of aggression, uncertainty about patients’ behaviours, and a perceived lack of protection or preparedness. Participants reported feeling unsafe, especially when alone as a female nurse in the ward or when confronted by unpredictable patients.

The accounts highlight the need for emotional support mechanisms and training in managing personal responses to aggression in psychiatric settings.

Anger functions as both a reaction to harm and a signal of boundary strain, surfacing when staff perceives misalignment between care ideals and ward realities. These insights connect directly to the research question by revealing how LPNs/ENs and NAs interpret violent acts through the prism of professional identity and ethical responsibility.

These reflections highlight the need for structured supports (trauma-informed education, regular debriefs, practical safety drills) tailored to LPN-levels who face frequent, direct exposure during routine care.

Discussion

By applying reflexive thematic analysis, the study was able to interpret patterns of shared meaning across participants’ reflections rather than focusing on individual narratives. This study explored the reflections of LPNs / (ENs) and NAs regarding patient-perpetrated violence in adult acute psychiatric wards and provides important insights into how violence is experienced and normalised within routine nursing practice. These categories of nursing personnel often provide direct care under challenging conditions, yet they may not receive the same level of professional development, supervision, or support as RNs. In South Africa, ENs and NAs play a supportive role in psychiatric settings, providing basic care and assisting with daily activities under the supervision of registered nurses [21]. Their scope of practice does not include independent clinical decision-making, which may contribute to feelings of fear or helplessness when managing violent behaviours without adequate supervision or continuing education [18].

The current study findings revealed that ENs and NAs frequently encounter various forms of aggression, ranging from verbal abuse to physical violence and property destruction, which contribute to fear, stress, and feelings of vulnerability during everyday clinical interactions such as meal distribution, patient supervision, and personal care. Their reflections also underscore the need for targeted training, emotional support, and institutional policies that prioritize the safety and professional development of all nursing staff cadres in psychiatric settings.

Consistent with international literature, the findings confirm that violence in psychiatric settings remains a pervasive occupational hazard for nursing staff [5, 6]. However, this study extends existing nursing knowledge by demonstrating that, for non-psychiatric trained nursing personnel, violence is not perceived as an exceptional or crisis-related event, but rather as an embedded and expected feature of everyday care delivery. Participants described physical and verbal aggression most commonly occurring during routine nursing activities such as mealtimes, patient supervision, and personal care. These findings align with previous research indicating that aggression in psychiatric settings often arises during ordinary interactions rather than acute clinical emergencies [7, 9]. The present study adds depth by illustrating how the routine nature of these encounters contributes to sustained fear, reduced professional confidence, and emotional exhaustion among ENs and NAs. This is particularly concerning given that these nursing categories frequently provide direct patient care despite limited psychiatric training and restricted scopes of practice.

A key contribution of this study is its emphasis on verbal violence and damage to ward infrastructure as significant and cumulative stressors for nursing staff. While physical assaults are often prioritised in institutional reporting and policy responses, participants described verbal abuse as emotionally draining and damaging to therapeutic relationships. This finding supports earlier studies identifying verbal aggression as a major source of psychological distress among nurses [22, 23]. In addition, repeated destruction of ward infrastructure symbolised a lack of environmental safety and institutional protection, reinforcing nurses’ perceptions of vulnerability. This aligns with emerging evidence highlighting the influence of environmental and organisational factors on experiences of violence in psychiatric wards [24, 25].

The emotional response of participants to violence reported in this study, fear, anger, and frustration, was closely linked to organisational conditions rather than individual coping limitations. Fear was associated with the unpredictability of patient behavior, understaffing, and a perceived lack of protection, particularly during unsupervised or high-risk shifts. Similar emotional responses have been reported among psychiatric nurses worldwide [8, 26].

Anger was frequently associated with experiences of humiliation or physical harm, reflecting the emotional strain of balancing professional caregiving responsibilities with repeated exposure to aggression. Some participants described struggling with the impulse to retaliate after being physically assaulted or verbally abused. This finding reflects what has been described in the literature as moral distress, where nurses experience tension between professional values and emotionally challenging clinical realities [27]. Persistent exposure to aggression may therefore challenge nurses’ ability to maintain therapeutic relationships while managing their own emotional responses.

Frustration was also a prominent emotional response and was strongly associated with perceptions of limited institutional support, inadequate supervision, and insufficient training for managing violent behaviour. These findings suggest that emotional distress among nurses is not solely a result of individual vulnerability but is also shaped by systemic conditions within healthcare organisations. Studies examining workplace violence in psychiatric settings similarly emphasise the influence of staffing levels, leadership support, and organisational culture on nurses’ experiences of violence and their ability to cope effectively [10, 28].

Importantly, this study highlights the particular vulnerability of Enrolled Nurses and Nursing Assistants working in psychiatric settings without specialised mental health training. Unlike psychiatric-trained nurses, these nursing cadres often operate within restricted scopes of practice yet remain heavily involved in direct patient care. This mismatch between professional preparation and clinical expectations may increase emotional strain and feelings of inadequacy when managing aggressive behaviour. Previous research has identified similar challenges among non-psychiatric nurses working in mental health environments, where limited training and unclear role boundaries contribute to stress and reduced confidence in managing violent incidents [18, 29, 30].

However, this study adds a unique dimension by focusing on Enrolled Nurses and Nursing Assistants, who are often excluded from psychiatric research. Unlike registered psychiatric nurses, these staff members typically lack formal training in mental health care, making them more susceptible to both physical harm and emotional strain. This supports the findings of Rahmani et al. (2021) and Gutierrez (2017), who highlighted the challenges faced by non-specialist nurses in managing patients with psychiatric conditions [29, 30].

Participants’ accounts of repeated exposure to aggression during routine care activities illustrate how violence becomes embedded within everyday ward practices. The findings further support conceptualizations of workplace violence as a socially and organizationally constructed phenomenon, shaped by ward culture, staffing practices, environmental design, and power relations [10, 31]. Participants described implicit workplace norms that influenced how aggression was tolerated, how emotions were expressed, and whether seeking support was viewed as acceptable. Similar observations have been reported by Delaney et al. (2021), who argue that psychiatric ward cultures strongly shape nurses’ responses to violence and emotional distress. In the absence of trauma-informed education and structured support systems, violence may become normalised and emotional suffering silenced [32].

From a nursing perspective, the erosion of therapeutic relationships described in this study is particularly concerning. Recurrent exposure to violence undermines trust, communication, and relational engagement between nursing staff and mental health care users, consistent with Peplau’s Interpersonal Relations Theory [33], which emphasises the importance of therapeutic nurse–patient relationships. Repeated exposure to violence disrupts these relational processes and may undermine nurses’ ability to maintain therapeutic engagement, weakening trust, communication, and collaborative care. When nurses feel unsafe or emotionally distressed, maintaining therapeutic interactions becomes increasingly difficult, which may further affect the quality of care provided to mental health care users. For ENs and NAs, whose roles are already constrained, this erosion further compromises professional identity and perceived effectiveness in care delivery.

The findings are consistent with South African and international evidence showing that non-psychiatric nurses in psychiatric settings often feel inadequately prepared to manage aggressive behaviour, increasing their risk of emotional exhaustion and secondary traumatic stress [34–37]. South African studies by Thandavhathu et al. (2024) have similarly reported high levels of aggression and significant psychological impact among nurses working in acute psychiatric wards [38], supporting the relevance and transferability of the present findings.

Overall, this study demonstrates that patient-perpetrated violence in adult acute psychiatric wards is not an isolated clinical challenge but a persistent feature of nursing practice with significant emotional and professional consequences for ENs and NAs. Addressing this issue requires organisationally embedded, nursing-focused interventions that prioritise trauma-informed education, structured supervision, environmental safety, and supportive ward cultures. Such approaches are essential for protecting the well-being of nursing staff and sustaining safe, therapeutic psychiatric care environments [15, 38–40].

Training and capacity building

The findings highlight an urgent need for specialized training in psychiatric care for ENs and NAs. Training should focus on de-escalation techniques, trauma-informed care, and the management of psychiatric emergencies. Incorporating these modules into continuing professional development programs could enhance staff preparedness and confidence.

Workplace safety and infrastructure

The frequent destruction of ward infrastructure and physical assaults point to the need for robust safety protocols. Hospitals should invest in secure ward designs, emergency response systems, and clear guidelines for managing violent incidents. Regular risk assessments and staff feedback mechanisms can help tailor interventions to specific ward needs.

Psychological support and wellness

Emotional effects such as fear, anger, and frustration must be addressed through structured support systems. These could include peer debriefing sessions, access to counselling services, and wellness programs. Supporting ENs’ and NAs’ mental health is not only ethical but also essential for sustaining quality care.

Staffing and supervision models

The placement of non-specialist nurses in high-risk psychiatric wards without adequate supervision is a systemic issue. Policymakers should review staffing models to ensure that all nursing categories receive appropriate support. This includes mentorship from psychiatric-trained professionals and access to clinical supervision.

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the understanding of violence in psychiatric settings as a socially constructed phenomenon, shaped by institutional norms, power dynamics, and environmental factors. It reinforces the need to view aggression not merely as individual pathology but as a product of complex interactions within the care environment.

Implications for practice and policy

The study has immediate implications for clinical practice and policy. Health institutions should prioritise targeted, trauma-informed training for LPNs and NAs working in psychiatric settings, focusing on de-escalation, emotional regulation, and ethical decision-making. Organisational policies must also address staffing adequacy, supervision, and environmental safety. These findings underscore the need for systemic interventions that acknowledge the emotional labour and occupational risks faced by non-psychiatrically trained nursing staff.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the participant sample was restricted to Enrolled Nurses (ENs/LPNs) and Nursing Assistants (NAs), thereby excluding other nursing categories such as registered nurses, student nurses, and psychiatric-mental health-trained professionals. Although the focus on ENs and NAs was intentional, given their vital role in psychiatric care, their perspectives may not fully capture the experiences and insights of the broader nursing workforce. Including a more diverse range of nursing professionals could have enriched the understanding of institutional and clinical dynamics related to patient-perpetrated violence. The sample was gender-balanced, with 16 females and 4 males. While this reflects broader national trends in nursing demographics, it may have influenced the findings, particularly regarding perceptions of safety, emotional responses to aggression, and coping strategies. Previous studies have suggested that gender can shape how nurses experience and respond to workplace violence, and a more balanced sample could have allowed for deeper exploration of gendered dynamics in psychiatric care [23].

Second, participants were recruited from four adult acute psychiatric wards within three districts of Limpopo Province, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions or healthcare contexts. However, the study sought to provide rich descriptions of participants’ experiences rather than statistical generalisation, and the detailed contextual information provided may allow readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar settings.

Third, the study excluded night-shift staff due to logistical and safety constraints during data collection, as well as limited access to participants during these hours. Night-time work in psychiatric wards may present distinct challenges, including reduced staffing levels, limited managerial support, and increased patient agitation. As a result, the findings primarily reflect experiences of nurses working during daytime shifts and may not capture the full range of workplace violence experienced across all shifts. The exclusion of night-shift staff is acknowledged as a significant limitation. Nighttime work in psychiatric wards may involve distinct risks, including reduced staffing, limited managerial presence, and heightened patient agitation. Consequently, the findings primarily reflect experiences during the day shift and may not capture the full spectrum of violence across all shifts. Future studies should specifically examine night-shift contexts to provide a more comprehensive understanding of occupational risk. The absence of these individuals may have limited the diversity of experiences represented, particularly regarding shift-specific challenges such as reduced staffing levels, heightened patient agitation at night, and variations in support system availability during off-peak periods.

Fourth, although interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred languages to encourage open expression, translating some interviews from Sepedi and Shangaan into English during transcription may have introduced subtle changes in meaning, despite careful translation procedures. Finally, member checking was not conducted, and interview transcripts were not returned to participants for verification. Although this may limit confirmability, several strategies were used to enhance trustworthiness, including maintaining an audit trail, engaging in reflexive discussions within the research team, and supporting interpretations with verbatim quotations from participants. In addition, while reflexive thematic analysis emphasises researcher interpretation, analytic decisions were documented through an audit trail to enhance transparency.

Future research could expand on these findings by including a broader range of nursing categories, examining experiences across different shifts, and incorporating mixed-methods approaches to explore the prevalence and organisational determinants of workplace violence in psychiatric settings. These insights might provide a critical foundation for policy reform and practice-based interventions to improve staff safety, emotional well-being, and the quality of psychiatric care. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the experiences of front-line nursing personnel who are frequently underrepresented in research on violence in psychiatric care settings.

Conclusions

This qualitative study provides insight into how Licensed Practical Nurses (Enrolled Nurses) and Nursing Assistants working in adult acute psychiatric wards experience and interpret patient-perpetrated violence. The findings demonstrate that violence, including physical assault, verbal abuse, and destruction of ward infrastructure, is frequently encountered during routine care activities and has significant emotional and professional consequences for these nursing cadres. Participants described persistent feelings of fear, anger, and frustration, reflecting the psychological strain associated with working in unpredictable and high-risk psychiatric environments.

By focusing on Enrolled Nurses and Nursing Assistants, this study highlights the experiences of a group that is often underrepresented in research on workplace violence in psychiatric care. These staff members provide substantial front-line patient care yet frequently lack specialised psychiatric training and structured institutional support. The findings therefore underscore the importance of recognising the unique vulnerabilities of non-psychiatrically trained nursing personnel within acute mental health services.

Addressing violence in psychiatric wards requires organisational responses that extend beyond individual coping strategies. Health institutions should prioritise trauma-informed training, structured supervision, and practical de-escalation skills for Enrolled Nurses and Nursing Assistants. In addition, improvements in ward infrastructure, staffing support, and post-incident debriefing systems are essential to enhance staff safety and emotional well-being.

Overall, the study contributes to the growing body of evidence demonstrating that workplace violence in psychiatric settings is not solely a behavioural issue, but a systemic challenge shaped by organisational, environmental, and professional factors. Strengthening institutional support systems for front-line nursing staff is therefore essential for promoting safer therapeutic environments for both healthcare workers and individuals receiving mental health care. Future research should examine organisational interventions and training strategies aimed at reducing violence and improving support systems for nursing personnel working in psychiatric settings.
