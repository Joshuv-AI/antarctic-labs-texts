Urban microclimate dataset from low-cost sensor networks in a hot and humid tropical climate: Reunion Island
Authors: Alexandre Lefevre, Joshua Sweetman, Bruno Malet-Damour, Harry Boyer, Garry Rivière
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes an urban microclimate dataset collected on Reunion Island, a hot and humid tropical island in the southwest Indian Ocean, between October 2024 and June 2025. Measurements were acquired from two neighborhood-scale monitoring networks installed in Saint-Denis (north coast) and Le Port (west coast), representing contrasting urban morphologies and local climatic contexts. The monitoring system was based on several LoRa communication networks connecting 25 low-cost weather stations developed by the authors and deployed on residential buildings. More than ten stations were installed in each neighborhood, providing dense spatial coverage suitable for fine-scale characterization of urban microclimate variability. This dataset contributes rare high-density urban microclimate observations for a small tropical island context.The dataset contains time series recorded at 10-minute intervals for air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, global horizontal solar radiation, and grey globe temperature. Publicly shared files also include metadata for each station, such as geographic coordinates, deployment period, sensor grouping, network allocation, and site characteristics relevant to urban climate analyses. Quality control procedures and routine maintenance operations were performed during the monitoring campaigns, and stations with incomplete records can be identified through the metadata files.The recorded variables allow calculation of additional indicators related to thermal environment assessment, including mean radiant temperature and the Universal Thermal Climate Index, using standardized procedures and open-source tools such as the Python library pythermalcomfort.The dataset can be reused for applications related to urban overheating, outdoor thermal comfort, and neighborhood-scale climate characterization in tropical environments. It may support calibration and validation of urban climate and building-environment models such as ENVI-met or STEVE, comparison between contrasting urban forms, evaluation of heat mitigation scenarios, development of low-cost sensing approaches, and cross-comparison with datasets from other cities and climatic regions.Keywords: Urban heat island, Urban climate, Outdoor thermal comfort, Tropical climate, Low-cost sensors, Environmental monitoring, Urban microclimate, Small island

Background

Urban overheating is an increasing concern in tropical cities, where high air temperature, humidity, solar exposure, and local ventilation conditions interact at the neighborhood scale. Small tropical islands remain poorly represented in urban climate datasets, despite their specific climatic constraints, limited land availability, and rapid coastal urbanization [11]. Reunion Island provides a relevant context for documenting urban microclimate conditions in a hot and humid island environment.

This dataset was compiled to provide high-resolution observations for two contrasting urban districts, using dense networks of low-cost weather stations measuring air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, global horizontal solar radiation, and grey globe temperature. The data support urban heat island assessment, outdoor thermal comfort calculations, and the calibration or validation of neighborhood-scale urban climate models.

This data article complements the associated research article published in Urban Climate, which used part of the dataset to analyze urban heat island intensity and outdoor thermal comfort in two neighborhoods of Reunion Island. The present article provides access to the full dataset and associated metadata, enabling more detailed reuse, including fine-scale intra-neighborhood variability, humidity-related analyses, wind conditions, and micro-scale effects linked to local urban form.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study area and monitoring strategy

Data were collected in two urban districts located on Reunion Island (21°S, 55°E), a mountainous tropical island situated in the southwest Indian Ocean. The island is characterized by strong climatic contrasts over short distances due to topography, trade-wind exposure, and coastal orientation. Two districts were selected in partnership with the local social housing operator SHLMR (Société d’Habitations à Loyer Modéré de La Réunion), which facilitated access to residential buildings used for sensor installation.

The selected districts are located in two contrasting coastal climatic sectors of the island: Saint-Denis on the humid north coast and Le Port on the drier west coast. This configuration allowed deployment under different urban and meteorological contexts while using the same monitoring protocol.

The first district is the Ruisseau neighborhood, located in Saint-Denis, the administrative capital and largest city of Reunion Island (Fig. 1). The monitored area corresponds to a dense residential district composed of apartment blocks, towers, mixed-use buildings, and narrow street canyons. Building heights locally exceed ten storeys. The urban form is compact, with reduced sky-view factor and heterogeneous shading conditions generated by surrounding buildings. According to the Local Climate Zone framework, much of the district corresponds to a compact high-rise or compact mid-rise urban fabric [12].

Deployment of low-cost weather stations in the Ruisseau district (Saint-Denis). Reproduced from [1].

The second district is located in Le Port, a coastal city developed around the commercial harbor on the west coast (Fig. 2). The monitored sector is situated near Square Jean XXIII and includes several residential compounds (ZUP, Les Étoiles, Héliotropes/Héliospaces). Compared with Ruisseau, this district presents a more open morphology with larger spacing between buildings, lower average building density, wider open spaces, and more direct solar exposure. Building heights are generally intermediate (four to eight storeys), corresponding to an open mid-rise urban form [12].

Deployment of low-cost weather stations in the Le Port district. Reproduced from [1].

The two districts also differ in regional climate [13]. Saint-Denis is influenced by the humid windward regime of the north coast, with higher annual rainfall and stronger exposure to maritime trade winds. Le Port is located in the leeward western sector, where rainfall is lower and dry-season conditions are more pronounced. These differences provide complementary measurement contexts for urban microclimate monitoring.

To document neighborhood-scale spatial variability, two dense monitoring networks were deployed using identical low-cost weather stations. A total of 25 stations were installed: 11 stations in the Ruisseau district and 14 stations in Le Port. More than ten stations were available in each district, allowing short-distance intra-neighborhood comparisons and distributed observations across multiple building groups.

Stations in Saint-Denis were organized into three operational groups (D, E, and F), each linked to a LoRa communication gateway installed on nearby roofs. In Le Port, stations were similarly distributed into three groups (A, B, and C) corresponding to the main residential sectors of the monitored area. This grouping improved wireless data transmission reliability and simplified maintenance logistics.

The location of all stations is presented in Fig. 1 for the Ruisseau district and Fig. 2 for Le Port.

Weather station design

Each station was designed by the authors as an autonomous low-cost monitoring unit combining environmental sensors, onboard data logging, photovoltaic power supply, rechargeable battery storage, and wireless LoRa communication. The integrated 6 W solar panel and rechargeable battery allowed off-grid operation without connection to the electrical network, enabling deployment on residential façades and reducing maintenance requirements. The approximate hardware cost of one complete unit was below 200 €. Each station included the following sensors:

Sensor validation and measurement quality

All sensors were validated before deployment following laboratory and field procedures developed for previous microclimate monitoring work [15,16]. The validation procedures, described in detail in [14], were designed to verify sensor performance under environmental conditions representative of tropical outdoor applications. Unlike a calibration procedure, no sensor-specific correction coefficients were derived or applied. Instead, each sensor type was compared against reference instruments, and only sensors meeting predefined performance criteria were retained for deployment.

Air temperature and relative humidity sensors (Sensirion SHT31) together with the DS18B20 globe temperature sensors were validated in a climatic chamber through multipoint experiments representative of tropical outdoor conditions. The validation protocol included stepwise heating from 25 to 55°C (5°C increments) with relative humidity varying between approximately 20 and 55 % RH, uncontrolled ambient conditions ranging from 25 to 30°C and 50 to 80 % RH, rapid heating and cooling experiments, and a one-month stability test. Measurements were compared with reference calibrated type-K thermocouples for temperature and calibrated Testo 174H thermo-hygrometers for relative humidity. The complete validation protocol and experimental results are described in detail in [14].

Wind speed sensors were validated in a wind tunnel at ESIROI (University of Reunion Island). Air velocity was compared against a calibrated Pitot tube connected to a KIMO C310 multifunction instrument over several velocity steps between 0.5 and 10 m s⁻¹. Solar radiation sensors were validated outdoors by comparison with a reference CMP11 pyranometer under natural exposure conditions following the protocol described in [14]. Only sensors exhibiting acceptable agreement with the reference measurements were retained for deployment [14].

The validation results are summarized in Table 2. Reported accuracies correspond to the maximum deviations observed during comparison with reference instruments. These values were used as acceptance criteria for sensor selection prior to deployment. Sensors not meeting these acceptance criteria were excluded from deployment, whereas compliant sensors were deployed without applying any post-processing correction or sensor-specific calibration coefficients. The obtained measurement accuracies were consistent with, and in several cases more restrictive than, the performance levels recommended by ISO 7726 for instruments used in thermal environment assessments [17]. These results indicate that the low-cost stations were suitable for the acquisition of urban microclimate variables and derived thermal comfort indicators [14].

Installation protocol

Stations were installed on residential buildings owned or managed by local housing operators. This arrangement simplified authorization procedures, facilitated access for routine maintenance, and improved long-term installation security.

Dedicated satellite-dish-type mounting brackets were installed on building façades to support the monitoring units. Sensors were positioned approximately 40-60 cm from the wall surface in order to reduce direct conductive influence from the façade while maintaining mechanical stability. The installation height was approximately 3 m above ground level, which reduced vandalism risk and unauthorized handling while remaining representative of near-building outdoor environmental conditions. An example of station installation is shown in Fig. 4.

Photograph of a low-cost weather station installed in the Ruisseau district of Saint-Denis. Reproduced from [1].

Station placement was selected to maximize spatial coverage at the neighborhood scale while accounting for practical constraints such as façade accessibility, surrounding obstacles, local geometry, and LoRa communication quality. Because the stations were powered by photovoltaic panels, façades receiving sufficient solar exposure were prioritized. South-facing façades were generally avoided, whereas north-, east-, or west-facing orientations were selected according to local shading conditions and available mounting locations. The final distribution of stations was validated during field visits conducted in collaboration with housing managers and building occupants.

Monitoring period and data acquisition

Monitoring activities were conducted between October 2024 and June 2025 for the two neighborhood networks. Measurements were recorded automatically at 10-minute intervals and periodically downloaded, checked, and archived during field visits.

Data acquisition was organized through several continuous monitoring campaigns distributed across the study period rather than as a single uninterrupted campaign. This operating mode reflected maintenance requirements, battery management constraints, and progressive optimization of the autonomous stations after installation. The selected monitoring phases were scheduled to capture different climatic periods representative of seasonal conditions on Reunion Island, including transition, warm-season, and austral summer conditions (Table 3).

The raw CSV files contain only valid observations and do not include quality flags or placeholder values for missing data. Missing observations are therefore identified by gaps in the timestamp sequence relative to the nominal 10-minute acquisition interval. Detailed station-level completeness statistics, including the monitoring window, expected number of records, available records, and coverage percentage for each campaign, are provided in Coverage_statistics_by_campaign.csv, while the expected monitoring windows are summarized in Measurement_campaign_windows.csv. These metadata files allow users to identify missing observations programmatically.

Data processing and quality control

Raw measurements transmitted by the stations were compiled and exported in comma-separated values (.csv) format. Data files were checked prior to publication to verify timestamp consistency, station identifiers, and file structure. Duplicate entries and incomplete records were screened during compilation. Missing or interrupted measurements associated with battery discharge, communication failure, maintenance operations, or station damage were retained as blank or missing values rather than interpolated.

Metadata were compiled in separate files and include station coordinates, façade orientation, installation height, operational group, deployment period, and local urban descriptors useful for data filtering and interpretation.

In addition to the measured variables, two derived thermal indicators were calculated and included in the published dataset: MRT and the UTCI. These variables were generated using the open-source Python package pythermalcomfort (version 3.9.8) [18]. To ensure complete reproducibility of the derived variables, the Python script used for these calculations (Processing_MRT_UTCI_example.py) is provided in the associated Mendeley Data repository.

MRT was calculated from air temperature, grey globe temperature, and wind speed according to the ISO 7726 grey globe method implemented in pythermalcomfort [17,19]. Limitations

The weather stations were mounted on building façades using satellite-dish brackets, with sensors positioned approximately 40-60 cm from the wall surface and installed at about 3m above ground level. This configuration was selected to reduce vandalism risk, improve maintenance access, and ensure long-term deployment, but it does not fully correspond to standard pedestrian-level measurements in free-standing street canyons. Measurements may therefore be influenced by local façade effects such as shading patterns, radiation exchanges with nearby surfaces, and locally modified airflow. This limitation may be more pronounced for grey globe temperature and wind speed.

Because anemometers were installed close to buildings, recorded wind speeds may represent localized ventilation conditions rather than undisturbed canyon-scale airflow. Users should consider this characteristic when applying the data to aerodynamic analyses.

Solar-powered stations were supplied by rechargeable batteries. During some monitoring periods, battery discharge or insufficient solar charging caused temporary interruptions or incomplete records for several stations.

One station in Le Port (B3) was stolen during the campaign, resulting in data loss after 12 December 2024. Minor vandalism or physical damage was also observed for stations B1 and E5.

The derived variables MRT and UTCI were computed from the measured meteorological variables using the procedures described in the manuscript. Although the accuracy of the input sensors was validated (Table 2), the combined uncertainty associated with the derived MRT and UTCI values was not explicitly assessed. Consequently, users requiring quantitative uncertainty estimates for thermal comfort studies should consider the reported sensor accuracies together with the sensitivity of these derived variables to the input measurements.

The monitoring campaign spans approximately nine months (October 2024 to June 2025) and therefore does not cover a complete annual cycle. Consequently, the dataset is well suited for neighborhood-scale spatial analyses and seasonal investigations within the monitored period, but it should not be used alone for full annual or interannual climate variability assessments.

Despite these constraints, all stations were deployed using a consistent protocol and measurement methodology, supporting robust relative comparisons across monitoring locations and acquisition periods.
