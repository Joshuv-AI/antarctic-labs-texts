Iwasawa theory for branched ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} -towers of finite graphs and Ihara zeta and L L -functions
Rusiru Gambheera, Daniel Vallières

Abstract
. We revisit the theory of Ihara L L -functions in the context initially studied by Bass and Hashimoto and more recently by Zakharov. In particular, we study if the Artin formalism is satisfied by these L L -functions. As an application, we give a proof of the analogue of Iwasawa’s asymptotic class number formula for the p p -part of the number of spanning trees in branched ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} -towers of finite connected graphs using Ihara zeta and L L -functions. Moreover, we relate a generator for the characteristic ideal of the finitely generated torsion Iwasawa module that governs the growth of the p p -part of the number of spanning trees in such towers with Ihara L L -functions.

1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, we use Serre’s formalism to describe graphs as detailed in
[
25
]
, and we let
p
denote a fixed rational prime. In
[
7
]
, we initiated the study of Iwasawa theory for
branched

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers of finite connected graphs arising from voltage assignments as explained in
[
7
, §4.3]
. If

X=X_{0}\leftarrow X_{1}\leftarrow X_{2}\leftarrow\ldots\leftarrow X_{n}\leftarrow\ldots

is such a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower,
[
7
, Theorem 5.6]
shows that there exist
\mu,\lambda,n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(1.1)

{\rm ord}_{p}(\kappa(X_{n}))=\mu p^{n}+\lambda n+\nu,

when
n\geq n_{0}
, where
\kappa(X_{n})
denotes the number of spanning trees of
X_{n}
, and
{\rm ord}_{p}
denotes the usual
p
-adic valuation on the field of rational numbers. This result can be seen as being a graph theoretical analogue of Iwasawa’s asymptotic class number formula in algebraic number theory (see
[
13
,
15
]
). Our approach in
[
7
]
was module theoretical. To every finite connected graph
X
is associated a finite abelian group
{\rm Pic}^{0}(X)
, called the Picard group of degree zero of
X
, whose cardinality is the number of spanning trees of
X
. We obtained the result (
1.1
) above by applying the usual structure theorem for finitely generated Iwasawa modules to the Iwasawa module, denoted by
{\rm Pic}_{\Lambda}^{0}
in
[
7
, §4.3]
, obtained by taking the projective limit of the Sylow
p
-subgroups of the finite abelian groups
{\rm Pic}^{0}(X_{n})
. In fact, the Iwasawa invariants
\mu
and
\lambda
appearing in (
1.1
) above are precisely the Iwasawa invariants of the Iwasawa module
{\rm Pic}_{\Lambda}^{0}
. We built upon previous works of Gonet in
[
9
,
10
]
, of Kleine and Müller in
[
17
]
, and of Kataoka in
[
16
]
all done in the
unramified
situation to obtain the result above in the
branched
situation.

On the other hand, in
[
33
,
21
,
22
]
, the authors obtained the result (
1.1
) above in the unramified situation by using the Ihara zeta and
L
-functions and their special value at
u=1
. The goal of this paper is to adapt those ideas to the branched situation to obtain another proof of (
1.1
) that makes use of Ihara zeta and
L
-functions. This approach is more in line with Iwasawa’s proof of his asymptotic class number formula for the minus part of the class number in cyclotomic
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-extensions of some cyclotomic number fields using his construction of
p
-adic
L
-functions and the analytic class number formula as detailed in
[
14
, §7]
. If
X
is a finite graph, then its Ihara zeta function is denoted by
Z_{X}(u)
and it is known to be the reciprocal of a polynomial with integer coefficients. The theory of Ihara zeta and
L
-functions for branched covers of finite graphs is more subtle than for unramified covers. In the unramified situation, there are clear notions of Galois covers and Galois groups as groups of deck transformations. Moreover, there is a Galois correspondence and a well-established theory of Ihara
L
-functions that satisfy the usual Artin formalism (see for instance the series of papers
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
by Stark and Terras). Any unramified Galois cover of finite graphs can be obtained by starting with a finite connected graph
Y
on which a finite group
G
acts without inversion and freely on
vertices
. Since the action is without inversion, the quotient graph
Y_{G}=G\backslash Y
makes sense, and since the action of
G
is free on vertices, the natural morphism of graphs
Y\rightarrow Y_{G}
is an unramified Galois cover with group of deck transformations isomorphic to
G
. It is therefore natural to consider more general covers of finite graphs by relaxing the condition that
G
acts freely on vertices. When a group
G
acts freely on vertices, it also necessarily acts freely on directed edges, so a first step would be to consider the situation where
G
acts freely on
directed edges
, but not necessarily on vertices. This leads to the notion of
branched cover
, and this is the type of covers we studied in
[
7
]
(branched covers are examples of harmonic morphisms as studied in
[
2
]
for instance, but not all harmonic morphisms are branched covers). In this situation, one also has the data of a stabilizer group at each vertex. Now, Corry pointed out for instance in
[
5
]
that the group of deck transformations of what ought to be a Galois cover in the branched setting is usually too big to get a neat Galois correspondence between subgroups of the group of deck transformations and (equivalence classes) of intermediate covers. Moreover, in
[
20
]
, it was pointed out that the graph theoretical analogue of Dedekind’s conjecture in algebraic number theory does not hold for branched covers of finite graphs. Indeed, Malmskog and Manes gave examples of branched covers
Y\rightarrow X
for which

Z_{X}(u)^{-1}\nmid Z_{Y}(u)^{-1}.

As far as we know, in the branched and even more general situation in which a group
G
acts without inversion but otherwise arbitrarily on a graph
Y
, Ihara
L
-functions have been introduced in
[
3
]
and
[
12
]
.

Bass’s starting point is a group
\Gamma
acting without inversion and discretely (meaning that the stabilizer at each vertex is finite) on a locally finite tree
T
for which the quotient
\Gamma\backslash T
is a finite graph. He does not assume though that
\Gamma
acts freely on directed edges. Such a group
\Gamma
is necessarily virtually free, and it seems to us that although this approach is quite general, it does not quite capture what we need for our purposes in this paper for a few different reasons. For instance, the natural graph
X_{\infty}
that resides at the top of the tower (
1.1
), obtained by taking the projective limit of the finite graphs
X_{n}
, is a
profinite
graph and is not locally finite when the tower is branched. Moreover, the profinite group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
acts on
X_{\infty}
, but the stabilizers at the vertices of
X_{\infty}
are not finite groups. We point out in passing that the Bass-Serre theory in geometric group theory has been extended to the profinite situation by Ribes and his collaborators (see for instance
[
23
]
) and this might well be the natural setup one should consider here.

Hashimoto’s starting point is one of a finite group acting on a finite graph just like we will consider in this paper with no assumption on the action whatsoever. On the other hand, there is no study of the Artin formalism which is needed for our purposes. Moreover, Ihara’s determinant formula (also known as the three-term determinant formula) is also not considered in
[
12
]
.

More recently, Zakharov explained in
[
34
]
how to adapt the theory of Ihara
L
-functions, as treated in
[
32
]
for instance, when a finite group
G
acts freely on the directed edges of a finite graph which is not necessarily a tree (and allowing inversion so that the notion of legs has to be taken into account as well). This approach is well suited for our purposes and can be applied at every level
n
of a branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower. There is one caveat though. The Ihara
L
-functions satisfy the usual additivity and induction properties of the Artin formalism, but
not
the inflation property in general. In retrospect, this failure of the inflation property is closely related to the fact that the analogue of Dedekind’s conjecture alluded to above is not satisfied in general for branched covers of graphs. Nonetheless, we show in this paper that one can study the Iwasawa theory of branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers of finite connected graphs arising from voltage assignments using Ihara zeta and
L
-functions despite the failure of the inflation property.

We could have used Zakharov’s approach directly, since
[
34
, Proposition 4.14 and Corollary 4.15]
give the needed product formula for the Ihara zeta function of the top graph
Y
in terms of Ihara
L
-functions associated to characters of
G
. But while thinking about this question, we wanted to understand how Bass’s approach was related to Zakharov’s approach. Therefore, instead of directly using the definition of Ihara
L
-functions included in
[
34
]
, we present here our own approach to Ihara
L
-functions which is a hybrid between Bass’s approach in
[
3
]
and Zakharov’s approach in
[
34
]
. In contrast to Bass’s approach, we will restrict ourselves to the abelian situation in order to avoid non-commutative determinants. Moreover, instead of having a (usually) infinite discrete group acting on a (usually) infinite tree, we will have a finite abelian group acting on a finite graph. This is sufficient for the applications we have in mind to branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers of finite connected graphs arising from voltage assignments. In contrast to Zakharov’s approach, our (finite) groups acting on (finite) graphs will be abelian, but we do not assume that
G
acts freely on directed edges. Moreover we do not study the situation where legs are allowed. Allowing legs should be possible with minor modifications throughout. Although allowing legs is advantageous and desirable for a few different reasons, we choose here to not include them, since our goal is to give a complementary approach to the results contained in
[
7
]
in which legs were not studied. We take the shortest path by having as our starting point Ihara’s determinant formula and reexpressing everything in an equivariant way. We obtain in this way a satisfactory theory of Ihara
L
-functions for the situation of an arbitrary finite abelian group acting on a finite graph without inversion, which we can apply to the study of branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers of finite connected graphs coming from voltage assignments. Everything is concrete enough that calculations can readily be made, but our exposition is incomplete in the sense that the non-commutative setting, the connections with the fundamental group of graphs of groups and its abelianization, the analogue of the Artin reciprocity map, the analogue of Frobenius automorphisms, and the connection with the work of Hashimoto in
[
12
]
are not studied here. Thus, there is still room for further studies and clarifications.

The paper is organized as follows. In

, we gather together some results from commutative algebra, representation theory, and on the

-adic valuation that we shall need thoughout the paper.

\mathbb{C}
-linear representation
V
of the underlying finite abelian group an Ihara

-function denoted by

. The Artin formalism satisfied by these functions is studied in
Section

5.4
and is viewed as a consequence of the Artin formalism studied previously in an equivariant setting in
Section

4.6
. In
Section

6
, we apply the theory of Ihara
L
-functions detailed in
Sections

4
and
5
to reprove the analogue of Iwasawa’s asymptotic class number formula for branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers of finite connected graphs arising from voltage assignments. This result is contained in

. In

, we relate a generator for the characteristic ideal of the finitely generated torsion Iwasawa module that governs the growth of the

-part of the number of spanning trees in such towers with Ihara

, §6]
from this point of view in

is an (not necessarily commutative)

R

-algebra, then by an

-bimodule with the right multiplication by elements of

For us, the commutative ring

2.1.1.
Polynomials and power series

If
R
is a commutative ring, we let

be the commutative ring consisting of polynomials with coefficients in

; it consists of expressions of the form

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}r_{i}u^{i},

where
r_{i}\in R
for all indices

and
r_{i}=0
for all but finitely indices
i
. Polynomials are added and multiplied in the usual way.

More generally, if
R
is a commutative ring and
A
is an (not necessarily commutative)

-algebra, we let

denote the
R[u]
-algebra of polynomials with coefficients in
A

\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\rightarrow B

Similarly, if

, then we let

be the abelian group consisting of polynomials with coefficients in

; it is an

-module, the action of

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}r_{i}u^{i}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}m_{j}u^{j}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i+j=k}r_{i}m_{j}\right)u^{k}.

If

of

-modules which is obtained by applying

-bimodule on

is also a free

-module of the same rank over

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and
M
a finitely generated
R
-module. The function

\omega_{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{R}(M)[u]\rightarrow{\rm End}_{R[u]}(M[u])

defined via
P\mapsto\omega_{M}(P)
, where

\omega_{M}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i}u^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}m_{j}u^{j}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i+j=k}f_{i}(m_{j})\right)u^{k}

is an isomorphism of
R[u]
-algebras.

Proof.

Everything is routine, except for the surjectivity which is also the only place where the hypothesis that
M
is a finitely generated
R
-module is used: If
f\in{\rm End}_{R[u]}(M[u])
, and
m\in M
, then

f(m)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i}(m)u^{i}

for some
f_{i}(m)\in M
that are all zero except for finitely many indices
i
. Since
f
is a morphism of
R[u]
-module, one readily checks that
f_{i}\in{\rm End}_{R}(M)
. Moreover the hypothesis that
M
is finitely generated over

implies that
f_{i}=0
for all but finitely many indices

; therefore,

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i}u^{i}\in{\rm End}_{R}(M)[u].

The result follows after noticing that
\omega_{M}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i}u^{i}\right)=f
.
∎

2.1.2.
Exponential and logarithm

If
A
is assumed to be a (not necessarily commutative)
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, then recall that we have the usual exponential power series given by

E=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{n}}{n!}\in 1+u\mathbb{Q}\llbracket u\rrbracket.

We then get the exponential function
{\rm exp}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}uA\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow 1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket
defined via

. If we let

\lambda=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{u^{n}}{n}\in u\mathbb{Q}\llbracket u\rrbracket,

\log_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow uA\llbracket u\rrbracket

We list in the following proposition a few properties of
\exp_{A}
and

that we shall need in this paper.

Proposition 2.4
.

The exponential and logarithm functions

\exp_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}uA\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow 1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket\text{ and }\log_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow uA\llbracket u\rrbracket

(1)

They are inverses of one another and are homeomorphisms for the
u
-adic topology.

(2)

If

is another
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, and if
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow B\llbracket u\rrbracket
is a morphism of
\mathbb{Q}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras, then

\varphi\circ{\rm exp}_{A}={\rm exp}_{B}\circ\varphi\text{ and }\varphi\circ{\rm log}_{A}={\rm log}_{B}\circ\varphi.

(3)

If
P_{1},P_{2}\in uA\llbracket u\rrbracket
commute, one has

{\rm exp}_{A}(P_{1}+P_{2})={\rm exp}_{A}(P_{1})\cdot{\rm exp}_{A}(P_{2}).

Proof.

See
[
4
, II, §6, no.1]
.
∎

Note in particular that if
P\in 1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket
, and

, then the expression

makes sense and is defined as usual via the equality

(2.2)

P^{a}=\exp_{A}\left(a\cdot\log_{A}(P)\right)\in 1+uA\llbracket u\rrbracket.

If

is another
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra and
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\rightarrow B
is a morphism of
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras, then it induces another

-algebra morphism

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, and let

(1)

If

(2)

If

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of

-algebra. Then, the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces uM_{n}(R\llbracket u\rrbracket)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces uR\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I+uM_{n}(R\llbracket u\rrbracket)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1+uR\llbracket u\rrbracket}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rm exp}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm tr}_{R\llbracket u\rrbracket}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm exp}_{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{R\llbracket u\rrbracket}}

commutes, where the left vertical arrow is the usual exponential of matrices, and
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

If
F
is a free
R
-module of finite rank and
f\in{\rm End}_{R}(F)
, then the trace and determinant functions

(2.3)

{\rm tr}_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{R}(F)\rightarrow R\text{ and }{\rm det}_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{R}(F)\rightarrow R

are defined as usual. Those definitions are extended to finitely generated projective modules over
R
and we now recall one way to do that (see for instance
[
8
]
). Let
M
be a finitely generated projective
R
-module and let
f\in{\rm End}_{R}(M)
. Since
M
is projective over
R
, there exists a free
R
-module
F
of finite rank and another
R
-module
N
such that
F=M\oplus N
. Consider now
f\oplus 0_{N},f\oplus{\rm id}_{N}\in{\rm End}_{R}(F)
, where
0_{N}
and
{\rm id}_{N}
are the zero and identity map on
N
, respectively. The trace and determinant of
f
are defined to be

{\rm tr}_{R}(f)={\rm tr}_{R}(f\oplus 0_{N})\text{ and }{\rm det}_{R}(f)={\rm det}_{R}(f\oplus{\rm id}_{N}).

It can be shown that those are well defined, meaning that the choices of
F
and
N
are irrelevant, and also that both the trace and the determinant satisfy the usual base change property with respect to a ring morphism
R\rightarrow S
. Moreover if
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are two finitely generated projective
R
-modules and
f_{i}\in{\rm End}_{R}(M_{i})
for
i=1,2
, then for
f_{1}\oplus f_{2}\in{\rm End}_{R}(M_{1}\oplus M_{2})
, one has

(2.4)

{\rm tr}_{R}(f_{1}\oplus f_{2})={\rm tr}_{R}(f_{1})+{\rm tr}_{R}(f_{2})\text{ and }{\rm det}_{R}(f_{1}\oplus f_{2})={\rm det}_{R}(f_{1})\cdot{\rm det}_{R}(f_{2}).

If
F
is a free
R
-module of rank one, then the function

(2.5)

{\rm End}_{R}(F)\rightarrow R

given by
f\mapsto r_{f}
, where
r_{f}
is the unique element in
R
satisfying
f(m)=r_{f}\cdot m
for all
m\in F
is an isomorphism of
R
-algebras, and it coincides with both
{\rm tr}_{R}
and
{\rm det}_{R}
.

We will also make use of the (Hattori-Stallings) rank of a finitely generated projective
R
-module
M
which is defined to be

{\rm rank}_{R}(M)={\rm tr}_{R}({\rm id}_{M})\in R.

The rank is additive, meaning that if

0\rightarrow M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}\rightarrow M_{3}\rightarrow 0

is a short exact sequence of projective
R
-modules, then

{\rm rank}_{R}(M_{2})={\rm rank}_{R}(M_{1})+{\rm rank}_{R}(M_{3}),

which follows from (
2.4
).

At last, we will make use of the following result on determinants of block matrices, for which the reader can find a proof in
[
26
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
R
and
S
be commutative rings, and assume that we are given a ring morphism
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\rightarrow M_{n}(R)
. Then the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(S)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{n}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(M_{n}(R))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{mn}(R)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{R}}

commutes, where the first vertical arrow is the one induced by
\rho
as explained in the first paragraph of
Section

2.2
.

2.3.
Representation theory

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. We remind the reader that the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
is semisimple, and thus in particular any
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module is projective. We choose to work with
\mathbb{C}
, but later in
Section

6.3
we will use
\mathbb{C}_{p}
. If the readers desire, they can pretend
\mathbb{C}
is an arbitrary algebraically closed field of characteristic zero throughout. In this paper, we use the module theoretical approach to representation theory of finite groups. Thus we view a finite dimensional linear representation of
G
over
\mathbb{C}
as a finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module which amounts to the same as a finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-linear representation of the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
, namely a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphism

(2.6)

\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V),

for some finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
V
. Moreover, we will refer to any such finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module
V
as an Artin representation of
G
. If
V
is an Artin representation of
G
, then we let
\psi_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denote its character which is obtained by composing (
2.6
) with the trace map
{\rm tr}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
from (
2.3
). Two Artin representations are isomorphic as
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules if and only if they have the same characters.

A character is called irreducible if it comes from an irreducible representation. The collection of all irreducible characters will be denoted by
{\rm Ir}(G)
. It is known to be a finite set, and since
G
is assumed to be abelian, there is a one-to-one correspondence between
{\rm Ir}(G)
and
\widehat{G}
, where
\widehat{G}
denotes the collection of group morphisms from
G
into
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Recall that for an arbitrary Artin representation,
\psi_{V}
is only a
\mathbb{C}
-linear morphism, and is not necessarily a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphism. On the other hand, if
V
is assumed to be an irreducible representation, then its character
\psi_{V}
is a morphism of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras. Given
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, we will use the same symbol
\psi
to denote the corresponding morphism of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. This should not cause any confusion, since the restriction of the latter to
G
is the same as the former.

If
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, we let

e_{\psi}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\psi(\sigma)\cdot\sigma^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}[G]

be the usual primitive idempotent of
\mathbb{C}[G]
associated with the character
\psi
. These idempotents are orthogonal, meaning that
e_{\psi_{1}}\cdot e_{\psi_{2}}=0
, when
\psi_{1}\neq\psi_{2}
, as a simple calculation using the usual orthogonality relation

(2.7)

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\psi_{1}(\sigma)\cdot\psi_{2}(\sigma^{-1})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\psi_{1}\neq\psi_{2};\\
1,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

shows. We will also make use of the following (usually non-primitive) idempotents: If
H\leq G
, we let

(2.8)

e_{H}=\frac{1}{|H|}N_{H}\in\mathbb{C}[G],

where

N_{H}=\sum_{h\in H}h.

The following lemma will be used often, and it is simple enough that we omit its proof.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and let
H\leq G
. Consider the natural ring morphism
\pi_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G/H]
and let
I(H)
be its kernel so that we have a short exact sequence

(2.9)

0\rightarrow I(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{H}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}[G/H]\rightarrow 0

of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. The
\mathbb{C}
-linear function
\iota_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G/H]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]
defined on cosets via

\sigma H\mapsto\iota_{H}(\sigma H)=\sigma e_{H}

is a well-defined morphism of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules which gives a splitting of the short exact sequence (
2.9
) so that one has
\mathbb{C}[G]=I(H)\oplus\iota_{H}(\mathbb{C}[G/H])
. Moreover,

\iota_{H}(\mathbb{C}[G/H])=\mathbb{C}[G]e_{H}\text{ and }I(H)=\mathbb{C}[G](1-e_{H}).

2.4.
Permutation modules

Let
T
be a finite set on which a finite abelian group
G
acts, and let
S=T_{G}
, where we write
T_{G}
instead of
G\backslash T
for the orbits. For
t\in T
, we let
G_{t}={\rm Stab}_{G}(t)
. Since
G
is abelian,
G_{t}
depends only on
s=Gt\in S
, and thus we shall often write
G_{s}
instead of
G_{t}
, where
s=Gt
. Let
\mathbb{C}T
denote the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space whose basis is given by the elements in
T
. In this section, we study the structure of
\mathbb{C}T
as a
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module (it is a permutation module).

We fix a labeling
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{g}\}
of
S
and for each
i=1,\ldots,g
, we let
t_{i}
be a fixed element of
T
such that
Gt_{i}=s_{i}
. For
i=1,\ldots,g
, we write
G_{i}
instead of
G_{s_{i}}
, and similarly we write
e_{i}
instead of
e_{G_{i}}
for the idempotent corresponding to the subgroup
G_{i}
that was defined above in (
2.8
). Since

\mathbb{C}T=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i},

we have to understand the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules
\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}
. For that purpose, let
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, and consider the natural surjective
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module morphism
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}
given by
\lambda\mapsto\phi(\lambda)=\lambda t_{i}
. The map
\phi
leads to a short exact sequence

(2.10)

0\rightarrow{\rm Ann}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(t_{i})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}\rightarrow 0

of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. In order to understand
{\rm Ann}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(t_{i})
, we shall make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

With the notation as above, if
\psi\in\widehat{G}
is a non-trivial character satisfying
G_{i}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)
, then in
\mathbb{C}T
, we have
e_{\psi}t_{i}=0
.

Proof.

Let
{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r}}
be a complete set of representatives of
G/G_{i}
. Then, we calculate

\displaystyle e_{\psi}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\psi(\sigma)\sigma^{-1}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{h\in G_{i}}\psi(\sigma_{j}h)(\sigma_{j}h)^{-1}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\left(\sum_{h\in G_{i}}\psi(h)\right)\sum_{j=1}^{r}\psi(\sigma_{j})\sigma_{j}^{-1}t_{i}

\displaystyle=0,

by the orthogonality relation (
2.7
).
∎

This last lemma allows us to describe
{\rm Ann}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(t_{i})
.

Corollary 2.10
.

With the notation as above, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}
, one has

{\rm Ann}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(t_{i})=\mathbb{C}[G](1-e_{i}).

Proof.

One has

e_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in\widehat{G}\\
G_{i}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\end{subarray}}e_{\psi},

and thus
Lemma

2.9
implies
\mathbb{C}[G](1-e_{i})\subseteq{\rm Ann}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(t_{i})
. Noticing that
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i})=(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{i})
, a dimension count combined with the short exact sequence (
2.10
) gives the desired result.
∎

Combining this last corollary with
Lemma

2.8
shows also that
\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[G/G_{i}]
as
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. An explicit isomorphism is given by the map
\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G/G_{i}]
defined via

\lambda t_{i}\mapsto\pi_{i}(\lambda),

where
\pi_{i}=\pi_{G_{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G/G_{i}]
is the natural projection map. Moreover, we also have that
\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}
by
Lemma

2.8
, where an explicit isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[G]t_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}
is given by

(2.11)

\lambda t_{i}\mapsto\lambda e_{i}.

It follows that if we let

(2.12)

M=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G](1-e_{i}),

then
\mathbb{C}T\oplus M\simeq\mathbb{C}[G]^{g}
and this gives a convenient way to view the projective
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module
\mathbb{C}T
as a direct summand of a free
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module of finite rank.

We end this section with the following proposition that gives the dimension of the isotypic components of the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module
\mathbb{C}T
. For that purpose, if
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, then we let

(2.13)

r_{T}(\psi)=|\{s\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
\psi\in\widehat{G}
. One has

\displaystyle{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}\mathbb{C}T)

\displaystyle=|\{s\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|

\displaystyle=|S|-r_{T}(\psi).

A
\mathbb{C}
-basis for the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
e_{\psi}\mathbb{C}T
is given by
\{e_{\psi}t_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{i}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}
.

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{C}T\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}.

If
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, then

e_{\psi}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}\simeq\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }G_{i}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi);\\
\mathbb{C},&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

It follows that
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}\mathbb{C}T)=|\{s\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|
.
∎

2.5.
The
p
-adic valuation

Although it is not strictly necessary, it is convenient in
Section

6
to work
p
-adically where
p
is a rational prime, so here we introduce some relevant notation. (One could instead choose to work globally with the cyclotomic number field of prime conductor
p
and the valuation associated to its unique prime ideal lying above
p
.) The symbol
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
denotes the ring of
p
-adic integers and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
the field of
p
-adic numbers. All the calculations will happen in an algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, but it is convenient to work in a completion of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
which we denote by
\mathbb{C}_{p}
. The usual
p
-adic valuation on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
will be denoted by
{\rm ord}_{p}
as well as its extension to
\mathbb{C}_{p}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
p
be a rational prime. If
P(T)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[T]
is a nonzero polynomial, then

{\rm ord}_{p}(P(p^{n}))=\lambda n+\nu

provided
n
is large enough, where

\lambda={\rm ord}_{T=0}(P(T))\text{ and }\nu={\rm ord}_{p}(a_{\lambda})

if
a_{\lambda}
is the coefficient of
T^{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Write

P(T)=a_{\lambda}T^{\lambda}+\ldots+a_{d}T^{d}

for some
a_{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
with
a_{\lambda}\neq 0
. Note that
{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{j}p^{jn})=jn+{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{j})
and thus

\lambda n+{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{\lambda})<{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{j}p^{jn})

for all
j>\lambda
provided
n
is large enough. It follows that

\displaystyle{\rm ord}_{p}(P(p^{n}))

\displaystyle={\rm min}\{{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{j}p^{jn})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j=\lambda,\ldots,d\}

\displaystyle=\lambda n+\nu,

provided
n
is large enough as we wanted to show.
∎

Recall that given a non-zero power series

P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}T^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket,

one defines

\mu(P(T))={\rm min}\{{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{i})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}\text{ and }\lambda(P(T))={\rm min}\{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm ord}_{p}(a_{i})=\mu(P(T))\}.

Lemma 2.13
.

Given
P(T)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
such that
P(T)\neq 0
, and letting
\mu=\mu(P(T))
and
\lambda=\lambda(P(T))
, there exists
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
such that if
n
is large enough, one has

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\in\mu_{p^{n}}\\
{\rm ord}(\zeta)=p^{j}\end{subarray}}{\rm ord}_{p}(P(\zeta-1))=\mu p^{n}+\lambda n+\nu,

where the second sum is over the
p^{n}
th roots of unity that have order precisely
p^{j}
.

Proof.

Let
R(T)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
be such that
P(T)=p^{\mu}R(T)
. Then, if
R(T)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e_{k}T^{k}
, we have that
\lambda={\rm min}\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm ord}_{p}(e_{k})=0\}
. Now, notice that it is enough to prove that for large
i
,

(2.14)

{\rm ord}_{p}(R(\zeta-1))=\frac{\lambda}{\phi(p^{i})}

where
{\rm ord}(\zeta)=p^{i}
, and
\phi
denotes the Euler
\phi
-function. We assume
i
is large enough so that
\lambda<\phi(p^{i})
as this ensures that the values

{\rm ord}_{p}(e_{k}(\zeta-1)^{k})={\rm ord}_{p}(e_{k})+\frac{k}{\phi(p^{i})}

are distinct for
k=0,...,\lambda
. (Indeed, if two such values were equal with
0\leq j<k\leq\lambda
, then
(k-j)/\phi(p^{i})\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0<(k-j)/\phi(p^{i})\leq\lambda/\phi(p^{i})<1
which is a contradiction.) From this, together with the fact that for
k<\lambda
one has

{\rm ord}_{p}(e_{k}(\zeta-1)^{k})>{\rm ord}_{p}(e_{k})\geq 1>\frac{\lambda}{\phi(p^{i})},

we obtain

{\rm ord}_{p}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\lambda}e_{k}(\zeta-1)^{k}\right)=\frac{\lambda}{\phi(p^{i})}.

In addition we have

{\rm ord}_{p}\left(\sum_{k=\lambda+1}^{\infty}e_{k}(\zeta-1)^{k}\right)\geq\min\Big\{\frac{k}{\phi(p^{i})}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k>\lambda\Big\}=\frac{\lambda+1}{\phi(p^{i})}

and (
2.14
) follows. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Graph theory

3.1.
Basic definitions

Throughout, we use Serre’s formalism for graphs as detailed in
[
25
]
and
[
30
]
for instance. Here, we are deliberately brief and we refer the reader to
[
7
]
for more details, since we are using the exact same notation. If
Y
is a graph,
V_{Y}
denotes its collection of vertices,
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
its collection of directed edges, and
E_{Y}
its collection of undirected edges. If
\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, then
o(\varepsilon)
denotes its origin vertex and
t(\varepsilon)
its terminus vertex. Moreover, the inversion map is denoted by
\varepsilon\mapsto\bar{\varepsilon}
. Given
w\in V_{Y}
, we let

\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}=\{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}o(\varepsilon)=w\},

and we set
{\rm val}_{Y}(w)=|\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}|
provided the set
\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}
is finite. The graph
Y
is called locally finite if
\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}
is a finite set for all
w\in V_{Y}
and is called finite if both
V_{Y}
and
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
are finite sets. Paths in graphs are defined as usual. A graph
Y
is connected if there is a path between any two vertices in
Y
.

Morphisms of graphs are defined as usual (see
[
7
, Definition 2.1]
). Branched covers are a particular kind of morphisms of graphs whose definition can be found in
[
7
, Definition 3.1]
. If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\rightarrow X
is a branched cover of finite graphs and
w\in V_{Y}
, we denote its ramification index by
m_{w}
. Under the assumption that
Y
and
X
are finite and connected, the quantity

\sum_{w\in f^{-1}(v)}m_{w},

does not depend on
v\in V_{X}
. This constant is denoted by
[Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X]
, and is called the degree of the finite branched cover.

3.2.
The Euler characteristic

Let
Y
be a finite graph. We set
C_{0}(Y)=\mathbb{C}V_{Y}
; using the notation of
[
7
]
, one has
C_{0}(Y)\simeq\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\rm Div}(Y)
. Inside
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, we let
W
be the
\mathbb{C}
-vector subspace generated by
\{\varepsilon+\bar{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}\}
, and we define
C_{1}(Y)=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}/W
. We have a natural morphism of
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces
\partial\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
defined on the
\mathbb{C}
-basis
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
via

\varepsilon\mapsto\partial(\varepsilon)=t(\varepsilon)-o(\varepsilon).

Since
\partial(\varepsilon+\bar{\varepsilon})=0
, the map
\partial
induces a morphism of
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces
\partial\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{1}(Y)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
. This gives us a chain complex of
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces

(3.1)

0\rightarrow C_{1}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial}}{{\longrightarrow}}C_{0}(Y)\rightarrow 0.

Definition 3.1
.

The Euler characteristic of
Y
is the Euler characteristic of the chain complex (
3.1
), that is
\chi(Y)={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{0}(Y))-{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{1}(Y))
.

The Euler characteristic of a finite graph can therefore be calculated using the following simple formula.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Y
be a finite graph, then
\chi(Y)=|V_{Y}|-|E_{Y}|
.

Proof.

This follows directly from
Definition

3.1
and the observations that

{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{0}(Y))=|V_{Y}|\text{ and }{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{1}(Y))=|E_{Y}|.

∎

In
[
2
]
, Baker and Norine proved an analogue of the Riemann-Hurwitz formula for branched covers of graphs (and more generally for harmonic morphisms). We will make use of this formula in several instances throughout this paper (see also
[
30
, Theorem 5.12]
). We record it here for future reference.

Theorem 3.3

(Riemann-Hurwitz formula)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\rightarrow X
be a branched cover of finite connected graphs. Then, one has

\chi(Y)=[Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X]\cdot\chi(X)-\sum_{w\in V_{Y}}(m_{w}-1).

3.3.
The laplacian operator

In this section, we define a few operators on
C_{0}(Y)
under the assumption that
Y
is locally finite. First, one defines the valency (or degree) operator
\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}(Y)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
via

w\mapsto{\rm val}_{Y}(w)\cdot w.

Second, one defines the adjacency operator
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}(Y)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
via

w\mapsto\mathcal{A}(w)=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}}t(\varepsilon).

If moreover
Y
is finite and one chooses a labeling
V_{Y}=\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{q}\}
of the vertices, then the matrices of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the ordered
\mathbb{C}
-basis
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{q})
are called the valency (or degree) matrix and the adjacency matrix of
Y
, respectively. Those two matrices are simply denoted by
D
and
A
. The laplacian operator is defined to be
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{A}
and its matrix with respect to the same ordered
\mathbb{C}
-basis as above is called the laplacian matrix. One sets
{\rm Pr}(Y)=\mathcal{L}(C_{0}(Y))
and when
Y
is finite and connected, one has the short exact sequence

(3.2)

0\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\sum_{w\in V_{Y}}w\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm Pr}(Y)\rightarrow 0

of
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces. We refer the reader to
[
7
, Theorem 2.3]
for more details. A consequence of (
3.2
) is that

(3.3)

{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{L})=0,

since the kernel of
\mathcal{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}(Y)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
is non-trivial.

3.4.
The Ihara zeta function

We refer the reader to
[
32
]
for the basic definitions and results pertaining to the Ihara zeta function of a finite graph, and here we will be deliberately brief. If
Y
is a finite graph, we let

Z_{Y}(u)=\exp_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}N_{k}\frac{u^{k}}{k}\right)\in 1+u\mathbb{Q}\llbracket u\rrbracket,

where
N_{k}
is the number of reduced closed paths in
Y
of length
k
. At first,
Z_{Y}(u)
is a formal power series with rational coefficients, but since

Z_{Y}(u)=\prod_{\mathfrak{c}}(1-u^{{\rm len}(\mathfrak{c})})^{-1},

where the product is over the primes
\mathfrak{c}
of
Y
and
{\rm len}(\mathfrak{c})
denotes the length of
\mathfrak{c}
, it follows that
Z_{Y}(u)\in 1+u\mathbb{Z}\llbracket u\rrbracket
. In fact,
Z_{Y}(u)
is the reciprocal of a polynomial with integer coefficients as the following theorem shows for which an elegant proof can be found in
[
18
]
for instance.

Theorem 3.4

(Ihara’s determinant formula)
.

Let
Y
be a finite graph. Its Ihara zeta function satisfies

Z_{Y}(u)^{-1}=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(Y)}\cdot{\rm det}(I-Au+(D-I)u^{2})\in\mathbb{Z}[u],

where
A
is the adjacency matrix and
D
the degree matrix of
Y
.

From now on, we let

c_{Y}(u)=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(Y)}\text{ and }h_{Y}(u)={\rm det}(I-Au+(D-I)u^{2}).

Note that
h_{Y}(u)\in 1+u\mathbb{Z}[u]
. We will be particularly interested in the special value at
u=1
of Ihara zeta and
L
-functions. For the following theorem, we refer the reader to
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
Y
be a finite connected graph. Then, one has

h_{Y}^{\prime}(1)=-2\chi(Y)\kappa(Y),

where
\kappa(Y)
denotes the number of spanning trees of
Y
.

4.
Equivariant graph theory

4.1.
Groups acting on graphs

A group
G
acts on a graph
Y
if we are given a group morphism
G\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(Y)
. The action is said to be without inversion if one has
\sigma\cdot\varepsilon\neq\bar{\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}
and all
\sigma\in G
. In this case, the quotient
G\backslash Y=(G\backslash V_{Y},G\backslash\mathbf{E}_{Y})
is a graph in itself with the incidence and inversion maps induced from the ones on
Y
. We shall write
Y_{G}
instead of
G\backslash Y
throughout.

As explained in
[
7
, Proposition 3.3]
, if
G
is a finite group acting on a graph
Y
without inversion and freely on
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, then the natural morphism of graphs
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\rightarrow X
, where
X=Y_{G}
, is a branched cover of graphs for which
m_{w}=|{\rm Stab}_{G}(w)|
for all
w\in V_{Y}
. From now on, we let
G_{w}={\rm Stab}_{G}(w)
. Note that since stabilizers of vertices in the same orbits are conjugate, the ramification indices
m_{w}
depend only on
f(w)
. We will often write
m_{v}
for the ramification index of a vertex
w\in V_{Y}
, where
v=f(w)
.

In this paper,
G
will always be a finite abelian group. Therefore, the stabilizer group
G_{w}
also depends only on
v=f(w)
, and we shall often denote this stabilizer group by
G_{v}
, where
v=f(w)
. Thus, when
G
is abelian
m_{v}=|G_{v}|
, and there are
(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v})
vertices in
Y
lying above
v
.

4.2.
Group actions obtained from voltage assignments

As explained in
[
7
, §4.1]
, one can use voltage assignments in order to construct graphs on which a group acts freely on directed edges. We refer the reader to
[
7
, §4.1]
for the detailed construction. Briefly, the construction starts with a base graph
X
, a group
G
, a function
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow G
satisfying
\alpha(\bar{s})=\alpha(s)^{-1}
for all
s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}
, and a collection of subgroups

\mathcal{G}=\{(v,G_{v})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}v\in V_{X}\text{ and }G_{v}\leq G\}.

To this data, one associates a graph
Y=X(G,\mathcal{G},\alpha)
which is a branched cover of
X
. Moreover, the group
G
acts without inversion on
Y
and freely on directed edges. See
[
7
, Proposition 4.1]
for the complete list of properties that this branched cover satisfies.

For us in this paper,
G
will always be a finite abelian group. One thing we did not mention in
[
7
]
is that one always has
Y_{G}\simeq X
. In fact, we leave it to the reader to check that the following more general statement holds true: If
H
is a subgroup of
G
and
\Gamma=G/H
, then the map

(4.1)

f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y_{H}\rightarrow X(\Gamma,\pi_{H}(\mathcal{G}),\pi_{H}\circ\alpha)

given by

H(v,\sigma G_{v})\mapsto f(H(v,\sigma G_{v}))=(v,\pi_{H}(\sigma)\pi_{H}(G_{v}))\text{ and }H(e,\sigma)\mapsto f(H(e,\sigma))=(e,\pi_{H}(\sigma))

is a well-defined
\Gamma
-equivariant isomorphism of graphs, where
\pi_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\rightarrow\Gamma
denotes the natural surjective group morphism, and where we use the notation of
[
7
, §4.2]
. This will allow us to identify
Y_{H}
with
X(\Gamma,\pi_{H}(\mathcal{G}),\pi_{H}\circ\alpha)
in the future. For more information on voltage assignments, and more generally on the classification of harmonic
G
-covers, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
.

4.3.
The equivariant Euler characteristic

Let
G
be a finite abelian group acting on a finite graph
Y
without inversion and let
X=Y_{G}
. The
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
C_{0}(Y)
becomes a
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module and is a permutation module so we can apply the results of
Section

2.4
. Thus, we now introduce a similar notation to the one used in
Section

2.4
for
C_{0}(Y)
. We fix a labeling
V_{X}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{g}\}
of the vertices of
X
and for each
i=1,\ldots,g
, we let
w_{i}
be a fixed vertex of
Y
lying above
v_{i}
. For
i=1,\ldots,g
, we write
G_{i}
instead of
G_{v_{i}}={\rm Stab}_{G}(w_{i})
.

The
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}
is also a permutation module, and therefore a similar notation could be used. In particular, we will use the notation
G_{\varepsilon}
to denote
{\rm Stab}_{G}(\varepsilon)
whenever
\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}
. Moreover,
W
is a
\mathbb{C}[G]
-submodule of
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, and is in fact a permutation module itself. Indeed, if
O
is an orientation for
Y
, that is
O\subseteq\mathbf{E}_{Y}
is such that

O\cap\bar{O}=\varnothing
and
\mathbf{E}_{Y}=O\cup\bar{O}
, then
\Omega=\{\varepsilon+\bar{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varepsilon\in O\}
is
G
-stable so that
\Omega
is a
G
-set, and
W\simeq\mathbb{C}\Omega
. Since both
W
and
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}
are
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules, then so is the quotient
C_{1}(Y)
.

The
\mathbb{C}
-linear morphism
\partial
can be checked to be
G
-equivariant, from which it follows that the chain complex (
3.1
) becomes a chain complex of finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. Since
\mathbb{C}[G]
is semisimple, both
C_{0}(Y)
and
C_{1}(Y)
are projective
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules and their (Hattori-Stallings) ranks over
\mathbb{C}[G]
therefore make sense, as explained in
Section

2.2
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
Y
be a finite graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion. One defines the equivariant Euler characteristic of
Y
to be

\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)={\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{0}(Y))-{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{1}(Y)).

Our next goal is to find an explicit formula for the equivariant Euler characteristic similar to the one in
Proposition

3.2
for the usual Euler characteristic.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group acting on a finite graph
Y
without inversion and set
X=Y_{G}
. Let
O_{X}
be an orientation of
X
. Then

\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)=\sum_{v\in V_{X}}e_{G_{v}}-\sum_{s\in O_{X}}e_{G_{s}}.

In particular, if
G
acts freely on directed edges, then

\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)=\sum_{v\in V_{X}}e_{G_{v}}-|E_{X}|.

Proof.

Since

C_{0}(Y)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G]w_{i}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i},

by the isomorphisms (
2.11
) and since the rank over
\mathbb{C}[G]
is additive (see
Section

2.2
), it suffices to understand the rank over
\mathbb{C}[G]
of the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules
\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}
. But a simple calculation using the supplement
\mathbb{C}[G](1-e_{i})
of
\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}
gives
{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i})=e_{i}
. Therefore, we have

(4.2)

{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{0}(Y))=\sum_{v\in V_{X}}e_{G_{v}}.

For
C_{1}(Y)
, we consider the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow W\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}\rightarrow C_{1}(Y)\rightarrow 0

of finitely generated projective
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. Both
W
and
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y}
are permutation modules and thus their rank can be calculated similarly as above. Noting that
G_{\varepsilon}=G_{\bar{\varepsilon}}=G_{\varepsilon+\bar{\varepsilon}}
, one calculates as above

{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y})=\sum_{s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}}e_{G_{s}}\text{ and }{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(W)=\sum_{s\in O_{X}}e_{G_{s}},

and thus

(4.3)

{\rm rank}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{1}(Y))=\sum_{s\in O_{X}}e_{G_{s}}.

Putting (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) together gives the desired result.

∎

4.4.
The equivariant Ihara zeta function

We place ourselves again in the situation where a finite abelian group
G
acts on a finite graph
Y
without inversion. Any
\sigma\in G
induces a bijection
\mathbf{E}_{Y,w}\rightarrow\mathbf{E}_{Y,\sigma\cdot w}
from which it follows that both the degree operator
\mathcal{D}
and the adjacency operator
\mathcal{A}
are morphisms of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. We let
\mathcal{I}
be the identity operator on
C_{0}(Y)
, and we define also
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{I}
. The following polynomial

(4.4)

\Delta(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A}u+\mathcal{Q}u^{2}\in{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{0}(Y))[u]

will play an important role. Via the isomorphism

\omega_{C_{0}(Y)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{0}(Y))[u]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(C_{0}(Y)[u])

given by
Theorem

2.1
, we will identify polynomials in
{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(C_{0}(Y))[u]
with endomorphisms in
{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(C_{0}(Y)[u])
. In particular, since
C_{0}(Y)[u]
is a projective
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-module, it makes sense to talk about the determinant of the
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-operator
\Delta(u)
defined above in (
4.4
), and we shall denote it by

\eta_{Y}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(\Delta(u))\in 1+u\mathbb{C}[G][u].

Moreover, the expression

\gamma_{Y}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)}\in 1+u\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket

makes sense as well and was defined in (
2.2
) above. Note that

\gamma_{Y}(u),\eta_{Y}(u)\in 1+u\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket^{\times}.

We can now define the equivariant Ihara zeta function
\theta_{Y}(u)
which is the central object of study in this paper.

Definition 4.3
.

With the notation as above, we define the equivariant Ihara zeta function
\theta_{Y}(u)
via

\theta_{Y}(u)^{-1}=\gamma_{Y}(u)\cdot\eta_{Y}(u)\in 1+u\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket^{\times}.

One way to calculate
\eta_{Y}(u)
explicitly, is to find a
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module
M
such that

C_{0}(Y)[u]\oplus M[u]\simeq(C_{0}(Y)\oplus M)[u]

is free of finite rank over
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
, and then calculate
{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(\Delta(u)\oplus{\rm id}_{M[u]})
. Such a module
M
is provided by (
2.12
), and in
Section

4.5
below we carry this out in detail.

4.5.
An explicit formula for the equivariant Ihara zeta function

We use the same notation as in the previous section. Given
w,w^{\prime}\in V_{Y}
, we define

a_{w}(w^{\prime})=|\{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}o(\varepsilon)=w^{\prime}\text{ and }t(\varepsilon)=w\}|,

and for
i=1,\ldots,g
, we also define
\ell_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{0}(Y)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]
via

w\mapsto\ell_{i}(w)=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{w_{i}}(\sigma w)\sigma^{-1}.

We leave it to the reader to check that
\ell_{i}
is a morphism of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. We will often use the fact that

(4.5)

a_{w}(\sigma w^{\prime})=a_{\sigma^{-1}w}(w^{\prime})

for all
w,w^{\prime}\in V_{Y}
and for all
\sigma\in G
, which follows from the observation that the function

\{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm inc}(\varepsilon)=(\sigma w^{\prime},w)\}\rightarrow\{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm inc}(\varepsilon)=(w^{\prime},\sigma^{-1}w)\}

given by
\varepsilon\mapsto\sigma^{-1}\varepsilon
is a bijection.

Proposition 4.4
.

For all
D\in C_{0}(Y)
, one has

\mathcal{A}(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(D)w_{i}

Proof.

Note that it suffices to show the claim for
D=w
, since the vertices of
Y
generate
C_{0}(Y)
as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space. Using (
4.5
), we calculate

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\in V_{Y}}a_{w_{0}}(w)w_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\sum_{\sigma\in G/G_{i}}a_{\sigma w_{i}}(w)\sigma w_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma w_{i}}(w)\sigma w_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{w_{i}}(\sigma^{-1}w)\sigma w_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\ell_{i}(w)w_{i},

as we wanted to show.
∎

From now on, we let

\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{g}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}.

Recall that we have a
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module isomorphism

(4.6)

C_{0}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y),

which is induced by the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module isomorphisms
\mathbb{C}[G]w_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{i}
from (
2.11
) above. We let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
be the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module morphisms
\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)\rightarrow\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)
that make the two diagrams

\begin{CD}C_{0}(Y)@>{\simeq}>{}>\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)\\
@V{}V{\mathcal{A}}V@V{}V{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}}V&\\
C_{0}(Y)@>{\simeq}>{}>\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)\end{CD}\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\begin{CD}C_{0}(Y)@>{\simeq}>{}>\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)\\
@V{}V{\mathcal{D}}V@V{}V{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}V&\\
C_{0}(Y)@>{\simeq}>{}>\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)\end{CD}

commutative, where the horizontal
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module isomorphisms are the map (
4.6
). The following lemma will simplify a few formulas later on.

Lemma 4.5
.

With the same notation as above, for all
i=1,\ldots,g
and all
D\in C_{0}(Y)
, one has

\ell_{i}(D)e_{i}=\ell_{i}(D).

Moreover,

\ell_{i}(w_{j})e_{j}=\ell_{i}(w_{j})

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,g
.

Proof.

It suffices to show this when
D=w
. Using (
4.5
), we calculate

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(w)e_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{w_{i}}(\sigma w)\sigma^{-1}e_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sum_{h\in G_{i}}a_{w_{i}}(\sigma w)(h\sigma)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sum_{h\in G_{i}}a_{w_{i}}(h^{-1}\sigma w)\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sum_{h\in G_{i}}a_{hw_{i}}(\sigma w)\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{w_{i}}(\sigma w)\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{i}(w).

The second claim is proved similarly, and we leave it to the reader.
∎

Consider now

\widetilde{\Delta}(u)=\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}u+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}u^{2}\in{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y))[u],

where
\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
is the identity operator on
\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)
. As before, we view

\widetilde{\Delta}(u)\in{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)[u])

via the isomorphism

\omega_{\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y))[u]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)[u])

given by
Theorem

2.1
. Recall now the
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module
M
from (
2.12
). We have

(4.7)

\widetilde{C}_{0}(Y)[u]\oplus M[u]\simeq(\mathbb{C}[G][u])^{g},

and therefore, we have

\eta_{Y}(u)={\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(\widetilde{\Delta}(u)\oplus{\rm id}_{M[u]}).

A simple calculation and
Lemma

4.5
shows that the matrix
(\widetilde{\delta}_{ij}(u))
for the
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-operator
\widetilde{\Delta}(u)\oplus{\rm id}_{M[u]}
with respect to the standard
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-basis of the free
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-module (
4.7
) is given by

\widetilde{\delta}_{ij}(u)=\begin{cases}-\ell_{i}(w_{j})u,&\text{ if }i\neq j;\\
1-\ell_{i}(w_{i})u+({\rm val}_{Y}(w_{i})-1)e_{i}u^{2},&\text{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

This last formula can be rephrased as follows.

Theorem 4.6
.

With the notation as above, let
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{a}_{ij})\in M_{g}(\mathbb{C}[G])
be the matrix given by
\mathbf{a}_{ij}=\ell_{i}(w_{j})
, and let
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{q}_{ij})\in M_{g}(\mathbb{C}[G])
be the diagonal matrix given by
\mathbf{q}_{ii}=({\rm val}_{Y}(w_{i})-1)e_{i}
. Then, one has

\eta_{Y}(u)={\rm det}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}u+\mathbf{Q}u^{2}),

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
g\times g
identity matrix in
M_{g}(\mathbb{C}[G])
.

This last theorem is convenient to calculate the polynomial
\eta_{Y}(u)
. We show how this can be done in the following example.

Example 4.7
.

We use the construction outlined in
Section

4.2
and explained in detail in
[
7
, §4.1]
to give an example. We take
X
to be the graph consisting of two vertices joined by two undirected edges. We label the vertices
V_{X}=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
and we let
s_{1},s_{2}
be the two directed edges going from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
. We take
G=\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
to be a cyclic group of order four. We take the voltage assigment
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow G
given by

\alpha(s_{1})=\bar{1},\text{ and }\alpha(s_{2})=\bar{2}.

Moreover, we let
G_{1}=\{\bar{0}\}
, and
G_{2}=\{\bar{0},\bar{2}\}=\langle\bar{2}\rangle
. The graph
Y=X(G,\mathcal{G},\alpha)
is a branched cover of
X
with ramification above the vertex
v_{2}

4.6.
The induction and inflation properties

Throughout this section,
G
will be a finite abelian group,
H\leq G
, and
\Gamma=G/H
. Let
Y
be a finite graph on which
G
acts without inversion. Then
H
also acts on
Y
without inversion. In addition to the natural projection map
\pi_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
, which is a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphism, we will introduce two functions

(4.8)

T_{G/H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[H]\text{ and }N_{G/H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[H]

that are not
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphisms in general, but are nonetheless useful. Their definitions are as follows. The module
\mathbb{C}[G]
is a free
\mathbb{C}[H]
-module of finite rank, for if
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\}
is a complete set of representatives for
\Gamma
, then the morphisms
\mathbb{C}[H]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[H]\sigma_{i}
of
\mathbb{C}[H]
-modules defined via
\lambda\mapsto\lambda\sigma_{i}
are isomorphisms for all
i=1,\ldots,n
; therefore,

\mathbb{C}[G]=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{C}[H]\sigma_{i}\simeq(\mathbb{C}[H])^{n}.

Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}[G]
, we let

m_{\lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]

denote the multiplication by
\lambda
map; it is a morphism of
\mathbb{C}[H]
-modules, and the maps
T_{G/H}
and
N_{G/H}
from (
4.8
) above are defined via

T_{G/H}(\lambda)={\rm tr}_{\mathbb{C}[H]}(m_{\lambda})\text{ and }N_{G/H}(\lambda)={\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[H]}(m_{\lambda}),

respectively. It follows that
T_{G/H}
is
\mathbb{C}[H]
-linear, and
N_{G/H}
is multiplicative. The two maps
T_{G/H}
and
N_{G/H}
can be extended to power series as follows. The
\mathbb{C}
-algebra of power series
\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket
is a free
\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket
-module of rank
n
as well. Therefore, if
P\in\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket
, then the multiplication by
P
map

(4.9)

m_{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket

is a morphism of
\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket
-modules, and one defines

T_{G/H}(P)={\rm tr}_{\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket}(m_{P})\text{ and }N_{G/H}(P)={\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket}(m_{P}).

As such, we obtain two functions

(4.10)

T_{G/H},N_{G/H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket,

which are
\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket
-linear and multiplicative, respectively.

The next property we study is the relationship between the function
T_{G/H}
and the equivariant Euler characteristic.

Lemma 4.10
.

With the notation as above, one has

T_{G/H}(\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y))=\chi_{\mathbb{C}[H]}(Y).

Proof.

Let
H,K\leq G
. We start by showing that

(4.12)

\frac{1}{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K)}T_{G/H}(e_{K})=\frac{1}{(H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\cap K)}e_{H\cap K}.

For that purpose, let
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\}
be a complete set of representatives of
G/H
. For
j=1,\ldots,n
, we have

\displaystyle e_{K}\cdot\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{k\in K}k\cdot\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{ij}(K)\cdot\sigma_{i},

where

\lambda_{ij}(K)=\sum_{x\in K\sigma_{j}\cap H\sigma_{i}}h(x),

with the convention that
h(x)
is the unique element in
H
satisfying
x=h(x)\sigma_{i}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle T_{G/H}(e_{K})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{ii}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in K\sigma_{i}\cap H\sigma_{i}}h(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in(K\cap H)\sigma_{i}}h(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in K\cap H}x

\displaystyle=\frac{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H)}{|K|}|K\cap H|e_{K\cap H}.

Using Lagrange’s theorem, a simple calculation shows that

\frac{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H)}{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K)}\frac{|K\cap H|}{|K|}=\frac{1}{(H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K\cap H)}

from which (
4.12
) above follows. Thus for each
w\in V_{Y}
, one has

T_{G/H}(e_{G_{w}})=\frac{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{w})}{(H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H_{w})}e_{H_{w}},

since
H_{w}=G_{w}\cap H
. The quotient group
\Gamma=G/H
acts on
Y_{H}
, and one has a short exact sequence

(4.13)

0\rightarrow H\cdot G_{w}/G_{w}\rightarrow G/G_{w}\rightarrow\Gamma/\Gamma_{Hw}\rightarrow 0

of groups, where
\Gamma_{Hw}={\rm Stab}_{\Gamma}(Hw)
. Since
H/H_{w}\simeq H\cdot G_{w}/G_{w}
, one has

\frac{(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{w})}{(H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H_{w})}=(\Gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma_{Hw}).

Now, since
(\Gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma_{Hw})
is precisely the number of vertices of
Y_{H}
that maps to the vertex
Gw\in Y_{G}
via the natural graph morphism
Y_{H}\rightarrow Y_{G}
, one obtains the equality

\sum_{v\in V_{Y_{G}}}T_{G/H}(e_{G_{v}})=\sum_{w\in V_{Y_{H}}}e_{H_{w}}.

A similar argument works for edges as well, and thus the result follows from
Theorem

4.2
.
∎

Next, we look at the relationship between
N_{G/H}
and the determinant in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module. Then the diagram

\begin{CD}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(M[u])@>{}>{}>{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[H][u]}(M[u])\\
@V{{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}}V{}V@V{}V{{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[H][u]}}V&\\
\mathbb{C}[G][u]@>{N_{G/H}}>{}>\mathbb{C}[H][u]\end{CD}

commutes, where the first horizontal arrow is obtained by viewing a
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-module operator on
M[u]
as a
\mathbb{C}[H][u]
-module operator which is possible, since
H\leq G
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the theorem for when
M
is free. So let us assume that
F
is a free
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module of finite rank. We fix an ordered
\mathbb{C}[G]
-basis
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
of
F
, and also an ordered
\mathbb{C}[H]
-basis
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
of
\mathbb{C}[G]
. Note that
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
is also an ordered
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-basis of
F[u]
and
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
an ordered
\mathbb{C}[H][u]
-basis of
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
. Consider now the ring morphism

\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G][u]\rightarrow M_{n}(\mathbb{C}[H][u])

given by
P\mapsto\rho(P)=[m_{P}]
, where
[m_{P}]
is the matrix representing the
\mathbb{C}[H][u]
-operator
m_{P}
with respect to the basis
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
.
Theorem

2.7
gives a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(\mathbb{C}[G][u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G][u]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{n}(\mathbb{C}[H][u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}[H][u]))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{mn}(\mathbb{C}[H][u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[H][u]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[H][u]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}[H][u]}}

Moreover, we also have another commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G][u]}(F[u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(\mathbb{C}[G][u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[H][u]}(F[u])}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{m}(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}[H][u])),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

where the first horizontal arrow is the one obtained by writing the matrix of a
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-operator on
F[u]
using the ordered
\mathbb{C}[G][u]
-basis
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
for
F[u]
, and the bottom horizontal arrow is the one obtained by writing the matrix of a
\mathbb{C}[H][u]
-operator on
F[u]
using the ordered
\mathbb{C}[H][u]
-basis
(\sigma_{1}f_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}f_{1},\sigma_{1}f_{2},\ldots)
of
F[u]
. Putting these two commutative diagrams together gives the desired result.
∎

We are now in a position to formulate our main result of this section which is the equivariant way of thinking about the induction property of the Artin formalism. (Compare with the second statement of
[
31
, Proposition 1.8 on page 87]
.) Since we need to keep track of the group that is acting on our graph
Y
, we shall write
\gamma_{(Y,G)}(u)
instead of
\gamma_{Y}(u)
, and similarly for
\eta_{Y}(u)
, and
\theta_{Y}(u)
.

Theorem 4.12
.

One has

N_{G/H}(\gamma_{(Y,G)}(u))=\gamma_{(Y,H)}(u)\text{ and }N_{G/H}(\eta_{(Y,G)}(u))=\eta_{(Y,H)}(u),

and thus, the following equality
N_{G/H}(\theta_{(Y,G)}(u))=\theta_{(Y,H)}(u)
also holds true.

Proof.

The first identity follows from
Lemmas

4.9
and
4.10
, and the observation that

\displaystyle T_{G/H}\left(-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)\cdot{\rm log}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(1-u^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=T_{G/H}\left(-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)\cdot{\rm log}_{\mathbb{Q}}(1-u^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=T_{G/H}\left(-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)\right)\cdot{\rm log}_{\mathbb{Q}}(1-u^{2})

\displaystyle=-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[H]}(Y)\cdot{\rm log}_{\mathbb{C}[H]}(1-u^{2}).

The second identity follows from
Lemma

4.11
, and the third one from the multiplicativity property of
N_{G/H}
.
∎

5.
Artin representations

5.1.
The Euler characteristic

As usual, we let
G
be a finite abelian group acting on a finite graph
Y
without inversion. Here, we view an Artin representation
V
as a
(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C}[G])
-bimodule with the right multiplication given by
v\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda v
. Given an Artin representation
V
of
G
, then tensoring the chain complex (
3.1
) with
V
over
\mathbb{C}[G]
gives another chain complex

0\longrightarrow V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[G]}C_{1}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{V}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[G]}C_{0}(Y)\longrightarrow 0

of
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces, where
\partial_{V}={\rm id}_{V}\otimes\partial
. From now on, if
M
is a finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-module and
V
is an Artin representation, then we let

T_{V}(M)=V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[G]}M.

The map
M\mapsto T_{V}(M)
is a covariant functor from the category of finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules to the category of finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces. If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\rightarrow N
is a morphism of finitely generated
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules, then we write
f_{V}
instead of
T_{V}(f)={\rm id}_{V}\otimes f
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
Y
be a finite graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion, and let
V
be an Artin representation of
G
. One defines

\chi_{V}(Y)={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(T_{V}(C_{0}(Y)))-{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(T_{V}(C_{1}(Y))).

Clearly,
\chi_{V}(Y)
depends only on the isomorphism class of
V
, and therefore we will often write
\chi_{\psi}(Y)
instead of
\chi_{V}(Y)
if
\psi
is the character of
V
, since two Artin representations have the same character if and only if they are isomorphic. From now on, if
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, we write

r_{0}(\psi)=|\{v\in V_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|\text{ and }r_{1}(\psi)=|\{s\in O_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|,

where
O_{X}
is an orientation of
X=Y_{G}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
Y
be a finite graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion, and let
V
be an Artin representation of
G
. Let also
X=Y_{G}
. One has

\chi_{V}(Y)=\psi_{V}(\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)).

Moreover, if
V
is irreducible with character
\psi
, then

\chi_{\psi}(Y)=\chi(X)-r_{0}(\psi)+r_{1}(\psi),

so that in particular, if
G
acts freely on
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, then
\chi_{\psi}(Y)=\chi(X)-r_{0}(\psi)
.

Proof.

Since both
\chi_{V}
and
\psi_{V}
are additive in
V
, and since any Artin representation is a direct sum of irreducible ones, it suffices to show the claim for an irreducible Artin representation
V
. Assume therefore that
V
is irreducible, and let
\psi=\psi_{V}
be its character. On one hand, by
Definition

5.1
we have

\chi_{V}(Y)={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}C_{0}(Y))-{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}C_{1}(Y)).

Using
Proposition

2.11
, one has

\displaystyle{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}C_{1}(Y))

\displaystyle={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}_{Y})-{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}W)

\displaystyle=|\{s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|-|\{s\in O_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{s}\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}|

\displaystyle=|E_{X}|-r_{1}(\psi),

where
O_{X}
is an orientation of
X
as usual, and similarly

{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}C_{0}(Y))=|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi),

so that

\chi_{V}(Y)=|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi)-(|E_{X}|-r_{1}(\psi))=\chi(X)-r_{0}(\psi)+r_{1}(\psi).

On the other hand,
Theorem

4.2
gives

\psi(\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y))=|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi)-(|E_{X}|-r_{1}(\psi))=\chi(X)-r_{0}(\psi)+r_{1}(\psi)

which concludes the proof.
∎

5.2.
The function
\sigma_{V}

Throughout this section,
G
is a finite abelian group acting on a finite graph
Y
without inversion. We let

{\rm\sigma}_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket

be the function which is obtained from the composition

(5.1)

\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho_{V}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)\llbracket u\rrbracket\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\omega_{V}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V\llbracket u\rrbracket)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket,

where the first map
\rho_{V}
is the
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebra morphism induced by the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphism

\rho_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)

associated to the Artin representation
V
, and the second map
\omega_{V}
is the isomorphism of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras of
Theorem

2.3
. The function
\sigma_{V}
is not a
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebra morphism in general, but is only multiplicative meaning that

{\rm\sigma}_{V}(P\cdot Q)={\rm\sigma}_{V}(P)\cdot{\rm\sigma}_{V}(Q)

whenever
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket
. We will use the function
\sigma_{V}
in
Section

5.3
below to define Ihara
L
-functions associated to Artin representations.

Remark 5.3
.

An argument similar to the one used in
Remark

4.8
shows that

\sigma_{V}(\mathbb{C}[G][u])\subseteq\mathbb{C}[u].

In this section, we study some properties satisfied by
\sigma_{V}
that we will need later.

Proposition 5.4
.

If
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are two isomorphic Artin representations, then one has

{\rm\sigma}_{V_{1}}={\rm\sigma}_{V_{2}}.

Proof.

Since
V_{1}\simeq V_{2}
, there exists an isomorphism
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
of
\mathbb{C}[G]
-modules. The map
f
induces an isomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras

f_{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{1})\rightarrow{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{2})

given by
\alpha\mapsto f_{*}(\alpha)=f\circ\alpha\circ f^{-1}
. Therefore, we get a natural morphism of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras

{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{1})\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{2})\llbracket u\rrbracket

which we denote by the same symbol
f_{*}
. The map
f
also induces an isomorphism

\tilde{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{1}\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow V_{2}\llbracket u\rrbracket

of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-modules which, in turn, induces an isomorphism

\tilde{f}_{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V_{1}\llbracket u\rrbracket)\rightarrow{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V_{2}\llbracket u\rrbracket)

of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras given by
\beta\mapsto\tilde{f}_{*}(\beta)=\tilde{f}\circ\beta\circ\tilde{f}^{-1}
. The result then follows from the commutativity of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{1})\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V_{1}\llbracket u\rrbracket)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{2})\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V_{2}\llbracket u\rrbracket)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{V_{1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\omega_{V_{1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{f}_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{V_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\omega_{V_{2}}}

which we leave to the reader to check, and the observation that

{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}\circ\tilde{f}_{*}(\beta)={\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(\beta)

for all
\beta\in{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V_{1}\llbracket u\rrbracket)
.
∎

Although
{\rm\sigma}_{V}
is only multiplicative in general, it is in fact a morphism of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras when
V
is irreducible as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
V
is an irreducible Artin representation, then
{\sigma}_{V}
is a
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebra morphism and is simply the
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebra morphism
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
, obtained from the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra morphism
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
applied to the coefficients of a power series in
\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket
, where
\psi
is the character of
V
.

Proof.

Since
G
is finite abelian,
V
is necessarily one dimensional over
\mathbb{C}
. The claim then follows from the commutativity of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}(V\llbracket u\rrbracket)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\omega_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm det}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm id}_{\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}}

for which we now explain the vertical morphisms involved. The first one on the left is the identity, the second one is the isomorphism of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-algebras induced from the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra isomorphism
{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}
coming from (
2.5
), the third one is the isomorphism coming from (
2.5
) applied to the free
\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket
-module
V\llbracket u\rrbracket
of rank one, and the fourth one is the identity. We leave it to the reader to check that the three squares in the diagram above commute, from which the result follows.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two Artin representations of

and let

. Then

{\rm\sigma}_{V}={\rm\sigma}_{V_{1}}\cdot{\rm\sigma}_{V_{2}}.

This follows directly from (

) and the observation that

\omega_{V}(\rho_{V}(P))=\omega_{V_{1}}(\rho_{V_{1}}(P))\oplus\omega_{V_{2}}(\rho_{V_{2}}(P))

for all

.
∎

below).

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
H\leq G
,
V
an Artin representation of
H
, and let

. Then, the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}[H]\llbracket u\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma_{W}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N_{G/H}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma_{V}}

Proof.

By

5.6
, and since induction is additive, meaning

, it suffices to show the claim when

is an irreducible Artin representation of

. Let

be the character of such an irreducible Artin representation
V
of

e_{\psi}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\widehat{G}\\
\varphi|_{H}=\psi\end{subarray}}e_{\varphi}.

It follows that one has

\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket e_{\psi}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\widehat{G}\\
\varphi|_{H}=\psi\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}[G]\llbracket u\rrbracket e_{\varphi}.

Let

induces by restriction a
\mathbb{C}
-linear morphism

which by (
5.2
) above satisfies

m_{P}^{\psi}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\widehat{G}\\
\varphi|_{H}=\psi\end{subarray}}m_{P}^{\varphi},

where

\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\sigma_{V}\circ N_{G/H}(P)

is given

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\widehat{G}\\

The result then follows from

5.6
and
5.5
.

5.3.
The Ihara

The multiplicative function
{\rm\sigma}_{V}
from

allows us to define the Ihara

-function associated to an Artin representation
V
. As before,
Y
is a finite graph on which a finite abelian group acts without inversion.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
V

G
. Then, we define the Ihara

L_{V}(u)={\rm\sigma}_{V}\left(\theta_{Y}(u)\right)\in 1+u\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket\subseteq\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket^{\times}.

The first thing to notice is that the functions

.

Let

and

.

This follows from
Proposition

5.4
.
∎

Because of

combined with the fact that two Artin representations are isomorphic as

-modules if and only if they have the same character, we will often write

instead of

, and similarly for

and

.

Theorem 5.10
.

With the notation as above, one has

c_{V}(u)=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi_{V}(Y)}\in\mathbb{C}\llbracket u\rrbracket.

Proof.

Because of the additivity property of

in

,

2.5
and
5.6
, it suffices to show the claim for irreducible Artin representations. If

is an irreducible Artin representation with character

is a

-algebra morphism. Thus, using

5.5
,

\displaystyle c(u,\psi)

\displaystyle=\psi\left((1-u^{2})^{-\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y)}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-u^{2})^{-\psi(\chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(Y))}

\displaystyle=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi_{\psi}(Y)},

and this ends the proof.
∎

Similarly, we can give an explicit description of the polynomial

. In order to do so, we need the following proposition.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\rm End}_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(M)[u]}

Given an Artin representation

.

With the notation as above, one has

∎

that if

and

.

.

5.4.
The Artin formalism

The setup is the usual one:
Y
is a finite graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion. In this section, we study the Artin formalism for the Ihara
L
-functions. By the Artin formalism, we mean the usual three properties of additivity, induction and inflation satisfied by the classical Artin
L
-functions in algebraic number theory as listed for instance in
[
31
, page 15]
. We start with the additivity property.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two Artin representations of
G
, then one has

c_{V_{1}\oplus V_{2}}(u)=c_{V_{1}}(u)\cdot c_{V_{2}}(u)\text{ and }h_{V_{1}\oplus V_{2}}(u)=h_{V_{1}}(u)\cdot h_{V_{2}}(u),

so that we also have

L_{V_{1}\oplus V_{2}}(u)=L_{V_{1}}(u)\cdot L_{V_{2}}(u).

Proof.

This follows directly from
Proposition

5.6
.
∎

The induction property is contained in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.15
.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
,
V
an Artin representation of
H
, and let
{\rm Ind}(V)
denote the induced representation from
H
to
G
. Then

c_{{\rm Ind}(V)}(u)=c_{V}(u)\text{ and }h_{{\rm Ind}(V)}(u)=h_{V}(u),

so that we also have

L_{{\rm Ind}(V)}(u)=L_{V}(u).

Proof.

This follows from
Theorems

5.7
and
4.12
.
∎

We point out that the inflation property is not satisfied in general in the situation of branched covers of graphs. This is already apparent from
Example

4.7
. Indeed, one calculates

h_{X}(u)=(1-u^{2})^{2}\text{ and }c_{X}(u)=1,

but

h(u,\psi_{0})=1-4u^{2}+3u^{4}\text{ and }c(u,\psi_{0})=1,

as we calculated in
Example

5.13
above. If the inflation property were satisfied, then we would have
h_{X}(u)=h(u,\psi_{0})
which is not the case.

As a direct consequence of
Theorem

5.15
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 5.16
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group acting without inversion on a finite graph
Y
. Then, one has

c_{Y}(u)=\prod_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}c(u,\psi).

Proof.

Let
V_{0}
be the trivial representation of the trivial group, and consider
V={\rm Ind}(V_{0})
, the induced representation up to
G
. The representation
V
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{C}[G]=\bigoplus_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}\mathbb{C}[G]e_{\psi}.

It follows from
Theorems

5.14
and
5.15
that

c_{Y}(u)=\prod_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}c(u,\psi)

as desired.
∎

Remark 5.17
.

By definition, we have
c_{Y}(u)=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(Y)}
and if
\psi\in\widehat{G}
and
G
acts freely on
\mathbf{E}_{Y}
, then

c(u,\psi)=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(X)+r_{0}(\psi)},

because of
Theorems

5.10
and
5.2
, where
X=Y_{G}
. Therefore, the conclusion of
Corollary

5.16
is true if and only if

(5.4)

\chi(Y)=|G|\cdot\chi(X)-\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}r_{0}(\psi).

In view of
Lemma

5.18
below, noting that
|G|=[Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X]
when
Y
is connected, (
5.4
) is equivalent to the Riemann-Hurwitz formula of Baker and Norine (
Theorem

3.3
) in our situation.

Lemma 5.18
.

One has

\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}r_{0}(\psi)=\sum_{w\in V_{Y}}(m_{w}-1).

Proof.

Observe first that if
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, then
Proposition

2.11
implies

{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}\,(e_{\psi}C_{0}(Y))=|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi).

Since

C_{0}(Y)=\bigoplus_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}e_{\psi}C_{0}(Y),

we have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{0}(Y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(e_{\psi}C_{0}(Y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}(|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi))

\displaystyle=|G||V_{X}|-\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}r_{0}(\psi).

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\psi\in\widehat{G}}r_{0}(\psi)

\displaystyle=|G||V_{X}|-{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(C_{0}(Y))

\displaystyle=|G||V_{X}|-\sum_{v\in V_{X}}(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V_{X}}(|G|-(G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V_{X}}\sum_{w\in f^{-1}(v)}(m_{w}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V_{Y}}(m_{w}-1),

and this ends the proof.
∎

.

Let

satisfying
\alpha(\bar{s})=-\alpha(s)
for all

, and a family

of closed subgroups of

, we consider the graphs

as explained in

. We assume that the graphs

are connected for all

. These graphs come with natural branched covers

of degree

which together form a branched

-tower of finite connected graphs

(6.1)

X=X_{0}\leftarrow X_{1}\leftarrow X_{2}\leftarrow\ldots\leftarrow X_{n}\leftarrow\ldots

as explained in more details in

6.1.
The number of spanning trees in abelian branched covers

We use the same notation as in
Section

4.3
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
Y
be a finite connected graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion and freely on directed edges, and let
X=Y_{G}
be the quotient graph. Let
C=(c_{ij})
be the
g\times g
diagonal matrix satisfying
c_{ii}=|G_{i}|
, for all
i=1,\ldots,g
. If
D
and
A
denote the degree and adjacency matrices of
X
, respectively, then

h(u,\psi_{0})={\rm det}(I-ACu+(DC-I)u^{2}),

where
I
is the identity matrix. Moreover, one has
{\rm ord}_{u=1}(h(u,\psi_{0}))\geq 1
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.6
, we have

\displaystyle h(u,\psi_{0})

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}({\rm det}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}u+\mathbf{Q}u^{2}))

\displaystyle={\rm det}(I-\psi_{0}(\mathbf{A})u+\psi_{0}(\mathbf{Q})u^{2}).

For a given
i\in\{1,\dots,g\}
, let
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{r}\}
be a complete set of representatives of
G/G_{i}
. Using (
4.5
), one has

\psi_{0}(\ell_{i}(w_{j}))=\sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{\sigma_{k}w_{i}}(w_{j}).

Since the function
\mathbf{E}_{Y,w_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbf{E}_{X,v_{j}}
is
m_{j}
-to-
1
, where
m_{j}=m_{w_{j}}
, one has

\psi_{0}(\ell_{i}(w_{j}))=a_{ij}|G_{j}|,

where
a_{ij}
is the number of directed edges of
X
going from
v_{j}
to
v_{i}
. It follows that

(6.2)

\psi_{0}(\mathbf{A})=AC.

Moreover,

\psi_{0}\left(({\rm val}_{Y}(w_{i})-1)e_{i}\right)=|G_{i}|{\rm val}_{X}(v_{i})-1,

from which it follows that

(6.3)

\psi_{0}(\mathbf{Q})=DC-I.

Putting (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) together gives

(6.4)

h(u,\psi_{0})={\rm det}(I-ACu+(DC-I)u^{2}),

and this ends the proof of the first part of the proposition. For the second part, using (
6.4
), one has

h(1,\psi_{0})={\rm det}(DC-AC)={\rm det}(\mathcal{L}_{X})\cdot{\rm det}(C),

where
\mathcal{L}_{X}
is the laplacian operator for
X
. By (
3.3
), we have
h(1,\psi_{0})=0
which ends the proof.
∎

This last proposition allows us to determine the order of vanishing at
u=1
of the polynomials
h(u,\psi)
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
Y
be a finite connected graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion and freely on directed edges. If
\chi(Y)\neq 0
, then

{\rm ord}_{u=1}h(u,\psi)=0

for all non-trivial character
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, and
{\rm ord}_{u=1}h(u,\psi_{0})=1
.

Proof.

By (
3.3
) and
Theorem

3.5
, we have
{\rm ord}_{u=1}(h_{Y}(u))=1
. Since by
Proposition

6.1
,
{\rm ord}_{u=1}(h(u,\psi_{0}))\geq 1
, the result follows at once from (
5.5
).
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
Y
be a finite connected graph on which a finite abelian group
G
acts without inversion and freely on directed edges. If

, then

(6.5)

h_{Y}^{\prime}(1)=h^{\prime}(1,\psi_{0})\cdot\prod_{\psi\neq\psi_{0}}h(1,\psi).

Proof.

This follows directly from

6.2
after differentiating (
5.5
) and evaluating at

.
∎

Combining (

) with

-2\chi(Y)\kappa(Y)=h^{\prime}(1,\psi_{0})\cdot\prod_{\psi\neq\psi_{0}}h(1,\psi),

provided

6.2.
Ihara

Throughout this section, we let

a finite abelian group,
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow G

Proposition 6.4
.

With the notation as above, for each

, let

. Then, for each

, one has

\mathcal{A}(w_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\lambda_{ij}w_{i},

where

{\rm inc}(s)=(v_{j},v_{i})\end{subarray}}\alpha(s)N_{G_{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}[G]\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G].

Proof.

We calculate

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(w_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\mathbf{E}_{Y,w_{j}}}t(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbf{E}_{X,v_{j}}}\sum_{h\in G_{j}}t((s,h))

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbf{E}_{X,v_{j}}}\sum_{h\in G_{j}}(t(s),h\alpha(s)G_{t(s)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathbf{E}_{X,v_{j}}}\sum_{h\in G_{j}}h\alpha(s)(t(s),G_{t(s)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\lambda_{ij}w_{i},

as we wanted to show.
∎

Definition 6.5
.

With the same notation as above, we define two matrices
\mathbf{A}_{\alpha},\mathbf{C}\in M_{g}(\mathbb{Z}[G])
as follows. The matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}=(\mathbf{a}_{ij}(\alpha))
is defined via

\mathbf{a}_{ij}(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}\\
{\rm inc}(s)=(v_{j},v_{i})\end{subarray}}\alpha(s),

and the matrix
\mathbf{C}=(\mathbf{c}_{ij})
is the diagonal matrix defined via
\mathbf{c}_{ii}=N_{G_{i}}
. We also let
\mathbf{D}=(d_{ij})\in M_{g}(\mathbb{Z})
be the usual degree matrix of
X
, and
\mathbf{I}
the
g\times g
identity matrix in
M_{g}(\mathbb{Z}[G])
. At last, we define

\xi_{Y}(u)={\rm det}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}\mathbf{C}u+(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})u^{2})\in 1+u\mathbb{Z}[G][u].

Given

, similarly to the polynomial

, we let

Theorem 6.6
.

With the notation as above, for each
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, one has

(1-u^{2})^{r_{0}(\psi)}\cdot h(u,\psi)=z(u,\psi).

Moreover, given
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, if we let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\alpha},\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}
be obtained from the corresponding matrices
\mathbf{A}_{\alpha},\mathbf{C}
, and
\mathbf{D}
, respectively, by removing the rows and columns corresponding to the indices

such that

, then one has

h(u,\psi)={\rm det}(\widetilde{I}-\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\alpha})\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})u+(\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}})\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})-\widetilde{I})u^{2}),

where
\widetilde{I}
is the identity matrix with
|V_{X}|-r_{0}(\psi)
number of rows and columns.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\widehat{G}
, and let
j
be such that
G_{j}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)
.
Lemma

4.5
shows that applying
\psi
to the
j
th column of
\mathbf{A}
gives a column consisting of zeros only. The same is true if one applies
\psi
to the
j
th column of
\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}\mathbf{C}
. Applying
\psi
to the
j
th column of
\mathbf{Q}
also gives a column consisting of zeros only, but applying
\psi
to the

th column of

gives a column consisting of zeros except in the

th row where one has a

. It follows that the
j
th column of

in the

th row, whereas the

th column of
\psi(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}\mathbf{C}u+(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})u^{2})
consists of zeros except for a

in the

th row. The claim will then follows if we show that the

th row of both

are the same whenever

. But this follows from

2.11
,
4.4
and
6.4
.
∎

Note that

(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}\mathbf{C}u+(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})u^{2})^{t}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}^{t}u+(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{I})u^{2}

and thus

\displaystyle L(u,\psi)^{-1}

\displaystyle=c(u,\psi)\cdot h(u,\psi)

\displaystyle=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(X)+r_{0}(\psi)}\cdot h(u,\psi)

\displaystyle=(1-u^{2})^{-\chi(X)}z(u,\psi),

where

z(u,\psi)={\rm det}\left(I-\psi(\mathbf{C})\psi(\mathbf{A}_{\alpha}^{t})u+(\psi(\mathbf{C})\psi(\mathbf{D})-I)u^{2}\right),

and we invite the reader to compare the expression for

above with the one contained in
[

in the situation where

is abelian and the action is without inversion (so that there are no legs).

Example 6.7

6.3.
The analogue of Iwasawa’s asymptotic class number formula

We start by reminding the reader about our notation. Let
p
be a rational prime,
X
a finite connected graph, and
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
a function satisfying
\alpha(\bar{s})=-\alpha(s)
for all
s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}
. Let also
\mathcal{G}
be a collection of closed subgroups
G_{v}
of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
indexed by the vertices of
X
. For every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we let
\Gamma_{n}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}
, and we have a natural surjective group morphism
\pi_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow\Gamma_{n}
. Given
v\in V_{X}
, we let
\Gamma_{n,v}=\pi_{n}(G_{v})\leq\Gamma_{n}
, and we set

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{(v,\Gamma_{n,v})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}v\in V_{X}\}.

The composition
\alpha_{n}=\pi_{n}\circ\alpha
also satisfies
\alpha_{n}(s)=-\alpha_{n}(s)
for all
s\in\mathbf{E}_{X}
, and for each
n\geq 1
, we consider the finite graph
X_{n}=X(\Gamma_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n},\alpha_{n})
. As explained in
[
7
, §4.2]
, the graphs
X_{n}
comes with natural branched covers which together form a branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-towers

X=X_{0}\leftarrow X_{1}\leftarrow X_{2}\leftarrow\ldots\leftarrow X_{n}\leftarrow\ldots

of finite graphs. We will always assume that all graphs
X_{n}
are connected (see
[
7
, §4.5]
for a condition that guarantees the connectedness of the graphs
X_{n}
). As in
[
7
]
, we let

V^{unr}=\{v\in V_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v}=0\}\text{ and }V^{ram}=\{v\in V_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G_{v}\neq 0\}.

Moreover, given
v\in V^{ram}
, we let
k_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be such that
G_{v}=p^{k_{v}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and we let

n_{1}={\rm max}\{k_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}v\in V^{ram}\}.

Note that

\Gamma_{n,v}\simeq p^{{\rm min}(k_{v},n)}\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}

so that if
n\geq k_{v}
, then one has

\Gamma_{n,v}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-k_{v}}\mathbb{Z}.

It follows that if
n\geq n_{1}
, then there are
p^{k_{v}}
vertices of
X_{n}
above
v
with ramification indices
m_{v,n}=p^{n-k_{v}}
if
v\in V^{ram}
, whereas if
v\in V^{unr}
, then there are
p^{n}
vertices of
X_{n}
lying above
v
and
m_{v,n}=1
. Just as before, we introduce a labeling
V_{X}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{g}\}
, but we also assume that

V^{unr}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}\}\text{ and }V^{ram}=\{v_{t+1},\ldots,v_{g}\}.

We will often replace a vertex
v_{i}
appearing in any notation by the corresponding index only. For instance, we will write
k_{i}
instead of
k_{v_{i}}
if
i\in\{t+1,\ldots,g\}
.

Throughout this section, we also assume that there exists
n_{2}\geq 1
such that
\chi(X_{n_{2}})<0
. Note that
Theorem

3.3
implies that
\chi(X_{n})\neq 0
for all
n\geq n_{2}
. If
n\geq n_{2}
, then (
6.6
) gives

(6.7)

-2\chi(X_{n})\kappa(X_{n})=h^{\prime}(1,\psi_{0})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\\
\psi\neq\psi_{0}\end{subarray}}h(1,\psi).

In order to understand
{\rm ord}_{p}(\kappa(X_{n}))
, we have to understand the
p
-adic valuation of
\chi(X_{n})
,
h^{\prime}(1,\psi_{0})
, and
h(1,\psi)
when
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}
is non-trivial. Therefore, it is convenient to work with
\mathbb{C}_{p}
instead of
\mathbb{C}
. Everything in this paper could have been done with
\mathbb{C}_{p}
instead of
\mathbb{C}
, since
\mathbb{C}_{p}
is also an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. So from now on, we will see all of the numbers involved inside
\mathbb{C}_{p}
.

Proposition 6.8
.

With the notation as above, there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

{\rm ord}_{p}(\chi(X_{n}))=\begin{cases}n+{\rm ord}_{p}(\chi(X)),&\text{ if }V^{ram}=\varnothing\text{ and }\chi(X)\neq 0;\\
c,&\text{ if }V^{ram}\neq\varnothing,\end{cases}

when
n
is large enough.

Proof.

Theorem

3.3
implies that if
n\geq n_{1}
, one has

\displaystyle\chi(X_{n})

\displaystyle=p^{n}\chi(X)-\sum_{w\in V_{X_{n}}}(m_{w}-1)

\displaystyle=p^{n}\chi(X)-\sum_{v\in V^{ram}}p^{k_{v}}(p^{n-k_{v}}-1)

\displaystyle=p^{n}(\chi(X)-|V^{ram}|)+\sum_{v\in V^{ram}}p^{k_{v}}

from which the result follows.
∎

For the sake of clarity in this section, we will write
h_{X_{n}}(1,\psi)
instead of simply
h(1,\psi)
as we have done so far, where
\psi
is a character of
\Gamma_{n}
.

Proposition 6.9
.

With the notation as above, there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\nu_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

{\rm ord}_{p}(h_{X_{n}}^{\prime}(1,\psi_{0}))=\lambda_{0}n+\nu_{0}

when
n
is large enough.

Proof.

We let
C_{n}=(c_{ij}^{(n)})
be the diagonal matrix from
Proposition

6.1
. If
n\geq n_{1}
, then one has

c_{ii}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}p^{n-k_{i}},&\text{ if }i=t+1,\ldots,g;\\
1,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proposition

6.1
implies the equality

h_{X_{n}}(u,\psi_{0})={\rm det}(I-AC_{n}u+(DC_{n}-I)u^{2}),

where
A
and
D
are the adjacency and the degree matrices of
X
, respectively. It follows that there exists

P(x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{g},u)\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{g},u]

such that

h_{X_{n}}(u,\psi_{0})=P(p^{n-k_{t+1}},\ldots,p^{n-k_{g}},u),

provided

. Therefore, there exists
Q(T)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[T]
such that

provided

. The result then follows from
Lemma

2.12
.
∎

From now on, whenever
0\leq j\leq n
we let

\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}=\{\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm ord}(\psi)=p^{j}\}.

Note that all
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}
for a fixed

have the same kernel, and moreover one has
|\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}|=\phi(p^{j})
, where
\phi
here is the Euler
\phi
-function.

Proposition 6.10
.

With the notation as above, if
0<j\leq n_{1}
, then there exists
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\nu_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

{\rm ord}_{p}\left(\prod_{\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}}h_{X_{n}}(1,\psi)\right)=\lambda_{j}n+\nu_{j}

when
n
is large enough.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}
with
0<j\leq n_{1}\leq n
, and let

V_{\psi,n}=\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma_{n,v}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}.

Note that the set
V_{\psi,n}
does not depend on
n
when
n\geq n_{1}
. Therefore we will simply write
V_{\psi}
for this set instead of
V_{\psi,n}
provided
n\geq n_{1}
. Consider the matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\alpha_{n}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{n}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}
from
Theorem

6.6
so that we have

h_{X_{n}}(u,\psi)={\rm det}(\widetilde{I}-\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\alpha_{n}})\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{n})u+(\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}})\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{n})-\widetilde{I})u^{2}),

from which it follows that

h_{X_{n}}(1,\psi)={\rm det}(\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}-\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\alpha_{n}})){\rm det}(\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{n})).

Observe that for
n\geq n_{1}
, the quantity

is independent of
n
. On the other hand, the matrix
\psi(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{n})
is a diagonal matrix whose elements on the diagonal are either
1
’s or of the form

, when

. Therefore, there exists
Q(T)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[T]
such that

h_{X_{n}}(1,\psi)=Q(p^{n}),

provided

. The result then follows from
Lemma

2.12
.
∎

Proposition 6.11
.

With the notation as above, there exists a power series
g(T)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
such that

for all
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}
whenever

. Therefore, if we let

n
is large enough.

If
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}
with

, then

for all

. Similarly as in the previous proof, consider the matrices

, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}
from

. One has

2.13
.
∎

6.4.
Revisiting the main conjecture

In this subsection we link the Iwasawa main conjecture proved in
[
7
, §5.2]
to the Ihara
L
-functions attached to each layer of a branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower. More precisely, we calculate the characteristic ideal of
{\rm Pic}^{0}_{\Lambda}
, which we are going to recall now, in terms of the power series
g(T)
that was defined in the previous subsection. Recall that
\Lambda
denotes the usual Iwasawa algebra of the profinite group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Definition 6.14
.

Let
X=(V_{X},\mathbf{E}_{X})
be a finite connected graph, and let
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a voltage assignment. Consider a family
\mathcal{G}
of closed subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
indexed by the vertices of
V_{X}
and consider the branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower of graphs

X=X_{0}\leftarrow X_{1}\leftarrow X_{2}\leftarrow\ldots\leftarrow X_{n}\leftarrow\ldots,

where
X_{n}=X(\Gamma_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n},\alpha_{n})
, and where we assume each
X_{n}
to be connected. Let
{\rm Pic}^{0}(X_{n})
be the Picard group of degree zero of
X_{n}
for each
n
(see
[
7
, §2.2]
for the precise definition). Then, we define

\mathrm{Pic}^{0}_{\Lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varprojlim_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Pic}^{0}(X_{n})[p^{\infty}],

where the projective limit is taken with respect to the natural projection maps (see
[
7
, §4.3]
).

It follows from
[
7
, Theorem 5.6]
that
\mathrm{Pic}^{0}_{\Lambda}
is a finitely generated torsion
\Lambda
-module, and thus one can talk about its characteristic ideal. For the following result, we use the usual non-canonical isomorphism
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket
.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
X=(V_{X},\mathbf{E}_{X})
be a finite connected graph, and let
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be a voltage assignment. Consider a family
\mathcal{G}
of closed subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
indexed by the vertices of
V_{X}
and consider the branched
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-tower of graphs

X=X_{0}\leftarrow X_{1}\leftarrow X_{2}\leftarrow\ldots\leftarrow X_{n}\leftarrow\ldots,

where
X_{n}=X(\Gamma_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n},\alpha_{n})
. Assume that all finite graphs
X_{n}
are connected. With the notation as above, one has

\mathrm{char}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket}(\mathrm{Pic}^{0}_{\Lambda})\cdot(T)=(g(T))\cdot\prod_{v\in V^{ram}}((1+T)^{p^{k_{v}}}-1)

Proof.

By
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]
, we have

\mathrm{char}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}\llbracket T\rrbracket}(\mathrm{Pic}_{\Lambda}^{0})\cdot(T)=(f_{X,\mathcal{I},\alpha}(T)),

where
f_{X,\mathcal{I},\alpha}(T)
is as defined in
[
7
, (6.2)]
. (In
[
7
]
, we used
\mathcal{I}
instead of
\mathcal{G}
to denote the collection of closed subgroups.) Now, observe that by the definitions of
f_{X,\mathcal{I},\alpha}(T)
and
g(T)
, we have

f_{X,\mathcal{I},\alpha}(T)=g(T)\cdot\prod_{v\in V^{ram}}((1+T)^{p^{k_{v}}}-1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Notice that
Remark

6.13
and
[
7
, Theorem 5.6]
give us two different ways to calculate the
\mu
and
\lambda
invariants appearing in
Theorem

6.12
. Comparing the
\mu
invariants, we have
\mu(\mathrm{Pic}_{\Lambda}^{0})=\mu_{unr}
. On the other hand, by
Theorem

6.15
, we have

(6.9)

\displaystyle\lambda({\rm Pic}^{0}_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=-1+\lambda_{unr}+\sum_{v\in V^{ram}}p^{k_{v}}

\displaystyle=-1+\lambda_{unr}+\sum_{j=0}^{n_{1}}p^{j}\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}=j\}|

Now, notice that for a character
\psi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{n}^{(j)}
with
0<j\leq n_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle V_{j}=V_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma_{n,v}\not\subseteq{\rm ker}(\psi)\}

\displaystyle=\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}<j\}.

Moreover, by the proof of
Proposition

6.10
,
{\rm ord}_{p}(h_{X_{n}}(1,\psi))=|V^{ram}\setminus V_{\psi}|\cdot n+C
for
n
large so that
\lambda_{j}=|V^{ram}\setminus V_{\psi}|
. Hence, by
Proposition

6.10
we have

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\phi(p^{j})\cdot|V^{ram}\setminus V_{j}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\phi(p^{j})\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}\geq j\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\phi(p^{k})\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}=j\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}(p^{j}-1)\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}=j\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{1}}(p^{j}-1)\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}=j\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{1}}p^{j}\cdot|\{v\in V^{ram}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k_{v}=j\}|-|V^{ram}|.

Now,
Remark

6.13
together with (
6.9
) and (
6.10
) give us the following corollary.

Corollary 6.16
.

Let
\lambda_{0}
be as defined in

. Then, we have

\lambda_{0}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }V^{ram}=\varnothing\text{ and }\chi(X)\neq 0;\\
|V^{ram}|-1,&\text{ if }V^{ram}\neq\varnothing.\end{cases}
