Treatable traits-based management of bronchiectasis: moving from theory to clinical practice
Authors: Guiyin Zhu, James D. Chalmers, Jin-Fu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bronchiectasis is a chronic airway disease characterized by irreversible bronchial dilatation and substantial clinical heterogeneity. Accumulating evidence from large international observational cohorts has highlighted wide variation in the features of this disease and unmet clinical needs. The treatable traits-based approach has emerged as a means to prioritize identifiable and clinically actionable problems over traditional diagnostic labels. In this review, we summarize the reported prevalence of major treatable traits across large cohorts and describe notable regional differences, alongside the ongoing absence of data relating to behavioral and environmental traits. We further update the treatable traits framework based on recent guideline recommendations, clarifying the strength of evidence supporting the interventions available. We also review selected molecular mechanisms relevant to key treatable traits. Despite growing interest, the use of treatable traits in routine practice is restricted by ambiguous terminology, insufficient prospective data, and inadequate evidence for many therapies. However, the fundamental constraint for implementation is not insufficient characteristic identification, but rather the attempt to manage a complicated and non-linear disease system with a static checklist. Future research should emphasize standardized definitions and real-world longitudinal studies, and develop practical tools to help clinicians determine which traits should be addressed first. Such efforts may support transparent, individualized, and adaptive decision-making in bronchiectasis.

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is characterized by irreversible airway dilatation on imaging, with a clinical diagnosis requiring the coexistence of compatible respiratory symptoms. Bronchiectasis is not a homogeneous disease entity; rather, patients differ significantly in their etiological, clinical, radiological, functional, and microbiological characteristics.1,2 Bronchiectasis, once regarded as an orphan disease, has gained significant attention over the past decade. This increase in interest is attributed to the growing prevalence of this disease and robust data from large international registries.3 Despite this, targeted therapies remain inadequately defined. Current management relies heavily on repurposed treatments from other airway diseases, often with limited evidence. A significant challenge in clinical practice and drug development is the heterogeneity of this condition.4,5 Consequently, conventional “one-size-fits-all” approaches fail to address the clinical complexity of patients who often exhibit overlapping endotypes, phenotypes, and exposures.

This limitation is not unique to bronchiectasis. More broadly, chronic airway diseases have traditionally been managed using a stepwise treatment approach, in which pharmacological treatments are added or withdrawn according to disease risk, severity and responsiveness to treatment.6 This approach contributed to reductions in hospital admissions and mortality during the 1990s and early 2000s. However, subsequent improvements at the population level were limited.7,8 In response, the concept of “treatable traits” was proposed in 2016,9 marking a paradigm shift from broad diagnostic labels to specific and manageable features. While this strategy was originally proposed for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, it is now increasingly recognized across the entire spectrum of airway pathology, encompassing both acute and chronic respiratory disorders.10 Despite general acceptance as a concept, the practical application of the treatable traits method in everyday bronchiectasis therapy continues to pose significant challenges.9 Crucially, we argue that the implementation bottleneck is triggered by attempting to control a dynamic and non-linear illness system with a linear and static checklist. In this review, we adapt the treatable traits framework originally proposed by Agusti et al,9 and integrate this with the 2025 European Respiratory Society (ERS) bronchiectasis guidelines.11 Our aim is to analyze challenges associated with the implementation of treatable traits in clinical practice and to propose strategies to address these barriers.

The concept of treatable traits

Precision medicine aims to tailor treatment to each individual to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing adverse effects. Within this framework, a treatable trait is a therapeutic target identified through phenotypic or endotypic recognition using a validated trait identification marker.9,12,13 Given its emphasis on identifying specific and modifiable patient characteristics and linking them to targeted interventions, the treatable traits strategy directly translates the principles of precision medicine into clinical practice and is therefore increasingly recognized as a practical framework for individualized care in respiratory medicine.14,15 This framework is particularly relevant to airway diseases because of their heterogeneity and complexity. To apply it effectively, it is essential to differentiate “heterogeneity” from “complexity”.12,16 Heterogeneity refers to inter-individual variability as not all pathological features are observed in every patient. Complexity refers to a system characterized by non-linear and dynamic interactions, in which biological, environmental, and psychosocial factors exert unpredictable influences on each other. Bronchiectasis exemplifies such a complex and heterogeneous disorder. Management based on the diagnostic label alone may therefore overlook clinically important and modifiable disease components.

To qualify as a treatable trait, a candidate characteristic should meet three criteria as follows.6,13 First, the trait should be clinically relevant, significant, and linked to specific disease consequences. The trait should also be indicative of future risks such as exacerbations, reduction in lung function, and mortality. Second, the trait must be identifiable through reliable measurement tools, which may include objective biomarkers (e.g., blood eosinophil count for type 2 inflammation) or validated patient-reported outcome measures (e.g., disease-specific questionnaires assessing symptom burden and quality of life). The selected measurement tools should be validated for the intended trait and clinical context; when diagnostic biomarkers are used, adequate specificity is required to support confident trait identification. Third, the trait must be treatable and targetable with a viable form of treatment. Those that are currently not fully treatable may be considered as potential targetable traits.17 Endotyping may help elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms of these candidate traits and prioritize targets for therapeutic development.

Eosinophilic airway inflammation represents a well-recognized example of a treatable trait. A prospective cohort study on bronchiectasis previously revealed that patients with elevated blood eosinophil levels (≥300 cells/μL) experienced a significantly shorter time to exacerbation than those with lower levels (<100 cells/μL; hazard ratio [HR] = 3.99; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.20–7.85; P < 0.0001). This endotype can be easily identified through blood eosinophil counts and is manageable with inhaled corticosteroids or anti-interleukin-5 (IL-5)/IL-5 receptor monoclonal antibodies. Eosinophilic airway inflammation meets the three criteria for classification as a treatable trait, as this condition is clinically significant, identifiable, and treatable.18

The “vicious vortex” and treatable traits

The pathophysiology of bronchiectasis has historically been explained by the “vicious cycle” hypothesis. This model suggests that an initial insult impairs mucociliary clearance, leading to the retention of secretions and subsequent chronic bacterial colonization. This chronic infection results in sustained inflammation that causes damage to the airway wall and disrupts mucociliary function, resulting in a feedback loop involving recurrent infection and progressive structural injury.19,20 Yet in practice, treatments targeting only one element often yield limited clinical benefit. In this regard, the “vicious vortex” model has been proposed. Four pathophysiological mechanisms are pivotal to this vortex: chronic infection, persistent inflammation, impaired mucociliary clearance, and structural lung damage. These ingredients are universally present and interact dynamically, forming a self-reinforcing mechanism that propels disease progression and persistence.4,11 Consequently, clinical management emphasizes addressing these four interrelated processes.

The “treatable traits” approach offers a framework by focusing on specific and modifiable elements within the vortex (Fig. 1). For example, airway infection is but one of several potentially coexisting treatable traits; other relevant traits may also contribute to symptoms or exacerbations.21 A multimodal strategy that identifies and addresses these coexisting traits in parallel is therefore advocated. Among extrapulmonary traits, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) was reported more frequently in USBRR than in the Asian registries included in Table 1. This difference may partly reflect variation in ascertainment and registry case mix, including the high proportion of patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease in USBRR. However, the available registry data do not establish the reasons for this variation. The prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in Europe is 32.5%, markedly higher than in Asian registries, indicating that the management of cardiovascular risk may be a critical area for addressing treatable traits in European populations. Data on behavioral and lifestyle traits were sparse or unavailable, preventing reliable estimates of their prevalence and highlighting an important gap in current bronchiectasis registries.

Although bronchiectasis research lacks a large-scale, real-world study that focuses on treatable traits, the NOVELTY study provides a relevant comparator. The NOVELTY study was a large, 3-year, real-world, prospective and observational study across 19 countries, enrolling 11,226 patients diagnosed with asthma (n = 5932, 52.8%), COPD (n = 3898, 34.7%), or asthma + COPD (n = 1396, 12.4%). A total of 30 treatable traits were assessed. This study confirmed that treatable traits were prevalent across chronic respiratory diseases, with three key findings. First, of the 30 treatable traits, 12 (40.0%) were linked to disease labels and classified as diagnostic treatable traits. Second, 20 treatable traits (66.6%) were significantly associated with severe disease. Third, the mean number of treatable traits per patient was 5.1 ± 2.7, indicating that treatable traits are rarely present in isolation.30

Treatable traits in bronchiectasis: from biological mechanisms to clinical practice

The concept of treatable traits has been integrated into the guidelines for asthma and COPD. Not surprisingly, the 2017 ERS bronchiectasis guidelines did not address treatable traits, given that the concept was first introduced in the respiratory field in 2016. The updated 2025 guidelines introduced this framework for the first time.11 This shift reflects the growing recognition of treatable traits in the management of bronchiectasis. This review updates the table of treatable traits previously proposed by Chalmers et al,31 integrating recommendations from the 2025 ERS bronchiectasis guidelines alongside relevant guidance for cystic fibrosis (CF),32 allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA),33 and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD).34 These revisions enhance the strength of recommendations, clarify the certainty of evidence for treatments, and outline future directions for research, with the aim of providing a comprehensive overview of the current status of treatable traits in the clinical management of bronchiectasis (Table 2). Overall, the certainty of evidence for most treatable traits is low, and only a few therapies have shown consistent efficacy in well-conducted randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Infection

Airway infection sits at the core of the vicious vortex in bronchiectasis. From a treatable-traits perspective, however, the presence of a microorganism in respiratory secretions does not always equate to clinically relevant infection. A microbiological finding becomes clinically actionable when the organism’s identity and persistence over time are tied to the patient’s current symptoms, future exacerbation risk, or disease progression, and when that information in turn leads to a change in management. Culture-based studies have identified Haemophilus influenzae and P. aeruginosa as prominent pathogens in bronchiectasis infection, together with Enterobacterales, Staphylococcus aureus, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.35

Among bacterial pathogens, P. aeruginosa warrants particular attention because it has the strongest organism-specific evidence on prognosis and therapy. Retrospective studies from China demonstrate that P. aeruginosa is commonly isolated, with its presence linked to elevated all-cause mortality (HR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.32–7.15) and an increased exacerbation burden (HR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.20–4.79).36 Similarly, a prospective study from South India found that patients infected with P. aeruginosa had more exacerbations, more frequent and prolonged hospitalizations, and poorer lung function than those infected with other bacteria.37 Taken together, these findings identify P. aeruginosa infection as an important prognostic factor and patients with this infection as a high-risk group.

Once chronic infection is established, P. aeruginosa is sustained more often by persistence and adaptation of a patient-specific strain than by repeated acquisition of new strains. Longitudinal genotyping of 203 isolates from 39 patients showed that a single P. aeruginosa strain persisted in 67% of patients over a median follow-up of 3.2 years, whereas acquisition of a new strain was not associated with exacerbations.38 Consistent with the finding that each patient harbors a largely patient-specific P. aeruginosa population, whole-genome sequencing of 2854 isolates from 180 patients showed that >99.9% of genetic diversity occurred between patients, indicating substantial heterogeneity among infecting strains. However, despite this genetic diversity, recurrent signatures of selection were identified in pathways related to motility, iron acquisition and the PA1874 efflux system.39 Such signatures indicate that genetically distinct strains undergo convergent functional adaptation. These findings suggest that chronic P. aeruginosa infection arises from patient-specific strains that undergo convergent functional adaptation within the bronchiectatic airway, rather than from a single shared clone.

One well-described manifestation of this adaptive process in P. aeruginosa is the transition toward a mucoid, biofilm-forming phenotype. Loss-of-function mutations in mucA release AlgU from negative regulation, increasing alginate production and promoting biofilm formation. The resulting extracellular matrix may impair phagocytic clearance and antibiotic penetration while generating metabolically heterogeneous bacterial populations with reduced antimicrobial susceptibility. In a study of 66 bronchiectasis isolates, mucA variants were frequent and mucoid isolates formed more biofilm than non-mucoid isolates. Given the role of alginate as a major component of the biofilm matrix, experimental disruption of algD and restoration of the iron-uptake regulator pirR were used to examine its contribution to antimicrobial susceptibility. These manipulations modified cefiderocol susceptibility, suggesting that alginate production and iron-dependent drug uptake pathways may interact.40 This result is mechanistically informative but should not be extrapolated to antibiotic resistance in general. Clinical observations support the relevance of this adaptive phenotype. In a small cross-sectional study, mucoid P. aeruginosa was associated with increased sputum viscoelasticity, greater radiological involvement and previous hospitalization.41 However, because the study did not compare mucoid and non-mucoid P. aeruginosa infections directly, the specific contribution of mucoid conversion to disease severity remains uncertain.

At the host–pathogen interface, P. aeruginosa flagellin is recognized by airway epithelial Toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5). Studies using primary human airway epithelial cells demonstrated that flagellin activates myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)–interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK)–TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6)-dependent NF-κB signaling, whereas flagellin-deficient bacteria induce minimal activation. This pathway provides a mechanistic link between bacterial recognition and epithelial inflammatory activation, including the production of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines.42 However, these experiments were performed using laboratory strains and epithelial models not derived from patients with bronchiectasis. During chronic airway adaptation, P. aeruginosa may downregulate flagellin expression or lose motility, thereby attenuating TLR5-mediated signaling without necessarily reducing overall airway inflammation. TLR5 signaling should therefore be regarded as one potential contributor to inflammatory activation rather than a universal feature or biomarker of chronic P. aeruginosa infection. In stable bronchiectasis, P. aeruginosa detection and higher bacterial load correlated with higher active sputum neutrophil elastase and lower microbial diversity.43 Sputum neutrophil elastase activity, in turn, could predict future exacerbations, severe exacerbations, and lung function decline.44 These observations support a feed-forward model in which persistent bacterial exposure drives neutrophilic inflammation, while protease-mediated epithelial injury and impaired mucociliary clearance reshape the airway environment to further favor bacterial persistence. The model links bacterial adaptation to clinical progression, though the direction and relative contribution of each component remain uncertain from observational data alone.

The mechanisms described above explain why the timing of treatment matters. When P. aeruginosa is first detected in respiratory cultures, eradication may be attempted before persistent infection becomes established. When serial cultures indicate chronic infection, complete clearance is less likely and treatment is directed toward reducing exacerbations and symptoms. Long-term suppressive antibiotics may reduce exacerbations in selected high-risk patients, but treatment burden, adverse effects, and antimicrobial resistance must be weighed against benefit. They are therefore considered only when chronic infection is accompanied by recurrent exacerbations or substantial daily symptoms.

The 2025 ERS guideline conditionally suggests eradication treatment after a first isolation or re-isolation following a prolonged period without detection; the certainty of evidence is very low.11 A multicenter, 2 × 2 factorial, randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled trial is now in progress to evaluate the efficacy of tobramycin inhalation solution, ciprofloxacin, and their combination for the eradication of newly isolated P. aeruginosa; results are anticipated to provide new evidence to inform eradication strategies in bronchiectasis.45

Chronic infection requires a different objective: suppression rather than eradication. The 2025 ERS guideline strongly recommends long-term inhaled antibiotics for patients with chronic P. aeruginosa infection who remain at high risk of exacerbations despite optimized care. Trials demonstrated both biological activity and heterogeneous clinical benefit. A phase 3, randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled trial assessed nebulized tobramycin inhalation solution (TIS) in adults with bronchiectasis. Compared with placebo, TIS resulted in a significantly greater reduction in P. aeruginosa density (adjusted mean difference = 1.74 log10 (CFU/g), 95% CI: 1.12–2.35, P < 0.001) and a greater improvement in the Quality-of-Life Bronchiectasis Respiratory Symptom Scale (adjusted mean difference = 7.91, 95% CI: 5.72–10.11, P < 0.001) at day 29, with similar findings observed at day 85.46 The effect of long-term inhaled antibiotic treatment on exacerbations has been less consistent. Two randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled trials using twice-daily inhalation of colistimethate sodium in patients with P. aeruginosa infection and at least two exacerbations provided additional evidence. In the PROMIS-I trial, 377 patients were included, and the annual exacerbation rate was lower with colistimethate sodium than with placebo (0.58 vs. 0.95; risk ratio (RR) = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.46–0.82; P = 0.001). However, these findings were not repeated in the PROMIS-II trial, which revealed no statistically significant differences between treatment groups in terms of exacerbation frequency or key secondary outcomes. The PROMIS-II trial was halted prematurely due to recruiting difficulties during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as concerns over randomization following the promising results of the PROMIS-I trial.47 Although the PROMIS-II trial yielded a neutral outcome, the overall data were indicative of efficacy. Cordeiro et al48 conducted a meta-analysis that included the PROMIS-I trial and discovered that the colistin subgroup was linked to a significant reduction in exacerbations (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.46–0.82), which was directly in line with findings from the PROMIS-I trial. While a contradiction between the two studies is acknowledged, available data from the positive trial and supportive meta-analysis suggest that the inhalation of colistimethate sodium may be a beneficial medication for reducing exacerbations in this specific patient population.

The P. aeruginosa–neutrophil pathway is not the only infection-related inflammatory mechanism in bronchiectasis. Aspergillus-associated type 2 inflammation represents a parallel pathway in which clinical significance is determined by the host immune response as well as fungal exposure. In bronchiectasis, structural airway damage and ciliary dysfunction can impair mucociliary clearance. This pre-existing clearance defect may allow A. fumigatus conidia and antigens to persist within airway mucus, prolonging epithelial exposure to fungal components. Experimental studies suggest that sustained exposure to A. fumigatus-derived allergens and proteases stimulates airway epithelial cells to release IL-25, IL-33 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin, which activate group 2 innate lymphoid cells and antigen-specific Th2 cells. IL-4 and IL-13 promote B-cell class switching to Aspergillus-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE), mast-cell activation and mucus hypersecretion, whereas IL-5 promotes eosinophil recruitment and survival.49,50 This mechanism is best established in ABPA; in bronchiectasis without ABPA, Aspergillus sensitization (AS) or isolated Aspergillus-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) identifies distinct patient subgroups that are increasingly recognized as clinically significant, being independently associated with worse outcomes including higher exacerbation rates and lung function decline.

The clinical significance of Aspergillus-related immune responses depends on the specific antibody pattern and the accompanying clinical features. Findings of the EMBARC study indicated a clinical spectrum comprising ABPA (6.1%), AS (5.7%), and elevated Aspergillus-specific IgG (8.1%).51 ABPA and AS are associated with IgE-mediated type 2 immune responses, whereas isolated Aspergillus-specific IgG may reflect fungal exposure, colonization, or infection and should not be considered equivalent to ABPA.52 Patients with ABPA, AS, or raised Aspergillus-specific IgG without sensitization had more severe disease at baseline.

These distinctions have direct therapeutic implications. Baseline assessment should integrate total IgE, Aspergillus-specific IgE and IgG, blood eosinophils and imaging rather than rely on a single positive result.33 Confirmed acute ABPA is treatable, with oral prednisolone or itraconazole recommended as initial monotherapy. By contrast, AS alone is not an indication for antifungal treatment, and inhaled corticosteroids should be prescribed only for an established indication such as coexisting asthma or COPD. Raised Aspergillus-specific IgG is associated with a greater exacerbation burden, but serology alone cannot establish active fungal disease or justify antifungal therapy.51 Prospective studies are required to determine whether this phenotype identifies a subgroup that benefits from antifungal treatment.

Together, these examples show that an infection-related trait becomes actionable only when microbiological findings are linked to a defined biological process and clinical consequence. For P. aeruginosa, serial microbiology distinguishes a new isolation, for which eradication may be attempted, from chronic infection, for which exacerbation and symptom burden determine the need for long-term suppression. For Aspergillus, treatment depends on evidence of an allergic or disease-specific host response, because fungal detection or seropositivity alone does not establish clinically active disease. Treatment decisions should therefore integrate microbiological or serological findings with the associated clinical phenotype, rather than rely on an isolated positive result.

Inflammation

Neutrophilic inflammation

Neutrophilic inflammation provides the main link between infection and airway injury. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), leukotriene B4 (LTB4) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) released following epithelial and endothelial activation promote neutrophil recruitment.53 However, reducing airway neutrophil numbers is not sufficient evidence of therapeutic benefit. In a 28-day phase IIa trial, the C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) antagonist AZD5069 reduced absolute sputum neutrophil counts by 69% vs. placebo, but infections and exacerbations occurred with similar frequency and no improvement in clinical outcomes was observed.54 The inflammatory abnormality also involves neutrophil function. Blood neutrophils from patients with bronchiectasis showed delayed apoptosis, greater CD62L shedding, increased CD11b expression and enhanced myeloperoxidase release, yet impaired P. aeruginosa phagocytosis and killing.55 Bronchiectasis is therefore characterized not simply by neutrophil excess, but by the coexistence of exaggerated activation and ineffective antibacterial defense.

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and neutrophil serine proteases (NSPs) provide two downstream links between this dysfunctional response and airway injury. NETs consist of extracellular chromatin decorated with histones and granule-derived proteins, including neutrophil elastase (NE), proteinase 3 (PR3) and cathepsin G (CatG).55,56 When NSP activity exceeds local antiprotease protection from secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor and α1-antitrypsin, these enzymes degrade extracellular matrix and epithelial junctional proteins, activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and promote goblet-cell metaplasia and mucin production. The resulting epithelial injury and mucus stasis further impair bacterial clearance, reinforcing the infection–inflammation cycle.57 In international multicohort analyses, sputum NET components were associated with the Bronchiectasis Severity Index, impaired quality of life, future hospital admission and mortality. Among 20 patients treated with intravenous antibiotics for exacerbations, clinical response was associated with a reduction in sputum NETs.58 Similarly, sputum NE activity was independently associated with future exacerbations, severe exacerbations and lung function decline.44 NE also slows ciliary beating; excessive neutrophilic inflammation may therefore sustain bacterial persistence while damaging mucociliary clearance. NET and NE measurements may serve as candidate biomarkers, but prospective studies are required to determine whether they can guide treatment selection.

Other pathways may sustain neutrophilic inflammation but remain preclinical or exploratory. A recent study found that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) provided NF-κB-dependent priming of epithelial NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3), whereas NET material acted as a secondary activation signal. Combined LPS and NET exposure increased epithelial Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC) and reduced zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and α-tubulin. MCC950 and Z-VAD-FMK inhibited inflammasome readouts, whereas GSK484 and LDC7559 reduced NET formation, supporting an NLRP3–NET loop in vitro.59 A separate translational study identified a gut–lung metabolic pathway. Eggerthella lenta (E. lenta) and its associated metabolite tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) were enriched in patients with bronchiectasis and were associated with disease severity; mechanistic experiments linked TUDCA to disruption of liver kinase B1 (LKB1)–AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling, reduced neutrophil ATP production and impaired P. aeruginosa clearance, whereas causal challenge and metformin rescue were undertaken in murine models.60 This work identifies a plausible microbiota–metabolite–neutrophil axis, but neither E. lenta, TUDCA nor AMPK activity is a validated biomarker or treatment target in clinical bronchiectasis.

The dipeptidyl peptidase-1 (DPP-1)–NSP pathway is the clearest example of successful therapeutic translation. DPP-1 activates NSPs, including NE, PR3, and CatG, during neutrophil maturation in the bone marrow. Inhibition of DPP-1 reduces the functional NSPs stored within the granules of mature neutrophils. Rather than blocking neutrophil recruitment or neutralizing proteases already present in diseased airways, this mechanism acts upstream during neutrophil maturation.61 In a phase 3, double-blind study, a once-daily dose of brensocatib reduced the annualized rate of pulmonary exacerbations relative to placebo in patients with bronchiectasis.62 Brensocatib subsequently received regulatory approval in the USA and the European Union in 2025.63 HSK31858, a novel and reversible inhibitor of DPP-1, was evaluated at once-daily doses of 20 mg and 40 mg in the phase 2 SAVE-BE trial. Both doses reduced the annualized exacerbation rate compared with placebo in patients with bronchiectasis. The overall incidence of adverse events was similar across groups, and neither HSK31858 dose was linked to an increased risk of adverse events of special interest, including hyperkeratosis, gingivitis, and life-threatening infections.64 Taken together, these trials validate DPP-1 inhibition as a therapeutic mechanism, but individual agents differ in development and regulatory status. Brensocatib should be used according to its approved indication, whereas HSK31858 remains investigational. DPP-1 inhibition is not, however, a biomarker-selected treatment. The pivotal trials enrolled patients principally according to previous exacerbation history, and clinical benefit was not restricted to those with high baseline sputum NE. No NE, NET or other molecular threshold has been validated to identify responders. Treatment selection should therefore follow the approved clinical phenotype, with regular monitoring of dermatological and periodontal adverse effects, rather than depending on sputum neutrophil counts or protease measurements.

Eosinophilic inflammation

Eosinophilic inflammation defines a smaller but clinically relevant subgroup. The canonical type 2 cascade, involving epithelial alarmins, type 2 innate lymphoid cells, T-helper 2 cells, IL-5-driven eosinophilia and IL-13-induced goblet-cell metaplasia, provides a plausible biological rationale, although its role in bronchiectasis has yet to be firmly established. In a multicohort study of adults with high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT)-confirmed bronchiectasis and a compatible clinical history, 215 of 951 patients (22.6%) without coexisting asthma or active ABPA had blood eosinophil counts ≥300 cells/µL.18

However, type 2 and neutrophilic inflammatory signals may coexist. Four inflammatory endotypes have been described: milder neutrophilic inflammation, neutrophilic mixed with type 2 inflammation, most severe neutrophilic inflammation, and type 2 mixed with epithelial inflammation. The mixed neutrophilic–type 2 inflammation endotype was associated with an increased risk of future exacerbations.56 Because blood eosinophil counts provide only an indirect measure of airway eosinophilic inflammation, sputum-based measurements may offer complementary information on the local eosinophilic component.18 A separate study measured five eosinophil-associated proteins in sputum from 180 patients: eosinophil peroxidase, eosinophil-derived neurotoxin, eosinophil cationic protein, major basic protein and galectin-10. Total eosinophil protein concentration correlated moderately with blood eosinophil counts (r = 0.33). Galectin-10, eosinophil cationic protein and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin showed weak associations with radiological severity, FEV1 and P. aeruginosa culture positivity, whereas none of the airway eosinophil protein concentrations was associated with exacerbation frequency.65 Direct measurement of eosinophilic airway inflammation therefore provides additional information beyond blood eosinophil counts, although its clinical utility remains to be established.

Current guidelines conditionally recommend against ICS in bronchiectasis without asthma or COPD because randomized evidence shows no overall benefit in unselected patients and certainty is low.11,66 Nevertheless, ICS remain widely prescribed in practice. In a prospective observational analysis of the EMBARC registry, 10,109 of 19,324 patients with bronchiectasis were receiving ICS at enrolment. Even after patients with physician-recorded asthma, COPD, or ABPA were excluded, 3174 of 9715 patients were receiving ICS at enrollment. Users of ICS exhibited more severe disease at baseline, with poorer lung function, elevated Bronchiectasis Severity Index (BSI) scores, and a higher frequency of exacerbations (P <0.0001). In follow-up assessments, ICS did not reduce the overall risk of exacerbation or hospitalization. However, patients with elevated blood eosinophil counts showed a significant reduction in the frequency of exacerbations (RR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.59–0.84; P <0.001).67 Because this was an observational, prevalent-user analysis, confounding by indication and treatment-selection bias cannot be excluded. Randomized controlled trials are therefore needed to assess the effects of ICS in patients with eosinophilic bronchiectasis.

An endotype‑based strategy for airway inflammation is increasingly preferred over a uniform approach in bronchiectasis. Neutrophilic and eosinophilic subtypes call for different therapeutic considerations: NSP inhibition for the former, and corticosteroid therapy for a subset of the latter, yet this requires validation. Biomarker use should be coupled with microbiology, exacerbation history, and comorbidities. Unresolved questions include endotype stability, optimal thresholds, and infection‑related changes—all of which need prospective stratification trials to resolve.

Abnormal mucus

Abnormal mucus is a clinically important but heterogeneous treatable trait in bronchiectasis. It encompasses excessive secretion, mucus hyperconcentration, altered composition and impaired ciliary or cough-mediated clearance. These abnormalities cannot be fully characterized by sputum volume alone.

In the BLESS cohort, sputum solid percentage and total mucin concentrations were approximately three-fold higher in patients with bronchiectasis than in healthy controls, whereas extracellular DNA was increased approximately 20-fold (P < 0.001). The increases in percentage of solids and total mucin concentration were reproduced in the University of North Carolina cohort. Mucin 5B (MUC5B) and MUC5AC concentrations, osmotic pressure and viscoelasticity were also increased.

Sputum solid percentage correlated weakly with forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) (r = −0.22, 95% CI: −0.43 to −0.01, P = 0.03) and positively with radiological disease extent (r = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.19–0.63; P < 0.001).68,69 Daily sputum volume was not significantly associated with FEV₁ but was positively associated with radiological disease extent, whereas total mucin concentration was associated with neither measure. Thus, sputum volume, mucus concentration, and mucin concentration reflect different aspects of mucus pathology and should not be treated as interchangeable measures. These cross-sectional data support mucus hyperconcentration as a marker and plausible mediator of mucostasis, but do not establish causality or disease progression.

Once mucus concentration rises, its osmotic pressure can compress the periciliary brush, restrict ciliary excursion, and increase adhesion to airway surfaces. The resulting failure of mucociliary clearance causes mucus stasis and accumulation, which may ultimately lead to the formation of adherent mucus plaques and airway-obstructing plugs. Mucus plugging may then generate a self-reinforcing epithelial response. In resected lungs from several muco-obstructive diseases, including bronchiectasis, epithelium beneath mucus plugs expressed markers of chronic hypoxia. In primary airway epithelial cultures, chronic hypoxia increased MUC5B production and, through hypoxia‑inducible factor (HIF)-1α/HIF-2α, upregulated the β- and γ-subunits of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), increasing Na⁺ and fluid absorption.70 This provides a plausible route from plugging to further mucus hyperconcentration, but its contribution to disease progression in vivo has not been quantified.

Inflammation provides a second amplification loop. In resected bronchiectasis lungs, proximal bronchioles upregulated both MUC5AC and MUC5B, whereas distal bronchioles showed selective MUC5B upregulation. Bronchiectasis sputum supernatants induced MUC5B, MUC5AC, X‑box binding protein 1 spliced (XBP1S) and anterior gradient protein 2 (AGR2) expression in airway epithelial cultures, responses that were attenuated by clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)‑mediated interleukin‑1 receptor type 1 (IL‑1R1) deletion.71 Consistently, high sputum IL-1β was associated with inflammasome activation, neutrophilic inflammation, increased mucus solids and viscoelasticity, and greater disease severity. Prolonged IL-1β exposure reduced ciliary beat angle and amplitude, cilia-driven flow and epithelial barrier integrity without altering beat frequency.72 Together, these data position IL-1β as a candidate link between inflammation, mucin production and secondary ciliary dysfunction. However, the clinical analyses were observational, the in vitro exposures do not establish causation in patients, and neither a predictive IL-1β threshold nor benefit from IL-1 blockade has been demonstrated.

Clinical evidence for current treatment is derived primarily from studies of airway-clearance and mucoactive therapies rather than from interventions targeting these molecular pathways. The 2025 ERS guideline recommends that patients with bronchiectasis should be taught personalized airway-clearance techniques, although the certainty of evidence is very low. Mucoactive treatment is conditionally suggested only when airway clearance fails to control symptoms and should be discontinued if no clinical benefit is evident after a reasonable trial period.11 The CLEAR trial found that neither 6% hypertonic saline nor carbocisteine significantly reduced exacerbations over 52 weeks in 288 adults; secondary outcomes were also similar between groups.73 Mucoactive therapy should therefore be considered a time-limited, symptom-focused trial rather than a routine strategy for preventing exacerbations.

The marked increase in extracellular DNA in bronchiectasis sputum might seem to suggest a straightforward therapeutic target. Yet in a 24-week randomized trial of 349 adults, recombinant human DNase unexpectedly increased exacerbations and produced a greater decline in FEV1 than placebo.74 This trial result illustrates why compositional abnormalities cannot be translated directly into therapy.74 Mucus composition alone should therefore not determine treatment, and therapeutic effects observed in other muco-obstructive diseases cannot be assumed to apply to bronchiectasis.

PCD provides an etiology-specific example of the distinction between addressing retained secretions and correcting the initiating defect. The CLEAN-PCD phase 2 crossover trial found that combining idrevloride with hypertonic saline (4.2%) resulted in a 1.5 percentage point increase in percent predicted FEV1 (95% CI: <0.1 to 3.0; P = 0.044) and a 1.4 percentage point increase in percent predicted forced vital capacity (95% CI: 0.2–2.5; P = 0.026) compared with hypertonic saline alone.75 This short-term signal is consistent with benefit from enhancing airway hydration despite persistence of the primary ciliary defect; however, it is specific to PCD, based on 28 days of treatment, and should not be extrapolated to bronchiectasis generally or to long-term clinical outcomes. By contrast, inhaled dynein axonemal intermediate chain 1 (DNAI1) mRNA is intended to correct a defined genetic cause of ciliary dysfunction. Although aerosolized DNAI1 mRNA restored ciliary activity in vitro and delivered translated protein to relevant airway cell types in non-human primates,76 first-in-human phase 1 studies found no detectable change in the average number of outer dynein arms per ciliary cross-section, ciliary movement, or DNAI1 immunofluorescence in post-treatment bronchial samples.77 Thus, current evidence in PCD supports targeting downstream mucus retention, whereas correction of the initiating ciliary defect remains experimental.

Respiratory comorbidity

Patients with bronchiectasis frequently have multiple comorbidities. An international multicentre study derived the Bronchiectasis Aetiology Comorbidity Index (BACI), a comorbidity-based score for predicting mortality, from 986 patients with bronchiectasis across four European centers. The median number of comorbidities per patient was 4 (Q1–Q3: 2–6; range: 0–20) and each one-point increase in the BACI was associated with an increased risk of mortality with an increased risk of mortality (HR: 1.18; 95% CI: 1.14–1.23).78 Respiratory comorbidities are frequently identified in patients with bronchiectasis,79 and contribute to the prognosis of the disease. The relationship between comorbidities and mortality has been further validated in additional populations.80,81

While not formally classified as a treatable trait, COPD is a common comorbidity in bronchiectasis, with significant phenotypic overlap with established pulmonary traits such as airflow limitation, chronic airway inflammation, and an increased risk of exacerbation. To retain conceptual rigor, COPD is viewed as a disease condition that influences the identification, prioritization, and management of coexisting treatable traits. In a retrospective, longitudinal and observational study based on large U.S. real-world data (RWD), in which all-cause mortality was the primary endpoint, coexisting COPD was associated with a further increase in mortality risk in bronchiectasis patients (HR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.40–1.44).82 In bronchiectasis–COPD overlap syndrome (BCOS), a post-hoc study of 201 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) II–IV COPD patients with long-term follow-up revealed a comparable pattern. Patients with eosinophilia (>300 cells/µL) who received inhaled corticosteroids experienced fewer exacerbations (P < 0.001) and fewer serious events (P = 0.011).83 Evidence suggests that asthma–bronchiectasis overlap (ABO) has a similar tendency to worsen outcomes.84 In a retrospective cohort study, researchers phenotyped patients with ABO using a two-step unsupervised clustering approach in an independent cohort (n = 76). When compared to asthma or bronchiectasis alone, ABO was linked with a higher disease severity and worse clinical outcomes.85 EMBARC registry data further elucidated this observation by identifying a specific bronchiectasis–asthma phenotype. This phenotype was linked to an enhanced risk of exacerbations after adjusting for disease severity. However, overall disease severity was lower, and the use of inhaled corticosteroids correlated with reduced mortality and hospitalization risk.27

Respiratory comorbidities modify the expression and management of treatable traits. Once a comorbid disease is diagnosed according to its specific criteria, treatment can be directed at components such as eosinophilic inflammation, reversible or fixed airflow limitation, and exacerbation susceptibility. This approach avoids treating overlapping diagnoses as a single entity and more accurately reflects the biological heterogeneity across these patients.

Non-respiratory comorbidity

Cardiovascular disease is a clinically relevant extra-pulmonary trait in bronchiectasis. In a population-based study, patients with bronchiectasis had higher adjusted odds of pre-existing coronary heart disease than the general population (odds ratio [OR]: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.25–1.41).86 A retrospective cohort study, including 3521 patients with bronchiectasis and 14,084 matched controls, yielded similar results. The prevalence of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) was greater in the bronchiectasis cohort than in the individuals without bronchiectasis (13.49 vs. 9.07 per 1000 person-years).87 In a separate Chinese cohort, coexisting cardiovascular disease was independently associated with exacerbations during 1 year of follow-up (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.30–4.82).88 These data support routine assessment and guideline-based management of conventional cardiovascular risk factors and established cardiovascular disease, particularly in patients with severe or frequently exacerbating bronchiectasis. However, evidence that a bronchiectasis-specific cardiovascular intervention improves respiratory outcomes is not yet available.

Alongside cardiovascular problems, psychological morbidity represents another significant non-respiratory treatable trait that contributes to negative clinical outcomes. A prospective cohort study identified depression as an independent risk factor for disease progression, correlating with a significantly increased risk of subsequent exacerbations (RR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.32–2.41; P < 0.001) and a reduced time to the first exacerbation event (HR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.61–2.80; P < 0.001).89 Data from the multicenter BronchUK cohort (n = 1341) further substantiated this burden, reporting a high prevalence of clinically significant anxiety and depression.90 These relationships are likely bidirectional: greater disease burden may worsen psychological health, whereas depression and anxiety may reduce physical activity, self-management and treatment adherence. Patients should therefore be screened for symptoms of anxiety and depression using a validated instrument, with clinical assessment and appropriate psychological or psychiatric referral after a positive screen.

Primary antibody deficiency (PAD) is an important treatable trait and a recognized underlying cause of bronchiectasis. Antibodies in the airway lining fluid are essential components of mucosal defense against respiratory pathogens. Quantitative or functional antibody deficiency predisposes patients to recurrent or persistent respiratory infections, thereby promoting airway inflammation, structural damage, and the development or progression of bronchiectasis. Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is a well-recognized form of PAD associated with bronchiectasis, and patients with CVID have shown evidence of airway inflammation, including elevated exhaled nitric oxide levels and increased inflammatory cell counts in sputum.91,92 Importantly, clinically relevant humoral immune dysfunction may be present even when total serum immunoglobulin concentrations are not markedly reduced. Previous research, including 56 individuals with bronchiectasis, reported that only three patients had low total serum IgG, whereas 29 exhibited diminished specific antibody responses to Haemophilus influenzae and Streptococcus pneumoniae.93 The identification of these immune traits has direct therapeutic implications. Patients with specific antibody deficiency may respond to targeted vaccination strategies, whereas those with more extensive deficiency may benefit from immunoglobulin replacement therapy, which has been shown to improve inflammatory markers and reduce pulmonary morbidity.

These systemic traits differ in their implications. Cardiovascular and psychological comorbidities influence prognosis and care delivery. PAD may contribute to infection and worsening disease. Their relative priority is guided by the nature of their connection to bronchiectasis and by the strength of evidence for intervention.

Behavior/lifestyle

Ambient exposures and behavioral traits are associated with mortality and clinical severity in bronchiectasis. Short-term interquartile-range increases in PM2.5, PM2.5–10, and ozone were associated with increases in bronchiectasis mortality of 3.18%, 4.14%, and 4.36%, respectively, at lag 0–2 days.94 An analysis of 4390 adults with bronchiectasis in the USBRR included 1005 patients with a current or former smoking history of >10 pack-years, of whom only 40 were current smokers. Among patients without airflow obstruction, those with a smoking history were diagnosed with bronchiectasis at an average age approximately 7 years older than never-smokers; however, exacerbation and hospitalization rates did not differ significantly between the two groups. This finding indicates a later age at diagnosis rather than a directly measured diagnostic delay, although the attribution of respiratory symptoms to smoking or COPD may contribute to delayed recognition of bronchiectasis. Among patients with bronchiectasis and a smoking history, coexisting airflow obstruction was associated with more frequent exacerbations (P = 0.002) and recurrent hospitalizations (P = 0.003) than preserved airflow status.95

Environmental and behavioral factors modify disease expression rather than cause bronchiectasis directly. Avoiding tobacco smoke and reducing harmful exposures are clinically sensible measures. Whether they improve bronchiectasis-specific outcomes, however, is less clear from the current evidence.

Implementation barriers for treatable traits in clinical practice

A number of practical obstacles persist in limiting the implementation of treatable traits in bronchiectasis. The obstacles to implementing the treatable traits framework in bronchiectasis clinical practice likely stem not from inadequate trait identification, but from the effort to manage a complex disease system defined by non-linear and dynamic interactions through a linear and static “trait checklist”. Bronchiectasis should be understood as a complex adaptive system in which individual characteristics create dynamic networks through multi-level interactions rather than existing in isolation. In this environment, trait complexity is evident not only in the co-occurrence of many qualities but also in their interactions and system-level emergence. Traits may mutually impact one another and create self-reinforcing cycles. Chronic infection induces neutrophilic inflammation, which subsequently causes airway injury and the hypersecretion of mucus, thus enhancing microbial survival.96 This fundamental loop is complicated further by the dynamic interaction of inflammatory endotypes with microbiota in the airway. The negative association between eosinophilic and neutrophilic inflammation, combined with bi-directional interactions between inflammatory status and infection, is known to complicate clinical presentation and exacerbation risk.18 Extrapulmonary comorbidities provide an additional layer of non-linear amplification to this system. The overlap of bronchiectasis and COPD serves as a synergistic amplifier, increasing bacterial infection, airway inflammation, and systemic inflammation, all of which lead to far worse clinical outcomes.97 Because attributes function within such complex networks, standalone treatments are frequently inadequate. Clinically, targeted airway clearance alone may improve symptoms and the quality of life associated with mucus abnormalities. Nevertheless, symptoms and exacerbations frequently remain despite the provision of adequate treatment.98 This emphasizes the importance of a multi-dimensional and personalized model of treatable characteristics that can provide more clinical benefit than a single-component strategy.

Comorbidities such as asthma and GERD are usually thought to be contributing factors to bronchiectasis; however, both of these diseases are common in the general population, therefore their causal role remains unclear.2 Furthermore, definitions differ greatly between studies. There is no universally accepted definition of “colonization” or of results such as “eradication” even for a condition as common as bacterial infection. Randomized studies have either omitted these definitions or employed criteria that vary significantly from one study to the next. Without uniform definitions and outcome measures, the assessment of data and the use of results in clinical practice become challenging.5 Evidence connecting certain traits to the disease primarily relies on retrospective analyses, and suggested interventions usually lack supportive evidence from well-designed prospective studies. This complicates the assessment of how these traits should guide clinical decision-making.

Eosinophilic inflammation exemplifies a potentially treatable trait in bronchiectasis that can illustrate these challenges. Eosinophilic inflammation is associated with a higher risk of exacerbations at the population level.99 However, the presence of mixed inflammatory patterns in many patients complicates the assessment of their clinical significance on an individual basis. Moreover, widely utilized biomarkers, including blood eosinophil counts, serve as inadequate surrogates for airway eosinophilia, and not all patients exhibiting a biomarker actually possess the characteristic. Therapeutic strategies targeted to this characteristic, such as inhaled corticosteroids or biologics, are primarily derived from asthma and COPD, with insufficient evidence supporting their efficacy in bronchiectasis.66,100 Similarly, mucus volume and viscosity are associated with worse outcomes and can be readily assessed in clinical practice. The CLEAR randomized trial revealed that neither hypertonic saline nor carbocisteine significantly reduced the incidence of pulmonary exacerbations over a 52-week period when compared to standard care. Secondary outcomes, such as health-related quality of life, and safety, were comparable across the groups.73 These findings may reflect the limitation of targeting a single trait without addressing the broader network of interacting drivers of disease. Overall, robust prospective evidence is still required to support the clinical efficacy of treatable trait-based approaches in bronchiectasis.

Future directions

An organized and multi-faceted research roadmap is essential if we are to develop the treatable traits paradigm in bronchiectasis; this roadmap should encompass trait prioritization, clinical application, and early disease intervention.

Refinement and hierarchical prioritization of treatable traits

The existing catalog of treatable traits in bronchiectasis is comprehensive and it remains important to identify those with the strongest influence on clinical outcomes. Developing prediction models for acute exacerbations based on these traits will require large real-world cohorts, similar to the NOVELTY study,30 with subsequent validation by RCTs. A significant challenge is the prioritization of treatable traits within complex clinical contexts. Some traits exert wider downstream effects and may be more clinically relevant.101 This concept is illustrated by evidence from obese patients with COPD: targeting obesity led to improvements not only in body weight but also in exercise capacity, muscle strength, mood, and cardiometabolic risk markers.102 This supports a shift toward the identification of highly inter-connected and high-impact traits in bronchiectasis that may offer amplified clinical benefit when targeted. Advanced analytical methods will be required as the number of traits increases and their interactions become more complex. Machine learning and artificial intelligence may assist in integrating multi-dimensional data, identifying new traits or biomarkers, and predicting disease progression and treatment response.10

Clinical implementation: pragmatic models across healthcare systems

Although the best manner to adopt treatable traits is still unknown, the roles of primary and secondary care are becoming more defined. Primary care providers consistently provide person-centered and holistic treatment, although time constraints frequently drive practice toward guideline-driven medication rather than the extensive assessment of behavioral or other extrapulmonary features. Focusing on a small number of key characteristics is therefore more realistic in this situation. Secondary care allows for more extensive and systematic assessments, which are supported by professional services, multi-disciplinary teams, and trait-specific examinations. Clear delineation of responsibilities across levels of care is essential to advance implementation of the treatable traits approach. Personalized and multi-disciplinary approaches will also be required, as patient demands and trait expression evolve over time.103, 104, 105 Furthermore, regional variations in patient attributes and reactions to targeted therapies may be crucial in prioritizing treatable traits.

Extending the paradigm to early and pre-clinical disease

The notion of pre-COPD highlights the importance of detecting the disease in its first, pre-clinical phase. Dharmage et al106 proposed an expansion of the treatable traits paradigm to include pre-disease conditions. Protracted bacterial bronchitis (PBB) is recognized as a potential precursor to chronic suppurative lung disease and may represent an early stage in a clinical spectrum that can progress to bronchiectasis in some children. Importantly, the underlying pathobiology and treatable traits throughout this range are dynamic and constantly changing. Aberrant innate immune responses, such as severe neutrophilic airway inflammation caused by elevated IL-1β, IL-8, and matrix metalloproteinase-9, as well as compromised alveolar macrophage phagocytic responses to apoptotic cells, drive dominant traits during the pre-disease or PBB stage.107,108 This clinically presents as recurrent endobronchial infections, primarily caused by Haemophilus influenzae, along with concomitant characteristics such as airway malacia or asthma.109 As the disease advances to established bronchiectasis, the mechanistic landscape transitions to the development of intricate bacterial community biofilms, significant microbiota dysbiosis, and persistent colonization by highly resistant pathogens.

Intercepting the disease at the PBB or early mild bronchiectasis stage offers a crucial therapeutic opportunity to modify the long-term clinical course. Prompt and specific antibiotic treatment can interrupt Cole’s “vicious circle” of persistent bacterial infection and inflammation, thus averting irreversible structural damage. Significantly, it is possible to reverse early cylindrical bronchiectasis in children with suitable therapy.110 To definitively demonstrate whether early intervention affects long-term outcomes, certain study approaches are required. Future research should focus on prospective and longitudinal cohort studies that follow newly diagnosed children with PBB from infancy to adulthood in order to define the natural history of disease and identify individuals at high risk for bronchiectasis. Simultaneously, multi-center RCTs are required to determine whether early treatment strategies, such as extending the duration of oral antibiotics or using long-term macrolides and inhaled antibiotics for recurrent pediatric bronchiectasis, effectively reduce exacerbations while maintaining long-term structural integrity. This conceptual shift broadens the treatable traits strategy beyond managing present morbidity to proactively preventing impending sickness.

Consolidating the evidence base for translation

Although the body of evidence for many therapies is inadequate, experience with other diseases of the airway indicates a treatable-traits strategy can provide considerable clinical benefits when numerous targeted interventions are combined. Progress in this field will require a shift from reliance on broad diagnostic labels toward clinical decision-making guided by identifiable and actionable traits. Conclusion

The treatable traits approach provides a useful lens for addressing the heterogeneity of bronchiectasis, although its application in practice is limited by variable definitions, insufficient prospective evidence, and uncertainty with regard to trait prioritization. Greater emphasis on standardization, longitudinal data, and clinically applicable tools will be needed to support its use in routine care. To fully harness the clinical potential of this paradigm, we need to move beyond the identification of isolated treatable traits and focus instead on the networks of interactions between traits. Only by adopting this systems‑based view can we deliver truly individualized care.
