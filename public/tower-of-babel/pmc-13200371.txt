Spatiotemporal controlled release of YCM/Cu2⁺ hydrogel microneedle system synergistically accelerates chronic wound healing in diabetes
Authors: Meng Zhang, Qiming Pang, Qiongfang Wang, Zejian Zhong, Qiaotian Yin, Meipei Gao, Xiu Zhang, Sungel Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The healing of diabetic wounds is critically impaired by a hostile microenvironment featuring barrier disruption, microvascular dysfunction, and immune dysregulation, which perpetuates infection and stalls tissue regeneration. Current clinical strategies lack the capacity for coordinated multiphasic intervention. To address this, we designed and fabricated a spatiotemporally orchestrated partitioned microneedle patch. The needle segment is fabricated from methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) hydrogels loaded with copper ions (Cu2⁺), while the backing matrix consists of aldehyde and methacrylate dual-modified hyaluronic acid (AHAMA) hydrogels encapsulating the young 3D spheroid mesenchymal stem cell-derived conditioned medium (YCM). Upon this patch application, the quick-dissolving tips release Cu2⁺ to exert immediate, potent antibacterial activity and initiate pro-angiogenic signaling. Subsequently, the sustained release of a multifaceted repertoire of bioactive factors from the YCM orchestrates key regenerative processes, including macrophage polarization to an anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative (M2) phenotype, restoration of mitochondrial function, and mitigation of oxidative stress, thereby resolving chronic inflammation. Collectively, in vitro and in vivo validation demonstrates that this integrated platform effectively eliminates infections, stimulates neovascularization, resolves pathological inflammation and oxidative damage, and facilitates functional extracellular matrix remodeling. Our work establishes a potent and translatable acellular strategy that synergistically combines antimicrobial action with active immunomodulation to disrupt the vicious cycle of non-healing wounds, offering a promising paradigm for the treatment of refractory diabetic wounds and advancing the frontier of regenerative medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04371-9.

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds represent one of the most severe complications of diabetes, imposing a substantial clinical burden on the aging population. Age-related decline in intrinsic healing capacity and impaired tissue regeneration markedly increase the risk of wound chronicity, making it the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputations [1, 2]. Pathophysiologically, local barrier disruption, microcirculatory disturbances, and diabetes-associated immunosuppression collectively create a complex and hostile wound microenvironment [3]. This milieu not only facilitates bacterial colonization and biofilm formation but also triggers systemic immune over activation via persistent antigen stimulation. Such regulatory imbalance leads to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within immune cells such as macrophages, thereby inducing mitochondrial dysfunction characterized by membrane potential collapse and impaired ATP synthesis, which ultimately triggers a cellular energy crisis [4, 5]. More critically, tissue hypoxia and sustained inflammation form a vicious cycle that exacerbates oxidative stress and ultimately disrupts the normal wound healing cascade. Current standard therapies, including repeated debridement, systemic antibiotics, and passive wound dressings, focus primarily on passive defense rather than active modulation, resulting in limited efficacy in infection control and failure to achieve the multiple therapeutic goals required for tissue regeneration [6]. Therefore, the development of advanced functional materials capable of actively remodeling the wound microenvironment to effectively promote tissue regeneration represents an urgent challenge in the field of regenerative medicine.

Against this background, hydrogel materials offer new opportunities for cutaneous wound therapy owing to their unique physicochemical properties [7, 8]. Nevertheless, constraints arising from their macroscopic structure lead to insufficient skin barrier penetration, low transdermal delivery efficiency of active ingredients, and poor adaptability to irregular wound surfaces. The integration of microneedle array technology provides an effective strategy to overcome these limitations [4, 9]. These micron-scale structures can painlessly penetrate the stratum corneum and establish direct drug delivery pathways to the dermis [10, 11]. Combined with controlled release mediated by matrix degradation, this approach significantly enhances drug bioavailability [12–14]. The establishment of such a high-efficiency delivery platform shifts the research focus from merely optimizing delivery efficiency toward the rational design of therapeutic payloads and the modulation of biological functions, laying a foundation for the construction of multifunctional wound treatment systems.

Despite the remarkable advantages of microneedle technology, existing bilayer or programmed microneedle systems still face critical obstacles. First, many systems rely on synthetic polymers or chemical crosslinking agents, whose incompletely degraded products may induce local inflammation and aggravate deterioration of the wound microenvironment. Second, inherent limitations exist in therapeutic loading strategies: high drug loading required for rapid initial bactericidal action often compromises the mechanical strength of microneedles, while cascade release designed for prolonged therapy involves complex multi-material systems, making it difficult to independently regulate the release kinetics of different agents.

To address the therapeutic loading limitations of the aforementioned microneedle systems, the deepening of tissue engineering concepts has provided new insights: research strategies are shifting from the direct use of living cells toward the application of their secreted bioactive components, particularly the development and application of engineered secretomes [15–18]. Unlike living cell therapies such as mesenchymal stem cell-based approaches, which suffer from challenges including immune rejection, low survival rates, and storage constraints, secretome-functionalized hydrogels retain the regenerative benefits of cell therapy while effectively circumventing these obstacles [19, 20]. By preserving natural components rich in growth factors, cytokines, and structural proteins, this strategy constructs a highly biomimetic microenvironment that provides critical biological support for cell proliferation, differentiation, and functional tissue regeneration [21]. However, the lack of inherent antibacterial activity in secretome-functionalized hydrogels limits their application in infected wounds. As a multifunctional component, Cu2⁺ exerts antibacterial effects through a multi-target mechanism: they initially disrupt bacterial membrane integrity via electrostatic interactions, causing lipid peroxidation and intracellular leakage [22, 23]. Once internalized, Cu2⁺ catalyzes Fenton-like reactions to generate highly reactive radicals that induce oxidative damage to proteins and DNA, and competitively displaces essential metal cofactors in metabolic enzymes, thereby impairing energy metabolism and protein synthesis [24]. This multi-target strategy confers potent antibacterial activity while reducing the risk of drug resistance. Beyond direct antibacterial effects, Cu2⁺ also promotes angiogenesis and modulates macrophage polarization, actively remodeling the wound microenvironment to support regeneration, making it a versatile component in advanced wound repair systems [25–28].

In this context, we developed a novel acellular microneedle patch. The patch employs methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) to form the needle tips. This natural polymer exhibits low immunogenicity and its degradation products are non-inflammatory, thus avoiding the biocompatibility risks commonly associated with synthetic materials at the source. The needle tips are loaded with high concentrations of Cu2⁺. The mechanical strength of HAMA guarantees the piercing performance of the microneedles, and its rapid degradation enables the rapid release of Cu2⁺ at the early stage of wound healing, ensuring sufficient drug loading for bactericidal efficacy without sacrificing microneedle integrity, a common problem in high-load designs. The base is embedded with AHAMA hydrogel loaded with YCM. AHAMA exhibits a relatively slow degradation rate, and its release is independently regulated by matrix degradation, forming a sequential complementarity with the rapid release from the needle tips: after rapid Cu2⁺ release controls infection, sustained YCM release guides macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, enhances mitochondrial function, reduces ROS accumulation, thereby optimizing the inflammatory microenvironment and promoting vascularization and matrix synthesis (Scheme 1). Results and discussion

Preparation and immunomodulatory properties of spheroid MSC conditioned medium

Figure 1A illustrates the standardized method we developed to isolate and identify the YCM and aged (ACM) spheroid MSCs, aiming to explore age-dependent functional differences in MSCs secretomes. Aged MSCs showed significantly higher protein expression of senescence markers than young MSCs, confirming the senescent phenotype of the cells at the molecular level (Fig. S1). Bone marrow-derived MSCs were induced to form spheroid aggregates and cultured under serum-free conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 48 h. Following centrifugation at 3000 × g for 5 min, cell debris was removed to obtain cell-free conditioned media. Live-dead staining confirmed the viability of the spheroid MSCs (Fig. 1B), and Western blot analysis verified the presence of various functional proteins in the conditioned media (Fig. 1C). Cytokine quantification analyses revealed significant age-related differences: the levels of regenerative factors IL-10, VEGF, IGF-1, HGF, and TSG-6 were significantly higher in YCM compared to ACM, whereas the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 was markedly lower (Fig. 1D, E). Further experimentation demonstrated that YCM effectively modulates macrophage polarization. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant reduction in M1 macrophages and an increase in M2 macrophages in the YCM-treated group compared to both the control and ACM groups (Fig. 1F, G). Additionally, RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that the YCM group exhibited significantly elevated mRNA expression levels of the M2 markers Arg-1 and CD206, while the expression of the M1 markers iNOS and CD86 was substantially decreased compared to the control and groups (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these findings indicate that conditioned medium derived from young spheroid MSCs possesses enhanced immunomodulatory ACMcapabilities, effectively promoting macrophage polarization toward a reparative M2 phenotype through its anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative cytokines. Synthesis and characterization of YCM + Cu2⁺@MN

In this study, aldehyde and methacrylate-modified hyaluronic acid (AHAMA) was synthesized [29]. 1H NMR spectroscopy displayed characteristic methacrylate C═C peaks at 5.7 ppm and 6.1 ppm, and new hemiacetal proton peaks at 4.9 ppm and 5.0 ppm from the aldehyde-hydroxyl interaction, confirming successful conjugation (Fig. 2A). Encapsulation of YCM within AHAMA allowed sustained release of its content over 30 days, with cumulative release reaching 50% by day 10 and stabilizing after 25 days, showcasing excellent sustained-release capabilities (Fig. 2B). Visual inspection indicated that uncross linked YCM@AHAMA-pregel was a flowable liquid, transforming into a resilient solid upon UV irradiation (Fig. 2C). In cell compatibility assessments, the viability of macrophages cultured with AHAMA hydrogels (Gel) and YCM-loaded hydrogels (Y-Gel) for 3 days showed no significant difference from the control group, affirming the material’s non-cytotoxicity (Fig. 2D). SEM analysis revealed that both MN and YCM@MN structures took on a regular pyramidal shape, with discernible secretomic components present in the YCM@MN hydrogel segments (Fig. 2E). Mechanical strength was evaluated using a universal testing machine. The force–displacement curve increased gradually at small displacements, followed by a steeper rise with further displacement. The curve remained continuous throughout, with no sudden drop, indicating that the microneedles maintained structural integrity within the tested range (Fig. S2). Under specific loads, the microneedles showed no visible deformation at 50 g and 100 g, with slight deformation observed at 200 g (Fig. 2F, G). These results suggest that the microneedles possess sufficient mechanical strength for transdermal application. (Fig. 2F, G). Ex vivo skin insertion tests showed successful penetration through the stratum corneum barrier, with puncture marks fading over time (Fig. 2H). We further evaluated the swelling behavior of the hydrogel. At equilibrium, the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN hydrogel exhibited a swelling ratio slightly higher than that of pristine MN, though the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. S3). As Cu2⁺ contributes to the antibacterial and angiogenic functions of our system, we further assessed its biocompatibility as part of our initial safety evaluation. In vitro cytotoxicity assays on vascular endothelial cells and macrophages—two cell types relevant to wound healing—showed cell viability above 95% at the copper concentrations released from the microneedles (Fig. S4A-B). In addition, serum biochemical analysis from the in vivo study did not indicate significant elevations in hepatic (ALT, AST) or renal markers (BUN, Cr) following a single treatment cycle (Fig. S4C-F), providing preliminary evidence of local and systemic compatibility under the current experimental conditions. We acknowledge, however, that more comprehensive evaluation will be needed to support clinical translation. Future studies incorporating pharmacokinetic analysis, long-term copper biodistribution, and assessment of potential organ accumulation in chronic models would help establish a more complete safety profile for this therapeutic approach. EDS elemental mapping revealed that Cu was concentrated at the tips of YCM + Cu2⁺@MN, confirming targeted loading of the active components (Fig. 2I). Collectively, YCM@MN offers excellent drug-release performance, biocompatibility, and mechanical strength, making it an ideal carrier system for transdermal therapeutic delivery.

YCM + Cu2⁺@MN promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype

To explore the underlying mechanism, we next assessed the direct regulatory role of YCM + Cu2⁺@MN in macrophage polarization. Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry were employed to assess the expression of the M1 marker CD86 and the M2 marker CD206. Confocal imaging revealed a decrease in CD86 expression in both the YCM@MN and Cu2⁺@MN treatment groups compared to the control, with a further reduction observed in the combination (YCM + Cu2⁺@MN) group, demonstrating an additive effect. A concurrent increase in CD206 fluorescence was also most pronounced in the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN group, indicative of a synergistic polarizing effect toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 3A). This shift was quantitatively confirmed, with a significant reduction in the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD86 (Fig. 3C) and an increase in the MFI of CD206 (Fig. 3D) from immunofluorescence images. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed these findings and further revealed a synergistic effect on macrophage polarization. While both individual treatments (YCM@MN and Cu2⁺@MN) modulated polarization, the effect was more pronounced with YCM@MN. The combined treatment (YCM + Cu2⁺@MN) yielded the greatest shift, exhibiting the most significant decrease in the M1 marker CD86 (Fig. 3E) alongside the most robust increase in the M2 marker CD206 (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis of key cytokine genes of delineated the distinct contributions of each component. Individual treatments with YCM@MN or Cu2⁺@MN downregulated pro-inflammatory genes (TNF-α, Il-1β, Il-6) and upregulated anti-inflammatory Il-10, with the effect of YCM@MN being consistently more potent than that of Cu2⁺@MN. Transcriptomic analysis of macrophages

To comprehensively elucidate the anti-inflammatory mechanism of YCM + Cu2⁺@MN at the molecular level, we performed RNA sequencing analysis on RAW264.7 macrophages. Sample correlation analysis revealed transcriptomic features significantly different between the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN-treated group and the control group (Fig. 4A). Comparative transcriptomic analysis identified 2,194 upregulated genes and 1,619 downregulated differentially expressed genes following YCM + Cu2⁺@MN treatment (Fig. 4B). Enrichment analysis consistently indicated profound effects on immune-related pathways: KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in multiple immune response pathways (Fig. 4C), while Gene Ontology (GO) biological process terms related to immune system processes also showed marked regulation (Fig. 4D). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) at pathway and biological process levels further validated the significant suppression of pro-inflammatory hallmarks (Fig. 4E, F). Concurrently, GSEA enrichment plots clearly demonstrated downregulation of key pro-inflammatory pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway and broad immune response pathways (Fig. S5A-B). Since the transcriptomic data strongly implicated the NF-κB pathway, a master regulator of inflammation, we validated its activity at the protein level. Western blot analysis delineated the distinct anti-inflammatory contributions of the individual components and their combination. While both YCM@MN and Cu2⁺@MN reduced the phosphorylation levels of IκBα and NF-κB p65 compared to the control, YCM@MN exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect than Cu2⁺@MN (Fig. 4H). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that YCM@MN led to significantly lower p-IκBα/IκBα and p-p65/p65 ratios relative to Cu2⁺@MN (Fig. 4I, J). Notably, the combined treatment (YCM + Cu2⁺@MN) resulted in lower levels of NF-κB pathway activation compared to either treatment alone, with phosphorylation ratios lower than those in the individual treatment groups. These results suggest that YCM@MN contributes to anti-inflammatory effects, and its combination with Cu2⁺@MN is associated with further inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway under the current experimental conditions. This observation may reflect complementary mechanisms between the two components. YCM contains anti-inflammatory cytokines that can modulate immune cell activity, while Cu2⁺ at certain concentrations has been reported to influence cellular signaling pathways related to inflammation. The NF-κB pathway plays a central role in regulating pro-inflammatory gene expression, and its reduced activation may partly explain the decreased inflammatory responses observed in our in vivo studies.

The effects of YCM + Cu2⁺@MN on macrophage mitochondrial function

Studies have shown that M1 macrophages generate substantial amounts of ROS through mitochondrial activity, which contributes to bacterial clearance and pro-inflammatory responses [30]. However, excessive ROS and inflammatory cytokines can lead to cellular damage and death. In contrast, M2 macrophages exhibit enhanced aerobic respiration capacity and produce ATP, anti-inflammatory cytokines, and other bioactive factors through pathways such as fatty acid oxidation, thereby promoting cell growth and tissue repair [31]. Therefore, restoring mitochondrial energy metabolism in macrophages is critical for diabetic wound healing.

Transcriptomic analysis in this study revealed that in the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN treatment group, genes related to cellular energy production and the metabolism of the three major nutrients were significantly altered, suggesting that macrophages treated with YCM + Cu2⁺@MN may have restored mitochondrial energy metabolism function (Fig. 4G). We subsequently assessed mitochondrial functional integrity by measuring mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using JC-1 staining and intracellular ROS levels. In healthy mitochondria, JC-1 forms red fluorescent aggregates, whereas in depolarized mitochondria, it remains in a green fluorescent monomeric form. Quantitative analysis of the red/green fluorescence ratio revealed that YCM + Cu2⁺@MN exhibited the most pronounced effect in restoring dissipated ΔΨm, with recovery capacity higher than that of other treatment groups (Fig. 5A, D). It is worth noting that Cu2⁺ has been reported to potentially induce ROS generation through Fenton-like chemistry, a mechanism that contributes to its antibacterial activity. However, our data show that Cu2⁺@MN treatment alone resulted in reduced intracellular ROS levels compared to the untreated control. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the concentration-dependent effects of Cu2⁺. At higher concentrations, Cu2⁺ can trigger oxidative stress, whereas at the relatively lower concentrations released from our microneedle system, Cu2⁺ may primarily participate as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and cytochrome c oxidase, thereby supporting cellular redox balance. Additionally, the improved mitochondrial membrane potential observed in Cu2⁺@MN-treated cells (Fig. 5A, D) suggests enhanced mitochondrial function, which may reduce electron leakage and subsequent ROS production. These findings indicate that, within the concentration range delivered by our system, Cu2⁺ contributes to restoring mitochondrial function and does not appear to induce oxidative stress under these conditions.

Furthermore, the combined treatment (YCM + Cu2⁺@MN) achieved protective effects superior to either monotherapy, indicating an enhanced capacity to restore mitochondrial bioenergetics and redox balance. To explore the molecular mechanism underlying this functional recovery, we analyzed the protein expression of key subunits representative of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system. Western blot analysis showed that the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN combination increased the expression levels of NDUFB8 (Complex I), SDHB (Complex II), UQCRC2 (Complex III), MTCO2 (Complex IV), and ATP5A (Complex V) (Fig. 5C, F-J). This upregulation across major OXPHOS complexes provides a mechanistic basis for the observed improvement in mitochondrial membrane polarization and reduction in ROS production, suggesting an additive benefit of combining young MSC-derived conditioned medium with copper ion-based therapy.

YCM + Cu2⁺@MN exhibits synergistic antibacterial and pro-angiogenic functions

We further evaluated the multifunctional therapeutic potential of the microneedles in contexts critical for infected wound healing: antibacterial activity and vascular regeneration. The disk diffusion assay demonstrated distinct antibacterial contributions from each component. While both Cu2⁺@MN and YCM@MN showed inhibitory effects against E. coli and S. aureus, Cu2⁺@MN generated larger inhibition zones, indicating its superior direct antibacterial capacity (Fig. 6A). This quantitative advantage was confirmed by bacterial viability assays, which showed the most potent bactericidal activity in the Cu2⁺@MN group among the single-component treatments (Fig. 6E). In parallel, we assessed the regenerative potential of the treatments on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). The scratch wound healing assay revealed that both individual treatments enhanced HUVEC migration compared to the control, with Cu2⁺@MN promoting a faster wound closure rate than YCM@MN at 12 h (Fig. 6B, F). Similarly, Cu2⁺@MN more effectively enhanced HUVEC adhesion (Fig. 6C) and profoundly stimulated angiogenic potential, as evidenced by its superior performance in increasing the number of nodes and total tube length in the tube formation assay (Fig. 6G). Immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis further indicated that Cu2⁺@MN induced a more marked upregulation of VEGF expression in HUVECs than YCM@MN (Fig. 6D, H), underscoring its central role in promoting angiogenesis. Importantly, the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN combination consistently produced the most robust pro-regenerative outcomes across all functional assays—achieving the highest migration rate, strongest adhesion, most complex tubular networks, and most intense VEGF expression—demonstrating a clear additive effect between the two components.

We acknowledge certain limitations in the present study. The in vivo evaluation was conducted using a mono-microbial infection model involving S. aureus, whereas chronic diabetic wounds in clinical settings frequently present as polymicrobial infections. In addition, our assessment was based on a single treatment administration, in contrast to the repeated interventions typically required in clinical management. Despite these constraints, this reductionist approach enabled us to establish, under well-controlled experimental conditions, the core antibacterial and pro-regenerative mechanisms of the YCM + Cu2⁺@MN system. Future investigations incorporating polymicrobial infection models and multi-dose regimens will be essential to further substantiate the translational relevance of this therapeutic strategy.

Methods and materials

Preparation of MSC-CM

Young (four weeks old) and aged (six months old) Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were selected for the extraction of young bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (Y-MSCs) and senescent bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (S-MSCs), respectively. The procedure was conducted in a sterile environment. Rats were euthanized, and sterile instruments were used to dissect the tibia and femur to expose the marrow cavity. Subsequently, the marrow cavity was flushed with sterile physiological saline to obtain a bone marrow cell suspension. This suspension was then transferred to sterile centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min to remove the supernatant plasma, while preserving the cell pellet. The cell pellet was resuspended in complete medium (DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and seeded into culture flasks. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 48 h to promote adherence and growth. To facilitate the formation of cell aggregates, the cells were digested with trypsin (Gibco, USA) until completely dissociated [32, 33]. The digestion was terminated with complete medium, and the resulting cell suspension was transferred to low-adhesion culture plates to promote spheroid formation. Cells were further cultured under the same conditions until spherical mesenchymal stem cells were observed. After 48 h of culture in serum-free basal medium, conditioned media (MSC-CM) were collected. The collected media were subjected to low-speed centrifugation (300 g for 10 min) to remove cell debris and impurities, followed by filtration through 0.22 μm membranes to ensure sterility. Finally, the conditioned media derived from young and aged MSC spheroids were aliquoted into sterile tubes and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. A portion of the samples was selected for ELISA assays (Meimian, China) to quantify the levels of cytokines within the conditioned medium.

Synthesis of AHAMA

The synthesis of AHAMA was executed via a two-stage chemical modification protocol encompassing controlled oxidation followed by methacrylation [29, 34–36]. Initially, high molecular weight hyaluronic acid (HA, 1 g) was meticulously dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water with continuous mechanical stirring at 300–350 rpm until complete homogenization was achieved. To facilitate oxidative cleavage of the vicinal diols present in the HA backbone, 5 mL of a 0.5 M sodium periodate solution was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was shielded from light and maintained at 250 rpm for 2 h to secure uniform oxidation. Subsequently, 1 mL of ethylene glycol (Macklin, China) was introduced to quench any residual oxidant. The resultant aldehyde-modified hyaluronic acid (AHA) underwent meticulous purification through extensive dialysis (14 kDa MWCO) against deionized water for 72 h, effectively removing low molecular weight byproducts, followed by lyophilization to yield a stable product.

In the subsequent methacrylation phase, 10 g of the purified AHA was re-dissolved in 500 mL of deionized water, achieving a homogenous solution under vigorous mechanical stirring at 230–350 rpm. To prevent premature polymerization, the reaction was conducted under stringent ice-bath conditions. A dropwise addition of 20 mL of methacrylic anhydride (MA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was performed, followed by the controlled introduction of 20 mL of 5 M sodium hydroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) over a span of 1.5 to 2 h, using a precision syringe pump to maintain a consistent pH throughout the reaction. The mixture was allowed to proceed overnight at 4 °C to ensure complete methacrylation. Post-reaction, purification was facilitated by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm for 15 min to eliminate insoluble aggregates, followed by dialysis (8 kDa MWCO) for an additional 72 h, with careful monitoring of the swelling characteristics of the modified polymer. The final AHAMA product was isolated as a white fibrous material after lyophilization and was stored at -20 °C to preserve its chemical integrity and stability. Characterization of the synthesized AHAMA was subsequently performed using 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Preparation and characterization of microneedles

Photo-crosslinked methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.5% (w/v) photo-initiator LAP, resulting in a 6% (w/v) prepolymer solution. The HAMA mixture was carefully dispensed into the tip cavities of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microneedle molds, ensuring that bubble formation was avoided. The treated molds were then placed in an oven at 37 °C, where they were subjected to multiple heating cycles to concentrate the HAMA solution. Following this, the molds were subjected to ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation for 3 min to initiate photopolymerization, resulting in solidified hydrogel tips. Subsequently, a 0.1 M solution of CuCl₂ (Fisher Scientific, USA) was added above the hydrogel microneedles and allowed to soak for 15 min, ensuring complete penetration of copper ions into the tips. Excess CuCl₂ solution was then removed, and the tips were rinsed with deionized water to eliminate any unbound copper ions. Freeze-dried MSC-CM were mixed with a 5% (w/v) AHAMA solution, allowing for high-concentration loading through a freeze-drying-resolubilization cycle. The loaded freeze-dried supernatant of AHAMA solution was injected into the base region of the microneedle mold, covering the previously solidified tip section. A 20% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution was then cast onto the surface of the base, and the mold was placed in a 37 °C oven for over 12 h to ensure thorough drying. The microneedles were subsequently separated from the mold. The obtained microneedles underwent a load-bearing test where weights of 50 g, 100 g, and 200 g were applied for 5 min to assess the hardness of the tips. All samples were examined via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEM-1200EX, Japan).

In vitro biocompatibility

To determine the cellular compatibility of the hydrogel microneedles, RAW264.7 cells (Gibco, USA) were seeded in the lower chamber of a Transwell insert (0.4 µm pores), while the microneedles (MN) and YCM + Cu2⁺@MN were placed in the upper chamber. Indirect co-culturing was conducted for 1, 2, and 3 days. Cell proliferation levels were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China). To evaluate cell viability, the culture medium was removed from the wells, and the plates were washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well of a 96-well plate (Beyotime, China), and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Release detection of soluble proteins from hydrogels

To investigate the release of MSC-CM proteins from the AHAMA hydrogel, YCM@AHAMA hydrogels were assembled in each well of a 96-well plate. After UV crosslinking, 50 mL of PBS (pH 6.8) was added to each well, and the plates were placed on a shaker at 37 °C with agitation at 100 rpm. At specified time intervals, 30 µL of supernatant was collected, and fresh PBS was added to replace the withdrawn volume. The protein released into the supernatant was quantitatively analyzed using an enhanced BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) to determine the total protein mass released at each time point.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR analysis (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells subjected to various treatments using an RNA extraction kit (Accurate Biology, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), ensuring A260/A280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1, indicative of high-quality RNA. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Beyotime, China), which provided a reliable template for downstream applications. qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green method on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. To normalize gene expression levels, GAPDH was used as an internal control. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method, which allows for the quantification of changes in gene expression relative to a control group. Primer sequences used for this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Flow cytometry analysis

Following specific treatment conditions, RAW264.7 macrophages (Puno-Sci Biological, China) were first incubated with PBS containing 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) at 4 °C for 20 min to block Fc receptors and reduce non-specific binding. Subsequently, the cells were divided into two separate tubes for independent labeling of surface markers. In the M1-type detection tube, CD86-APC antibody (1:100, BioLegend, USA) was added, while in the M2-type detection tube, CD206-PE antibody (1:100, BioLegend, USA) was included. Both tubes were incubated on ice in the dark for 25 min to allow optimal binding of the antibodies to their respective surface markers. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS at 300 × g for 5 min to remove unbound antibodies. After washing, the cells were re-suspended in PBS containing 1% BSA and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to prepare a single-cell suspension. Data acquisition was performed using a high-throughput flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, China).

Transcriptome analysis and RNA sequencing

In this study, transcriptomic analysis of RAW264.7 cells was performed utilizing Illumina high-throughput sequencing technology. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method, followed by digestion with DNase I to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The integrity of RNA was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, ensuring the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of at least 8.0. To enrich mRNA, oligo(dT) magnetic beads were employed, and subsequently, the mRNA was fragmented and converted into double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA). This cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and the ligation of Illumina sequencing adapters. The resulting library was then PCR-amplified to enrich the desired fragments. The quality of the library was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to verify fragment distribution, targeting a peak size range of 250–300 bp, and the concentration was quantified using Qubit. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the paired-end 150 (PE150) sequencing strategy, employing a sequential synthesis and sequencing technique to generate raw data. Each sample achieved an average sequencing depth of 40 million clean reads, ensuring the accuracy of gene expression quantification.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment using JC-1 staining

The JC-1 fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China) was employed alongside laser scanning confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) to perform high-resolution imaging of mitochondrial membrane potential in RAW264.7 macrophages. Cells were seeded in confocal-compatible culture dishes and subjected to various treatments. Following treatment, cells were gently washed twice with pre-warmed PBS at 37 °C to remove residual culture medium. The cells were then incubated in culture medium containing 5 μM JC-1 dye for 20 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere, ensuring optimal uptake of the dye into the mitochondrial matrix. Upon completion of the staining period, cells were washed gently three times with pre-warmed JC-1 wash buffer to eliminate unbound dye. Subsequently, 2 mL of fresh pre-warmed phenol red-free medium was added to maintain cell viability. The confocal microscope was utilized to analyze changes in mitochondrial membrane potential based on the fluorescence intensity and distribution of JC-1.

ROS detection

To assess ROS levels in RAW264.7 macrophages, begin by aspirating the culture medium from each well of a 24-well plate and washing the cells once with PBS to eliminate any residual media. Next, add an appropriate volume of DCFH-DA staining solution (Beyotime, China) to each well and incubate the plate at 37 °C in a humidified chamber for 30 min. This incubation allows for cellular uptake of the dye and its conversion to the fluorescent form in the presence of ROS. After incubation, carefully remove the staining solution and wash the cells twice with PBS to reduce background fluorescence. Utilize a laser scanning confocal microscope to observe the cells and capture high-resolution images. Finally, use ImageJ software to quantify the DCF fluorescence intensity.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay and adjusted to equal amounts before mixing with 4 × Laemmli buffer and boiling at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (8–12% gels) and transferred to PVDF membranes using wet transfer (100 V, 90 min). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at RT, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (NDUFB8, SDHB, UQCRC2, MTCO2, ATP5A, β-actin, NF-κB p65, phospho-NF-κB p65, total-IκBα, and phospho-IκBα; diluted 1:1000, Proteintech, China). Following TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) were applied for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate and analyzed by ImageJ. β-actin served as loading control.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded onto confocal microscopy plates and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. To block nonspecific binding sites, the cells were treated with 5% BSA dissolved in PBS for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (CD86, CD206, VEGF, all diluted 1:200, Proteintech, China). The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min each) and then incubated in the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488/594, diluted 1:1000, Proteintech, China) in the dark for 1 h. For VEGF staining in HUVECs, after the secondary antibody incubation, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-phalloidin (Proteintech, China) was applied for an additional 30 min. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, Beyotime, China) for 5 min. After mounting the slides, images were acquired using a confocal microscope, enabling detailed visualization of the immunofluorescence results.

Scratch assay for cell migration

In the scratch assay, hydrogel microneedles composed of MN, YCM@MN, Cu2⁺@MN, and YCM + Cu2⁺@MN were placed in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (0.4 µm pore size), while HUVEC cells were seeded in the lower chamber. Once the cells reached confluence (70–80%), a scratch was created using a 200 µL pipette tip. The scratched area was washed three times with PBS to eliminate the detached cells, following which a serum-free medium was added to inhibit cell proliferation and prevent interference with the experimental results. The migration of cells into the central scratched area was then observed at various time points (0, 6, and 12 h).

In vitro antibacterial activity

To assess the in vitro antibacterial activity of different hydrogel microneedles, we used E. coli and S. aureus as model bacteria. The microneedles, including MN, YCM@MN, Cu2⁺@MN, and YCM + Cu2⁺@MN, were immersed in 1 mL of pH 5.5 buffer for 48 h to allow for extraction of potential antibacterial compounds. Following this, the extracts of the microneedles were co-cultured with bacterial suspensions (1 × 104 CFU/mL) in a 48-well plate for 14 h. An empty control group without materials and a pH 5.5 buffer control were included for comparison. Following the co-culture, the treated bacterial solutions were transferred to solid agar plates, which were incubated for an additional 14 h. The resulting cultures were photographed to assess bacterial growth and inhibition.

In vivo evaluation of wound healing in a diabetic wound model

All animal experimental procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the Animal Management Regulations issued by the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. Six-week-old male SD rats (weighing 200–250 g) were selected for the study. After four weeks of high-fat diet feeding, a single intraperitoneal injection of STZ (MedChemExpress, USA) at a dose of 35 mg/kg (dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5) was administered to establish the model. One week after injection, rats with fasting random blood glucose levels consistently exceeding 16.7 mM were selected as type II diabetic model rats. Following an 8‑week standard maintenance period, type II diabetic model rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 4% (w/w) pentobarbital sodium (0.2 mg per 100 g body weight). After shaving and disinfecting the dorsal skin, a single full‑thickness circular excisional wound (1.5 cm in diameter) was surgically generated on the midline of the back. Each wound was then topically inoculated with 100 µL of a S. aureus suspension (1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) [37, 38]. After 24 h of bacterial infection, the diabetic wounds were subjected to topical treatment with one of the following (0 day): MN, YCM@MN, Cu2⁺@MN, or the combined YCM + Cu2⁺@MN formulation. Wound images were captured on days -1, 3, 7, and 14 post-treatment to monitor the healing progress. The wound healing rate was calculated using the following formula: Healing Rate (%) = [(A₀ − Aₙ) / A₀] × 100%, where A₀ and Aₙ represent the wound area on day -1 and day n, respectively. Wound tissue samples were collected on day 14 for subsequent histological and immunofluorescence analyses.

Histological and immunofluorescence staining

For histological examination and immunofluorescence analysis, the collected skin tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, sequentially embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were subjected to H&E staining following standard procedures to evaluate general tissue morphology, Masson’s trichrome staining using a commercial kit (Leagene, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to assess collagen deposition and distribution, and immunofluorescence staining to detect macrophage polarization markers (CD86 and CD206), and angiogenesis and vessel maturation markers (CD31 and α-SMA).All stained sections were imaged using a Zeiss microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data from cellular and animal experiments are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Each quantitative assay was repeated at least three times. The residual area of in vitro wound healing, in vivo intervention effects, and histological parameters were quantified using ImageJ software, while flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo software (USA). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, USA). Differences between two groups were assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test where appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Conclusion

This study developed a composite microneedle system with a compartmentalized architecture and spatiotemporally controlled release of Cu2⁺ and YCM, whose combined therapeutic effects were observed both in vitro and in vivo. At the cellular level, this system alleviated inflammatory responses and was associated with improved cellular energy metabolism. Furthermore, the system exhibited antibacterial activity and pro-angiogenic capacity, and was associated with accelerated endothelial cell migration, adhesion, and tube formation. In a diabetic rat model with full-thickness skin defects, treatment with YCM + Cu2⁺@MN was associated with enhanced wound closure, epidermal regeneration, collagen deposition, and neovascularization.
