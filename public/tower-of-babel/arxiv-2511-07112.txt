More Agents Helps but Adversarial Robustness Gap Persists *Equal contribution


Abstract
When LLM agents work together, they seem to be more powerful than a single LLM in mathematical question answering. However, are they also more robust to adversarial inputs? We investigate this question using adversarially perturbed math questions. These perturbations include punctuation noise with three intensities ( 10 % 10\% , 30 % 30\% , 50 % 50\% ), plus real-world and human-like typos ( WikiTypo , R 2 ATA) . Using a unified sampling-and-voting framework (Agent Forest), we evaluate six open-source models (Qwen3-4B/14B, Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Gemma3-4B/12B) across four benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU–Math, MultiArith), with various numbers of agents n ∈ { 1 , 2 , 5 , 10 , 15 , 20 , 25 } n\in\{1,2,5,10,15,20,25\} . Our findings show that (1) Noise type matters: punctuation noise harm scales with its severity, and the human typos remain the dominant bottleneck, yielding the largest gaps to Clean accuracy and the highest ASR even with a large number of agents. And (2) Collaboration reliably improves accuracy as the number of agents, n n , increases, with the largest gains from n = 1 n{=}1 to n = 5 n{=}5 and diminishing returns beyond n ≈ 10 n{\approx}10 . However, the adversarial robustness gap persists regardless of the agent count. Keywords: LLM agents, adversarial robustness, math question answering

1.  Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have made remarkable progress in solving a variety of reasoning-intensive tasks
35
, including mathematical problem solving. Beyond simply scaling model size, recent work shows that treating multiple LLM instances as
agents
and combining their outputs through debate, voting, or self-consistency can substantially boost performance.
24
demonstrate that even a simple sampling-and-voting strategy, essentially allowing multiple agents to propose answers and then aggregating them, can rival more sophisticated collaborative frameworks. These findings suggest that collaboration between agents is a powerful and relatively simple way to enhance reasoning accuracy.
At the same time, another body of research highlights a major weakness of LLMs: their fragility when inputs are perturbed. While the semantic meaning of a math problem may remain unchanged, minor surface-level changes, such as synonym replacements
30
, typos
13
, or even random punctuation insertions, can significantly reduce model accuracy. For instance,
1
show that inserting punctuation noise, despite leaving the problem content intact, can cause accuracy drops on well-established benchmarks like GSM8K
3
and MultiArith
27
.
These two perspectives converge on an important open question:
If multiple agents can enhance reasoning performance on
Clean
inputs, can they also improve robustness under noisy conditions?

Understanding whether ensemble-based multi-agent setups show resilience to noise would provide valuable insights for designing more reliable and trustworthy LLM systems.

To address this gap, we investigate the robustness of Agent Forest (a multi-LLM-agent setup) to adversarially perturbed math questions. We develop a taxonomy of noise types for adversarial modification and systematically introduce them across multiple reasoning datasets for mathematical question answering, enabling a controlled evaluation of their impact.
We use ArithmAttack
1

2

9

We assess the impact of input noise perturbations on open-source LLMs, specifically Qwen
34
, Llama
5
, Mistral
18
, and Gemma
12
, across multiple parameter scales. By doing so, we evaluate not only how noise affects individual models but also whether multi-agent collaboration enhances resilience and preserves reasoning accuracy in the presence of such perturbations.

2.  Related Work

Recent work has shown that current open-source large language models (LLMs) exhibit limited resilience to naturally occurring input noise, such as spelling mistakes, typographical errors, and minor punctuation artifacts, raising serious concerns about their reliability in real-world deployments
4
;
1
.
A substantial body of research has analyzed the impact of input perturbations across multiple granularities. At the character level, studies examine misspellings and typos
10
;
23
; at the word level, attacks involve synonym substitution or word shuffling
11
;
26
; and at the sentence level, robustness is tested via paraphrasing or insertion of irrelevant context
29
;
22
. Even seemingly innocuous changes, such as random punctuation insertion, have been shown to significantly degrade performance
1
.
These perturbations have been evaluated across a range of tasks and settings. Prior analyses of LLMs under noisy inputs include text classification
6
, natural language inference
8
;
7
, and multilingual scenarios
2
, offering valuable insights into task-specific vulnerabilities.

In parallel, a growing line of work
31
;
28
;
32
demonstrates that multi-agent or ensemble approaches, where multiple LLM instances generate and aggregate responses, can substantially boost reasoning performance on
Clean
inputs.
25
show that simple strategies like self-consistency or majority voting often rival more complex debate frameworks, and ensembles of smaller models match or exceed the performance of much larger single models.

15
provide preliminary evidence that multi-agent reasoning can degrade under high noise levels, highlighting a potential vulnerability. Nevertheless, a systematic evaluation across diverse noise types, reasoning tasks, and agent scales has not yet been conducted.
Together, these observations reveal a critical gap: while both noise robustness and multi-agent collaboration are active research areas, their intersection, how ensemble-based reasoning fares under diverse, realistic input corruptions, has not been systematically explored.

3.1.  Noisy Dataset Creation

To systematically analyze the influence of noise on multi-agent LLMs, we introduce a taxonomy that distinguishes between synthetic noise and human-like noise. This taxonomy enables a structured evaluation of model performance under both artificially generated perturbations and noise patterns that more closely resemble human input.

Synthetic noise.

Following
ArithmAttack

1
, we implement synthetic noise with
punctuation insertion
, where symbols from the set {".", ",", "!", "?", ";", ":"} are randomly added to sentences. To study the effect of perturbation strength, each noise type is applied at three intensity levels (
10\%
,
30\%
, and
50\%
), allowing a controlled analysis of model performance under progressively noisier conditions.

Human-like noise.

To mimic realistic human errors, we consider two complementary approaches. The first is the Adversarial Typo Attack (ATA)
9
, which evaluates the robustness of LLMs to typographical errors by iteratively introducing common character-level mistakes typically caused by keyboard usage. These include letter duplication, character substitution, deletion, or insertion, and removal of spaces, all targeted toward words that are important for the query, with the goal of degrading model performance. For this, we rely on the
R
2
ATA
benchmark, which applies ATA to GSM8K, BBH, and MMLU tasks. The second approach leverages naturally occurring spelling mistakes extracted from Wikipedia edit histories
2
. Specifically, we use the
WikiTypo
dictionary, which provides a collection of real-world human spelling errors, to replace words in the datasets with authentic typo variants.

3.2.  Agent Forest

We evaluate multi-agent performance using
Agent Forest

24
, which consists of two phases: sampling and voting.
For each question
x
from a dataset, we query the same base model
\mathcal{M}
multiple times with independent random seeds and fixed decoding settings. Each call acts as one agent and produces one candidate solution
s_{i}
. After
N
calls we obtain the set of candidates
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{N}\}
.

Answer extraction and normalization.

For arithmetic datasets, we extract the final numeric answer from each
s_{i}
using simple patterns that look for common markers such as
Answer
or
\boxed{}
. We then extract the value by removing formatting artifacts, converting textual numbers to numerals, and standardizing whitespace. This yields a canonical answer
\mathrm{canon}(s_{i})
for each sample.

Aggregation by voting.

To specify the final answer, we aggregate candidates by majority vote over canonical answers.

Protocol and replication.

For every model and dataset, we generate exactly
25
independent single-agent outputs per question with fixed decoding parameters. To study different agent counts
n\in\{1,2,5,10,15,20,25\}
, we partition the
25
samples into
\lfloor 25/n\rfloor
disjoint groups of size
n
in their generation order. Each group forms one independent
n
agent ensemble whose vote yields one prediction for that question. Thus we obtain
25
independent predictions for
n=1
,
12
for
n=2
,
5
for
n=5
, and
2
for
n=10
. For
n=15
,
n=20
, and
n=25
, only one ensemble is available, so we use the first
n
outputs. At the dataset level, we compute accuracy for each independent group and report the mean and the standard deviation across groups when multiple groups exist. No fine-tuning or task-specific supervision is used, and decoding settings are identical for
Clean
and noisy inputs so that any change in accuracy is attributable to aggregation by Agent Forest.

3.3.  Evaluation Metrics

We report accuracy and Attack Success Rate (ASR). Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a data set with gold answers
y_{i}
. Let
f_{n}
denote the
n
agent ensemble predictor that maps an input
x
to a single answer by Agent Forest voting as defined in Section
3.2
. The metric is:

Accuracy.

Clean
accuracy for
n
agents:

\mathrm{Acc}(n)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,f_{n}(x_{i})=y_{i}\,\right].

Attack Success Rate (ASR).

ASR measures the fraction of originally correct predictions that are flipped to incorrect after applying the noise. Formally,

\mathrm{ASR}(n)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,f_{n}(A(x_{i}))\neq y_{i}\,\right]}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,f_{n}(x_{i})=y_{i}\,\right]}.

ASR is thus defined on the subset of items that the model with
n
agents solves correctly in the
Clean
setting and takes values in
[0,1]
. A lower value indicates better robustness.

Reporting.

As described in Section
3.2
, for each model and dataset, we generate
25
independent single-agent outputs per question and form disjoint groups to realize different agent counts
n\in\{1,2,5,10,15,20,25\}
. For
n\in\{1,2,5,10\}
this yields multiple independent groups; we compute
\mathrm{Acc}
and
\mathrm{ASR}
for each group and report the mean and the standard deviation across groups. For
n\in\{15,20,25\}
only one group is available; we report the single value.

4.  Experiments

All experiments were executed on HPC GPU Cluster 8 x NVIDIA GA100 [A100] GPUs with 80GB, running under CUDA 12.2.
We used Python 3.10 with the PyTorch 2.2 and HuggingFace Transformers
33
libraries.
For efficient inference, we employed vLLM
21
, which allows fast batched decoding for large-scale evaluation.

4.1.  Datasets

Evaluation of mathematical reasoning capabilities is conducted across four established benchmarks:
GSM8K

3
,
MATH

17
, the mathematics subset of
MMLU

16
, and
MultiArith

27
. This selection provides a comprehensive assessment spanning difficulty levels from basic arithmetic to advanced competition problems while incorporating both open-ended and multiple-choice formats.

GSM8K

3
contains 8.5K linguistically diverse grade school math word problems, with evaluation performed on the standard 1.3K test questions requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. Its broad adoption as a mathematical problem-solving benchmark offers a standard reference point for studying reasoning robustness under input noise.

The
MATH
dataset
17
consists of 12.5K challenging mathematics competition problems covering advanced topics, including algebra, geometry, and number theory. These problems require symbolic manipulation and multi-step derivations, enabling assessment of how adversarial noise affects complex problem-solving capabilities compared to elementary-level reasoning.

Evaluation using
MMLU

16
focuses specifically on the mathematics subsets, which provide multiple-choice questions testing factual recall and mathematical reasoning. This format complements the open-ended generation tasks from other datasets.

MultiArith

27
consists of 600 arithmetic word problems requiring multi-step operations, with evaluation on the standard 180-question test set. This dataset serves as a lightweight benchmark for studying noise effects on simple yet compositional reasoning tasks.

4.2.  Models

To evaluate a diverse range of capabilities while maintaining computational efficiency, a selection of modern open-weight language models of varying parameter sizes was chosen for evaluation to analyze the impact of model scale. The models comprise Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-14B-Instruct
34
, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
5
, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
18
, along with Gemma3-4B-IT and Gemma3-12B-IT
12
. These models will be subsequently referred to as Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-14B, Llama3.1, Mistral-7B, Gemma3-4B, and Gemma3-12B, respectively.

As shown in Figure

n{=}1

percentage points compared to the

5.2.  Datasets

Figure
5
and Figure
6
reveal distinct robustness profiles by dataset.
MATH is the most difficult benchmark in absolute terms. Accuracy starts lowest and improves with more agents, yet gaps to the
Clean
baseline remain under all types of adversarial inputs even at large agent counts. Its ASR is also the highest among the datasets and hardly declines with increasing
n
, indicating that long, multi-step solutions are sensitive to both synthetic punctuation and human-like typos.

For the GSM8K dataset, moving from one to five agents recovers much of the loss from punctuation noise, and ASR drops steadily with
n
.
WikiTypo
consistently induces the largest degradation at a fixed agent count, suggesting heterogeneous human errors are more disruptive than punctuation insertions on these short, compositional problems.

MMLU (math subset) is comparatively stable. Accuracies cluster tightly and rise smoothly with agent count, but its ASR is slightly higher than in GSM8K across noise types.

MULTIARITH is near the ceiling. Accuracy saturates by approximately five agents across all noise types, and ASR is minimal and continues to decrease with
n
. The short, structured arithmetic chains offer limited headroom for noise to reduce performance or for collaboration to yield large additional gains.

Overall, easier datasets with simpler tasks (GSM8K, MULTIARITH) are less affected by noise, which is reflected in their lower ASR values. Any performance degradation can be compensated for more easily through multi-agent collaboration. In contrast, more challenging datasets (MATH and MMLU) suffer greater absolute performance drops under noise, and even with larger agent counts, the recovery remains incomplete, highlighting that task difficulty amplifies the impact of input perturbations.

6.  Conclusion

This work provides a systematic study of multi-LLM-agent robustness for question answering under synthetic and human-like input noise. Using a unified sampling-and-voting framework across four datasets (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU–Math, MultiArith) and six open-source models, we examined accuracy and ASR as a function of the number of collaborating agents. Three findings emerge.
First, collaboration reliably improves accuracy, with the largest gains between one and five agents and diminishing returns beyond ten. However, ASR remains almost stable as the agent count increases. This pattern holds across models and datasets, indicating that multi-agent collaboration via majority voting is effective in improving accuracy, but the adversarial robustness gap persists.

Second, the noise type matters. Punctuation corruption degrades performance roughly in proportion to its intensity, yet is largely neutralized by five to ten agents. Human typos remain the dominant bottleneck:
WikiTypo
consistently induces the largest gaps to
Clean
accuracy and the highest ASR, even at high agent counts.
R
2
ATA
occupy a middle ground.
Finally, model-level comparisons show that robustness does not strictly correlate with parameter count. While the midsize models show the highest attack success rates, both the larger and the smaller ones show more robustness. Within families such as Qwen and Gemma, larger models consistently achieve lower ASR values and thus demonstrate stronger inherent robustness. Their smaller counterparts show similar robustness while benefiting more from collaboration. Promising directions include noise-aware sampling and aggregation objectives, verifier- or tool-assisted agents, and training-time augmentation targeted at human typos. Extending beyond our scope, open-source models, and character-level corruptions to other domains, languages, and real-world pipelines will test the breadth of these gains.

7.  Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we restrict evaluation to English, math-reasoning benchmarks, and character-level corruptions; we do not test multilingual data, formatting/layout perturbations (e.g., LaTeX/OCR noise), or adaptive adversaries that react to model outputs. Second, our multi-agent scheme is a single instantiation-independent sampling from the same base model with fixed prompts/decoding and simple majority voting, so we do not compare against debate, tool-augmented agents, verifier-guided aggregation, diversity-promoting sampling, or cost/latency-aware strategies; the efficiency–robustness frontier therefore remains underexplored.
Third, answer extraction is based on rule-based numeric parsing. Residual parsing errors can inflate ASR or deflate accuracy. Fourth, all agents share the same base model, so errors can be correlated, and our analysis does not disentangle correlation structure from the benefits of voting.

8.  Ethical Considerations

We evaluate only open-weight LLMs on public math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU–Math, MultiArith), which contain no personal or sensitive data. No human subjects were involved. Our findings highlight both the benefits and limitations of multi-agent setups; they should not be interpreted as guarantees of reliability in high-stakes domains. Practitioners must assess risks carefully when transferring these methods beyond research.
