Monitoring immune cell dynamics for potential prognostic biomarker of sepsis
Authors: Huili Ma, Jia Di, Shuang Xiao, Li Chen, Weifeng Zhao, Juanjuan Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by organ dysfunction due to dysregulated host immune responses. This study investigates dynamic changes in immune cells during sepsis progression and evaluates their utility as predictive biomarkers for sepsis prognosis.MethodsIn this retrospective study, immune cells from 220 patients with sepsis were compared with those of 70 healthy controls. Patients with sepsis were stratified by 28-day outcome into survivors (n = 148) and non-survivors (n = 72). Immune cells included the counts and percentages of neutrophils, lymphocytes and monocytes; the percentages of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells. The neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte/lymphocyte ratio (MLR) were also calculated. Immune parameters were monitored on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 after sepsis diagnosis to characterize dynamic trajectories.ResultsThe count and percentage of neutrophils, the count of monocytes, NLR, MLR and CRP in patients with sepsis were significantly increased, while the count and percentage of lymphocytes, the percentage of monocytes, the percentages of CD3+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells were lower as compared to the control. The NLR demonstrated a diagnostic accuracy for sepsis with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.981 (95% CI: 0.965 ~ 0.998). The percentage of neutrophils achieved an AUC of 0.980 (95% CI: 0.958 ~ 1.000). The percentage of neutrophils and NLR performed comparably to CRP and outperformed other immune cell indices. The count of monocytes on day 7, the percentage of monocytes on day 7 and 21, the count of lymphocytes on day 7 and 14, the percentage of lymphocytes on day 7, 14 and 21 in the non-survival group were lower than those in the survival group. The percentage of neutrophils and NLR on day 7, 14 and 21 in the non-survival group were higher than that in the survival group.ConclusionsThe percentages of lymphocytes and monocytes in the survivors of sepsis trend upward over time. The percentage of neutrophils and NLR show potential as a prognostic biomarker for sepsis and may be useful to predict 28-day hospital mortality in patients with sepsis. Day 7 may represent a critical window for prognostic assessment in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12048-1.Keywords: Sepsis, Immune cells, Dynamic monitoring, Prognosis

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by dysregulated host responses to infection, affecting 50 million people and causing 11 million deaths annually worldwide [1, 2]. In China (2017–2019), it accounted for 13.1% of all deaths, with 28.4% occurring in hospitals [3]. The severity of sepsis is assessed in clinical practice using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, which helps quantify the extent of organ dysfunction [4]. Early antibiotic treatment within one hour of septic shock onset significantly improves survival [5]. Consequently, early diagnosis and prompt intervention are critical in reducing sepsis-related mortality [6].

Immune cells are crucial biomarkers for the diagnosis of sepsis and the evaluation of host immune status. In the early stages of sepsis, pathogen recognition triggers emergency granulopoiesis and suppresses neutrophil apoptosis, resulting in prominent neutrophilia [7]. Activated neutrophils extensively infiltrate tissues, eliminating pathogens by releasing reactive oxygen species, elastase and forming neutrophil extracellular traps, but concurrently inflict substantial damage to host tissues and induce microvascular dysfunction [8].

As sepsis progresses, significant dysregulation of the adaptive immune system occurs. A substantial loss of CD4+ T cells, B cells, and follicular helper T cells markedly impairs the adaptive immune response to antigens and secondary infections [9]. Concurrently, accelerated lymphocyte apoptosis induces lymphopenia and immunoparalysis, which are strongly associated with adverse prognoses [10]. These opposing effects create a dramatic neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) that demonstrates superior diagnostic performance for early sepsis detection compared to traditional markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin [11]. While the white blood cell count remains a fundamental clinical parameter due to its rapid availability and sensitivity to acute physiological changes [12], a comprehensive assessment incorporating dynamic changes in immune cells shows an indication for enabling more accurate prognosis prediction and personalized treatment strategies in patients with sepsis.

This study aims to identify prognostic immune cell markers for sepsis. By investigating dynamic changes in immune cells during sepsis progression, our findings may provide a theoretical framework for developing targeted immunotherapies in sepsis management.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study enrolled 290 participants between August 2022 and October 2024, including 220 inpatients with sepsis from the Department of Infectious Diseases, the Department of Intensive Care Medicine, and the Emergency Department and 70 healthy individuals in the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. The inclusion criteria for the sepsis group was based on “The Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3)” [4]. Exclusion criteria included: age < 18 years, pregnancy, hematologic diseases, autoimmune disease, trauma, burns, chronic liver insufficiency and chronic renal insufficiency (Fig. 1). Healthy control group inclusion criteria: age ≥ 18; no pregnancy. We collected data on clinical characteristics, including sex, age, comorbidities, number of dysfunctional organs, and SOFA score within 24 h of confirmed sepsis. Cell counts of lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils; percentages of lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils; monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR); and NLR were collected on the 1st, 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21st, and 28th days after the diagnosis of sepsis. CRP served as a reference indicator. Patient survival status was recorded on the 28th day after diagnosis.

The study flow of patient selection

C-reactive protein, cell counts and percentages of lymphocytes, monocytes and neutrophils measurement

The blood was collected in EDTA-anticoagulated tubes within 24 h of diagnosis. CRP, cell counts and percentages of lymphocytes, monocytes and neutrophils were detected using immunoturbidimetry with a Mindray-CAL 8000 (Mindray, Shenzhen, China).

Percentages of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells measurement

Heparin-anticoagulated blood was collected in tubes within 24 h of diagnosis. The percentages of T lymphocyte subsets (CD3+ cells, CD3+CD4+ cells, CD3+CD8+ cells) were detected using flow cytometry by BD Multitest™ 6-Color TBNK kit (BD, New Jersey, USA) with BD FACSLyric™ according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using either the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The diagnostic performances of the biomarkers were assessed by drawing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). DeLong’s test was used to determine whether there were significant differences between the immune indicators and CRP by comparing the AUCs. The limited missing data were handled using multiple imputation with predictive mean matching. All tests were two-tailed, and P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; San Diego, CA, USA) or SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Correlation between immune cells and sepsis severity

Sepsis severity was measured using SOFA score. The count (r = 0.1808; P = 0.0072) and percentage (r = 0.1629; P = 0.0156) of neutrophils, as well as the NLR (r = 0.1524; P = 0.0237), were positively correlated with the SOFA score; while the percentage of lymphocytes (r = -0.1639; P = 0.0149) and the percentage of monocytes (r = -0.2025; P = 0.0025) were negatively correlated with the SOFA score (Fig. 2).

In terms of diagnostic sensitivity, CRP had the highest value (97.8%), followed by the percentage of neutrophils (96.0%) and NLR (95.50%). In terms of diagnostic specificity, the count and percentage of lymphocytes, the count of neutrophils, NLR, CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD8+ T cells, and CRP were all 100% (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Discussion

Sepsis progresses through two distinct phases: initial hyperinflammation with cytokine storm-induced organ damage, followed by immunosuppression with impaired pathogen clearance [13–15]. Dynamic monitoring of immune cells enables real-time assessment of immune status and could guide individualized precision therapy. In a multicenter, open-label Phase 1/2 trial, intravenous nimotuzumab was associated with increases in lymphocyte count and monocytic HLA-DR expression [16]. This study monitored the dynamic changes of immune cells and found lymphocytes and neutrophils exhibited characteristic dynamic expression patterns in patients with sepsis.

Lymphocytes are central to adaptive immunity and immune regulation [17]. Based on CD surface markers, they are classified into CD4+ helper T cells (which activate immune responses via cytokine secretion) and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (which directly kill infected cells) [18]. CD3+ expression identifies all mature T cells, with their quantity and function reflecting overall immune status [19]. Lymphopenia demonstrates significant diagnostic and prognostic value in sepsis. Liu et al. established that the lymphocyte count is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of sepsis. When the peripheral blood lymphocyte count is 0.84 × 109/L, the sensitivity for diagnosing sepsis is 85.4% [20]. Drewry et al. identified persistent lymphopenia (≤ 0.6 × 10⁹/L at day 4 post-diagnosis) as a significant predictor of 28-day mortality [21]. Zhang et al. identified that the percentage of lymphocytes and SOFA score were independent risk factors for the prognosis of patients with sepsis. The AUC of lymphocyte percentage for predicting mortality was 0.812, with a sensitivity of 88.37% and a specificity of 65.60% [22]. Jia et al. developed a predictive model combining absolute lymphocyte count and naïve CD4+ T cell percentage to estimate the 28-day mortality risk in septic patients. This model achieved an AUC of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.602 ~ 0.901), with a sensitivity of 55.6%, a specificity of 100%, and an overall accuracy of 80.0% [23].

The count and percentage of lymphocytes, the percentages of CD3+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells of patients with sepsis were all lower than in healthy controls, suggesting early-onset lymphocyte reduction. Non-survivors exhibited persistently increases in the percentage of neutrophils and NLR, accompanied by the decreases in the percentages of lymphocytes and monocytes. We hypothesize that this pattern reflects sustained overactivation of the innate immune response coupled with adaptive immune suppression. The count and percentage of lymphocytes were 1.10 × 109/L and 19.60%, the sensitivity for diagnosing sepsis were 74.1% and 94.5%, with a specificity of 100.0% for both, indicating that the percentage of lymphocytes was a valuable diagnostic indicator. In contrast to some prior reports, we found that at baseline the percentage and count of lymphocytes had limited prognostic value for 28-day mortality. The significant prognostic divergence materialised only at day 7 and day 14, where survivors demonstrated higher percentage and count of lymphocytes than non-survivors. This pattern may reflect the highly heterogeneous initial host response, which may obscure early risk stratification [24]. The immune reconstitution trajectory observed during the second week provides a clearer prognostic window. Consequently, dynamic monitoring of the percentage of lymphocytes is essential for prognostic assessment over the clinical course.

Neutrophils, representing 40–70% of circulating leukocytes, serve as primary effectors of innate immunity through pathogen clearance [25]. In sepsis, neutrophil dysfunction manifests through impaired chemotaxis, altered antimicrobial activity, and delayed apoptosis, contributing to both immune paralysis and persistent inflammation [26, 27]. Our longitudinal analysis demonstrated significantly lower percentages of neutrophils in survivors versus non-survivors at days 7, 14, and 21. Furthermore, the percentage of neutrophils showed a positive correlation with SOFA score, establishing their value as both prognostic markers and indicators of sepsis severity.

The host immune response to sepsis is characterized by a complex and dynamic dysregulation, encompassing simultaneous hyperinflammation and immune suppression [28]. The dynamics of neutrophils and lymphocytes represent a crucial component of the immune response to pathogen infection during sepsis [15]. NLR serves as an important indicator of immune status by reflecting the balance between neutrophil and lymphocyte counts [29]. A meta-analysis of 14 studies (n = 14,999) reported NLR’s diagnostic efficacy for neonatal sepsis, with the sensitivity of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.61 ~ 0.83) and specificity of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.73 ~ 0.95) [30]. Prior research has established a correlation between elevated NLR and the severity of sepsis [31]. We observed that an NLR cutoff of 3.75 exhibited excellent diagnostic performance for sepsis (AUC = 0.981, sensitivity = 95.5%, specificity = 100%). Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between NLR and the SOFA score. Dynamic monitoring revealed that NLR levels in non-survivors were significantly higher than those in survivors on days 7, 14, and 21, underscoring the clinical utility of NLR in assessing sepsis severity and prognosis.

Serial NLR measurement provides a readily available and cost-effective strategy for dynamic risk assessment in sepsis. This method enables early detection of high-risk patients who may not be identified by conventional scoring systems and provides clinicians with an objective, quantifiable trend to inform critical bedside decisions—such as when to escalate or de-escalate treatment. As NLR is calculated directly from routine complete blood counts, it can be seamlessly integrated into existing clinical workflows, holding significant potential to enhance personalized patient management and improve outcomes. Our results, which validate the prognostic relevance of serial NLR assessment, are consistent with increasing efforts to translate host response profiles into actionable clinical tools.

This study found no significant differences in immune cells between sepsis non-survivors and survivors on the first day after diagnosis. This lack of initial divergence may be associated with confounding factors such as heterogeneous infection types, varying disease severity, and differing treatment regimens among the patients [32]. Notably, our study demonstrated that on day 7 post-diagnosis, significant differences in immune indicators (the count and percentage of monocytes, the count and percentage of lymphocytes, the percentage of neutrophils, and NLR) were observed between survivors and non-survivors of sepsis. This suggests that day 7 may represent a critical window for prognostic assessment in sepsis. Dynamic immune monitoring around this time point could offer clinicians valuable insights for outcome prediction and the implementation of personalized treatment strategies.

Our study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other centers. Second, the inherent complexity of immune responses and the dynamic progression of sepsis could influence the interpretation of immune parameters. In future studies, more refined subgroup analyses (specific types of infection or pathogens) could be conducted to explore potential differences. Third, treatment heterogeneity may significantly influence sepsis progression and outcomes, potentially confounding biomarker-outcome associations. Future research should focus on immunotherapy’s impact on clinical prognosis in sepsis.

Conclusion

The diagnostic performance of the percentage of neutrophils and NLR for sepsis was non-inferior to that of CRP and superior to that of other immune cells. The percentages of lymphocytes and monocytes in the survivors of sepsis showed a gradually increasing trend. The percentage of neutrophils and NLR showed potential as prognostic biomarkers for sepsis and may be useful to predict 28-day hospital mortality in patients with sepsis. Day 7 may represent a critical window for prognostic assessment in sepsis.
