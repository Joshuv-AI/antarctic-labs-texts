Upcycling Agave cantala leaf waste into biocomposite leather-like material via structural design and fibre reinforcement
Authors: Ashni Dilina, Vikum Rajapakse, Nadishan Shanthikumar, Ruchira N. Wijesena, Nadeeka D. Tissera, Shantha M. Egodage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a bio-based leather-like composite developed from Agave cantala leaf waste, valorizing the succulent fraction that constitutes nearly 70% of leaf biomass discarded during decortication. Through oxidative alkaline modification and mechanical exfoliation, the native technical fibre bundles are transformed into a hierarchical network of flexible cellulose micro ribbons, while the amorphous parenchymatous phase forms a porous cellular morphology. Together, these phases form a synergistic fibre-reinforced matrix system that provides a leather-like structure at the micro level, without implying molecular or biochemical replication of animal collagen. When integrated with a biodegradable polymer binder and cotton twill backing, the resulting composite exhibited a tensile strength of 8.51 ± 0.17 MPa and tear resistance of 30.37 ± 0.87 N mm−1, outperforming established commercial plant-based leathers such as Desserto® and Piñatex® in tear resistance. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis and thermogravimetry analysis (TGA) confirmed selective delignification, cellulose enrichment, and the formation of thermally stable condensed structures supporting interfacial interaction and char formation. By utilizing agricultural by-products, this approach offers a sustainable alternative to conventional leather production while avoiding chrome-based tanning processes and reducing the environmental burden associated with biomass waste. Although a detailed life cycle assessment is beyond the scope of this study, the results demonstrate a scalable pathway for upcycling Agave cantala leaf waste into a bio-based leather-like composite with competitive mechanical performance for selected flexible sheet applications.

Introduction

Animal leather has a long history of use in clothing, protection, footwear, upholstery and other applications because of its unique collagen-based hierarchical structure, durability and tactile properties.1 In contemporary materials research, leather and leather-like alternatives are often discussed in relation to resource efficiency, waste utilization, processing chemistry and end-of-life considerations.2 It is important to recognize that hides and skins used in the leather industry are primarily by-products of the meat industry, and leather manufacturing converts these protein-rich by-products into durable materials.3 At the same time, tanning and finishing processes involve water, chemicals and effluent management requirements, and the sector has progressively adopted cleaner processing technologies and stricter environmental control practices.4 In this context, bio-based leather-like composites should not be viewed as direct replacements for natural leather, but as engineered imitation materials designed to reproduce selected form, handle and functional attributes for specific applications.5–7 Consequently, the development of vegan leather materials, defined as animal free alternatives that replicate the mechanical and aesthetic properties of leather, has gained global momentum.

Leather-like alternatives are broadly classified into synthetic systems, such as polyurethane (PU) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and bio-based systems derived from plants, microorganisms, fungi or agricultural residues. PU and PVC artificial leathers can provide useful flexibility and processability, but they depend on petrochemical feedstocks and are generally less favorable in terms of biodegradation and end-of-life management.8,9 Their incineration produces toxic gases such as hydrogen cyanide (from PU) and hydrogen chloride (from PVC).10 Current research therefore increasingly explores plant-based, fungal and agricultural-waste-derived flexible sheet materials.11 Plant resources, including pineapple (Ananas comosus) leaves, cactus (Opuntia ficus-indica), banana stems, cork bark, apple pomace, mango peels and grape skin, provide renewable feedstocks rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin and other biopolymers.12–14 These constituents can be engineered into fibre mats, films or reinforced composites that imitate selected physical and aesthetic features of leather-like materials, while remaining fundamentally different from collagen-based natural leather.15 Recent life-cycle assessments indicate that several commercially available plant-based leather-like materials have the potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions relative to conventional bovine leather, although reported values vary depending on system boundaries, material composition, and allocation methodology.16

Among commercial exemplars, Piñatex®, derived from pineapple leaf fibre, exhibits tensile strengths of 6–12 MPa and tear resistance comparable to many bio composites, while Desserto®, based on nopal cactus, achieves soft texture and durability with minimal land and water use.17–19 Similarly, AppleSkin™, a leather derived from apple processing residues, contains up to 50% organic apple pomace and attains tensile strengths of 10–18 MPa with 30–40% elongation.20 However, despite these advances, the sustainability of such leather can be offset by the partial inclusion of PU binders which limit biodegradability at end of life.21 Hence, the current frontier in sustainable leather research emphasizes fully bio based composite systems in which plant polysaccharides, proteins, and natural rubber analogues form the continuous matrix.

Within this context, Agave cantala (Haw.) Roxb. represents an underexplored yet promising feedstock for sustainable composite fabrication. Agave cantala is a xerophytic monocot adapted to arid climates and requires less than 500 mm of annual rainfall. Its leaves comprise two anatomically distinct regions as fibrous vascular bundle and succulent parenchyma matrix: the fibrous vascular bundles, rich in cellulose (60–65%), hemicellulose (18–22%), and lignin (12–15%); and the succulent parenchyma matrix, compose of non-fibrous polysaccharides, pectin, and mucilage with high water retention capacity.22,23 The plant thrives on marginal soil without irrigation or fertilizer inputs, making it a low carbon, low resource crop. During the decortication process, the leaves yield long, strong fibres traditionally used in cordage, mats, and handicrafts. The remaining succulent fraction comprising both fibrous and non-fibrous plant matter, accounting for nearly 70% of the total leaf biomass, is a waste by product of Agave cantala leaf decortication.24 Improper disposal of this biomass can lead to localized methane emissions under anaerobic degradation, contributing approximately 0.6–1.2 kg CO2 eq per kg waste.25–27 Valorizing this by-product as a bio composite precursor, therefore, offers a dual benefit of waste mitigation and greenhouse gas reduction.

The present study explores this underutilized succulent fraction of Agave cantala leaf fibre extract as a functional biopolymeric matrix in combination with its fibrous phase and a natural flexible polymer to fabricate a leather-like composite. The dual fibrous and non-fibrous morphology of the succulent enables the formation of an entangled fibre network embedded in a cellular plant-derived matrix. Chemical hydrolysis and mechanical exfoliation of the succulent biomass promote fibrillation and facilitate homogeneous dispersion of microfibres within the polymeric matrix, yielding a fibre-reinforced architecture. Unlike many commercial artificial leathers that rely partly on synthetic polyurethane coatings, this approach maximizes the use of bio-based constituents, including cellulose microfibrils, natural flexible polymers and polysaccharide-based additives. Furthermore, the use of a twill-woven cotton fabric backing provides textile reinforcement and improves dimensional stability and tear resistance; it is not intended to simulate the grain layer of animal hide.

However, a comprehensive life-cycle assessment is required to quantify these potential benefits. Comparative assessments indicate that bovine leather contributes ∼36.3 kg CO2 eq per m2, PU leather ∼10.1 kg CO2 eq per m2, cactus and pineapple-based leathers ∼3–6 kg CO2 eq per m2 and MyCoWork’Reishi™ ∼2.6 kg CO2 eq per m2. Owing to the use of agricultural by products and low energy processing, Agave cantala-derived composites are expected to fall at or below the lower end of this range.28,29 Moreover, the process eliminates chrome-based tanning systems, which are regulated due to potential Cr(vi) formation under certain conditions and requires less water than conventional leather tanning. By transforming a local craft industry waste into a high value, biodegradable material, the study aligns with circular economy principles and offers socioeconomic benefits to rural producers in arid regions.

This research introduces a bio-derived Agave cantala leather-like composite that integrates fibrous and non-fibrous phases of the leaf succulent with bio-based polymer to reproduce selected sheet-forming, flexibility and mechanical characteristics required for leather-like applications, while remaining structurally and chemically distinct from natural animal leather.

Experimental

Materials

Agave cantala leaves, collected from multiple agroecological regions of Sri Lanka to ensure representative plant maturity and compositional variability were selected for succulent extraction and subsequent modifications for use as the bio-filler. Fresh mature leaves containing balanced proportions of fibrous vascular tissue and non-fibrous succulent parenchyma were selected for extraction and subsequent modification.

A twill woven cotton fabric (150 GSM), a binder system and a silicone-based mold release agent were used to prepare composite sheets. Specific trade names and formulations are not disclosed due to third party confidentiality agreements.

Commercially available leather materials, including natural animal leather provided as a sample for analysis by SA Perera Pvt Ltd, Sri Lanka (A local animal leather manufacturer) and three representative plant-based leather alternatives, namely Piñatex®, Desserto® 1 and Desserto® 2, were selected for comparative evaluation of mechanical performance. Natural animal leather was included as the conventional benchmark owing to its well-established structural and mechanical characteristics. Piñatex®, produced primarily from pineapple leaf fibres reinforced with a bio-based polymer matrix, represents one of the most widely commercialized plant-based leather alternatives. Desserto®, manufactured from Opuntia ficus-indica (nopal cactus), was evaluated using two commercially available grades (Desserto® 1 and Desserto® 2), which differ in their backing fabric and surface construction. These commercially available materials were selected to provide an objective comparison of the tensile and tear performance of the developed Agave cantala-based leather-like composite against representative products currently available in the vegan leather market.

The binder system consisted of a bio-based polymer dispersion, a polysaccharide-derived thickening and film-forming agent containing hydroxyl and carboxylate functionalities, a hydroxyl-rich plasticizing component, and an ionic crosslinking agent capable of interacting with the polysaccharide-rich domains. Due to third-party confidentiality agreements associated with the industrial collaborator, the exact commercial grades and supplier-specific identities of these materials are not disclosed. However, their chemical classes, functional roles, and compositional ranges are provided to improve traceability and reproducibility.

• The dry-solid formulation was maintained within the following optimized ranges:

• Agave biomass bio filler: 64–66 wt% of total dry solids.

• Bio-based polymer dispersion: 27–29 wt% of total dry solids.

• Polysaccharide-derived thickening and film-forming agent: 2.0–2.5 wt% of total dry solids.

• Hydroxyl-rich plasticizing component: 2.0–2.5 wt% of total dry solids.

• Ionic crosslinking component: 1.0–1.5 wt% of total dry solids.

The formulation ranges reported in this study were established through iterative experimental optimization based on slurry stability, composite formation, handling characteristics, and mechanical performance. The compositions are presented as ranges rather than exact values to protect commercially sensitive formulation details.

Methodology

Extraction of leaf fibres and succulent fraction

Agave cantala leaves were washed thoroughly with clean water to eliminate surface impurities, soil particles, and adhering waxes. The marginal spines and basal root segments were trimmed to ensure safe and uniform feeding during mechanical decortication.

Mechanical decortication

Mechanical decorticator fabricated at a local engineering workshop in Kandy, Sri Lanka was used to extract succulent form plant leaf. The device comprised a leaf feeding mouthpiece, fluted feed rollers, a stationary bar, and a rotating stripping drum equipped with blunt serrated blades (SI, Fig. S1). High speed laboratory blender (1300 W, maximum speed of 27 000 rpm) was used for homogenization and defibrillation of succulent samples.

During mechanical decortication, the feed rollers guided the leaf through the stationary bar, allowing the stripping drum to abrade the outer parenchymal tissue and separate the fibrous bundles from the fleshy matrix. This process produced two distinct outputs: a fiber extract comprising of long, lignocellulosic fibres suitable for conventional cordage and handcraft applications and a succulent extract, the decortication residue, consisting of a mixture of short fibres, microfibrils, and non-fibrous parenchymatous tissue, representing approximately 65–70 wt% of the total leaf mass (Fig. 1). The succulent extract, typically regarded as agricultural waste, was collected and utilized as the precursor material for subsequent composite fabrication. Valorizing of this low value by product supports a circular economy approach and reduces biomass disposal impacts.

Graphical illustration of machine decorticated Agave Cantala leaf consisting of fibre extract and succulent extract.

Post extraction washing

Following separation, the succulent extract was washed three times with clean running water to remove loosely bound and water-soluble compounds. Washing was performed using a biomass-to-water ratio of approximately 1 : 10 (w/v) for 10–15 min per cycle, repeated 3 times. Literature on Agave species indicates that such residues contain appreciable amounts of soluble carbohydrates (glucose, fructose, sucrose), pectin, saponins, phenolic compounds, waxes, alkaloids, and inorganic salts.30,31 These components can influence surface reactivity and compatibility within composite matrices if not removed. Washing reduces the concentration of these extractives, leading to improved fibre matrix adhesion, enhanced mechanical stability, and reduced microbial degradation tendencies during storage. Excessive washing was deliberately avoided to preserve structural polysaccharides such as cellulose and hemicellulose, which are essential for composite reinforcement.32

The washed succulent was air-dried under ambient conditions to remove residual moisture before undergoing further modification and homogenization. The resulting biomass retained both fibrous and non-fibrous constituents, forming the heterogeneous reinforcement matrix used for developing Agave cantala based vegan leather composites.

Chemical and physical modification of the extract succulent

The succulent extract obtained from mechanical decortication contained short staple fibres, microfibrils, embedded within a non-fibrous parenchymatous matrix. The fibrous components provided tensile reinforcement, while the non-fibrous fraction contributed to uniformity and compactness in the composite structure.

To improve homogeneity and compatibility with the polymeric binder, the cleaned dried succulent extract underwent a combined physical and chemical modification process. The material was cut into smaller fragments to facilitate uniform processing (SI, Fig. S2). Controlled oxidative alkaline hydrolysis using an oxidizing agent in an alkaline medium was carried out for 48 hours under ambient agitation.

After hydrolysis, the succulent material was washed repeatedly with clean water until neutral pH was achieved and then mechanically dewatered. The material was subsequently homogenized using the high-speed blender promoting fibrillation and uniform dispersion of fibrous and non-fibrous constituents (SI, Fig. S3). The homogenized succulent material was oven dried at 80 °C to moisture equilibrium and stored under ambient laboratory conditions. This modification sequence collectively enhanced fibre matrix interaction, thermal stability, and structural uniformity of the Agave based material.

Preparation of composite sheets

The modified Agave cantala succulent served as the primary bio-filler. Two composite configurations were prepared: (i) fabric reinforced composite with cotton twill backing (ii) a non-reinforced composite formed solely from the succulent matrix.

For composite fabrication, the oven dried succulent material was reconstituted into a semi fluid slurry by gradual mixing with the binder system in the high-speed mechanical blender. This process ensured homogeneous dispersion of fibrous and non-fiber fractions, which is critical for achieving uniform film density and mechanical isotropy.

The stainless-steel mold (210 mm × 300 mm × 18 mm) coated with a silicone-based release agent was used to cast sheets. For the reinforced configuration, a single layer of the cotton twill fabric was placed at the base of the mold as a textile backing layer to improve dimensional stability and resistance to tear propagation. The cotton backing is not presented as a simulation of the grain layer of animal hide, which has a fundamentally different collagen-based microstructure and composition. The cotton twill layer was used only as a textile reinforcement backing. The prepared slurry was evenly spread across the mold using a squeegee to ensure consistent thickness and distribution of the fibrous and non-fibrous phases (SI, Fig. S4).

For the non-reinforced composite, the same procedure was followed without the fabric layer (SI, Fig. S4(B))

A two-stage curing process was applied: 80 °C for 6 h followed by post curing at 120 °C for 1 h. Finally, the sheets were conditioned at 25 ± 2 °C and 60 ± 5% RH for 48 h prior to testing.

Characterization of modified and un-modified succulent and composite

Morphological, thermal, and chemical characterization

The morphology of the extracted succulent materials and fibers were analyzed using a Zeiss Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE SEM, Zeiss EVO 18). Imaging was conducted up to ×25 000 magnification to evaluate fibrillation, surface topology, and phase distribution.

Thermal degradation of the extracted and treated succulent materials was assessed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Approximately 10 mg sample was analyzed using a thermogravimetric analyzer (model SDT Q600 V20.9, TA Instruments) heated from room temperature (30 °C) to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C min−1 under a nitrogen purge of 100 mL min−1. The resulting thermograms were used to evaluate moisture content, thermal stability, and decomposition stages associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin components within the biomass.

Fourier transformed infrared (FTIR) spectra of samples were obtained using Bruker ALPHA spectrophotometer. Measurements were carried out in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode, with the samples mounted on a diamond crystal. Spectra were recorded over the wavenumber range of 4000–900 cm−1 at a resolution of 1 cm−1, with 256 scans per sample. FTIR analysis was used to identify chemical changes induced by the modification process.

Mechanical properties

Tensile strength (ISO 3376) and tear resistance (ISO 3377 1) of the prepared composite sheets and the commercial leather materials such as animal Leather, Piñatex®, Desserto® 1, Desserto® 2 were measured using a James Heal Titan Universal Testing Machine. Specimen orientation, dimensions, and calculation equations were clarified to ensure reproducibility and standards compliance. These analyses enabled evaluation of structural integrity, damage tolerance, and durability of the composites in comparison with commercial leather alternatives.

Benchmarking against commercial vegan leather alternatives

To benchmark performance, the mechanical properties of the developed Agave cantala-based composites were evaluated against with selected commercially available plant-based leather alternatives. Piñatex®, comprising of approximately 72% pineapple leaf fibre, 18% polylactic acid (PLA), and a bio-based polyurethane blend, was selected as a representative example of widely adopted biodegradable vegan leather. Two variants from the Desserto® cactus-based leather product range were also included. Desserto 1, with a backing composed of 50% cotton and 50% polyester and Desserto 2, a premium grade with cotton backing and enhanced cactus-based surface formulation. These materials enabled a systematic comparative assessment of mechanical performance, reinforcement efficiency, and relative sustainability of the developed composite in relation to leading commercial bio leather products.

Results and discussion

SEM characterization of Agave cantala fibres and succulent fractions

Fig. 2(a) shows the washed and oven dried Agave cantala leaf fibres, which appear as coarse, ribbon like bundles characteristic of hard leaf fibres. An individual fibre observed in Fig. 2(b) exhibits an almost rectangular cross-section with diameters in the range of 270–295 µm, confirming that the extracted material at this stage consist of multicellular technical fibre bundle rather than single elementary fibre.

Morphological analysis of Agave cantala leaf fibres before modification (a) extracted fibres after washing and drying (b) single fibre morphology and diameter distribution (c) morphology of the fibre (d) magnified image of the surface of the fibre.

The SEM images in Fig. 2(c) and (d) further reveal the longitudinally aligned fibrillar structure of the technical fibre. The surface appears relatively smooth with shallow ridges and minimal defects, indicating that the decortication process effectively removes most of the epidermal tissues without significantly damaging the underlying sclerenchyma. The absence of deep surface cracks is advantageous as it minimizes stress concentration sites when the fibres are embedded in a polymer matrix.

The longitudinal view showed parallel lamellae aligned with the fibre axis, while the cross-section revealed polygonal fibre cells arranged around a central lumen region. The magnified cross-section clearly showed a honeycomb like packing of individual cells, indicating the presence of a multi layered cell wall (including the middle lamella, primary wall and secondary wall layers).33

This hierarchical organization is schematically illustrated in Fig. 3(a), which represents a generalized lignocellulosic plant fibre cell-wall model relevant to Agave fibres. The primary wall surrounds the secondary wall, which consists of S1, S2 and S3 sub-layers; S1 denotes the outer secondary wall, S2 the thick middle secondary wall that usually dominates axial stiffness, and S3 the inner secondary wall adjacent to the lumen. The middle lamella and lignin-rich regions provide transverse cohesion between neighbouring fibre cells. Fig. 3(b) shows cemented Agave cantala bundles enclosed within a lignocellulosic sheath, in which individual fibres are bonded by pectin, waxes and residual plant matter. The SEM micrograph in Fig. 3(b) further confirms the encapsulation of fibre bundles within this sheath, along with the presence of a surface waxy layer.

(a) Schematic illustration of the hierarchical cell-wall structure of a lignocellulosic fibre showing the middle lamella, primary wall and secondary wall layers (S1, S2 and S3). (b) SEM micrograph of a cemented Agave cantala technical fibre bundle.

Cotton fibres, by contrast, are single-cell seed hairs characterized by a twisted ribbon or convoluted morphology and are held together mainly by mechanical entanglement and weak inter-fibre adhesion. This comparison highlights the need to partially delaminate the cemented Agave technical bundles into finer and more flexible elements. Oxidative-alkaline treatment followed by mechanical exfoliation achieves this partial separation, increasing the accessible fibre surface area and improving load transfer within the composite. The resulting reinforcement should therefore be understood as a plant-fibre-reinforced network rather than a plant-fibre-reinforced structure.

Effect of chemical and physical modification on fibrous and non-fibrous fractions

The effect of the modification protocol on the fibrous fraction of the succulent biomass is clearly evident in Fig. 4. The low-magnification image (Fig. 4(a)) shows an exfoliated fibre mesh in which large technical fibres are split into numerous slender, ribbon-like elements. The magnified view (Fig. 4(b)) reveals partially separated elementary fibres with frayed edges and exposed microfibrils, indicating that the oxidative alkaline treatment has weakened the middle lamella and disrupted lignin–hemicellulose networks. Fig. 4(c) and (d) show twisted, deconvoluted cellulose ribbons with diameters in the range of 15–21 µm range, consistent with elementary sclerenchyma cells.

SEM micrograph of the plant fibre after modification (a) exfoliated fibre mesh (b) magnified image of the exfoliated fibres (c) twisted deconvoluted structure of the cellulose fibres (d) size distribution of the fibre diameter.

This represents nearly an order of magnitude reduction in effective fibre dimension compared with the 270–295 µm technical fibre width observed in Fig. 3, and corresponds well with FTIR evidence of hemicellulose and lignin removal (e.g. loss of the 1735 cm−1 carbonyl band, reduction in aromatic peaks). The finer, flexible cellulose ribbons form an interconnected three-dimensional entangled fibre network when combined with the non-fibrous plant-derived cellular phase and the bio-based polymer matrix. This hierarchical fibre–matrix architecture promotes efficient stress transfer, restricts fibre pull-out, and likely contributes to the enhanced tear resistance observed in the composite.

The improved fibrillation also correlates with the observed changes in the treated fibres. Enrichment of the cellulose fraction and reduced compositional heterogeneity, contribute to increased thermal stability and a more consolidated degradation profile.

Role of the non-fibrous succulent fraction

Fig. 5 illustrates the morphology of the modified succulent non-fibrous fraction. At lower magnification (Fig. 5(a)), the material appears as irregular flakes and fragments. At higher magnification (Fig. 5(b)) a distinct honeycomb-like architecture is observed, corresponding to the residual cell walls of parenchymatous tissue after removal of cell sap and soluble contents.

(a) SEM micrograph of modified succulent (b) magnified image of the modified succulent showing a honeycomb structure.

The thin, interconnected cell wall struts delimit polygonal pores, resulting a high specific surface area and a compliant, energy absorbing matrix. This morphology is consistent with the TGA profile, which shows a secondary high-temperature degradation step and increased char yield, characteristic of condensed polysaccharide phenolic networks.

From a composite design perspective, the honeycomb-like parenchyma-derived cellular phase serves as a lightweight bio-based structural component that complements the network of fibrillated cellulose-rich fibres. During composite consolidation, the continuous bio-based polymer matrix infiltrates the cellular framework and the interstitial spaces between adjacent fibres, forming an integrated fibre–matrix architecture. This intimate interaction between the cellular phase, cellulose ribbons, and polymer matrix enhances interfacial bonding, promotes efficient stress transfer, limits fibre pull-out, and improves energy dissipation under mechanical loading. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 6, the developed composite comprises a parenchyma-derived cellular phase, an interconnected network of exfoliated cellulose-rich fibres embedded within a continuous bio-based polymer matrix, and a woven cotton twill backing layer that provides additional dimensional stability and improved resistance to tear propagation. Together, these structural features produce a hierarchical bio-composite architecture that provides a mechanistic explanation for the enhanced toughness, flexibility, and tear resistance observed in the developed material.

Schematic illustration of the hierarchical microstructure of the Agave cantala-based bio-composite.

Structure–property relationships of the fibre-reinforced composite

The combined SEM, FTIR and TGA analyses demonstrate the transformation of Agave cantala leaf biomass into a fibre-reinforced architecture suitable for bio-based leather-like composites with competitive mechanical properties. SEM observations (Fig. 2) show that native Agave fibres initially exist as coarse, densely cemented technical bundles with a multicellular organization and tightly packed lamellar structure. Following oxidative-alkaline modification, disruption of lignin- and hemicellulose-rich middle lamella regions promotes partial delamination of these bundles, as evidenced by changes in chemical functionality (Fig. 7) and thermal degradation behavior (Fig. 8 and 9). Subsequent mechanical exfoliation results in extensive fibrillation, yielding a fine, randomly oriented network of flexible cellulose-rich ribbons. In parallel, the non-fibrous parenchymatous residue forms a porous cellular morphology that acts as a compliant matrix precursor with high surface area and energy-dissipation capability. When combined with a bio-based polymer binder and supported by a twill-woven cotton substrate, these fibrous and cellular fractions form a densely interlocked three-dimensional composite architecture. This structure supports stress distribution, crack deflection and tear resistance, while remaining chemically and structurally distinct from the collagen network of natural leather.34

Thermal behavior of untreated and modified Agave cantala fractions

Fig. 8 (DTG) and Fig. 9 (TGA) present the thermal degradation behavior of four systems: untreated fibre, treated fibre, treated non-fibrous fraction, and treated succulent biomass (combined fibrous and non-fibrous fractions). All samples exhibit multi-step degradation characteristic of lignocellulosic materials; however, the position and intensity of degradation peaks depend strongly on oxidative-alkaline modification and the relative proportions of fibrous versus amorphous plant components.

The untreated fibre exhibits three distinct weight-loss regions. The initial loss below 150 °C, corresponds to moisture evaporation (9 wt%), associated with free and loosely bound water, consistent with the broad O–H stretching band at 3342 cm−1 observed in the FTIR spectrum, indicating a hydrogen bonded cellulose water network. The second region, with a DTG peak at 293 °C and 14% weight loss, is attributed to degradation of amorphous polysaccharides, including hemicellulose, pectin and residual extractives in the fibre.

This behavior correlates with the strong carbonyl band at 1735 cm−1 in FTIR, assigned to acetyl and uronic ester groups of hemicellulose and pectic substances. The third region, marked by a DTG peak at 355 °C represent the decomposition of crystalline cellulose. The second DTG peak at 355 °C marks the main decomposition of crystalline cellulose, accounting for approximately 64% weight loss. Above 400 °C, gradual weight loss is dominated by lignin decomposition and char rearrangement, yielding a residual weight of ≈13%, consistent with the presence of lignin-associated aromatic bands near 1607 cm−1 in FTIR spectrum. Overall the broad and overlapping DTG profile of the untreated fibre confirms the heterogeneous nature of the lignocellulosic fibre.40

In contrast, the treated fibre exhibits a significantly simplified thermal profile, with a single dominant DTG peak centered at ∼344 °C. The suppression of the low temperature peak indicates substantial removal of amorphous polysaccharides during oxidative alkaline treatment. The concentration of the main mass loss (≈67 wt%) within a narrower temperature range reflects increased chemical uniformity and cellulose enrichment. Moisture loss remains comparable to the untreated fibre (≈10 wt%), indicating minimal change in hygroscopic behavior. Notably, the treated fibre yields a higher char residue at 1000 °C, suggesting the formation of thermally stable condensed structures.41 These thermal changes directly correlate with FTIR observations, including the disappearance of the 1735 cm−1 carbonyl band and reduced intensity of the aromatic lignin peak at 1607 cm−1, alongside intensified cellulose related C–O–C and C–O stretching bands at 1240 and 1030 cm−1. Together, these results confirm selective removal of hemicellulose and partial delignification, resulting in enhanced thermal stability and suitability for thermally assisted composite processing.42

The treated non fibrous fraction (amorphous parenchymatous matrix) shows a distinctly different thermal profile with two clear stages. Onset 200–220 °C, DTG peak at 306 °C (26 wt% loss) stage arises from decomposition of modified hemicellulose, pectin and residual low molecular weight polysaccharides that dominate the non fibrous matrix.43 Second DTG peak was observed at 460 °C with an additional 57 wt% loss. The high temperature event is attributed to rearranged lignin rich domains and condensed carbohydrate residues created during oxidative treatment. The higher decomposition temperature indicates formation of more thermally stable crosslinked structures.

The relatively smaller initial mass loss due to moisture (6 wt%) and the presence of a pronounced high temperature peak show that the non-fibrous fraction behaves as a thermally resilient, char forming phase, which can contribute to dimensional stability and fire resistance of the composite. FTIR spectra of the treated succulent non fibrous show reduced hemicellulose carbonyl peaks but still display features of pectic and lignin derived structures (bands around 1719 cm−1 and 1595 cm−1), which can undergo condensation at high temperature explaining the delayed second degradation step during the thermal analysis.44

The treated succulent biomass, containing both the fibrillated cellulose-rich fibrous fraction and the amorphous non fibrous matrix, displays combined characteristics. The initial moisture loss is similar (10 wt%). The first DTG peak at 310 °C corresponds mainly to degradation of amorphous polysaccharides and partially modified hemicellulose in the matrix, together with less ordered cellulose.43,44 A second broad peak at 457 °C reflects decomposition of more ordered cellulose regions and condensed lignin or phenolic rich char, inherited from the non-fibrous fraction. The staged mass losses in the TGA plot (48% then 29%) indicate that the treated succulent behaves as a hierarchical dual phase system, a moderately labile polysaccharide component that decomposes first, and a thermally robust char forming component that persists to higher temperatures.

FTIR analysis of the treated succulent shows a broad and intense O–H stretching band at 3344 cm−1, confirming the retention of a hydrogen-bonded cellulose and hemicellulose network. Although the intensities of carbonyl (CO) and aromatic skeletal vibrations decreased, distinct peaks at 1719 cm−1 and 1595 cm−1 were still evident. This indicates the presence of residual pectic substances and condensed phenolic/lignin-derived structures within the treated biomass. When considered together with the TGA results, these findings demonstrate that the oxidative-alkaline treatment did not fully remove non-cellulosic components to yield a purely cellulosic structure. Rather, the process generated a partially delignified, chemically activated cellulose-rich network stabilized by a thermally resilient amorphous matrix. This hybrid structural composition is advantageous for producing an entangled plant-fibre-reinforced composite, but no molecular-level equivalence to collagen is implied.

According to the results obtained from thermal analysis, there are major implications for composite design and performance. Considering the thermal processing window for the composite preparation, all treated systems remain thermally stable up to 250–270 °C, and the major decomposition of the modified succulents occurs above 300 °C. This is well above the temperatures used for drying and mild thermal curing of the composite (≤120 °C), ensuring that no significant thermal damage occurs during processing.

Char formation and fire behavior of the material suggest that the increased char residue in the treated fibre and the high-temperature decomposition of the non-fibrous fraction may improve thermal shielding and dimensional stability compared with the raw fibre. The thermally stable amorphous matrix can act as a protective char-forming phase within the composite.

From the FTIR and thermal analyses, a clear structure–property relationship can be established. FTIR confirms delignification, reduction of hemicellulose content and increased cellulose accessibility, while TGA demonstrates that these chemical modifications yield a more homogeneous cellulose-dominated fibrous fraction together with a char-forming non-fibrous fraction. When combined, the treated succulent biomass forms a fibrillated reinforcement phase embedded within a thermally stable matrix phase, supporting the observed tear resistance of the leather-like composite.45

These TGA results also enable comparison with other plant-based leather alternatives in terms of thermal stability. The main degradation peaks for the treated succulent (≈310 °C and ≈457 °C) fall within the upper range reported for pineapple leaf or cactus-based systems, demonstrating comparable or superior thermal resistance.46,47 This supports the suitability of Agave cantala succulent as a robust and thermally stable feedstock for vegan leather.

Proposed reaction pathway and mechanism in composite preparation

The successful formation of the vegan leather composite is governed by a sequence of interfacial and molecular level interactions among the chemically modified succulent biomass, and its cellulose microfibrils (A), the thickening agent containing carboxylate and hydroxyl functional groups (B), the natural polymer dispersion (C), and the hydroxyl rich plasticizer (D). Each component contributes synergistically to establishment of a stable multi-phase network, enabling uniform dispersion and effective reinforcement within the final composite. The proposed reaction pathway is schematically illustrated in Fig. 10.

Proposed reaction pathway and network formation mechanism during preparation of the bio based composite.

Stage 1 – hydration and activation of thickening agent (A & B)

Upon dispersion in an aqueous medium, the thickening agent (B) ionizes and adopts an extended, into a flexible polyelectrolyte conformation due to the presence of –COO− and –OH functional groups. These groups facilitate hydrogen bonding and ionic coordination with exposed hydroxyl groups on cellulose microfibrils (A) generated during the oxidative–alkaline modification step:A–OH⋯HO–B(hydrogen bonding)A–OH⋯OOC–B(ionic coordination)

These interactions sterically separate the cellulose fibrils, suppressing agglomeration and the formation of a stable colloidal hydrogel, in which the fibrous and non fibrous fractions of the treated succulent biomass are uniformly dispersed.

Stage 2 – interfacial association with natural polymer (C)

The natural polymer (C), present as colloidal particles stabilized at alkaline conditions, interacts with the polyelectrolyte wrapped cellulose fibrils through protein or phospholipid mediated hydrogen bonding, amphiphilic entanglement, and steric interpenetration. Although both the thickening agent (B) and polymer (C) carry net negative charges, partial adsorption of the polymer chains of (B) onto the particle surface enables the formation of a soft interphase layer. This interphase acts as a compatibilizing bridge between the hydrophilic cellulose phase and the relatively hydrophobic natural polymer phase (C), leading to the development of a dynamic three phase interfacial network rather than discrete phase separation.

Stage 3 – plasticizer interaction (D)

The hydroxyl rich plasticizer (D) subsequently diffuses into both polymer phases, reducing intermolecular friction among cellulose chain bundles while preserving mobility within the amorphous domains of polymer (C). These effects impart the flexibility and compliance required for leather like drape and handle. Plasticizer interactions are governed by multiple reversible hydrogen bonding exchanges, which mitigate brittleness and enhance elastic response under tensile and tear loading.

Stage 4 – network formation during drying and consolidation

During drying or coagulation, particles of natural polymer (C) coalesce, entrapping the uniformly dispersed cellulose fibrils (A) and the surrounding thickening agent (B). The plasticizer (D) remains interpenetrated within the evolving polymer network, forming hydrogen bonded interactions with both phases and thereby reducing internal stresses while improving ductility and toughness.

Composite microstructure development

The final dried composite comprises of:

• A fibrillated cellulose reinforcement phase (A) providing load bearing capacity,

• A carboxylate rich interphase layer (B) enhancing interfacial compatibility and reducing filler pull out,

• A continuous natural polymer matrix (C) imparting flexibility and cohesion,

• A plasticized network enhanced by (D) improving toughness and tear resistance.

This fibre-reinforced composite architecture consists of a fibrillated cellulose-rich phase embedded within a bio-based polymeric matrix and plasticized interphase. This network provides a structural basis for the observed tear resistance and deformability of the reinforced and non-reinforced composite sheets, without implying direct replication of collagen fibre bundles or the proteoglycan matrix of natural leather.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high-value utilization pathway for the underexploited succulent fraction of Agave cantala leaf waste through the development of a bio-based leather-like composite with competitive mechanical performance. Oxidative-alkaline treatment followed by mechanical fibrillation generates cellulose-rich micro-ribbons embedded within a honeycomb-like parenchymatous matrix, producing an interlocked plant-fibre-reinforced architecture. The reinforced composite exhibits tear resistance exceeding that of several commercial plant-based alternatives, supported by efficient load transfer arising from improved fibre-matrix interfacial compatibility. These structure–property relationships are supported by SEM, FTIR and TGA analyses. The use of low-temperature processing, bio-based constituents and agricultural residue valorization supports the circular-materials potential of the process, although full life cycle assessment and long-term durability evaluation remain outside the scope of the present study. Overall, this work establishes the technical feasibility and scalability of Agave cantala-based leather-like composites as environmentally responsible materials for selected consumer and industrial applications.
