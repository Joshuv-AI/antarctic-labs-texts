Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists for weight loss in end-stage heart failure patients considered for heart transplantation
Authors: Sambavan Jeyakumar, Ragavi Jeyakumar, Hunter Eckford, Desiree Robson, Liarna Honeysett, Lisa Raven, Ricardo Deveza, Andrew Jabbour, Eugene Kotlyar, Anne Keogh, Jerry R. Greenfield, Peter Macdonald, Christopher Hayward, Kavitha Muthiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe obesity is a relative exclusion criterion for heart transplantation. This study assessed glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) for weight loss as a bridge to heart transplantation candidacy. A retrospective study of end-stage heart failure (ESHF) outpatients commenced on semaglutide compared demographic, metabolic, and transplant listing data pre- and post-treatment. Nine patients (median pre-GLP-1 RA body mass index [BMI]: 35.9 kg/m2 [IQR 1.3]) received semaglutide for a median of 4 months (IQR 9). Seven patients (78%) had an initial BMI >35 kg/m2; an exclusion criterion for transplantation. Post-treatment, median BMI decreased to 32.2 kg/m2 (IQR 4.1), representing a 5.0 kg (IQR 6.3) median weight loss. All patients were subsequently transplant-listed, and 7 (78%) patients underwent transplantation. No significant adverse effects were reported. These preliminary findings suggest semaglutide may facilitate heart transplantation eligibility in ESHF patients with severe obesity, warranting larger studies to guide GLP-1 RA use in transplant protocols.

Background

The current gold standard treatment for end-stage heart failure (ESHF) is orthotopic heart transplantation. However, as transplantation is an intensive procedure, comorbid predictors such as metabolic parameters and frailty have been protocolized in international guidelines to aid in the identification of suitable transplant candidates.1

According to the 2016 International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation listing criteria,2 severe obesity is a major exclusion criterion for heart transplantation, with a body mass index (BMI) less than 35 kg/m2 recommended prior to cardiac transplant listing. A pre-transplant BMI greater than 35 kg/m2 is associated with an elevated risk of primary graft dysfunction, rejection, and post-transplant mortality.3, 4 Consequently, there is a growing impetus to study weight-loss agents as a potential bridge to candidacy to reduce ESHF morbidity and mortality and improve transplantation outcomes. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), such as semaglutide, produce significant, sustained weight loss.5, 6 However, there are limited studies to support its use as a bridge to candidacy for heart transplantation. With International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation criteria limiting transplant listing for high-BMI patients, this study aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of semaglutide as a weight-loss agent as a bridge to candidacy for heart transplantation.

Methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted with approval from the St. Vincent’s Hospital Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (2025/ETH00093). Electronic records of ESHF patients under consideration for heart transplantation whose sole contraindication to transplantation was obesity were analyzed between June 2022 and December 2024. Included patients included received 0.25-1 mg subcutaneous semaglutide weekly. Demographic data, metabolic parameters, cardiac function, and transplant listing status was evaluated and compared at the beginning of the study, “pre-semaglutide use,” and at the end of the study or at the time of transplantation, “post-semaglutide use.” Operation and anesthetic reports, intensive care unit notes, and medical records were reviewed for any peri-operative complications. Descriptive analysis was conducted using jamovi (2.6) to observe changes in central tendencies following semaglutide use.

Results

Nine ESHF patients (mean age: 53.8 ± 9.5 years; mean left ventricular ejection fraction, LVEF: 20.6% ± 3.9%) had severe obesity with a median “pre-semaglutide use” BMI of 35.9 kg/m2 (IQR 1.3) (Table 1). Four patients were previously implanted with a HeartMate 3 LVAD, and 44% had known type 2 diabetes mellitus. Seven (78%) patients had an initial WHO obesity class of II or III, with a BMI > 35 kg/m2, a relative exclusion criterion for heart transplantation (Table 2). Patients were commenced on 0.25-1 mg subcutaneous semaglutide weekly for a median duration of 4 months (IQR 9). At follow-up, the patients had a median “post-semaglutide use” BMI of 32.2 kg/m2 (IQR 4.6) with pairwise comparisons depicting a median weight decrease of 5.0 kg (IQR 6.3) (Figure 1). Discussion

We report the safety and efficacy of semaglutide as a weight loss agent to bridge ESHF to transplant candidacy, where transplant would otherwise be precluded due to severe obesity. All recruited patients experienced weight loss in the study period and were transplant-listed. This is the first study to our knowledge that shows the efficacy of semaglutide in enabling transplant candidacy.

In our cohort, 2 heart transplant recipients who continued semaglutide therapy postoperatively maintained significant weight loss over 14 and 22 months, respectively. However, due to the small sample size and confounding factors in those who discontinued treatment, it is not possible to determine whether ceasing semaglutide post-transplant has any consistent or meaningful effect on weight outcomes. Interestingly, semaglutide use in our cohort may have helped offset steroid-related weight gain in the early post-transplant period; however, further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to clarify the role of ongoing GLP-1RA therapy in this setting.

One of the primary safety considerations of GLP-1 RAs is an increased risk of bronchoaspiration secondary to reduced peristalsis and gastric emptying in the peri-operative period.7 The American Society of Anesthesiologists recommends that patients on weekly dosing withhold the medication at least 1 week prior to the procedure.8 As heart transplantation is an emergency procedure, cessation was not possible for the patients in our study. However, no peri-operative complications associated with semaglutide use were reported for any of the patients during transplantation. In future studies, it would be imperative to monitor for longer-term post-operative complications, including effects on skeletal muscle mass and function. Indeed, with similar outcomes and weight loss data seen in renal and lung transplant cohorts,9, 10 larger studies are essential to provide clinical support for GLP-1 RA use in heart transplantation populations.

Our findings are inherently limited by the single-center, retrospective nature of the study design and the exclusive inclusion of a small sample size of male patients. A larger, multi-center cohort study that is more reflective of the ESHF population would improve external validity. The maximum dose of semaglutide used (1 mg) was reflective of the available medication in Australia. Future research including higher-dose semaglutide and other combination therapies such as tirzepatide is warranted. Additionally, semaglutide use is limited by inequitable access due to its high out-of-pocket cost. Without subsidization through government initiatives or public health agencies, widespread use remains limited, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. The use of similar, albeit less effective, analogs such as liraglutide may potentially address this gap.

Conclusion

This preliminary study indicates a potential role for semaglutide as a safe bridging therapy for ESHF patients with severe obesity to enable heart transplantation candidacy. Larger studies are needed to further inform the safe and effective use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in transplant guidelines.
