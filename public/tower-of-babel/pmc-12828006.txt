Ultrabroadband hybridized-resonance terahertz metamaterial absorber in a multilayer composite architecture
Authors: Yingying Niu, Nianlong Ding, Meng Chen, Qing Xu, Xiangjie Qi, Xueying Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the challenge of extending terahertz absorbers toward the low-frequency end without sacrificing broadband efficiency or polarization/angle insensitivity, we introduce a hybridized-resonance architecture that couples in-plane resonances, longitudinal Fabry–Pérot (FP) cavity modes, and inter-cell air-gap waveguide modes.This coupling pushes the lower band edge to 0.20 THz while preserving high absorptance and incident-angle robustness, a capability valuable for applications spanning 0.1–2 THz. By exploiting controlled modal coupling, the structure promotes broadband impedance matching and controlled dissipation, thereby mitigating sensitivity to polarization and incidence. Full-wave simulations predict an average absorptance of 95% across 0.20–2.54 THz, a peak absorptance ≈ 100%, and a fractional bandwidth of ≈ 328% (normalized to the geometric center frequency), corresponding to a bandwidth ratio of 12.7:1 (i.e., 1270%). High absorptance is retained for linear polarization angles from 0° to 90° and for incidence angles up to 30°, making the device attractive for terahertz sensing and electromagnetic countermeasure applications and providing a practical blueprint for future absorber designs.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Terahertz (THz; 0.1–10 THz) radiation lies between microwaves and the infrared. Owing to its low photon energy and high transmittance through many dielectrics (e.g., polymers) and low-water-content biological tissues, it has attracted sustained interest for security screening, biomedicine, communications, and archaeology1–4. Within these applications, the 0.1–1 THz sub-band is particularly important and was highlighted in the 2021 Next-Generation 6G Wireless Communications white paper for joint communication–sensing5. However, intrinsic material losses, impedance mismatch, and the limited tunability of natural media hinder the simultaneous achievement of thin form factors, wide bandwidth, and high absorptance in this range6–8. Artificial electromagnetic metamaterials—periodic arrays of subwavelength unit cells whose effective permittivity and permeability are tailorable via geometry and material choice—enable engineered resonance spectra9,10. Leveraging these properties, metamaterial absorbers combine impedance matching with controlled dissipation within subwavelength thicknesses and have become a key platform for high-performance THz absorption and modulation11.

Since Landy et al. introduced the metal–insulator–metal (MIM) perfect absorber12, terahertz metamaterial absorbers have been intensively explored, driven by applications such as radar cross-section reduction, broad-spectrum imaging, and high-speed links that benefit from broadband absorption over 0.1–2 THz2,4. Two strategies have dominated. The first employs passive multi-resonance superposition—often reinforced by in-plane plasmonic coupling—to broaden the band. For example, a multilayer graphene–polyimide stack sustains > 90% absorptance from 0.86 to 3.54 THz13, and a patterned doped-silicon platform achieves > 95% from 0.79 to 4.115 THz14; however, these passbands commence well above the 0.1–0.5 THz region, leaving the low-frequency sub-band under-covered and sensitive to geometry–loss trade-offs. The second route leverages actively tunable media (e.g., graphene and VO₂). Under carrier modulation, graphene metasurfaces can realize high absorptance within 0.30–0.75 THz, but the response typically consists of multiple discrete peaks with comparatively narrow linewidths15. Likewise, thermally switchable VO₂ metasurfaces in the metallic state deliver > 90% absorptance across 0.398–1.356 THz, yet they require external thermal bias and operate in discrete on/off states rather than continuous tuning16; moreover, VO₂-based broadband absorbers often exhibit angle dependence in practice17. Taken together, although these strategies have substantially broadened absorption bandwidths, current designs still suffer pronounced attenuation in the 0.1–1 THz sub-band. Consequently, achieving a large fractional bandwidth (FBW, normalized to the geometric center frequency) while maintaining polarization- and angle-insensitive performance remains a central challenge for contemporary metamaterial absorbers, which motivates the present work18.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce a hybridized-resonance architecture that concurrently couples in-plane dipolar resonances, higher-order Fabry–Pérot (FP) cavity modes, and inter-cell air-gap waveguide modes. By co-optimizing spectral overlap and dissipation pathways, the design extends bandwidth while maximizing and stabilizing absorptance. Structurally, the absorber is a periodic superstructure comprising three patterned Ni layers separated by SiO₂, moderately doped Si, and heavily doped Si. The three Ni layers carry windmill, ring, and slotted-disk patterns to excite multiple in-plane resonances; the through-thickness refractive-index gradient supports higher-order FP modes; and subwavelength gaps between neighboring cells act as air-gap waveguides. Together, these mechanisms promote broadband impedance matching and efficient loss, yielding polarization- and angle-robust absorption. Full-wave simulations predict an average absorptance of 95% across 0.20–2.54 THz, a peak absorptance ≈ 100%, and a fractional bandwidth of ≈ 328% (bandwidth ratio 12.7:1), comparing favorably with representative metamaterial absorbers. Absorptance remains ≥ 90% for linear polarization angles from 0° to 90° and incidence angles up to 30°, with only minor bandwidth contraction. The proposed absorber thus combines ultrabroadband performance, polarization- and angle-robustness, and near-unity peak absorptance, offering a practical blueprint for future THz devices.

Structure design

We recast the metal–dielectric–metal (MDM) paradigm as a hybridized-resonance architecture for ultrabroadband THz absorption while retaining its inherent impedance-matching advantage19,20. Windmill, ring, and slotted-disk resonators are patterned on the upper, middle, and lower metallic layers, respectively, to excite multiple modes via strong interlayer coupling; the superposition of their spectra broadens the absorption band. The vertical alternation of metal and dielectric layers forms a graded-impedance transition that stepwise matches free space, improving energy coupling and dissipation. Subwavelength gaps between neighboring unit cells provide additional in-plane coupling pathways. Nickel is chosen as the metallic constituent because its intrinsic loss in the THz band exceeds that of noble metals, thereby affording stronger energy dissipation and superior broadband modulation21. The metal‑layer thickness serves as a design variable that, together with the multilayer dielectrics, tunes the overall equivalent impedance to achieve strong absorption without compromising bandwidth. The dielectric stack comprises SiO₂ followed by two silicon layers with distinct doping concentrations. SiO₂ (n ≈ 1.98, tan δ ≈ 0.02 within the target band22 provides a favorable impedance match to free space, suppresses interfacial reflections and promotes efficient energy coupling. Its low loss also supports local standing‑wave formation, which reinforces multistep interference and boosts absorption in the underlying doped silicon layers. For sample 2, the corresponding parameters are = 12.0, = 8.07 × 1014 rad/s, and = 3.39 × 10131/s. In the proposed structure, sample 1 serves as the primary absorbing layer, offering strong electromagnetic energy dissipation. Although sample 2 exhibits a negative real permittivity and thus quasi metallic behaviour, its relatively large imaginary permittivity still provides substantial loss. With appropriate geometric design and inter layer coupling, sample 2 supplements the low frequency absorption, thereby enhancing the overall broadband performance.

Based on the foregoing structural concept and material selection, a detailed unit-cell model of the absorber was established and simulated using a frequency-domain finite-element full-wave solver. Periodic boundary conditions were applied along the x- and y-directions, whereas an open (absorbing) boundary was imposed along the z-axis to emulate free-space propagation. Excitation was provided by a unit-cell (Floquet) port at normal incidence, with the mode count set to include all propagating diffraction orders across 0.20–2.54 THz; an adaptive tetrahedral mesh with a minimum edge length of 0.1 μm was used.

Practical suitability was assessed by continuously rotating the incident linear‑polarization angle from 0° (TE) to 90° (TM). As shown in Fig. 3(a), high absorptance is maintained over the full polarization range, underscoring robustness to arbitrary polarization states. Across polarization angles θ from 0° to 90°, the absorption remains at or above 0.90 over most of the band, except in the vicinity of a few isolated frequencies (notably around 0.20 and 1.16 THz). To make this dependence explicit, we selected six representative frequencies (0.20, 0.68, 1.16, 1.64, 2.12, and 2.47 THz) from Fig. 3(b) and plotted absorptance versus polarization angle. At 0.20 THz and 1.16 THz, absorption decreases as θ increases and drops sharply for θ greater than about 45°, indicating higher polarization sensitivity at these points. By contrast, the other four frequencies vary only slightly across 0° to 90°, supporting polarization insensitivity elsewhere. Overall, aside from localized sensitivity near those isolated frequencies, the design maintains stable polarization performance; further improvement at those points is expected from increasing structural symmetry. Because oblique incidence is important in practice, we next evaluated TE-polarized absorption for incident angles from 0° to 60° at a fixed polarization. The two-dimensional map in Fig. 4(a) shows absorption close to 0.90 across most of the spectrum even at large incident angles, demonstrating wide-angle stability. To further quantify the effect of incidence angle, we analyzed the same six representative frequencies used in Fig. 4(b) and compared their absorption at multiple incident angles. From the color‑map, frequency points near 0.20 THz and 1.16–1.64 THz were found to be angle‑sensitive: when the incidence angle reached 60°, their absorptance declined from 0.90 to ≈ 0.65 (0.20 THz) and 0.72 (1.16–1.64 THz), demonstrating pronounced directional dependence. This behaviour is attributed to impedance mismatch within these bands, which is exacerbated at oblique incidence, thereby increasing reflection and reducing absorption. By contrast, the remaining frequency points exhibit good angular stability, showing only minor absorptance fluctuations between 0° and 60°, which indicates that the structure is largely insensitive to the incidence direction across most operating bands.

In summary, under TE‑polarized excitation the absorber demonstrates wide‑angle absorption—particularly in the mid‑ and high‑frequency bands—and maintains strong directional stability; as a result, angle‑induced degradation is suppressed, underscoring its promise for terahertz applications. For the angle‑sensitive low‑frequency region, future work will focus on optimizing geometry and material parameters to improve oblique‑incidence impedance matching and thereby enhance overall angular adaptability.

By systematically varying the metal pattern, dielectric layer thickness and other variables, their effects on absorptance are quantified to elucidate how each parameter contributes to bandwidth extension, resonance tuning and structural optimization, thereby providing design guidance for subsequent performance tailoring. At 1.38 THz, the valley arises from coupling between the transverse air-gap waveguide mode and the longitudinal FP cavity. Changing modifies the air–dielectric fraction along , thereby fine-tuning the effective permittivity. Increasing to 255.5 μm widens the slots, lowers the permittivity, shortens the optical path, and closes the FP phase condition at a slightly higher frequency, yielding a modest blueshift. The reduced permittivity also moves the surface impedance toward 377 Ω, improving matching and deepening the valley. Conversely, decreasing raises the permittivity, lengthens the optical path, redshifts the resonance, and makes the valley shallower owing to poorer impedance matching.

At the 1.96 THz valley with µm, the -slot is narrowest, the fill factor highest, and the effective index along maximal; the air-gap waveguide mode therefore couples most strongly to the longitudinal FP cavity, the surface impedance approaches the free-space value of 377 Ω, and the reflection valley is deepest. Expanding to 225.5 μm and 255.5 μm relaxes slot confinement, lowers both index and capacitance, undercompensates the longitudinal phase, detunes impedance, and raises the valleys sequentially, while the resonance frequency drifts negligibly—evidence that the -direction air-gap-waveguide–cavity phase lock persists and that valley depth is controlled chiefly by the -dependent capacitance–impedance channel.

For the 2.47 THz valley, µm simultaneously satisfies the air-gap waveguide phase, air-gap waveguide impedance, and longitudinal cavity phase, producing the deepest absorption. Shifting the period disrupts this triple lock: reducing to 215.5 μm over-compensates phase but under-matches impedance, whereas increasing it to 255.5 μm under-compensates phase but over-matches impedance; both mismatches raise the valley. The resonance remains pinned at 2.47 THz, demonstrating that this higher-order air-gap-waveguide–cavity mode demands tight longitudinal–transverse phase synchronization, with only one tunable parameter.

Taken together, and cooperatively modulate the 0.2–2.5 THz spectrum through distinct mechanisms: tunes the air-gap waveguide phase and input impedance, making higher-order resonance frequency and depth highly sensitive to its variation, whereas adjusts impedance by altering the effective permittivity along and the slot capacitance. Their orthogonal coupling offers independent, complementary geometric degrees of freedom without changing cavity thickness, enabling precise control over the position and strength of ultrabroadband absorption peaks.

Conclusions

This work proposes and systematically evaluates a hybridized-resonance architecture for broadband THz metamaterial absorption. The design recasts the conventional metal–dielectric–metal (MDM) stack into three patterned Ni layers interleaved with three SiO₂ impedance-transition films and two doped-silicon dielectrics. Full-wave simulations predict A ≥ 0.90 across 0.20–2.54 THz (mean 95%), corresponding to FBW = 328% and a bandwidth-multiplication factor of 12.7, exceeding all surveyed designs. Absorptance peaks occur at 0.26, 0.81, 1.38, 1.96, and 2.47 THz and high absorptance is retained for linear polarization angles from 0° to 90° and for incidence angles up to 30°. Mechanistically, the ultrabroadband response arises from hybridized coupling among (i) successive in-plane resonances of the three geometric motifs, (ii) longitudinal Fabry–Pérot cavity modes supported by the multilayer dielectric stack (SiO₂ and doped-Si), and (iii) air-gap waveguide resonances introduced by subwavelength inter-cell slots. Systematic parameter sweeps show that the vertical cavity length, in-plane period, and pattern geometry provide complementary levers for spectral shaping, yielding a wide design margin for further optimization. Collectively, these results establish a generalizable hybridized-resonance architecture for ultrabroadband THz absorbers employing refractory metals (e.g., Ni), with relevance to high-sensitivity sensing, passive imaging, 6G near-field links, electromagnetic countermeasures, and broadband stealth.
