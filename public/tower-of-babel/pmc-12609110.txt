An ultrasensitive and modular platform to detect Siglec ligands and control immune cell function
Authors: Zeinab Jame-Chenarboo, Edward N. Schmidt, Madeline Crichton, Kei Takahashi-Yamashiro, Guilherme M. Lima, Liany Luna-Dulcey, Jaesoo Jung, Sabine Ivison, Chris D. St. Laurent, Jhon R. Enterina, Sung-Yao Lin, Susmita Sarkar, Maju Joe, Fahima Mozaneh, Reni John, Som G. Nanjappa, Stacy A. Malaker, Megan K. Levings, Jamey D. Marth, Ratmir Derda, Matthew S. Macauley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Siglecs are immunomodulatory receptors that regulate immune cell function. A fundamental challenge in studying Siglec-ligand interactions is the low affinity of Siglecs for their ligands. Inspired by how nature uses multivalency, we developed Siglec-liposomes as a highly multivalent and versatile platform for detecting Siglec glycan ligands in which recombinant Siglecs were conjugated to liposomes using the SpyCatcher-SpyTag system. Siglec-liposomes offer tunable multivalency and a modular assembly, enabling presentation of different Siglecs on the same liposome. Using Siglec-liposomes, we profiled Siglec ligands on human leukocytes, revealing distinct patterns of Siglec ligands. Moreover, Siglec-liposomes are in vivo compatible, where we demonstrated that Siglec-7–liposomes bind to the brain vasculature in a mucin domain–dependent manner. Given the abundance of Siglec ligands on T cells, we investigated whether Siglec-liposomes modulate T cell function and find that Siglec-7–liposomes increase T cell proliferation in an ST3Gal1-dependent and CD43-independent manner. Together, Siglec-liposomes are a versatile and sensitive tool for detecting Siglec ligands and immunomodulation.

INTRODUCTION

Sialic acid–binding immunoglobulin-type lectins (Siglecs) are a family of transmembrane receptors that regulate immune cell function through interactions with their glycan ligands (1). Siglec-ligand interactions play many important roles in human health and disease such as cancer (2). These interactions can take place with ligands on the same cell (cis) or another cell (trans). It is notable that interactions of Siglecs with their trans sialoglycan ligands occurs with high multivalency, which is essential to compensate for their inherent low monovalent affinity (3). The low affinity of Siglecs for their ligands makes it challenging to study Siglec-ligand interactions outside the context of a highly multivalent cell surface. Tools to leverage avidity of Siglecs are needed to study the biological roles of Siglecs outside the context of a lipid bilayer. Moreover, as Siglec family members are expressed together on immune cells (1), a modular platform to multiplex numerous Siglecs would more faithfully mimic how Siglecs function together in nature.

For over 30 years, Siglec-Fc chimeras have been the tool of choice to study Siglecs outside the context of a lipid bilayer, which have facilitated many important discoveries (4–8). Siglec-Fc proteins have also been extensively used for probing ligands on cancer cells, with many studies documenting the up-regulation of Siglec ligands on a wide variety of cancers (9). These findings have motivated innovated approaches to block Siglec-ligand interactions to enhance antitumor immunity (2, 3, 10). Siglec-Fc proteins are likewise useful in drilling deeper into the precise biochemical nature of Siglec ligands; recently, Siglec-Fc proteins have been instrumental in the discovery that carbohydrate sulfation enhances the binding of Siglec-3, -5, -7, -8, and -15 to ligands presented on glycan microarrays (11, 12) and cells (13–15).

Siglec-Fc proteins cannot be used in two-step methods such as traditional primary and secondary antibodies. Instead, Siglec-Fc proteins are typically precomplexed with αhuman immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibody, which eliminates a wash step and increases the valency from two to at least four. However, there are several limitations with this approach such as high background signal when staining primary cells and tissues due to the recognition of IgG adsorbed to the cells and tissues by the αhuman IgG secondary antibody. Moreover, precomplexed Siglec-Fc would not be suitable for in vivo studies because of the noncovalent assembly of the complex.

Previously, liposomes have been used to present Siglec ligands, which have been successful in probing Siglec-ligand interactions and modulating immune cell function (3, 16). For example, liposomes have recently been used to dissect Siglec-glycolipid interactions (6, 17), and regulate the proximity of Siglecs to other immune cell receptors (18, 19). Liposomes are in vivo compatible (20), enabling liposomes displaying Siglec ligands to be used in many applications for targeting Siglecs and modulating immunity (21, 22). We hypothesized that liposomes could also be used as a useful multivalent presentation platform for displaying Siglecs. Testing this hypothesis requires robust bioorthogonal chemistry to link the Siglec to liposome, ideally in a site-specific manner. Recently, we demonstrated that SpyCatcher (SC)–SpyTag (ST) (23) bioconjugation can successfully immobilize Siglec-7 on DNA-barcoded bacteriophages, resulting in constructs that can detect Siglec-7 ligands on cells (7). On the basis of a proven biocompatibility of liposomes in vivo, we reasoned that they could offer a more robust and sensitive platform for detecting Siglec-ligand interactions, particularly for in vivo studies.

Here, we leveraged SC-ST bioconjugation (23) to present Siglecs in a multivalent and modular manner from liposomes and demonstrated that the SC-ST system creates a tunable multivalent scaffold that can profile Siglec ligands on different cells and tissues in a sensitive and selective manner. Profiling Siglec ligands on regulatory T (Treg) cells revealed the higher levels of multiple Siglec ligands on Treg compared to conventional T (Tconv) cells. Moreover, Siglec-liposomes can be multiplexed to simultaneously detect different Siglec ligands. Siglec-liposomes can detect Siglec ligands in vivo, where we found that brain vasculature has abundant levels of mucin domain–containing glycoproteins that serve as ligands for Siglec-7. Last, we found an immunomodulatory role for Siglec-7, wherein Siglec-7–liposomes stimulated T cell proliferation in an ST3Gal1-dependent and CD43-independent manner.

RESULTS

Design, expression, and purification of Siglec-ST proteins

Aiming to use the SC-ST system (23) for efficient bioconjugation of Siglecs to a multivalent platform, we started from our first-generation Siglec-Fc construct (Fig. 1A) and incorporated an ST at two different locations (Fig. 1B). In version 1 (V1), the ST was placed between the Siglec and the Fc (Siglec-ST-Fc), and in V2, the ST was placed at the C terminus of the Fc (Siglec-Fc-ST). To test these constructs in liposomes, we initially focused on Siglec-7 because it expresses well (4) and shows consistent and reliable binding to cells in a mucin-type O-glycan–dependent manner (13–15, 24). Accordingly, we expressed V1 Siglec-7–ST-Fc and V2 Siglec-7–Fc-ST from Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and doubly purified them using the His6 and Strep-tag II affinity tags (fig. S1A). Moreover, we used tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease to turn V1 into monomeric Siglec-ST (fig. S1B).

Preparation of liposomes displaying SC

Aiming to develop multivalent Siglec-liposomes, we leveraged an S49C SC mutant (7) for site-specific conjugation to 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE)–polyethylene glycol (PEG)–maleimide (Fig. 1C), which we showed to be successful by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Fig. 1D). To create multivalent SC-liposomes, we used the Doxil formulation (25) and substituted a portion of the 5% PEG-DSPE with SC-PEG-DSPE to tune the multivalency of SC (Fig. 1E). In addition, we added 0.1% AF647-PEG-DSPE to the liposomal formulation for detection by flow cytometry. We conjugated SC-liposomes with either V1 monomers or V2 dimers of Siglec-7 and verified the conjugation of V2 Siglec-Fc–ST to SC protein and SC-liposomes by SDS-PAGE (fig. S2, A and B). We also expressed and conjugated R124A Siglec-7, which cannot interact with its sialic acid–containing glycan ligands (4, 13). We quantified the number of V2 Siglec proteins on 0.33 mol % SC-liposomes using a standard curve generated from SC conjugated to V2 Siglec, which revealed that ~160 copies of V2 Siglec are conjugated to each liposome (fig. S2, C to F). In addition, we used dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure the size distribution and zeta potential for naked liposome, 0.33 mol % SC-liposome, and 0.33 mol % SC-liposome conjugated to V2 Siglec-7, which revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 133 ± 8, 145 ± 1.5, and 149 ± 1 nm (fig. S2, G to I) and a zeta potential of −11 ± 0.6, −8.7 ± 0.7, and −8 ± 1.2 mV, respectively (fig. S2J). These results indicate a small increase in size and decrease in the surface charge upon SC incorporation and V2 Siglec-7 conjugation to liposomes.

To test the SC-liposomes conjugated with V1 Siglec-7 (Siglec-7–ST) or V2 Siglec-7 (Siglec-7–Fc–ST), we analyzed their binding to monocytic U937 cells by flow cytometry (Fig. 1F). Both wild-type (WT) versions of Siglec-7 displayed on the liposomes showed robust binding to U937 cells, with the V2 dimeric Siglec-7–liposomes showing significantly higher binding (twofold) than the V1 monomeric version. This observation is consistent with our previously reported enhanced ligand binding properties of multivalent V2 Siglec-7 on bacteriophage when compared to multivalent display of V1 Siglec-7 (7). This enhancement may be the effect of local clustering of glycan binding (V-set) domains, resulting from the dimeric nature of V2 Siglec-7. In either case, R124A Siglec-7 showed no binding to cells. To assess how the density of Siglec-7 on the liposomes affects binding, we titrated the amount of V2 Siglec-7 in two different ways: by varying the amount of SC displayed on the liposomes and saturating it with V2 Siglec-7 (fig. S3, A and B) or keeping the amount of SC displayed on the liposome constant and varying the amount of V2 Siglec-7 (Fig. 1G). For both titrations, we observed a sharp increase of 20- to 40-fold in binding when V2 Siglec-7 was increased from 0.01 to 0.03 mol %. Above 0.1 mol % and up to 0.33 mol % Siglec-Fc, there was a modest increase (twofold) in binding of V2 Siglec-7 to U937 cells. Compared to Siglec-7–Fc precomplexed with Strep-Tactin (4), Siglec-7–liposomes demonstrated an eightfold increase in detecting Siglec-7 ligands on the cell surface (Fig. 1H). Titrating the amount of AF647 on the Strep-Tactin and liposomes consistently demonstrated the superior detection sensitivity of Siglec-7–liposomes, ruling out differences in the amount of fluorophore (fig. S4, A and B). We also confirmed that V2 Siglec-1– and V2 Siglec-2–liposomes showed enhanced ligand detection on U937 cells compared to using Strep-Tactin as a precomplexing agent, confirming the compatibility of liposomes with other Siglecs (Fig. 1, I and J). To measure the stability of SC-liposomes at 4°C, we assessed their size distribution by DLS and found that the size of SC-liposomes largely remained unchanged and fluctuated modestly over 30 days (fig. S4, C to L). We also measured Siglec-7–liposomes binding to U937 cells at 4-day intervals over 30 days, which showed only moderate day-to-day variation (fig. S4, C to M).

Enhancing sensitivity of the platform using Siglecs expressed from CMAS−/− CHO cells

As Siglecs are naturally masked by cis ligands on cells (6), we hypothesized that cis masking may also be in effect for Siglec-liposomes and that eliminating the sialic acid on the V2 Siglec-Fc–ST proteins would enhance the ability of Siglec-liposomes to detect cellular ligands. To test this hypothesis, we expressed V2 Siglec-7 from genetically engineered cytidine 5′-monophosphate–sialic acid synthetase deficient (CMAS−/−) CHO cells. To compare the binding of V2 Siglec-7 expressed in CHO CMAS−/− with the V2 Siglec-7 expressed in CMAS+/+ CHO cells, we conjugated both versions to our optimized SC-liposome formulation (0.33 mol % SC-liposome) and tested their binding to U937 cells (Fig. 2A). V2 Siglec-7 expressed in CHO CMAS−/− showed higher binding to U937 cells compared to V2 Siglec-7 expressed in CMAS+/+ CHO cells. Accordingly, we expressed the entire human Siglec family as their V2 Siglec-Fc–ST proteins, along with an essential arginine mutants, from CMAS−/− CHO cells (fig. S5). We incorporated these proteins into SC-liposomes and tested for binding to cells by flow cytometry, which demonstrated that the sialic acid–deficient Siglec proteins showed enhanced binding compared to their counterparts with sialic acid, except Siglec-6 and -11 that did not bind U937 cells (Fig. 2, B to F, and fig. S6). None of the arginine-mutated versions of Siglecs showed binding to cells. To compare the sensitivity of Siglecs expressed in CMAS−/− CHO cells in SC-liposome with Strep-Tactin, we precomplexed all Siglecs expressed in CMAS−/− CHO cells with Strep-Tactin and conjugated them to SC-liposome and tested their binding to cells. All Siglecs showed a better binding to cells in SC-liposomes compared to the Strep-Tactin, while we observed no binding for the arginine-mutated constructs (fig. S7). As an added benefit, sialic acid–deficient Siglec-Fc proteins expressed at a two to four times higher yield (fig. S8, A to D). In addition to binding of Siglec-liposomes to cells, we investigated the ability of Siglec-liposomes to be internalized within cells. To do this, we incorporated 0.1% pHrodo-PEG-DSPE into our liposome formulation, in addition to the AF647-PEG-DSPE fluorophore and measured the uptake of Siglec-7-liposomes by U937 cells at 4° and 37°C over 120 min, as we have done previously with liposomes bearing Siglec ligands (6, 26). Signal from the pHrodo fluorophore incorporated in Siglec-7–liposomes increased over time at 37°C, whereas signal at 4°C remained unchanged, which is strongly indicative of internalization (fig. S8, E to G).

Using SC-liposomes to profile Siglec ligands on primary cells

Siglec-Fc proteins precomplexed with secondary αhuman IgG show high background binding to primary immune cells due to the high concentration of IgG (6 to 17 g/liter) present in blood (fig. S9) (27). Siglec-liposomes avoid the need for using αhuman IgG as secondary. Therefore, we used Siglec-liposomes to probe Siglec ligands on immune cells from human peripheral blood. To profile Siglec ligands, we performed flow cytometry to distinguish B cells (CD19+), CD4+ T cells (CD3+CD4+), CD8+ T cells (CD3+CD8+), mature natural killer (NK) cells (CD16+CD56dim), neutrophils (CD15+CD16+), and monocytes (CD14+) isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and initially compared the binding of Siglec-2 precomplexed with Strep-Tactin with Siglec-2–liposome (fig. S10). Siglec-2–liposomes showed a higher sensitivity for binding to all immune cells compared to Siglec-2 precomplexed with Strep-Tactin, while no binding was observed for R120A Siglec-2 in either format.

Accordingly, we analyzed Siglec ligands on immune cells isolated from fresh peripheral blood of four healthy individuals (Fig. 3A and figs. S11 to S16). Siglec-liposomes showed diverse and broad binding to immune cells, with several notable observations: Siglec-1, -2, -3, -4, -7, and -10 showed binding to all different immune cells, with the strongest binding of Siglec-1 to monocytes, Siglec-2 to B cells, Siglec-3 and Siglec-8 to monocytes and CD8+ T cells, Siglec-7 to T cells and NK cells, Siglec-9 to NK cells, and Siglec-10 to B cells (Fig. 3B). We observed no binding for arginine-mutated versions of Siglecs, except for Siglec-6, where WT and R122A Siglec-6 bound at almost the same level to the B cells and CD8+ T cells. This latter finding is consistent with our prior observation that the Arg122 is dispensable for recognizing sialylated ligands (6). We also tested the ability of several murine Siglec-liposomes (Siglec-E– and Siglec-15–liposomes) to bind immune cell subsets from mouse spleen, including B cells (B220+), CD4+ T cells (CD3+CD4+), CD8+ T cells (CD3+CD8+), NK cells (NK1.1+), and neutrophils (LY6G+). The results show that all immune cells have Siglec-E ligands, with the highest levels of ligands on T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils (fig. S17). Modest levels of Siglec-15 ligands were observed on neutrophils, B cells, and CD8+ T cells.

As some of Siglec-Fc proteins bound quite broadly to some immune cell subsets, we wondered whether this was due to heterogeneity of the Siglec-liposomes or heterogeneity of the cells. To address the former, we used streptavidin-beads with preferred ligand of Siglec-8 (α2-3–linked 6,6′-disulfated Gal-Glc biotin sialoside; fig. S18A) (13, 14). Monitoring the binding of Siglec-8–liposomes to beads by flow cytometry (fig. S18B), we find that with saturating amounts of the ligand on beads, binding of Siglec-8–liposomes was uniform and relatively broader binding was observed at lower loading of the beads with ligand (fig. S18, C and D). At saturating amounts of ligand on the beads, Siglec-8–liposomes showed a clear dose-dependent response (fig. S18E). These results suggest that it is not heterogeneity of the Siglec-liposomes but rather heterogeneity of the immune cells that leads to a broad binding profile. As T and B cells have many different subsets and generally have high levels of Siglec ligands, we further investigated Siglec ligands on T and B cell subsets, starting with T cells. We focused on Treg cells, a subset of CD4+ T cells, because of their critical roles in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing allergies, inflammation, and autoimmune diseases (28). Cancers exploit the suppressive function of Treg cells by expanding these regulatory cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby, inhibiting antitumor immunity (29). Notably, Treg cells exhibit distinct glycosylation patterns compared to Tconv cells (30, 31), and efforts have been made to identify ligands of some Siglecs such as Siglec-1 on Treg cells (32). To profile Siglec ligands on Treg cells and compared them with Tconv cells, we isolated these cells from fresh peripheral blood of five individuals and stained them with Siglec-liposomes (Fig. 3C). Specifically, we compared the levels of Siglec ligands on naïve (CD45RA+) and memory (CD45RA−) Treg and Tconv cells (Fig. 3, D and E, and fig. S19). Several Siglec-liposomes including Siglec-1, -8, -9, -10, and -15 showed increased binding to Treg cells compared to Tconv cells, and this difference was more pronounced in naïve (CD45A+) compared to memory (CD45A−) Treg cells (Fig. 3F). As Treg cells constitute ~2 to 5% of periphery T cells, clinically relevant numbers of Treg cells can be prepared for clinical and preclinical trials using ex vivo expanded Treg cells (28). Therefore, we were curious whether Siglec ligands on ex vivo expanded Treg cells showed similar patterns and profiled Siglec ligands on autologous ex vivo expanded Treg cells and Tconv from five donors (fig. S20). For the expanded Treg cells and Tconv, only Siglec-10–liposomes showed higher binding to Treg compared to Tconv cells. We also examined Siglec ligands on naïve (CD19+CD20+ and CD27−IgD+), memory isotype–unswitched (CD27+IgD+IgM+), and memory isotype–switched (CD27+IgD−IgM−) B cells (fig. S21, A to C). Overall, Siglec ligands were higher on both subsets of the memory B cells compared to naïve B cells, and levels were similar between the two subsets of memory B cells (fig. S21, D to O).

Multiplexing Siglec-liposomes

The modular process by which Siglec-liposomes are formulated makes them suited for multiplexing in two different ways. The first way is to present different Siglecs on different liposomes that each contain a unique fluorophore to simultaneously detect multiple Siglec ligands in a single experiment (Fig. 4A). Accordingly, we prepared SC-liposomes with AF488-, AF555-, or AF647-PEG-DSPE, and conjugated with V2 Siglec-1, V2 Siglec-7, and V2 Siglec-2, respectively. We chose these Siglecs because they have nonoverlapping ligands (15). After conjugation, we mixed all three Siglec-liposomes and tested for binding to U937 cells by flow cytometry. In parallel, we tested binding of each individual Siglec-liposome to U937 cells. We observed no significant difference in binding to cells for AF488–Siglec-1–liposomes compared to when they were multiplexed with the other two liposomes (Fig. 4B). Similarly, binding of AF555–Siglec-7–liposomes and AF647–Siglec-2–liposomes showed no significant difference in binding compared to the multiplexed liposomes (fig. S22, A and B). We also created different combinations of multiplexed liposomes consisting of WT and arginine-mutated Siglecs and tested their binding to U937 cells, which demonstrated that the presence of one Siglec does not interfere with the ability of another Siglec to engage their glycan ligands on cells (Fig. 4C and fig. S22, C to H).

The second way we multiplexed Siglec-liposomes is by displaying multiple Siglecs on the same liposome. To test this, we initially conjugated V2 Siglec-2 and V2 Siglec-7 on the same liposome and probe their binding to U937 cells via flow cytometry (Fig. 4D). Liposomes presenting V2 Siglec-2 and V2 Siglec-7 bound to cells in a positively cooperative manner. We prepared control multiplexed liposomes including liposomes displaying one Siglec (Siglec-2 or Siglec-7) or displaying WT and arginine-mutated of these Siglecs, and the results demonstrate that Siglec-liposomes bind to their cellular ligands in all different combinations of multiplexed liposomes without interfering with each other (fig. S23A). We also found that multiplexed V2 Siglec-1 and V2 Siglec-2 bound to their ligands on the cells in a positively cooperative manner (Fig. 4E and fig. S23B). These results demonstrate that Siglec-liposomes can be used as a multiplexed platform to detect different Siglec ligands on the cell simultaneously or display different Siglecs on one liposome to mimic a genuine immune cell.

In vivo compatibility of Siglec-liposomes

As the base liposome formulation used in our studies have a long in vivo circulatory half-life (25, 33), we felt that they would be well suited for detecting their ligands in mice. We tested Siglec-2– and Siglec-7–liposomes as they show strong binding to immune cells isolated from mouse splenocytes (fig. S24). As Siglecs expressed in CMAS−/− CHO cells will have terminal galactose residues that can be taken up by the liver (34), we used V2 Siglec-2 and V2 Siglec-7 expressed from CMAS+/+ CHO cells to avoid this issue. We formulated Siglec-7–liposomes on SC-liposomes at different densities (0.003, 0.01, 0.03, and 0.1 mol %) and injected these into mice intravenously (fig. S25). After 1 hour, we euthanized the mice and assessed binding of Siglec-7–liposomes to different immune cells in splenocytes by flow cytometry. Consistent with our in vitro staining of mouse immune cells with Siglec-7–liposomes, Siglec-7-liposomes showed in vivo binding to T cells in a manner that was dependent on the density of Siglec-7 on the SC-liposomes.

We administered two concentrations of Siglec-7–liposomes (Fig. 5A and fig. S26) or Siglec-2–liposomes (Fig. 5B and fig. S26), with 0.1 mol % V2 Siglec, to mice. We isolated mouse splenocytes and assessed the binding of Siglec-liposomes to different immune cells including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and neutrophils by flow cytometry. WT Siglec-7– and WT Siglec-2–liposomes showed substantial binding to T cells and B cells, respectively, at both 1.3 and 4 mM total lipid concentrations (9.8 × 1015 and 3 × 1016 particles/liter, respectively). In contrast, we observed no binding for R124A Siglec-7– or R120A Siglec-2–liposomes. We also assessed Siglec-7–liposomes binding to T cells in the spleen through immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy, which demonstrated clear colocalization with CD3+ T cells (Fig. 5C and fig. S27).

In addition, we investigated in vivo binding of multiplexed liposomes, where we administered multiplexed Siglec-2–liposomes (AF647) and Siglec-7–liposomes (AF488) into mice, and assessed their binding to immune cells from mouse splenocytes (Fig. 5D and fig. S28). Multiplexed WT Siglec-7– and WT Siglec-2–liposomes showed strong binding to T cells and B cells, respectively. Multiplexed R124A Siglec-7– and R120A Siglec-2–liposomes showed no binding to immune cells isolated from mouse splenocytes (Fig. 5D and fig. S28). This observation was consistent with our in vitro staining of immune cells from mouse splenocytes, where Siglec-7–and Siglec-2–liposomes showed strong binding to T cells and B cells, respectively, while arginine-mutated of these Siglecs showed no binding.

In addition to spleen, we also examined Siglec ligands in other tissues. Mice injected with Siglec-7–liposomes showed a pattern of fluorescent signal from the liposomes in the brain and kidney that resembled the vasculature. Therefore, we costained these tissues with αCD31 antibody to detect endothelial cells and found that CD31 staining overlapped with the signal from Siglec-7–liposomes, strongly suggesting that Siglec-7–liposomes bind to endothelial cells on blood vessels (Fig. 5E and figs. S29 and S30). In follow-up ex vivo staining of mouse brain slices, Siglec-7–Fc and CD31 signal also overlapped (Fig. 5F), while we observed no signal for R124A Siglec-7–Fc (fig. S31A). Mouse brain slices pretreated with the mucinase StcE (35) demonstrated that the ability of Siglec-7–Fc to recognize vasculature was abolished (Fig. 5G and fig. S31B). Together, these results show that Siglec-liposomes enable Siglecs to detect Siglec ligands in vivo in both single color and multiplexed formats. Notably, Siglec-7–liposomes detect mucin domain–containing glycoproteins within the brain vasculature.

Immunomodulation by Siglec-liposomes

Antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells) express Siglecs (1), and T cells express ligands for Siglec-1, -2, -3, -5, -7, -9, and -10, which suggest a functional role for Siglec-ligand interactions in cell-cell interactions between APCs and T cells. We felt that Siglec-liposomes would be a suitable tool to test this by determining whether they can modulate T cell proliferation. Therefore, we isolated human T cells from peripheral blood of healthy donors and activated them with αCD3/28 beads along with coculturing with Siglec-liposomes (Fig. 6A). As a control, T cells were cocultured with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), naked liposome, SC liposome, or the arginine-mutated versions of the Siglecs. We analyzed T cell proliferation by labeling CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with cell trace violet (CTV) fluorophore and measured fluorescence dilution by flow cytometry after 96 hours. Coculturing T cells with liposomes displaying Siglec-3, -5, -9, or -10 did not affect their proliferation compared to controls (fig. S32, A to F). In contrast, liposomes displaying Siglec-7 enhanced the proliferation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6, B and C). The effects of Siglec-1– and Siglec-2–liposomes on T cell proliferation were donor dependent, with some biological samples showing that Siglec-1– and Siglec-2–liposomes could stimulate T cell proliferation, but overall, these Siglec-liposomes did not increase proliferation relative to controls (fig. S32, G to J). Coculturing T cells with soluble Siglec-7–Fc increased the T cell proliferation although to a lesser extent than Siglec-7–liposomes (fig. S33).

To study the effect of Siglec-7–liposomes in increasing T cell proliferation in more detail, we investigated the effect of Siglec-7–liposomes in T cells isolated from mouse splenocytes and found that they had a similar stimulatory effect as human T cells (Fig. 6, D and E). ST3Gal1 is a major contributor to the biosynthesis of sialylated mucins, and mucin-type O-glycans are strongly implicated as ligands for Siglec-7 (15). Therefore, we tested the ability of Siglec-7–liposomes to stimulate proliferation in T cells from ST3Gal1−/− mice (36). To do this, we isolated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from WT and ST3Gal1−/− mice, cocultured them with WT or R124A Siglec-7–liposomes and assessed T cell proliferation. Compared to T cells from WT mice, T cells from ST3Gal1−/− mice displayed a hyperproliferation phenotype in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets (36), which was previously attributed to enhanced apoptosis of these cells. Therefore, we investigated T cell proliferation after 72 hours and found that Siglec-7–liposomes did not increase T cell proliferation in T cells from ST3Gal1−/− mice (Fig. 6, F and G). We also analyzed T cell proliferation in WT mouse CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after 96 hours and observed that the effect of Siglec-7–liposomes on T cell proliferation remained consistent between 72 and 96 hours (fig. S34, A and B). Comparing the viability of T cells after 72 and 96 hours of stimulation in WT and ST3Gal1−/− T cells using a live-dead stain, we found no significant differences between any of the conditions (fig. S34, C and D).

To test the binding of Siglec-7–liposomes to ST3Gal1−/− versus WT mice, we stained the T cells from WT and ST3Gal1−/− mice with Siglec-7–liposomes. We found that Siglec-7-liposomes bound to ST3Gal1−/− T cells 3-fold less to CD4+ T cells and 15-fold less to CD8+ T cells, suggesting that ST3Gal1 plays a major role in generating ligands for Siglec-7 in mouse T cells (Fig. 6, H and I). Nevertheless, it is interesting that there are ST3Gal1-independent Siglec-7 ligands, particularly on CD4+ T cells.

CD43 is a glycoprotein that has been demonstrated to be a carrier of ligands for Siglec-7 (24, 37–39). To test whether CD43 contributes to the immunostimulatory role we observed for Siglec-7–liposomes, we isolated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from WT and CD43−/− mice along with coculturing them with WT and R124A Siglec-7–liposomes. The T cell proliferation was analyzed after 96 hours (fig. S35, A to D). Analyzing T cell proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell from WT and CD43−/− mice demonstrated that WT Siglec-7–liposomes increased T cell proliferation in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets from both WT and CD43−/− mice, while no effect was observed for R124A Siglec-7-liposomes. We confirmed that Siglec-7–liposome binding was modestly decreased on CD43−/− T cells compared to T cells from WT mice (fig. S35, E and F). Overall, these results implicate an ST3Gal1-dependent and CD43-independent mechanism by which Siglec-7–liposomes enhance T cell proliferation.

DISCUSSION

Similar to other glycan-binding proteins, multivalency is a key factor in Siglec-glycan interactions (3, 40). Siglec-liposomes enable multivalency to be experimentally controlled, which we accomplished by robust site-specific conjugation using the SC-ST system (23). Highly multivalent presentation of Siglecs enabled greatly improved detection of Siglec ligands on cells and tissues. A key feature that helped unlock the full potential of Siglec-liposomes was the expression of recombinant Siglecs from CHO cells that lack sialic acid. Production of Siglec-Fc proteins from Lec2 CHO cells or treatment of Siglec-Fc proteins with neuraminidase is carried out in some laboratories to prevent undesired cis ligand interactions that might mask the ability of Siglec-Fc proteins to recognize trans ligands on cells (41). However, there is limited experimental evidence outside the context of a cell surface that such masking is at play. We provide clear evidence that sialic acid–deficient Siglec-liposomes are much more capable of binding trans ligands on cells, indicating that masking of Siglecs by cis ligands is present on a multivalent platform such as the liposomes.

Although Siglec-Fc proteins are dimeric, they require further multimerization to detect ligands on cells, with αhuman IgG precomplexing being the most common approach (3). Siglec-liposomes avoid the use of αhuman IgG and, therefore, avoids detection of adsorbed IgG on human samples. This feature enabled a detailed investigation of Siglec ligands on primary human immune cells. Each Siglec-liposome showed a unique pattern for binding to human immune cells. For example, we observed strong binding of Siglec-2–liposomes to B cells, Siglec-7–liposomes to T cells and NK cells, Siglec-9–liposomes to NK cells and monocytes, and Siglec-10–liposomes to B cells. Results with Siglec-9 and -10 were not observed in our previous study using precomplexed Strep-Tactin (4), likely reflecting the enhanced sensitivity of Siglec-liposomes. It is intriguing that in these examples, the cell types expressing high levels of specific Siglec ligands also typically express that Siglec at high levels (1). These results are consistent with the ability of cis ligands to regulate the function of Siglecs; for instance, masking of the Siglec to only allow for trans ligand interactions under the appropriate physiological circumstance, such as within an immunological synapse (42–45).

The ability of trans interactions between Siglecs and their ligands on other cells to regulate immunobiology is quickly gaining recognition as a major role for Siglecs (18, 37). For example, numerous lines of evidence have been put forward for Siglecs–inducing effects in T cells (37, 46, 47). Our results with Siglec-liposomes demonstrate that Siglec-7 engaging its ligands on T cells can stimulate T cell proliferation. It was previously demonstrated that Siglec-7–Fc inhibits T cell proliferation (37), which is the opposite of what we observed. Differences between the studies that could explain this are the enhanced multivalency of liposomes and the precise T cell stimulation protocol; the previous study used soluble αCD3/28 antibodies to activate T cells (37), while we activated T cells using αCD3/28 antibodies adsorbed to beads. Previous studies demonstrated that α2-3–linked sialic acids on mucin-type O-glycans serve as ligands for Siglec-7 (1, 15). While ST3Gal1−/− T cells did not completely abrogate Siglec-7 ligands, the T cell stimulatory effect of Siglec-7–liposomes was completely abolished, demonstrating that the increase in T cell stimulation by Siglec-7–liposomes is mediated by ligands created by ST3Gal1. It is interesting that ST3Gal1−/− CD8+ T cells were hyperproliferative, which has not been previously reported, but may be directly connected to the enhanced rate of apoptosis observed previously on ST3Gal1−/− CD8+ T cells (36). Consistent with previous work demonstrating that T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation abrogates the apoptotic signal induced in ST3Gal1−/− T cells (48), we observed no poststimulation significant differences in number of dead ST3Gal1−/− T cells compared to WT T cells. As ST3Gal1−/− T cells were hyperproliferative, it is possible that this phenotype may have hidden the stimulatory effect of Siglec-7–liposomes, although the significant decrease in Siglec-7 ligands on ST3Gal1−/− T cells suggests that loss of ligands was responsible for loss of the stimulatory effect. CD43 is a glycoprotein ligand for Siglec-7 on K562 CML (chronic myeloid leukemia) cells (24, 38), healthy human T cells (37), and chronic lymphocytic leukemia cancer cells (39). CD43−/− T cells displayed moderately attenuated Siglec-7–liposome binding, confirming the previous observations that CD43 is a carrier for Siglec-7 ligands. However, CD43−/− T cells are still susceptible to the stimulatory effect of Siglec-7–liposomes, demonstrating that other ST3Gal1-dependent ligands mediate this stimulatory effect. Aside from CD43, other Siglec-7 ligands have also been identified, such as CD162 and CD45 (37, 39).

While our results demonstrate that Siglec-liposomes can modulate T cell proliferation, the implications of these findings within the context of an immunological synapse between a T cell and APC (49) are complex because Siglec ligands and receptors that activate T cells (e.g., TCR) will be coengaged. One study found that Siglecs on APCs inhibit T cell responses (50). However, assessing the impact of Siglecs in the complex environment of an immunological synapse is not straightforward, and, in this regard, APCs express diverse and broad levels of Siglecs (1). For example, macrophages express Siglec-1 and -15; dendritic cells express Siglec-2, -3, -5, -7, -9, and -15; and B cells express Siglec-2 and -10. Our liposome platform is suited to exploring this in greater detail in future studies by combining Siglecs and molecules that activate T cells to coengage/cocluster these receptors on T cells. The implications of our work on Siglec-liposomes inducing T cell proliferation are also intriguing considering our observation that Treg cells express higher levels of Siglec ligands compared to Tconv cells, including Siglec-1, -8, -9, -10, and -15, and that roles for Siglecs engaging their ligands on Treg cells have been proposed (32). It was previously demonstrated that compared to Tconv cells, Treg cells have altered expression of N-glycans (51, 52), which is relevant because many Siglecs (Siglec-1, -9, -10, and -15) recognize N-glycans as their sialoglycan ligands (14, 15, 53). Consistent with our findings for Treg cells isolated from PBMCs, ex vivo–expanded Treg cells showed more ligands for Siglec-10; however, we did not observe an increase in binding of the other Siglecs in ex vivo–expanded Treg cells compared to Tconv cells. This difference may be because the ex vivo–expanded Treg and Tconv cells were derived from human thymus, which may differ from Treg and Tconv cells isolated from human blood (28). As tumor-associated macrophages express high levels of Siglec-10 (54, 55), our observation of higher levels of Siglec-10 ligands on Treg cells may have important implication in maintaining an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

A major advantage of Siglec-liposomes is their ability to be multiplexed, which we demonstrated in two ways: (i) mixing Siglec-liposomes with different fluorophores or (ii) creating liposomes with different Siglecs on the same liposome. The ability to study multiple Siglecs functioning together is intriguing in light of differences in glycan specificity, with some Siglecs recognizing a nonoverlapping set of sialoglycan ligands (1). For our multiplexing studies, we used Siglec-1–, Siglec-2–, and Siglec-7–liposomes because these Siglecs have nonoverlapping glycan specificity: Siglec-1 prefers α2-3–linked sialosides of many classes of glycans (56–58), Siglec-2 prefers α2-6–linked sialosides on N-glycans (42, 59), and Siglec-7 prefers sialylated mucin–type O-glycans (15, 37, 60). Our results demonstrated that binding of these Siglecs to their respective glycan ligands on cells is not competitive in both types of multiplexing. Copresenting Siglec-2 and Siglec-7 or Siglec-2 and Siglec-1 on one liposome showed positive cooperative binding. In principle, multiplexing of Siglec-liposomes with different fluorophores is limited by the availability of small-molecule fluorophores, although spectral flow cytometry makes it feasible to use many more fluorophores. An intriguing approach warranting future investigation is a genetic barcode associated with each liposome that enables a more direct comparison between binding of each Siglec to their ligands.

Most immune cells express multiple Siglecs, and an open question is how Siglecs function together to recognize trans ligands. For example, NK cells express Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 (61, 62), while B cells express Siglec-2 and Siglec-10 (42, 54, 55, 63, 64). Our modular liposome platform provides an opportunity to address this physiologically relevant question, which would not be possible to systematically address with previously developed approaches to precomplex and detect Siglec ligands. In the future, this multiplexing approach can be highly useful to investigate the functional impact on immune cells, like the immunostimulatory effect we observed on T cells for individual Siglecs.

Most precomplexed forms of Siglecs involve noncovalent assembly with αhuman IgG or Strep-Tactin, which are not suitable for in vivo applications. Recently, we demonstrated that Siglecs can be displayed on genetically encoded bacteriophage for in vivo applications (7). However, the extremely short circulatory half-life of bacteriophage (65) limits in vivo applications using this platform. Liposomes are commonly used for different in vivo applications such as mice immunization (63, 66), immunomodulation (63), and drug delivery in the laboratory mouse (67, 68). We demonstrated that Siglec-7–liposomes and Siglec-2–liposomes showed robust in vivo binding to T cells and B cells from mice, respectively. Moreover, we demonstrated that these two liposomes can be multiplexed with different fluorophores to detect their ligands on T and B cells within the same mouse. Recently, it was shown that brain vasculature has a dense array of mucin domain–containing glycoproteins (69). Our findings that Siglec-7–liposomes bind to endothelial cells in the brain, via glycoproteins within the mucin domains, are highly consistent with these findings. A potential application of these findings would be active delivery of a drug to the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Moreover, as engagement between Siglecs and their ligands on endothelial cells can induce trans signaling (70–72), another application for Siglec-7–liposome would be modulating the function of endothelial cells. We speculate that this could relate to a natural role for Siglec-7 in modulating the function of endothelial cells, which is particularly intriguing in light of diminished mucin domain–containing glycoproteins on the BBB in the context of neurodegeneration (69).

In summary, we have developed Siglec-liposomes as a multivalent, modular, and versatile platform that enables the detection and functional manipulation of Siglec ligands on cells. More importantly, Siglec-liposomes are highly sensitive and can be used to detect their glycan ligands in vivo. Moreover, owing to the modular assembling of Siglec-liposomes, multiplexing enables multiple Siglec ligands to be detected simultaneously. Having a complete set of human Siglecs with a genetically encoded ST will facilitate more innovative approaches in the future. While these recombinant Siglecs do not perfectly mimic the presentation of Siglecs within their native environment of the cell membrane, this discrepancy is not unique to our platform. In the future, it may be possible to create synthetic membranes with embedded full-length Siglecs that contain their natural transmembrane segment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal

All mice used in this study were on a C57BL/6J genetic background. WT B6J mice (the Jackson Laboratory) were bred in-house. The CD43−/− and ST3Gal1−/− mice were provided to us by S. G. Nanjappa (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, USA) and J. Marth (Sanford Burnham Prebys, USA), respectively. The animals used in this study were bred and maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions. All animal procedures described in the study were approved by the University of Alberta Animal Care committee in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines (#AUP00002467).

Experiments using human blood

All experiments were approved by the human research ethics board (REB Pro00092144) biomedical panel at the University of Alberta and the University of British Columbia (REB H17-01490). Donors used in this study aged between 20 and 50 years old, were nonsmokers, and were in good overall health.

Cell lines and cell culture

CHO cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 5% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine albumin (Gibco), penicillin (100 U/ml; Gibco), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml; Gibco). Human monocytic U937 cells (American Type Culture Collection) were grown in RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine albumin, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml). Cells were grown at 37°C in 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) incubator. To generate ex vivo–expanded Treg cells and Tconv cells, human thymuses were processed, and T cells were isolated, stimulated, and expanded as previously described (73, 74). The cells were cryopreserved 3 days after restimulation on day 11 of the expansion. Ex vivo–expanded Treg cells and Tconv cells were cultured in ImmunoCult-XF T Cell Expansion Medium (Stemcell Technologies) with hIL2 (100 IU/ml; Stemcell Technologies).

Cloning of V1 and V2 constructs

To make V1 (Siglec-ST-Fc) and V2 (Siglec-Fc-ST), two different plasmid backbones were generated. For the V1, two successive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) reactions were performed. The first PCR reaction was performed using primers P1 and P2 (table S1), in which the pcDNA-5 containing Fc, Strep Tag II and His-tag was used as a template. The PCR product size was validated using a 1% agarose gel and was excised from the gel. The PCR product was then purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The second PCR reaction was performed using primers P2 and P3 (table S1) and used the PCR product form the 1st PCR as a template. An 1% agarose gel was used to validate the size of the 2nd PCR. The PCR product was then excised and cleaned up as described above. For V2, one PCR reaction was performed to add the ST peptide to the C terminus of the Fc contract using primers P4 and P5 (table S1) and pcDNA-5 containing Fc, Strep Tag II, and His6-tag as a template. The size of the PCR product was validated by using a 1% agarose gel, and it was cleaned up as described above. In both V1 and V2, AgeI and Xmal restriction sites were added to 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. Both final PCR products for the V1 and V2 were treated with AgeI and Xmal restrictions enzymes (New England Biolabs) for 1 hour at 37°C. The double digested products were then run on a 1% agarose gel, and the gene was excised and cleaned up as described above. To insert V1 and V2 into pcDNA5, the plasmid was treated with AgeI restriction and phosphatase enzymes. V1 and V2 were then ligated into the pcDNA-5 and transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli DH5α (New England Biolabs). Colonies were then picked and grown in liquid culture [lysogeny broth plus ampicillin (100 μg/ml)] overnight in a shaking incubator at 37°C. The plasmids were then purified using the GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The successful incorporation of V1 and V2 were then confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing.

The pcDNA-5 plasmids containing genes of the WT and arginine mutant of SIGLEC1, CD22, CD33, MAG, SIGLEC5, SIGLEC6, SIGLEC7, SIGLEC8, SIGLEC9, SIGLEC10, SIGLEC11, and SIGLEC15 from the first generation of Siglec-Fc–Strep Tag II (4) were treated with NheI and AgeI restrictions enzymes (New England Biolabs) for 1 hour at 37°C to yield the Siglec genes containing 5′ NheI and 3′ AgeI restriction sites. The restriction digestion was confirmed by using a 1% agarose gel. The double-digested genes were then excised and purified as above. To incorporate the Siglec genes into pcDNA5 containing Fc and ST, the plasmid was digested with NheI and AgeI enzyme, ran on an 1% agarose gel and the double cut plasmid was excised from the gel. WT SIGLEC7 and R124A SIGLEC7 were then ligated into both pcDNA5-Fc-ST and pcDNA5-ST-Fc. The plasmids were then transformed, grown up, and purified as described above. The successful incorporation of SIGLEC7 (WT and R124A) was then confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing. All other abovementioned SIGLECs in both WT and arginine-mutated versions were ligated into pcDNA5-Fc-ST and transformed, grown up, purified, and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, as described above.

Generation of CMAS−/− CHO Flip-in cells using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing

Custom CRISPR RNA (crRNA) (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) was designed to target exon 5 of hamster CMAS (sequence = ATTGATGGATGTCTCACCAA). CHO Flip-In cells were seeded at 500,000 cells per well the day of transfection in a six-well tissue culture plate, in 1.3 ml of growth media [DMEM/F12 containing 5% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine albumin (Gibco), penicillin (100 U/ml; Gibco), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml; Gibco)]. For one well of a six-well plate, 20 pmol of Cas9 nuclease (IDT), 20 pmol of ATTO-647–labeled crRNA:tracrRNA (IDT) duplex, 8 μl of Cas9 Plus reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 16 μl of CRISPRMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 665 μl of Opti-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were incubated together for 15 min and then added to the cells. One day after transfection, cells were removed from the plate, washed, resuspended in 300 μl of cell sorting medium (PBS, 1% fetal bovine serum, and 1 mM EDTA), and stored on ice until sorting. Cells were sorted using a FacsMelody cell sorter (BD). The brightest 10% of cells stained with ATTO-647 were sorted as single cells into 96-well plates containing regular culture medium. Cells were grown for 2 weeks until colonies were large enough to be screened. For the screening, CMAS−/− clones were screened with diCBM40− and Peanut agglutinin (PNA+). The clones which were PNA+ and diCBM40− were then expanded. Relevant portion of the genome was amplified by PCR using P6 and P7 primers (table S1), and CRISPR−/− was validated by Sanger sequencing of the PCR product.

Stable Siglec-Fc transfection

WT or CMAS−/− CHO Flip-In cells were cultured as described above. To stably transfect recombinant Siglec-Fcs in CHO cells, 400,000 cells were seeded per well in a six-well cell culture dish. The following day, 2 μg of pcDNA-5–Siglec-Fc plasmid, 2 μg of pOG44 plasmid, and 4.7 μl of Lipofectamine plus reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to 500 μl of Opti-MEM media (Gibco) and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Next, 15.7 μl of Lipofectamine LTX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the mixture and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. During the incubation of the DNA mixture, the cells were washed with 1.5 ml of Opti-MEM media (Gibco). After 30 min, the DNA mixture was added to the cells and cells were left in the growing conditions described above. In the next day, 2 ml of DMEM/F12 media with the abovementioned supplements was added to cells. The cells were then selected by gradually increasing the Hygromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) concentration (from 0.5 to 1 mg/ml) for 2 weeks. The media for the cells were also replaced every other day.

Recombinant Siglec-Fc expression and purification

Cells were cultured in three 15-cm dishes. Once the cells reached confluency, they were distributed into 10 15-cm dishes and placed at 32°C incubators with 5% CO2. Ten days after the cells reached confluency, the medium was then harvested, put through a 0.22-μm filter, and stored at 4°C for later purification. The Siglec-Fcs were purified using a Ni2+–affinity chromatography (Cytiva) and Strep-Tactin affinity column (iba Lifescience). For the Ni2+–affinity chromatography, the column was first equilibrated with 20 CV (column volumes) of equilibrium buffer [20 mM sodium phosphate and 0.5 M NaCl (pH 7.4)]. Next, the supernatant was loaded on the column, followed by 20 CV of wash buffer [50 mM imidazole, 20 mM sodium phosphate, and 0.5 M NaCl (pH 7.4)]. The protein was then eluted using 20 CV of elution buffer [500 mM imidazole, 20 mM sodium phosphate, and 0.5 M NaCl (pH 7.4)] into fractions of 1 ml. Fractions were then combined and diluted 10× with buffer W [100 mM tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA (pH 8)]. The Strep-Tactin affinity column was equilibrated with 15 CV of buffer W, followed by loading the protein. Next, the column was washed with 15 CV of buffer W and the protein was eluted using 15 CV of elution buffer [100 mM tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 10 mM desthiobiotin (pH 8)]. Fractions were then combined and dialyzed against 1× PBS (pH 7) overnight. The following day, protein was concentrated using AMICON Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein’s size was validated using SDS-PAGE, and protein concentration was then measured using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The proteins were then either stored at 4°C or lyophilized.

Production of V1 construct

To yield the monomeric Siglec-ST, the purified protein was incubated with 10 M excess of TEV protease enzyme overnight at 4°C. To remove Fc and TEV, Ni2–affinity chromatography was used (Cytiva). The column was first equilibrated with 20 CV of equilibrium buffer [20 mM sodium phosphate and 0.5 M NaCl (pH 7.4)], and then the protein was loaded onto the column. The flowthrough of the column containing Siglec-7–ST was collected and dialyzed against 1× PBS (pH 7) overnight. The protein was then concentrated using AMICON Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein’s size was validated by SDS-PAGE, and the protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay. Protein was then stored at 4°C.

Expression and purification of S49C SC

S49C SC plasmid was transformed into chemically competent E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (New England Biolabs). The cells were then cultured overnight into 100 ml of liquid culture [lysogeny broth plus ampicillin (100 μg/ml)]. The following day, the culture was added to two 500 ml of liquid cultures [lysogeny broth plus ampicillin (100 μg/ml)] and incubated at 37°C, 220 rpm shaking until the optical density (OD) reached 0.6. Once the OD was ~0.6, the S49C SC expression was induced using a final concentration of 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 37°C, 220 rpm for 3 hours. Cells were then collected and centrifuged at 3400g for 20 min, 4°C, and the pellets were resuspended in binding buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole (pH 8)] with lysozyme (300 μg/ml) and incubated for 1 hour on ice. The cells were then sonicated using constant pulse for 15 s on and 45 s off on ice for 10 times. The cells were centrifuged for 35 min at 18,000g and at 4°C. The supernatant was then filtered and stored at 4°C for purification. For protein purification, a 5-ml Ni2+–affinity chromatography column (Cytiva) was equilibrated with 15 CV of binding buffer, and the supernatant was then loaded onto the column. The column was then washed with 100 ml of wash buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 60 mM imidazole (pH 8)]. The S49C SC protein was then eluted from the column using elution buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole (pH 8)] into 1-ml fractions. The fractions containing protein were then combined and the protein dialyzed against 1× PBS (pH 7) overnight at 4°C. The protein was then concentrated using AMICON Ultra Centrifugal Filters, and the size of the protein was validated by SDS-PAGE. The concentration of the protein was then validated using a BCA assay.

Expression and purification of StcE enzyme

StcE plasmid was transformed into chemically competent E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (New England Biolabs). The cells were then cultured overnight into 100 ml of liquid culture [lysogeny broth plus kanamycin (100 μg/ml)]. The following day, culture was added to 1 liter of liquid culture [lysogeny broth plus kanamycin (100 μg/ml)] incubated at 37°C, 220 rpm shaking until it reached OD 0.6. Once OD was ~0.6, the StcE expression was induced using a final concentration of 1 mM IPTG and incubated at 16°C, overnight. The following day, cells were centrifuged at 3000g for 35 min, at 4°C, and the pellets were collected and resuspended in binding buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole (pH 8)] with lysozyme (300 μg/ml) and incubated for 1 hour on ice. The cells were then sonicated using constant pulse for 15 s on and 45 s off on ice for 10 times. The cells were centrifuged for 35 min at 18,000g and at 4°C. The supernatant was then filtered and stored at 4°C for purification. A 5-ml Ni2–affinity chromatography column (Cytiva) was equilibrated with 15 CV of binding buffer; the supernatant was then loaded onto the column. The column was then washed with 100 ml of wash buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 60 mM imidazole (pH 8)]. The StcE protein was then eluted from the column using elution buffer [50 mM tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 200 mM imidazole (pH 8)] into 1-ml fractions. The fractions containing protein were then combined and the protein dialyzed against 1× PBS (pH 7) overnight at 4°C. The protein was then concentrated using AMICON Ultra Centrifugal Filters, and the size of the protein was validated by SDS-PAGE. The concentration of the protein was then validated using a BCA assay.

Conjugation of SC protein to DSPE-PEG (2000)–maleimide

To conjugate SC to DSPE-PEG-maleimide, 5 mg of purified SC was reduced by dithiothreitol overnight at 37°C shaking incubator. The reduced SC protein was run on a 1.0, 27-cm chromatography column (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 7371032) filled with Sephadex G-25 resin, fine (Cytiva, catalog no. 17-0032-01), which was equilibrated with 100 mM NaOAc buffer (pH ~ 5.5). Fractions containing SC proteins were combined, and the concentration was measured by Nanodrop and DSPE-PEG (2000)–maleimide (Avanti, SKU, 990126P) with the molar ratio of 1:10 [SC: DSPE-PEG (2000)–maleimide] was added to reduced SC protein. The mixture was then transferred to a 25-ml round-bottom flask, and the flask was sealed using a rubber septum. The O2 in the flask was then removed by vacuum, and flask was then filled with N2 gas. A ballon filled with N2 was inserted into the flask with a syringe, and the reaction was done overnight at room temperature. The following day, the mixture was run on a 1.0, 15-cm chromatography column (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 7371052) filled with Sephadex G-100 resin, medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Ref# G100120-50G) to separate unconjugated SC. Fractions containing conjugated SC were combined, and the concentration was measured by BCA assay. The mixture was then stored at −80°C for use.

SC liposome preparation

Lipids including, 57 mol % 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) (Avanti, CAS: 816-94-4), 38 mol % cholesterol (Avanti, CAS: 57-88-5), and 5 mol % PEG-DSPE (PEG45-DSPE molecular weight 2000) (Avanti, CAS: 474922-77-5) were dissolved in chloroform to yield the desired mole % used in liposome formulation. After dissolving lipids, the chloroform was evaporated using nitrogen gas to obtain a lipid thin film. The functional lipids used in this study were conjugated to DSPE-PEG(2000)–amine as previously described (6, 19, 26). Briefly, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester of dye (AF647, AF488, or pHrodo) was added to DSPE-PEG(2000)–amine at a 1.2:1 molar ratio [NHS-ester of the dye: DSPE-PEG(2000)–amine] in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in the presence of a base (DIPEA). The reaction was left at room temperature overnight. The following day, the mixture was run on a on a 1.5, 15-cm chromatography column (Bio-Rad, catalog no. 7371552) filled with Sephadex G-75 resin, medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Ref# G75120) to separate unconjugated dye from the conjugated product. Fractions containing conjugated product combined and transfer to a flask and lyophilized overnight. The following day, the conjugated product was dissolved in DMSO, aliquoted, and stored at −80. The AF555-PEG-DSPE was purchased (BROADPHARM, catalog no. BP-40230). Once the lipid film formed, 100 μl of DMSO containing desired functional lipid (AF647-, AF488-, AF555- or pHrodo-PEG-DSPE) was added to the dried lipid at a final ratio 0.1 mol % of the total lipid. The solution was then stored at −80°C until completely frozen. The DMSO was then removed through lyophilization overnight.

Liposome extrusion

Lipid thin film was hydrated with a total amount of 1 ml of 1× PBS and the appropriate amount of SC-PEG-DSPE. The lipids in 1× PBS were then sonicated in a sonicator for cycles of 1 min on and 5 min off for five times. Lipids were then extruded using an Avanti mini extruder in 1000-μl syringe (SKU: 610017-1Ea), first with an 800-nm filter (Cytiva, 10417304) and then with a 100-nm filter (Cytiva, 800309), 20 times. To measure the size distribution and zeta potential of naked liposome, SC-liposome, and SC-liposomes conjugated to V2 Siglec-7, a Malvern Panalytical Zetasizer Nano S was used. SC-liposomes were then stored at 4°C and used for maximum of 30 days.

Liposome stability measurement

The stability of SC-liposomes was analyzed using two methods. First, size distribution of liposomes stored at 4°C was measured with a Malvern Panalytical Zetasizer Nano S. Second, functional stability of the same liposomes was assessed by measuring the binding of 100 μM total lipid (7.5 × 1014 particles/liter) SC-liposomes conjugated to V2 Siglec-7 to 400,000 WT U937 cells using flow cytometry. The measurements were repeated at 4-day intervals for 30 days.

Preparation of Siglec-liposomes and Siglec–Strep-Tactin

Lyophilized Siglec-Fcs (Siglec-Fc–Strep Tag II) were hydrated using nuclease-free water. Siglecs were then added to AF647–Strep-Tactin with the molar ratio of 1:5 (monomer Strep-Tactin: Siglec-Fc), where the final concentration of Siglec-Fc was either 8 or 16 μg/ml. The precomplexing of Siglec–Strep-Tactin was then performed for 30 min on ice.

Preparation of Siglec-liposomes

Liposomes were typically extruded at a total lipid concentration of 2 mM (1.5 × 1016 particles/liter). For in vitro assays, appropriate working concentrations of 25, 50, or 100 μM total lipid (1.9 × 1014, 3.8 × 1014, and 7.5 × 1014 particles/liter, respectively) were prepared by diluting the stock solution of liposomes in 1× PBS, followed by conjugation to V1 or V2 Siglec at a 1:1.2 molar ratio (SC: Siglec-Fc) for 1 hour at room temperature. Binding assays of the conjugated Siglec-liposomes with cells were then performed in 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). For in vivo assays, liposomes were generally extruded at 6 mM (4.5 × 1016 particles/liter). Similar to in vitro assays, liposomes used for injection of 1.3 or 4 mM total lipid (9.8 × 1015 or 3 × 1016 particles/liter, respectively) were prepared by diluting the stock in 1× PBS and subsequently conjugating to V2 Siglec-Fc.

Bead assay

Synthesis of α2-3–linked 6,6′-disulfated Gal-Glc biotin sialoside (general)

Reactions were carried out in oven-dried glassware. All reagents were purchased from commercial sources and were used without further purification unless noted. When necessary, the reaction solvents were dried by storing more than 4-Å molecular sieves (48 hours). Unless stated otherwise, all reactions were carried out at room temperature under a positive pressure of argon or nitrogen and were monitored by thin-layer chromatography on Silica Gel 60 F254 (0.25 mm, E. Merck). Spots were detected under ultraviolet light or by charring with acidified p-anisaldehyde solution in EtOH. Unless otherwise indicated, all column chromatography was performed on Silica Gel (40 to 60 μM). The ratio between silica gel and crude product ranged from 100 to 50:1 (w/w). 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded at 700, 600, or 500 MHz, and chemical shifts were referenced to either TMS (0.0, CDCl3), CD3OD (3.30, CD3OD), or HOD (4.78, D2O). 1H data were reported as though they were first order. 13C NMR (APT) spectra were recorded at 150 or 125 MHz, and 13C chemical shifts were referenced to internal CDCl3 (77.23, CDCl3), CD3OD (48.9, CD3OD), or external acetone (31.07, D2O). Organic solutions were concentrated under vacuum at <40°C. Electrospray mass spectra were recorded on samples suspended in mixtures of tetrahydrofuran with CH3OH and added NaCl. Further information on synthesis of α2-3–linked 6,6′-disulfated Gal-Glc biotin sialoside is provided in the scheme section of the Supplementary Materials.
