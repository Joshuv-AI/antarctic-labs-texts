Biocontrol potential of endophytic Sphingobium xenophagum J-1 against Fusarium oxysporum causing root rot in Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis
Authors: Qing Shu, Ran Guo, Jiao Yin, Siman Gu, Yueyan Luo, Xinyan He, Anzhong Peng, Haifeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis (PPY) is a valuable medicinal plant frequently threatened by root rot caused by pathogenic fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum. Traditional chemical control methods pose environmental and resistance risks, highlighting the need for sustainable alternatives. This study investigated the antifungal potential of the endophytic bacterium Sphingobium xenophagum J-1, isolated from healthy PPY, against F. oxysporum in vitro. The ethyl acetate crude extract of J-1 fermentation broth exhibited significant concentration-dependent inhibition of spore germination, with a 94.64% inhibition rate at 12.5 mg/mL. Microscopic and physiological analyses revealed that the extract severely damaged fungal hyphae, causing swelling, fragmentation, cytoplasmic leakage, and intracellular cavitation. Furthermore, the extract appeared to disrupt cell membrane integrity. These findings suggest that J-1 may act through inhibition of spore germination, hyphal degradation, and membrane disruption. This study provides preliminary in vitro evidence that S. xenophagum J-1 exhibits antifungal activity against F. oxysporum. These findings suggest that strain J-1 represents a promising candidate with in vitro antifungal potential, although its biocontrol efficacy in planta remains to be validated through greenhouse and field experiments.

Introduction

Paris polyphylla var. yunnanensis (PPY) is an important medicinal plant in China with broad pharmacological activities1–3. However, its cultivation is severely threatened by root rot disease, leading to significant reductions in yield and quality4. The pathogenic fungi causing root rot are widely present in soil. While traditional chemical fungicides may cause environmental pollution, pathogen resistance, and potential impacts on the medicinal value of PPY, biological control methods are considered a promising alternative due to their environmental friendliness, efficiency, and sustainability 5–7.

As a rare medicinal plant, PPY forms complex symbiotic relationships with its endophytes, which influence its growth, development, and stress resistance8,9. Studies suggest that PPY harbors a rich endophytic microbial community with untapped bioactive potential. In addition, Sphingobium is a genus of Gram-negative bacteria that has attracted widespread attention for its outstanding ability to degrade environmental pollutants and metabolize complex organic compounds. Existing studies mainly focus on the environmental remediation function and metabolic pathway analysis of the bacteria of this genus, while there are few systematic reports on their role in biological control of plant diseases, especially the antifungal activity of Sphingobium xenophagum. Unlike the previously reported Sphingobium isolates, S. xenophagum J-1 used in this study was isolated from healthy PPY, and had a clear plant endophytic property. Its biocontrol potential and mode of action remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, we employed a spore germination inhibition assay to evaluate the effects of S. xenophagum J-1 crude extract on F. oxysporum, while microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and conductivity measurements were used to investigate its impact on hyphal morphology and membrane permeability. This study provides preliminary in vitro evidence that the bacterium exhibits significant antagonistic activity against F. oxysporum, suggesting its potential as a candidate strain for biological control of PPY root rot.

Materials and methods

The endophytic bacteria of PPY

The research group previously isolated 89 strains of endophytic bacteria from healthy PPY roots, rhizomes, and leaves9.

Pathogenic fungi

The strains Fusarium oxysporum G1F1 and F. solani G1F2 are preserved in the Microbial Culture Collection of the Yunnan Key Laboratory of Screening and Research on Anti-pathogenic Plant Resources from Western Yunnan.

Ethyl acetate crude extract preparation of endophytic bacteria

The strain was initially inoculated as a single colony into LB broth and incubated at 28 °C under constant shaking (160 rpm) over a 24-hour period. Subsequently, a 15% (v/v) inoculum was transferred to SGTYP liquid medium and further incubated under the same conditions for 3–5 days. After fermentation, the reaction was terminated by adding 1.5 volumes of ethyl acetate, followed by thorough mixing and static layering. The organic layer was separated, then evaporated under vacuum at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator to obtain the ethyl acetate crude extract from the fermentation broth. This extract was dissolved in DMSO to create a stock solution (25 mg/mL) and stored at 4 °C for later use.

Screening of antifungal activity of endophytic bacteria

The antifungal activity of endophytic bacteria against PPY pathogenic fungi was evaluated using the filter paper disk diffusion method10. Uniform 5-mm mycelial plugs from the actively growing edge of pathogenic fungal colonies were inoculated onto PDA plates. 5 mm diameter sterile filter paper disks were placed approximately 2.5 cm around the fungal plug. For all treatments, the ethyl acetate crude extract was prepared at the same concentration (mg/mL), and an equal volume (4 µL) was applied to each sterile filter paper disk to ensure complete and consistent saturation across treatments. The control group was added with the same volume of sterile water, and the positive drug group was added with nystatin. All tests were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 5–7 days in a constant-temperature incubator, and fungal growth inhibition was monitored. Among the tested strains, J-1 exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect on F. oxysporum and was therefore chosen for further experimentation.

Identification of strain J-1

To examine the colonial characteristics of strain J-1, the bacterium was cultured on R2A agar at 28 °C for three days. Subsequently, its morphological features, including cell shape and size, were analyzed using a TESCAN LYRA3 scanning electron microscope (Brno, Czech Republic).

The genomic DNA of strain J-1 was obtained employing a Rapid Bacterial Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (manufactured by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene was carried out with primer pairs 27 F and 1492R. The PCR amplification was performed in a 25 µL reaction system containing 2 µL of genomic DNA, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 8.5 µL of double-distilled water, and 12.5 µL of 2× Taq PCR Master Mix. After amplification, the PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using a DiaSpin PCR Product Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai). The cleaned PCR amplicons were then bidirectionally sequenced with the original primer sets at Sangon Biotech’s sequencing facility in Shanghai.

The sequences generated were checked and assembled using software SeqMan, and then subjected to BLASTn searches in the National Center for Biotechnology Information(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) GenBank nucleotide database, and the corresponding sequences with high similarity were downloaded. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Neighbor-joining method in MEGA 11.0, with branch support assessed through 1000 bootstrap replicates. Novosphingobium aromaticivorans DSM 12,444 served as the outgroup for phylogenetic comparison.

Determination of the activity of four enzymes by strain J-1 from PPY

The enzymatic activities of strain J-1 were assessed using plate-based methods. Protease activity was evaluated on casein agar plates incubated at 26 °C. Clear zones around colonies indicated casein hydrolysis11,12. Chitinase activity was tested on chitin agar plates incubated at 26 °C. Formation of transparent zones around colonies indicated chitin degradation13. β-1,3-Glucanase activity was determined on Poria cocos powder medium incubated at 26 °C. Clear zones around colonies reflected glucan degradation13. Cellulase activity was assessed using carboxymethyl cellulose agar plates. After incubation at 28 °C for 48 h, plates were stained with Congo red (1 mg/mL, 20 min), destained with 1 mol/L NaCl (15 min), and rinsed. Clear halos indicated cellulose hydrolysis14,15. All assays were performed in triplicate, and zone formation was periodically monitored or measured after treatment.

Effect of ethyl acetate crude extract from strain J-fermentation broth on F. oxysporum spore germination

Three 5-mm mycelial plugs of F. oxysporum were transferred into potato dextrose broth (PDB) and incubated at 25 °C with agitation (120 rpm) for 48 h. The fungal culture was filtered through a four-layer sterile gauze, and the resulting filtrate was centrifuged (8,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged again under identical conditions. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in PBS to achieve a spore concentration of 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL for subsequent experiments16.

The spore suspension was mixed with sterile potato dextrose broth (PDB) containing different concentrations of the crude extract (12.5, 6.25, 3.125, 1.5625, and 0.7813 mg/mL). An equivalent volume of sterile water supplemented with the same concentration of DMSO was used as the control. The mixtures were incubated at 28 °C for 12 h. Spore germination was assessed using a hemocytometer under a light microscope at 40× magnification. When the germination rate in the control reached approximately 90%, the spore germination inhibition rate for each treatment was calculated according to Eqs. 1–1. Effect of ethyl acetate crude extract from strain J-fermentation broth on mycelial morphology of F. oxysporum

The cover slip method was employed for mycelial observation17. A sterilized cover slip was inserted at a 45° angle at two-thirds of the Petri dish radius, and a mycelial plug of F. oxysporum was inoculated near the insertion point. The culture was incubated upside-down at 28 °C until the mycelium covered approximately two-thirds of the coverslip. The cover slip with adhered mycelium was carefully removed and transferred into sterile PDB medium containing 12.5 mg/mL of the crude extract (the control group used PDB supplemented with an equal volume of sterile water). After 24 h of incubation at 28 °C, the cover slip was gently rinsed with sterile water to remove residual medium and observed under an optical microscope for mycelial morphological changes. Observations were made from three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), F. oxysporum was cultured on PDA medium at 28 °C for 7 d. A small cube from the colony edge was excised, excess agar was trimmed, and the mycelial block was transferred into sterile PDB medium containing 12.5 mg/mL crude extract (control: PDB with sterile water). After 24 h at 28 °C, the treated mycelial blocks were fixed with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) followed by a 1:1 ethanol/amyl acetate mixture. After critical-point drying and gold coating, mycelial ultrastructural changes were examined by SEM10.

Effect of ethyl acetate crude extract from strain J-fermentation broth on cell membrane integrity of F. oxysporum

The impact of the ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 fermentation broth on the cell membrane of F. oxysporum was evaluated using a staining method. Crystal violet staining was employed to observe changes in the fungal cell membrane. Damaged hyphae with compromised cell membranes stained dark purple, while untreated hyphae appeared light purple.

Three 5-mm mycelial plugs were inoculated into PDB liquid medium and cultured at 25 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 48 h to obtain young hyphae. The hyphae were then transferred to sterile PDB medium containing 12.5 mg/mL of the crude extract and incubated for 24 h, followed by three washes with sterile water. Untreated hyphae served as the blank control. After crystal violet staining for 5 min, hyphal staining patterns were observed under a microscope. Observations were based on three independent replicates (n = 3).

Young hyphae cultured under identical conditions were treated with 12.5 mg/mL crude extract in sterile PDB medium and shaken for 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 10 h, 12 h, 24 h. After centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was collected, and its electrical conductivity was determined using a conductivity meter. Data are mean ± SD of three replicates (n = 3). To control for background conductivity, sterile PDB medium supplemented with the same concentration of crude extract but without fungal hyphae was measured in parallel, and no significant change in conductivity was observed during the experimental period.

Results

Inhibitory activity of strain J-1 against PPY root rot pathogenic fungi

The results demonstrated that the ethyl acetate extract of strain J-1 exhibited significant antifungal activity, inhibiting the growth of F. oxysporum by 69.34% and F. solani by 19.83% (Fig. 1).

The taxonomic classification of strain J-1

The colonies of strain J-1 are yellow, circular, smooth, convex, moist, and glossy (Fig. 2A).Scanning electron microscopy revealed that strain J-1 exhibited a typical rod-shaped morphology, approximately 2 μm in length, with bluntly rounded ends and a relatively smooth surface. No obvious flagella or pili structures were observed (Fig. 2B and C). The amplified 16 S rDNA sequence had a length of 692 bp. The phylogenetic tree showed that strain J-1 clustered with Sphingobium xenophagum JMA12 (OM533738.1), sharing 99.57% homology (Fig. 3).

Enzyme activity analysis results

Strain J-1 did not form transparent halos on casein medium (Fig. 4A), Poria cocos powder medium (Fig. 4C), chitin medium (Fig. 4E), or carboxymethyl cellulose culture medium agar plates (Fig. 4G), indicating its inability to secrete extracellular proteases, chitinases, β-1,3-glucanases, or cellulases. In contrast, F. oxysporum also exhibited no detectable enzymatic activity on casein (Fig. 4B), Poria cocos powder (Fig. 4D), or chitin medium (Fig. 4F). However, a distinct transparent halo was observed in carboxymethyl cellulose medium (Fig. 4H), confirming its cellulase-secreting capability. It should be noted that these plate-based enzyme assays are qualitative in nature and may not detect low-level enzyme production. In addition, extracellular enzyme production can be influenced by culture conditions, medium composition, and incubation time. Therefore, the absence of halos does not definitively exclude the possibility of enzyme secretion by strain J-1.

Comparison of enzyme production capabilities between strain J-1 (A,C,E,G) and F.oxysporum (B,D,F,H).

These results suggest that extracellular hydrolytic enzymes may not be the dominant antifungal mechanism under the tested conditions. However, additional quantitative enzyme assays under different growth conditions would be necessary to further clarify the role of hydrolytic enzymes in the antagonistic activity of strain J-1.

The ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 fermentation broth exhibits a destructive effect on the hyphal structure of F. oxysporum

Following the culture protocol outlined in Sect. 2.8, microscopic examination of F. oxysporum slides was performed for both control and PPY root rot pathogen treatment groups using optical microscopy.The results demonstrate that treatment with the ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 fermentation broth caused notable damage to F. oxysporum hyphae. In the control group, the hyphae of F. oxysporum exhibited robust growth, with a plump, smooth, and elongated morphology. The hyphae appeared uniform and intact, with no leakage of cellular contents, representing normal hyphal morphology (Fig. 6A). In contrast, the hyphae treated with the crude extract (Fig. 6B) showed significant damage, including fragmentation, swelling, increased transparency, and the formation of large intracellular cavities. Extensive leakage of cellular contents was observed, leaving the hyphae hollow and beaded in appearance.

The effect of the crude extract from strain J-1 on the hyphal morphology of F. oxysporum was observed under scanning electron microscopy. The results showed that the hyphae of the control group exhibited normal morphology, with a linear structure and smooth surface (Fig. 7A). In contrast, after treatment with the strain J-1 crude extract, the hyphae of F. oxysporum displayed shrinkage, withering, breakage, and cytoplasmic leakage (Fig. 7B and C).

The ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-fermentation broth disrupts the cell membrane structure of F. oxysporum

The F. oxysporum mycelium not treated with the crude extract of ethyl acetate from the fermentation broth of strain J-1 was stained light purple with crystal violet, and the cell wall was intact and clearly visible(Fig. 8A). In contrast, the mycelium of F. oxysporum treated with the crude extract was stained deep purple(Fig. 8B), indicating that the cell membrane was damaged with prolonged treatment time.

As shown in Fig. 9, the electrical conductivity of the control group gradually increased over time, rising from approximately 70 µS/cm to nearly 100 µS/cm, with an overall stable trend. In contrast, the electrical conductivity of the experimental group rose sharply within the first 10 h, increasing from about 70 µS/cm to 120 µS/cm, after which it stabilized with minor fluctuations, remaining within the range of 110–120 µS/cm. These results indicate that the ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 fermentation broth significantly damages the cell membrane of F. oxysporum, causing massive leakage of intracellular electrolytes and consequently leading to a rapid increase in electrical conductivity. The conductivity values of the extract-only control remained low and stable throughout the assay, indicating that the observed increase in conductivity was primarily attributable to intracellular electrolyte leakage from F. oxysporum.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the antifungal activity and possible mechanisms of the endophytic bacterium S. xenophagum J-1 against F. oxysporum, the major causal agent of root rot in PPY. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on environmental functions of Sphingobium species, our findings demonstrate that strain J-1 exhibits pronounced antagonistic activity against a phytopathogenic fungus, highlighting its potential application in biological control.

The ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition on F. oxysporum conidial germination, reaching maximal inhibition (94.64%) at 12.5 mg/mL. Microscopic and physiological analyses revealed that the crude extract induced morphological abnormalities in fungal hyphae, including fragmentation, swelling, cytoplasmic leakage, and vacuolization. These observations suggest that the extract may exert its antifungal effects partly through disruption of cell wall or membrane structures. As the critical barrier for fungal survival, cell wall degradation can lead to osmotic imbalance and cell death18,19. Membrane disruption has been established as a key antifungal mechanism20, supported by our conductivity measurements showing significant electrolyte leakage from F. oxysporum cultures following treatment. This finding was further corroborated by crystal violet staining results, consistent with previous reports on the antifungal potential of Sphingobium spp.21. However, the observed effects (spore inhibition, hyphal damage, staining) could be interrelated consequences of a primary stress, such as membrane disruption, rather than fully independent mechanisms. Direct mechanistic validation through quantitative membrane integrity assays or compound identification is needed in future studies.

Endophytic microorganisms derived from medicinal plants such as P. polyphylla var. yunnanensis may play important ecological roles in host protection. These endophytes are adapted to the host plant microenvironment and may contribute to disease resistance, stress tolerance, and metabolite production. The isolation of strain J-1 from healthy PPY tissues suggests that this bacterium may participate in the natural defense system of the host plant. Such plant-associated endophytes may exhibit improved colonization ability and ecological compatibility compared with non-native biocontrol strains, which may enhance their practical application potential.

Increasing research has focused on antagonistic microorganisms22. Various microbes have been found effective in controlling root rot. For example, Trichoderma asperellum, Hypocrea lixii, T. harzianum, and T. atroviride significantly inhibit the growth of F.oxysporum, reduce disease incidence and severity, and enhance plant growth and productivity23. The extracellular hydrolases produced by Streptomyces can inhibit Fol mycelial growth, causing structural damage such as cell wall roughness, abnormal swelling, membrane disintegration, and breakdown of nuclear membranes and chromatin fibers24. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens produces polyketide antibiotics that disrupt the cell membrane integrity of F. oxysporum, interfere with membrane-related metabolic pathways, and inhibit key enzyme activities, ultimately causing membrane damage and pathogen death25. T. harzianum and T. tomentosum primarily secrete chitinase and cellulase to degrade pathogen cell walls and directly parasitize F. oxysporum26.

However, this study has several limitations, First, plate-based enzyme assays cannot completely exclude the possibility of low-level or condition-dependent extracellular enzyme production. Second, the bioactive compounds responsible for the observed antifungal effects remain unidentified. Future studies should focus on purification and structural characterization of the active metabolites, as well as genomic analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters. In addition, field trials are necessary to evaluate its rhizosphere colonization capacity and interactions with soil microbiota.

Conclusion

In this study, the endophytic bacterium S. xenophagum J-1 isolated from healthy PPY exhibited significant in vitro antagonistic activity against F. oxysporum. The ethyl acetate crude extract of strain J-1 effectively inhibited spore germination, induced severe hyphal deformation, and disrupted cell membrane integrity of the pathogen. This study demonstrates that S. xenophagum J-1 exhibits significant in vitro antifungal activity against F. oxysporum. However, the biocontrol efficacy of strain J-1 under greenhouse and field conditions remains to be evaluated. Further studies focusing on in planta validation, rhizosphere colonization, and active compound identification are required before practical agricultural application.
