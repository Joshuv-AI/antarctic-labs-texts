Physical activity and dietary patterns of older women with hypertension: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alireza Gholampour, Mehdi Ajri-Khameslou, Afrouz Mardi, Robab Hosseinpour, Sevin Alaei Namaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a chronic disease and one of the most significant health problems in the older people. Studies have shown that regular physical activity and healthy dietary patterns can help reduce blood pressure. This study aimed to determine the dietary patterns and physical activity levels of older women with hypertension.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 334 older women with hypertension who were attending hospitals and health care centers affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. A convenience sampling method was utilized. Anthropometric measurements, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ), and the Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) were employed to gather data. The data were analyzed using SPSS 22 and Nutrition 4 software, utilizing one-sample T-test, independent T-test, chi-square, one-way ANOVA, and linear regression.ResultsThe mean age of older women was 69.5 ± 3.12, ranging from 65 to 87 years, and 73.2% were married. 60.1% of them had high physical activity levels. There was an inverse association between systolic blood pressure and physical activity levels. The average daily energy intake was 1999.4 ± 579.58kcal. Predictors of systolic blood pressure included body mass index, daily energy intake, calcium, magnesium, and fiber intake.ConclusionThis study has demonstrated that the physical activity and dietary habits of older women with high blood pressure are crucial in managing their condition. It highlights the significance of promoting physical activity and healthy eating among this demographic to control high blood pressure effectively.

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a chronic disease and one of the most significant health problems worldwide. It is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and kidney failure [1]. HTN is diagnosed by high blood pressure when the mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) is consistently at least 140 mmHg and the diastolic blood pressure (DBP) is at least 90 mmHg [2]. The history of HTN dates back to the nineteenth century, when blood pressure was first measured systematically. Our understanding of this disease and its risk factors has increased with scientific and medical advances. In recent decades, several studies have been conducted on the relationship between HTN and factors such as age, gender, and socioeconomic status [1, 3]. Additionally, social and economic factors, such as education and access to health services, can affect the prevalence of this disease [4].

HTN caused 7.86 million deaths and 146 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 1990 to 2019 [5] and is also the main risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, accounting for about 13% of global deaths annually [6]. The epidemiology of HTN in Iran and worldwide shows that this disease is increasingly spreading, with a prevalence of 68% among people aged 60 and older, higher in women (74%) compared to men (60.7%) [7].

In older women, hormonal changes after menopause can lead to an increased risk of HTN [8]. Research has shown that older women with high blood pressure may also face psychological problems such as depression and anxiety, which can decrease their quality of life [9, 10]. A systematic review indicated that treatment of HTN in older people the significantly reduced the risk of common cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke, by 26% [11].

Managing high blood pressure in older women requires lifestyle changes, drug therapy, and social support. Studies have shown that regular physical activity and dietary patterns can help reduce blood pressure and improve quality of life in this age group. Non-pharmacological interventions, such as dietary changes and physical activity, are essential for blood pressure management [12–14]. Systematic studies demonstrate that regular physical activity, including aerobic and resistance training, consistently decreases blood pressure in older people with hypertension [15, 16]. Furthermore, effective dietary strategies include reducing sodium intake and increasing consumption of fruits and vegetables, are crucial for blood pressure management [17]. Micronutrients such as potassium, calcium, and magnesium play a vital role in regulating blood pressure, with optimal consumption improving the cardiovascular system, and are provided by consuming fresh fruits and vegetables, nuts, and seafood [18, 19].

Therefore, it is crucial to focus on this disease and develop practical solutions for its management, particularly in elderly women. With limited studies conducted in this area in Iran, this study aimed to investigate physical activity and dietary patterns of older women with high blood pressure.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted from November to February 2023 and assessed 334 older women with hypertension attending hospitals and health care centers affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences in Iran using a convenience sampling method. Patients who had previously been diagnosed with hypertension by physicians, provided informed consent, were over 65 years old, and did not have any acute physical or mental problems or major surgery in the last month, and did not use alcohol or substances leading to addiction, not having a specific or incurable disease or being treated with immunosuppressive drugs or chemotherapy and resided in Ardabil province, were included in the study. Patients who did not respond to all items, did not engage in physical activity, and followed herbal or therapeutic diets such as DASH were excluded from the study. Finally, twenty patients were excluded from the study due to incomplete or incorrect completion of the questionnaires, and the study ended with 334 participants.

Data was collected using three questionnaires. The older women generally came to health centers and hospitals with one of their children who lived with them, so the researcher relied heavily on their assistance to get answers to questions.

A short self-developed questionnaire gathered demographic data, including age, weight (in kg), height (in meters), education level (illiterate, primary, secondary, or higher education), marital status (single, married, divorced, or widowed), income level, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Blood pressure for each patient was measured three times by trained personnel using a valid sphygmomanometer (Omron M10-IT model, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan). The average of the last two measurements was used for analysis. Blood pressure measurements were taken with patients in a seated position after at least 5 min of rest, using a cuff of appropriate size for the arm according to the 2024 European Association guidelines of the Blood Pressure [2].

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire- Short Form (IPAQ) was utilized to determine the intensity, duration, and frequency of physical activity [20]. This questionnaire consists of 7 questions. To calculate the energy expenditure of each individual in physical activity, the energy consumption of each activity was multiplied by the number of days the activity was performed and the duration of the activity. This calculation was based on the MET (Metabolic Equivalent), which represents the amount of energy expended during an activity. For example, walking is equal to 3.3METs, moderate activity is equal to 4 METs, and vigorous activity is equal to 8 METs [21]. Vigorous physical activity includes activities such as heavy lifting, gardening, aerobic exercise, fast cycling, and running, which require significant physical strength and cause the individual to breathe heavily. Moderate physical activity encompasses activities like carrying light loads or biking at a moderate pace, which require moderate physical exertion and cause the individual to breathe slightly faster than usual. This category includes walking at work, at home, while traveling between locations, and any other form of walking done for recreation, exercise, or leisure. In a study by Moghadam et al., the Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the questionnaire was found to be 0.7 [22].

The Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) has been validated and proven reliable for evaluating the food consumption of Iranian society by Esfahani et al. [23]. The diet assessment was conducted using a 142-question questionnaire in a Willet format. This tool covers common foods in the Iranian diet and allows for a detailed analysis of dietary intake. It records the frequency and amount of food items consumed based on household scale guides for daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly intake. The data is then converted to provide the average and standard deviation of daily intake of energy, protein, carbohydrates, fats, and micronutrients [23]. Caloric intake, micronutrients (such as calcium, magnesium, potassium, and …) and macronutrients (such as Omega-3 fatty acid, protein) affecting blood pressure were compared to standard values for analysis ​​ [24–30].

The data was analyzed using SPSS software version 22 utilizing descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) and inferential statistics (one -sample T-test, independent T-test, one-way ANOVA, chi-square, and linear regression) along with Nutrition 4 software. The normality of the data was checked using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Dietary analysis involved calculating daily nutrient intake by multiplying the frequency of consumption of each food item by its nutrient content. Nutrient intake data were obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) National Nutrient Database and Iran Food Composition Tables. Additionally, to analyze patients’ diets, their food consumption was recorded using home scales and converted to daily amounts for analysis using Nutrition 4 software.

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the ethics committee at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences with the code IR.ARUMS.REC.1402.188. The study protocol adheres to the ethical guidelines outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, including considerations such as voluntary participation and obtaining written informed consent. All women were assured of the privacy of their data and were informed that all data would be analyzed anonymously.

Discussion

This study revealed that the majority of older women with hypertension demonstrated a good level of physical activity, and there was a significant relationship between the level of physical activity and SBP, suggesting that increasing physical activity can improve blood pressure conditions, and higher levels of physical activity help reduce hypertension. These findings align with the findings of many studies [32–35]. While numerous studies support the positive effects of physical activity on blood pressure, some studies indicate mixed results [36, 37]. A survey by Ahmadi et al. showed that regular physical activity, along with lifestyle changes, is effective in managing blood pressure in the older people. Additionally, women, married people, and those with a lower level of education were more willing to be educated about smoking cessation, weight loss, and increased physical activity [38]. It seems that physical activity is effective in managing blood pressure in the older people, but it should be planned considering various factors for other people. Regarding the high physical activity of the old people in this study, it can be explained that this study excluded people who had physical activity limitations, and given the active lifestyle of people in Ardabil province, who are mainly engaged in agriculture and animal husbandry, the prevalence of vigorous physical activity was high. Meanwhile, in this study, factors such as the duration of hypertension and the type of medication used that affects blood pressure were not measured, considering the aim of the study. These factors should be considered when applying the findings of this research.

The study found that body mass index and higher caloric intake were associated with higher systolic pressure. Hall et al. found that obesity can lead to an increase in blood pressure [39]. The results of Nakano et al. study have shown that caloric restriction lowers blood pressure in obese hypertensive patients, suggesting that weight loss can improve blood pressure regulation [40]. In an intervention study by Hasandokht et al. conducted on women with high blood pressure, it was found that weight loss is also an essential factor in controlling blood pressure [41]. Eshkoor et al. also found that high dietary carbohydrate intake can increase blood pressure in elderly Malaysians, potentially due to its effects on obesity, which is a known risk factor for hypertension [42]. Therefore, it can be said that managing calories and sugar consumption can indirectly improve the effectiveness of blood pressure management.

The study found that average calorie intake, daily intake of calcium, potassium, sodium, sugar, fiber, and saturated fat among older women with hypertension were above recommended levels, while daily intake of magnesium and omega-3 fatty acids was below recommended levels. Some studies suggest that saturated fat intake may have a protective effect on blood pressure, especially in certain groups, while others show no significant impact. Takeuchi et al. conducted a study on Japanese women with fat mass and obesity-related gene variants. They found a significant negative correlation between saturated fatty acid intake and systolic and diastolic blood pressure [43]. Jayedi et al. reported that higher dietary calcium intake, independent of other minerals associated with blood pressure, slightly reduced the risk of hypertension [44]. Han et al. After adjusting for key lifestyle and dietary variables, they observed an independent and significant inverse relationship between dietary magnesium and high blood pressure [45]. These results suggest that other micronutrients and macronutrients may be more effective in managing blood pressure. While several studies have shown the beneficial effects of micronutrients on blood pressure control, there is still uncertainty among experts about whether a combination of micronutrients and a dietary approach will help regulate blood pressure [47]. Hajjar's study shows that increasing calcium intake to the recommended amount can reduce systolic blood pressure in the older people [46]. Adequate magnesium intake is also necessary to maintain a healthy sodium and potassium balance, which is essential for blood pressure regulation [47]. The balance between calcium and magnesium is crucial. Deficiency in any micronutrient has a negative impact on blood pressure. While micronutrients such as potassium, calcium, and magnesium are essential for controlling blood pressure, they may not be sufficient on their own. A high-fiber diet not only provides these micronutrients but also improves metabolic processes critical to blood pressure management [48]. This suggests that a combination of micronutrients and macronutrients may be more effective in regulating blood pressure.

In this study, fiber consumption had a lower blood pressure effect, consistent with the studies of Du et al. [49] and Ghavami et al. [50]. Fiber supplementation may significantly lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the older people. Xu et al. (2022) showed that increasing fiber intake is generally associated with lowering blood pressure in different populations. The specific effects on elderly women have not been precisely determined [51].

In summary, for better management of blood pressure in older women, a balanced program of physical activity coordinated with individual-social factors, along with a diet containing recommended and balanced calories, micronutrients, and macronutrients, can be effective.

Conclusion

The physical activity and dietary patterns of older women with high blood pressure play a crucial role in managing and controlling blood pressure. Older women who engage in regular physical activity tend to have better- controlled blood pressure compared to those with a sedentary lifestyle. Additionally, the dietary patterns of older women were found to be linked to controlled blood pressure, with calorie restriction, and higher fiber intake being key factors. This study highlights the importance of educating and promoting physical activities and healthy eating habits among older women.

Limitations

Factors such as cultural dietary practices, antihypertensive medication, duration of hypertension, comorbidities, and community support systems may not have been adequately considered, and the use of convenience sampling and self-reporting bias may potentially impact the results of the study. It is recommended to consider designing localized questionnaires, collecting accurate data from medical records, establishing specific inclusion criteria, strengthening social support systems, and using random sampling methods and external data to address these limitations in the next studies.
