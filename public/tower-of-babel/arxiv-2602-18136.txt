Exact response functions for a compressible thin fluid layer with odd viscosity
Abdallah Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Yuto Hosaka, Shigeyuki Komura

Abstract
Fluids composed of chiral active components can exhibit odd viscosity, a property that breaks time-reversal and parity symmetries. We investigate the hydrodynamic response to monopole and dipole singularities in a compressible thin fluid layer with odd viscosity, supported by a conventional lubrication layer. Using the two-dimensional Green’s function in Fourier space, we derive exact analytical solutions for the flow and pressure fields. These solutions provide a detailed description of the hydrodynamic interactions governing the motion of colloidal particles and microswimmers in confined chiral fluids, offering insight into the role of odd viscosity in modifying particle dynamics and collective behavior. The derived results are directly applicable to modeling transport, control, and self-organization phenomena in active and chiral microfluidic systems.

In these non-equilibrium states, systems can exhibit a novel transport coefficient known as odd or Hall viscosity

.
This is a non-dissipative, antisymmetric contribution to the fluid viscosity tensor that drives flows transverse to the velocity gradient
Banerjee et al. 2017
;
Liebchen and Levis 2022
;
Hosaka and Komura 2022
;

In quasi-two-dimensional (2D) setups, such behavior clearly emerges under compressibility conditions and leads to a variety of remarkable transport phenomena, such as topological waves at fluid boundaries
Souslov et al. 2019
, and nonreciprocal and transverse hydrodynamic responses
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
;
Hosaka, Andelman, and Komura 2023
;
Lier et al. 2023
;
Mecke et al. 2023
.
Consequently, the linear relation between hydrodynamic forces and the resulting velocities is no longer constrained by classical Onsager reciprocity
Markovich and Lubensky 2024
or the Lorentz reciprocal theorem in low-Reynolds-number fluid dynamics
Fruchart, Scheibner, and Vitelli 2023
;
Hosaka, Golestanian, and Vilfan 2023
.

Compressible fluids with odd viscosity are often modeled as thin fluid layers with momentum leakage to a surrounding bulk or substrate
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
;
Lier et al. 2023
.
While odd viscosity does not manifest itself in incompressible fluids with no-slip boundary conditions

;
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021b
, it produces pronounced effects in three-dimensional (3D) bulk fluids.
Examples include transverse or lift forces

;
Yuan and Olvera de la
Cruz 2023
;
Aggarwal, Kirkinis, and Olvera de la Cruz 2023
;
Hosaka, Golestanian, and Vilfan 2023
;
Everts and Cichocki 2024
;
Khain et al. 2024
, chirotactic response of microswimmers
Hosaka et al. 2024
, translation-rotation coupling of moving objects
Lier 2024
;
Chatzittofi and Hosaka 2025
, and pattern formation in turbulence
de Wit et al. 2024
.
Importantly, the notion of transverse stresses induced by odd viscosity has also been applied beyond its original setting to include other odd transports, such as odd-elastic materials
Lin et al. 2024
;
Veenstra et al. 2025
and non-conservative responses
Caporusso, Gonnella, and Levis 2024
;

In the study of hydrodynamics with odd viscosity, the central theoretical foundation is the determination of the fundamental solution of the Stokes equations with odd viscosity, i.e., the Green’s function.
This solution forms the basis for constructing resistance tensors, multipole expansions, and boundary integral methods
Pozrikidis 1992
.
Early contributions in this research area focused on the linear hydrodynamic response of a 2D compressible odd-viscous layer
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
.
These studies derived the Green’s function semi-analytically, revealing how odd viscosity introduces an antisymmetric component to the hydrodynamic propagator and generates perpendicular flow components.

Subsequent works have explored the dynamical implications of the Green’s function in more complex settings
Duclut et al. 2024
, including analyses of the lift force on a translating disk in odd viscoelastic media. More recently, some of the present authors employed a 2D Fourier transform approach to analytically determine the velocity and pressure fields around a disk-shaped inclusion in a supported fluid layer, enabling estimation of the resistance coefficients for a disk in motion
Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Vilfan, and Hosaka 2025
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
. Meanwhile, the hydrodynamics of model self-propelled microswimmers in chiral fluids has been investigated
Hosaka, Golestanian, and Daddi-Moussa-Ider 2023
, showing that a pair of swimmers exhibits a rich spectrum of two-body dynamics, governed by their initial relative orientations and the specific propulsion mechanisms of each swimmer.

Despite these recent advances, the exact linear response functions of a 2D compressible fluid layer with odd viscosity, together with conventional shear and dilational viscosities, remain unresolved. The primary challenge arises from the nontrivial coupling between the degrees of freedom introduced by odd viscosity and the compressibility of the fluid layer.

In this work, we derive the exact Green’s functions using a 2D Fourier transform formulation. Building on these results, we determine the hydrodynamic fields generated by monopole and dipole singularities and analyze the transverse flow response induced by odd viscosity. We find that the interplay between the 2D fluid layer and the underlying 3D hydrodynamics gives rise to rich flow structures, including vortical patterns. The hydrodynamic screening lengths that characterize these structures are determined solely by the conventional (even) viscosities, while the emergent transverse response is governed exclusively by the odd-viscosity coefficient.

II
Governing equations

We consider a 2D compressible fluid layer supported by an incompressible 3D bulk fluid, which is confined by a rigid wall and has 3D shear viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{B}}
.
We denote the depth of the underlying 3D fluid by
h
; see Fig.
1
for a schematic illustration of the system.
We define the position vector on the 2D fluid layer as
\bm{r}=(x,y)
.
The momentum balance for the 2D fluid layer is governed by

\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\bm{f}_{\rm B}+\bm{f}=\mathbf{0}\,,

(1)

where
\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}
denotes the 2D gradient operator,
\bm{\sigma}
is the hydrodynamic stress tensor,
\bm{f}_{\rm B}
is the bulk force applied by the underlying bulk fluid, and
\bm{f}
represents any additional force density exerted on the 2D fluid.

The bulk force
\bm{f}_{\rm B}
exerted by the underlying bulk fluid on the 2D fluid layer is obtained by solving the corresponding hydrodynamic equations
Barentin et al. 1999
. The bulk fluid is governed by the 3D incompressible Stokes equations

\eta_{\rm B}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\bm{u}-\bm{\nabla}p=\mathbf{0}\,,\quad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0\,,

(2)

wherein
\bm{u}
and
p
denote the 3D velocity and pressure fields, respectively, and
\eta_{\mathrm{B}}
the shear viscosity of the underlying 3D fluid.
The 3D flow satisfies no-slip boundary conditions at the impermeable rigid wall at
z=0
, i.e.,
\bm{u}(\bm{r},0)=\bm{0}
; see Fig.
1
.
Additionally, continuity of velocity at
z=h
requires
\bm{u}(\bm{r},h)=\bm{v}(\bm{r})
, where
\bm{v}(\bm{r})
is the velocity of the 2D fluid layer.
By using the lubrication approximation, which is valid when the layer thickness
h
is much smaller than any lateral dimension, the bulk velocity is expressed as
Barentin et al. 1999

\bm{u}(\bm{r},z)=\frac{1}{2\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}\,z(z-h)\,\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}p+\frac{z}{h}\,\bm{v}\,.

(3)

The force applied by the 3D bulk on the 2D fluid layer is obtained by projecting the hydrodynamic traction onto the horizontal
(x,y)
plane as
Barentin et al. 1999

\bm{f}_{\rm B}=-\frac{h}{2}\,\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}p-\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}{h}\,\bm{v}\,.

(4)

The viscous stress tensor in Eq. (
1
) for the 2D compressible fluid with odd viscosity is given by
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a

\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{E}}+\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{O}}\,,

(5)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle=2\eta_{\mathrm{S}}E_{ij}+(\eta_{\mathrm{D}}-\eta_{\mathrm{S}})\,\delta_{ij}E_{kk}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathrm{O}}\left(\epsilon_{ik}E_{kj}+\epsilon_{jk}E_{ki}\right),

(6b)

with
\delta_{ij}
denoting the Kronecker delta and
\epsilon_{ij}
the 2D Levi-Civita symbol, defined by
\epsilon_{xx}=\epsilon_{yy}=0
and
\epsilon_{xy}=-\epsilon_{yx}=1
.
Here,
\eta_{\mathrm{S}}
,
\eta_{\mathrm{D}}
, and
\eta_{\mathrm{O}}
denote the 2D shear, dilatational, and odd viscosities, respectively.
The shear viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{S}}
characterizes resistance to shape-changing deformations, the dilatational viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{D}}
quantifies dissipation associated with isotropic compression or expansion, and the odd viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{O}}
represents a nondissipative, parity-odd contribution that couples stresses perpendicular to velocity gradients.
In addition,
E_{ij}=(\partial_{i}v_{j}+\partial_{j}v_{i})/2
denotes the symmetric rate-of-strain tensor, while
\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{E}}
and
\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{O}}
denote the even and odd contributions to the stress tensor, respectively.

From the momentum balance equation (
1
), together with Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), the hydrodynamic equation for a 2D compressible fluid is derived as
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a

\bm{\eta}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}^{2}\bm{v}+\eta_{\mathrm{D}}\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}(\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}\cdot\bm{v})-\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}{h}\,\bm{v}-\frac{h}{2}\,\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}p+\bm{f}=\bm{0}\,,

(7)

where we have defined the 2D viscosity tensor

\bm{\eta}=\eta_{\mathrm{S}}\mathds{1}+\eta_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{\epsilon}\,,

(8)

where
\mathds{1}
denotes the 2D identity tensor.
Our goal in this work is to derive an exact analytical solution to Eq. (
7
) for a point-force singularity acting on the 2D medium.

Integrating the 3D divergence of the bulk fluid velocity
\bm{u}(\bm{r},z)
over the thin lubrication layer for
z\in[0,h]
and setting it to zero to enforce incompressibility of the underlying fluid gives the relation

\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}\cdot\bm{v}=\frac{h^{2}}{6\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}^{2}p\,.

(9)

This indicates that the 2D fluid is compressible due to the incompressibility constraint of the 3D bulk fluid.
For additional theoretical details on the model and the derivation of the governing equations, we refer the reader to the references
Barentin et al. 1999
;
Elfring, Leal, and Squires 2016
.

For convenience, we introduce two hydrodynamic inverse screening lengths,

\kappa=\sqrt{\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}{h\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}}\,,\qquad\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{2\eta_{\mathrm{B}}}{h\bar{\eta}}}\,,

(10)

which determine the length scale, beyond which the fluid layer transfers momentum to the bulk fluid.
Here, we have defined the mean even viscosity as

\bar{\eta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta_{\mathrm{S}}+\eta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)\,.

(11)

III
Green’s functions in 2D Fourier space

The hydrodynamic response of the thin fluid layer is described by the Green’s function which connects the force applied to the fluid with the resulting flow velocity.
For a 2D point-force singularity
\bm{f}=\bm{F}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{0})
applied at position
\bm{r}_{0}
in the 2D fluid layer, the induced velocity field is expressed as

\bm{v}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}\,\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{F}\,,

(12)

with
\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{r})
denoting the velocity Green’s function, which is a 2D tensor.
The corresponding solution for the pressure field is given by

p(\bm{r})=\frac{\kappa}{h}\,\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{F}\,,

(13)

with
\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{r})
denoting the pressure Green’s function, which is a 2D vector.

Due to the 2D geometry of the fluid layer, the Green’s function is conveniently obtained using 2D Fourier transforms. This method has been widely used in low Reynolds number hydrodynamics to solve a variety of flow problems in the viscously dominated regime
Felderhof 2009
;
Bickel 2007
;
Bickel et al. 2019
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Guckenberger, and Gekle 2016
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider and Gekle 2016
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider and Gekle 2018
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2018
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2019
.

We proceed by transforming Eqs. (
7
) and (
9
) into Fourier space, mapping the spatial position vector
\bm{r}
to its corresponding 2D wavevector
\bm{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
, and denoting the transformed quantities with a tilde.
We denote the magnitude of
\bm{k}
by
k=|\bm{k}|
, and the phase angle by
\phi=\arctan(k_{y}/k_{x})
.
For convenience, we introduce two orthogonal unit vectors,
\bm{k}_{\parallel}=\bm{k}/k
and
\bm{k}_{\perp}=(-k_{y},k_{x})/k
, oriented parallel and perpendicular to the wavevector, respectively.

In the 2D Fourier space, Eq. (
12
) takes the form

\widetilde{\bm{v}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}\,\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{k})\cdot\bm{F}\,.

(14)

The detailed derivation of the Green’s functions in Fourier space can be found in Ref.
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
. Here, we directly present the expressions and focus on the inverse Fourier transform, which constitutes the main contribution of this paper.
The Fourier-transformed Green’s function is obtained as

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{k})=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}\left(k^{2}+\kappa^{2}\right)\mathds{P}_{\parallel}+2\bar{\eta}\left(k^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)\mathds{P}_{\perp}-\eta_{\mathrm{O}}k^{2}\bm{\epsilon}}{2\bar{\eta}\left(k^{2}+\kappa^{2}\right)\left(k^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)+\eta_{\mathrm{O}}^{2}\eta_{\mathrm{S}}^{-1}k^{4}}\,,

(15)

with the dyadic projectors
\mathds{P}_{\parallel}=\bm{k}_{\parallel}\,\bm{k}_{\parallel}
and
\mathds{P}_{\perp}=\bm{k}_{\perp}\,\bm{k}_{\perp}
, which provide an orthogonal decomposition of the identity in 2D, namely

\mathds{P}_{\parallel}+\mathds{P}_{\perp}=\mathds{1}
, with
\mathds{P}_{\parallel}\mathds{P}_{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
.

By rescaling lengths with
\kappa^{-1}
in Eq. (
15
) and defining the scaled wavenumber via
u=k/\kappa
, the Green’s function in Eq. (
15
) can be written as

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}(u,\phi)=\frac{5\left(\alpha u^{2}+1\right)\mathds{1}-3\left(\beta u^{2}+1\right)\mathds{R}_{\phi}-2\mu u^{2}\bm{\epsilon}}{2\kappa^{2}\left[4\left(u^{2}+1\right)\left(\xi^{2}u^{2}+1\right)+\mu^{2}u^{4}\right]}\,,

(16)

where we have defined

\mu=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{O}}}{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}\,,

(17)

representing the scaled odd viscosity, and

\xi=\frac{\kappa}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{1+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{D}}}{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}}\,,

(18)

representing the viscosity ratio, quantifying the contribution of the dilatational viscosity to the resistance to deformation relative to shear.
In addition, the tensor

\mathds{R}_{\phi}=2\mathds{P}_{\parallel}-\mathds{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\phi)&\sin(2\phi)\\[3.0pt]
\sin(2\phi)&-\cos(2\phi)\end{pmatrix},

(19)

represents a 2D Householder reflection across the line oriented at an angle
\phi
with respect to the
x
-axis.
The dimensionless numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
appearing in Eq. (
16
) are defined as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\left(4\xi^{2}+1\right)=\frac{1}{5}\left(2+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{D}}}{\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}\right),

(20a)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(4\xi^{2}-1\right)=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{D}}}{3\eta_{\mathrm{S}}}.

(20b)

Hence, note that
\xi\geq 1/2
,
\alpha\geq 2/5
, and
\beta\geq 0
.

Similarly, the Green’s function for the hydrodynamic pressure in 2D Fourier space can be expressed as

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(u,\phi)=-\frac{6i}{\kappa^{2}u}\frac{(u^{2}+1)\,\bm{k}_{\parallel}-\mu u^{2}\,\bm{k}_{\perp}}{4\left(u^{2}+1\right)\left(\xi^{2}u^{2}+1\right)+\mu^{2}u^{4}}\,,

(21)

noting that
\bm{k}_{\parallel}
and
\bm{k}_{\perp}
depend on the polar angle
\phi
.

In the next section, we derive the expressions for the velocity and pressure fields in the real space via the inverse 2D Fourier transform.
For convenience, a summary of the mathematical background on inverse Fourier transform in the system of polar coordinates is provided in the Appendix.

IV
Green’s functions in 2D real space

IV.1
Velocity field

Upon taking the inverse 2D Fourier transform of Eq. (
16
), the velocity Green’s function in real-space is obtained as

\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{r})=Q_{0}\mathds{1}+Q_{1}\mathds{R}_{\theta}+Q_{2}\bm{\epsilon}\,,

(22)

where we have defined

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{2}\left(\Phi_{0}^{0}+\alpha\Phi_{0}^{1}\right),

(23a)

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\left(\Phi_{1}^{0}+\beta\Phi_{1}^{1}\right),

(23b)

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mu\Phi_{0}^{1}\,.

(23c)

Here,
\Phi_{m}^{q}
with
m,q\in\{0,1\}
denote the improper integrals defined by

\Phi_{n}^{q}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{u^{2q+1}J_{2n}(\rho u)}{4\left(u^{2}+1\right)\left(\xi^{2}u^{2}+1\right)+\mu^{2}u^{4}}\,,

(24)

with
\rho=\kappa r
denoting the scaled polar distance, and
J_{2n}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
2n
.
Note that
\mathds{R}_{\theta}
is a 2D Householder reflection defined by Eq. (
19
), with
\theta=\arctan(y/x)
denoting the polar angle in the real space

The integral given by Eq. (
24
) is convergent and an analytical evaluation is possible using the method of residues.
The core idea is to define a function
f(\zeta)
of a complex variable
\zeta
as

f(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cfrac{\zeta^{2q+1}H_{2n}^{(1)}(\rho\zeta)}{4\left(\zeta^{2}+1\right)\left(\xi^{2}\zeta^{2}+1\right)+\mu^{2}\zeta^{4}}\,,

(25)

where
H_{2n}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind of order
2n
, defined as
Abramowitz and Stegun 2000

H_{2n}^{(1)}(\zeta)=J_{2n}(\zeta)+iY_{2n}(\zeta)\,,

(26)

and
Y_{2n}
is the Bessel function of the second kind of order
2n
.
The function
f(\zeta)
is meromorphic, being analytic everywhere except at a finite set of poles.

. The contour consists of four segments: two semicircles and two straight lines along the real axis.

\epsilon\to 0
and
R\to\infty

We integrate
f(\zeta)
along a contour composed of the following segments: the upper side of the branch cut for
\zeta\in[-R,-\epsilon]
, a small clockwise semicircle of radius
\epsilon
around the origin of the complx plane, the positive real axis for
\zeta\in[\epsilon,R]
, and finally the upper half of the circle
|\zeta|=R
traversed counterclockwise; see Fig.

.
The function is chosen so that the integral over the large semicircle vanishes in the limit
R\to\infty
.
For further technical details on the mathematical steps, we refer the reader to the Appendix of Ref.
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025

In the upper half of the complex plane, the function
f(\zeta)
has two poles located at
\zeta=iA_{\pm}
, where

A_{\pm}=\sqrt{\frac{2\left(1+\xi^{2}\pm\sqrt{\delta}\right)}{4\xi^{2}+\mu^{2}}}\,,

(27)

with

\delta=\left(1-\xi^{2}\right)^{2}-\mu^{2}\,.

(28)

Note that
\sqrt{\cdot}
denotes the principal value of the square root representing the branch with nonnegative real part.

The poles lie in the upper half of the complex plane, as the imaginary part of
\zeta=iA_{\pm}
is always positive.
Note that for
\delta<0
,
A_{+}
and
A_{-}
form a pair of complex conjugates, whereas for
\delta\geq 0
,
A_{\pm}
are real numbers satisfying
A_{+}\geq A_{-}
, such that
\zeta=iA_{\pm}
are purely imaginary, as shown in Fig.
2
.
The integral in Eq. (
24
) is thus given by the sum of the residues as

\Phi_{n}^{q}=\Gamma_{n}^{q}(A_{-})-\Gamma_{n}^{q}(A_{+})+\frac{1}{4\pi\rho^{2}}\,\delta_{n1}\delta_{q0}\,,

(29)

where

\Gamma_{n}^{q}(\zeta)=\frac{i}{16\sqrt{\delta}}\,(-1)^{q}\,\zeta^{2q}H_{2n}^{(1)}(i{\rho}\zeta)\,.

(30)

The term containing the Kronecker deltas in Eq. (
29
) arises from the integration over half of the small circle around the origin, which exists only when
n=1
and
q=0
.

By combining these results and using the integral expression derived above, the coefficients
Q_{n}
for
n\in\{0,1,2\}
defined in Eqs. (
23
) can be written in the form

Q_{n}=\frac{i}{32\sqrt{\delta}}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{-}-\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{+}\right)+\frac{3}{8\pi\rho^{2}}\,\delta_{n1}\,,

(31)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=a_{\pm}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(i\rho A_{\pm}\right),

(32a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=b_{\pm}H_{2}^{(1)}\left(i\rho A_{\pm}\right),

(32b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=2\mu A_{\pm}^{2}\,H_{0}^{(1)}\left(i\rho A_{\pm}\right),

(32c)

with the abbreviations

a_{\pm}=5\left(1-\alpha A_{\pm}^{2}\right),\quad b_{\pm}=3\left(1-\beta A_{\pm}^{2}\right).

(33)

This represents the central result of this paper.
Remarkably, Eqs. (
32c
) show that only
\mathcal{Q}_{2}^{\pm}
, associated with the antisymmetric part, is an odd function of the odd viscosity, whereas the symmetric parts
\mathcal{Q}_{0}^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{\pm}
depend quadratically on
\mu
; see the definition of
A_{\pm}
in Eq. (
27
). This reflects the fundamental symmetry relation commonly observed in odd fluids
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
;
Khain et al. 2022
;
Lier et al. 2023
;
Yuan and Olvera de la
Cruz 2023
;
Everts and Cichocki 2024

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{r},\mu)=\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{r},-\mu)^{\top}\,,

(34)

with
\top
denoting the matrix transpose.
This is known as the Onsager–Casimir reciprocity for systems with external sources of time-reversal breaking
Fruchart, Scheibner, and Vitelli 2023
and is consistent with the Lorentz reciprocal theorem in the presence of odd viscosity tensors
Hosaka, Golestanian, and Vilfan 2023
.

In the case
\mu=0
and
\xi=1
(equivalently,
\lambda=\kappa
), the solution for the velocity Green’s function can be expressed in terms of the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Using the connection formula valid for
\rho>0
,

Y_{\nu}(i\rho)=i^{\nu+1}I_{\nu}(\rho)-\frac{2}{\pi}\,i^{-\nu}K_{\nu}(\rho)\,,

(35)

with
I_{\nu}
and
K_{\nu}
denoting modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind, respectively,
we obtain

\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\rho,\theta)=\frac{3}{16\pi}\left[\frac{5}{3}\,K_{0}(\rho)\,\mathds{1}+\left(\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}-K_{2}(\rho)\right)\mathds{R}_{\theta}\right],

(36)

which is consistent with previously reported results for the Green’s function of a 2D fluid layer in the absence of odd viscosity
Hosaka, Komura, and Andelman 2021a
.

The velocity Green’s function in Eq. (
36
) can be expressed in the far field
\rho\gg 1
as

\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\rho,\theta)\approx\frac{3}{8\pi\rho^{2}}\,\mathds{R}_{\theta}\,.

(37)

On the other hand, it can be expressed in the near field
\rho\ll 1
as

\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\rho,\theta)\approx\frac{3}{32\pi}\left[\frac{10}{3}\left(\ln\frac{2}{\rho}-\gamma\right)\mathds{1}+\mathds{R}_{\theta}\right],

(38)

wherein
\gamma\approx 0.5772

IV.2
Pressure field

The pressure can be derived directly in the real space by applying the inverse Fourier transform to Eq. (
21
), involving the evaluation of another set of improper integrals that can also be handled using the method of residues.

The pressure vector can be expressed in the real space, in polar coordinates, as

\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{r})=P_{\rho}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\rho}+P_{\theta}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta}\,,

(39)

where
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\rho}
and
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta}
are the unit basis vectors of the polar coordinate system.
In addition,

P_{\rho}=\Psi^{0}+\Psi^{2}\,,\quad P_{\theta}=-\mu\Psi^{2}\,.

(40)

Here, we have introduced the series of improper integrals

\Psi^{q}=\frac{3}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{u^{2q}J_{1}(\rho u)}{4\left(u^{2}+1\right)\left(\xi^{2}u^{2}+1\right)+\mu^{2}u^{4}}\,,

(41)

where
q\in\{0,1\}
.

Likewise, the integral defining
\Psi^{q}
is convergent, allowing for an analytical evaluation by using the method of residues.
As for the integrals associated with the velocity field, we define a function in the complex plane and evaluate it by integration along a contour, as shown in Fig.
2
.
We obtain

\Psi^{q}=H^{q}(A_{-})-H^{q}(A_{+})+\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\,\delta_{q,0}\,,

(42)

where
A_{\pm}
are defined in Eq. (
27
) and

H^{q}(\zeta)=\frac{3}{8\sqrt{\delta}}\,(-1)^{q}\,\zeta^{2q-1}H_{1}^{(1)}(i\rho\zeta)\,,

(43)

where
\delta
was defined in Eq. (
28
).

Combining these results and using the integral expression given above,
P_{i}
for
i\in\{\rho,\theta\}
can be cast in the form

P_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i}^{-}-\mathcal{P}_{i}^{+}+\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\,\delta_{i\rho}\,,

(44)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rho}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{8\sqrt{\delta}}\left(A_{\pm}^{-1}-A_{\pm}\right)H_{1}^{(1)}\left(i\rho A_{\pm}\right),

(45a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\mu}{8\sqrt{\delta}}\,A_{\pm}\,H_{1}^{(1)}\left(i\rho A_{\pm}\right)\,.

(45b)

For
\mu=0
and
\xi=1
(equivalently,
\lambda=\kappa

\bm{\mathcal{P}}(\rho,\theta)=\frac{3}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-K_{1}(\rho)\right)\hat{\bm{e}}_{\rho}\,.

(46)

In the far field
\rho\gg 1
, Eq. (

(47)

In the near field
\rho\ll 1

(48)

V
Dipolar flow field

The exact analytical calculations presented in the previous section provide the foundation for determining higher-order singularities. Here, we focus on the leading-order contribution, namely the force dipole, which is obtained by placing two force monopoles infinitesimally close to each other. This flow represents the velocity field generated by various self-propelling active microswimmers, including bacteria and algae
Lauga 2016
.

We define the unit vector
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)^{\top}
to represent the orientation of the force dipole with respect to the
x
-axis and denote by
\sigma
the strength of the dipole singularity. The dipole flow field can be written as

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{D}}=-\sigma\left(\hat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}\right)\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}\cdot\hat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}\right).

(49)

Defining the unit vectors
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\perp}=(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi)^{\top}
and
\hat{\bm{u}}_{n}=(\cos\vartheta_{n},\sin\vartheta_{n})^{\top}
, with
\vartheta_{n}=(n+1)\theta-n\varphi
for
n\in\{1,2\}
, the dipole flow field can be written in the compact form as

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{r})=\kappa\sigma\left[\bm{\mathcal{U}}+\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-}-\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{+}+\left(\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{-}-\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{+}\right)\cos(\theta-\varphi)\right],

(50)

where

\bm{\mathcal{U}}=\frac{3}{4\pi\rho^{3}}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{2}\,.

(51)

In addition,

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{\pm}=\frac{2i}{32\sqrt{\delta}\rho}\,b_{\pm}H_{2}^{(1)}(i\rho A_{\pm})\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{2}\,,

(52)

and

\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{\pm}=\frac{A_{\pm}}{32\sqrt{\delta}}\left(b_{\pm}\hat{\bm{u}}_{1}-a_{\pm}\hat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}+2\mu A_{\pm}^{2}\hat{\bm{e}}_{\perp}\right)H_{1}^{(1)}(i\rho A_{\pm})\,,

(53)

where
a_{\pm}
and
b_{\pm}
were defined earlier in Eq. (
33
).

In the isotropic case with
\xi=1
and
\mu=0
, the dipole flow field simplifies to

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(\rho,\theta)=\frac{3\kappa\sigma}{16\pi}\left[K_{1}(\rho)\,\hat{\bm{w}}+\frac{2}{\rho}\left(\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}-K_{2}(\rho)\right)\hat{\bm{u}}_{2}\right],

(54)

where we have defined

\hat{\bm{w}}=\left(\frac{5}{3}\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{1}\right)\cos(\theta-\varphi)\,.

(55)

In the far field
\rho\gg 1
, Eq. (
54
) can be expressed as

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(\rho,\theta)\approx\frac{3\kappa\sigma}{4\pi\rho^{3}}\,.

(56)

In the near-field limit
\rho\ll 1
, we obtain

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{D}}(\rho,\theta)\approx\frac{3\kappa\sigma}{16\pi\rho}\left(\hat{\bm{w}}+\hat{\bm{u}}_{2}\right).

(57)

VI
Summary and Discussion

In summary, we have derived exact analytical expressions for the linear response functions of a 2D compressible fluid layer with odd viscosity, together with conventional shear and dilational viscosities. These results provide a complete description of the hydrodynamic fields generated by monopole and dipole singularities, highlighting the emergence of transverse flows and vortical patterns driven by the antisymmetric stress contributions from odd viscosity. The interplay between the 2D fluid layer and the underlying 3D hydrodynamics gives rise to rich flow structures, with hydrodynamic screening lengths determined solely by the even viscosities and the transverse responses governed exclusively by the odd-viscosity coefficient.

Momentum in a fluid layer is conserved over distances smaller than the hydrodynamic screening length, but beyond this length scale, it leaks into the surrounding fluid
Diamant 2009
.
As shown in Eq. (
37
), the velocity Green’s function decays as
1/\rho^{2}
at large distances
\rho\gg 1
. This algebraic decay arises because mass is conserved in two dimensions, whereas total momentum is not conserved due to the presence of an underlying rigid substrate.
At small scales
\rho\ll 1
, the velocity instead decays logarithmically, as in Eq. (
38
), consistent with 2D momentum conservation. The behaviors of higher-order singularities can be inferred similarly. For instance, a first-order flow singularity is expected to scale as
1/\rho^{3}
in the far field (
\rho\gg 1
) and as
1/\rho
in the near field (
\rho\ll 1
). These limiting behaviors correspond to those of a dipolar flow and agree with Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
).
Similar crossover behaviors have also been reported for incompressible fluid layers supported by a substrate
Oppenheimer and Diamant 2010
;
Hosaka et al. 2017
.

Our work builds on the broader framework of hydrodynamics in chiral active matter, where the violation of time-reversal and parity symmetries leads to nonreciprocal flow behavior and the emergence of odd viscosity as a novel transport coefficient. The exact Green’s functions derived here serve as a fundamental building block for resistance tensors, multipole expansions, and boundary integral methods
Masoud and Stone 2019
;
Pozrikidis 1992
, enabling precise predictions of hydrodynamic interactions in compressible odd-viscous fluids. These findings are particularly relevant for understanding the dynamics of microswimmers, driven rotor collectives, and other active matter systems in confined or supported geometries, where the presence of odd viscosity can strongly affect propulsion, alignment, and collective behavior.

Looking ahead, our results provide a foundation for exploring more complex settings, including multi-particle interactions and various types of boundary conditions at the substrate. They also motivate experimental studies aimed at probing the characteristic transverse flows and nonreciprocal responses in engineered odd-viscous fluid layers. By bridging exact theoretical predictions with potential applications in active and chiral fluid systems, this work paves the way for a deeper understanding of transport phenomena in non-equilibrium, symmetry-breaking environments.
