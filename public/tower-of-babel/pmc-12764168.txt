Body mass index and sleep duration may regulate the effect of heavy metals on age at menopause
Authors: Guohuan Yin, Shuanzheng Zhao, Jingtao Wu, Qun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeavy metals are known endocrine disruptors that can interfere with ovarian function, but there are few epidemiological studies studying the impact of heavy metals on menopause.MethodIn this cross-sectional survey, we recruited 414 naturally postmenopausal women in the early postmenopausal stage (12 months ≤ time since last menstruation ≤ 24 months). We collected information on women's menopausal age through questionnaires. Blood concentrations of five metals, including chromium (Cr), cadmium (Cd), vanadium (V), manganese (Mn), and lead (Pb) were measured. General linear regression and Quantile g-computation model were employed to explore the individual and combined impact of heavy metals on the natural menopausal age. Finally, Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) was utilized to assess the reproducibility of the result in the two aforementioned models.ResultsThe study revealed that for each unit increase in the ln-transformed Cd, the average menopausal age was delayed by 1.3 years (β = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.12, 2.48), while Pb is associated with an average advancement in menopausal age by 1.25 years (β = -1.25, 95% CI: -2.43, -0.07). Metal mixtures had no significant association on natural menopause age in Quantile g-computation. BKMR findings were consistent with the above results. Further stratified analyses indicated that the associations of Cd and Pb on natural menopausal age were significantly lower in populations with BMI < 25 kg/m2 or sleep duration ≥ 7 h.ConclusionOur study indicates that heavy metals can influence the natural menopausal age, and maintaining a healthy weight and ensuring sufficient sleep may mitigate these adverse associations. Given the cross-sectional design and reverse temporality, findings represent associations and should be interpreted with caution.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25306-3.Keywords: Metal mixtures, Age at menopause, Combined effect, Sleep duration

Introduction

The onset of menopause reflects the decline in ovarian function and the cessation of reproductive ability in women, typically indicating non-interventional amenorrhea for a consecutive period of 12 months or longer [61]. While menopause is a normal manifestation of female physiological functions, research indicates that postmenopausal women often have an associated increased risk of various diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases [14], lipid metabolism disorders [30], breast cancer [32], ovarian cancer [54], and all-cause mortality [17].The age of menopause is influenced by various factors, including genetics, lifestyle habits (e.g., smoking, drinking, oral contraceptives), nutritional factors (e.g., low weight, low height), and environmental factors [40, 45, 52, 55]. Existing research indicates that various environmental endocrine disruptors, such as polychlorinated biphenyls, perfluoroalkyl substances, polyfluoroalkyl substances, and phthalates, can influence the age of menopause in women [9–11, 21]. However, there is relatively limited research on the impact of heavy metals, as an endocrine disruptor, on menopause.

Heavy metals are ubiquitous in nature and can enter the human body through oral ingestion, dermal absorption, and air inhalation [2], increasing the risk of reproductive damage, such as estrogen disturbance, secondary ovarian dysfunction, and fertility decline [35, 48], secondary ovarian dysfunction [22] and fertility decline [24]. Clinical studies have shown that female menopause is a manifestation of ovarian aging, which is associated with a decline in the quality and quantity of oocytes, a decrease in the number of follicles, and an increase in loss rate [31, 41]. Animal experimental research studies indicate that heavy metals such as chromium(Cr), cadmium(Cd), lead(Pb), and vanadium(V) can associated with severe damage to the ovaries, follicles, and uterus of experimental mice and wild polecat mustelids, resulting in reproductive toxicity and triggering reproductive dysfunction in animals [3, 36, 53, 57]. Therefore, this prompts us whether heavy metals are related to female menopausal age, and whether they can affect the advance or delay of age of menopause?

Currently, scholars from multiple countries have paid attention to this issue and have provided evidence of the impact of heavy metal exposure on the natural age of menopause in women. However, the current research results are inconsistent. A cross-sectional study from the United States, as well as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), suggests that Pb may have detrimental associations on estrus and menstrual patterns [44] and even at lower levels of Pb exposure, it could potentially shorten the reproductive lifespan [34]. Additionally, a study from the Reproductive Health Survey Project of female workers in China found that occupational Cd exposure may increase the risk of early menopause [8]. On the other hand, a cohort study in the United States, along with a Nurses' Health Study cohort, also observed a significant association between cumulative Pb exposure and an earlier age of menopause [15, 56]. Chen et.al found that the menopausal age of women living in Cd-contaminated areas was significantly higher than that in control areas, indicating that Cd exposure may associated with delayed menopausal age [33]. However, two cohort studies conducted in the United States indicated that there is no association between Cr, Cd, Manganese(Mn), V, and age of menopause [56, 58].

However, the real exposure environment of human beings is complex, which also suggests that we should pay more attention to the influence of metal mixture on menopausal age [16]. But at present, only two studies have discussed the association of metal mixture on menopausal age and the results are inconsistent. Alexandra J et al. investigated the association between metal mixtures, including Cr, Cd, V, Mn, Pb in toenails and the age of menopause in a sister cohort from 2003 to 2009. The results indicated that low levels of essential metals such as Cr and Mn might contribute to an earlier onset of menopause in women, while non-essential metals like Cd, Pb, and V showed no correlation with the age of menopause [58]. Another prospective cohort study of 1082 women aged 45–56 years found that exposure to metal mixture such as Cd, Mn, and Pb is related to early natural menopause age [56].

In summary, they mostly focus on the influence of individual metal on menopausal age, and no methods are put forward to avoid or weaken the possible adverse associations of heavy metals. Therefore, our study not only investigated the individual associations of specific heavy metals (Cr, Cd, V, Mn, Pb) and metal mixtures on the age of menopause but also conducted stratified analyses to further observe the associations of heavy metals on the natural age of menopause in different BMI or sleep duration populations. This was done to identify high-risk groups and propose potential intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The study participants were recruited from a heavy metal pollution area in northeastern China. The pollution originated from a ferroalloy factory established in 1961, which has been active for over 60 years. Additional details regarding the research site have been previously documented in other studies [59]. In summary, this study originated from a cross-sectional survey conducted between 2016 and 2019. Basic information and biological samples were collected from the participants.

A total of 1,513 participants were recruited for this cross-sectional survey. Initially, 413 participants who had not experienced menopause, 351 participants with missing questionnaire information or no blood metal values, and 63 participants who had undergone non-natural were associated withs of menopause due to surgery, illnesses drugs, etc. were excluded. To obtain a metal burden closer to the time of menopause occurrence, reduce recall bias, and ensure that participants had entered the menopausal stage, we included participants in the early postmenopausal stage (stage 1b: the last menstrual period was greater than or equal to 12 months but less than or equal to 24 months) based on the date of their last menstrual period reported in the questionnaire and the definition of menopause(Absent menstruation in the past 12 months [61]). Stage 1b represents the final stage with rapid changes in hormone levels and other indicators in women's bodies, which is closest to the menopausal state [23]. Ultimately, 273 participants whose last menstrual period was more than 24 months away from the date of investigation were excluded, and 414 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Flow chart for the study population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and the CIOMS/WHO International Ethical Guidelines for Health-related Research Involving Humans (2016; updated 2021), and complied with the Measures for the Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Humans (NHFPC of the PRC, 2016). Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. 056–2019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; data were de-identified prior to analysis and used solely for research purposes.

Measurement of blood metals

The five-metal panel (Cr, Cd, V, Mn, Pb) was chosen a priori based on the study site’s exposure profile and biologic relevance to female reproductive aging; this choice also ensured adequate variability with limited collinearity to support mixture modeling (e.g., Quantile g-computation and BKMR) and maintained continuity with prior cohort work.

Clinical professionals collected fasting blood samples from participants using heparin-free anticoagulant tubes (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) and stored them at −80 °C. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (PerkinElmer NexION 350D, PerkinElmer, USA) was employed to measure the levels of Cr, Cd, V, Mn and Pb in the blood. Blind and QC samples were included; spike-recovery ranged from 91.16% to 107.42%. Intra- and inter-batch CVs were < 5% and < 10%, respectively. Values < LOD were imputed as LOD/2. For more details, refer to our previous studies [60].

Demographic and menopausal information collection

Menopausal age and demographic characteristics, including age, nation, occupation, education level, personal income, and lifestyle habits such as smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, and physical activity were collected using standardized questionnaires. Additionally, we gathered information on participants' pregnancy and menopause-related details, including the number of pregnancies, miscarriages, mode of delivery, menopausal status, date of the last menstrual period, and other factors that might influence menopause, such as contraceptive methods, delivery methods, and breastfeeding. Physical examinations were conducted using uniform measuring instruments to collect participants' height and weight, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2). To ensure consistency in information collection and assessment, all investigators underwent training before the survey. The completeness of questionnaire information was verified by quality control personnel at the end of the survey.

Covariates

Based on the previous research and the results of DAG diagram, we classified BMI, nation (han or other), occupation(farmer or other), smoking status (never smoked, former or current smoker), alcohol consumption (never drank, previous or current drinkers), annual household income (< 2000, 2000–5000 or > 5000 yuan), intensity of physical activity (light, moderate, and heavy). Furthermore, considering the potential impact of factors such as pregnancy history, history of miscarriages, contraceptive methods, etc., we also further adjusted for prior pregnancies, prior miscarriages, prior live births, mode of delivery (vaginal or C-section), and contraceptive mode (contraceptive ring, other) in the model.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (25th percentile, 75th percentile), and categorical variables as number (percentage). The Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated for the five metal concentrations. Due to the right-skewed distribution of heavy metal concentrations, ln-transformation was performed to improve normality and better fit with the models.

We employed a generalized linear model (GLM) to investigate the impact of individual heavy metals on the age of natural menopause. To obtain more accurate results, we implemented three adjustment model strategies: a crude model, a model adjusting for basic information and lifestyle habits as covariates, and a model further adjusting for covariates such as pregnancy history, history of miscarriages, contraceptive methods, etc. The results were presented as β with a 95% CI.

In previous studies, a single exposure model is typically used, where each model outcome corresponds to a single exposure variable. However, due to the potential for random chance in multiple comparisons, researchers should apply the false discovery rate (FDR) to adjust for multiple comparisons between exposure variables for each result. Given the complex relationship between heavy metals and outcomes, we employed FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

To further investigate whether metal mixtures impact the age of natural menopause and understand which metals contribute the most to the overall effect, we employed the Quantile g-computation model. It not only calculates the overall effect on the age of natural menopause when all heavy metals increase simultaneously by a quantile but also generates the proportion of positive or negative contributions of each metal to the overall effect. This is indicated by positive or negative weight values, with the sum of weights in both directions equaling 1 or −1. We adjusted for BMI, nation, annual household income, education, occupation, smoking, drinking, physical activity, sleep duration, prior pregnancies, prior live births, prior miscarriages, delivery mode, contraceptive mode, breastfeeding in the model.

To validate the overall effects obtained from the Quantile g-computation model and reproduce the results of individual heavy metals on the age of menopause from the GLM model, and to explore whether there is a potential non-linear relationship between heavy metals and the age of menopause, we further employed the Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) model [7]. By running 10,000 iterations of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm, we primarily assessed three key outcomes: 1) the overall effect of the mixture on the outcome, estimating changes in the age of menopause when all five metals are simultaneously fixed at different percentiles compared to when fixed at the median; 2) the dose–response relationship, examining the exposure–response relationship between individual metals and the age of menopause when the other four metals are fixed at the median, allowing observation of non-linear associations between heavy metals and the age of menopause; and 3) univariate associations, measuring the difference in the association between a single metal and the age of menopause when the other four metals are fixed at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles while the metal itself is positioned at the 75th and 25th percentiles, respectively.Additionally, the model determined the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) for each metal, considering a metal as dominant when PIP ≥ 0.5. We adjusted for the same covariates as in the Quantile g-computation.

Descriptive analyses and GLMs were performed in SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA); qgcomp (v2.10.1) and BKMR (v0.2.2) were implemented in R 4.0.5. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population and distribution of blood metals

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and menopause-related information of 414 participants. The majority of participants were Han Chinese (70.3%) farmers (89.1%), with a mean age (SD) of 51.1 (3.4) years and a BMI (SD) of 24.9 (3.5) kg/m2. The average menopausal age for all participants is 49.7 years. Participants with a family annual income greater than 5000 yuan accounted for a slightly higher proportion (39.1%), but the educational level was predominantly elementary school or illiterate (53.6%). Only a small percentage of participants reported smoking (14.9%) or alcohol consumption (11.8%). Moderate physical activity was prevalent among participants (37.4%), and a majority reported having more than 7 h of sleep per day (57.5%). The vast majority of women chose vaginal delivery (95.7%) and breastfeeding (96.4%), with most having a history of two pregnancies (43.5%) or deliveries (48.6%). Contraceptive ring use was chosen by 56.8% of participants, and 51.4% reported no history of miscarriage.

The association between individual heavy metals and natural menopausal age

The results of the GLM revealed associations between individual heavy metals and natural menopausal age (Table 2). In both the unadjusted model and the model adjusted for covariates, a positive association was observed between blood Cd levels and menopausal age (βmodel1 = 1.63; 95%CI: 0.43, 2.83; P = 0.01; βmodel2 = 1.40; 95%CI: 0.19, 2.61; P = 0.02; βmodel3 = 1.30; 95%CI: 0.12, 2.48; P = 0.03), while a negative association was noted between blood lead (Pb) levels and menopausal age (βmodel1 = −1.35; 95%CI: −2.53, −0.17; P = 0.03; βmodel2 = −1.29; 95%CI: −2.48, −0.10; P = 0.03; βmodel3 = −1.25; 95%CI: −2.43, −0.07; P = 0.04). This suggests that for each unit increase in ln-transformed Cd and Pb, the average delay in menopausal age is 1.3 years and an advancement of 1.25 years, respectively.

The association between the metal mixture and natural menopausal age

Table S1 and Figure S2 illustrate the impact of the metal mixture on natural menopausal age and the proportional contributions of each metal to the overall association in the Quantile g-computation model. The results indicate that the metal mixture does not exhibit statistically significant associations with menopausal age(β = −0.11; 95%CI: −0.76, 0.53; P = 0.73). However, we observe that Cd carries the largest positive weight in the overall association (WeightCd = 0.49), while V and Pb both contribute negative weights(WeightV = −0.67; Weight Pb = −0.33).

In the BKMR model, we initially estimated the Prior Inclusion Probabilities (PIPs) for the five metals, and we observed that the PIPs for Cd and Pb were both greater than 0.5 (Table S2). This suggests that Cd and Pb have the most substantial impact on the overall association of the metal mixture. Furthermore, we conducted additional assessments of the dose–response relationships between individual metals and menopausal age (Fig. 2A). This helps us discern potential non-linear associations between exposure and outcome. We observed that Cr has a minimal impact on menopausal age, while Cd and Mn exhibit approximately linear positive relationships with menopausal age, and V and Pb show approximately linear negative relationships. Figure 2B assesses the impact of the metal mixture on menopausal age, revealing a negative trend without statistical significance. Figure 2C evaluates the influence of individual heavy metals on menopausal age, and we observe a positive association with Cd and a negative association with Pb. Moreover, with increasing concentrations of Cd and Pb, menopausal age experiences slight increases or decreases, respectively.

Stratified analysis

To identify high-risk populations for the impact of heavy metals on natural menopausal age and strategies to mitigate the adverse associations of heavy metals, we conducted further stratified analyses based on BMI (< 25 or ≥ 25) and sleep duration (< 7 h or ≥ 7 h). The results are presented in Fig. 3. We observed that, compared to individuals with BMI < 25 (βCd = −0.11; 95%CI: −0.76, 0.53; P = 0.83; βPb = −1.04; 95%CI: −2.60, 0.52; P = 0.19), both Cd and Pb exhibited significant association estimates in individuals with BMI ≥ 25 (βCd = 2.40; 95%CI: 0.71, 4.10; P = 0.01; βPb = −1.52; 95%CI: −2.99, −0.04; P = 0.04). Similarly, compared to individuals with sleep duration ≥ 7 h(βCd = 0.17; 95%CI: −1.25, 1.59; P = 0.81; βPb = −0.79; 95%CI: −1.43, −0.16; P = 0.02), Cd (βCd = 2.79; 95%CI: 0.29, 5.29; P = 0.03) and Pb (βPb = −3.64; 95%CI: −5.80, −1.47; P = 0.00) showed significant association estimates in individuals with sleep duration < 7 h. While the metal mixture did not demonstrate significant significance in the stratified analysis, the individual metal associations remained consistent with the results from the stratified analysis of the GLM (Figure S3-S5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the associations of individual and metal mixture on the natural age of menopause, and the results indicated an association between heavy metals and the age of menopause. The heavy metal Cd was found to delay the age of menopause, while Pb was associated with an earlier onset of menopause. Through further stratified analysis, we observed significant variations in the associations of Cd and Pb on the age of menopause among different BMI and sleep duration groups. Additionally, greater adverse associations were observed in individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and a sleep duration < 7 h, highlighting the importance of maintaining a healthy weight and adequate sleep. Our study provides new insights into identifying adverse factors that influence the natural age of menopause in women and offers directions for avoiding or mitigating the impacts of these adverse factors.

Cd

Cd is a recognized endocrine disruptor, and numerous studies have reported adverse effects on female reproduction. Higher Cd exposure has been associated with delayed menarche [49], reduced fertility and fecundity [1, 5, 6], and increased risk of spontaneous abortion, potentially through alterations in reproductive hormone secretion, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways. However, evidence directly linking Cd to age at natural menopause is limited [43]. In our data, higher blood Cd was associated with an older age at natural menopause in single-metal models; after FDR control, the association was significant or borderline depending on covariate adjustment. A study from China likewise reported a higher menopausal age in Cd-contaminated areas compared with a control area [8]. By contrast, an occupational study observed increased odds of menopause onset with Cd exposure in unadjusted models, but the association attenuated to non-significance after adjustment—potentially reflecting limited sample size and exposure misclassification (self-reported history rather than biomarkers) [33]. Mechanistically, Cd has demonstrated metalloestrogenic activity via ERα activation in experimental systems, but such findings do not establish effects on menopausal timing in humans [19]. Human observational work has also linked Cd to PCOS-related endocrine features—for example, altered androgens, sex hormone–binding globulin, and anti-Müllerian hormone—which could, in theory, align with a later menopausal age; however, directions vary by age and context, and we did not measure these pathways [28]. Epidemiologic evidence on female reproductive timing is likewise mixed: several cohorts report later menarche with higher Cd, whereas studies directly addressing natural menopause remain sparse and heterogeneous [47]. one large NHANES analysis found that higher blood Cd was associated with a longer reproductive lifespan (menarche-to-menopause span), an indirect proxy for older menopause [33].Taken together, we view the Cd finding as hypothesis-generating and emphasize the need for longitudinal studies with repeated metal measures, metal speciation, and reproductive-endocrine profiling to clarify causality.

Pb

Pb is a hazardous metal with no known health benefits to the human body. Pb can adversely affect numerous organs, especially the kidneys, nervous system, and reproductive system [13]. Animal studies have demonstrated that Pb can disrupt follicle formation in female mice, leading to follicle development disorders [53]. Research has suggested that the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, which plays a vital role in regulating the reproductive process of mammals [26], is impacted by Pb, disrupting its normal function and resulting in abnormal follicle development [46]. Two cross-sectional studies conducted in the United States have reported that Pb exposure in women with occupational exposure can associated with early menopause and shorten reproductive years [44] and can shorten reproductive years [34]. Furthermore, a US cohort study has linked low levels of cumulative Pb exposure to an earlier onset of menopause age [15]. Our study also observed that blood Pb was associated with an earlier onset of menopause, which is consistent with previous findings.

Mn, Cr and V

Mn is an essential metal in the body and is involved in various physiological processes, including skeletal system development, energy metabolism, enzyme activation, nervous system function, and immune system function [51]. Studies have also shown that Mn can play an important role in the reproductive system by interfering with the regulation of hormone secretion related to puberty [12]. In vitro animal experiments have demonstrated that even low concentrations of Mn can produce toxic and genotoxic associations on ovarian cells of Chinese hamsters [18]. Yangho Kim et al. have also demonstrated that Mn may produce related toxicokinetics to female menopausal states [29]. Cr has two valence states, with Cr(III) being an essential metal for human metabolism, while Cr(VI) is harmful to the human body and carcinogenic [25]. Animal studies have found that maternal mice exposed to Cr(VI) during pregnancy can associated with premature ovarian failure in the offspring [50]. V is a non-essential metal for the human body, and animal experiments have reported that V has a significant association on the content of glycogen in uterine and ovarian tissues of lactating rats(Mohammad Mehdi Ommati 2021). We did not observe an association between Cr, Mn, and V and the natural age of menopause in our study, which is consistent with the findings of two cohort studies conducted in the United States [56, 58]. However, these animal studies suggest that the relationship between Cr, Mn, V, and the natural age of menopause warrants further investigation through epidemiological and clinical trials.

Joint association

Currently, there is limited research on the association between metal mixtures and natural age of menopause. To our knowledge, only two studies have explored this relationship, and both observed that metal mixtures can associated with an earlier onset of menopause [56, 58]. In our study, although the results from Quantile g-computation and BKMR showed a negative association of metal mixtures on the age of menopause, no significant association was observed. This may be due to the following three reasons: 1) Our study observed that Cd can delay the age of menopause, while Pb can advance it, and the independent opposite associations of these metals offset each other, resulting in a non-significant mixed effects; 2) Different methods were used to assess the levels of metal mixtures. We used logarithmically transformed metal values, while they constructed a composite environmental risk score to represent the levels of metal mixtures; 3) Different biological media were used for metal assessment. Our study measured blood metals, which may better reflect the internal exposure load of certain metals, such as Pb, compared to nail metals and urinary metals [27].

BMI

BMI reflects the nutritional status of participants and has a significant impact on the health and quality of life of menopausal women [20]. Existing research indicates that individuals with lower BMI have a higher risk of early menopause, while those with overweight or obesity have a higher risk of delayed menopause [62], and premenopausal obese women tend to have lower estrogen levels [39]. This suggests an association between BMI and age of menopause. However, to our knowledge, the current research on the relationship between heavy metals and natural age of menopause has not specifically investigated the impact of BMI on this association. The research indicates that, compared to females with a BMI < 25, those with a higher BMI exhibit a higher incidence of polycystic ovary syndrome. Our stratified analysis results suggest that heavy metals have different associations on the age of menopause in populations with different BMI levels (< 25 or ≥ 25). Additionally, for individuals with BMI ≥ 25, heavy metals demonstrate a stronger adverse impact, which may be a result of the synergistic interaction between BMI and heavy metals.

Duration of sleep

The duration of sleep can affect various endocrine hormones in the body [37], and shorter sleep duration has been shown to impact the menstrual cycle [4]. A study from Japan indicated that sleep duration less than 6 h was associated with a later onset of menopause [38]. Currently, it is commonly considered that a sleep duration of 7 h is optimal [42]. Therefore, using 7 h as a cutoff point, we investigated the variations in the associations of heavy metals on the age of menopause among different sleep durations (< 7 h or ≥ 7 h). The results indicated that a sleep duration shorter than 7 h seemed to exacerbate the associations of heavy metals on natural menopause.

Limitation

This study provides evidence and direction for the association between heavy metals and the age of menopause, as well as strategies to mitigate this association. However, our study also has some limitations: 1) Given the cross-sectional design, blood metals were measured after menopause; thus, associations cannot be interpreted causally and reverse causation cannot be excluded. In addition, our ≤ 24-month inclusion window—implemented to reduce recall error and better match biomarker timing—is not biologically anchored and may introduce selection bias if excluded women differ systematically. We therefore interpret all associations cautiously.; 2) Our study population was relatively homogeneous (e.g., 89.1% farmers and 53.6% with low education) and was drawn from a single, long-standing ferroalloy-related pollution site, so the findings may not be generalizable to women in other settings with different exposure profiles and sociodemographic characteristics; 3) In this study, we did not differentiate the valence states of metals, such as Cr(III) being beneficial to the human body while Cr(VI) being harmful; 4)While other metals (e.g., arsenic, mercury) may also be relevant to reproductive aging, we prioritized a focused five-metal panel (Cr, Cd, V, Mn, Pb) to balance biological relevance, laboratory feasibility, archived sample volume, and statistical interpretability for mixture analyses in this cohort; future work could expand the panel to additional elements and matrices and incorporate metal speciation.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that higher Cd was associated with an older age at menopause, while higher Pb was associated with a younger age at menopause; however, reverse temporality precludes causal inference.. Maintaining a reasonable BMI and having sufficient sleep can mitigate the adverse associations of Cd and Pb on the age of menopause. Our research provides new insights into identifying factors that influence the natural age of menopause and methods to mitigate adverse associations. Future research should further conduct longitudinal studies and utilize biomarkers related to the natural age of menopause as outcomes to further confirm this association.
