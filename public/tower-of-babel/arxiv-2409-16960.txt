Unified quantitative analysis of the Stokes equations in dilute perforated domains via layer potentials
Wenjia Jing, Yong Lu, Christophe Prange

Abstract
. We develop a unified method to obtain the quantitative homogenization of Stokes systems in periodically perforated domains with no-slip boundary conditions on the perforating holes. The main novelty of our paper is a quantitative analysis of the asymptotic behavior of the two-scale cell correctors via periodic Stokes layer potentials. The two-scale cell correctors were introduced and analyzed qualitatively by Allaire in the early 90’s [ 3 ] . Thanks to our layer potential approach, we also provide a novel explanation of the conductivity matrix in Darcy’s model, of the Brinkman term in Brinkman’s model, and explain the special behavior for d = 2 d=2 . Finally, we also prove quantitative homogenization error estimates in various regimes of ratios between the size of the perforating holes and the typical distance between holes. In particular we handle a subtle issue in the dilute Darcy regime related to the non-vanishing of the Darcy velocity on the boundary. Key words : periodic homogenization, perforated domain, Darcy’s law, Brinkman’s law, Stokes layer potentials Mathematics subject classification (MSC 2020) : 35B27, 35B40, 35J08, 35Q35, 76D07



1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to a classical problem in the homogenization of fluids, namely the Stokes problem in a perforated structure
\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta}
with
\varepsilon
-periodic distributed holes of size
\varepsilon\eta
, where
\eta
measures the ratio between the size of the holes and the mutual distance between the holes:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u^{\varepsilon,\eta}+\nabla p^{\varepsilon,\eta}=f,&\quad&x\in\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta},\\
&\nabla\cdot u^{\varepsilon,\eta}=0,&\quad&x\in\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta},\\
&u^{\varepsilon,\eta}=0,&\quad&x\in\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where as usual
u^{\varepsilon,\eta}
is the velocity field and
p^{\varepsilon,\eta}
the pressure. Here we stress that the domain, the velocity and the pressure all depend on
\varepsilon
and
\eta
. This dependence on two parameters is at the heart of this work and our goal is to provide a unified construction and analysis of the two-scale correctors that depend on both
\varepsilon
and
\eta
. Later on, in order to alleviate the notations, we will remove the explicit dependence on
\eta
.

The homogenization theory concerns the asymptotic behavior of (
1.1
) in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that
\eta^{d}
is essentially the volume fraction of the holes and the parameter
\eta
plays an important role in the homogenization of (
1.1
). We say the geometric configuration of the holes is in the
dilute
setting if
\eta\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(e.g., if
\eta=\varepsilon^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
) and is in the
classical
setting if otherwise, i.e., if
\eta
is of order one. See Figure
1
for an illustration of dilute holes.

Figure 1.
The periodically perforated domain

Although this problem has a long history, dating back to the first derivation of Darcy’s law by Tartar
[
27
]
, there is a recent surge of research activity around system (
1.1
) that mainly focuses on two issues: (i) optimal quantitative estimates, (ii) the large-scale/uniform regularity theory. Our work focuses on the first question. We develop a method based on the quantitative analysis of two-scale cell correctors via layer potentials that enables to treat in a unified way all dilute and non-dilute regimes for the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\eta
. This line of research was pioneered by Allaire in
[
3
]
for the Stokes system, but it was not until recently that a systematic quantitative study involving layer potentials was carried out by the first author of this paper, first for the Laplace equation
[
11
]
and then for the Lamé system
[
12
]
; for a qualitative study in the context of the Stokes equations, see
[
15
]
.

For the non-dilute classical case, the first quantitative result for the homogenization of (
1.1
) was given by Marušić-Paloka and Mikelić in
[
16
]
, where
O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{6}})
convergence rates were shown in dimension two (
d=2
), and the proof there applies to the stationary Navier-Stokes equations also. This result was improved recently by Shen in
[
22
]
where sharp convergence rate of order
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
was achieved for all
d\geq 2
. Shen also studied the uniform regularity problem for (
1.1
) in
[
21
]
, and established large-scale interior
C^{1,\alpha}
and interior Lipschitz estimates for the velocity as well as the corresponding estimates for the pressure field. Moreover, uniform
W^{k,p}
estimates for any
k\geq 1,\ 1<p<\infty
was also established in
[
21
]
for the smooth setting, which offered a rigorous proof for the main results claimed by Masmoudi in
[
17
]
. Recently in
[
5
]
, Balazi
et. al.
considered perforated domain with possibly connected solid structure, which is more physical relevant compared with isolated solid obstacles and they obtained sharp
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
convergence rate.

Quantitative homogenization of elliptic equations with high contrast coefficients has been studied intensively as well. In
[
23
]
and furthermore in
[
26
]
, quantitative convergence rates, together with the uniform weighted Lipschitz and
W^{1,p}
estimates were given for scalar equations. See
[
20
,
9
]
for convergence rates and uniform Lipschitz regularity in elliptic systems with high contrast coefficients, and see
[
29
]
for almost-sharp error estimates for linear elasticity systems in periodically perforated domains. In those works, the geometric configuration of the perforating holes or the high contrast inclusions is the non-dilute classical one.

For the dilute case with multi-scale period structure, in a series of papers, Shen
et. al.
studied the uniform regularity theory for the scalar Poisson equation in perforated domains with zero Dirichlet boundary there. In
[
24
]
, nearly optimal
W^{1,p},\ 1<p<\infty
estimates with bounding constants depending explicitly on the small parameters
\varepsilon
and
\eta
were established; the results were further improved in
[
25
,
18
]
.

Our goal in this paper is to present a unified proof of convergence rates of the Stokes system for three different regimes of dilute perforated domain where the limit systems are Darcy’s law for the case of relatively large holes, Brinkman’s law for the case of critical size holes, and the full domain Stokes equations for relatively small holes. Our proof relies on layer potential analysis of the periodic two-scale cell problem.

1.1.
Criticality: a short state of the art

The homogenized equation for (
1.1
) in the classical setting, i.e.,
\eta
is a fixed number, is Darcy’s law. It can be derived using the two-scale expansion method; see e.g.,
[
13
,
19
]
. The formal arguments were first made rigorous by Tartar
[
27
]
, where a cell problem plays a key role: it is the building block for the construction of oscillating test functions.

The asymptotic behavior of (
1.1
) in the dilute setting, i.e.
\eta=\eta(\varepsilon)
tending to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
, was systematically studied by Allaire
[
1
,
2
]
. These two works were significant generalizations of an earlier work by Cioranescu and Murat
[
6
]
for the homogenization of Poisson equations in dilutely perforated domains. Allaire introduced the scaling factor

\sigma_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\kappa_{\eta}^{-1}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon|\log\eta|^{\frac{1}{2}},&\qquad d=2,\\
\varepsilon\eta^{-\frac{d-2}{2}},&\qquad d\geq 3,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\kappa_{\eta}:=\begin{cases}|\log\eta|^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad&d=2,\\
\eta^{\frac{d-2}{2}},\qquad&d\geq 3.\end{cases}

(1.2)

and according to the asymptotic behavior of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, he identified three different regimes for the homogenization of (
1.1
) in the dilute setting:

•

If
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\sigma_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\sigma_{0}
is a positive number comparable to
1
, i.e.,
\eta=O(\varepsilon^{2/(d-2)})
for
d\geq 3
or
|\log\eta|\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
for
d=2
, we say the configuration is in the
critical
setting.

•

If
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, i.e.,
\eta\gg\varepsilon^{2/(d-2)}
for
d\geq 3
or
|\log\eta|\gg\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
for
d=2
, we say the configuration is in the
dilute super-critical
setting.

•

If
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, i.e.,
\eta\ll\varepsilon^{2/(d-2)}
for
d\geq 3
or
|\log\eta|\ll\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
for
d=2
, we say the configuration is in the
sub-critical
setting.

Note that the classical setting, where
\eta\sim 1
and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\sim\varepsilon
, is a super-critical one.

The qualitative homogenization theory in those dilute settings is as follows: (i) For the critical setting, the homogenized system for (
1.1
) is Brinkman’s law where an extra zero-order term appears compared with the Stokes operator. (ii) For the dilute super-critical setting, the homogenized system is the Darcy’s law but there are two main differences compared with the non-dilute setting. First,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
varies with
\eta
and determines the
L^{2}
-scale under which the velocity field converges to the limit; secondly, the permeability matrix in the Darcy’s law remains the same, i.e., does not vary with
\eta
, for all dilute super-critical settings and is different from the non-dilute super-critical one. (iii) For the sub-critical setting, the homogenized system is the unperturbed Stokes system in the whole domain without perforation.

For quantitative convergence rate of periodic homogenization of (
1.1
) in bounded perforated domain, the critical and sub-critical settings are simpler because the boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
for the velocity field in the limit system agrees with (
1.1
). This is not the case for the super-critical case, in either the classical or the dilute settings; indeed, the velocity field
u
in Darcy’s law only satisfies
u\cdot\nu=0
on
\partial\Omega
and its tangential component can be of order one. It is known that the standard cut-off useful for convergence rate in the homogenization of elliptic equations does not work well for the super-critical settings also because of the incompressibility condition. Construction of boundary layer correctors for
d=2
was given by Marušić-Paloka and Mikelić
[
16
]
and they proved
O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{6}})
convergence rate. Their method worked for certain stationary Navier-Stokes equations but is restricted to
d=2
because the tangential velocity corrector is constructed via stream functions. Recently, in
[
22
]
and dealing with the more general case of
d\geq 2
and non-zero Dirichlet data of order
O(\varepsilon^{2})
on the outer boundary
\partial\Omega
, She proved the sharp
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
homogenization rate for (
1.1
) by introducing two boundary layer correctors, with properly chosen data on
\partial\Omega
, one tangential and the other normal to
\partial\Omega
. It was observed in
[
28
]
that the so-called radial test functions (see section
5.3
below) can be constructed so that a boundary cut-off argument goes through for the convergence rate to Darcy’s law. In section
5.3
of this paper, we use this observation for the convergence rate proof for the dilute Darcy’s law, using only a cut-off argument.

Very recently after the completion of our work, we noticed that, in
[
5
]
, Balazi, Allaire and Omnes studied the homogenization (
1.1
) for
d\geq 2
in the more physical relevant case of connected solid structures and obtained
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
convergence rate. Their method utilizes a cut-off argument only because they managed to construct a velocity field supported near
\partial\Omega
which plays the role of a cut-off of the limit velocity field
u
but remains incompressible. Their construction generalizes an earlier construction in
[
10
]
for
d=2
. In some sense, the construction is achieved by a remarkable and simple observation that one can insert the usual cut-off inside the
\mathrm{curl}
operator to simultaneously achieve cut-off and preserve incompressibility. Clearly, we can use this construction in section
5.3
alternatively.

1.2.
Allaire’s two-scale cell correctors: a unified approach to homogenization in perforated domains

As in the case of Poisson equations
[
11
]
and of Lamé systems
[
12
]
, our strategy to establish quantitative homogenization in periodically dilute perforated domains, with Dirichlet type boundary conditions in the holes, is to build two-scale cell correctors.

In this paper,
\eta
depends in general on
\varepsilon
, and we hence need to study the limit of
(\chi_{k},\omega_{k})
as
\eta
converges to zero with
\varepsilon
. We find it more convenient to work with a rescaled cell problem, defining
\chi^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\eta^{d-2}\chi_{k}(\eta x)
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\eta^{d-1}\omega_{k}(\eta x)
. These satisfy the following Stokes system in the rescaled torus
\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d}
, with a hole
\overline{T}
of unit size removed:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\chi^{\eta}_{k}+\nabla\omega^{\eta}_{k}=\eta^{d}e_{k},&\quad&x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\nabla\cdot\chi^{\eta}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\chi^{\eta}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\partial T.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

For each fixed
\eta\in(0,1)
, the classical theory for Stokes system applies to (
1.3
), which asserts that a weak solution
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
is uniquely determined in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
is unique up to an additive constant. The correctors
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
are extended by zero in
T
so that they are functions on the torus. The precise choice of
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
(outside the hole) is made clear in (
4.3
).

Finally, the building blocks for oscillating test functions are given by the further rescaled functions

v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\chi^{\eta}_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta}\right)\;&\text{if }d\geq 3\\
\frac{1}{|\log\eta|}\chi^{\eta}_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta}\right)\;&\text{if }d=2,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta}\omega^{\eta}_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta}\right)\;&\text{if }d\geq 3\\
\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta|\log\eta|}\omega^{\eta}_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta}\right)\;&\text{if }d=2.\end{cases}

(1.4)

Clearly, the pair
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
is
\varepsilon\mathbb{T}^{d}
-periodic and satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta v^{\varepsilon}_{k}+\nabla q^{\varepsilon}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}e_{k},&\quad&x\in\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{f},\\
&\nabla\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{f},\\
&v^{\varepsilon}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{f}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

Note that the holes in
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
coincide with (part of) those in
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{f}
. Hence, given any scalar valued test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
,
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\varphi
is easily checked to be an oscillatory function in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})
and we will use it as the test function for (
1.1
).
Our method for unified quantitative homogenization results relies on the asymptotic analysis for
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
, in the limit when
\varepsilon\to 0
, see Theorem
1.4
.

Let us mention that although Allaire did not use the two-scale cell problems (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) to prove homogenization results in
[
1
,
2
]
, but he studied those problems in
[
3
]
to establish a continuity of the effective conductivity matrix
M=M_{\eta}
in Darcy’s law, in the limit when the hole-cell ratio
\eta
vanishes to zero. In fact, he showed that
(\chi^{\eta}_{k},\omega^{\eta}_{k})
converge in some proper sense to solutions of a problem which he named the
local
problem: for
d\geq 3
and for every
k\in\{1,\ldots\,d\}
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta w_{k}+\nabla q_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\nabla\cdot w_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&w_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\partial T,\\
&w_{k}\to M^{-1}e_{k},\qquad&\text{as }|x|\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.6)

Here,
M
is a symmetric matrix defined by the components
(m_{ik})
in (
1.18
) below. The behavior at infinity of
w_{k}
, the well-posedness of (
1.6
) and the positivity of the matrix
M
are all established in
[
3
]
, as parts of the proof of the convergence of
(\chi^{\eta}_{k},\omega^{\eta}_{k})
. Using those results, one can devise a unified proof based on the classical oscillating test function argument for qualitative homogenization that works for all regimes of vanishing
\eta
; see
[
15
]
.

1.3.
Outline of the main results of the paper

In this paper, we provide a unified quantitative homogenization method for all values of the dilution parameter
\eta
. Our method grounds on quantitative estimates of two-scale correctors via layer potential techniques. Indeed, we quantify the convergence results for the unified correctors
(v^{\eta}_{k},q^{\eta}_{k})
, see the definition (
1.5
), first obtained qualitatively by Allaire
[
3
]
.
The advantages of our method are the following. Firstly, the method provides natural correctors
(v^{\eta}_{k},q^{\eta}_{k})
that enable us to treat all asymptotic regimes, determined by the relative smallness of
\eta
compared with
\varepsilon
, in a unified manner. Secondly, we obtain explicit representation formulas which lead to quantitative convergence rates for the correctors
(v^{\eta}_{k},q^{\eta}_{k})
.
Thirdly, by exploring the properties of layer potentials, we provide a novel explanation of the conductivity matrix in Darcy’s model, of the Brinkman term in Brinkman’s model, and explain the special behavior for
d=2
.

We start with the limit and estimates for the velocity field
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
. Let
M
be the symmetric positive definite matrix defined by

M:=\begin{cases}(A_{T})^{-1}\qquad&\text{for }d\geq 3,\\
{4\pi}I\qquad&\text{for }d=2.\end{cases}

(1.7)

Here, for
d\geq 3
,
A_{T}
is the matrix defined by in Lemma
3.1
using layer potentials. In fact,
M=A_{T}^{-1}
is the analog of Newtonian capacitance associated to
T
in the setting of homogeneous Stokes system.

Theorem 1.1

(quantitative analysis of two-scale cell correctors)
.

We assume that Assumption
1
holds. Let
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
,
k=1,\dots\,d
, be the velocity field defined by (
1.4
) and solving (
1.5
). There exists a universal constant
C>0
so that the following results hold.

(1)

For each
k
and for all regimes,

\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}.

(1.8)

(2)

In the critical setting and for all
k
,

\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rightharpoonup 0,\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

(1.9)

(3)

In all dilute regimes and for all
k\in\{1,\ldots\,d\}
, with
p=\frac{2d}{d-2}
for
d\geq 3
and
p=2
for
d=2
, we have

\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\kappa_{\eta}.

(1.10)

Note that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
and
\kappa_{\eta}=\varepsilon/\sigma_{\varepsilon}
are defined by (
1.2
).

In particular, the result in item (3) above shows,
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
converges in
L^{p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the constant vector field
M^{-1}e_{k}
. A qualitative strong convergence result itself can be proved by weak compactness arguments as done in
[
3
]
. Notice that the rate given here is new and is a byproduct of the layer potential method developed in our paper.

Theorem 1.2

(quantitative homogenization)
.

We assume that Assumption
1
holds. Assume further that, in each regime, the homogenized problem has a solution
u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
and
p\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
. We can find constants
C
that depends only on
d
,
\Omega
and
T
, such that

(1)

In the
dilute super-critical
setting,

\big\|\frac{u^{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}-u\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p^{\varepsilon}-p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\kappa_{\eta}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\varepsilon}})\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}.

(1.11)

The limit
(u,p)
is the unique solution to the following Darcy’s law:

u=M^{-1}(f-\nabla p)\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,\qquad\text{and}\quad u\cdot n=0\quad\text{in}\quad\partial\Omega.

(1.12)

(2)

In the
critical setting
, with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\sigma_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\big\|u^{\varepsilon}-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}\big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\big\|p^{\varepsilon}-p-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(\kappa_{\eta}+\left|1-\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}/\sigma^{2}_{0}\right|\right)\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}.

(1.13)

The limit
(u,p)
is the unique solution to the following Brinkman’s law

-\Delta u+\nabla p+\frac{M}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}u=f\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,\qquad\text{and}\quad\quad u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\partial\Omega.

(1.14)

(3)

In the
sub-critical setting
, we have

\|u^{\varepsilon}-v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p^{\varepsilon}-p\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(\kappa_{\eta}+\sigma^{-2}_{\varepsilon}\right)\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}.

(1.15)

The limit
(u,p)
is the unique solution to the unperturbed Stokes problem

-\Delta u+\nabla p=f\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\cdot u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,\qquad\text{and}\quad u=0\quad\text{in}\quad\partial\Omega.

(1.16)

Note that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
and
\kappa_{\eta}=\varepsilon/\sigma_{\varepsilon}
are defined by (
1.2
).

The convergence rates in Theorem
1.2
were given by Allaire in
[
1
,
2
]
in all three cases, except that in the dilute super-critical setting, the trace of
u
on the outer boundary
\partial\Omega
was implicitly assumed to be zero. In Section
5
we establish the convergence rate for the homogenization of (
1.1
) in the dilute super-critical case in the general setting, i.e. without assuming
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
. This turns out to be a very non-trivial task, as is already the case in the classical setting with
\eta=1
; see
[
22
]
for the discussion of the problem and for the proof in the classical setting.

In the dilute super-critical setting, the additional small parameter
\eta
only makes the problem even more technical; see in particular the comment below (
5.2
). Thanks to the estimates of
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
achieved in our paper, and in particular the
L^{\infty}
estimate (
4.17
) for the stress, it is possible to follow and generalize the argument of Shen
[
22
]
to the dilute setting. It turns out, however, an easier approach can be followed using the so-called
radial
cut-off function proposed by Wang, Xu and Zhang
[
28
]
. We follow this second approach.

Remark 1.3

(an explicit representation of Darcy’s conductivity matrix and Brinkman’s term)
.

As a byproduct of the analysis of the layer potentials developed in this paper, we obtain explicit formulas for the conductivity matrix in Darcy’s regime.
The basis
\{\phi^{*}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,d}
, found in (
3.3
), is closely related to the so-called
local problems
introduced by Allaire in
[
1
]
. Indeed, for each
k=1,\dots,d
, let
(u_{k},p_{k})
be
(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{k}]+a^{*}_{k},\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{k}])
in
T_{+}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
, then they satisfy (for
d\geq 3
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u_{k}-\nabla p_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\nabla\cdot u_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&u_{k}=0,\qquad&\text{in }\partial T,\\
&u_{k}\to a^{*}_{k},\qquad&\text{as }|x|\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.17)

For
d=2
, the last line should be changed to

u_{k}\sim\Gamma_{k}(x)+a^{*}_{k},\qquad\text{as }\,|x|\to\infty.

Compare the equations above with those in the local problem of
[
3
, Eqn. (2.4)]
, or (
1.6
) above. We can check that, for
d\geq 3
, the solutions to the local problem are given by the functions
w_{k}:=m_{ki}u_{i}
and
q_{k}:=m_{ki}p_{i}
, where the numbers
m_{ij}
are the components of
M
. Notice that
M=A_{T}^{-1}
, where
A_{T}
is defined by (
3.2
). We check that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\nabla w_{i}:\nabla w_{k}

\displaystyle=m_{is}m_{k\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\nabla u_{s}:\nabla u_{\ell}

(1.18)

\displaystyle=m_{is}m_{k\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\nabla\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{s}]:\nabla\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=-m_{is}m_{k\ell}\int_{\partial T}\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{s}]\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi^{*}_{\ell}]}{\partial\nu}\Big|_{+}=m_{is}m_{k\ell}a^{*}_{s}\cdot e_{\ell}=m_{ik}.

where
\mathcal{S}_{T}
is the single-layer potential defined by (
2.5
).
This shows that the definition
M:=A^{-1}_{T}
, defined by quantities coming from layer potential theory, agrees with that of Allaire
[
3
]
, defined using the above ‘local problems’.

1.5.
Geometric setup and main assumptions

We first describe the geometric setup of the perforated domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
; see Figure
1
. Let
T\subset Q_{1}
be a set satisfying the following conditions.

(A1)

T
is an open set and satisfies
B_{1/16}\subset T\subset B_{3/8}
. Roughly speaking,
T
is relatively centered at the origin and is well separated from
\partial Q_{1}
.

(A2)

T_{+}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
is connected;
\partial T
is of class
C^{1,\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and, for simplicity,
T
is simply connected.

Assumption (A2) simplifies the analysis of single- and double-layer potential operators. The fact that
\partial T
is of class
C^{1,\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
implies that
\mathcal{K}_{T}-\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*}
is a compact operator; see Section
3
. The fact that
T
is simply connected implies in particular that that there is just one hole and that
\partial T
is connected; see Remark
3.6
.

Fix a parameter
\eta\in(0,1]
, we define the perforated unit cell to be
Y_{\rm f}=\overline{Q}_{1}\setminus(\eta\overline{T})
; this set is the building block for
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. The subscript
f
denotes the fluid part of the domain. By the assumptions above, both
Y_{\rm f}
and the removed set
\eta T
are connected. In general, we can allow
T
to have multiple connected components as long as
Y_{\rm f}
remains connected. For simplicity of the presentation, however, we impose (A2) above. We then take copies of
Y_{\rm f}
, distribute them periodically and glue them together; the resulting set is
\cup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(z+Y_{\rm f})
and is denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
. Clearly,

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\cup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left(z+\eta\overline{T}\right).

We emphasize that
Y_{\rm f}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
depend on
\eta
, as
\eta\overline{T}
is the model hole removed in those sets. Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we rescale
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
to
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
defined by

\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\cup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\;\varepsilon\left(z+\eta\overline{T}\right)

which is an
\varepsilon
-periodic perforated domain; the array of holes
\cup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\varepsilon(z+\eta T)
is denoted by
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm s}
. Fix an open set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that
\Omega
satisfies the following assumptions.

(A3)

\Omega
is bounded open and simply connected, and the boundary
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
.

It would be natural to define the perforated domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
by
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}\cap\Omega
. However, the small holes in
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
may cut
\partial\Omega
in very irregular ways (possible cusps…). To avoid such situations, we modify the definition by removing (i.e., filling) the holes near
\partial\Omega
. More precisely, for any small number
t>0
, let
\Omega_{(t)}
denote the set
\{x\in\Omega\,:\,d(x,\partial\Omega)>t\}
. We define

\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta}:=\Omega\setminus\cup_{z\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\;\varepsilon(z+\eta\overline{T}),

(1.19)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is an index set defined by
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}:=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,:\,\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})\subset\Omega_{(\varepsilon)}\}
. By this construction, the holes in
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
never cut
\partial\Omega
; in fact, there is no hole in the layer
\Omega^{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega_{(\varepsilon)}
which has a width of order
O(\varepsilon)
. We define
K_{\varepsilon}:=\Omega\setminus\cup_{z\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\;\varepsilon\left(z+\overline{Q}_{1}\right)
; this set contains the layer and serves as a buffer area between
\partial\Omega
and the
\varepsilon
-cells containing holes.

To summarize the geometric setup of the perforated domain, we group the assumptions above and invoke throughout the paper the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in(0,1]
, let
T
and
\Omega
satisfy the assumptions (A1), (A2) and (A3), and let
\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\Omega^{\varepsilon,\eta}
be constructed by (
1.19
).

This is the standing assumption in the paper and, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we always assume Assumption
1
.

2.
Layer potentials for the Stokes system

Here, we state some results on layer potentials associated to the Stokes system. In particular, we choose to study the double-layer potential proposed in
[
8
]
, which is defined by a particular choice of conormal derivative that is different from the physical one used in Ladyzhenskaya
[
14
]
. The main advantage of our choice is the eigenspace of the so-called Neumann-Poincaré operator associated to the layer potential operators has a simpler structure.
We also introduce the corresponding periodic layer potentials, and study their mapping properties. Those results on layer potentials will be the main tool for the asymptotic analysis of rescaled problems.

2.1.
Layer potential operators

The fundamental solution pair for the Stokes system in the free-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by
(\Gamma,\Theta)
, where
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{k}^{j})_{kj}
is the Kelvin matrix function,
\Theta=(\theta_{k})_{k}
is the pressure field vector. They are defined by the formulas:

\Gamma_{k}^{j}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2d(d-2)\varpi_{d}}\left[\frac{\delta_{jk}}{|x|^{d-2}}+(d-2)\frac{x^{j}x^{k}}{|x|^{d}}\right]\qquad&d\geq 3,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\delta_{jk}\log|x|-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{x^{j}x^{k}}{|x|^{2}}\qquad&d=2,\end{cases}

(2.1)

and

\theta_{k}(x)=-\frac{x^{k}}{d\varpi_{d}|x|^{d}},\qquad d\geq 2.

(2.2)

Here,
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker’s
\delta
-symbol, and
\varpi_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each
k=1,2,\dots,d
, the velocity field
\Gamma_{k}=(\Gamma_{k}^{1},\Gamma_{k}^{2},\dots,\Gamma_{k}^{d})
formed by the
k
th row of
\Gamma
, together with the pressure field
\theta_{k}
formed by the
k
th component of
\Theta
, solve the system

\Delta\Gamma_{k}(x)-\nabla\theta_{k}(x)=\delta_{0}(x)e_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\cdot\Gamma_{k}(x)=0,\qquad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.3)

Here,
\delta_{0}(x)
denotes the Dirac distribution with mass at
0
. Let
\Gamma_{\Delta}
be the fundamental solution to the scalar Laplace equation, i.e., solving
-\Delta\Gamma_{\Delta}=\delta_{0}
with proper decay condition at infinity. In fact,
\Gamma_{\Delta}
is given by
-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x|
for
d=2
and by
\frac{1}{d(d-2)\varpi_{d}}|x|^{2-d}
for
d\geq 3
. By direct computations we check that
\theta_{k}=\partial_{k}\Gamma_{\Delta}
.

Define
\Gamma_{k}(x,y):=\Gamma_{k}(x-y)
and
\theta_{k}(x,y):=\theta_{k}(x-y)
, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x\neq y
. It is easy to check that

\Delta_{x}\Gamma_{k}(x,y)-\nabla_{x}\theta_{k}(x,y)=\delta_{y}(x){e}_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{x}\cdot\Gamma_{k}(x,y)=0,\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.4)

In other words, the family
(\Gamma_{k}(x,y),\theta_{k}(x,y))
,
k=1,\dots,d
, is the family of fundamental solutions to the Stokes problem with a singularity at
y
.

Let
\phi\in L^{2}(\partial T)
. The velocity field of the single-layer potential with momentum
\phi
, associated to
\partial T
, is defined by

\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi](x)=\int_{\partial T}\Gamma_{k}(x-y)\phi^{k}(y)\,d\sigma_{y},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

(2.5)

Recall that summation over
k
is assumed here and the right hand sides defines a vector.
We comment on the notation. We may think of
\Gamma
as vector field valued tensor, and the operator between
\Gamma
and
\phi
yields a vector, i.e.,
\Gamma(\phi)=\sum_{k}\Gamma_{k}\phi^{k}
. We may think
\Theta
as a scalar valued tensor, and
\Theta(\phi)
is computed as
\sum_{k}\theta_{k}\phi^{k}
.
The pressure field of the single-layer potential is defined by

\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi](x)=\int_{\partial T}\theta_{k}(x-y)\phi^{k}(y)\,d\sigma_{y},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

(2.6)

Note that the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi](x)
makes sense also for
x\in\partial T
; in contrast,
\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi](x)
is not defined for
x\in\partial T
unless we use take the principal value of the integral in (
2.6
). Direct computation shows that
(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi])
satisfies the homogeneous Stokes system in
T
and in
T_{+}
.

To define the double-layer potential, we need to choose a conormal derivative, associated to a pair
(u,p)
of velocity field
u
and pressure field
p
, on any surface contained in the domain of fluid flow. In this paper, we find it is most convenient to use the one proposed in
[
8
]
, defined by

\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}:=-pN+(\nabla u(x))N=(-pN^{i}+N^{j}\partial_{j}u^{i}).

(2.7)

Here,
N
is the unit outer normal field along a chosen surface in the region of fluid flow. We emphasize that this choice of conormal derivative is different from the physical one, which reads

\frac{\partial[u,q]}{\partial\rho}:=-pN+2\mathbb{D}(u)N,\qquad\mathbb{D}(u)=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\rm T}).

(2.8)

In fluid mechanics,
\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\rho}
is the stress tensor imposed by the fluid flow on the surface. In comparison,
\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}
is an artificial modification of the physically meaningful conormal derivative. The reason for our choice is the following: it yields a Neumann-Poincaré operator whose eigenspace associated to the eigenvalue
\frac{1}{2}
is simpler. Using integration by parts, we verify that, for any pair
(u,p)
of velocity and pressure field, and for any velocity field
v
, with sufficient regularity, the following Green’s identity holds:

\int_{\partial T}\frac{\partial[u,q]}{\partial\nu_{y}}\cdot v=\int_{T}(\Delta u-\nabla q)\cdot v-q(\nabla\cdot v)+\int_{T}\nabla u:\nabla v.

(2.9)

Applying this identity to two pairs
(u,q)
and
(v,p)
, with
u
and
v
being solenoidal, we also obtain the second Green’s identity:

\int_{T}(\Delta u-\nabla q)\cdot v-(\Delta v-\nabla p)\cdot u=\int_{\partial T}\left[\frac{\partial[u,q]}{\partial\nu}\cdot v-\frac{\partial[v,p]}{\partial\nu}\cdot u\right].

(2.10)

In view of those Green identities and in view of (
2.4
), we define the velocity field of the double-layer potential, associated to the momentum
\phi\in L^{2}(\partial T)
on
\partial T
, by

\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi](x)=\int_{\partial T}\frac{\partial[\Gamma_{k},\theta_{k}](y-x)}{\partial\nu_{y}}\phi^{k}(y)\,dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

(2.11)

Note that,
(\Gamma_{k}(y-x),\theta_{k}(y-x))
satisfies (
2.4
) which has a singularity at the point
x
. In the computation of
\partial/\partial\nu_{y}
, derivatives are taken with respect to the
y
-variable. For the associated pressure field, define,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial T
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{T}[\phi](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial T}N^{\ell}_{y}\frac{\partial\theta_{k}(x-y)}{\partial y_{\ell}}\phi^{k}(y)\,dy

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d\varpi_{d}}\int_{\partial T}N^{\ell}_{y}\left(\frac{-\delta_{k\ell}}{|x-y|^{d}}+\frac{d(x-y)^{k}(x-y)^{\ell}}{|x-y|^{d+2}}\right)\phi^{k}(y)\,dy.

We verify that
(\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{P}_{T}[\phi])
solves the homogeneous Stokes system in
T
and in
T_{+}
, i.e.,

\left(\Delta_{x}\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi]-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{P}_{T}[\phi]\right)(x)=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

If we try to extend the definitions of
\mathcal{S}_{T}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{T}
,
\mathcal{D}_{T}
and
\mathcal{P}_{T}
to
x\in\partial T
, we can check that
\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]
is well defined, but not the other operators. In fact,
\mathcal{Q}_{T}
involves an integral kernel of order
-(d-1)
,
\mathcal{P}_{T}
has an integral kernel of order
-d
, even on smooth domains. For
\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi]
, the integral kernel is:

\displaystyle K_{jk}(x,y)=

\displaystyle-\theta_{k}(y-x)N_{y}^{j}+N^{\ell}_{y}\partial_{y_{\ell}}(\Gamma_{k}^{j}(y-x))

(2.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{y}^{k}(x-y)^{j}-(x-y)^{k}N_{y}^{j}}{2d\varpi_{d}|x-y|^{d}}-\frac{1}{2\varpi_{d}}\frac{\langle N_{y},x-y\rangle(x-y)^{j}(x-y)^{k}}{|x-y|^{d+2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2d\varpi_{d}}\frac{\langle N_{y},x-y\rangle\delta_{jk}}{|x-y|^{d}}.

For
x,y\in\partial T
with
x\neq y
, the function
K_{jk}
can be viewed as an integral kernel of order
-(d-1)
. In fact, for
\partial T
of class
C^{1,\alpha}
, using

|\langle N_{y},x-y\rangle|\leq C|x-y|^{1+\alpha},\qquad|N_{x}-N_{y}|\leq C|x-y|^{\alpha},

(2.14)

we see that the last two terms in the expression of
K_{jk}
contributes to a weakly singular integral operator. The first term, however, results in a singular integral operator. By the standard but deep theory of Coifman, McIntosh and Meyer
[
7
]
,

(\mathcal{K}_{T}[\phi])^{k}(x):=\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\partial T}K_{jk}(x,y)\phi^{j}(y)dy,\qquad x\in\partial T

(2.15)

defines a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}(\partial T)
to
L^{2}(\partial T)
, and
\mathcal{K}_{T}
(and, equally often, its adjoint operator) is referred to as the Neumann-Poincaré operator.

2.2.
Jump relations

The layer-potentials are very useful in solving boundary value problems for the homogeneous Stokes system, because one can derive trace formulas for them. For a function (vector-valued or scalar-valued)
F
that is defined in
T_{-}:=T
and/or in
T_{+}
, we denote its trace from
T_{-}
on
\partial T
by
F\rvert_{-}
, and denote its trace from
T_{+}
on
\partial T
by
F\rvert_{+}
:

F\rvert_{\pm}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0+}F(x\pm tN_{x}),\qquad x\in\partial T.

(2.16)

Here,
N_{x}
is the unit outer normal vector at
x
.

For the double-layer velocity field
\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi]
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi]\big\rvert_{\pm}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0+}\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi](x\pm tN_{x})=\left(\mp\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}\right)[\phi](x),\qquad x\in\partial T.

(2.17)

For the derivatives of the single-layer velocity field, i.e.
\nabla\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]
, and for the single-layer pressure field
\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi]
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{j}(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi])^{i}\big\rvert_{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{2}\left\{N_{x}^{j}\phi^{i}(x)-N^{j}_{x}N^{i}_{x}\langle N_{x},\phi(x)\rangle\right\}+\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\partial T}\partial_{j}\Gamma^{i}_{k}(x-y)\phi,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi]\big\rvert_{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{1}{2}\langle N_{x},\phi(x)\rangle+\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\partial T}\theta_{k}(x-y)\phi^{k}(y).

Combining the terms above according to our choice (
2.7
) of conormal derivative, we check that

\frac{\partial[\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi]]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{\pm}(x)=\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*}\right)[\phi](x).

(2.19)

Here
\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*}
is defined by

(\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}[\phi])^{i}(x)=\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\partial T}K^{*}_{ik}(x,y)\phi^{k}(y)dy,

(2.20)

and the integration kernel
K^{*}_{ik}
is given by

K^{*}_{ik}(x,y)=-\theta_{k}(x-y)N^{i}_{x}+N^{j}_{x}\partial_{j}\Gamma^{i}_{k}(x-y).

We refer to
[
8
]
for those formulas.

Direct computation shows that
K^{*}_{ik}(x,y)=K_{ki}(y,x)
, so
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{K}_{T}
, and
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
is a non-compact bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\partial T)
. We then compute that

\displaystyle(K^{*}_{ik}-K_{ik})(x,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varpi_{d}}\frac{\langle N_{x}+N_{y},x-y\rangle(x-y)^{i}(x-y)^{k}}{|x-y|^{d+2}}+\frac{1}{2d\varpi_{d}}\frac{\langle N_{x}+N_{y},x-y\rangle\delta_{ik}}{|x-y|^{d}}

(2.21)

\displaystyle+\frac{(N_{x}-N_{y})^{k}(x-y)^{i}-(N_{x}-N_{y})^{i}(x-y)^{k}}{2d\varpi_{d}|x-y|^{d}}.

In view of (
2.14
), the integral operator
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}-\mathcal{K}_{T}
is compact on
L^{2}(\partial T)
. This fact plays an important role in the study of those layer potential operators in
[
8
]
.

3.
Mapping properties of the layer potentials

In this section, we study the mapping properties of some operators related to the single- and double-layer potentials, and establish layer potential representation formulas for the cell problem (
1.3
) that play an essential role in the homogenization of (
1.1
). Since the cell problem is a Dirichlet boundary value problem, we focus on the double-layer potential representation of the solution, in particular, on the mapping properties of the corresponding Neumann-Poincaré operator
\mathcal{K}_{T}
and its adjoint
\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*}
.

3.1.
Free-space Stokes potentials

In the sequel,
L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
denotes the subspace of
L^{2}(\partial T)
consisting of mean-zero functions on
\partial T
. We have the following important results:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Then the following results hold.

(1)

The operator
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
has range in
L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
, and the restricted operator
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}:L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)\to L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
is a bijection.

(2)

The operators
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
and
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}
both have closed ranges in
L^{2}(\partial T)
, and both have kernels of dimension
d
.

(3)

The space
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})
has basis
\{e_{\ell}\,:\,1\leq\ell\leq d\}
. There is a basis
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d}
for the space
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
and there are
d
vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

-\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi_{j}](x)=a_{j}\;\text{in }\overline{T},\qquad\int_{\partial T}\phi_{j}=e_{j}.

(3.1)

Moreover, the matrix
A_{T}
defined by

A_{T}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{d}\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

where the
a_{j}
’s are written as column vectors, is symmetric for
d\geq 2
; for
d\geq 3
,
A_{T}
is also positive-definite.

(4)

The following direct-sum decompositions hold:

\displaystyle L^{2}(\partial T)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ran}(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*})\oplus\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\partial T)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ran}(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})\oplus\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}).

We emphasize that identities in (
3.3
) are, in general, not orthogonal decompositions, and that
A_{T}
defined by (
3.2
) can be degenerate for
d=2
(see Remark
3.3
below).
The first two items of the lemma above are proved by Fabes, Kenig and Verchota
[
8
]
in the setting that
\partial T
is Lipschitz.
Because the non-physical conormal derivative (
2.7
) is used in the double-layer potential (
2.11

Proof of Lemma

Proof of item (3)
.
Characterization of
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})
: Consider the unit constant vector fields

,
\ell\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
. In view of Green’s identity (
2.9
), they satisfy

\mathcal{D}_{T}[e_{\ell}](x)=\begin{cases}0,\qquad&x\in\overline{T}^{c},\\
e_{\ell},\qquad&x\in T.\end{cases}

Using Green’s identity (
2.9
) again with
(u,p)=(\Gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
and
v=e_{\ell}
in the domain
T\setminus\overline{B}_{\delta}(x)
, and by explicit computations of the resulting boundary integral over
T\cap\partial B_{\delta}(x)
and sending
\delta\to 0
, we also have

\mathcal{K}_{T}[e_{\ell}](x)=\frac{1}{2}e_{\ell},\qquad x\in\partial T,\quad\forall\ell=1,\dots,d.

(3.4)

Hence,
\mathrm{span}\{e_{\ell}\,:\,1\leq\ell\leq d\}
is a subspace of
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})
with dimension

. As a result, the latter must be spanned by
e_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dots,d
.

Characterization of
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
: we use an argument in
[
4
, Theorem 2.26]
. Let
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the product Hilbert space equipped with the standard inner product

\langle(\varphi,a),(\psi,b)\rangle=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)}+a\cdot b,\qquad\text{for }\,\varphi,\psi\in\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}),\,a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This is a Hilbert space with dimension
2d
. The product space
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the inner product on it are defined in the same way. Consider the following mapping:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{T}\quad:\quad\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\to\;\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3.5)

\displaystyle(\phi,a)

\displaystyle\mapsto\;(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]+a,\int_{\partial T}\phi).

Here,
\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]
is understood as the trace of the single-layer velocity field on
\partial T
. The mapping is well defined because
\phi
being in
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
implies that
\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]
is a constant in
\overline{T}
. Indeed, let
(u,p):=(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi])
in
T
and in
T_{+}
. Then, due to the jump relation (
2.19
) and to the fact that
(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})[\phi]=0
,

\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{-}=0,\qquad\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{+}=\phi.

By applying Green’s identity in
T
, we conclude that
u
is a constant in
\overline{T}
.

We show next that
\mathcal{A}_{T}
is a bijection
.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{T}
is a linear mapping between two Hilbert spaces of the same finite dimension, it suffices to check the injectivity of
\mathcal{A}_{T}
. To this end, suppose
\phi\in\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]+a=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\partial T}\phi=0.

Let
(u,p)=(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi])
in
T
and in
T_{+}
. Then in addition to
u
being a constant in
T
, we also have sufficient decay of
u
in
T_{+}
at infinity (for
d=2
we use the fact
\phi\in L^{2}_{0}
). This allows us to apply Green’s identity in
T_{+}
for all
d\geq 2
; it follows that
u
is a constant in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, by the jump relation,
\phi=0
. By the formula above,

as well. This verifies the bijectivity of
\mathcal{A}_{T}
.

As an application, for each
j=1,\dots,d
, there is a unique pair
(\phi_{j},a_{j})
so that
\mathcal{A}_{T}(\phi_{j},a_{j})=(0,e_{j})
. This is precisely the solution pair to (
3.1
). Moreover,
\phi_{j}
’s are clearly independent and form a basis for
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
, via (
2.19
). This provides a basis for
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}^{*})
.

Symmetry and positivity of the matrix
A_{T}
: we first check that the
(i,j)
-element of the matrix
A_{T}
defined by (
3.2
) is given by

(a_{j})^{i}=-e_{i}\cdot\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi_{j}]=-\left(\int_{\partial T}\phi_{i}\right)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi_{j}]=-\langle\phi_{i},\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi_{j}]\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)}.

Since the restriction of
\mathcal{S}_{T}
on
\partial T
is clearly a self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\partial T)
, we get
(a_{j})^{i}=(a_{i})^{j}
. That is,
A_{T}
is symmetric.
For the positivity of
A_{T}
, let
d\geq 3
. Fix any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and compute

\langle c,A_{T}c\rangle=-c^{i}\langle\phi_{i},\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi_{j}]\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)}c^{j}=-\langle\phi,\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)},\qquad\text{with}\;\phi=c^{j}\phi_{j}.

Clearly,
\phi\in\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
, and
\int_{\partial T}\phi=c
. Let
(u,p)
be
(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi])
in
T
and in
T_{+}
. We deduce, as before,
u
is a constant in
\overline{T}
and
\phi=\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\rvert_{+}
on
\partial T
. Since
d\geq 3
,

has sufficient decay at infinity and we check that

-\langle\phi,\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi]\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)}=-\int_{\partial T}\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{+}\cdot u=\int_{T_{+}}|\nabla u|^{2}.

The quantity above is non-negative, and it equals zero if and only if
u
is a constant in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which would imply
\phi=0
and
c=0
. As a result,
A_{T}
is positive definite for
d\geq 3
.

Proof of item (4)
. We have seen that
\mathrm{ran}(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
is
L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
, whose codimension agrees with
\dim\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})
. For the first identity in (
3.3
), it suffices to show the right hand side is a direct sum. Suppose
\phi=(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})[g]
and
(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})[\phi]=0
, then as an element in the range of
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
, we have
\phi\in L^{2}_{0}
. Now we can argue as before for the pair
(u,p)=(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}_{T}[\phi])
, defined in
T
and in
T_{+}
. In particular, we can apply Green’s identity in
T_{+}
for all

and eventually deduce

. This establishes the first identity in (

). The second identity then follows from the

L^{2}(\partial T)=\mathrm{ran}(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T})\oplus\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})=\mathrm{ran}(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T})\oplus\ker(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}).

The proof of the lemma is hence complete.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

In this paper we assumed that
T
is connected, i.e., there is only one hole inside the unit cell. This simplifies matter for our approach that is based on layer potential theory. Namely, it allows us to use results from
[

directly (where a single hole was assumed), and, moreover,

.

3.2.
Periodic Stokes potentials

To find a representation formula for the solution of the cell problem (
1.3
), we define periodic versions of the layer potential operators for the Stokes system. The fundamental solutions of the Stokes system on the unit torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, denoted by
(G_{k},p_{k})
, for each
k=1,\dots,d
, satisfy the equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta G_{k}(x)-\nabla P_{k}(x)=(\delta_{0}(x)-1)e_{k},\\
&\nabla\cdot G_{k}(x)=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Here,
G_{k}
and
P_{k}
are understood as functions defined on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, or, equivalently, as periodic functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The existence and uniqueness (up to additive constants) for
(G_{k},P_{k})
is a standard result, and explicit formulas can be obtained in terms of Fourier series. To fix the constants, we assume that
G_{k}
and
P_{k}
are mean zero on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. It is also clear that we can write

G_{k}(x)=\Gamma_{k}(x)+R_{k}(x),\quad\text{and}\quad P_{k}(x)=\theta_{k}(x)+h_{k}(x),

(3.9)

where
R_{k}
is a vector field on the unit cube
Q_{1}
and
h_{k}
is a scalar field on
Q_{1}
. Moreover, by subtracting the equations satisfied by
(\Gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
from those satisfied by
(G_{k},P_{k})
, we can derive equations satisfied by
(R_{k},h_{k})
, in
Q_{1}
, and deduce that
R_{k}
and
h_{k}
are smooth functions in the closed cube
\overline{Q}_{1}
, although
\nabla R_{k}
and
h_{k}
(if extended periodically) are not continuous across
\partial Q_{1}
.

Taking divergence on the first equation for
(G_{k},P_{k})
, we find that

-\Delta P_{k}=\partial_{k}(\delta_{0}-1)\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d}.

This implies that, up to an additive constant,
P_{k}
is given by

P_{k}=-\partial_{k}G_{\Delta},

(3.10)

where
G_{\Delta}
is the fundamental solution to the Laplace equation on the unit flat torus. This can be viewed as an analog of the formula (
2.2
).

On the rescaled torus
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the fundamental solutions
(G^{\eta}_{k},P^{\eta}_{k})
are given by

G^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\eta^{d-2}G_{k}(\eta x),\qquad P^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\eta^{d-1}P_{k}(\eta x),\qquad x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}.

and they satisfy

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta G^{\eta}_{k}(x)-\nabla P^{\eta}_{k}(x)=(\delta_{0}(x)-\eta^{d})e_{k},\\
&\nabla\cdot G^{\eta}_{k}(x)=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}.

(3.11)

For
\eta
small, those functions are perturbations of the free-space fundamental solutions:

G^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\Gamma_{k}(x)+\eta^{d-2}R_{k}(\eta x),\quad P^{\eta}_{k}(x)=\theta_{k}(x)+\eta^{d-1}h_{k}(\eta x).

(3.12)

Note also, slightly abusing the notation, the functions
G^{\eta}_{k}(x,y):=G^{\eta}_{k}(x-y)
and
P^{\eta}_{k}(x,y):=P^{\eta}_{k}(x-y)
, for
x,y\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
, satisfy

\Delta_{x}G(x,y)-\nabla_{x}P^{\eta}(x,y)=(\delta_{y}(x)-\eta^{d})e_{k},\qquad\text{in }\;\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Now we introduce the periodic layer potentials for Stokes systems on the torus
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
and associated to the set
\overline{T}
. We always assume that
T
satisfies the conditions in Assumption
1
.

The periodic single-layer velocity field and pressure fields are defined by:

\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x):=\int_{\partial T}G^{\eta}_{k}(x-y)\phi^{k}(y)d\sigma_{y},\\
&\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x):=\int_{\partial T}P^{\eta}_{k}(x-y)\phi^{k}(y)d\sigma_{y},\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

(3.13)

It is straightforward to check that

\Delta\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]-\nabla\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]=-\eta^{d}\int_{\partial T}\phi,\qquad x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

Hence, the pair
(\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi],\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi])
solves the homogeneous Stokes system away from
\partial T
if and only if
\phi
is mean-zero on
\partial T
.

The periodic double-layer velocity and pressure fields are defined by:

\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x):=\int_{\partial T}\frac{\partial[G^{\eta}_{k},P^{\eta}_{k}](y-x)}{\partial\nu_{y}}\phi^{k}(y)d\sigma_{y},\\
&\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x):=\int_{\partial T}N_{y}^{i}\partial_{y_{i}}[P_{k}^{\eta}(y-x)]\phi^{k}(y)d\sigma_{y},\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\partial T.

(3.14)

It can be checked that the pair
(\mathcal{D}^{\eta}[\phi],\mathcal{P}^{\eta}[\phi])
solve the homogeneous Stokes system away from
\partial T
for all
\phi
in
L^{2}(\partial T)
. In view of the perturbative formula (
3.12
), the operators
\mathcal{D}_{T}^{\eta}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T}
,
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\eta}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}
, etc., are perturbations of the corresponding operators defined in the free-space. In particular, we can check from direct computations that

\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x)=\mathcal{D}_{T}[\phi](x)+\eta^{d-1}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x),\qquad\forall x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\partial T,

(3.15)

where
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\eta}[\phi]
is defined as

\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x):=\int_{\partial T}[-h_{k}(\eta(y-x))N_{y}+N^{\ell}_{y}\partial_{y_{\ell}}R_{k}(\eta(y-x))]\phi^{k}(y)\,d\sigma_{y}.

Since
h
and
\nabla R_{k}
are bounded functions over
Q_{1}
, the integral operator
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
has an integration kernel that can be uniformly bounded in
\eta
(in fact, smallness can be explored). It follows that the periodic double-layer velocity field has the following jump condition across
\partial T
:

\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]\Big\rvert_{\pm}(x)=\left(\mp\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}\right)[\phi](x),\qquad x\in\partial T.

(3.16)

Here
\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
is simply given by

\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}:=\mathcal{K}_{T}+\eta^{d-1}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T},

and
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
is defined as before but with
x\in\partial T
. Because
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
has a uniformly bounded integration kernel,
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
maps
L^{2}(\partial T)
to
L^{\infty}(\partial T)
, and
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
is compact as an operator on
L^{2}(\partial T)
.

In a similar way, the conormal derivative of the periodic single-layer potentials satisfies the following jump formula:

\frac{\partial(\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T},\mathcal{Q}_{T}^{\eta})[\phi]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{\pm}(x)=\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}\right)[\phi](x).

(3.17)

Here,
\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
, and it can be written as
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}+\eta^{d-1}(\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T})^{*}
.

Because
\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
(respectively,
\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}
) is a small (for small
\eta
) and compact perturbation of
\mathcal{K}_{T}
(respectively,
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
), many mapping properties of
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
(and
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}
) follow from those of
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}
; in particular, the following results are useful.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
d\geq 2
. The following statements hold.

(1)

For each
e_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,d
, the identity
(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T})[e_{k}]=-\eta^{d}|T|e_{k}
holds.

(2)

The kernel of
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
is
\{0\}
.

(3)

The operator
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}:L^{2}(\partial T)\to L^{2}(\partial T)
is invertible.

Proof.

First item.

We take
x\in T
and apply the Green’s identity in
T
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{T}^{\eta}[e_{k}](x)=\int_{\partial T}\frac{\partial[G^{\eta}_{\ell},P^{\eta}_{\ell}](y-x)}{\partial\nu_{y}}\delta_{k\ell}d\sigma_{y}=\delta_{\ell k}\int_{T}(\delta(y-x)-\eta^{d})e_{\ell}dy=(1-\eta^{d}|T|)e_{k}.

Sending
x
to the boundary
\partial T
, we get the first result. As a consequence, for any
\phi\in\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T})
, the following holds:

\int_{\partial T}e_{k}\cdot\phi=-\frac{1}{\eta^{d}|T|}\int_{\partial T}(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T})[e_{k}]\cdot\phi=-\frac{1}{\eta^{d}|T|}\int_{\partial T}e_{k}\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T})[\phi]=0.

In other words, we have
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T})\subseteq L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
.

Second item.

Since
\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
(respectively,
\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}
) is a compact perturbation of
\mathcal{K}_{T}
(respectively,
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{T}
) and
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}
is Fredholm (as in Lemma
3.1
), we conclude that the operator
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
and its adjoint operator are Fredholm. It suffices to check that
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T})
contains only
0
. Take any element
\phi
in this kernel, we have seen that
\phi\in L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
. Let
(u,p)
be the pair
(\mathcal{S}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]|,\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]\rvert)
, then they satisfy the homogeneous Stokes system in
T
and in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. By the trace formulas, we have

\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{-}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T}\right)[\phi]=0,\qquad\phi=\frac{\partial[u,p]}{\partial\nu}\Big\rvert_{+},\qquad\text{in }\partial T.

Applying Green’s identity, first in
T
and then in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
, we conclude that
u
is a constant in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
p=0
in
T
, and
p
is a constant in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. Denote this constant by
p_{+}
. Then
\phi=-p_{+}N
on
\partial T
. We observe from the definition of the single-layer pressure that

\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi](x)dx=\int_{\partial T}\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}}P^{\eta}_{k}(x-y)dx\phi(y)dy=0.

In other words,
p=\mathcal{Q}^{\eta}_{T}[\phi]
averages to zero in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Since
p\rvert_{T}=0
, it follows that
p_{+}=0
and, hence,
\phi=0
. This verifies
\ker(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta,*}_{T})=\{0\}
and proves item two.

Third item.

It follows immediately since we have shown that
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T}
is Fredholm and is injective on
L^{2}(\partial T)
. The proof of the lemma is now complete.
∎

4.
The two-scale cell problems

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
. We first obtain a representation formula for the two-scale correctors by inverting the periodic layer potentials defined in Section
3.2
.

4.1.
Layer potential representation formulas

Our strategy for solving the two-scale corrector problem (
1.3
) is based on the comparison with the system (
3.11
). Indeed, we seek for
(\Phi^{\eta}_{k},\psi^{\eta}_{k})
so that the following formulas hold:

\chi^{\eta}_{k}=G^{\eta}_{k}+\Phi^{\eta}_{k},\qquad\omega^{\eta}_{k}=P^{\eta}_{k}+\psi^{\eta}_{k},\qquad\text{in }\,\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}.

(4.1)

The pair
(\Phi^{\eta}_{k},\psi^{\eta}_{k})
then solves the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta\Phi^{\eta}_{k}-\nabla\psi^{\eta}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\nabla\cdot\Phi^{\eta}_{k}=0,&\quad&x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T},\\
&\Phi^{\eta}_{k}=-G^{\eta}_{k}(\cdot,0),&\quad&x\in\partial T,;\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

notice that we use assumption (A1), namely that
0\in T
.
This is a Dirichlet boundary value problem on the perforated torus
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
, with smooth Dirichlet data
-G^{\eta}_{k}
given on
\partial T
. The following lemma provides explicit formulas for the solution.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and suppose Assumption
1
holds. Then, for each
k=1,\dots,d
, the unique solution
(\chi^{\eta}_{k},\omega^{\eta}_{k})
(for
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
the uniqueness is up to an additive constant) to (
1.3
) is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{\eta}_{k}(x)=G^{\eta}_{k}(x)+A_{T}e_{k}+\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x)+r^{\eta}_{k},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\omega^{\eta}_{k}(x)=P^{\eta}_{k}(x)+\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x),

for
x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. Here,
r^{\eta}_{k}
is a constant vector whose norm is of order
O(\eta^{d-2})
;
t^{\eta}_{k}
is a real number of order
\eta^{d-1}
;
g
is a function in
L^{2}(\partial T)
with mean value
\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}
on
\partial T
, and
\widetilde{g}:=g-\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}
is the mean-zero part of
g
. Moreover, there exists some universal constant
C>0
so that

|\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}|\leq C\eta^{-1},\qquad\|g-\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}\|_{L^{2}(\partial T)}\leq C.

(4.4)

Proof.

Step 1: a decomposition of the boundary data.

In view of the jump condition (
3.15
) and the invertibility established in Lemma
3.7
, the equations (
4.2
) can be solved by periodic double-layer potentials. However, in order to prove the estimates, we first decompose the boundary data
-G^{\eta}_{k}\rvert_{\partial T}
according to (
3.3
). Recall the projection operators in Proposition
3.4
; set

-G^{\eta}_{k}=c^{\eta}_{k}+h^{\eta}_{k},\qquad c^{\eta}_{k}=\Pi_{0}[-G^{\eta}_{k}\rvert_{\partial T}],\;h^{\eta}_{k}=\Pi_{1}[-G^{\eta}_{k}\rvert_{\partial T}].

(4.5)

Then according to (
3.7
) and the perturbative formula (
3.12
), we have

\displaystyle c^{\eta}_{k}=\Pi_{0}[-G^{\eta}_{k}\rvert_{\partial T}]=\Pi_{0}[-\Gamma_{k}\rvert_{\partial T}]+{\widetilde{r}}^{\,\eta}_{k}=A_{T}e_{k}+{\widetilde{r}}^{\,\eta}_{k},

\displaystyle({\widetilde{r}}^{\,\eta}_{k})^{\ell}=-\eta^{d-2}\langle\phi_{\ell},R_{k}(\eta x)\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial T)},

where
R_{k}
is defined by (
3.9
). In view of the uniform boundedness of
R_{k}
, we check that
|\widetilde{r}^{\,\eta}_{k}|\leq C\eta^{d-2}
for all
k
, for some universal constant
C
.

Step 2: layer-potential formulation for the two-scale cell problem.

The previous step suggests that the velocity field of (
4.2
) can be solved by
\Phi^{\eta}_{k}(y)=A_{T}e_{k}+\widetilde{r}^{\,\eta}_{k}+\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[g](y)
and
\psi^{\eta}_{k}(y)=\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[g](y)
. Indeed, by the trace formula (
3.15
), we only need to find
g\in L^{2}(\partial T)
so that

(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T})[g]=h^{\eta}_{k},\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\partial T).

We further write
g
as the sum of its mean value
\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}
and its fluctuation part
\widetilde{g}=g-\langle g\rangle
, the lattering being in
L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
. In view of Lemma
3.7
, we rewrite the equation above as

(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}+\eta^{d-1}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T})[\widetilde{g}]-\eta^{d}|T|\langle g\rangle=h^{\eta}_{k},\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\partial T).

Step 3: solution and estimates for
g
.

Let the projection operators
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{0}
act on the equation above. We get

\displaystyle(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}+\eta^{d-1}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T})[\widetilde{g}]=h^{\eta}_{k},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\eta^{d-1}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]-\eta^{d}|T|\langle g\rangle=0.

Since the operator
\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T}
is uniformly bounded independent of
\eta
, for
\eta
sufficiently small, the operator on the left hand side in the first line is a small perturbation of
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}
, which is invertible on
L^{2}_{0}
. By the standard perturbation theory, we get

\widetilde{g}=(-\tfrac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}_{T}+\eta^{d-1}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{R}^{\eta}_{T})^{-1}[h^{\eta}_{k}],\qquad\text{and}\quad\|\widetilde{g}\|_{L^{2}(\partial T)}\leq C\|h^{\eta}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\partial T)}.

The estimate for
\langle g\rangle
then follows from the second equation of (
4.6
). For
x\in\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
, the contribution
\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\langle g\rangle]
is the constant
-\eta^{d}|T|\langle g\rangle
which is of order
O(\eta^{d-1})
. Add this constant to
\widetilde{r}^{\eta}_{\eta}
to define the constant
r^{\eta}_{k}
, which is still of order
O(\eta^{d-2})
.

For
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
, we note that
\psi^{\eta}_{k}=\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[g]=\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]
and
(\Phi^{\eta}_{k},\psi^{\eta}_{k})
solves the problem (
4.2
). Hence, the second line of (
4.3
) holds.
The proof is now complete.
∎

The formulas (
4.1
) specify the values of
(\chi^{\eta}_{k},\omega^{\eta}_{k})
in the perforated torus
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
, and we extend their values by zero in
T
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Lemma
4.1
hold. Let
\langle\chi^{\eta}_{k}\rangle
and
\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle
denote the mean values of
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
in
\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then there exists a universal constant
C
, such that the inequalities

\left\|\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\left|\langle\chi^{\eta}_{k}\rangle\right|\leq\begin{cases}C\;&\text{if }d\geq 3\\
C|\log\eta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\;&\text{if }d=2,\end{cases}\qquad\left|\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle\right|\leq C\eta^{d},

(4.7)

\text{and}\quad\|\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle\|_{L^{2}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\begin{cases}C,\quad&\text{if }d\geq 3,\\
C|\log\eta|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad&\text{if }d=2.\end{cases}

(4.8)

hold for all
k=1,\dots,d
. Moreover, when
d\geq 3
, we also have

\left|\langle\chi^{\eta}_{k}\rangle-A_{T}e_{k}\right|\leq C\eta^{d-2}.

(4.9)

Proof.

Step 1: the proofs for (
4.7
).

Integrate
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
against the first equation in (
1.3
) and apply the Poincaré inequality. This results in the estimates for
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
in (
4.7
). To control the mean value of
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
, in view of the formula (
4.3
), it suffices to show the integrals of
P^{\eta}_{k}
and of
\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]
over the torous
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
are bounded uniform in
\eta
. In view of the oddness of
P^{\eta}_{k}
and the fact that
\overline{T}\subset B_{1}
,

\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}P^{\eta}_{k}(y)dy=\int_{B_{1}\setminus\overline{T}}P^{\eta}_{k}(y)dy\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
d
. For the integral of
\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]
, we compute relying on (
3.10
)

\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x)dx=\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\int_{\partial T}N_{y}^{i}\partial_{y_{i}}\left(-(\partial_{k}G^{\eta}_{\Delta})(y-x)\right)\widetilde{g}^{k}(y)d\sigma_{y}dx.

In the integral on the right hand side above, we can rewrite
\partial_{k}G^{\eta}_{\Delta}(y-x)
as
-\partial_{x_{k}}(G^{\eta}_{\Delta}(y-x))
and then take
\partial_{x}
outside of the integral over
\partial T
. We get

-\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x)dx=-\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\partial_{x_{k}}\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}[\widetilde{g}^{k}](x)dx,

where
\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}[\widetilde{g}^{k}]
is the double-layer potential associated to the Lapace equation in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Finally, by the divergence theorem, the integral above becomes

\int_{\partial T}N^{k}_{y}\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}[\widetilde{g}^{k}]\big\rvert_{+}(y)d\sigma_{y}=\int_{\partial T}N^{k}_{y}\left(-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}\right)[\widetilde{g}^{k}](y)\,d\sigma_{y}.

Here, we used the trace formula for the double-layer potential
\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}
. For
\eta\in(0,1)
, the operator
-\frac{1}{2}I+\mathcal{K}^{\eta}_{T,\Delta}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\partial T))
(more properties are established in
[
11
]
), and by (
4.4
), the above is uniformly bounded in
\eta
. Hence, the second inequality of (
4.7
) holds.

Step 2: the proof of (
4.9
).

For the average of
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
, we compute from (
4.3
) and (
3.12
)

\langle\chi^{\eta}_{k}\rangle-A_{T}e_{k}=\frac{1}{|\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Gamma_{k}(x)+\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x)\,dx+O(\eta^{d-2}).

Here
\widetilde{g}\in L^{2}_{0}(\partial T)
and
\|\widetilde{g}\|_{L^{2}(\partial T)}
is bounded by a universal constant. For the integral of
\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]
, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\int_{\partial T}[-P^{\eta}_{k}(y-x)N^{i}_{y}+N^{\ell}_{y}\partial_{y_{\ell}}(G^{\eta}_{k}(y-x))^{i}](\widetilde{g}(y))^{i}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\int_{\partial T}[\partial_{x_{k}}G^{\eta}_{\Delta}(y-x)N^{i}_{y}-N^{\ell}_{y}\partial_{x_{\ell}}(G^{\eta}_{k}(y-x))^{i}](\widetilde{g}(y))^{i}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial T}\int_{\partial T}[-G^{\eta}_{\Delta}(y-x)N^{k}_{x}N^{i}_{y}+N^{\ell}_{y}N^{\ell}_{x}(G^{\eta}_{k}(y-x))^{i}](\widetilde{g}(y))^{i}dydx.

In view of the uniform (in
\eta
and in
y
) integrability of
G^{\eta}_{\Delta}(y-x)
and
G^{\eta}_{k}(y-x)
over
x\in\partial T
, the integral above is uniformly bounded.

For the integral of
\Gamma_{k}
, we only consider the case
d\geq 3
. By the estimate
|\Gamma_{k}(x)|\leq C|x|^{-(d-2)}
(for
d\geq 3
) and a direct computation, we have

\left|\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\Gamma_{k}(x)\,dx\right|\leq C\eta^{-2}.

We then conclude that (
4.9
) holds for
d\geq 3
.

Step 3: the proof for (
4.8
).

We need to use the restriction and extension operators in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. By rescaling the results in Proposition
A.2
, we get a restriction operator
\mathcal{R}
for vector fields
H^{1}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d})
that satisfies

\|\nabla(\mathcal{R}u)\|_{L^{2}(\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T})}\leq C\left[\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\eta\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right],

where
C
depends only on
d
and
T
. This restriction operator then defines an extension operator
\mathcal{E}
for mean-zero pressure fields in
L^{2}_{0}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T})
; for
p\in L^{2}_{0}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T})
,
\mathcal{E}p
is determined by the relation

\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\mathcal{E}p)\,\nabla\cdot u=\int_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}p\,\nabla\cdot\,(\mathcal{R}u),\qquad\forall u\in H^{1}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Let
\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}^{d}_{\eta,f}}
denote the mean value of
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. In view of the second inequality in (
4.9
) and that the volume fraction of the hole is of order
\eta^{d}
, we check that
|\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}^{d}_{\eta,f}}|
is of order
O(\eta^{d})
. By repeating the arguments that lead to (
A.8
), we can find a universal constant
C
such that

\|\mathcal{E}(\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}^{d}_{\eta,f}})\|_{L^{2}(\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\begin{cases}C\quad&\text{if }d\geq 3,\\
C|\log\eta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad&\text{if }d=2.\end{cases}

(4.10)

It is also clear that
\mathcal{E}(\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}^{d}_{\eta,f}})
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle
only differ by a constant in
\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}
. Moreover, this constant is precisely

\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}^{d}_{\eta,f}}-\left|\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}\right|^{-1}\int_{T}\mathcal{E}(\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle),

which is of order
O(\eta^{d})
. Hence, in (
4.10
) we can replace the extended function by
\omega^{\eta}_{k}-\langle\omega^{\eta}_{k}\rangle
. This proves (
4.8
).
∎

4.2.
Asymptotic limits and quantitative estimates

Thanks to the explicit formulas for the solutions
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
and
\omega^{\eta}_{k}
to the cell problem (
1.3
), we are able to characterize the limits of
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
, and, furthermore, to quantify some of the convergence results. We first prove Theorem
1.1
stated in the Introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Step 1: estimates for
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
.

By periodicity we decompose the defining integral of
\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
into pieces over
\varepsilon
-cubes that intersect
\Omega
. Using the definition (
1.4
) and by rescaling the estimate of
\|\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
in (
4.7
), we see that
\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
in each
\varepsilon
-cube is of order
O(\varepsilon^{d-2}\kappa_{\eta}^{2})
. The desired result then follows by counting the number of
\varepsilon
-cubes in
\Omega
, which is of order
O(\varepsilon^{-d})
.

Step 2: weak convergence results for
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in the critical setting.

The result is due to the periodicity of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and can be viewed as a version of the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Because
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it suffices to prove, for each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)\varphi(x)dx\to 0,\qquad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}).

For each fixed
\varepsilon
, let
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
be the set of indices
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
Q_{\varepsilon,j}=\varepsilon(j+Q_{1})
is contained in
\Omega
, and let
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
be the set of
j
such that
Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\partial\Omega
is non-empty. Hence,
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
, take into account the interior cubes and the boundary
\varepsilon
-cubes, respectively, that have non-empty intersection with
\Omega
. Let
d\geq 3
for the moment. The above integral is then rewritten as

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I_{\varepsilon}}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,i}}\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\varphi+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J_{\varepsilon}}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega}\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\varphi\,=\,(\varepsilon\eta)^{d-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I_{\varepsilon}}}\int_{Q_{\frac{1}{\eta},i}}(\partial_{\ell}\chi^{\eta}_{k})(y)\varphi(\varepsilon\eta y)\,dy+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J_{\varepsilon}}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega}\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\varphi.

We used the change of variable
\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta}\mapsto y
in the last step for the interior integrals. Because
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
is periodic, each of the interior integrals for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is computed as

\int_{Q_{\frac{1}{\eta},i}}(\partial_{\ell}\chi^{\eta}_{k})(y)\varphi(\varepsilon\eta y)=\int_{\frac{1}{\eta}Q_{1}}(\partial_{\ell}\chi^{\eta}_{k})(y)\left[\varphi(\varepsilon\eta y+\eta^{-1}i)-\varphi(\eta^{-1}i)\right].

Since
\varphi
is smooth, by Taylor’s theorem we also have

\left|\varphi(\varepsilon\eta y+\eta^{-1}i)-\varphi(\eta^{-1}i)\right|\leq\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}|\varepsilon\eta y|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}},\qquad\forall y\in\frac{1}{\eta}Q_{1}.

Using the estimate (
4.7
), and the fact that
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
has cardinality of order
O(\varepsilon^{-d})
, we see that the total contribution from interior integrals is bounded by

\left|(\varepsilon\eta)^{d-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{Q_{\frac{1}{\eta},i}}(\partial_{\ell}\chi^{\eta}_{k})(y)\varphi(\varepsilon\eta y)\right|\leq\eta^{d-1}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}|Q_{\frac{1}{\eta}}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\eta^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}},

For a typical boundary cube
Q_{\varepsilon,j}
, with
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
, the integral over
Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega
is controlled by

\left|\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega}(\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k})\varphi\right|\leq\|\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{2}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon^{d-1}\eta^{\frac{d-2}{2}}.

The estimate above is uniform in
j
. Adding these estimates for
j
in
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
, which has cardinality of order
O(\varepsilon^{-d+1}
), we see that the total contribution from the boundary integrals is of order
O(\eta^{\frac{d-2}{2}})
. We hence proved (
1.9
) for
d\geq 3
.

When
d=2
, there is a further factor of order
|\log\eta|^{-1}
in the definition of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in terms of
\chi^{\eta}_{k}
; see (
1.4
). On the other hand,
\|\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d})}
is of order
|\log\eta|^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Taking those modifications into account, we verify that, given
\varphi\in C^{1}(\Omega)
, the integral of
(\partial_{\ell}v^{\varepsilon}_{k})\varphi
is of order
O(|\log\eta|^{-\frac{1}{2}})
and it vanishes in the limit as
\eta
and
\varepsilon
go to zero.

Step 3: The limit of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
.

We first consider the setting of
d\geq 3
and take
p=\frac{2d}{d-2}
. We compute

\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{p}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{p},

where
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
denotes the set of indices
j
for which
Q_{\varepsilon,j}
has nonempty intersection with
\Omega
. By periodicity, the integral over
Q_{\varepsilon,j}
is uniform in
j
and it is computed and controlled by

\int_{Q_{\varepsilon}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{p}\leq C\int_{Q_{\varepsilon}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle|^{p}+|\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{p}.

(4.11)

Here,
Q_{\varepsilon}
is the
\varepsilon
-cube centered at
0
and
\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle
is the average of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in
Q_{\varepsilon}
. By scaling, we check that
\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle=\langle\chi^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}}
. The inequality (
4.9
) then shows

\int_{Q_{\varepsilon}}|\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{p}\leq C\varepsilon^{d}\eta^{2d}.

(4.12)

On the other hand, we have the Sobolev embedding

\int_{Q_{\varepsilon}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle|^{p}\leq C\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}^{p}=C\left[(\varepsilon\eta)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\|\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right]^{p}.

(4.13)

Here, the bounding constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
, because the Sobolev embedding with critical component
p=2d/(d-2)
is scaling-invariant. The integral above is hence of order
O(\varepsilon^{d}\eta^{d})
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
has cardinality of order
\varepsilon^{-d}
, we combine the estimates above and check that (
1.10
) holds for
d\geq 3
.

Finally we consider the setting of
d=2
. In view of the definition (
1.4
) and the formula (
4.3
), we compute and get, for
x\in Q_{\varepsilon,j}
but outside
\varepsilon(j+\eta\overline{T})
,

v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)-M^{-1}e_{k}=v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{4\pi}e_{k}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\log|x/\varepsilon|}{|\log\eta|}e_{k}+\frac{1}{|\log\eta|}\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](\frac{x}{\varepsilon\eta})+O(|\log\eta|^{-1}).

Here,
\widetilde{g}=g-\langle g\rangle_{\partial T}
is specified in Lemma
4.1
. For
x
in the holes,
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is zero and the above is
-M^{-1}e_{k}
. Let
p=2
; we have

\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{Q_{\varepsilon,j}\cap\Omega}|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle|^{2}+|\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle-M^{-1}e_{k}|^{2}.

(4.14)

Here,
\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle
is the average on the
\varepsilon
-cube
Q_{\varepsilon}
. The representation formula for
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}
together with rescaling show that

\left|\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle-M^{-1}e_{k}\right|\leq C|\log\eta|^{-1}\left(1+\left|\langle\log|x|\rangle_{Q_{1}}\right|+\left|\langle\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}]\rangle_{\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\overline{T}}\right|\right).

From the computations in the proof of Corollary
4.2
, the right hand side above is of order
O(|\log\eta|^{-1})
. On the other hand, the usual Poincaré inequality shows

\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\langle v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\rangle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon,j})}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon,j})}=C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\eta|^{-2}\|\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\eta^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}|\log\eta|^{-1}.

We combine those results and use them in (
4.14
). Since the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is of order
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
, we check that (
1.10
) holds also for
d=2
.
∎

For the pressure field
q^{\varepsilon}_{k}
defined in (

Lemma 4.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

(1)

For all

(4.15)

(2)

(4.16)

For

Proof.

By the definition in (

in each
\varepsilon

Lemma 4.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

, we have

\left|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)\right|+|q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\kappa_{\eta}^{2},\qquad x\in\varepsilon\partial Q_{1}.

(4.17)

Proof.

The result follows from the explicit formulas of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
p^{\varepsilon}_{k}
. We only provide the details for

; the case for

is similar. By rescaling, by the formula (

) of

and the perturbative formula (

\displaystyle\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta}(\nabla\chi^{\eta}_{k})(y)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta}\left[\nabla\Gamma_{k}(y)+\eta^{d-1}(\nabla R_{k})(\eta y)+(\nabla\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}])(y)\right],

\displaystyle q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta}\omega^{\eta}_{k}(y)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta}\left[\theta_{k}(y)+\eta^{d-1}h_{k}(\eta y)+\mathcal{P}^{\eta}_{T}[\widetilde{g}](y)\right].

where

is the rescaled variable and belongs to

5.
Homogenization errors in the dilute Darcy’s regime

Our goal here is to prove Theorem
1.2
. We establish the convergence rate for the homogenization of (
1.1
) in the dilute super-critical case, namely (
1.11
), in the general setting, i.e. without assuming the homogenized solution satisfies
u\rvert_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

5.1.
Equations for the cell functions

In the construction of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
, we removed (filled in) some holes of
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
near the boundary
\partial\Omega
; see (
1.19
). As a consequence, there is a mismatch between the sets
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
and
\Omega\cap(\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f})
. To derive convergence rates for homogenization, we need to use the equations for the cell functions
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,d
, in the modified domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Direct computations using integration by parts show that the first line of (
1.5
) should be changed to (with a slight abuse, we use the same notation for the solutions of (
5.1
) and (
1.5
))

-\Delta v^{\varepsilon}_{k}+\nabla q^{\varepsilon}_{k}=\frac{e_{k}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+s_{\varepsilon,k}\qquad\text{in }\;\Omega^{\varepsilon},

(5.1)

where
s_{\varepsilon,k}\in H^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})
is defined by

\langle s_{\varepsilon,k},\varphi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}:=-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}\left[\int_{\Omega\cap\varepsilon(z+\eta T)}\frac{e_{k}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\cdot\varphi+\int_{\overline{\Omega}\cap\varepsilon(z+\partial(\eta\overline{T}))}\frac{\partial(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},p^{\varepsilon}_{k})}{\partial\nu}\cdot\varphi\right],\quad\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon}).

(5.2)

Here, the sum over
z
is taken over
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}:=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,:\,\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})\cap\partial\Omega\neq\varnothing\}
. In other words, they correspond to the removed (filled) holes near
\partial\Omega
.
Compare the equations (
5.1
) in
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
and (
1.5
) in
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}
, we see the latter have an extra term
s_{\varepsilon,k}
which accounts for the mismatch between the two domains. We will see later that this term causes some difficulty in the convergence rates analysis, more so in the dilute setting than in the classical setting
[
22
]
.

For each
t>0
small, let
\Omega_{(t)}:=\{x\in\Omega\,:\,d(x,\partial\Omega)\leq t\}
defines a
t
-neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
. Recall that
K_{\varepsilon}
is the neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
in which holes were filled.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
satisfies
\varphi=0
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\left|\langle s_{\varepsilon,k},\varphi\rangle\right|\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}.

(5.3)

Proof.

Extend
\varphi
by zero in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
so
\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Multiply the equation (
1.5
) and take the integral over the domain
\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}\setminus(\Omega\setminus K_{\varepsilon})
. Via integration by parts we get

-\int_{\partial K_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega}\frac{\partial(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})}{\partial\nu}\cdot\varphi+\sum_{z\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\varepsilon(z+\eta\partial T)}\frac{\partial(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})}{\partial\nu}\cdot\varphi=\int_{K_{\varepsilon}\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\rm f}}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}e_{k}\cdot\varphi-(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathbf{I}):\nabla\varphi\,dx.

Plugging this into the definition (
5.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle s_{\varepsilon,k},\varphi\rangle=\ &-\sigma^{-2}_{\varepsilon}\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}e_{k}\cdot\varphi+\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathbf{I}):\nabla\varphi+\int_{\partial K_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega}N_{y}\cdot(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{I})\cdot\varphi\,dy\\
=\ &I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.\end{split}

(5.4)

For
I_{1}
, observe that
K_{\varepsilon}
is a subset of
\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)}:=\{x\in\Omega\,:\,d(x,\partial\Omega)\leq 2\varepsilon\}
. Using Poincaré’s inequality
\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}\leq C\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}
, we have

|I_{1}|\leq\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\|e_{k}\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}.

For
I_{2}
, we break the integral over
K_{\varepsilon}
to the sum of integrals over
\varepsilon
-cubes
\{\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})\}
,
z\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
. Note that the square of the
L^{2}
norms of
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
q^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in each
\varepsilon
-cube is of order
O(\varepsilon^{d-2}\kappa_{\eta}^{2})
(see Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
4.3
), and that the number of
\varepsilon
-cubes included in
K_{\varepsilon}
is of order
O(\varepsilon^{-d+1})
. We deduce

|I_{2}|\leq\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\leq C(\varepsilon^{-1}\kappa_{\eta}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}.

For
I_{3}
, we use the
L^{\infty}
estimate (
4.17
) of
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathbf{I}
on
\varepsilon\partial(z+Q_{1})
,
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and conclude that

|I_{3}|\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\kappa_{\eta}^{2}\int_{\partial K_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega}|\varphi(x)|\,dx

Note that
\partial K_{\varepsilon}\cap\Omega
consists of sides of
\varepsilon
-cubes
\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})
with
z\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
. In view of the elementary estimate

\int_{\varepsilon(z+\partial Q_{1})}|\varphi|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\int_{\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+C\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\varepsilon(z+Q_{1})}|\varphi|^{2}.

We obtain that

|I_{3}|\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\kappa_{\eta}^{2}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(K_{\varepsilon})}\right)\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\kappa_{\eta}^{2}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(2\varepsilon)})}.

Note that
\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}\sigma^{-2}_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\kappa_{\eta}\sigma^{-1}_{\varepsilon}
, and so the bound for
I_{2}
dominates the others. We conclude that (
5.3
) holds.
∎

5.2.
Discrepancy functions

As usual, the quantification of convergence rates amounts to choosing correctors
r_{\varepsilon}
and
t_{\varepsilon}
for the velocity field and, respectively, the pressure field, which are small and which make the corrected discrepancy functions

u^{\varepsilon}/(\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}\wedge 1)-u-r_{\varepsilon}
and
p^{\varepsilon}-p-t_{\varepsilon}
small. Our method is based on the classical two-scale expansion method, and this expansion suggests natural candidates for correctors, at least in the classical setting. Again, by formally treating
\eta
as a parameter, the two-scale expansion inspires us to consider the following discrepancy functions:

\zeta^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{u^{\varepsilon}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}-v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)[f^{k}-\partial_{k}p](x),\qquad\tau^{\varepsilon}:=p^{\varepsilon}(x)-p(x)-\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)[f^{k}-\partial_{k}p](x).

(5.5)

Here,
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
are the rescaled cell functions solution to (
1.5
).
It turns out that, the above choice works perfectly in the (dilute) super-critical setting. This is expected because, in the classical setting where
\sigma_{\varepsilon}=1
, the terms subtracted are precisely the leading ones in the two-scale expansion. In the critical and sub-critical settings, we will slightly modify the above to come up with proper discrepancy functions, see Appendix
D
.

We can rewrite
f-\nabla p
as
Mu
where
u
is the homogenized solution; see (
1.12
). We consider the discrepancy functions
\zeta^{\varepsilon}
and
\tau^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
5.5
), hence choosing the correctors

r_{\varepsilon}=(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})(Mu)^{k},\qquad t_{\varepsilon}=\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}.

(5.6)

The smallness of
\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|t_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}
is immediately seen from (
1.10
) and (
4.15
).
To estimate the discrepancy functions
(\zeta^{\varepsilon},\tau^{\varepsilon})
, we check by direct computation (using (
1.1
) and (
5.1
)) that they satisfy the following equations in the perforated domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\Delta\zeta^{\varepsilon}+\nabla\tau^{\varepsilon}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\nabla\cdot[v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k}]+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k}+\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}\check{s},\\
&\nabla\cdot\zeta^{\varepsilon}=-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.7)

Here,
\check{s}\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
is defined by

\langle\check{s},\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle-s_{\varepsilon,k},(Mu)^{k}\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}\qquad\text{for}\;\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(5.8)

The equations above form a standard Dirichlet boundary value problem once augmented with boundary data at
\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Since
u^{\varepsilon}
and the
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
’s all vanish at the boundaries of the holes, we get

\zeta^{\varepsilon}(x)=-v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\partial\Omega}(x),\qquad x\in\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon}.

(5.9)

In the super-critical setting, the homogenized solution
u
in (
1.12
) does not need to vanish on
\partial\Omega
, and, hence,
\zeta^{\varepsilon}\not\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})
in general. This fact makes the quantification of convergence rates quite a difficult task for the super-critical setting, as is already the case in the classical setting
[
22
]
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
: the cut-off function approach

In Appendix
C
we give a unified treatment of the qualitative convergence. In Appendix
D
we give a unified treatment of the qualitative convergence, further imposing that the homogenized solution
u
satisfies
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
in the super-critical setting. We focus here on the most difficult case, namely the dilute super-critical case without assuming
u\rvert_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

The system (
5.7
) is a special case of the non-homogeneous Stokes system (
B.1
) with
\sigma=\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, and

\displaystyle b=\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathbf{I})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k},\quad

\displaystyle F=\sigma_{\varepsilon}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k},

(5.10)

\displaystyle g=-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k},\quad

\displaystyle s=\sigma_{\varepsilon}\check{s},\qquad h=-v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}.

Note that
g
and
h
above do satisfy the compatibility condition (
B.2
). We can apply the basic energy estimate of Stokes system (see Proposition
B.1
) to the pair
(\zeta^{\varepsilon},\tau^{\varepsilon})
.

To this end, we use the so-called radial cut-off function of Wang, Xu and Zhang
[
28
]
, to construct a proper vector field
H\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to plug into (
B.3
). For
t>0
sufficiently small, the authors of
[
28
]
constructed a cut-off function
\phi_{t}
satisfying
\phi_{t}=0
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{(3t)}
and
\phi_{t}=1
in
\Omega_{(t)}
and the support of
\nabla\phi_{t}
is contained in
\Omega_{(3t)}\setminus\Omega_{(2t)}
and
|\nabla\phi_{t}|\leq Ct^{-1}
. Moreover, in the support of
\nabla\phi_{t}
,
\nabla\phi_{t}(x)
is proportional to
N_{x^{\prime}}
, where
x^{\prime}=Px
is the orthogonal projection of
x
to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

For
t>0
small to be chosen, let

H(x)=\phi_{t}(x)v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}(x),\qquad x\in\Omega.

(5.11)

Then
H
satisfies
H+\zeta^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(due to
\phi_{t}=1
on
\partial\Omega
) and
H+\zeta^{\varepsilon}=0
in the holes of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
(due to the presence of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in both functions).
By computation we check

\displaystyle\nabla H

\displaystyle=(\nabla\phi_{t})v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}+\phi_{t}(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k})(Mu)^{k}+\phi_{t}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k},

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot H

\displaystyle=(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot(\nabla\phi_{t})(Mu)^{k}+\phi_{t}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k}+(\nabla\phi_{t})\cdot u.

We first estimate
\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Since
\phi_{t}
and
\nabla\phi_{t}
are supported in
\Omega_{(3t)}
and
\Omega_{(3t)}\setminus\Omega_{(2t)}
, respectively, we use Hölder’s inequality and the bound
|\nabla\phi_{t}|\leq Ct^{-1}
and deduce

\displaystyle\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}C\left[t^{-1}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}+\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\right](\|(Mu)^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{(3t)})}+\|\nabla(Mu)^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{(3t)})})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}C\left[t^{-1}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}+\|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\right]\|(Mu)^{k}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}.

For the
L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})
estimates of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and of
\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, we break the integration region
\Omega_{(3t)}
into
\varepsilon
-cubes. The number of such cubes is of order
O(t\varepsilon^{-d})
. Inside each
\varepsilon

is of order
O(\varepsilon^{d-2}\kappa_{\eta}^{2})
, and that of

is of order

. We conclude that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}\left[t^{-1}(\varepsilon^{d}t\varepsilon^{-d})^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\varepsilon^{d-2}\kappa_{\eta}^{2}t\varepsilon^{-d})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=C(\sigma_{\varepsilon}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}+t^{\frac{1}{2}})\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}.

Now we estimate
\|\nabla\cdot H\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. By the same reasoning as above, and choosing
p=2d/(d-2),q=d
for

, and
p=2,q=\infty
for

, we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot H\|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}C\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\|1\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\left[t^{-1}\|(Mu)^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}+\|\nabla(Mu)^{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\right]

(5.12)

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\phi_{t}\cdot u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ct^{\frac{1}{q}}\big(t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{H^{1}}+t^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{H^{2}}\big)\max_{k}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega_{(3t)})}+\|\nabla\phi_{t}\cdot u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{(3t)})}.

By (

)-(
4.13
), in each
\varepsilon
-cube,

is of order

for

\max_{k}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega_{(3t)})}\leq Ct^{\frac{1}{p}}\kappa_{\eta},\qquad d\geq 2.

The first part in the last line of (

) is bounded by
C(\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{H^{1}}+t\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{H^{2}})
.

Finally, we control
\|\nabla\phi_{t}\cdot u\|_{L^{2}}
. It is for this term that we use the special property of the radial cut-off function: the alignement of
\nabla\phi_{t}
with
N_{x^{\prime}}
, see above. This property allows us to use the condition
N\cdot u=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

We assume that
f\in C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Omega})
so that the
C^{2}
regularity (up to the boundary) for the second order elliptic equation with constant coefficient for
p
holds. More precisely,

\|\nabla p\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla^{2}p\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Omega})}.

Since

, we can replace the
\nabla p
on the left by

and replace

by
\nabla u
, and the estimate still holds. Now, for any

Note that

This estimate holds uniformly in

Combining all the estimates above, and choosing

Note that, under the assumption that

Appendix A
Some analysis tools for perforated domains

For a scalar or vector field
F
defined in a perforated domain, namely on
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
, by
zero
or
trivial
extension, we mean
F
is set as zero inside the holes. The zero extension is often denoted by
\widetilde{F}
. Let

denote the subspace

It consists of functions with vanishing traces at the boundaries of the holes. A further subspace of

is

belongs to

The following version of Poincaré inequality plays an important role in our analysis.

Proposition A.1

(A Poincaré inequality)
.

Let

. Let

be two positive real numbers and

. Then there exists a constant

satisfying

in

\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\leq\begin{cases}CR(\frac{r}{R})^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})},&\qquad d\geq 3,\\
CR|\log(\frac{r}{R})|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})},&\qquad d=2.\end{cases}

(A.1)

It is clear that if one changes the balls to cubes in the statement, the inequality still holds with
C
changed slightly. We refer to
[
2
, Lemma 3.4.1]
for the proof.
Extend the velocity field
u^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.1
) by zero inside the holes. Then inside each
\varepsilon
-cube

, we can apply (

) with

and

. The bounding constant is then
C\sigma_{\varepsilon}
with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
defined in (

).

Combining the resulted estimates in each
\varepsilon

\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}.

(A.2)

First clarified in
[
2
]
, this inequality is the key to derive energy estimates for the system (
1.1
) in the dilute setting. It takes into account the various asymptotic regimes for the system depending on the relative smallness of
\eta
with respect to
\varepsilon
.

As a general method to construct proper test functions for the weak formulation of (

\mathcal{R}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to V_{\varepsilon},\qquad\mathcal{E}:L^{2}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})\to L^{2}_{0}(\Omega).

They satisfy the important duality relation

\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{E}p)\;\nabla\cdot u=\int_{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}p\;\nabla\cdot(\mathcal{R}u),\qquad\forall u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\;\forall p\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon}).

Proposition A.2

(Restriction operator in the unit cell)

For each fixed
\eta\in(0,1)

that maps

(2)

If

(3)

There exists a constant
C
, independent of
\eta
or

\|\nabla(\mathcal{R}u)\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1}\setminus\eta\overline{T})}\leq C\left[\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}+\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}\right].

(A.4)

where

is defined in (

)

We then apply this construction with proper scaling in each

For each pair of

Appendix B
Basic energy estimates

We first record some basic energy estimate for Stokes systems, which will be used systematically in our convergence rates analysis.

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds, and
(v,p)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\sigma^{2}\Delta v+\nabla p=b+\sigma(\nabla\cdot F+s),&\qquad\text{in }\Omega^{\varepsilon},\\
&\nabla\cdot v=g,&\qquad\qquad\text{in }\Omega^{\varepsilon},\\
&v=\mathbf{1}_{\partial\Omega}(x)h(x),&\qquad\text{in }\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon},\end{aligned}\right.

(B.1)

where

is a real number,

is a vector field,

is a matrix field,

is a scalar field and

. Moreover,
v
and
p
are extended by zero in the holes of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
;
p
is mean zero in
\Omega
;

and

satisfy the compatibility assumption

\int_{\Omega}g=\int_{\partial\Omega}N\cdot h.

(B.2)

Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that, for any

(
H
is a lifting of the boundary data
h
on
\partial\Omega
) that satisfies

and
H+v=0
in the holes of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq

\displaystyle C\left\{\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}+\|s\|_{H^{-1}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}\right.

(B.3)

\displaystyle+\left.\|\nabla\cdot H\|_{L^{2}}+\sigma\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}}\right\}

Note that by the assumptions
v
and
p
are uniquely determined.

Proof.

Step 1: estimates for the pressure field.

Let
\hat{p}
be the extension of
p
and let
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be a vector field so that
\nabla\cdot w=\hat{p}
. Let
\mathcal{R}w
be the restricted field, then

\|\mathcal{R}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\sigma\|\nabla\mathcal{R}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\hat{p}\|_{L^{2}}.

Test
\mathcal{R}w
against the equation of
(v,p)
, we get

\displaystyle\|\hat{p}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\int\nabla v:\nabla\mathcal{R}w-\int b\cdot\mathcal{R}w+\sigma\int F:\nabla\mathcal{R}w-\sigma\langle s,\mathcal{R}w\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sigma\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}+\|b\|_{L^{2}}+\|F\|_{L^{2}}+\|s\|_{H^{-1}}\right)(\sigma\|\nabla\mathcal{R}w\|_{L^{2}}).

We can hence bound the pressure field by

\|\hat{p}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(\sigma\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}+\|b\|_{L^{2}}+\|F\|_{L^{2}}+\|s\|_{H^{-1}}\right).

Step 2: estimates for the velocity field.

By assumption
H+v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})
. Test it against the equations of
(v,p)
, we obtain

\sigma^{2}\int_{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}\nabla v:(\nabla v+\nabla H)=\int_{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}p\nabla\cdot(v+H)+b\cdot(v+H)-\sigma F:(\nabla v+\nabla H)\,dx+\sigma\langle s,v+H\rangle.

From this we easily get

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}}+\|\hat{p}\|_{L^{2}}(\|g\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot H\|_{L^{2}})

\displaystyle+(\|b\|_{L^{2}}+\|F\|_{L^{2}}+\|s\|_{H^{-1}})(\sigma\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}+\sigma\|\nabla H\|_{L^{2}}).

The desired estimate (

) then follows immediately.

Appendix C
The unified framework for qualitative convergence

For the sake of completeness, we recall here the qualitative results from the paper
[
15
]
. The idea is to combine the classical oscillating test function method (see e.g.
[
27
]
) with the asymptotic analysis of the two-scale cell problems to prove qualitative homogenization results.

The basic energy estimates (

) and (

[
1
,
2
]
.

Theorem C.1
.

Under Assumption
1
, let
M
be the symmetric positive definite matrix defined in (

(1)

In the

\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as

to
u
, and
\mathcal{E}p^{\varepsilon}
converges strongly to

in

In the

and strongly in

to
u
, and
\mathcal{E}p^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in

to
p
.

\varepsilon\to 0

\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon})\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{k})\cdot u^{\varepsilon}+q^{\varepsilon}_{k}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\phi-\hat{p}^{\varepsilon}v^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\phi=\int_{\Omega}f\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{k}\phi-\frac{u^{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\cdot e_{k}\phi.

Super-critical size ratio

In this setting,

&n\cdot M^{-1}(f-\nabla p)=0\qquad&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that the above problem is a rephrasing of (
1.12
), and it admits a unique solution. As a result, the possible limit for any converging subsequence of
(u^{\varepsilon},p^{\varepsilon})
is uniquely determined. Hence, the whole sequence converges and this establishes the qualitative convergence result in the super-critical setting.

C.2.
Critical size ratio

In this setting,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is of order one and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\sigma_{0}
for some positive number
\sigma_{0}
. By (
A.7
) and (
A.8
), the sequence
u^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\hat{p}^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Hence, there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
\varepsilon\to 0
, such that
u^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
to some vector field
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
\hat{p}^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
to some scalar field
p
.

Examine (
C.1
) along this subsequence. Because
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly and
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
converges strongly, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{1}=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u)\cdot(M^{-1}e_{k}).

For the same reason
I_{2}
converges and its limit is zero due to (
1.9
). The limits of
I_{4}
and
I_{5}+I_{6}
are also clear. For
I_{3}
, we use (
4.16
) and the uniform bound on
\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}
to conclude that, for
d\geq 3
,

|I_{3}|\leq C\eta^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\|u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\phi\|_{H^{1}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

The same limit also holds for
d=2
. We hence get, along the chosen subsequence,

\int_{\Omega}(M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\left[\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u-p\nabla\phi\right]=\int_{\Omega}M^{-1}e_{k}\cdot(\phi f)-e_{k}\cdot\frac{u}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\phi,\qquad\forall\,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

After multiplying
M
from the left on both sides of the equality and integration by parts, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi-p\nabla\phi+\frac{Mu}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\phi=\int_{\Omega}f\phi,\qquad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

This shows that
(u,p)
solves the problem (
1.14
) in the distributional sense. Since
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
p\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the pair
(u,p)
also solve the above problem in the weak sense. On the other hand, it is clear from the positivity of
M
that the weak solution is unique. We conclude that the limiting point of
(u^{\varepsilon},\hat{p}^{\varepsilon})
is uniquely determined, and the whole sequence converges accordingly.

C.3.
Sub-critical size holes

In this setting,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
vanishes in the limit of
\varepsilon\to 0
. In view of (
A.7
), there exist subsequences along which
u^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H^{1}
and strongly in
L^{2}
to some vector field
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
\hat{p}^{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
L^{2}
to some scalar field
p
.

The argument that we use to characterize the possible limiting points
(u,p)
is essentially the same as in the critical case, except that
I_{3}
is simpler to deal with. Indeed, it vanishes in the limit in view of (
4.15
). After sending
\varepsilon\to 0
along the converging subsequence, (
C.1
) becomes

\int_{\Omega}(M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\left[\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u-p\nabla\phi\right]=\int_{\Omega}M^{-1}e_{k}\cdot(\phi f),\qquad\forall\,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Arguing as in the critical setting, we conclude that any limit
(u,p)
of
(u^{\varepsilon},\hat{p}^{\varepsilon})
must be given by the unique solution of (
1.16
). The whole sequence of
(u^{\varepsilon},\hat{p}^{\varepsilon})
hence converges to the unique solution of (
1.16
). This completes the proof of Theorem
C.1
.

Appendix D
The unified framework for quantitative convergence

D.1.
Critical holes

We modify (
5.5
) and consider the following discrepancy functions:

\zeta^{\varepsilon}=u^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau^{\varepsilon}=p^{\varepsilon}(x)-p(x)-\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}.

(D.1)

Direct computation then shows that the following equations hold in the perforated domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\zeta^{\varepsilon}+\nabla\tau^{\varepsilon}=\left(({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}-1\right)\Delta u+2({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}\partial_{\ell}[(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\partial_{\ell}(Mu)^{k}]+({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}\check{s}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad-({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})^{i}\Delta(Mu)^{k}-({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k},\\
&\nabla\cdot\zeta^{\varepsilon}=-({\sigma_{\varepsilon}}/{\sigma_{0}})^{2}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(D.2)

Moreover, since the solution
u
of the homogenized problem (
1.14
) vanishes on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary condition
\zeta^{\varepsilon}=0
holds on
\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. We recognize the system as a special case of (
B.1
) with the data:
\sigma=1
,
h=0
(since
u=0
),
s=s_{1}+s_{2}
, and

\displaystyle g=-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k},\quad F=2(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\nabla(Mu)^{k},

\displaystyle b=[(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}-1]\Delta u-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}\Delta(Mu)^{k}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k}),\quad s_{1}=(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}\check{s},

and

\langle s_{2},\varphi\rangle=-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}\int q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k}\cdot\varphi.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
in the critical setting.

Note that in the critical setting, we have
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\sim\sigma_{0}\sim 1
and
\kappa_{\eta}\sim\varepsilon
. By the estimates of
(v^{\varepsilon}_{k},q^{\varepsilon}_{k})
’s, we easily get

\|g\|_{L^{2}}+\|F\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\kappa_{\eta}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}},\quad\|b\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(\kappa_{\eta}+|1-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}|)\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}}.

For the distributions
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, by (
5.3
) and following the analysis in the previous setting, and noting that
\kappa_{\eta}\ll\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we check

\|s_{1}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}.

For
s_{2}
, we use (
4.16
) to get
\|s_{2}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
.

Combine all the estimates above and apply (
B.3
). We conclude that

\|\zeta^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\kappa_{\eta}+|1-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}|+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}.

Note that
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is much larger than
\kappa_{\eta}
since
\kappa_{\eta}\sim\varepsilon
, so the
\check{s}
term makes the largest contribution to the error. The above establishes the first part of (
1.13
). The second part follows from the estimate of
\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon}
and the fact that
\int\tau^{\varepsilon}
is of order
O(\kappa_{\eta})
, which in turns follows from (
4.16
).
∎

Remark D.1
.

Note that the choice (
D.1
) corresponds to setting the following correctors

r_{\varepsilon}=(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})(Mu)^{k}+[(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}-1]u,\quad t_{\varepsilon}=(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}.

Clearly we have

\|r_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(\kappa_{\eta}+|1-(\sigma_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{0})^{2}|)\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}.

This allows us to show smallness of
\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{2}}
. Note, however, the
L^{2}
norm of the pressure corrector does not seem small.

D.2.
Sub-critical holes

We modify (
5.5
) and consider the discrepancy functions:

\zeta^{\varepsilon}=u^{\varepsilon}-v^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau^{\varepsilon}=p^{\varepsilon}(x)-p(x)-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(x)(Mu)^{k}.

(D.3)

Direct computation shows that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta\zeta^{\varepsilon}+\nabla\tau^{\varepsilon}=-\sigma^{-2}_{\varepsilon}Mu+\check{s}-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\Delta(Mu)^{k}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\partial_{\ell}[(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})^{i}\partial_{\ell}(Mu)^{k}]-q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k},\\
&\nabla\cdot\zeta^{\varepsilon}=-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(D.4)

Again the boundary condition
\zeta^{\varepsilon}=0
on
\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon}
is satisfied.

We recognize the above system for
(\zeta^{\varepsilon},\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon})
, where
\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon}
denotes the mean zero part of
\tau^{\varepsilon}
, as a special case of (
B.1
) with the following data:
\sigma=1
,
h=0
,
s=s_{1}+s_{2}
, and

\displaystyle b=-\sigma^{-2}_{\varepsilon}Mu-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\Delta(Mu)^{k},\quad

\displaystyle F=2(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\otimes\nabla(Mu)^{k},

\displaystyle g=-(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})\cdot\nabla(Mu)^{k},\quad

\displaystyle s_{1}=\check{s},\quad\langle s_{2},\varphi\rangle=-\int q^{\varepsilon}_{k}\nabla(Mu)^{k}\cdot\varphi.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
in the sub-critical setting.

From our estimates of
v^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
q^{\varepsilon}_{k}
’s, we obtain

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}},\quad\|s_{2}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}},

\displaystyle\|b\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}+\kappa_{\eta}\|\Delta u\|_{L^{\infty}}).

The estimate of
s_{1}
is still a consequence of (
5.3
) and reads

\|s_{1}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\kappa_{\eta}+\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}.

Note that in the sub-critical setting
O(\kappa_{\varepsilon})\ll O(\varepsilon)
, and
O(\kappa_{\varepsilon}/\sigma_{\varepsilon})\ll O(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2})
. All the results above combined yield

\|\nabla\zeta^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}\right)\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}

The above establishes the first part of (
1.15
). The second part also holds because the mean of
\tau^{\varepsilon}
is smaller, of order
O(\kappa_{\eta})
.
∎

Remark D.2
.

Note that (
D.3
) corresponds to choosing the correctors

r_{\varepsilon}=(v^{\varepsilon}_{k}-M^{-1}e_{k})(Mu)^{k},\qquad t_{\varepsilon}=q^{\varepsilon}_{k}(Mu)^{k}.

Clearly,
\|r_{\varepsilon}\|\leq C\kappa_{\eta}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
and
\|t_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
. The second part of (
1.15
) then follows.
