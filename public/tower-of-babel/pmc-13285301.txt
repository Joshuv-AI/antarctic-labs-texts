Awareness, attitudes and acceptance among undergraduate students at king faisal university regarding intersex surgeries: an online survey
Authors: Shahad Alshamrani, Fadwa Alqasem, Sadeem Alanazi, Munerah Almulhem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntersex surgery, formerly known as ‘sex correction’, is a medical procedure that aligns an individual’s physical characteristics with their assigned sex at birth. Awareness and attitudes among university students in Saudi Arabia regarding intersex surgeries remain largely unexplored.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among undergraduate students at King Faisal University. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from various colleges. The self-administered questionnaire included four sections: (1) demographic information, (2) awareness of intersex surgery, (3) knowledge of distinguishing intersex from transgender identity, and (4) attitudes toward individuals who have undergone intersex surgery. The internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.96).ResultsA total of 400 students participated in the study, including 270 females (67.5%) and 130 males (32.5%). Of these, 189 (47.4%) demonstrated a high level of understanding of the difference between sex correction and gender transition, and 197 participants (49.3%) showed positive attitudes. Awareness was significantly associated with gender, age group, and college type (p < 0.05). Attitudes were significantly associated with age group (χ² = 8.338, p = 0.015), with participants aged 21–24 years exhibiting the highest percentage of positive attitudes.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the need for culturally sensitive educational initiatives to improve awareness and address misconceptions among university students in Saudi Arabia about intersex surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27516-9.

Background

Intersex is a broad term used to describe individuals born with reproductive or sexual characteristics that do not conform to typical definitions of male or female [1]. This can include conditions where individuals have external features typically associated with one sex but internal reproductive anatomy that aligns with another [2]. Alternatively, some individuals may be born with ambiguous genitalia, which does not fit conventional definitions of male or female. For example, a girl may be born with a noticeably large clitoris or without a vaginal opening, or a boy may have a very small penis or a scrotum that is divided, resembling labia.

Most individuals are born with 46 chromosomes in each cell, which carry their genetic information [3]. These chromosomes are organized into 23 pairs, with one pair, the sex chromosomes, playing a crucial role in determining whether an individual develops typically male or female physical traits. Those with one X and one Y chromosome are typically identified as male, whereas those with two X chromosomes are identified as female [4, 5]. However, in some cases, individuals may be born with atypical chromosomal arrangements that affect their sexual development [6]. For instance, a person may have only one X chromosome or an abnormal combination such as XXY. Some individuals may also experience a phenomenon known as mosaicism, where different cells in their body have different chromosomal compositions, such as some cells having XY chromosomes while others have XX [6].

Up to 1.7% of the global population may have intersex traits [7]. These conditions can sometimes be inherited genetically, passing through generations within families. Intersex traits may occur due to various factors, such as changes in the androgen hormone receptor gene, exposure to synthetic or natural hormones during embryonic development, a missing or misplaced sex-determining region Y (SRY) gene, or other genetic conditions that cause abnormal hormone levels affecting genital development.

Intersex individuals are often viewed through the lens of cultural taboos because their biological characteristics do not conform to traditional binary gender roles [8, 9]. This leads to societal challenges, including stigma and lack of awareness. In Saudi Arabia, official statistics on the prevalence of intersex conditions are lacking; however, several documented cases have been reported over the past 40 years [10–14]. These cases have significant psychological, ethical, social, and medical implications, and their management requires a skilled multidisciplinary team. Many intersex individuals may be unaware of their condition, and others may choose to keep it hidden to avoid the social challenges associated with non-conformity to gender norms [13].

One medical approach to addressing intersex is surgery, previously called sex correction and sometimes referred to as sex reassignment. In this context, intersex surgeries are considered to align an individual’s physical characteristics with a more definitive gender identity. However, importantly, gender transition without biological ambiguity is not accepted in Islamic jurisprudence. In Saudi Arabia, intersex surgeries are conducted only after careful medical consideration and adherence to Islamic legal principles. Knowledge is lacking regarding the public’s acceptance of and attitudes to intersex surgery in Saudi Arabia, which underscores the need for further research in this area.

Understanding awareness, attitudes, and acceptance among undergraduate students concerning intersex surgery is essential in Saudi Arabia, given the cultural and religious context that shapes perspectives on this sensitive topic. Existing literature on this subject is scarce, and data is lacking on the perceptions of young adults who are likely to influence or seek healthcare services related to intersex surgery in the future. In Saudi Arabia, the term ‘sex correction’ refers to medically indicated procedures that align an individual’s biological characteristics with their assigned sex at birth, often involving intersex cases. This is distinct from ‘gender correction’, which in some cultural contexts may refer to transitioning for transgender individuals and carries different cultural and religious implications.

Clarifying the distinction between intersex surgery and transgender identity is important, especially in the context of this study. In Saudi Arabia, ‘sex correction’ refers to medically indicated procedures aimed at changing congenital or biological differences, such as intersex conditions, under strict ethical and religious guidelines. Conversely, ‘transgender’ refers to individuals who seek to transition from their assigned sex at birth to another gender identity, often without biological ambiguity. This distinction is crucial in Islamic and cultural contexts because intersex surgery is sometimes medically and religiously sanctioned, whereas transgender transitions are generally not accepted. The phrasing of some questionnaire items reflects common misunderstandings among the general population, which this study aimed to explore and measure.

Objective

This study aimed to assess awareness, conceptual understanding, and attitudes among undergraduate students at King Faisal University concerning medically indicated intersex surgeries. It also examined how these outcomes varied by gender, age group and college type.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a cross-sectional design, using a self-administered online questionnaire. The data were collected between January and April 2024 at King Faisal University (KFU) in Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. This is a large public university with a diverse undergraduate population across various academic faculties, including both health and non-health disciplines.

Study population and sampling

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among undergraduate students at KFU, including participants from both health-related (medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and applied medical sciences) and non-health-related colleges (humanities, business, science, and engineering).

The sample size was calculated using EPI Info (software developed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention for epidemiological studies), based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, a 50% expected frequency, and a design effect of 1. The minimum required sample was 379 participants.

Before launching the main survey, a pilot test was conducted with 30 students to evaluate the clarity and cultural appropriateness of the questionnaire. Minor adjustments were made accordingly, and the pilot data were excluded from the final analysis.

A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed, and the final sample comprised 400 fully completed responses. The exclusion criteria were students who were not currently enrolled in undergraduate programmes and those who did not complete the questionnaire in full.

Instrument development and validation

The questionnaire was developed based on a review of both regional and international literature related to intersex conditions, intersex surgeries, and associated social attitudes-particularly within conservative and Islamic contexts. The initial items were informed by Ahmer et al. (2021) and refined through consultations with public health experts and contextual specialists to ensure cultural and clinical appropriateness in the Saudi setting [15].

To support the content validity of the instrument, the questionnaire was reviewed by a subject matter expert in intersex surgeries in Saudi Arabia. Although a formal Content Validity Index (CVI) was not calculated, the expert review process ensured that all items aligned with accepted content standards. This approach is consistent with early validity frameworks such as that proposed by Lynn (1986), who emphasized expert judgment as a core component of content validation [16].

A pilot test involving 30 undergraduate students was conducted to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural relevance of the items. Minor revisions were made based on student feedback. Internal consistency was assessed for the attitude domain (seven Likert-type items), yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96, indicating excellent reliability.

Data collection and management

The questionnaire was developed using Google Forms and distributed through social media platforms commonly used by students, including WhatsApp and Twitter, to maximize reach across various colleges. It included four main sections: (1) demographic information (sex, age group, and college type); (2) awareness of intersex surgery, assessed with a single yes/no item; (3) conceptual understanding, evaluated using eight true/false items designed to assess students’ ability to differentiate between intersex surgery and gender transition; and (4) attitudes and acceptance, assessed using seven statements rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree). The responses were exported to Microsoft Excel for data cleaning.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize demographic characteristics and item responses. Awareness of the term ‘sex correction’ was analyzed as a binary variable (yes/no). Conceptual understanding was assessed by summing the correct responses to eight true/false items that distinguished between intersex and transgender identity. Participants who answered five or more items correctly were classified as having high conceptual understanding. Attitudes were measured using the mean score of seven Likert-scale items (rated 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree). A cutoff of ≤ 1.5 was used to classify participants as having a positive attitude, whereas scores > 1.5 indicated a negative attitude. Chi-square tests were used to assess the associations between awareness and attitude categories and sociodemographic variables.

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.19.3; University of Amsterdam). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data availability

The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available because they are intended for internal use only. However, they are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Not applicable.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Deanship of Scientific Research at KFU (reference number KFU-REC-2023-JAN-ETHICS465). Participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous, with informed consent obtained from all participants before their involvement. To ensure confidentiality, access to the electronic forms and datasets was restricted.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the awareness, attitudes, and acceptance among undergraduate students at KFU in Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia, regarding intersex surgeries. The results revealed a significant lack of awareness regarding these procedures, which are medical interventions designed to address congenital or acquired conditions affecting an individual’s biological sex alignment. This gap in understanding highlights the necessity of integrating more focused educational initiatives into the medical curriculum, ensuring that students are well-equipped with accurate knowledge of these sensitive issues.

The study revealed prevalent confusion among students between intersex and transgender identities. This misunderstanding points to the importance of providing clear, context-specific information that differentiates these concepts, particularly in light of Islamic principles, where transgender identities may face societal and religious rejection. Addressing these misunderstandings through educational programmes could promote more informed attitudes to intersex surgeries as a legitimate medical procedure, distinct from the broader discussion of gender identity.

In comparison with existing literature, a study conducted in Pakistan on medical students reported that one-third of the population did not understand the distinction between transgender and intersex [17]. Similarly, research conducted among college students in religious populations showed some familiarity with the term intersex, but many demonstrated a higher level of awareness regarding the distinction between intersex and transgender [18]. These findings contrast with our results, where many students had limited awareness. However, similar to our study, students in these studies showed conservative views towards intersex individuals, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding of the topic across cultures.

Intersex surgeries, as an intervention for intersex variations, have been met with mixed reactions. Although some college students support surgery for intersex individuals [17, 18], decisions regarding such procedures must consider medical, psychological, social, ethical, and religious factors. Given the sensitive nature of these decisions, collaboration between medical experts and religious scholars is essential for ensuring a holistic approach that respects both clinical practices and religious considerations while safeguarding patient confidentiality and well-being.

Importantly, gender transition surgery is generally prohibited under Islamic law, particularly when not associated with a medically diagnosed intersex condition. This likely contributes to negative attitudes toward intersex surgery. The confusion between intersex and transgender, often associated with gender identity changes, may exacerbate these negative perceptions because transgender identities are not accepted within the context of Saudi societal and religious norms.

Another important consideration in the context of intersex-related procedures in Saudi Arabia is the potential for confusion between medically diagnosed intersex conditions and rare cases of potential misunderstanding or misclassification. Such cases may involve claims of intersex status to seek approval for procedures that are not aligned with established medical or religious guidelines. This highlights the need for rigorous diagnostic protocols and clear classification criteria to avoid misdiagnosis and to ensure that interventions remain consistent with both Islamic ethical principles and clinical best practices.

The findings of this exploratory study suggest a need for educational interventions aimed at raising awareness among university students about sex correction and its distinction from transgender identity. Healthcare providers could benefit from culturally sensitive training to handle such cases if encountered in clinical practice. These results also highlight the importance of fostering a dialogue that considers medical, ethical, and religious perspectives to ensure appropriate patient care.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings. Additionally, the use of an online survey may have introduced other potential biases, including response bias, social desirability bias, accessibility bias, and the potential for fraudulent or insincere participation. Despite assurances of anonymity, the sensitive nature of the topic may have further influenced the honesty of participant responses. Although efforts were made to minimize these issues through data cleaning and anonymity, they should still be considered when interpreting the results. The lack of diverse perspectives on this topic in Saudi Arabia may also limit the depth of contextual analysis. Another limitation is the use of a design effect of 1 for the sample size calculation. We acknowledge that using a design effect of 1 may underestimate potential clustering effects in the context of non-probability sampling. However, given the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of prior data to estimate clustering, we chose a conservative approach. Future studies should consider using a higher design effect to account for potential clustering and improve the precision of estimates.

A significant strength of this study lies in its focus on a sensitive and often avoided topic, contributing valuable insights into the awareness and attitudes of Saudi youth regarding intersex surgery.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into the awareness, attitudes, and acceptance among participants concerning intersex surgery. The findings indicate a generally low level of awareness, with many students demonstrating limited differentiation between the concepts of intersex and transgender identity. Additionally, the study observed varying attitudes and levels of acceptance towards individuals who have undergone intersex surgeries, highlighting the need for improved awareness among those involved in the management of intersex variations. Furthermore, cultural factors, national regulations, and religious considerations should be carefully integrated into the decision-making process to ensure patient-centered care that aligns with ethical and societal frameworks. These findings underscore the importance of fostering culturally sensitive education and clinical practices to enhance awareness and appropriate patient care.
