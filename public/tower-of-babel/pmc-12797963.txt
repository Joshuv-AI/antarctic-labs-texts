Exosomal regulation of cellular reprogramming and polarization in the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Mobina Bayat, Parviz Shahabi, Tohid Rezaei, Javid Sadri Nahand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe reprogramming and polarization of cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) are fundamental processes that critically influence the dynamics of tumor progression and response to therapy. These processes can either promote tumor evasion and metastasis or mount effective anti-tumor responses, thereby determining the overall tumor landscape. At the center of this dynamic interplay are exosomes that facilitate crucial intercellular communication by transferring bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. While the roles of individual TME components in cancer progression have been extensively characterized, the precise mechanisms through which the reprogramming of these elements favors tumor advancement remain inadequately elucidated. This review investigates the complex functions of exosomal signaling in the reprogramming and polarization of TME constituents, highlighting their dual capacity to orchestrate tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing signals. This synthesis aims to elucidate how the modulation of TME cell behavior impacts cancer progression and contributes to the broader understanding of tumor biology.Graphical abstract Keywords: TME, Cellular reprogramming, Polarization, Exosomal dynamics, Intercellular communication

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex and dynamic ecosystem comprising malignant and non-malignant cellular components, including immune cells, stromal cells, blood vessels, and extracellular matrix elements [1]. Far from merely supporting tumor growth, the TME actively influences tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis, shaping the tumor’s biological characteristics and impacting therapeutic efficacy [2].

Central to TME dynamics are cellular reprogramming and polarization, processes crucial for determining tumor cell fate. Malignant cells can reprogram their surroundings, leveraging the capabilities of non-malignant cells to establish a supportive niche that promotes their growth and survival [3]. This adaptive reprogramming is vital for tumors to navigate the evolving landscape of external pressures. Additionally, polarization within the TME allows immune and stromal cells to functionally adapt, either facilitating or inhibiting tumor growth. This polarization reflects the complex signaling environment shaped by the tumor, contributing to an immunosuppressive landscape that enables immune evasion and fosters a permissive environment for cancer progression [1, 4].

Exosomes play a pivotal role in this intricate network, acting as mediators of intercellular communication and transmitting a diverse array of signals that can reinforce oncogenic processes or promote anti-tumor immunity [5]. These extracellular vesicles carry bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, capable of modifying the functional states of both tumor and non-tumor cells [6]. They are integral to reprogramming and polarization within the TME, influencing cellular responses to various stimuli.

The integration of exosomal signaling with TME dynamics sheds light on the complex interactions that shape tumor biology, emphasizing the urgent need for dedicated research efforts to unravel these intricate relationships [6]. A deeper exploration into the interplay of cellular reprogramming, polarization, and exosomal communication will pave the way for innovative and targeted therapeutic interventions, marking a significant advancement in the field of oncology.

Tumor microenvironment components

The TME is a complex and diverse assembly of cell types, including myeloid immune cells (e.g., tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and neutrophils), adaptive immune cells (such as T cells and natural killer (NK) cells), stromal cells (like fibroblasts and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)), and vascular cells (including blood and lymphatic endothelial cells) (Graphical Abstract) [5]. Additionally, cancer-derived exosomes (Ca-Exo) serve as important mediators of communication within the TME [6]. Understanding these interactions offers potential avenues for innovative therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting the supportive role of the TME in cancer development. Given the significance of exosomes and their components within the TME, the following section will provide a detailed discussion on the role of various exosomal components in the reprogramming and polarization of cells within the TME.

The role of Ca-Exo in reprogramming of fibroblasts to CAF

Ca-Exo proteins in CAF

Fibroblasts hold a critical yet complex role in the TME, which is not fully understood. In healthy tissues, fibroblasts maintain homeostasis and facilitate wound healing and tissue regeneration. However, in cancer, their influence becomes dualistic—initially capable of tumor suppression by enhancing immune responses and inhibiting proliferation, they can later reprogram to support tumor growth and metastasis [7, 8]. This transformation is closely related to their activation state, characterized by the overexpression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). Activated fibroblasts produce an extracellular matrix that provides structural support and biochemical signals, thereby shaping the behavior of neighboring cells [9, 10]. Tumor progression is influenced not only by malignant cells but also by CAFs, which promote tumor advancement, angiogenesis, and immune evasion [11]. Understanding the interactions between CAFs and cancer cells is essential for developing targeted therapies aimed at preventing the transformation of normal fibroblasts into CAFs. CAFs enhance cancer cell invasiveness and metastasis by secreting cytokines and chemokines that activate signaling pathways associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [12], and by producing a specialized extracellular matrix that promotes cancer cell adhesion and invasion [13].

Bidirectional communication between cancer cells and fibroblasts involves neoplastic cells releasing cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-8, which not only activate fibroblasts but also stimulate their proliferation [14]. In response, activated CAFs secrete chemokines like SDF-1α that further enhance tumor growth [15]. Recent studies have identified various CAF subtypes—myofibroblastic, inflammatory, and antigen-presenting CAFs—each exhibiting distinct transcriptional profiles and functional roles [16]. Despite efforts to characterize these interactions, the mechanisms driving the reprogramming of quiescent fibroblasts into CAFs remain poorly defined. Notably, cancer-derived exosomes are instrumental in this transformation, delivering bioactive molecules such as cytokines and RNAs that induce CAF differentiation. This section will explore the role of exosomes in fibroblast reprogramming, emphasizing their potential as therapeutic targets and biomarkers in cancer treatment (Fig. 1).

Emerging evidence points to members of the Wnt protein family in the extracellular environment facilitating stromal-epithelial communication [17]. Certain extracellular Wnt proteins, particularly Wnt2B, have been linked to fibroblast survival and activation via Wnt/β-catenin signaling, promoting a pro-tumorigenic environment [18–21]. Research indicates that Wnt2B is enriched in exosomes from cervical cancer cells, activating fibroblasts upon uptake [20], underscoring that exosomal Wnt2B derived from cervical cancer fosters the reprogramming of fibroblasts into CAFs by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) are crucial in determining cellular fate and facilitating the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, specifically through the promotion of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) and the chemokine CXCL12 in breast cancer [22, 23]. The regulation of ROS by antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), is vital for cellular function; SOD1 knockdown can lead to fibroblast senescence and impair wound healing [24, 25]. Additionally, Survivin, often overexpressed in breast cancer, is secreted by tumor cells via exosomes, which modulate ROS levels and promote the conversion of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [26–29]. Exosomes from dormant lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cells contain integrin beta 6 (ITGB6), activating the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) pathway in fibroblasts, thereby enhancing their transition to CAFs [30–33]. CAFs can originate from various sources, including bone marrow, endothelial cells, pericytes, and adipocytes [34–36]. Recent research indicates that gastric cancer exosomes can reprogram pericytes into CAFs by inducing phenotypic changes and activating the PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways via bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) [37]. The TGF-β superfamily, particularly BMP2, also plays a role in this transition, and inhibitors of BMP signaling can prevent the conversion of pericytes to CAFs [38, 39].

Gutkin et al. [40] demonstrated that hTERT mRNA is transferred from cancer cells to telomerase-negative fibroblasts via exosomes. Internalization of hTERT mRNA induces increased proliferation and delays cellular senescence. In subsequent work, they showed that exosomal hTERT not only activates telomerase but also alters the miRNA transcriptome profile in fibroblasts, contributing to CAF formation [41] Specifically, miR-342 plays a significant role in this process, promoting a proliferative phenotype and enhancing tumor progression [41]. Thus, these studies underscore the critical influence of exosomal hTERT on fibroblast reprogramming within the TME.

Ca-Exo non-coding RNAs in CAFs

MicroRNAs

MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs crucial for post-transcriptional gene regulation across various cell types [42]. In oncology, they are key modulators of processes such as proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and invasion [43–45]. Recent studies indicate miRNAs not only affect cancer cells directly but also alter the TME by influencing the miRNA profiles of neighboring cells [5, 46–48]. This includes roles in tumor angiogenesis, immune invasion, and stromal interactions.

Tumor-derived exosomal miRNAs promote the transformation of fibroblasts into CAFs. For instance, exosomal miR-9 from breast cancer cells induces a CAF-like state in fibroblasts, contributing to tumor growth (Fig. 2) [49]. Wang et al. [50] found that exosomal miR-27a from gastric cancer cells facilitates this transformation, enhancing cancer cell proliferation and motility. Similarly, miR-10b-5p, enriched in gastric cancer, targets the anti-fibrotic KLF11, activating the TGF-β pathway in fibroblasts and promoting their transition to CAFs [50–53]. Exosomal miR-10b from colorectal cancer (CRC) cells inhibits fibroblast proliferation while promoting TGF-β and α-SMA expression, enhancing CAF traits [54]. Furthermore, miR-224 activates AKT signaling in fibroblasts, contributing to their reprogramming [55].

The Wnt signaling pathway, comprising multiple ligands and receptors, is critical for fibroblast differentiation into CAFs. Exosomal miR-146a from breast cancer cells activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promoting CAF characteristics [56]. In contrast, exosomal miR-34c inhibits this transformation, while reduced levels of miR-124 in fibroblasts result in increased α-SMA and FAP expression [57, 58]. Exosomal transfer of miR-124 has been shown to reverse these features by targeting SPHK1 in ovarian cancer [58]. Additionally, H. pylori-activated gastric fibroblasts downregulated miR-124-3p, promoting proliferation and migration [59]. Furthermore, the TP53 gene, a crucial tumor suppressor, is often inactivated in cancer. TP53 loss in cancer cells activates JAK2-STAT3 pathways, affecting the TME and enhancing angiogenesis and tumor growth [60, 61]. Moreover, altered TP53 expression influences the secretion of miRNAs in exosomes, connecting to the modulation of the TME [62–64].

Yoshii et al. [65] found that exosomes from TP53-deficient cancer cells carry miRNAs that downregulate TP53 in fibroblasts, promoting their transition to CAFs. Specifically, exosomal miR-1249-5p, −6737-5p, and − 6819-5p can suppress TP53 expression in these cells. Additionally, exosomal miR-125b from metastatic breast cancer enhances CAF transformation by downregulating TP53 and its partner, Tp53inp1 [66]. Likewise, exosomal miR-375 from Merkel cell carcinoma promotes this reprogramming by targeting both TP53 and RBPJ [67]. These findings highlight the critical role of TP53 dysregulation and miRNA-mediated communication in shaping the TME.

LncRNAs

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), characterized as non-protein-coding RNAs greater than 200 nucleotides in length, have emerged as significant regulators of several cellular pathways [68–70]. Among their many roles, tumor-secreted exosomal lncRNAs are predominantly crucial in reprogramming the TME [71]. These exosomes facilitate the uptake of lncRNAs by recipient cells, leading to phenotypic and functional changes that can promote tumor progression [72]. Studies have indicated that lncRNAs within tumor-derived exosomes can drive the transformation of NFs into CAFs [73].

For instance, exosomal LINC00691 originated from gastric cancer cells has been shown to activate normal fibroblasts via modulation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [74]. Additionally, lncRNA Gm26809 (ENSMU-SG00000097815) has established to be significantly upregulated in cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLL2) upon exposure to exosomes from B16F0 melanoma cells [75]. Hu et al. [76] that these exosomes promote the transformation of NIH/3T3 fibroblasts into CAFs, increasing CAF markers like α-SMA and FAP and enhancing cell migration. The presence of Gm26809 in these exosomes was crucial for this transformation, as knockdown led to reduced tumor cell proliferation and migration [76]. Another study highlighted that the exosomal lncRNA linc00881 from osteosarcoma cells promotes the conversion of lung fibroblasts into CAFs in the pre-metastatic niche. Co-treatment with osteosarcoma-derived exosomes increased linc00881 and decreased miR-29c-3p expression, facilitating fibroblast transformation prior to lung metastasis [77]. Activated CAFs subsequently secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, altering the TME and promoting osteosarcoma cell migration.

Exosomes from oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells containing lncRNA Lnc-CAF were shown to activate CAFs in stromal fibroblasts, establishing a feedback loop that supports tumor growth (Fig. 2) [73].). Lnc-CAF and IL-33 co-expression was linked to increased CAF markers such as α-SMA and vimentin. Silencing IL-33 reduced Lnc-CAF-driven fibroblast activation and tumor cell proliferation [73]. Moreover, Tong et al. [78] reported that lncRNA POU3F3 expression is significantly upregulated in normal fibroblasts exposed to esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cell-derived exosomes. This lncRNA transfer helps promote CAF transformation by increasing α-SMA expression [78]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the crucial roles of Ca-Exo lncRNAs in the intricate interactions among tumor cells, fibroblasts, and the TME, underscoring their potential as significant therapeutic targets in cancer treatment.

The role of Ca-Exo in reprogramming of myeloid immune cells

Tumor-associated macrophages

TAMs constitute a crucial element of the TME and significantly influence various aspects of tumor biology, including initiation, progression, and metastasis [79].). Traditionally, TAMs were thought to primarily originate from bone marrow-derived monocytes migrating into the TME in response to tumor-promoting cytokines, such as CCL2/5 and CSF-1 [80, 81]. However, it is now recognized that tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs), derived from embryonic precursors, also contribute significantly to the TAM population [82, 83]. Despite extensive research, our understanding of the origins and functional diversity of macrophages within tumors remains limited.

Macrophages exhibit remarkable plasticity, allowing them to adopt various activation states based on environmental signals. They are typically categorized into two main polarization states: classically activated M1 macrophages and alternatively activated M2 macrophages [81]. The diversity among TAMs arises from their varied origins and the complex signals within the TME. While M1 and M2 represent polarization extremes observed in vitro, actual TAMs often exist along a spectrum, displaying a mix of both characteristics [84–86].

M1 macrophages, activated by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) or microbial lipopolysaccharides, are associated with pro-inflammatory responses and exhibit anti-tumor activity by releasing inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Conversely, M2 macrophages, induced by IL-4 and IL-13, support tumor development, metastasis, and immune evasion through the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β [87, 88]. During early tumor development, macrophages tend to polarize towards the M1 phenotype; however, as tumors progress, a shift towards M2-like TAMs is observed [89]. Macrophage polarization is influenced by various factors, including cytokines, proteases, exosomes, and intracellular signaling pathways, all intricately modulated by the TME [90]. Given the pivotal role of exosomes in mediating macrophage polarization, acting either as facilitators of tumor progression or inhibitors, there is a burgeoning interest in harnessing these vesicles for therapeutic applications. Consequently, this section will delve into the impact of exosomes on macrophage polarization towards M1 or M2 phenotypes within the TME, elucidating their potential as innovative targets for cancer therapy.

Ca-Exo proteins in TAM

The T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain (Tim) family includes type-I transmembrane proteins critical for regulating T cell activation, maintaining immune tolerance, and influencing various immune responses in both humans and rodents. Tim-3, or HAVCR2, was initially identified in activated T helper type 1 (Th1) cells but is now known to be expressed in regulatory T cells (Tregs), macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and several tumor cells [91, 92]. Tim-3 modulates macrophage activity in various diseases, fostering immune tolerance during infections by inhibiting lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and Toll-like receptor (TLR) activation via STAT1 signaling [93]. Additionally, in diabetic nephropathy, Tim-3 promotes M2 macrophage differentiation while exacerbating podocyte damage [94]. Cheng et al. [95] observed that in osteosarcoma, Tim-3 levels are elevated and can be transferred to macrophages via exosomes, promoting M2 polarization. Similarly, exosomal Tim-3 from melanoma cells reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines, facilitating M2 polarization [96]. Collectively, these observations highlight the multifaceted role of Tim-3 in immune modulation, particularly within the context of the TME.

Integrin αvβ6, typically absent in normal adult epithelial tissues, is upregulated during wound healing and various malignancies, influencing tumor dynamics by facilitating cellular invasion, inhibiting apoptosis, and regulating matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression [97–100]. Notably, integrin αvβ6 can drive macrophage polarization toward M2 via increased IL-34 [101].

Moreover, Lu et al. [102] demonstrated the exosomal transfer of αvβ6 from cancer cells to monocytes, promoting M2 polarization through downregulation of STAT1 and MX1/2, thus enhancing tumor progression. In contrast, monocytes lacking αvβ6 exhibited heightened M1 polarization [102]. Additionally, prostate cancer-derived exosomal CXCL14 promotes M2 macrophage polarization by activating NF-κB signaling, a pathway crucial for macrophage polarization during tumor progression (Fig. 3) [103–108]. However, exosomal HMGB1 can inhibit NF-κB activity, thereby facilitating M2 polarization [109]. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a key immune regulator expressed on various cells, binds to PD-1 on T cells, dampening their activation [110]. Recent studies indicate that PD-L1 can also influence macrophage polarization. Wie et al. [111] found that PD-L1 engagement with PD-1 promotes M2 polarization and enhances mitochondrial function through the Erk/Akt/mTOR pathway. Exosomal PD-L1 from lung cancer cells was shown to promote M2 polarization and support cancer cell invasion and migration [112]. Yuan et al. identified that endoplasmic reticulum stress in OSCC cells leads to exosomal PD-L1 release, further facilitating M2 macrophage polarization [113].

CMTM6, a protein modulating PD-L1 expression, plays a critical role in suppressing anti-tumor responses [114, 115]. M2 macrophages often exhibit high PD-L1 levels in various cancers and, as reported by Pang et al. [116] exosomal CMTM6 activates ERK1/2 signaling, promoting M2 polarization and enhancing cancer cell invasion [116]. Enolase 2 (ENO2) is a glycolytic enzyme whose expression is upregulated in several malignancies. Elevated serum ENO2 correlates with poorer prognoses in patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and DLBCL [117–119]. ENO2 promotes M2 polarization in DLBCL by enhancing glycolytic activity [120–123]. Recently, Shao et al. [124] demonstrated that exosomal ENO2 from DLBCL enhances macrophage glycolysis and polarization, with the glycolytic inhibitor 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) counteracting this effect [124]. Additionally, ENO2 influences the β-catenin/c-Myc glycolytic pathway, promoting M2 polarization [125–129]. In a related study, Ling et al. [130] reported that exosomal GP130 from DLBCL activates STAT3 signaling, leading to the upregulation of M2-like markers and oncogenic genes [130]. Overall, these findings reveal how tumor-derived exosomes can influence macrophage polarization to a protumor phenotype in the TME, with significant implications for cancer progression and therapeutic strategies.

Ca-Exo non-coding RNAs in TAM

MicroRNAs

The phosphoinositide 3-kinase and AKT (PI3K-AKT) signaling pathways are crucial for cellular growth and survival, particularly influencing the polarization of M1 and M2 macrophages. Research, including knockout mouse studies, emphasizes the pathway’s significant role in promoting M2 macrophage activation [131]. Inhibitors targeting key components of this pathway, such as PI3K, AKT, and mTOR, have been developed to clarify their biological implications.

Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) functions as a tumor suppressor and a negative regulator of the PI3K pathway [132]. Recent findings indicate that exosomal miRNAs from tumor cells can modulate macrophage polarization by disrupting the PTEN/AKT pathway. For instance, exosomal miR-934 from colorectal cancer cells enhances M2 polarization by suppressing PTEN expression, similarly to miR-203a-3p (Fig. 4) [133, 134]. Other miRNAs, such as miR-301a-3p from ESCC and miR-21 from bladder cancer, follow this mechanism to foster M2 polarization through PTEN inhibition [135–137]. Notably, exosomal miR-320a from cancer-associated fibroblasts also upregulates the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting M2 polarization [138].

Beyond PTEN, programmed cell death 4 (PDCD4) emerges as a critical negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and facilitates M1 macrophage polarization [139–141]. Additionally, Ying et al. [142] found that exosomal miR-106b from epithelial-mesenchymal transition CRC cells drives M2 polarization by downregulating PDCD4, engaging the PI3Kγ/AKT/mTOR pathway. In addition, glioma-derived exosomal miR-3591-3p targets CBLB, enhancing M2 polarization via the JAK2/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways [142]. The JAK2 pathway activates MAPK and synergizes with PI3K/AKT to support cellular proliferation and survival. Activated JAK2 phosphorylates STAT3, which then regulates genes involved in M2 polarization [143, 144]. Thus, PDCD4 suppresses M2 polarization by inhibiting JAK2-related pathways.

MiR-21 targets RhoB, a member of the Rho GTPase family known for its involvement in cellular functions like cytoskeletal dynamics. Recent studies show RhoB is crucial for driving M1 macrophage polarization by modulating cytokine production [145–148]. In this context, Yu et al. [149]. demonstrated that exosomal miR-21-5p from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) promotes M2-like polarization by activating the ERK signaling pathway via RhoB [149]. Similarly, Hu et al. [150] showed that HCC-derived miR-21-5p enhances TAM M2 polarization by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines [150].

Additionally, Hsu et al. [151] emphasized that Snail, an epithelial-mesenchymal transition transcription factor, not only attracts TAMs but also promotes their M2 polarization. Their follow-up research found that miR-21 in exosomes from Snail-expressing head and neck squamous cell carcinoma downregulates PDCD4 and IL12A, further facilitating M2 macrophage polarization [152]. These findings highlight the role of tumor-derived exosomal miR-21 in modulating macrophage polarization in the TME, suggesting miR-21 as a potential therapeutic target.

MiR-146a influences tumor progression and immune responses by regulating macrophage polarization in various contexts [153–156]. It maintains a balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals, affecting tumor progression [154, 157, 158]. Research indicates that miR-146a-5p can stimulate M1 polarization in macrophages. For instance, exosomes enriched with miR-146a-5p from neonatal cardiomyocytes have been shown to target TRAF6, initiating M1 polarization while exerting anti-inflammatory effects [159]. Conversely, evidence also suggests that miR-146a is predominantly expressed in M2 macrophages, where it reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances M2 markers [160, 161]. Yan et al. [162] illustrated that exosomal miR-146a from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells can inhibit M1 polarization in naïve M0 macrophages by targeting TRAF6 and IRAK-1, key signaling proteins in innate immune responses. This reduction in inflammatory cytokine secretion characterizes M1 macrophages, suggesting that targeting TLR4 or TRAF6 could shift the balance towards M2 polarization [163–166].

The transcription factor STAT3 plays a vital role in M2 polarization through cytokines like IL-10 and IL-6. STAT3 regulates genes critical for M2 functions, including IL-10 and arginase 1 [167, 168]. Notably, miR-146a expression is also influenced by STAT3; the SALL4-STAT3 axis enhances miR-146a-5p expression, which contributes to M2 polarization by repressing M1-associated genes [162]. In summary, exosomal miR-146a significantly impacts macrophage polarization through various mechanisms, informed by the specific TME and signaling pathways. Understanding these dynamics may pave the way for innovative therapeutic approaches in cancer and other diseases.

Cytokines are crucial for macrophage polarization, with pro-inflammatory cytokines like IFN-γ and TNF-α promoting M1 polarization, while IL-10 and IL-6 encourage M2 polarization [169, 170]. Exosomes from cancer cells can modulate these cytokines, influencing macrophage polarization. For instance, exosomes from hypoxic cancer cells enhance M2 polarization by increasing IL-6 levels [171]. while prostate cancer-derived exosomes alter the STAT3 signaling pathway to favor M2 polarization [172]. Exosomal miRNAs also modulate cytokine levels and macrophage polarization. For example, miR-200b-3p from HCC alters the M2 phenotype by upregulating IL-4 and downregulating ZEB1, a transcription factor that promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion [173]. Other studies highlight the ability of various exosomal miRNAs to adjust the M2 to M1 ratio by modulating cytokine levels, which can impact tumor progression.

For instance, miR-130 and miR-33, derived from breast cancer cells, enhance M1 polarization through IL-1β and TNF-α secretion [174]. Exosomal miR-16 from EGCG-treated breast cancer cells inhibits M2 polarization via NF-κB inactivation and contributes to a favorable M1 environment by altering pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [175]. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted roles of exosomal miRNAs in modulating cytokines, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets for the manipulation of immune responses in cancer.

LncRNAs

In various cancer tissues, both tumor cells and TAMs express a diverse repertoire of lncRNAs that play essential regulatory roles in cancer progression. Among these lncRNAs, some are particularly significant in modulating tumor growth by influencing macrophage polarization. Furthermore, lncRNAs linked to macrophages and TAMs are pivotal in regulating the secretion of various mediators, which in turn affects the behavior of adjacent cancer cells and endothelial cells [176].

Recent research has increasingly centered on the role of lncRNAs mediated by exosomes as critical modulators in macrophage polarization, especially concerning the distinction between M1 and M2 phenotypes [177, 178]. For instance, exosomal lncRNA H19 promotes M1 macrophage polarization by enhancing stimulatory factors such as CXCL10 and TNF-α [178]. while lncRNAs like MALAT1, ElNF1-AS1, and TP73-AS steer macrophages toward the M2 phenotype. Exosomal LINC00460 from PANC-1 cells significantly increases M2 markers [179], and FGD5-AS1 from pancreatic cancer activates STAT3/NF-κB pathways to promote M2 polarization [180]. A particularly impactful lncRNA is RPPH1, found upregulated in CRC and associated with poor prognosis [181]. RPPH1 enhances tumor cell migration and invasion through interaction with TUBB3, inhibiting its ubiquitination, which activates the Snail pathway that promotes EMT and metastasis. Notably, RPPH1 can be transferred via exosomes to macrophages, facilitating their polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Its presence in plasma suggests it may serve as both a diagnostic marker and a therapeutic target in CRC [181]. Collectively, these studies underscore the multifaceted roles of exosomal lncRNAs in cancer biology, with a particular focus on their impact on macrophage polarization and tumor progression.

Emerging research indicates that estrogen significantly influences exosome release in various cancers. For example, studies have shown that estrogen signaling in breast cancer cells is linked to the exosome-mediated transfer of metastasis-associated protein 1 (MTA1) [182]. Estrogen can also induce the release of exosomes containing lncRNA H19 from cholangiocytes, thereby affecting tumor biology [183]. Recently, Liang et al. [184] demonstrated that estrogen-induced exosomes promote tumorigenesis in ovarian cancer by mediating M2 polarization through lncRNA SNHG17, which activates the PI3K/Akt pathway. Additionally, the CCL13-CCR2 axis was highlighted as crucial in this mechanism, promoting M2 polarization and enhancing ovarian cancer growth and metastasis [184].

lncRNA TUC339 from HCC has been correlated with M2 macrophage polarization, marked by IL-4 signaling, which impairs macrophage phagocytic capacity and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production [185]. Another lncRNA, RP11-1100 L3.8, identified in HCC as lncMMPA, drives M2 polarization in macrophages [186]. Exosomal ZFPM2-AS1 induces M2 polarization in HCC through modulation of the miR-18b-5p/PKM axis [187], and lncRNA HMMR-AS1 promotes M2 polarization by upregulating ARID3A [188]. lncRNA HEIH enhances M2 polarization by acting as a miRNA sponge for miR-98-5p, upregulating STAT3 [186]. Similar mechanisms are also observed in other cancers, such as renal cell carcinoma via lncARSR and lncRNA AP000439.2, which steer macrophages toward the M2 phenotype by activating the STAT3 pathway [189, 190].

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are crucial in regulating gene expression and have significant roles in macrophage polarization. Class I HDACs and Class IIb HDAC6 promote M1 polarization [191, 192], while inhibiting HDAC1 has been shown to favor M2 polarization [193]. Functional interaction of HDAC1 with domain-associated protein 6 represses IL-6 transcription and its inhibition correlates with increased STAT3 activity [194]. Recent findings show that exosomal lncRNA RNF157 from prostate cancer enhances M2 polarization by degrading HDAC1 [195]. Overall, these findings underscore the complex interplay between lncRNAs, exosome-mediated signaling, and macrophage polarization in within the TME, implicating these pathways as potential therapeutic targets in cancer treatment.

Circular RNAs

CircRNAs represent a unique class of non-coding RNAs generated through the back-splicing of precursor mRNAs, resulting in the covalent connection of the 3′ and 5′ ends [196]. They are more stable than linear mRNAs and primarily function to sequester miRNAs, playing significant roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation [197, 198]. Dysregulated circRNA expression is linked to various pathological conditions, including cardiovascular diseases [199], infection diseases [200], and various cancers [201].

Cancer cells utilize circRNAs to promote tumor progression and evade immune responses, particularly through the secretion of exosomal circRNAs [202]. Certain exosomal circRNAs are aberrantly expressed in tumor tissues, influencing mechanisms such as immune evasion, angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, drug resistance, cellular proliferation, and metastasis [203]. Exosomal circRNAs also modulate macrophage polarization within the TME. For instance, exosomal circVCP from CRC cells promotes M2 macrophage polarization by targeting the miR-9-5p/NRP1 axis (Fig. 5) [204]. In gliomas, circNEIL3 is packaged into exosomes, transferred to TAMs, and stabilizes IGF2BP3 to confer immunosuppressive properties [205]. Similarly, exosomal circ_0001142 from breast cancer reprograms macrophages and facilitates M2 polarization by targeting the miR-361-3p/PIK3CB pathway during endoplasmic reticulum stress [206]. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), exosomal circZNF451 promotes the ubiquitination of FXR1, activating the ELF4-IRF4 signaling pathway to alter macrophage polarization and dysregulate CD8 + T cells [207]. Exosomal circFARSA in NSCLC fosters M2 polarization by degrading PTEN and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [208].

Exosomal circ0048117 from ESCC induces M2 polarization in macrophages [209], while hsa_circ_0074854 suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) migration by interacting with human antigen R (HuR) and inhibiting M2 polarization [210]. Notably, hsa_circ_0017252 from gastric cancer inhibits M2 polarization and reduces gastric cancer invasiveness by sponging miR-17-5p [211]. CircRNAs also drive macrophage recruitment to the TME through various chemokines. For example, circ_0003410 promotes HCC progression by sponging miR-139-3p, leading to increased CCL5 expression, which recruits M2 macrophages [212]. Conversely, circ_0074854 inhibits HCC tumorigenesis by suppressing M2 polarization [210]. CircASAP1 regulates CSF-1, promoting CD68 + TAM infiltration and HCC growth [213]. The interplay between exosomal circRNAs and macrophage polarization can signify potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment.

The balance of STAT1 activation and STAT3/6 pathways critically regulates macrophage polarization, where STAT3/6 activation is often linked to M2 polarization and the suppression of anti-tumor immune responses [214]. CircRNAs influence the STAT3/STAT6 pathways by acting as miRNA sponges. Exosomal circ_001264 from acute myeloid leukemia enhances M2 polarization via the p38-STAT3 pathway [215], while exosomal circSAFB2 promotes M2 polarization in renal cell carcinoma through the miR-620/JAK1/STAT3 axis [216]. Additionally, circPOLQ in CRC facilitates M2 macrophage polarization via the IL-10/STAT3 pathway by sponging miR-379-3p [217]. Recent findings by Deng et al. [218] identified circATP8A1, significantly upregulated in gastric cancer, correlating with poor prognosis. CircATP8A1 induces M2 polarization through the STAT6 pathway by sponging miR-1-3p, enhancing gastric cancer cell migration [218]. Transcription factors (TFs), such as TFEB, also regulate macrophage polarization; downregulation of TFEB promotes M2 polarization and enhances tumor growth [219]. Exosomal circRHCG from triple-negative breast cancer facilitates M2 polarization by degrading TFEB [220]. TLR4 activation is essential for macrophage differentiation and M2 polarization, particularly in TAMs within lung cancer [221]. TLR4 activation across cancers often enhances M2 polarization, promoting tumor growth and invasion [222]. In ESCC, hypoxic conditions increase the secretion of exosomal hsa-circ-0048117, which promotes M2 polarization by upregulating TLR4 through miR-140 competitive binding [223]. M2 macrophages subsequently enhance tumor invasiveness through secretion of factors like arginase 1, IL-10, and TGF-β [223].

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of neutrophils

Neutrophils function as the body’s primary defense mechanism against infections and respond broadly to various inflammatory signals, including those linked to cancer. These immune cells are distinguished by their remarkable plasticity, enabling them to adjust their roles according to different inflammatory scenarios [224]. In the context of the TME, neutrophils can assume dual roles, acting either as protectors of the tumor or promoting its progression [225].

Various mediators, such as cytokines, chemokines, lipids, and growth factors produced by both cancer and stromal cells, draw neutrophils into the TME, where they subsequently influence tumor growth and metastasis. While extensive research has examined the anti-cancer attributes of neutrophils, emerging studies suggest that these cells can undergo significant phenotypic and functional transformations within the TME that may favor tumor growth [225]. Neutrophils exhibit a distinct polarization, differentiated into anti-tumor (N1) and pro-tumor (N2) phenotypes, each substantially affecting cancer progression [224]. Particularly, N2-polarized neutrophils engage in pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive activities, complicating the immune response to tumors [226]. Despite the critical implications of neutrophil polarization in human cancers, this aspect remains inadequately explored. This section will delve into how exosomes derived from human cancer cells impact neutrophil polarization within the TME. For instance, Zhang and colleagues [227] examined the effects of exosomes from gastric cancer cells on neutrophils, revealing that these exosomes not only prolonged neutrophil survival but also stimulated inflammatory factor expression within these cells. As a result, the neutrophils activated by exosomes were found to enhance the migration of gastric cancer cells [227].

This raises questions about the mechanisms through which exosomes activate neutrophils and contribute to cancer progression. The study indicated that exosomes from gastric cancer cells carry high mobility group box-1 (HMGB1), a key molecule that initiates the NF-κB signaling pathway through interaction with TLR4. This interaction leads to an increased autophagic response in neutrophils [227]. Recent findings have established a connection between heightened autophagy and the polarization of neutrophils towards a pro-tumoral phenotype [228]. Importantly, inhibiting the HMGB1/TLR4 interaction, along with blocking the NF-κB pathway and autophagy, reversed the activation of neutrophils induced by exosomes. Furthermore, silencing HMGB1, which is notably upregulated in gastric cancer tissues, corroborated its role as a key factor in exosome-mediated neutrophil activation [227]. Mechanistically, exosomal HMGB1 from gastric cancer cells drives neutrophil polarization towards the N2 phenotype by positively modulating the NF-κB pathway and autophagy through TLR4 interaction.

In another study, the interactions between exosomes derived from CRC cells and neutrophils, including those from CRC cells with circ-CTNNB1 silenced were evaluated. The results revealed that exosomes from CRC cells inhibited neutrophil apoptosis while promoting N2 polarization; conversely, exosomes from circ-CTNNB1-silenced CRC cells showed minimal impact on neutrophil function [229]. Notably, circ-CTNNB1 was found to upregulate IKBKB, a crucial component of the NF-κB signaling pathway, by repressing miR-3619-5p. Furthermore, activation of the NF-κB pathway by circ-CTNNB1 increased PD-L1 expression, further driving N2 polarization and aiding in both CRC cell proliferation and immune evasion [229]. Thus, exosomal circ-CTNNB1 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, promoting the polarization of neutrophils towards the N2 phenotype, potentially enhanced by the presence of PD-1, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive environment conducive to cancer progression.

In conclusion, the interaction between neutrophils and the TME reveals a complex duality in their roles, as they can act as both immune defenders and promoters of tumor progression. The polarization of neutrophils into N1 and N2 phenotypes, driven by tumor-derived exosomal factors like HMGB1 and circ-CTNNB1, underscores the mechanisms by which cancer cells modulate immune responses. This shift towards a pro-tumoral N2 phenotype enhances migratory and immunosuppressive capabilities, facilitating cancer growth and metastasis. Consequently, elucidating the factors influencing neutrophil behavior in the TME is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies to reprogram these cells and improve cancer outcomes.

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of MDSCs

MDSCs are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells that accumulate in the TME and exert potent immunosuppressive effects, contributing to tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. These cells primarily suppress anti-tumor immunity by inhibiting T cell activation, promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs), and depleting key amino acids such as arginine and cysteine via the enzymes arginase-1 (ARG1) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). In addition to their role in immune suppression, MDSCs contribute to tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance [230–236].

Emerging evidence highlights that tumor-derived exosomes (TEXs) and cancer cell-derived exosomes (Ca-Exo) play a critical role in modulating the function and expansion of MDSCs. These exosomes transfer bioactive molecules such as miRNAs and proteins, which activate pathways like STAT3 and PI3K/AKT, thereby enhancing MDSC-mediated immune suppression [234, 235, 237–240]. For example, TEXs from various cancers, including breast cancer and glioma, transfer cargoes like miR-29a/31 and S100A8/A9, which increase MDSC suppressive activity, elevating the expression of ARG1 and iNOS and inhibiting T cell function [234, 235, 237–240]. In prostate cancer, Ca-Exo promote MDSC differentiation from monocytes via TGF-β and PGE2 signaling, contributing to an immunosuppressive niche [234, 235, 239, 240]. Additionally, MDSC-derived exosomes reciprocally support tumor growth by transferring miRNAs, such as miR-126a, which promote angiogenesis and metastasis, or by inducing M2 macrophage polarization via miR-93-5p/STAT3 downregulation [235, 237, 238].

These interactions underscore the complex role of Ca-Exo in MDSC-mediated immune evasion and tumor progression. Despite significant progress, the precise mechanisms and exosomal cargo involved in MDSC reprogramming remain underexplored, representing a crucial area for future research. Given these insights, therapeutic strategies targeting the modulation of exosomal cargo or inhibiting exosomal uptake could hold promise for restoring anti-tumor immunity and improving cancer treatment outcomes [234, 235, 237–240].

The literature supports the significant role of MDSCs and tumor-derived exosomes in immune suppression and tumor progression, with multiple studies identifying key molecular pathways and potential therapeutic opportunities. However, further research is needed to fully elucidate the exosomal cargo and mechanisms involved in MDSC reprogramming and to translate these findings into clinical interventions.

The role of Ca-Exo in reprogramming of adaptive immune cells

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of T cells

T-cell lymphocytes are integral to the anti-tumor immune response [241]. The activation of T cells is facilitated through their interaction with antigenic ligands presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules. Specifically, CD4 + T cells primarily identify exogenous antigens through MHC II, while CD8 + T cells interact with endogenous antigens displayed by MHC I [242, 243]. Once activated, CD8 + T cells eliminate tumor cells via the secretion of cytotoxic molecules such as perforin and granzymes. Moreover, they can induce apoptosis in target cells through interactions with the Fas ligand and its receptor [244–246].

CD4 + T cell activation occurs in response to soluble antigens released by tumor cells, which are presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). These activated CD4 + T cells play a key role in amplifying the immune response by stimulating B cells and further enhancing CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity [202, 247, 248]. However, cancer cells have developed elaborate strategies to evade immune surveillance. Techniques include downregulating or eliminating tumor-associated antigen expression and creating an immunosuppressive TME that inhibits immune recognition. Additionally, cancer cells can produce immune checkpoint inhibitors that disrupt T cell functions and recruit immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [249]. A critical aspect of immune evasion in cancer involves the suppression of effector functions of infiltrating T cells due to various immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME that undermine the activity of cytotoxic T cells [250]. Tumor-derived exosomes significantly influence T-cell reprogramming in this environment. For instance, exosomes from melanoma cells can modify the transcriptome of cytotoxic T cells, impairing their mitochondrial respiration and inhibiting proliferation in response to IL-2 (Fig. 6) [251]. Exosomes derived from irradiated glioblastoma cells promote the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs) via B7-H4, leading to reduced Th1 polarization (lower T-bet) through the STAT1 signaling pathway and ultimately facilitating tumor growth [252]. Furthermore, exosomal circUSP7 from NSCLC inhibits the functionality of CD8 + T cells by promoting SHP2 expression, thereby downregulating key cytotoxic cytokines [253]. The interplay between exosomes and T cell activity is intricate; while some exosomal components can enhance immune suppression, others hold the potential to activate anti-tumor responses. For example, exosomes enriched with miR-16-5p from M1 macrophages can effectively target PD-L1 in gastric cancer cells, driving a more robust T cell response [254]. Conversely, in HCC, exosomes expressing circCCAR1 can aggravate CD8 + T cell dysfunction by stabilizing PD-1 protein levels, leading to T cell exhaustion [254]. In addition to conventional T cells, γδ T cells represent a unique subset that does not require MHC molecules for antigen presentation. They are categorized into Vδ1 and Vδ2 subsets, with Vγ9Vδ2 T cells predominating in peripheral blood and contributing to tumor surveillance and antimicrobial defense [255, 256]. Tumor-derived exosomes notably modulate the activity of Vγ9Vδ2 T cells within the TME. For instance, exosomes from gastric cancer cells, upon internalization, can diminish cell viability and reduce production of critical cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α [257]. Conversely, exosomal thrombospondin-1 from gastric cancer enhances the cytotoxic functions of Vγ9Vδ2 T cells, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production and exerting potent anti-tumor effects [258]. Furthermore, Tregs are essential for maintaining immune homeostasis and fostering immune tolerance within the TME. They can inhibit the activation and differentiation of effector T cells required for targeting self and tumor-associated antigens [259]. Tumor-derived exosomes contribute to the Treg phenotype, suppressing anti-tumor immunity. For instance, exosomes from nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells can redirect T lymphocytes towards a Treg phenotype while impeding their differentiation into Th1 and Th17 cells [260]. In contrast, exosomal miR-451 from gastric cancer has been shown to promote differentiation into Th17 cells, indicating the complex and sometimes contradictive roles that exosomes can play in immune regulation [261].

The balance between Tregs and Th17 cells is emerging as a critical determinant in cancer dynamics. Variations in the Treg/Th17 ratio can influence tumor progression and are reliant on cancer type and TME characteristics [262–267]. Recent studies demonstrated that elevated Treg/Th17 ratios in epithelial ovarian cancer tissues correlate with adverse prognostic outcomes, emphasizing their potential as targets for therapeutic intervention. Exosomes from TAMs enriched with specific miRNAs can skew this ratio towards immunosuppression, enhancing Treg populations at the expense of Th17 cells [267]. the intricate interactions among T cells, exosomes, and the TME underscore the potential for developing innovative cancer immunotherapy strategies. By manipulating exosomal content and targeting specific signaling pathways, it may be possible to restore effective anti-tumor immunity and improve patient outcomes in the fight against cancer. Understanding these mechanisms holds promise for advancing therapeutic approaches that could re-establish immune equilibrium and enhance the efficacy of cancer treatments.

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of natural killer cells

NK cells are vital cytotoxic lymphocytes in the innate immune system, constituting approximately 15% of circulating lymphocytes. Unlike adaptive immune cells, NK cells can rapidly eliminate multiple adjacent cells that display surface markers indicative of oncogenic transformation without prior antigen sensitization [268]. However, recent studies indicate that exosomes derived from cancer cells significantly modify NK cell dynamics within the TME, primarily by promoting immunosuppression and undermining anti-tumor responses. Understanding these interactions is crucial for developing novel cancer immunotherapies.

Numerous studies demonstrate that tumor-derived exosomes can impair NK cell cytotoxicity through various mechanisms. For instance, exosomes from acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells have been shown to reduce NK cell effectiveness [267]. In addition, exosomal miR-552-5p released from gastric cancer cells has been implicated in the upregulation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, a process that subsequently diminishes NK cell activation, reduces cytokine production, and curtails cytotoxic capabilities [269].

Research by Labani-Motlagh et al. [270] focused on the effects of exosomes from epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) cells on NK cell functionality. Their findings revealed that EOC exosomes containing NKG2D ligands notably downregulated NKG2D receptor expression on peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which inhibited NKG2D-mediated degranulation and cytotoxicity. This highlights how exosomal content can contribute to immune evasion by compromising NK cell activation and facilitating tumor progression [270].

In another investigation, Xia et al. [271] showed that primary tumor cells induce NK cell dysfunction through an exosome-mediated mechanism enriched with TGF-β1, a key factor in NK cell impairment. In vitro studies indicated that exposure to these tumor exosomes activates the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway, leading to NK cell dysfunction. Importantly, this impairment could be reversed through TGF-β knockdown, underscoring the factor’s critical role in mediating the effects of tumor-derived exosomes on NK cells [271]. Moreover, exosomes from HCC cells have been shown to reprogram NK cells by transferring miR-17-5p, which targets RUNX1—a transcription factor essential for NKG2D expression. Suppression of RUNX1 by miR-17-5p reduces NKG2D expression and impairs NK cells’ ability to recognize and eliminate HCC cells. This mechanism exemplifies how tumor-derived exosomes facilitate immune evasion by modulating NK cell activity, ultimately contributing to tumor advancement and an increasingly immunosuppressive TME [272]. These findings accentuate the intricate interplay between tumor-derived exosomes and NK cell functionality, underscoring the potential for developing therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting these interactions. By addressing such crosstalk, it may be feasible to enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies and yield improved clinical outcomes for patients.

The role of Ca-Exo in reprogramming of vascular cells

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of blood vascular endothelial cells

Endothelial cells (ECs) constitute a vital monolayer that lines blood vessels, playing an indispensable role in sustaining vascular integrity and function. However, endothelial cells associated with tumors display considerable heterogeneity and plasticity, significantly impacting the transport of proteins, immune cells, oxygen, and fluids to surrounding tissues. In contrast to normal ECs, tumor-associated endothelial cells exhibit distinct characteristics, such as diminished adhesion molecule expression, leading to compromised barrier function, and increased levels of immunoinhibitory checkpoint molecules, which together foster the immunosuppressive landscape characteristic of tumors [4].

Recent studies have demonstrated that exosomes released by tumor cells profoundly alter the TME by modifying the phenotype of the tumor-associated endothelium compared to normal blood vessels [273]. These exosomes serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication between malignant cells and ECs, particularly under hypoxic conditions. For instance, Kucharzewska et al. [274] found that exosomes from glioblastoma (GBM) cells in a hypoxic environment induce significant reprogramming of endothelial cells, enhancing their functional capabilities. These hypoxic exosomes promote the secretion of growth factors and cytokines critical for tumor development, aiding the recruitment and activation of pericytes necessary for stabilizing blood vessels while triggering the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is well-known for its roles in cell survival, proliferation, and migration. This dynamic facilitates pericyte migration toward the tumor site, bolstering vascular support for tumor growth. Moreover, hypoxic GBM-derived exosomes also elicit an autocrine, promigratory response within GBM cells, enhancing their migratory and invasive potential. This interplay creates a feedback loop, conditioning surrounding endothelial cells to accommodate the tumor’s metabolic demands while amplifying tumor aggressiveness [274]. Understanding the role of exosomes in modifying EC behavior and interactions with tumors could provide crucial insights for developing therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting this nefarious loop and restoring normal vascular function.

Research by Guo et al. identified exosomal miR-204-3p as a significant regulator of angiogenesis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), particularly through its interaction with the ATXN1/STAT3 signaling pathway. Under hypoxic conditions, GBM cells upregulate the SUMOylation of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (hnRNP A2/B1), resulting in the degradation of miR-204-3p, a key tumor-suppressive microRNA. The consequent reduction in miR-204-3p disrupts the regulatory balance within endothelial cells, facilitating the activation of angiogenic pathways. This downregulation heightens the activity of the ATXN1/STAT3 pathway, promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and new blood vessel formation [275] (Fig. 7). This mechanism illustrates how GBM cells leverage hypoxic conditions to create a supportive microenvironment for their growth through enhanced blood supply facilitated by exosomal signaling. Such interactions underscore the complex relationship between tumor cells and the vascular microenvironment, highlighting tumors’ capacity to manipulate endothelial responses to sustain their progression.

Similarly, exosomes from ESCC have been implicated in promoting endothelial cell migration, invasion, and proliferation [276]. This indicates a broader phenomenon where various tumor types utilize exosomal signaling to modulate angiogenic processes, facilitating their growth and metastasis. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for devising targeted therapies capable of disrupting detrimental tumor-endothelial interactions and enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

Exosomal microRNAs, such as miR-455 from hypoxic nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells [277], miR-23a, originating from lung cancer [278], miR-105, derived from metastatic breast cancer cells [279], have been shown to promote increased permeability of endothelial cells by negatively regulating tight junction protein ZO-1. This downregulation compromises the integrity of endothelial barriers, potentially resulting in heightened vascular permeability and exacerbating metastatic and inflammatory processes [277, 278].

Wang et al. [280] further demonstrated that exosomal miR-181a from hypoxic papillary thyroid cancer cells is crucial in stimulating proliferation, angiogenesis, and migration in HUVECs. MiR-181a targets the MLL3 gene, leading to the upregulation of VEGF while downregulating DACT2, a known suppressor of the Wnt signaling pathway. This mechanism enhances VEGF levels and promotes aggressive cancer behaviors by inhibiting factors that would typically restrict tumor advancement. Additionally, exosomes from NSCLC contain miR-3157-3p, which enhances angiogenesis and vascular permeability by upregulating occludin, VEGF, and MMP2/9 while targeting TIMP2 and KLF2 in endothelial cells [281]. Similarly, exosomal miR-25-3p from CRC targets KLF2 and KLF4, further promoting angiogenesis and vascular permeability [282]. Collectively, these molecular alterations augment endothelial cell functionality, fostering the formation of new blood vessels and enhancing the permeability of existing vasculature, key processes that facilitate tumor growth and metastasis. Understanding these mechanisms offers valuable insights for potential targeted therapeutic interventions capable of disrupting the pathological interactions between tumor-derived exosomes and endothelial cells.

Ca-Exo in reprogramming of lymphatic endothelial cells

Lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) are fundamental in establishing the structural framework of lymphatic vessels, which are essential components of the TME. Within this context, lymphatic vessels serve as conduits for the dissemination of malignant cells while also collaborating with blood vessels to facilitate tumor advancement. Recent research has emphasized the proactive involvement of LECs in shaping anti-tumor immune responses and affecting the efficacy of immunotherapeutic strategies [283, 284]. Notably, LECs have the ability to present tumor-associated antigens and express immune checkpoint molecules, significantly influencing immune recognition and response dynamics within the TME [285, 286]. Cancer-associated LECs, alongside various other cell types, contribute to the establishment of a suppressive TME that allows tumors to evade the host immune system [287]. Gaining deeper insights into the immunosuppressive mechanisms utilized by cancer-associated LECs may enable the development of more effective immunotherapeutic approaches.

Despite their importance, the mechanisms by which LECs modulate anti-tumor immunity remain poorly defined. Zhou et al. [288] explored the impact of cancer-derived exosomes on the immunosuppressive reprogramming of LECs. Their research identified a positive correlation between elevated levels of miR-142-5p and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) expression in tumor-associated lymphatic vessels in advanced CSCC. The study demonstrated that exosomal miR-142-5p from CSCC cells is taken up by LECs, leading to CD8 + T cell exhaustion through an increase in lymphatic IDO expression—an effect that could be reversed using an IDO inhibitor. Mechanistically, miR-142-5p downregulates the AT-rich interactive domain-containing protein 2 (ARID2), which inhibits the recruitment of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) to the IFN-γ promoter, enhancing IFN-γ transcription and IDO activity. Notably, higher serum levels of exosomal miR-142-5p alongside elevated IDO activity correlate with CSCC progression [288].

In a related examination, the same research group investigated the role of exosomal miR-1468-5p in promoting immune evasion mechanisms in cervical cancer [289]. They found that cancer cells release exosomal miR-1468-5p, which reprograms LECs to enhance PD-L1 expression and support lymphangiogenesis, thereby impeding CD8 + T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses. The molecular mechanism involves miR-1468-5p downregulating HMBOX1-SOCS1 expression, activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in LECs, which facilitates immune evasion by elevating PD-L1 expression. Importantly, the study suggests that overexpressing HMBOX1 could counteract the effects of miR-1468-5p, making it a potential therapeutic target for restoring immune functionality. Moreover, the correlation between elevated miR-1468-5p levels in serum exosomes and clinical markers of immune suppression underscores its potential as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target in cervical cancer management [289].

Conclusion

The TME represents a complex network that profoundly influences tumor progression and therapeutic response, with exosomes emerging as crucial mediators of intercellular communication. These extracellular vesicles facilitate the exchange of bioactive molecules, thereby fostering both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing signals. The interplay of cellular reprogramming and polarization within the TME underscores the intricate dynamics between malignant cells and their surrounding non-malignant counterparts [290, 291].

Across various TME cell types, common mechanisms of exosomal-mediated reprogramming emerge, underscoring a core regulatory network. Non-coding RNAs, including miRNAs (e.g., miR-9, miR-146a, and miR-21 targeting PTEN/AKT or JAK/STAT pathways), lncRNAs (e.g., LINC00691 activating JAK2/STAT3), and circRNAs (e.g., circVCP and circNEIL3 promoting M2 polarization via miRNA sponging), frequently drive pro-tumor shifts in fibroblasts (to CAFs), macrophages (to M2-like TAMs), MDSCs (enhancing immunosuppression), neutrophils (to N2 phenotype), and endothelial cells (enhancing lymphangiogenesis). Metabolic reprogramming is another shared theme, exemplified by exosomal modulation of ROS levels and HIF-1α in fibroblasts, glycolytic enzymes like ENO2 in macrophages, and hypoxia-induced exosomal release across the TME, which collectively foster immunosuppression and tumor progression. Protein cargoes, such as Wnt2B (activating β-catenin in fibroblasts) and Tim-3 (promoting M2 polarization in macrophages), often converge on overlapping signaling hubs like PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and TGF-β pathways (Fig. 3, 4, 5). This interconnected network highlights exosomes as central orchestrators, suggesting that targeting these hubs—e.g., via inhibitors of Rab27a for exosome biogenesis or pathway-specific drugs—could yield broad therapeutic benefits.

Recent research highlights the role of hypoxia in enhancing exosomal production, indicating that hypoxic carcinoma cells release significantly more exosomes than their normoxic counterparts [292]. Intriguingly, more than half of the secreted proteome from these hypoxic cells is associated with exosomes, establishing compelling evidence of their pivotal role in mediating cellular responses to hypoxic stress [293]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies should focus not solely on individual exosomal components but rather on the fundamental mechanisms of exosome-mediated intercellular communication, such as Rab27a regulation of exosome secretion and receptor mechanisms of exosome uptake (encompassing exosome formation, stability, and uptake by recipient cells).

Despite these advances, significant research gaps remain in understanding exosomal regulation across the TME, particularly in underrepresented areas like neutrophil polarization. For instance, while Ca-Exo induces N2 neutrophil polarization via HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB-mediated autophagy (as discussed in Sect. "Ca-Exo in reprogramming of Neutrophils "), the metabolic mechanisms—such as how exosomal cargoes modulate glycolytic shifts in neutrophils to sustain pro-tumor functions—are limited and warrant further investigation, especially given the Warburg effect’s role in TME immunosuppression. Similarly, gaps exist in elucidating receptor-specific uptake mechanisms (e.g., endocytosis via integrins or tetraspanins) that enable selective exosomal delivery to recipient cells, which could explain variability in reprogramming efficiency across cancers. Future directions should prioritize these gaps: for example, exploring the reversal effect of glycolysis inhibitors (e.g., 2-Deoxy-D-glucose) on neutrophil N2 polarization induced by Ca-Exo. Additionally, investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting Rab27a and other regulators of exosome secretion to disrupt tumor-promoting exosomal communication. Clarify the receptor mechanisms of exosome uptake in different TME cell types to identify new intervention points. Expand in vivo studies to validate the efficacy and safety of exosome-targeted therapies, especially in the context of immune cell reprogramming.

Additionally, exosomes derived from cancer cells have been implicated in modulating the polarization of immune cells, particularly neutrophils, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive environment that enables tumors to evade immune surveillance. This capacity to manipulate the immune landscape reinforces the importance of exosomal signaling in establishing a permissive niche for tumor growth [5].

As we advance our understanding of these intricate relationships, it becomes increasingly clear that a comprehensive approach incorporating exosomal dynamics within the TME is essential for enhancing cancer therapy. By elucidating the mechanisms underlying cellular reprogramming, polarization, and exosomal communication, researchers can identify novel strategies aimed at disrupting the supportive frameworks that tumors exploit, ultimately improving therapeutic efficacy.

Targeting specific components of exosome biology is a promising preclinical avenue with emerging translational strategies. The small GTPase Rab27a, a critical regulator of exosome formation, has emerged as a viable target for intervention [294]. Preclinical studies suggest that targeting exosome biogenesis and secretion, such as through Rab27a inhibition, can suppress tumor progression and enhance therapeutic responses in various cancer models [295–302]. However, clinical translation of these strategies is still in its early stages, and further research is required to establish their safety and efficacy in patients.

In conclusion, the complex crosstalk between exosomal signaling and TME dynamics calls for a paradigm shift in cancer treatment methods. Understanding the core mechanisms of exosome biology and their regulation of tumor–immune interactions—including the gaps and directions outlined above—can drive the development of next-generation therapeutic strategies. A multidisciplinary framework that integrates molecular biology, immunology, and clinical innovation is essential to advance effective treatments and improve patient outcomes. Across diverse TME cell types, cancer-derived exosomes mediate reprogramming through conserved mechanisms—such as transfer of non-coding RNAs, metabolic modulation, and activation of key signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/AKT, STAT3, Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β). These interconnected regulatory networks collectively sustain tumor progression, immune evasion, and stromal adaptation. Elucidating these shared pathways provides a unified understanding of exosome-driven TME remodeling and identifies promising universal therapeutic targets.
