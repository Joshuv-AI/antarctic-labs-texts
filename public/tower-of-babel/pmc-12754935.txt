Comment on: “Identification of macrophage polarisation and mitochondrial-related biomarkers in diabetic retinopathy”
Authors: Jiaoyu Deng, Dianbo Xu
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the recent article by Liu et al. titled “Identification of macrophage polarisation and mitochondria-related biomarkers in diabetic retinopathy” [1]. The authors present a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis identifying PTAR1 and SLC25A34 as potential biomarkers for diabetic retinopathy (DR). We commend the authors for their systematic approach, which represents a valuable computational contribution to DR research.

While the study is commendable, we wish to highlight several methodological aspects that, if clarified, could further strengthen the interpretation and impact of the findings.

Tissue source and composition of validation dataset require clarification

The study employs GSE160306 as a key validation set, describing it in the Methods as “blood from 20 DR samples and 20 control samples”. However, the official Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) entry for GSE160306 (available at: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE160306) and its original publication [2] unequivocally state that this dataset comprises post-mortem human retinal tissues (both macula and periphery), not peripheral blood. Furthermore, the dataset’s composition is more complex than reported; it includes samples from 20 non-diabetic controls, 20 donors with diabetes but no retinopathy, and 39 donors with DR (both non-proliferative DR and proliferative DR).

This discrepancy in tissue source is critical. The study’s premise of validating blood-based biomarkers in an independent blood cohort is not supported, as the validation was actually performed in retinal tissue. The oversimplification of the cohort’s composition also raises questions about the precise case-control definitions used in the re-analysis. We encourage the authors to clarify these points, as accurate characterization of public data sources is fundamental for reproducibility and correct scientific inference. This does not invalidate the findings but significantly alters their interpretation: the results support the role of PTAR1 and SLC25A34 in the retinal pathology of DR, rather than confirming their utility as peripheral biomarkers.

Opportunity to enhance mechanistic hypotheses

Should the validation in retinal tissue be confirmed, we believe the study could be strengthened by a more detailed exploration of the mechanistic links between the biomarkers and macrophage polarization. For instance, while the discussion notes that PTAR1 facilitates FBXL2 prenylation and PI3K-AKT signaling—a pathway relevant to cell survival—it could be further elaborated how this process specifically influences macrophage or microglial phenotype switching in the diabetic retinal environment. Similarly, for SLC25A34, hypothesizing about the specific metabolites it transports and how these might influence immunometabolic pathways central to macrophage polarization would greatly enrich the biological narrative.

We believe that developing these mechanistic hypotheses would not only strengthen the current work but also provide a valuable framework to guide future experimental validation in this field.

Concluding remarks

In summary, the study by Liu et al. provides valuable candidate genes for understanding DR. Clarifying the tissue source and composition of the validation dataset and enriching the discussion of potential mechanisms would, in our view, solidify the foundation of this work and enhance its translational relevance. We thank the authors for their stimulating contribution.
