INTRODUCTION

Organ donation represents a profound act of compassion and solidarity, offering hope to those grappling with end stage organ failure.[1] By facilitating life-saving transplants, donors and their families become catalysts for transformative change, forging connections that transcend boundaries of caste, creed, and community. In a nation grappling with healthcare challenges,[2] organ donation is a beacon of hope, uniting individuals in a shared quest for health and wellbeing. The decision to donate organs is often intertwined with cultural, religious, and social dimensions.[3]

Beneath the surface of the noble gesture of organ donation lies complex social dynamics that shape the impact of organ donation decisions. The availability and accessibility of organ donation services reflect broader societal disparities, highlighting the urgent need for equitable healthcare access.[4]

Understanding and navigating the social consequences of these decisions can foster a culture of empathy, compassion, and solidarity, where every act of donation becomes a testament to the inherent dignity and interconnectedness of humanity.[5]

Moreover, there exists a pervasive lack of awareness and understanding regarding brain death and organ donation throughout the Indian populace, perpetuating widespread myths and misconceptions.[678] Consequently, decision-makers face numerous challenges during times of bereavement, necessitating extensive efforts in persuasion and clarification upon the declaration of death.[910]

While many families ultimately choose to proceed with organ donation, a significant portion remains hesitant or outrightly refuse. Even among those who consent, feelings of marginalization and a lack of societal support post donation contribute to a sense of isolation. However, on a personal level, these families often find solace and pride in their decision, knowing they have contributed to saving lives.[11] The ripples of the decision to donate organs are felt for a long time after the decision has been undertaken. To comprehend this phenomenon, a qualitative investigation into the consequences of organ donation decision was undertaken.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Settings and design

A qualitative descriptive study design was chosen to generate rich, informative data to evaluate the after effects of organ donation decision at a tertiary care hsoptial in North India. The decisions leading to successful organ donation between July 2019 and June 2023 were considered. All the 108 families who had consented were approached for participation in the study. Out of these 108 families, 12 people did not pick up the call, the phone numbers of six people were unreachable, five people picked the call and fixed the appointment but did not pick up the call at the appointed time, two people did not give their consent for participation, and two persons got emotionally overwhelmed during the interview and could not be included. Thus, a purposive sample of 81 decision-makers participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 75% due to their potential to provide relevant data.

Data collection

Data collection involved telephonic interviews with family members, scheduled according to their availability. In cases where multiple next-of-kin were involved in the decision-making, priority was given to the consent giver or the individual most actively engaged in the decision-making process. One interview had to be repeated. The interview was not recorded, but additional notes were taken alongside the interview. Each interview lasted an average of 21 min and was conducted by a trained female transplant coordinator (PG) with a clinical background, under the supervision of a trained qualitative researcher (VT). The researcher (PG) had previously interacted with the participants in the hospital during the process of organ donation. Participants were explained the purpose of the interview, and verbal consent was obtained before proceeding.

Study tools

A semistructured questionnaire about the experience of decision-makers after the organ donation process was used. The questionnaire was pilot-tested with seven donor families to ensure clarity and relevance. Content validity was established by experts including hospital administrators and transplant coordinators.

An outline for interview was drawn by the research team inititially and revised after conducting preinterviews with seven organ donor families, leading to final interview outline. The interviews were conducted in Hindi and/or English, and the data collected were transcribed verbatim from Hindi and translated to English before being analyzed by a thematic-content approach. The researchers administered the questionnaire during their workplace hours, while participants could have been at home or any other location, potentially in the presence of their family members.

The questionnaire comprised the following inquiries: First, participants were asked whether they had shared the decision to donate with their family or society after donation. If participants responded negatively to this question, they were further asked to elucidate the reasons behind their decision not to share. Additionally, participants were questioned about their views, after donation, on whether donor families should receive any incentives or help, and if so, what specific types of incentives or assistance they deemed appropriate. Participants were also asked to provide suggestions on measures that could be taken to increase organ donation rates. Finally, participants were requested to reflect on their feelings regarding their decision to donate.

The research received approval from the Ethics Committee of the institute.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were selected based on specific inclusion criteria to ensure the relevance. The participants had to be the next of kin of the donor, actively involved in the decision-making process regarding organ donation. Additionally, they had to be over 18 years of age and capable of communicating effectively in either English or Hindi. To ensure adequate emotional distance and reflection, a minimum gap of 3 months between the organ donation and the interview was required.

The questionnaire collected demographic details, including age, sex, religion, occupation, education, and location of the participants.

Thematic analysis

Responses were entered into Microsoft Excel (Manufactured by Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States of America (USA)) and analyzed by two researchers, PG and VT. Each response was read multiple times to promote familiarity, and after coding, themes and subthemes were identified. Data were coded and synthesized by one researcher and reviewed by another qualitative data investigator for consistency in identification and classification of themes.

These themes were reviewed by a team consisting of three researchers. Any disagreements were discussed, and consensus was reached. Minor themes were merged with major themes for ease of data analysis. It was decided that the participants’ feedback will be obtained during the next donor meet.

Reporting

The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines were used in this study for the qualitative research.[12] This study was part of a larger study, and results of quantitative component may be published elsewhere.

The decision was shared with others

For those families who openly shared their decision to donate organs, a diverse range of social feedback was encountered, comprising a mix of positive and negative reactions often influenced by socioeconomic levels, geography, cultural myths, and supernatural beliefs. Positive reactions highlighted recognition and inspiration, showing how the decision can inspire others and promote broader understanding and acceptance of organ donation.

However, families also faced significant criticism regarding the sanctity of the body, with some adhering to the empty body myths. Accusations and blame were common, leading to ostracization, social isolation, and threats of legal action. The community exerted pressure through criticism, elders’ disapproval, economic prejudices, and financial misconceptions. A prevalent lack of awareness and education compounded these challenges as uninformed opinions fuelled emotional distress and psychological trauma. Moral and ethical dilemmas also arose, creating conflicts between personal values and community morality. Moreover, these families often endured police harassment and faced challenges with insurance companies, highlighting systemic and bureaucratic hurdles.

Keeping the Decision Private (reasons for concealing the decision)

By keeping the decision private, families aimed to protect themselves from additional emotional distress and maintain a sense of peace during the challenging period of making and implementing the decision to donate organs.

These families aimed to protect themselves from negative community perceptions, fear of critical comments, and accusations of financial motives, particularly influenced by regional and cultural beliefs in backward areas. Gender-specific challenges and community pressure further complicated their decision. The conservative societal norms and high levels of educational deficits contributed to a lack of understanding and nonacceptance of organ donation. Amid profound sadness and emotional distress, families preferred anonymity and limited disclosure to close family members to avoid social backlash and criticism.

Thoughts on incentives

Most participants (91%) believed that incentives for organ donor families should not be monetary. Instead, they emphasize altruistic decision-making and genuine support, particularly for those from poor backgrounds. Many suggest nonfinancial assistance, such as educational and nutritional support, job quotas, and healthcare benefits. Recognition and appreciation from the government and society are also crucial. Participants highlight the need for a compassionate system, advocating for psychological help for grieving families and sensitivity from law enforcement. They stress that while organ donation should be motivated by altruism, robust support systems are essential to help families, especially those facing financial hardship.

To increase organ donation, providing comprehensive support for donor families is essential. This includes emotional support, public recognition, healthcare benefits, and financial assistance, ensuring families feel valued and secure. Implementing policy options like an opt-out system and mandatory registration during license renewals can further boost donation rates. These measures collectively foster a supportive, informed, and motivated community, encouraging more people to consider organ donation.

DISCUSSION

The current evidence on experiences post organ donation is limited in Indian scenario. To the best of researchers’ knowledge, this study is one of the first to specifically probe the views and experiences of decision-makers after donation. The thematic analysis of experiences of key decision-makers revealed a complex interplay of cultural, economic, and social factors influencing emotions related to the perceived correctness of the decision.

For those who had shared their decision to donate organs with others, social feedback was a mix of positive and negative reactions. Positive reactions, including appreciation and inspiration, were significant. Such responses often served as a source of solace and pride, reinforcing the positive impact of their decision. Approval and appreciation of the decision to donate highlighted its importance and worthiness and instilled a sense of pride and honor.[13] The negative feedback was influenced by cultural myths and supernatural beliefs, accusations and blame, elders’ disapproval, economic prejudices, social criticism for sanctity of the body, illiteracy and lack of awareness, emotional distress, moral conflict, and bureaucratic hurdles. Studies have reported negative impacts of organ donation on donor families like donating organs for money, disfigurement of body, donors after life, and disapproval of core family members.[1415] The apprehension of regret or guilt may also act as a deterrent against decision to donate.[16] Many families chose not to share their decision to donate organs with other relatives or the community after successful organ donation also. They found it best to keep their decision private due to several reasons. Communication apprehension about death is an individual’s fear associated with real and anticipated communication about the experience of dying and death.[17] The decision to keep the donation private was primarily motivated by a desire to avoid social criticism and emotional distress. Families feared negative perceptions and accusations of financial motives, which were exacerbated by social and cultural beliefs. Ideally, criticism for organ donation should not be there as it is a highly personal decision.[18] It has been reported that only half of the respondents had ever discussed decisions regarding organ donation with their family members.[19]

Regarding incentives, most participants emphasized the need for nonmonetary support. Nonmonetary incentives, such as educational and job opportunities, have been shown to be more acceptable to donor families. This preference for benefits was driven more by their socioeconomic situation than as a bargaining tool for the decision to donate. A study conducted in the UK by Sharp and Randhawa[20] found that participants were motivated by altruism to donate organs but would accept reciprocity, such as contributions toward funeral expenses, once consent was given. Similarly, a review by Shumin et al.[15] in China emphasized that while altruism remains central to organ donation, the introduction of incentives or social benefits could help overcome barriers and enhance donation rates.[21] The policy makers should, thus, re-examine the value of using financial incentives to increase the supply of organs for transplantation.[22] For increasing organ donation, the next of kin highlighted the lack of specific policies and the slow progress from the concerned authorities in resolving legal proceedings and insurance claims. Meanwhile, it was noted that the lack of insurance coverage and the harassment by authorities faced by families devalued their decision to donate. A descriptive analysis of the national donation trend in US indicated an increase in donation rates after the policy change.[19] One important aspect that emerged was that most participants favored increasing awareness to boost organ donation. They also recommended support for families as a significant motivator for promoting donation.[1023] Various strategies have been described to increase organ donation like education, outreach programs, spreading awareness, support to family, legislative measures, and policy decisions.[24] Organ donation rates can be increased by familiarizing the public with the subject of organ donation to promote a positive attitude based on accurate and clear information.[1525]

Despite other experiences, at a personal level, every decision-maker felt proud and satisfied with their choice of decision and experienced it as a cathartic release from the grief they felt after losing their loved ones. Approval and appreciation of their decision highlighted the importance and worthiness of their decision and gave them senses of pride and honor.[13]

The limitation of the study was that the time elapsed between the organ donation decision and the interview varied from 3 months to 49 months, leading to response bias because of recency and latency.

CONCLUSION

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of donor families’ post donation experiences, revealing both positive and negative impacts. Appreciation and inspiration from others fostered pride and satisfaction, while negative reactions, rooted in cultural beliefs, led to social challenges such as accusations and isolation. Interestingly, despite fears of societal criticism, most community members appreciated the decision to donate organs. Establishing clear guidelines and support systems can help alleviate the emotional burden on donor families and encourage more individuals to consider organ donation. One interesting finding of the study was that during the decision making people had unfounded fear of being criticized by society at large, but most of the members of society appreciated the decision to donate organs.
