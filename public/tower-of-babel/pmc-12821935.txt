Mapping the path to reform: a qualitative study of stakeholder expectations for managerial assessment centers in Iran’s health system
Authors: Maryam Naghshi, Ali Janati, Rahim khodayari-Zarnaq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficiency of the Assessment Centers as the tools of measuring the managerial competencies requires the constant monitoring and the perpetual improvement. In the system of health in Iran, where the structural and cultural peculiarities are unique, there is an Assessment Center, though its development is necessary. Earlier studies concentrated on operational issues but overlooked an important antecedent of the reform meaningfulness, namely getting the foundational expectations of key individuals. This paper attempted to address this gap by working into those expectations to inform the optimization of the managerial Assessment Center.MethodsQualitative research based on inductive content analysis was executed in 2024. Purposive and snowball sampling was done to conduct 29 semi-structured interviews with Iranian health system managers, policy experts, and psychologists. The initial symptoms were coded to obtain themes and analyzed manually, using the help of the MAXQDA 2018 software. Member checking and peer debriefing had been used to ensure trustworthiness.ResultsAnalysis revealed a multi-layered framework of expectations, synthesized into three primary themes. First, stakeholders expect robust policy-level foundations, including a more independent structure, a localized competency model, and the use of the center as a lever to institutionalize meritocracy. Second, regarding core elements, expectations emphasized the need for a more comprehensive competency set, validated assessment tools, and highly qualified assessors. Third, for the implementation phase, participants highlighted the necessity of a more transparent process and stronger executive oversight.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that stakeholders view the assessment center as an institution that is deeply integrated into the structure of the Iranian health system. The success of the reforms depends on adaptation to local conditions, scientific and ethical rigor, and support from senior leadership. This study provides a roadmap for policymakers to make decisions to preserve the center and shows that stakeholder engagement is critical to creating a relevant and effective institution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13912-1.

Background

Managers are critically important to healthcare systems. It is their duty to ensure that health resources are planned, organized, directed, and controlled to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of health systems. Good managers are able to enhance the quality of services, reduce expenditure, and improve access to healthcare. They are also important in the effective execution of health programs, initiatives, and strategies to prevent diseases [1–3]. Skills and behaviors that are essential to organizational success constitute competencies and are extremely important in many human resource processes: recruitment, succession planning, career development, job rotation, project team formation, and the assessment of performance and training [4, 5].

Assessment Centers (ACs) are used by many organizations, including some health systems, to ensure that managers possess the required skills and competencies [6–8]. There are three broad purposes of assessment centers: to forecast future decision-making behavior, diagnose developmental needs, and assess behavioral constructs of interest [9]. The assessment center process begins with a comprehensive analysis of the job, based on which major competencies are identified. Participants’ responses to a set of designed exercises and activities form the basis for evaluating these competencies. Assessors are trained for the exercises [10].

ACs are also characterized by unique attributes: they involve simulation of job situations rather than real-world scenarios, are aimed at eliciting observable behaviors through structured exercises, and, in contrast to traditional long-term measures, involve systematic analysis by trained assessors within a brief period [11].

Assessment centers (ACs) have been demonstrated to be more successful than cognitive ability tests and usually demonstrate a high degree of content-related validity [12, 13]. However, there is also debate regarding the construct validity of ACs, particularly concerning their effectiveness in measuring the intended dimensions accurately [14, 15].

The successful implementation of ACs is highly reliant on their fit with the cultural, structural, and managerial context of the host organization [16]. Global guidelines stress that the mere importation of assessment instruments without appropriate adjustment to the local setting may compromise their validity and impartiality [17]. This is especially important in complex healthcare facilities where, even under the best circumstances, ACs may pose significant implementation challenges [18].

The Iranian health system has its own peculiarities, such as a centralized government system and particular resource limitations, which necessitate tailored interventions for implementing complex initiatives such as ACs [19].

Although recent studies have begun examining operational challenges and pathologies of existing ACs in Iran, such as those by Vajir et al. aimed at identifying weaknesses in current centers [20], a critical precursor to their meaningful reform and improvement remains unaddressed. A significant knowledge gap persists regarding key stakeholders’ expectations of an ideal AC. Clarifying these pre-reform perspectives is essential for redesigning and optimizing a center that is legitimate, relevant, and accepted from the outset, thereby addressing the evident pathologies in existing centers.

This study aims to investigate the fundamental expectations of the main stakeholders in the Iranian health system regarding a managerial assessment center. The findings are expected to provide an evidence-based framework for enhancing the existing center to ensure its contextual relevance, legitimacy, and ultimate effectiveness in improving managerial performance.

Methods

This study employed a qualitative research design using an inductive content analysis approach [21, 22]. This method was deemed appropriate for developing categories and themes directly from the textual data without the constraints of pre-existing theories, making it ideal for exploring the underexplored area of expectations towards Assessment Centers (ACs) in Iran’s health system.

The study protocol received approval (IR.TBZMED.REC.1401.171) from the Research Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. All interviews were conducted in 2024 following ethical guidelines.

Sampling

Participants were selected using a combination of purposeful and snowball sampling techniques to ensure access to information-rich cases from across the Iranian health system. The sampling framework aimed for national representation, encompassing stakeholders from the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, provincial health departments, hospitals, and medical universities beyond Tabriz.

The target group was strategically composed of key stakeholders integral to the AC ecosystem, and included: (1) psychologists, (2) health system managers, and (3) professors and experts in health management and policy.

Health System Managers and Executives: This group included senior and mid-level managers who are the primary end-users of AC results for strategic decision-making, promotion, and organizational development.

Health Management and Policy Experts: Professors and specialists with executive, research, or educational experience in evaluating managers, who provide insights into the systemic and policy dimensions of managerial competencies.

Industrial-Organizational Psychologists: This group was included for two critical and methodologically sound reasons. First, psychologists are fundamental to the design and implementation of valid ACs. Their expertise is crucial for developing behavioral anchored rating scales (BARS), simulation exercises, and ensuring the reliability of assessments, which are the technical bedrock of any AC [23]. Second, modern managerial competencies extend beyond technical knowledge to encompass core psychological constructs such as leadership, emotional intelligence, conflict resolution, and resilience, which are best identified and evaluated through psychological science [24]. Including psychologists ensures the study captures expectations for assessing this essential behavioral dimension of management.

Interview guide

Potential participants were contacted via phone and email. Interview times and locations were arranged according to their preferences. All interviews were conducted in a quiet, private setting with only the interviewer and the participant present to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. Prior to each interview, written or oral informed consent was obtained, along with permission to audio-record the session. In addition to audio recording, brief field notes were taken during the interviews to capture contextual observations and non-verbal cues. The interviews lasted between 45 and 65 min. Data saturation was reached after 26 interviews. To confirm this, three additional interviews were conducted, bringing the total number of participants to 29.

Theme 1: Expectations from AC policymakers

This theme encapsulates the foundational requirements that participants believed must be driven by high-level policy and decision-making to ensure the AC’s legitimacy and long-term success.

Structure of the AC

Participants overwhelmingly emphasized the need for a formally independent and well-structured AC. A central expectation was the development of a localized competency assessment model specifically designed for the Iranian health context, moving beyond mere adaptation of foreign models. This was seen as critical for ensuring relevance and fairness. Establishing a formal code of ethics to govern all operations was identified as a non-negotiable prerequisite for building trust. As one manager highlighted, the credibility of the center hinges on its structural integrity:

Preparing an ethical rule for AC can be useful, and if we can develop a specific model according to the context of our own country, it will be effective. (Participant 15)

Preparing an ethical rule for AC can be useful, and if we can develop a specific model according to the context of our own country, it will be effective. (Participant 15)

Institutionalization of meritocracy

Participants viewed the AC not just as an assessment tool but as a powerful lever to institutionalize meritocracy within the health system’s culture. Key to this was the unwavering support of senior management and mandating that all managerial appointments be channeled through the AC process. Creating a comprehensive database of managers and their assessed competencies was proposed to make promotion decisions more transparent and data-driven. One expert stressed this transformational role:

We must try to institutionalize meritocracy in the health system, and this can be done through the AC. The senior managers must believe in the AC, and all appointments must be based on the AC. (Participant 22)

We must try to institutionalize meritocracy in the health system, and this can be done through the AC. The senior managers must believe in the AC, and all appointments must be based on the AC. (Participant 22)

Competency development courses

There was a clear consensus that assessment alone is insufficient; it must be intrinsically linked to development. Expectations for training courses were that they be highly practical and operational, moving away from traditional theoretical lectures. The courses should adhere to the principles of adult education, featuring job-specific simulators and being integrated into continuous in-service training programs. A participant explained, “Since the target group in developmental training courses are adults, it is necessary to create educational conditions suitable for this group. The more operational and practical these courses are, the more effective they will be.” (Participant 11).

Theme 2: Expectations regarding the core elements of the AC

This theme focuses on the essential components that constitute the AC itself, detailing what stakeholders expect to find within the center’s operational framework.

Competencies for assessment

A wide range of competencies was deemed essential for evaluating health system managers. Beyond general management skills like leadership, decision-making power, and budgeting, participants highlighted competencies critical to the public health context, such as crisis management, political skills, and negotiation abilities. The need for job-specific technical competencies was also strongly emphasized. As one participant noted, “Political and leadership skills, decision-making power… if they are examined in assessment centers, the problems that our organizations are dealing with now can be solved.” (Participant 18).

Assessment tools

The expectations for tools centered on validity, security, and modernity. Participants called for the use of scientifically valid and reliable tools, including advanced psychometric tests and challenging job simulators. A significant concern was the prevention of content leakage to maintain the tool’s integrity over time. The involvement of experts to develop and regularly update these tools was considered vital. One expert warned, “The tools used must be innovative and updated, and arrangements should be made to prevent these tools from being leaked because if they are leaked, the center’s performance will be impaired.” (Participant 17).

Assessors

The profile of assessors was a subject of rigorous expectation. They were expected to possess a dual expertise: deep technical knowledge of assessment methodologies and substantial relevant managerial experience. Personal attributes such as impartiality, strong communication skills, and professionalism were considered equally important. One participant summarized this by stating, “The assessors must have sufficient knowledge in the field of supervision, they must care to record the content, be impartial, and not suffer from errors.” (Participant 6).

Theme 3: Expectations in the implementation phase

This theme addresses the “how-to” aspects, detailing the expectations for the actual running of the AC and the individuals involved.

The assessment process

The respondents described the necessity of a standardized, effective, and systematic process. This encompassed high compliance to executive standards, keen pairing of assessors with applicants depending on the field of management, as well as the formidable framework of giving precise feedback to applicants on the results. Also, the participants proposed the periodical re-evaluation of managers, and the review of the own performance of the assessors to maintain its continuing quality. An important question which was also posed by one of the participants was concerned with transparency:

I have no idea whether the integration of people into the center is tracked… Are they provided with feedback after the center on their score on each of the competencies? (Participant 13)

I have no idea whether the integration of people into the center is tracked… Are they provided with feedback after the center on their score on each of the competencies? (Participant 13)

Prerequisites of facilitators

Facilitators were to be more than merely administrators since they provide guidelines to candidates and evaluators on the process. They must be knowledgeable in the evaluation procedure with assessment tools and have outstanding relational and communication capabilities to handle the circumstances in the most successful clients. They perceived their work as a key to the smoother experience: “The facilitators should at least be good communicators who have a thorough understanding of the procedure of the center to be able to lead the management applicants.” (Participant 7).

Attributes of executive officers

The single (or few) people in charge of the overall AC activities were supposedly powerful personalities with the orientation of quality assurance. Their main functions would be completion of strategic planning, checking on how they are loyal to the process and most importantly performance evaluation of the assessors. According to one of the participants, he recommended, “When he is the administrator, he would check the performance of the assessors periodically and see their records and the way they graded them” (Participant 10).

Discussion

Our results highlight the fact that stakeholders perceive the AC as a trigger of systemic change, in particular the institutionalization of meritocracy. This is in line with the initial role of the ACs as personnel selection and development tools [9], yet with a specific degree of urgency in a system whereby the participants feel that personnel can be hired based on non-meritocratic considerations.

The high demand of a localized competency model as well as autonomous AC structure is a critical result. This assumption is evidenced by the newest studies that prove the nomological validity of ACs that emphasize the fact that Assessment Centers are effective in gauging the intended constructs when exercises are programmed to evoke the corresponding characteristic of a particular situation [26]. An Iran-specific model should therefore be developed to make sure that dimensions that are key in determining managerial success in the Iranian health system are measured and evaluated appropriately.

Specifications of assessors–that they must be a mixture of technical and managerial experience, of high moral character, and that they must undergo continuous technical training in order to be empowered to do so [11] indicate that stakeholders view the role of the assessor as not simply that of an evaluator but as a moral agent, the credibility of whom is essential to the overall validity of the system. This view can be correlated with the approach of socioanalytic theory that focuses on the judgment of knowledgeable observers as the most accurate approach to measuring motivation and social competence [27].

Comparison with existing literature and theoretical contributions

Although our results on core competencies (e.g., leadership, crisis management) are similar to global research on health management [28], it is interesting to note that political skills and negotiation have high priority. This is probably indicative of the multi-stakeholder nature of the Iranian health system, in which managers are forced to negotiate through complex governmental systems. This finding underscores the critical importance of developing a situational and construct-related competency model that is specifically tailored to this unique context.

The agreement with the use of multiple, safe and validated assessment tools is consistent with the best practices described in the literature [29–31]. Nevertheless, the fact that the participants were very concerned about the leakage of tools also demonstrates that there is a certain contextual difficulty associated with the issue of institutional trust and process security.

The research by Kückelhaus et al. (2022) has a major theoretical contribution. Their study has shown that the interactive relationship between observer-rated motivation for success and observer-rated social competence accounted for the variance in sales performance over the influence of AC exercises and the rating as a whole, which enhanced operational validity by a quarter [27]. Our stakeholders are also heavily focused on high-quality assessors who can evaluate these multifaceted attributes and this observation offers strong theoretical justification. The socioanalytic approach may be well utilized to improve the predictive validity of ACs in the selection of health system managers.

Implications on policy and practice

The results provide specific and practical policy suggestions to policymakers:

Policy Level: The next important action is to ensure the structural independence of the AC and to allocate resources for constructing a local competency model. This approach is supported by research confirming the nomological validity of ACs, which demonstrates that focusing on well-defined, construct-based dimensions (such as those related to social influence and problem-solving) is fundamental to an effective assessment process [26].

Operational Level: It is critical to invest in rigorous training and certification of the assessors, and the focus on observation-based assessment (as opposed to self-report) as suggested by socioanalytic theory [11, 27].

Educational Integration: The formal connection between the AC and the health system in-service training infrastructure would make it not a one-time evaluation process, but the linkage within a cycle of continuous learning, which is evidenced in literature of utilizing AC feedback in developing processes [32].

Future research and limitations

The main constraints included failure to interview some of the key senior managers because of time or lack of belief in the concept of AC. Moreover, this investigation presents a roadmap of change instead of assessing the viability of a better AC by taking expectation before a significant change.

Future studies need to be action-based. Longitudinal research to monitor the predictive validity of the improved AC to managerial performance after its implementation is the next logical step. It is also possible that future research might directly evaluate the socioanalytic approach in the context of health management ACs to determine whether the combination of assessor-rated motivation and social competence is the same predictive variable as the field of selling presented by Kückelhaus et al. [27]. Building on the need for a localized model identified in this study, future research could investigate the effectiveness of leaner AC formats that prioritize the most critical competencies for the Iranian context. Comparing the implementation of different AC frameworks across diverse provincial health systems in Iran would also be a fruitful avenue for further study.

Conclusion

To sum up, this paper has shown that a managerial AC is expected to meet a multi-layered set of expectations by stakeholders within the Iranian health system. They see it as a powerful, independent motor of meritocracy, which is locally competent, is staffed with excellent assessors, and is carried out with integrity. Life imitation or surface alteration is not likely to work. The success of the AC will depend on its strong adherence to a contextual adaptation, ethical guidelines, and its unbroken integration into the wider managerial selection and development system. We, together with the international evidence, point to the fact that abandoning subjective holistic ratings in favor of the mechanical synthesis of construct-based, localized dimensions scores [26] and the exploitation of strong theoretical bases such as socioanalytic theory to design assessment [27] are the focal points to the development of an AC that is scientifically sound and widely agreeable and eventually transformative to the health system.
