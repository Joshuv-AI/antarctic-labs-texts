Sedation Practices in Interventional Pulmonology (SEDIP): Results of an Italian cross-sectional survey
Authors: Paolo Formenti, Paolo Carlucci, Dejan Radovanovic, Andrea Smargiassi, Gianluca Russo, Vito Torrano, Alessandro Zanforlin, Sara Cinquini, Giuseppe Cipolla, Riccardo Inchingolo, Giosuè Angelo Catapano, Gino Soldati, Francesco Tursi, AdET study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSedation has become an essential component of modern interventional pulmonology, accompanying the progressive expansion of bronchoscopic procedures from conventional diagnostic bronchoscopy to advanced diagnostic and therapeutic airway interventions. Despite its increasing relevance, sedation practices remain heterogeneous, and dedicated national recommendations specifically addressing interventional pulmonology are still lacking in many countries.MethodsA national cross-sectional survey was conducted among physicians involved in interventional pulmonology practice through the Accademia di Ecografia Toracica (AdET) network. The questionnaire consisted of 29 structured items covering organizational models, pharmacological strategies, monitoring approaches, and safety-related aspects of procedural sedation. Responses were analyzed descriptively using proportions, medians, and interquartile ranges. Agreement was operationally defined as a Likert score of 4–5 in at least 70% of responses.ResultsForty-one responses were included in the final analysis. Sedation was routinely integrated into procedural activity in most centers, although relevant variability emerged regarding sedation management, monitoring practices, and anesthesiology involvement. The strongest agreement concerned the need for national recommendations to standardize sedation practices (80.5% agreement), followed by recognition that anesthesiologist presence is required during high-risk interventional procedures (73.2%). Substantial agreement emerged regarding pulmonologist-led moderate sedation in selected settings (68.3%), while capnography did not reach clear consensus (44.7%). Limited anaesthesia availability was considered a relevant organizational factor by 63.2% of respondents.ConclusionsAmong participating clinicians, sedation was widely integrated into routine interventional pulmonology practice but remained organizationally heterogeneous. The survey identified broad agreement regarding the need for national harmonization and dedicated anaesthesiology support for high-risk procedures. Given the network-based recruitment strategy and the absence of procedural volume data, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than fully representative of national practice.

Introduction

Interventional pulmonology (IP) has undergone rapid development over the past two decades, evolving from traditional diagnostic bronchoscopy to a complex discipline that includes advanced diagnostic and therapeutic procedures such as endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA), transbronchial cryobiopsy, bronchoscopic lung volume reduction, airway stenting, and tumor debulking [1,2]. As these techniques have become increasingly sophisticated and widely adopted, the need for appropriate sedation strategies has become a central component of procedural safety and patient comfort [3]. Adequate sedation during bronchoscopic procedures improves patient tolerance, reduces anxiety and movement, and facilitates procedural performance [4]. For this reason, most bronchoscopy guidelines recommend the routine use of sedation in the absence of contraindications [5,6]. However, sedation practices during interventional pulmonary procedures remain highly heterogeneous [7]. Differences exist regarding the depth of sedation, the pharmacological agents used, the type of monitoring applied, and, importantly, the professional responsible for sedation management. In many centers sedation is administered by anaesthesiologists, whereas in others it may be managed by trained pulmonologists under procedural sedation protocols [8]. Nevertheless, these recommendations are mainly focused on diagnostic bronchoscopy and do not fully address the increasing complexity of modern interventional pulmonology procedures [9]. In parallel, the anaesthesiology literature has increasingly examined the specific challenges associated with airway interventions. Interventional pulmonology procedures often involve a shared airway, complex ventilation strategies, and variable levels of sedation or anaesthesia. Recent reviews emphasize that anesthetic management must be tailored to the type and complexity of the procedure and to the patient’s respiratory reserve [10]. While these contributions provide valuable insight into intraprocedural anesthetic management, they are generally focused on technical aspects of anaesthesia rather than on the broader organizational and clinical frameworks within which sedation is delivered. In many countries, including Italy, dedicated national recommendations specifically addressing sedation strategies for interventional pulmonology procedures are still lacking [11]. As a result, sedation practices may vary significantly among centers depending on institutional resources, local expertise, and the availability of anaesthesiology support.

Understanding real-world practices and identifying areas of agreement and variability among clinicians involved in interventional pulmonology is therefore an important step toward improving procedural safety and organizational efficiency. We conducted a national cross-sectional survey aimed at exploring current sedation practices among clinicians involved in interventional pulmonology procedures. The objectives of the study were to describe sedation strategies, explore organizational and safety-related aspects of procedural sedation, identify unmet needs and the perceived necessity for national harmonization, and generate exploratory data that may inform future initiatives aimed at improving consistency and safety in interventional pulmonology practice

Materials and methods

Study design and survey development

The study was designed as a national cross-sectional survey aimed at describing current sedation practices in interventional pulmonology and identifying areas of agreement and variability among clinicians involved in these procedures. The questionnaire explored key aspects of procedural sedation, including organizational models, pharmacological approaches, monitoring strategies, and safety requirements in different interventional pulmonology settings. The study was not designed as a Delphi process or as a formal guideline-development initiative, but rather as an exploratory survey of current practice patterns and perceived unmet needs. Questionnaire development was based on a preliminary review of the available literature on bronchoscopy sedation, anaesthetic management of interventional pulmonology procedures, and shared-airway interventions, followed by internal discussion among the study promoters in order to identify clinically relevant domains requiring exploration in real-world practice. No formal psychometric validation or pilot testing was performed. The questionnaire should therefore be regarded as an exploratory descriptive tool rather than a validated instrument.

The final questionnaire consisted of 29 items, including demographic questions, multiple-choice items, Likert-scale statements, and one open-text question (supplementary materials Table S1). Five items were specifically designed as consensus-oriented statements and were rated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In addition to opinion-based items, demographic information and professional background data were collected in order to characterize the responding population.

Participants and survey administration

The questionnaire was distributed electronically through Google Forms to physicians attending the national meeting of the Accademia di Ecografia Toracica (AdET), specifically targeting clinicians directly involved in interventional pulmonology practice. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and respondents completed the survey independently during the predefined collection period (November 2025–February 2026). Because the questionnaire was initially distributed within a scientific meeting setting and subsequently circulated among colleagues belonging to the same professional network, an exact denominator of all potential recipients could not be formally established; therefore, a formal response rate was not calculated. Given the exploratory and network-based nature of the project, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Only one completed response per participant was included in the final dataset.

Statistical analysis

Survey responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute numbers and percentages, while Likert-scale responses were analyzed through calculation of mean values, standard deviations, median scores, and proportions of agreement or disagreement. For interpretative purposes, practical consensus was considered present when at least 70% of respondents selected agreement values corresponding to scores 4 or 5.

This threshold was used as an operational indicator of agreement within the respondent sample.

Because of the exploratory design of the study, no inferential statistical comparisons were planned.

Conceptual synthesis of survey findings

Following analysis of survey responses, a survey-derived conceptual practice proposal was developed to summarize the organizational logic emerging from respondents’ answers. The proposal was based on the observed patterns of agreement across Likert-scale statements, descriptive responses regarding sedation organization, and procedure-specific sedation approaches reported by participants. It was intended as an exploratory framework to facilitate interpretation of current practice variability. The framework stratifies procedures according to perceived complexity and links increasing procedural risk with progressively greater sedation depth, monitoring requirements, and need for anaesthesiology support.

Results

A total of 41 responses from clinicians involved in interventional pulmonology practice were included in the final analysis. The survey explored respondent characteristics, sedation organization, monitoring strategies, safety perception, and consensus-oriented statements across six predefined domains. Although the present analysis was primarily descriptive, item-level percentages were calculated for consensus statements in order to better characterize areas of convergence and variability across Italian interventional pulmonology centers. Response distributions for the five Likert-scale statements are shown in Figure 1.

Respondent characteristics and institutional profile

Most respondents were experienced physicians actively involved in interventional pulmonology practice, with the majority reporting regular use of sedation during routine procedures. Participants were mainly affiliated with hospital-based centers, including academic and general hospitals, confirming that the survey captured real-world activity from centers routinely performing advanced bronchoscopic procedures. Sedation was described as an integral component of procedural practice in most centers.

Current sedation organization in Italian interventional pulmonology centers

Considerable heterogeneity emerged regarding sedation management (Table 1). In many centers, sedation was directly managed by pulmonologists, whereas anaesthesiologist involvement remained more frequent during advanced or higher-risk procedures such as cryobiopsy, medical thoracoscopy, and therapeutic bronchoscopy. Midazolam remained the most frequently reported sedative drug, often combined with short-acting opioids, while propofol was mainly associated with more complex procedures and with centers where anaesthesiology support was routinely available. Standardized sedation protocols were not universally implemented, with several respondents reporting either absence of formal protocols or ongoing plans for local protocol development.

Monitoring practices and safety perception

Routine monitoring during sedated procedures generally included pulse oximetry and non-invasive blood pressure measurement, whereas ECG monitoring was more frequently adopted during advanced procedures. Capnography showed marked variability across centers, with systematic use limited to a minority of respondents. Sedation-related complications were reported as relatively uncommon overall, with desaturation representing the most frequently encountered event. However, medico-legal concerns remained relevant, particularly regarding legal coverage for non-anaesthesiologist -administered sedation.

Procedure-specific sedation approaches

Sedation was routinely adopted for advanced procedures including EBUS-TBNA, therapeutic bronchoscopy, cryobiopsy, and medical thoracoscopy. A clear distinction emerged between lower-complexity and higher-complexity procedures. Most respondents considered anaesthesiology support mandatory primarily for high-risk procedures, while moderate sedation led by trained pulmonologists was considered acceptable in selected lower-risk settings.

Consensus statement analysis

The five Likert-scale consensus statements showed heterogeneous but clinically meaningful patterns of agreement (Table 2). The strongest agreement emerged from the statement supporting the need for national consensus recommendations to standardize sedation practices in interventional pulmonology, with 80.5% of respondents assigning a score of 4 or 5 and a median score of 5, representing the highest convergence observed in the survey.

Consensus Statement analysis.

Substantial agreement emerged regarding the possibility that trained pulmonologists may safely manage moderate sedation, with 68.3% agreement and a median score of 4, indicating growing acceptance of structured pulmonologist-led sedation pathways in selected clinical settings. Responses concerning the impact of anaesthesia availability on procedural access showed 63.2% agreement, with broader dispersion (IQR 2.0), suggesting that lack of dedicated anaesthesiology support remains a relevant organizational issue, although not uniformly perceived across centers. The only statement not reaching practical agreement concerned universal mandatory capnography during all sedated procedures, with 44.7% agreement and a median score of 3, confirming that this remains an area of ongoing debate.

Discussion

Sedation has progressively become an integral component of interventional pulmonology, paralleling the expansion of procedural complexity from conventional bronchoscopy to advanced diagnostic and therapeutic airway interventions [4]. As procedural invasiveness has increased, sedation can no longer be considered merely an adjunct to improving comfort, but rather a determinant of procedural feasibility, operator performance, and patient safety. This concept is well established in bronchoscopy literature, where adequate sedation has been associated with improved patient tolerance, reduced procedural interruption, and greater overall procedural acceptability [5].

The SEDIP survey suggests that this evolution is clearly reflected in current Italian practice. Most respondents described sedation as routinely integrated into daily interventional activity, but organizational approaches remain highly heterogeneous across centers. This variability concerns not only the drugs used, but also the professional responsible for sedation, monitoring strategies, and local protocol availability [12]. Similar heterogeneity has also been reported internationally, particularly in centers where bronchoscopy programs developed independently from formal procedural sedation pathways [13]. One of the strongest findings of the present survey is the broad agreement regarding the need for national recommendations. More than 80% of respondents supported the statement that sedation practices in interventional pulmonology should be standardized through dedicated consensus recommendations, making this the strongest area of convergence observed in the survey. This result is particularly relevant because current international recommendations mainly address flexible bronchoscopy and conventional procedural sedation, while advanced interventional procedures remain only partially covered by existing documents [14].

A second major finding concerns the role of anaesthesiology support. High agreement emerged regarding the need for anaesthesiologist presence during high-risk procedures, although with broader variability than observed for national harmonization, suggesting that organizational models remain partially center-dependent. This finding is fully coherent with recent anaesthesiology -oriented reviews emphasizing that advanced airway procedures represent a specific scenario of shared airway management, often requiring individualized ventilation strategies, rapid control of hypoxemia, and immediate rescue capability [15–17]. This aspect becomes particularly relevant during procedures such as transbronchial cryobiopsy, therapeutic bronchoscopy, airway debulking, and medical thoracoscopy, where bleeding risk, airway obstruction, respiratory reserve, and procedure duration substantially increase procedural complexity [18]. In these settings, sedation depth often approaches deep sedation or general anaesthesia, and anaesthesiology support becomes functionally integrated into procedural safety rather than optional organizational support [19].

Substantial agreement also emerged regarding pulmonologist-led moderate sedation in selected settings, with more than two thirds of respondents considering this approach feasible when appropriate training, monitoring, and rescue competencies are available. This reflects an important evolution already observed in several bronchoscopy programs, where structured moderate sedation administered by trained non-anaesthesiologists has shown acceptable safety profiles when strict patient selection, adequate monitoring, and rescue competencies are present [20,21]. This dual model- anaesthesiology support for complex procedures and structured pulmonologist-led sedation for selected lower-risk interventions, probably reflects current clinical practice more realistically than rigid procedural categories. An additional relevant finding concerns anaesthesia availability as an organizational limiting factor. In the present survey, most respondents agreed that limited access to dedicated anaesthesiology support may restrict advanced procedural activity. This reflects a practical issue frequently encountered in daily clinical activity, especially in systems where bronchoscopy scheduling depends on anaesthesia allocation rather than procedural demand.

The statement regarding capnography deserves separate consideration. Unlike other domains, this item did not reach strong agreement. Current procedural sedation literature increasingly supports capnography because of earlier detection of hypoventilation compared with pulse oximetry alone, particularly during propofol-based sedation [22]. However, the present survey suggests that its implementation remains inconsistent in real-world bronchoscopy settings, likely reflecting differences in resource availability and perceived necessity according to sedation depth.

Finally, the survey indicates that clinicians already perceive procedural complexity as the main determinant guiding sedation choices, even in the absence of formally shared national standards. Although the survey was not designed to generate formal procedural recommendations, respondents’ answers consistently suggested that sedation choices are implicitly modulated according to perceived procedural complexity. On this basis, a conceptual stratification was constructed to summarize the prevailing organizational trends emerging from the survey and to facilitate interpretation of the main findings (Table 3). Lower-complexity procedures are generally perceived as compatible with moderate sedation, often under direct pulmonologist management when adequate training and monitoring are available. Intermediate procedures occupy a more variable area, reflecting differences in local expertise, duration, and patient-related factors. By contrast, high-complexity interventions are consistently associated with deeper sedation requirements and a stronger perceived need for anaesthesiology support. This conceptual framework should not be interpreted as a prescriptive classification, but rather as a synthesis of the practical logic emerging from current responses collected through this professional network.

The present study has several limitations. First, responses reflect self-reported attitudes and organizational practices rather than objective procedural outcome data and therefore cannot establish comparative safety between different sedation models. Second, recruitment was conducted through the AdET network and may have preferentially involved clinicians with a particular interest in thoracic ultrasound and procedural respiratory medicine. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the entire spectrum of Italian interventional pulmonology practice. Third, information regarding annual procedural volume, referral activity, center complexity, and experience with advanced procedures such as rigid bronchoscopy, airway stenting, robotic bronchoscopy, advanced pleural interventions, and multimodality image-guided procedures were not collected. Therefore, stratified analyses according to procedural complexity or center expertise were not possible. Fourth, because the denominator of potential recipients could not be established, a formal response rate could not be calculated. Finally, the questionnaire was not formally validated and should be regarded as an exploratory descriptive tool.

Conclusion

The survey provides an exploratory overview of sedation practices among participating Italian clinicians involved in interventional pulmonology. Sedation appears widely integrated into routine procedural activity, although substantial variability persists regarding organizational models, professional responsibility, monitoring strategies, and protocol availability. The strongest area of agreement concerned the perceived need for national harmonization and the recognition that high-risk procedures require dedicated anaesthesiology support. Because of the network-based recruitment strategy and the absence of detailed procedural volume data, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than fully representative of the national landscape. Future studies should involve broader and predefined respondent panels, high-volume referral centers, and detailed characterization of procedural activity to support more robust national recommendations.
