Feasibility of serial perioperative lung ultrasound to monitor fluid shifts during thulium laser prostate enucleation
Authors: Philipp Kuhn, Thomas Wieser, JoEllen Welter, Roman Rechner, Jan T Klein, Thomas R W Herrmann, Alexander Dullenkopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIrrigation fluid absorption (IFA) during transurethral surgery of the prostate continues to pose a risk for fluid overload and electrolyte imbalance, yet reliable methods for quantifying absorption remain limited. Lung ultrasound (LU), increasingly used to detect pulmonary pathology, provides a noninvasive approach to assess fluid accumulation.MethodsIn this prospective single-center observational study, 36 patients aged ≥ 60 years undergoing elective urethral-sparing Thulium: YAG anatomical endoscopic enucleation of the prostate (AEEP; ThuLEP) were examined. LU was performed preoperatively and postoperatively by trained investigators, and a modified Lung Ultrasound Score (mLUS) was calculated. Perioperative data included body weight, fluid balance, biochemical parameters, and hemodynamic variables. Associations between mLUS and perioperative parameters were evaluated using Spearman correlation and linear regression analyses.ResultsMedian postoperative mLUS values increased significantly from baseline (0 (IQR 0–1) vs. 7.5 (IQR 4.5–10.5), p < 0.0001). Overall fluid balance correlated with both postoperative mLUS (ρ = 0.51, p = 0.003) and change in mLUS (ρ = 0.40, p = 0.023). The change in body weight correlated with intraoperative fluid balance (ρ = 0.66, p < 0.001), overall fluid balance (ρ = 0.58, p < 0.001), and serum chloride increase (ρ = 0.53, p = 0.007). In a simple linear regression model, overall fluid balance was associated with the change in mLUS (β = 0.00073, 95% CI 0.00015–0.00131, p = 0.015). In a multivariable linear regression model, greater overall fluid balance (p = 0.015) and chloride increase (p = 0.001) were associated with larger increases in body weight. The median weight of removed prostate tissue was 61.5 g (IQR 42–88.5), with 0.92 g per minute.ConclusionLU detected postoperative pulmonary fluid accumulation and correlated with perioperative fluid balance in elderly patients undergoing AEEP. Changes in serum chloride concentration and body weight were associated with systemic fluid uptake.Keywords: Postoperative outcome, Lung ultrasound, Transurethral surgery, Thulium laser prostate enucleation, ThuLEP, AEEP, EEP

Introduction

Systemic uptake of irrigation fluid (irrigation fluid absorption, IFA) is a long known side effect during transurethral surgery. Depending on the absorbed volume and fluid type, it may cause fluid overload and symptomatic electrolyte dilution, leading to transurethral resection (TUR) syndrome [1]. While TUR syndrome most often occurs after transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) [2], it has also been observed after cystoscopy, arthroscopy, and various endoscopic gynecologic procedures that use irrigation fluid [3–5].

The major pathophysiologic mechanisms of TUR syndrome are hypervolemia and hyponatremia, which usually become clinically significant after IFA exceeds 1000 mL and severe when IFA exceeds 2500 mL [6]. Hypervolemia can impair cardiovascular function, leading to heart failure and pulmonary edema, particularly in elderly patients undergoing prostatic surgery [7, 8]. Dilutional hyponatremia results from the absorption of hypotonic, nonconductive irrigation fluids such as glycine or sorbitol-mannitol mixtures (cytal solution), either through open venous sinuses or by extravasation into the perivesical space and abdominal cavity [6]. Acute hyponatremia may cause cerebral edema with symptoms ranging from nausea and confusion to seizures, permanent brain damage, respiratory arrest, brain stem herniation, coma, and death [9]. Hemolysis and glycine intoxication are additional risks associated with classical TUR syndrome [1].

To detect early neurological symptoms, spinal anesthesia was traditionally preferred for TURP, as it allows the patient to remain responsive. Under general anesthesia, however, clinical signs of hyponatremia may go unnoticed, and frequent serum sodium monitoring is impractical and costly.

The introduction of bipolar and laser resection systems permits the use of isotonic saline as irrigation fluid, reducing the risk of dilutional hyponatremia [6, 10, 11]. Nevertheless, iso-osmolar hypervolemia may still develop, depending on surgical duration, pressure gradients, tissue vascularity, systemic resistance, and the total volume of irrigation fluid used [6, 7]. Morcellation of enucleated tissue during transurethral enucleation of the prostate requires bladder distension with elevated intravesical pressure, which may further promote IFA [2, 12]. In addition, isotonic saline absorption can result in metabolic hyperchloremic acidosis [13]. Hypervolemia and electrolyte disturbances especially put patients with cardiac, pulmonary or renal comorbidities at risk [8]. As TURP is frequently performed in elderly patients, who tend to present more often with comorbidities, it still presents a clinically relevant problem.

IFA can be quantified using several methods [5, 6]. Direct measurement of fluid balance by weighing the irrigation fluid and drainage bags during the surgery allows the calculation of absorbed volume but is cumbersome, time-consuming, and prone to inaccuracy because of possible leakage around the shaft of the resectoscope [14–16]. Alternatively, measuring ethanol concentrations in expired breath after adding ethanol to the irrigation fluid correlates well with absorbed volume and distinguishes intra- from extravascular absorption [17–19]. However, ethanol must be added to the irrigation fluid, and the method is not routinely feasible in the operating room. When isotonic saline is used, an increase in plasma chloride concentration indicates IFA, while hemoglobin dilution may provide an inverse estimate. Hemodynamic monitoring may also reflect intravascular volume increases through changes in cardiac output, stroke volume, and blood pressure, as long as cardiac function remains compensated. Overall, estimating IFA remains an imprecise process that is rarely undertaken outside of research settings, with monopolar resection generally producing higher absorption than bipolar systems [5, 20, 21].

Recently, lung ultrasound (LU) has gained popularity as a noninvasive tool to assess volume state and pulmonary morbidity [22–24]. This development led us to investigate whether IFA correlates with LU findings and whether it is feasible to perform serial LU examinations before and after transurethral surgery of the prostate in elderly patients. The primary objective was to assess perioperative changes in modified lung ultrasound score and their relationship with markers of fluid absorption. Since our institution is a reference center for thulium laser enucleation of large prostates, all procedures were performed as anatomical endoscopic enucleation of the prostate (AEEP).

Materials and methods

Setting and participants

This prospective, single-center, observational study was conducted from August 2024 to March 2025 at a Swiss acute care hospital performing approximately 9,000 anesthetics and 1,000 urologic surgeries per year. Approval for this study was granted by the local Ethics Committee of Eastern Switzerland (EKOS 24/121, 06.08.2024), and it was registered with German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00035045).

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Eligible patients undergoing AEEP were recruited during the predefined study period. Patients meeting eligibility criteria and completing all protocol-defined assessments were included in the final analysis. Study inclusion was dependent on the availability of trained investigators to perform the protocol-required assessments.

The primary inclusion criteria were age ≥ 60 years and elective AEEP. The main exclusion criteria were known structural lung pathology, secondary surgery during the same anesthetic, and obesity WHO class II and higher, defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35 kg/m2. Additionally, we excluded patients undergoing emergency treatment, unable to provide informed consent (e.g., language problems, psychological disorders, dementia), participating in another study, or previously enrolled in this investigation. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Conduct of anesthetic

Preoperatively, patients fasted for 6 h for solids and were allowed clear liquids up to hospital check-in. The anesthetics were either conducted as propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia, supplemented as required with fentanyl, remifentanil, and sevoflurane according to institutional standards, or as spinal anesthesia. The airway was secured using either a tracheal tube or a laryngeal mask, as decided by the attending anesthetist. All patients had preoperative intravenous access and received Ringer’s acetate as maintenance fluid, as well as isotonic saline as a carrier for additional medications as needed. After surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) until meeting criteria for transfer to the ward. The patients were continuously monitored for vital signs, including ST-segment analysis, from the start of anesthesia until their transfer to the general ward.

Surgery

All patients underwent urethral sparing en-bloc AEEP using a pulsed Thulium: YAG laser (ThuLEP) [25, 26]. Procedures were performed either by an experienced surgeon or under direct supervision as part of a teaching protocol. A 26-Fr continuous-flow resectoscope (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) equipped with a separate working channel accommodating a 550-µm laser fiber was used. Visualization of the lower urinary tract was achieved using a 30° lens system, and laser energy was delivered via a reusable 550-µm fiber connected to a Thulium: YAG laser system (Lisa Laser Products GmbH, Kaltenburg-Lindau, Germany). Prostatic tissue morcellation was performed using a morcellator (Drill cut X II, Karl Storz. Tuttlingen, Germany), with blade rotation speeds initially set at 2000 rpm and occasionally reduced to 800 rpm as needed. Bipolar coagulation was applied in all cases to ensure effective hemostasis.

The procedure began with a mucosal incision approximately 1 cm proximal to the verumontanum horizontally to the lateral lobe mucosa through the mucosa and submucosa down to the transitional zone, followed by a horseshoe-shaped incision at the 12 o’clock position. These incisions were connected to complete circumferential apical mucosa /submucosa and thereby “urethra sparing”. Mechanical shearing forces were then used to expose the surgical capsule: the resectoscope tip was positioned just above the verumontanum and moved laterally in a shearing motion to reveal the dissection plane. Enucleation of the prostate lobes was initiated mechanically using the resectoscope tip, while adhesions were released using cutting or coagulation modes to maintain optimal visualization. Upon reaching the bladder neck, lateral dissection of the middle lobe was performed, and the detached lobes were pushed into the bladder.

After adenoma enucleation, hemostasis was achieved using bipolar coagulation to seal mucosal edges at the bladder neck, optimizing visibility for subsequent morcellation. A secondary irrigation system was connected to the morcellator to maintain clear visualization. To minimize urethral trauma, sheath movement was restricted during morcellation, keeping the sheath centered within the bladder. The morcellator blade was oriented upwards and maintained within the surgeon’s line of sight for continuous monitoring.

The procedure concluded with a thorough inspection of the resection cavity after removal of the morcellator and reinsertion of the working element into the resectoscope sheath. As the bladder collapsed, the bladder neck mucosal boundary became visible, allowing additional coagulation if necessary. Finally, a 22-Fr Tiemann catheter was inserted under digital rectal guidance, and the catheter balloon was inflated to position it securely at the bladder neck without slipping into the prostatic fossa.

Lung ultrasound

LU was performed using a point-of-care ultrasound device (Venue Go™; GE Healthcare, Opfikon, Switzerland) equipped with a 1.4- to 5.7-MHz convex probe (GE Healthcare C1-5-RS; GE Healthcare, Opfikon, Switzerland). Examinations were performed with patients in the supine position for anterior and lateral areas and in the sitting position for posterior areas. Each ultrasound examination was done by one of two trained physicians experienced in LU and lasted approximately 15–20 min per session.

Each hemithorax was divided into six regions: anterior, lateral, and posterior, each further subdivided into cranial and caudal zones using the mammillary line perpendicular to the back. This approach yielded twelve areas per patient [27]. Each area was examined in B-mode across multiple intercostal spaces to identify B-lines and consolidations. A modified Lung Ultrasound Score (mLUS) from 0 to 3 was assigned to each area according to Table 1. The twelve regional scores were summed to obtain a total mLUS for each examination ranging from 0 to 36 ( 0 = no or rare B-lines and 36 = consolidation in every area) [28]. Each patient underwent two ultrasound examinations: one preoperatively and one postoperatively, when ready for transfer from PACU to the ward. The same examiner performed both examinations for a given patient.

Variables and data collection

Demographic data, prostate size, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, and baseline blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, and oxygen requirement were extracted from urologic and pre-anesthesia records. As same-day surgery cases, patients checked in approximately 90 min prior to anesthesia. After check-in and changing into hospital attire, body weight was measured, and preoperative LU was performed, with time recorded. Following intravenous access placement, hemoglobin concentration was assessed.

Intraoperatively, the amount of intravenous fluid administered was recorded. Operating room staff documented the volume of irrigation fluid instilled and collected. Surgery duration was obtained from anesthesia records. In the PACU, once patients met discharge criteria, blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, and hemoglobin concentration were re-assessed. Body weight was again measured using a standing scale, ensuring the urinary catheter bag was excluded. The amount of intravenous fluid and oral fluids, irrigation fluid administered in the PACU, and urinary output were recorded. The postoperative LU was then performed, with time documented, and any oxygen supplementation noted.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage). Preoperative versus postoperative comparisons were made using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or exact McNemar test. Correlations between changes in modified lung ultrasound score, body weight, and perioperative variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. To further evaluate the relationship between overall fluid balance and perioperative change in mLUS, a simple linear regression model was performed. A multivariable linear regression model was additionally used to evaluate associations between clinically relevant markers of perioperative fluid absorption and change in body weight. Predictors for the multivariable model were selected based on clinical relevance and biological plausibility, while limiting the number of predictors to reduce the risk of model overfitting. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

One hundred patients undergoing AEEP were screened for inclusion between August 2024 and March 2025. Three patients declined participation, eight were younger than 60 years, one had a BMI > 35 kg/m2, six underwent combined surgical procedures beyond prostate resection, four had structural lung disease, and 42 were excluded because no investigator trained in LU was available on the day of surgery (Fig. 1). Thus, 36 patients were included in the final analysis. Their demographic and baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2. Eighteen patients (50%) had arterial hypertension, 14 (39%) had a cardiovascular diagnosis, and four (11%) had a non-structural pulmonary diagnosis.

In the operating room, patients received a median of 1,000 (IQR 750–1,350) mL of intravenous fluid and a median of 22,700 (IQR 18,000–31,000) mL of irrigation fluid. With a recorded median irrigation fluid output of 23,000 (IQR 17,200–31,000) mL, the calculated operating room fluid balance was a median of + 1,875 (IQR 1,125–3,100) mL. In the PACU, patients received an additional 200 (IQR 122.5–400) mL of intravenous fluid and 6,000 (IQR 5,550–8,000) mL of irrigation fluid. Oral intake was 75 (IQR 0–200) mL, and fluid output was 6,275 (IQR 5,200–9,550) mL, resulting in a PACU fluid balance of 0 (IQR − 525–285) mL and an overall fluid balance of 1,980 (IQR 850–3,155) mL.

Regarding clinical signs of fluid overload, cardiac troponin measurements were not performed as no ST-segment depression was observed. Furthermore, no patient required postoperative high-dose oxygen therapy, intubation, or vasopressor support with noradrenaline.

To further evaluate the relationship between fluid balance and pulmonary fluid accumulation, a simple linear regression model was performed with change in modified lung ultrasound score as the dependent variable. Overall fluid balance was positively associated with the change in mLUS (β = 0.00073, 95% CI 0.00015–0.00131, p = 0.015), explaining 18% of the observed variability (R² = 0.18).

A multivariable linear regression model including overall fluid balance, chloride change, operative duration, and resected prostate weight was used to evaluate factors associated with body weight change. The model explained 56% of the variability in body weight change (R² = 0.63, adjusted R² = 0.56, p = 0.0004). Greater overall fluid balance (β = 0.00031, 95% CI 0.00007–0.00055, p = 0.015) and greater chloride increase (β = 0.29, 95% CI 0.13–0.45, p = 0.001) were associated with larger increases in body weight. In contrast, greater resected prostate weight was associated with smaller increases in body weight (β = -0.016, 95% CI -0.027 to -0.006, p = 0.005). Operative duration was not significantly associated with body weight change (p = 0.187).

Discussion

In this prospective single-center study, we evaluated the feasibility and potential utility of perioperative LU to detect pulmonary effects of IFA in elderly patients undergoing AEEP. Provided that appropriate examiners were available, serial LU examinations were feasible in routine clinical practice and could be performed without interfering with perioperative workflow. Postoperative mLUS correlated with overall fluid balance and intraoperative fluid balance, indicating sensitivity to perioperative volume shifts. However, mLUS values did not directly correlate with body weight change, whereas serum chloride increase and overall fluid balance were associated with body weight gain in multivariable analysis, suggesting that body weight and chloride changes may reflect systemic saline accumulation, whereas LU appears to capture pulmonary fluid redistribution.

Irrigation fluid absorption in modern endoscopic prostate surgery

The incidence of TUR syndrome has markedly declined with the introduction of bipolar and laser technologies allowing isotonic saline as irrigation fluid [6, 29]. Nevertheless, clinically relevant IFA persists, particularly during prolonged procedures and large-gland enucleations [7, 13, 20]. Contemporary studies on bipolar TURP and laser enucleation consistently report measurable saline absorption, occasionally exceeding 1–2 L, even in the absence of hyponatremia [6]. In contrast to monopolar resection, the pathophysiological profile has shifted from dilutional hyponatremia toward iso-osmolar hypervolemia and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis [6, 30]. Our data align with this paradigm. While no clinically overt TUR syndrome occurred, significant positive fluid balances were observed, and changes in serum chloride correlated with body weight gain. In multivariable analysis, both the increase in serum chloride concentration and overall fluid balance were associated with weight change, supporting their potential value as a surrogate marker of isotonic saline uptake. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that hyperchloremia and reduced strong ion difference may accompany large-volume saline absorption during endoscopic urological procedures [31]. Intraoperative fluid balance was associated with higher postoperative mLUS values, while operative duration showed a positive, non-significant correlation with postoperative mLUS. This observation is pathophysiologically plausible, as absorption is driven by exposure time, intravesical pressure gradients, and the extent of opened venous sinuses [2, 6, 12, 29]. During AEEP, en bloc enucleation followed by morcellation requires sustained bladder distension, which may promote systemic uptake. Although laser techniques are associated with improved hemostasis compared to monopolar TURP, they do not eliminate the risk of IFA.

High-end fluid monitoring systems, such as the Endoscopic Surgical Monitoring System (ESMS), have been described for measuring fluid absorption during endourological procedures. However, they are not routinely used in most centers. In contrast, ultrasound is widely available, noninvasive, and already integrated into routine perioperative care. The strength of our study approach lies in its simplicity and broad clinical applicability. It offers a pragmatic option for perioperative assessment of fluid status during prolonged ThuLEP procedures [16, 32–35].

Lung ultrasound and perioperative pulmonary congestion

LU has become an established bedside modality for the assessment of pulmonary congestion [23, 24]. B-lines reflect increased extravascular lung water and correlate with invasive measurements of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and lung water content in cardiology and critical care settings [15, 22]. In perioperative medicine, increases in B-lines have been associated with liberal fluid administration and postoperative pulmonary complications [24].

In the present study, postoperative mLUS increased and correlated with overall fluid balance, which was also associated with the change in mLUS in linear regression analysis, suggesting that LU may be sensitive to perioperative pulmonary interstitial fluid accumulation. Importantly, these changes were observed despite stable oxygen saturation and hemodynamic parameters, indicating that LU may detect subclinical congestion before overt respiratory compromise becomes apparent. This is particularly relevant in elderly patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve, who comprised the majority of our cohort.

However, mLUS changes did not correlate with body weight gain. This finding highlights a key distinction: systemic fluid absorption does not uniformly translate into pulmonary interstitial edema. The distribution of absorbed fluid depends on cardiac function, vascular compliance, capillary permeability, and lymphatic clearance. In compensated patients, intravascular expansion may be accommodated without significant pulmonary extravasation, whereas in susceptible individuals, even modest volume shifts may lead to detectable B-lines [36]. Thus, LU appears to reflect organ-specific consequences of fluid absorption rather than total absorbed volume.

Clinical implications

Although the classical TUR syndrome has become rare, iso-osmolar hypervolemia remains clinically relevant, especially in elderly patients with cardiovascular comorbidities [6, 8]. In our cohort, half of patients had arterial hypertension and 39% had established cardiovascular disease. In such populations, early detection of pulmonary congestion may have practical implications for postoperative monitoring and fluid management.

Our findings suggest that LU can be integrated into perioperative care as a noninvasive adjunct to standard monitoring. While it does not replace quantitative methods of measuring IFA, it provides complementary information regarding pulmonary tolerance of perioperative fluid shifts. In high-risk patients, an increase in postoperative mLUS values may prompt closer observation, restrictive fluid strategies, or earlier diuretic therapy where appropriate. The consistent association between serum chloride increase and body weight change further supports routine perioperative electrolyte monitoring in laser enucleation procedures. In the setting of isotonic irrigation, chloride may serve as a more sensitive biochemical marker of saline absorption than sodium concentration or hemoglobin dilution.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, standardized LU protocol with a 12-zone modified lung ultrasound score, and detailed perioperative fluid accounting. The cohort reflects contemporary practice in a reference center for thulium laser enucleation, enhancing clinical relevance.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The sample size was limited, and an a priori power calculation was not performed. Furthermore, not all eligible patients could be enrolled. The study protocol required two standardized ultrasound examinations at predefined perioperative time points performed by one of two trained investigators. Since these examinations had to be integrated into routine perioperative workflows and could not always be performed within the required time, some otherwise eligible patients were not included. Consequently, selection bias cannot be excluded. In addition, the single-center design may limit generalizability. LU assessments were performed at only two time points, precluding analysis of dynamic intraoperative changes. Moreover, body weight and calculated fluid balances are indirect measures of IFA and susceptible to measurement inaccuracies. Finally, we did not assess hard clinical endpoints such as postoperative pulmonary complications or perioperative cardiac biomarkers, which would be necessary to establish the prognostic value of mLUS changes.

Future perspectives

Future larger, ideally multicenter studies should evaluate whether intraoperative or early postoperative LU-guided fluid management can improve outcomes in endoscopic prostate surgery. Integration of LU with advanced hemodynamic monitoring or noninvasive cardiac output assessment may further clarify the relationship between systemic absorption and organ-specific congestion. Defining clinically meaningful mLUS thresholds in this surgical setting will be essential before routine implementation can be recommended.

Conclusions

In elderly patients undergoing AEEP with isotonic saline irrigation, LU is feasible and detects perioperative pulmonary fluid accumulation. Postoperative mLUS and changes in mLUS were associated with overall fluid balance, supporting the ability of LU to reflect perioperative fluid shifts. Changes in serum chloride concentration and body weight provided complementary information regarding systemic saline uptake. LU may therefore serve as a complementary tool to detect subclinical pulmonary congestion in high-risk patients rather than as a direct measure of total absorbed irrigation volume.
