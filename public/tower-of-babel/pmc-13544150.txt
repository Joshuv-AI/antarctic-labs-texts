Letter to the Editor Regarding “Effect of Remimazolam Versus Propofol Sedation on Postoperative Sleep Quality Following Gastroscopy: A Propensity Score-Matched Cohort Study” [Response to Letter]
Authors: Xi Qiao, Chao Li, Wei Du
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We greatly appreciate the constructive letter from Dr. Rijuan Jin and Dr. Yijie Lou regarding our recently published study comparing remimazolam and propofol sedation on postoperative sleep quality in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy. Their comments addressed important methodological considerations and helped to contextualize our findings for readers. We are pleased to respond to the issues raised point-by-point.

First, applicability of the RCSQ and clinical significance. As mentioned in our manuscript, we acknowledge that the Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) was initially developed for ICU populations.1 However, accumulating evidence has validated its reliability and validity in non-ICU postoperative patients,2 home sleepers,3 and those living in the community.4 It is positioned as a tool for subjective sleep perception. Given that gastroscopy is a short-term surgical sedation, the RCSQ remains a practical and widely accepted tool in this research field. Regarding the clinical significance of the 3-point median difference in total RCSQ scores, we fully agree that statistical significance does not equate directly to clinical benefit. The 10-point minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold was derived from ICU patients undergoing major surgery, who experience far greater postoperative sleep disruption and higher baseline symptom burden. Gastroscopy is a brief, minimally invasive procedure, and the expected magnitude of sleep disturbance is inherently much smaller. In this context, even a modest improvement in first-night sleep quality may be clinically relevant for patients who return home and resume daily activities immediately after discharge. Nevertheless, we concur that the MCID for RCSQ in the gastroscopy sedation population remains to be formally established, and our findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence rather than definitive clinical benefit.

Second, the confounding effect of BIS differences and causal pathway adjustment. The observed difference in BIS values between groups reflects the distinct pharmacological profiles of the two agents, rather than a true disparity in clinical sedation depth. It is well documented that benzodiazepines produce less suppression of cortical electroencephalographic activity than propofol at equivalent sedation endpoints.5 Remimazolam, as an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, exhibited higher BIS values than propofol when titrated to the same clinical target.6,7 Therefore, the higher BIS in the remimazolam group does not indicate “shallower sedation,” but a pharmacodynamic difference. With respect to the adjustment strategy, we included BIS and intraoperative hypotension in the regression models to account for any potential differences. Notably, the estimated effect of remimazolam on postoperative RCSQ scores became larger (β = 7.985 in the fully adjusted model) rather than smaller after adjustment. If the sleep advantage were driven by “shallower sedation” (higher BIS), adjusting for BIS would have attenuated the coefficient towards smaller. This finding suggests that the observed sleep benefit is not explained by shallower sedation. Instead, it supports an independent pharmacological effect of remimazolam. We acknowledge that BIS may partially lie on the causal pathway. Propensity score matching balances baseline characteristics but cannot address intraoperative confounders on the BIS and RCSQ pathway. Therefore, a valid mediation partition is not achievable in this PSM study design. Mediation decomposition requires untestable assumptions beyond what PSM can address.8 The adjusted β provides a conservative estimate of the drug’s independent effect.

Third, statistical interpretation of subgroup analyses. We fully agree with the concerns regarding multiple comparisons in subgroup analyses. In our manuscript, we explicitly stated that subgroup analyses were exploratory and were not adjusted for multiple testing. The significant interaction observed for baseline PSQI ≥ 7 should be interpreted as a signal that patients with pre-existing sleep impairment may derive benefit from remimazolam, rather than a conclusive finding. We appreciate the emphasis on this point and concur that validation in prospective, adequately powered studies is necessary.

Fourth, blinding reliability. This was a retrospective observational cohort study, not an RCT. We acknowledge that we did not formally validate the success of blinding in this study, which is a limitation of the retrospective design. However, several measures were taken to minimize detection bias. First, the research staff who administered the RCSQ via telephone interview were completely blinded to group allocation and had no access to intraoperative anesthesia records. Second, the patients were not informed of which sedative agent they received. Nevertheless, we agree that formal blinding assessment would strengthen the objectivity of subjective sleep outcomes.

Fifth, temporal limitation of single-night assessment. We concur that sleep assessment limited to the first postoperative night cannot definitively distinguish between acute residual drug effects and sustained improvement in sleep architecture. This limitation was explicitly stated in the Discussion section of our manuscript. However, for patients who underwent gastroscopy, sleep quality on the first postoperative night is the most clinically relevant patient-centered outcome, as it directly affects their immediate recovery and return to daily activities. The faster clearance and minimal residual sedation of remimazolam are precisely the pharmacological properties that confer this early clinical benefit. Longer follow-up (eg, postoperative nights 2 and 3) would help clarify the duration of effect and underlying mechanisms.

Sixth, stratification by baseline PSQI scores. We appreciate the careful reading and would like to clarify that baseline PSQI was indeed included as a core covariate in the propensity score matching, and the two groups were well balanced after matching (SMD = 0.013). Furthermore, we performed subgroup analysis stratified by baseline PSQI score (<7 vs ≥7) and tested the interaction term between treatment and PSQI status. The results, presented in Figure 4, showed a significant treatment-by-PSQI interaction (P for interaction = 0.001). We recognize that this information was presented in graphical form and may not have been immediately apparent. We appreciate the opportunity to clarify this point.

In conclusion, we thank Dr. Rijuan Jin and Dr. Yijie Lou for their constructive comments, which have deepened the methodological concerns and provided valuable direction for future investigations. Our study provides preliminary evidence rather than definitive clinical benefit in patients with gastroscopy. However, we fully acknowledge the methodological limitations inherent to a retrospective observational design. Further prospective randomized controlled trials incorporating objective sleep monitoring, longer follow-up, and formal blinding validation are warranted to confirm these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
