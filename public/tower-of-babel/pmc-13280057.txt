Letter to the Editor Regarding: "OrthoKids and OrthoInfo Response to Training a Bilingual Artificial Intelligence Model on Pediatric Orthopaedic Resources to Enhance the Readability of Patient Education Materials"
Authors: Margaret Siobhan Murphy-Zane
Published: 2026

To the JPOSNA® Editors,

I read with interest the recent JPOSNA® article by Mauffrey et al., Training a Bilingual Artificial Intelligence Model on Pediatric Orthopaedic Resources to Enhance the Readability of Patient Education Materials (PEMs). I would like to offer a brief perspective for the authors and JPOSNA® readership. The editorial teams behind American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS)’s Orthoinfo.org and Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA)’s Orthokids.org are actively aware of and are engaging with artificial intelligence tools, and we are exploring their use in a thoughtful and measured way. We do not anticipate a wholesale transition to fully artificial intelligence (AI)-generated content but rather seek a balanced approach that incorporates these tools where they add value.

As the current pediatric section editor of OrthoInfo and committee chair of OrthoKids, I am proud of the education materials produced by the orthopaedic surgeons involved in these national committees over the past 2 decades. Our content has consistently been written in the way we explain information to patients: with clarity, accuracy, and an appropriate level of complexity. We have aimed for a sixth grade reading level. Recent AI tools have been useful for evaluating grade-level readability. However, to have this done accurately, each reviewed article needs to be reformatted separately, with pictures, headings, links, and punctuation like colons and dashes removed. Even so, different AI tools (Readable, ChatGPT, Gemini, CoPilot, etc.) will sometimes rate the same article at a different grade level. We have used ChatGPT to reformat and grade level articles, but the prompts need to be iterative and specific. All AI-generated text still requires careful human proofreading for accuracy. We regularly review content and readability on both sites. Currently, 84% of OrthoKids “conditions” articles are already written at an eighth-grade level or less. None are written at higher than a ninth-grade level. The process of reviewing and updating this content with peer review is time-intensive and ongoing.

The committees are also continuing to create new content. This year, OrthoKids has created a completely new and expanded fracture section, coming out in the next month and benchmarked at the sixth-grade reading level.

Against this backdrop, it is worth considering how the current readability literature fits in. According to PubMed, more than 22,000 readability papers have been published in the past 5 years. Much of this literature evaluates patient education materials (PEMs) by national organizations or well-known hospitals for adherence to a sixth-grade reading level. Several papers, like the one by Mauffrey et al. have uploaded existing online PEM text into ChatGPT (or another platform) to rewrite the text at the sixth-grade reading level. The results are variable and frequently fall well below the sixth-grade level. These studies also do not assess the contributions of pictures and videos, which are well known to enhance comprehension and retention. Pictures and videos are featured extensively in both OrthoKids and OrthoInfo.

It may be reasonable to reconsider whether the sixth-grade (or lower) benchmark remains universally appropriate. The readability literature consistently cites a single 2007 AMA monograph: Weiss, Health literacy and patient safety: help patients understand. This publication offered thoughtful guidance on communicating across literacy levels. While it acknowledged that the average U.S. adult reads at an eighth-grade level, it nevertheless suggested a sixth-grade reading level in the context of PEMs addressing life-threatening issues like medication instructions. Just as significantly, it recommended tailoring materials to the target audience and highlighted the importance of illustrations and videos. Weiss did not, of course, anticipate modern online resources or contemporary AI tools. Nearly 20 years later, families have many sources of medical information (good and bad). ChatGPT answers medical questions at a seventh-eighth-grade reading level. It would seem to make sense, then, that orthopaedic PEMs be benchmarked at about the same level.

Finally, to the authors of readability papers: your interest in patient education is genuinely appreciated. I would encourage you to step in from the outside and join the AAOS and POSNA committees engaged in the ongoing work of developing these educational resources. Collaboration will only strengthen the materials we provide for the families we all serve.

Sincerely,
