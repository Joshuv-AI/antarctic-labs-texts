Diagnostic performance and pathologic associations of the UroVysion assay in patients with upper tract lesions and negative urinary cytology: a large-scale real-world study of 1,256 patients
Authors: Longtao Zheng, Bao Guan, Zheng Wu, Yi Ding, Bei Zhang, Xuesong Li, Yanqing Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose The UroVysion assay is widely used for diagnosing upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC), but its value in patients with negative urinary cytology remains unclear. This study evaluated its diagnostic performance and pathological associations in this specific population.Methods This retrospective study included 1,256 patients with upper tract lesions on imaging, negative urinary cytology, and preoperative UroVysion testing. Pathological diagnosis served as the reference standard. Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between UroVysion positivity and high-grade disease, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), and muscle invasion (≥ pT2). Subgroup analyses were performed according to tumor size and location.Results Among the 1,256 patients, 1,007 (80.2%) had UTUC and 249 (19.8%) had benign lesions. The UroVysion assay showed a sensitivity of 66.9%, specificity of 82.7%, PPV of 94.0%, and NPV of 38.2%, and an overall accuracy of 70.1% for UTUC diagnosis. UroVysion positivity was independently associated with high-grade disease (OR = 2.10, p < 0.001), LVI (OR = 1.63, p = 0.023), and muscle invasion (OR = 1.32, p = 0.049). Interaction tests showed no significant heterogeneity by tumor size or tumor location.Conclusion In patients with upper tract lesions and negative cytology, the UroVysion assay provides complementary diagnostic information and is associated with adverse pathological features. No significant heterogeneity in these associations was observed according to tumor size or location.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00345-026-06876-2.Keywords: Diagnostic performance, Pathologic associations, Upper tract urothelial carcinoma, Urinary cytology, UroVysion assay

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) accounts for 5–10% of urothelial malignancies and is characterized by relatively aggressive biological behavior despite its rarity [1]. Radical nephroureterectomy remains the standard treatment, while kidney-sparing surgery has emerged as a viable option to preserve renal function for patients with low-risk UTUC [1]. Thus, accurate preoperative assessment is essential to guide individualized treatment decisions. Computed tomography urography (CTU) offers high sensitivity for tumor detection but provides limited accuracy for pathological staging [2, 3]. Urinary cytology, while highly specific, has a sensitivity of only 40–60% [4, 5]. Ureteroscopic biopsy can yield histological confirmation but carries a risk of sampling error, under-staging, under-grading, and intravesical recurrence [6, 7]. The UroVysion assay is a commercially available fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-based test that detects aneuploidy of chromosomes 3, 7, 17 and homozygous deletion of 9p21 in exfoliated urothelial cells, reflecting genomic instability associated with urothelial carcinoma [8]. Compared with cytology, the UroVysion assay provides objective molecular information and has demonstrated complementary diagnostic value in urothelial carcinoma [9–13]. Previous studies have also suggested potential associations between UroVysion positivity and adverse pathological characteristics [14–16].

Nevertheless, reported diagnostic performance of FISH has varied widely across studies, probably because of differences in patient risk profiles across cohorts. Most published studies have been limited by modest sample sizes and have typically compared UTUC patients with healthy controls. To date, no large-scale investigation has specifically focused on patients with upper tract lesions and negative cytology, a clinically challenging scenario where non-invasive adjunctive tools are most needed. To address this gap, we conducted a large, real-world study to systematically evaluate the diagnostic performance of the UroVysion assay and investigate its association with adverse pathological features in patients with upper tract lesions and negative urinary cytology.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at Peking University First Hospital and Beijing Miyun Hospital. A total of 1,309 patients with upper tract lesions on imaging, negative urinary cytology, and preoperative UroVysion testing were identified between January 2012 and June 2025 and assessed for eligibility. Additional inclusion criteria were surgery or ureteroscopic biopsy within one month after UroVysion testing and availability of sufficient diagnostic information to establish the final reference diagnosis. Patients who did not meet these inclusion criteria were excluded, as were those with prior or concurrent bladder cancer, contralateral UTUC, or incomplete clinical data. After exclusion of 53 patients, 1,256 patients were included in the final analysis. Among them, UTUC was confirmed in 1,007 patients, all of whom underwent definitive surgical resection. The remaining 249 patients comprised the non-cancer group, which included those with benign pathology after surgical resection and those with negative biopsy results who showed no evidence of disease progression on imaging during at least six months of follow-up. Tumor size and tumor location used in the multivariable and subgroup analyses were determined from preoperative imaging. Pathological features including tumor stage, histological grade, and lymphovascular invasion (LVI) were obtained from the final surgical pathology reports. Pathological staging followed the contemporaneous American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM classification, and histological grade was classified as low or high grade according to the World Health Organization 2004/2016 classification. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Protocol No.: 2025R0389; Date of approval: September 13, 2025) prior to its initiation and was conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Urinary cytology and UroVysion assay

Urine specimens were collected prior to surgery or diagnostic procedures. For urinary cytology, voided morning urine samples were obtained on three consecutive days, with a volume of at least 50 mL each time. Cytological assessment was independently performed by two experienced cytopathologists. Before The Paris System (TPS) was implemented, cytology was reported according to conventional institutional criteria. After TPS implementation, only “Negative for High-Grade Urothelial Carcinoma” (NHGUC) was classified as negative. Historical specimens were not retrospectively reclassified according to TPS. Patients were included only when all three consecutive cytology examinations were reported as negative according to the reporting system in use at the time.

The UroVysion assay was performed using the UroVysion kit (Vysis, USA) strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A single urine specimen was used for UroVysion testing prior to surgery or diagnostic procedures. According to established criteria, a positive UroVysion result was defined as meeting one of the following: (1) at least 4 of 25 morphologically abnormal cells showing gains of two or more probes; or (2) at least 12 cells showing homozygous deletion of 9p21. Evaluable specimens not meeting these thresholds were classified as negative. Specimens with assay failure or suspicious results underwent repeat urine sampling and UroVysion testing. All UroVysion assay results were interpreted by technicians blinded to clinical and pathological information.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies with percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To assess the diagnostic performance of the UroVysion assay, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between UroVysion positivity and adverse pathological features of UTUC. Interaction terms between UroVysion status and tumor size or tumor location were introduced into separate multivariable models to formally assess heterogeneity across subgroups. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diagnostic performance of the UroVysion assay

A total of 1,256 patients with upper tract lesions were included in this study (Fig. 1). Among them, 1,007 patients (80.2%) were diagnosed with UTUC (cancer group), and 249 patients (19.8%) had benign lesions (non-cancer group), including 173 diagnosed by biopsy and 76 by surgical resection. Two initial UroVysion assays failed and four yielded suspicious results, and all six patients ultimately obtained evaluable results after repeat testing. The diagnostic performance of the UroVysion assay for UTUC is summarized in Table 1. The overall sensitivity was 66.9% (95% CI: 64.0%–69.8%), specificity was 82.7% (95% CI: 77.5%–86.9%), PPV was 94.0% (95% CI: 92.0%–95.5%), NPV was 38.2% (95% CI: 34.2%–42.4%), and overall accuracy was 70.1% (95% CI: 67.5%–72.5%) (Fig. 2).

Among the 1,007 patients with UTUC, 947 patients underwent radical nephroureterectomy and 60 patients underwent segmental ureterectomy. Among the 76 surgically confirmed benign cases, 55 were UroVysion-negative and 21 were UroVysion-positive, corresponding to a specificity of 72.4%. Among the 173 patients in the biopsy-negative follow-up subgroup, 151 were UroVysion-negative and 22 were UroVysion-positive, corresponding to a specificity of 87.3%.

The 76 surgically confirmed benign lesions comprised inflammatory lesions (47 cases, 61.8%), polypoid hyperplasia (19 cases, 25.0%), tuberculosis (seven cases, 9.2%), hemangioma (two cases, 2.6%), and lymphoproliferative disorder (one case, 1.3%). Among the 21 surgically confirmed benign cases with false-positive UroVysion results, pathological diagnoses included inflammatory lesions (15 cases,71.4%), polypoid hyperplasia (five cases, 23.8%), and tuberculosis (one case, 4.8%).

Association between UroVysion Positivity and Pathological Features

From the total cohort, we further analyzed 1,007 patients with pathologically confirmed UTUC to evaluate the association between UroVysion positivity and pathological features. Clinical and pathological characteristics stratified by UroVysion result are presented in Table 2. The cohort comprised 498 male (49.5%) and 509 female (50.5%) patients, with ages ranging from 31 to 90 years. UroVysion positivity was observed in 674 patients (66.9%). Overall, 480 patients (47.7%) had non-muscle-invasive disease (< pT2), while 527 (52.3%) had muscle-invasive disease (≥ pT2); 133 patients (13.2%) had low-grade tumors and 874 (86.8%) had high-grade tumors; lymph node metastasis was present in 49 patients (4.9%), and LVI in 138 patients (13.7%). No significant differences were observed between the UroVysion-positive and UroVysion-negative groups with respect to age, tumor location, tumor size, multifocality, or N stage (all p > 0.05). However, the UroVysion-positive group exhibited higher proportions of adverse pathological features, including advanced tumor stage (p = 0.029), higher tumor grade (p < 0.001), and presence of LVI (p = 0.014).

Furthermore, we performed exploratory stratified analyses by tumor size and tumor location (Fig. 3). Using the 2-cm cutoff, UroVysion positivity was significantly associated with high-grade disease in both tumors ≤ 2 cm (OR = 2.97, 95% CI: 1.65–5.34, p < 0.001) and tumors > 2 cm (OR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.00-2.70, p = 0.049). For LVI, a significant association was observed in tumors ≤ 2 cm (OR = 3.98, 95% CI: 1.16–13.72, p = 0.029), but not in tumors > 2 cm. No significant association with muscle-invasive disease was observed within either size subgroup. Formal interaction tests did not demonstrate significant heterogeneity according to tumor size for high-grade disease (p for interaction = 0.128), LVI (p for interaction = 0.087), or muscle-invasive disease (p for interaction = 0.522) (Table S1).

When stratified by tumor location, UroVysion positivity was significantly associated with high-grade disease in ureteral tumors (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.63–5.37, p < 0.001) and tumors involving both the renal pelvis and ureter (OR = 3.63, 95% CI: 1.06–12.44, p = 0.040), but not in renal pelvic tumors. Associations with LVI and muscle-invasive disease did not reach statistical significance within the location subgroups after adjustment. Formal interaction tests did not show significant heterogeneity by tumor location for high-grade disease (p for interaction = 0.137), LVI (p for interaction = 0.769), or muscle-invasive disease (p for interaction = 0.803).

Discussion

Urinary cytology and FISH-based assays, including the UroVysion assay, are commonly used non-invasive approaches for evaluating UTUC, yet they differ fundamentally in their principles. Compared with cytology, the UroVysion assay provides objective molecular information and has demonstrated complementary diagnostic value in urothelial carcinoma [8–10]. Although previous diagnostic studies have reported the sensitivity and specificity of the UroVysion assay, their control groups mainly consisted of non-tumor patients or individuals undergoing routine medical check-ups. In clinical practice, however, this test is often used in patients with suspicious UTUC. Therefore, evaluating its diagnostic accuracy in this specific population is important for clarifying its complementary role in clinical evaluation. In this cohort, we found that the UroVysion assay exhibited favorable diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 66.9%, specificity of 82.7%, and accuracy of 70.1%. Notably, the PPV reached 94.0%, indicating that the UroVysion assay serves as a reliable confirmatory test in this selected population. However, the NPV was only 38.2%, indicating that a negative UroVysion result does not rule out UTUC. Therefore, for patients with negative UroVysion results but highly suspicious imaging findings, management decisions should still integrate CT urography features, ureteroscopy evaluation, and close follow-up.

The inclusion of patients with surgically confirmed benign lesions reflects the real-world diagnostic challenge of upper tract lesions. Among the 76 patients with benign final pathology, the predominant diagnoses were inflammatory lesions, polypoid hyperplasia, and tuberculosis, all of which had been considered clinically suspicious for UTUC before definitive pathological evaluation. These cases therefore represent clinically relevant mimickers of UTUC rather than healthy controls, which also distinguishes the present cohort from many previous diagnostic studies. Notably, specificity differed according to the reference standard used to define the non-cancer group. It was lower in the surgically confirmed benign group (55/76, 72.4%) than in the biopsy-negative follow-up subgroup (151/173, 87.3%). The latter subgroup contributed more to the overall specificity of 82.7%. Given the different reference standards and diagnostic pathways of the two subgroups, this difference should be interpreted cautiously and does not necessarily indicate a true difference in UroVysion performance.

Kidney-sparing surgery has become increasingly important in UTUC management over the past decade. According to the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, patients with low-risk UTUC may be considered for kidney-sparing approaches to preserve renal function. Nevertheless, discordance between preoperative assessment and final pathology remains a substantial clinical challenge: up to 20–30% of patients classified as low-risk preoperatively are ultimately found to have muscle-invasive or high-grade tumors, suggesting that they may not benefit from kidney-sparing surgery and may face an elevated risk of recurrence or progression. Thus, improving preoperative identification of patients with adverse pathological features remains an important clinical challenge. Within this context, urine FISH has increasingly been explored as an adjunctive tool for preoperative risk stratification, given its capacity to detect chromosomal aberrations characteristic of urothelial carcinoma. Su et al. reported that UroVysion was associated with muscle invasion and high-grade tumors in UTUC [14]. Xu et al. further found that UroVysion positivity was associated with adverse pathological features among patients considered low risk by conventional criteria [15]. In the present study, multivariable regression analyses similarly showed that UroVysion positivity was independently associated with high-grade disease, LVI, and muscle-invasive disease, with the strongest association observed for tumor grade. This stronger association with tumor grade may be biologically plausible because UroVysion detects chromosomal abnormalities reflecting genomic instability, which tends to be more pronounced in biologically aggressive urothelial tumors. However, the present study assessed the assay primarily as a binary positive/negative result and therefore cannot establish a direct mechanistic relationship.

However, whether these associations differ across clinically relevant UTUC subgroups remains insufficiently characterized [14–16]. Tumor size and tumor location are clinically relevant components of preoperative UTUC assessment. Leveraging our large cohort of 1,007 patients, we therefore performed exploratory stratified analyses to determine whether the pathological associations of UroVysion positivity were also observed across clinically relevant subgroups.

Tumor size was categorized using a 2-cm threshold consistent with the EAU risk-stratification framework. Using the 2-cm threshold, the association with high-grade disease was maintained across tumor-size categories, while formal interaction testing did not support significant effect modification by tumor size. Similarly, interaction testing provided no evidence that tumor location materially modified the associations between UroVysion positivity and the evaluated pathological features. For LVI and muscle-invasive disease, the subgroup patterns were less consistent, suggesting that these associations should be interpreted more cautiously. Overall, the most consistent pathological association of UroVysion positivity was with high-grade disease, including among tumors ≤ 2 cm. This finding may be clinically relevant because tumor size is an important component of contemporary UTUC risk assessment. UroVysion should not be considered a stand-alone tool for treatment selection or kidney-sparing surgery; however, it may provide complementary information when conventional preoperative assessment is inconclusive.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of the study carries an inherent risk of selection bias. Second, defining a reliable standard for the non-cancer group was challenging. For patients with negative biopsy results, we applied a strict inclusion criterion requiring at least six months of imaging follow-up with no evidence of disease progression. However, a very low probability of false-negative classification remains. Third, the high PPV and low NPV should be interpreted cautiously because predictive values are influenced by disease prevalence, which was high in this clinically selected cohort. Fourth, cytological reporting criteria evolved during the study period, and retrospective harmonization according to a single classification system was not feasible. Fifth, the study population was restricted to patients with radiologically detected upper tract lesions and negative urinary cytology. Therefore, the diagnostic performance observed in this cohort may not be generalizable to unselected patients undergoing evaluation for hematuria or to patients with positive urinary cytology. Further large-scale, prospective, multicenter trials are needed to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

In patients with upper tract lesions and negative cytology, the UroVysion assay has an overall accuracy of 70.1% for detecting UTUC, and is independently associated with adverse pathological features. No significant heterogeneity in these associations was demonstrated according to tumor size or location.
