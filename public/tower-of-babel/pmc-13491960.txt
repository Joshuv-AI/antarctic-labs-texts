Composite Plug-Removal Technology for Polymer Profile Control Wells in Sandstone Reservoirs
Authors: Linshuo Yan, Dingxue Zhang, Zilong He, Yong Wang, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the problems of high injection pressure, unclear plugging mechanisms, and poor performance of conventional stimulation and plugging-removal measures in polymer-containing profile-control wells of the Sha-3 reservoir in the Liuzan North Block, the plugging mechanism was investigated by X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and core flooding experiments. The results show that the plugging materials are composite plugging materials formed by the interaction of retained profile-control polymers in the reservoir with heavy components and clay minerals. These plugging materials are characterized by stable structures and complex compositions. Based on the characteristics of the plugging materials, a composite plug-removal agent system was optimized, and the corresponding treatment parameters were determined. The optimized plugging-removal system was composed of 0.5 wt % surfactant as an oil-washing agent, mud acid (12 wt % HCl + 2 wt % HF) as an inorganic dissolution agent, and 5 wt % JD-Y as a polymer degradation agent. This system exhibited high dissolution efficiency for different types of plugging components. After the addition of functional additives, including 1.0 wt % HS-3 corrosion inhibitor, 1.5 wt % EG-13 iron ion stabilizer, and 2.0 wt % LA5 clay stabilizer, the corrosion rate of the acid system decreased to 3.16 g·m–2 ·h–1, effectively reducing corrosion of metal equipment. The antiswelling rate reached 82.01%, and the iron-stabilizing capacity was 207.89 mg/mL, indicating excellent antiswelling performance and iron-stabilizing ability. When the composite plug-removal agent system was injected at the optimal dosage ratio of 1:1:3 and reacted at 90 °C for 4 h, both the degradation rate and permeability recovery exceeded 80%, and the core permeability increased by approximately 13 times. The proposed composite plug-removal agent system can effectively dissolve complex plugging materials formed during polymer flooding and provides a technical reference for plugging removal in sandstone reservoirs.

Introduction

The Sha-3 reservoir in the Liuzan North Block is a weakly cemented, unconsolidated sandstone reservoir. After long-term waterflooding development, it has entered a stage characterized by high water cut and rapid production decline. To maintain stable production and achieve efficient reservoir development, polymer flooding was introduced in this block as the primary enhanced oil recovery technique. − In field applications, deep profile control and flooding were implemented using an alternating slug system composed of cross-linked polymer and microspheres, which increased the overall recovery factor of the block by 4.1%, demonstrating significant effectiveness. However, with the continued implementation of profile control and flooding, injection pressure increased continuously, forcing a reduction in liquid injection intensity and leading to increasingly severe underinjection. Relevant literature indicates that similar well blocks commonly experience increased injection pressure and aggravated underinjection after polymer flooding, which seriously compromises oilfield development and production efficiency. −

For reservoir plugging induced after polymer flooding, chemical plugging removal is currently the most widely applied and effective method. − Zhang et al. systematically analyzed the mechanisms by which acidic solutions and oxidizing agents affect polyacrylamide-based polymer plugging, pointing out that acidic solutions merely cause the polymer chains to contract and entangle, thereby reducing viscosity, whereas oxidizing agents can achieve complete degradation by breaking the polymer molecular chains. This study charted a research direction for well unblocking through polymer injection; however, it was primarily based on a single polymer system in a laboratory setting and did not fully account for the synergistic effects of minerals, crude oil, inorganic salts, and various types of plugging agents in complex reservoirs. Wan et al. developed a polymer deblocking agent composed of an oxidizing agent, an acid, and a chelating agent. This agent effectively breaks down polymers primarily through the synergistic effects of disrupting the network structure and reducing viscosity, achieving dissolution rates ranging from 15.76% to 53.42%, resulting in a significant increase in core permeability. However, the acid and oxidizing agent may cause corrosion, secondary precipitation, or reservoir damage, and the long-term deblocking effectiveness and suitability for field applications still need to be evaluated. Wang et al. developed an alkali-activated sodium persulfate (PDS/NaOH) polymer deblocking system. This system primarily relies on the oxidative action of hydroxyl radicals to degrade polymer blockages, effectively restoring reservoir permeability while exhibiting a low corrosivity. In field applications, the peroxysulfate system may be affected by ions in formation water, reducing agents, and residual oil, leading to fluctuations in the oxidation efficiency; its long-term stability still requires further verification. Chen et al. found that polymer-driven wellbore blockages are characterized by the coexistence of inorganic precipitates and cross-linked polymers, resulting in a large blockage radius and a limited effectiveness of conventional acidizing treatments. Through an analysis of the blockage mechanism, they developed a method for identifying and evaluating the wellbore blockages. This study primarily focused on distinguishing blockage types and understanding the underlying mechanisms, but it provided a relatively limited evaluation of the suitability and penetration depth of different unblocking agent systems.

In the early stage, plugging removal treatments were conducted using a system composed of solid acid and a depolymerizing agent. However, the injection pressure of most wells did not decrease significantly after treatment, and the overall plug removal performance varied considerably among wells. Therefore, it is urgent to investigate the plugging mechanisms of profile control wells in this block, develop a plug removal system tailored to the identified plugging mechanisms, and optimize the corresponding treatment parameters. This would enable the establishment of a composite plug removal technology suitable for polymer-containing profile control wells in the block, thereby effectively improving the efficiency of waterflooding development.

Experiments

Materials and Equipment

Crude oil (the heavy fraction remaining after crude oil distillation), natural rock cores, and HCB polymer profile modifiersformed by the reaction of polyacrylamide (HPAM, relative molecular mass 2500 × 104, hydrolysis degree of 10–15%, viscosity of 1850 mPa·s in a 0.3 wt % aqueous solution) and a phenolic resin cross-linking system; DNM polymer nanospheres (prepared by AM/AMPS reverse emulsion copolymerization and modified with M-SiO2, with an average particle size of 162.2 nm and a swollen size of 5.29 μm); hydrochloric acid, hydrofluoric acid, HS-3 high-temperature corrosion inhibitor, EG-13 iron ion stabilizer, LA5 antiswelling agent, and JD-Y organic salt depolymerization agent, provided by the Jidong Oilfield Branch of China National Petroleum Corporation; simulated formation water with a salinity of 2304 mg/L (prepared in-house in the laboratory); sodium chloride and glacial acetic acid (Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.); octadecyl hydroxy sulfopropyl betaine (HSB1618) and Tween 80 (Shandong Yousuo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd.); potassium chloride and ammonium chloride (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.); calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, sodium carbonate, and sodium sulfate (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.).

An HH-4 constant-temperature water bath, an LC-NDJ-991 digital rotary viscometer, an LC-202-00 forced-air drying oven (Shanghai Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), a large-scale core flooding apparatus (Jiangsu Bocheng Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), an SU8000 scanning electron microscope (Shanghai Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.), a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Shanghai Erdi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), a Texas-500 interfacial tensiometer (Beijing Global Hengda Technology Co., Ltd.), and a HARKE-500C Kruss oil–water contact angle meter (Beijing Hake Experimental Instrument Factory) were used.

Experimental Methods

Wettability Test

Natural rock core sections were cut into regular shapes approximately 3–5 mm thick. After washing with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol, the sections were dried and subjected to a hydrophobic treatment. The sections were then placed in a drying oven at 60 °C until their mass remained constant; after cooling, the initial contact angle was measured. The core sections were then placed in a solution of the surfactant to be tested and soaked at 90 °C for 24 h. After removal, they were lightly rinsed, and the contact angle was measured again. The measurement was repeated three times for each concentration, and the average value was recorded.

Interfacial Tension Test

The spinning-drop method is highly sensitive to low interfacial tensions (10–3–10–1 mN/m). By imaging and measuring the stable shape of an oil droplet in the surfactant solution at a certain rotational speed, the interfacial tension between the surfactant solution and crude oil can be determined. Prior to the experiment, surfactant solutions of different concentrations were prepared, and both the crude oil and the surfactant solutions were preheated to 90 °C. The surfactant solution was injected into a rotating droplet generator, and a small amount of oil was added; measurements were then conducted at a constant temperature of 90 °C. Once the oil droplet morphology stabilized, images were captured, and the interfacial tension was calculated based on the droplet size and the density difference between the two phases. Each concentration was measured three times, and the results were averaged.

Degradation Rate Evaluation

Weigh 10 g of the aged profile-adjusting agent sample and add 20 g of a depolymerization agent solution of varying concentrations at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2. React the mixture in a sealed vessel at 90 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, filter and collect the residue, rinse it with deionized water, dry it, and weigh it. Calculate the degradation rate based on the mass loss. Repeat the measurement three times for each concentration and take the average of the degradation rates.

Dissolution Rate Evaluation

After grinding and sieving the rock samples, dry them at 60 °C until the mass remains constant. Weigh 3 g of rock powder, add 30 mL of acid solution of varying concentrations at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10, and react at a constant temperature of 90 °C for 4 h. After the reaction, filter to collect the residual rock powder, rinse with deionized water until nearly neutral, dry at 60 °C until the mass remains constant, and then weigh it. Calculate the dissolution rate based on the change in mass before and after dissolution. Conduct three parallel tests for each acid system and take the average value as the dissolution rate.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Core samples that had undergone profile adjustment and aging were selected, and transverse sections were cut along the axial direction at positions corresponding to 1/4, 2/4, and 3/4 of the core length. After lightly washing the rock samples with anhydrous ethanol, they were dried at 60 °C until their mass remained constant. The surface topography of the edge and central zones of the different cross sections was then observed.

Core Flooding Experiment

Acid Sensitivity

The washed and dried cores were evacuated under vacuum and saturated with KCl brine having the same salinity as the formation water. After soaking and aging for 40 h, they were set aside for use. Subsequently, the initial brine permeability of the cores was determined using KCl brine as the displacement medium. Inject 0.5 PV of soil acid (12% HCl + 2% HF) in the reverse direction, shut off the displacement pump to simulate well shutdown, and maintain a constant temperature of 90 °C for 1 h. After the reaction is complete, inject KCl brine in the forward direction; once the pressure stabilizes, measure the post-treatment brine permeability to evaluate changes in core permeability before and after acid treatment.

Core Preparation

The cores were first subjected to oil washing and drying, and their initial permeability was then measured using simulated formation water. Subsequently, the cores were vacuumed for 12 h and saturated with oil, followed by primary water flooding at a constant rate until the water cut reached 80%. A mixture of 0.25 PV polymer profile control agent and nanospheres was then injected, followed by 0.1 PV heavy components. The cores were aged at 90 °C for 48 h. After aging, subsequent water flooding was conducted until the water cut reached 90%, and the permeability after profile control and plugging was measured.

Plugging Removal of Plugged Cores

After profile adjustment, three types of unblocking agentsacid solution, depolymerizer, and surfactantwere injected into the core in sequence at different proportions. After shutting off the pump and allowing the reaction to proceed at 90 °C, water displacement was initiated at a constant rate of 0.2 mL/min. When the injection pressure exceeded 2p (where p is the stable pressure after profile adjustment), the injection was switched to constant-pressure mode. Changes in pressure and produced fluid during the displacement process were recorded, and the water displacement permeability after unblocking was measured once flow had stabilized. Results and Discussion

Plugging Mechanism

Crude Oil Component Analysis

Analysis of field data showed that the total content of resins and asphaltenes in the crude oil reached 15.60%, while the wax content was 18.60%, indicating a relatively high abundance of the heavy components. These heavy components are prone to deposition with temperature variations and can adhere to or coat the surfaces of the rocks and polymer profile control agents. This behavior further complicates the composition of the plugging materials, hinders the contact between chemical plug-removal agents and the plugging materials, increases the difficulty of plug removal treatment, and ultimately reduces plug removal efficiency.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

After the rock samples from the oil-bearing interval were washed and dried, they were crushed and ground. Then, 3–5 g of the sample was sieved (200 mesh) and placed into a sample pan, where it was compacted and leveled to ensure a flat test surface. Each set of samples was tested three times. The major mineral contents obtained from these repeated tests were analyzed, and the experimental results were averaged. The mineral contents are shown in Figure .

Analysis of the mineral composition characteristics and clay mineral contents of reservoir rock samples from the pay zone.

XRD mineral composition results indicate that the oil reservoir cores consist primarily of quartz and feldspar, with relatively low carbonate mineral content; the average clay mineral content is 5.9%, with a relatively complex composition dominated by illite and chlorite, accompanied by kaolinite and illite. Among these, the water-sensitive illite accounts for as much as 35%, while chloritewhich possesses strong adsorption propertiesaccounts for 30%. These minerals are prone to interacting with external fluids, leading to swelling, dispersion, or particle migration, which can cause pore-throat blockage.

Clay minerals are typically composed of silicate tetrahedra and aluminosilicate octahedra, with their structures containing a large number of Si–O and Al–O bonds. Relying solely on hydrochloric acid is insufficient to fully remove blockages caused by silicates and clay minerals; it is usually necessary to introduce HF-containing acid solutions to enhance the dissolution of aluminosilicate minerals. Once HF enters the pore throat, F– can form relatively stable fluorosilicate and fluoraluminum complexes with elements such as Si and Al, thereby promoting the dissolution and erosion of the clay mineral crystal framework.

Both montmorillonite and chlorite are aluminosilicate minerals; however, differences in their crystal structures, interlayer cation types, and specific surface areas result in variations in their acid dissolution behavior, and different types of clay minerals exhibit varying degrees of sensitivity to HF. In the case of montmorillonite, HF not only dissolves its aluminosilicate framework but also may affect the interlayer cations and swelling structure, thereby altering the dispersibility and mobility of the clay particles.

In the case of chlorite, reaction with HF releases large amounts of strongly complexing metal ions, such as Fe3+ and Al3+. These ions readily form precipitates upon interaction with inorganic anions and certain organic components, thereby exacerbating reservoir plugging. , Therefore, the acid system should be formulated to achieve the optimal concentration and supplemented with functional ionic additives to ensure the effective dissolution of clay minerals while minimizing the risk of secondary damage. Acid Sensitivity Assessment

Acid sensitivity generally reflects the impact on reservoir rock permeability resulting from changes in residual acid during the acidization process and its reactions with the reservoir rock minerals. The purpose of acid sensitivity evaluation tests is to determine whether the acid solution will cause damage to the reservoir and, if so, the extent of that damage, thereby providing a scientific basis for designing more reasonable and effective acidization treatment plans and analyzing the mechanisms of damage to the oil and gas reservoirs. Acid sensitivity evaluation tests were conducted in the laboratory using natural rock cores, and the results are shown in Figure .

Soil acid sensitivity evaluation curve.

As shown in Figure , the initial permeability of the core was 39.54 mD; after injection of soil acid, the permeability decreased to 31.85 mD, resulting in an acid-induced damage rate of 19.4%. The core exhibits a weak sensitivity to HF. When selecting HF for acidizing operations, the potential damage that acid sensitivity may cause to the formation must be considered. It is necessary to supplement the treatment with agents that suppress or mitigate HF sensitivity, thereby reducing the potential damage to the reservoir during the acidizing process.

Two regions, namely, the edge zone and the axial center zone, were selected from the cross sections at these three positions for scanning electron microscopy observation. Cross-sectional morphologies at three different positions after profile control: (a) the 1/4 position, (b) the 1/2 position, and (c) the 3/4 position.

Microstructures of different regions of the core.

The plugging was mainly concentrated within a concentric cylindrical zone at approximately 1/3 of the core sample’s radius. The content of the profile control agent generally decreased gradually from the center to the outer edge. In the axial center region of the core after profile control aging, the pore channels were almost completely filled with the polymer profile control agent. Although the outer edge region retained a relatively intact mineral and pore structure, a certain amount of plugging material was still present, and the degree of plugging was generally lower than that in the central region.

In addition to plugging the pore channels, the polymer entering the core also exhibits adsorption during the migration. Polymer adsorption is not a uniform monomolecular-layer adsorption process. − Due to factors such as polymer properties, fluid characteristics, and reservoir conditions, polymers tend to adhere to clay minerals and heavy components, forming stable composite blockages after the profile control agent has aged. This exacerbates the blockage of flow pathways, resulting in a reduced permeability and increased injection pressure.

Evaluation and Optimization of the Composite System

At present, a single chemical agent cannot effectively dissolve composite plugging materials formed by heavy components, polymers, and clay minerals. Therefore, targeted plugging removal still requires the combined use of multiple plug-removal agents that act on different components of the plugging materials. The multifunctional composite plug removal system consists of an organic salt depolymerizing agent, acid, and surfactant. The depolymerizing agent effectively restores the seepage capacity of plugged flow channels by decomposing the polymer profile control agent. The surfactant promotes the detachment of residual organic matter and asphaltenes by altering the rock wettability and reducing interfacial tension. Meanwhile, the acid effectively dissolves migrated inorganic clay minerals and other inorganic substances through acid dissolution and complexation with metal cations.

Heavy-Component Elution Capacity

Using simulated formation water as the control group, the prepared surfactant systems composed of HSB1618 and Tween 80 at concentrations of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0 wt % were tested for oil–water interfacial tension and contact angle at 90 °C. The results are shown in Figure .

Effect of surfactant concentration on contact angle and interfacial tension.

As shown in Figure , as the concentration of the surfactant system increases, both the oil–water interfacial tension and the wetting angle on the rock surface decrease significantly, but the rate of decrease gradually slows. When the surfactant concentration is 0.5 wt %, the interfacial tension between the oil and water phases can be reduced to 0.008 mN/m, reaching the order of 10–3 mN/m; the apparent contact angle on the rock surface decreases from an initial 134° to 52.5°, indicating that the rock surface transitions from a strongly oil-wetting state to a hydrophilic state, and the system exhibits good interfacial tension reduction and wetting reversal capabilities.

Surfactants reduce interfacial tension by adsorbing directionally at the oil–water interface, thereby lowering the interfacial free energy between the two phases; adsorption on the rock surface modulates the solid surface energy, causing the rock to transition from an oil-wetting to a water-wetting state, which facilitates the detachment of heavy components, colloidal matter, and asphaltenes from the pore walls. As the concentration continues to increase, the rate of decrease in interfacial tension and contact angle slows, indicating that adsorption at the interface and on the rock surface gradually approaches saturation. Therefore, a 0.5 wt % surfactant system can achieve ultralow interfacial tension and effective wetting reversal at a relatively low dosage, facilitating the entrainment and recovery of heavy components during subsequent displacement operations.

After shaking to ensure uniform mixing, the test tubes were placed in a thermostatic water bath at 90 °C and allowed to react for 48 h. After the reaction, the oil-washing capacity of each surfactant solution was observed and compared. The experimental results are shown in Figure .

Oil-washing performance of surfactants at different mass fractions: (a) simulated formation water without surfactant; (b) 0.1 wt % surfactant; (c) 0.3 wt % surfactant; (d) 0.5 wt % surfactant; and (e) 1.0 wt % surfactant.

When the surfactant concentration was within the range of 0.1 wt %–0.3 wt %, the oil-washing performance improved significantly with increasing concentration; however, part of the heavy oil still accumulated, indicating poor dispersion. When the concentration exceeded 0.5 wt %, the improvement in oil-washing efficiency gradually leveled off with further increases in concentration. At this concentration, the surfactant system was able to detach heavy components from the surface of the plugging materials, thereby increasing the contact area among the depolymerizing agent, acid, and plugging materials and providing favorable conditions for the effective dissolution of composite plugging materials.

Dissolution Performance of Inorganic Minerals

The acid dissolution results are shown in Figure . There are significant differences in the dissolution capacities of different acid systems on the rock sample. The dissolution rate in the pure HCl system increased only from 3.4% to 5.3%, indicating that the acid dissolution effect on this rock sample was limited; in the HF system, the dissolution rate increased from 16.36% to 31.56% as the HF concentration rose, indicating that HF significantly enhances the dissolution of silicates and clay minerals; the dissolution rate in the 12% HCl + HF system remained largely stable at 19.5%–21.5%, showing relatively mild and consistent dissolution characteristics.

Effect of concentration in different acid systems on the dissolution rate of rock samples.

Although hydrofluoric acid (HF) has a high dissolution efficiency, high concentrations of HF may lead to excessive reactions, posing risks of reservoir damage, such as rock matrix destruction, particle migration, or secondary precipitation. It must, therefore, be supplemented with functional additives to improve the control and safety of the acidizing process. During actual acidizing operations, the HF concentration should also be appropriately controlled to minimize the risk of side reactions while ensuring effective blockage removal. Although the dissolution capacity of the HCl+HBF4 system is lower than that of the hydrofluoric acid system, its reaction is milder and more controllable, making it a viable alternative for reducing the risk of damage caused by the rapid reaction of strong acids.

Performance Evaluation of the Clay Stabilizer

When externally introduced fluids enter the reservoir, they can readily hydrate clay minerals, inducing clay swelling and particle dispersion/migration, which consequently reduces the pore-throat radius. For reservoirs with high contents of water-sensitive minerals, such as mixed-layer illite/smectite, clay stabilizers are commonly introduced to inhibit clay hydration swelling and fines migration, thereby reducing the risk of pore-throat plugging and reservoir damage. − Take a 0.5 g sample of bentonite and place it in a centrifuge tube. Add simulated formation water, kerosene, and LA5 antiswelling agent at different concentrations in a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10. Shake thoroughly to ensure the bentonite is evenly mixed with the solution, then let it stand at 25 °C for 2 h. Record the swelling volume of the bentonite in the different solutions. Repeat the measurement three times for each group and calculate the average value. The experimental results are shown in Figure .

Trends in swelling inhibition rates under different chemical concentrations.

As shown in Figure , as the concentration of the LA5 antiswelling agent increased from 0.1 to 1.5 wt %, the antiswelling rate rose from 59.94% to 78.17%, representing a relatively significant increase; however, when the concentration was further increased from 1.5 to 2.5 wt %, the swelling inhibition rate rose from 78.17% to 82.01%, representing only a slight increase with no significant change observed, indicating that the swelling inhibition effect gradually stabilized.

The fact that the swelling inhibition rate increases with an increasing LA5 concentration indicates that LA5 can effectively suppress the hydration swelling of clay minerals. It adsorbs onto the clay surface, thereby reducing the hydration and dispersion capacities of clay particles. The smaller increase in the antiswelling rate at higher concentrations suggests that the effective active sites on the clay surface are approaching saturation. Considering both reservoir characteristics and the blocking mechanism, 2.0 wt % is determined to be the optimal concentration of the antiswelling agent to ensure a more stable antiswelling effect and effective utilization rate.

Performance Evaluation of the Corrosion Inhibitor

During injection, plug-removal agents inevitably come into contact with surface treatment equipment and transportation pipelines, among which acidic components can readily corrode metallic materials. − To prevent excessive consumption of acidic components, which could weaken the deblocking effect, and to reduce the risk to equipment and construction safety, corrosion rate tests were conducted on HS-3 corrosion inhibitor solutions of varying concentrations using the loss-on-drying method. A 20% HCl solution without the corrosion inhibitor was used as a blank control. N80 steel specimens were ground, cleaned, and degreased, then dried in an oven for 20 min. After the specimens cooled to room temperature, their precorrosion mass was weighed, and their dimensions were measured to calculate their surface area. A 20 wt % HCl solution was used as the corrosive medium, and different concentrations of the corrosion inhibitor were added to prepare the test solutions. The treated N80 steel specimens were completely immersed in the test solution at 90 °C and allowed to react at a constant temperature for 4 h. After the reaction, the specimens were rinsed sequentially with distilled water and anhydrous ethanol, then dried. Once cooled, they were weighed, and the corrosion rate was calculated based on the mass loss before and after the reaction. Three experiments were conducted for each concentration; the corrosion rates were calculated separately and averaged. The experimental results are shown in Figure .

Corrosion rates of N80 steel specimens at different corrosion inhibitor concentrations.

As shown in Figure , when no HS-3 corrosion inhibitor was added, the corrosion rate of the N80 steel specimen in the acid solution was relatively high, at 19.01 g·m–2·h–1; after adding the corrosion inhibitor, the corrosion rate decreased rapidly. When the corrosion inhibitor concentration was increased to 1.0 wt %, the corrosion rate had dropped to 3.16 g·m–2·h–1, representing a significant reduction. Upon further increasing the corrosion inhibitor concentration to 1.2 wt %, the rate of change in the corrosion rate decreased markedly, remaining stable at approximately 3 g·m–2·h–1; this indicates that the corrosion inhibition performance of the system had approached a stable state at this point.

The addition of the corrosion inhibitor significantly reduced the corrosion rate of the N80 steel specimen in the acid solution and effectively suppressed the corrosive action of the acid on the metal surface. The corrosion inhibitor molecules adsorb onto the steel surface and form a protective layer, reducing direct contact between corrosive components in the acid solutionsuch as H+ and Cl–and the metal substrate, thereby inhibiting the metal dissolution process. When the concentration exceeded 1.0 wt %, the corrosion rate decreased and leveled off, indicating that the adsorption-based protective effect on the metal surface had gradually reached saturation; further increasing the corrosion inhibitor dosage would yield only limited improvement in corrosion inhibition. The concentration of the HS-3 corrosion inhibitor was therefore set at 1.0 wt %.

Performance Evaluation of the Iron Stabilizer

To prevent Fe3+ and Fe2+ from combining with anions to form insoluble precipitates and causing secondary reservoir damage, iron stabilizers are generally used to complex iron ions with different valence states into soluble complexes, thereby preventing their precipitation reactions with other substances. − A 20 mL sample of the iron ion stabilizer was taken, dissolved in distilled water, and diluted to a final volume of 500 mL to prepare the test sample solution. Transfer 10, 25, and 35 mL of the test sample solution, respectively, add the corresponding volumes of Fe3+ standard solution, stir thoroughly, adjust the pH to 5–6 using a 5 wt % sodium carbonate solution, and heat to boiling. Once boiling has ceased, observe the clarity of the solution. If it remains clear, continue adding 0.5–1.0 mL of Fe3+ standard solution, and repeat the procedure until the system becomes slightly turbid. The total volume of Fe3+ standard solution added before slight turbidity appears was recorded to evaluate the stabilizing capacity of the iron ion stabilizer. The experimental results for evaluating the iron-stabilizing capacity of the EG-13 iron ion stabilizer at different concentrations are shown in Figure . Each concentration of the iron ion stabilizer was tested three times, and the results were averaged.

Iron-stabilizing capacity of EG-13 at different concentrations.

As shown in Figure , the addition of EG-13 significantly improved the system’s ability to stabilize Fe3+. When the EG-13 concentration was 0.1 wt %, the Fe3+ stabilization capacity was 2.52 mg/mL. As the EG-13 concentration increased, the Fe3+ stabilization capacity rose significantly; when the concentration reached 1.0 wt %, the stabilization capacity increased to 207.89 mg/mL. Within this concentration range, the stabilizing effect of EG-13 on Fe3+ was notably enhanced. When the concentration was further increased to 1.5 wt %, the stabilization capacity showed little change.

During acidification, Fe3+ is prone to hydrolysis and precipitation due to the rising pH, which can lead to pore blockage and secondary damage. The addition of a stabilizer enhances the stability of Fe3+ in solution and reduces the risk of iron precipitation.

In addition to reacting with rock minerals in the near-wellbore zone, the soil acid also reacts with polymers to form granular precipitates that deposit in the pore channels, blocking the remaining narrow throats. The acid’s ability to dissolve and clear blockages is less than the blocking effect caused by the newly formed precipitates, resulting in increased pressure. Upon switching to surfactants or depolymerizing agents, permeability also decreased to varying degrees, demonstrating severe incompatibility between the surfactants, depolymerizing agents, and the acid solutiona finding consistent with the results of static compatibility tests.

Because of the poor compatibility among the fluids injected into the well, contact between the three unblocking agents during the injection process tends to generate a large amount of insoluble precipitate, which deposits within the flow channels and causes secondary contamination of the core. Therefore, it is proposed to place the acid solution at the rear of the unblocking packer and insert a barrier packer between the incompatible unblocking agents.

Effect of Reaction Time on Plug-Removal Performance

While keeping the dosage ratios of the three deblocking agents constant, different reaction time gradients were set for the depolymerization agent, surfactant, and acid solutions, respectively. The concentration gradients were determined based on the trends in reaction performance observed in preliminary experiments to ensure that the selected range covered the optimal reaction interval. The relationship between different deblocking times and the deblocking rate is shown in Figure .

Effect of different reaction times on plug-removal performance.

The plug-removal performance of the depolymerizing agent improved significantly with increasing reaction time. When the reaction time was 1 h, the plug-removal efficiency was negative, indicating that the polymer profile control agent had not been completely degraded. The residual polymer could readily plug small pore throats, resulting in a slight decrease in permeability. When the reaction time exceeded 4 h, the plug-removal efficiency became nearly stable, reaching approximately 70%. Therefore, to effectively disrupt the polymer network structure, the reaction time of the depolymerizing agent should be controlled within the range of 4–5 h. The surfactant mainly removes heavy components through detachment and mobilization. Within 1–5 h, the plug-removal efficiency increased slowly with increasing reaction time; however, when the reaction time exceeded 5 h, no further significant improvement in plug-removal efficiency was observed. After surfactant molecules come into contact with heavy components attached to the surfaces of rocks and polymers, a reaction time of 4–5 h is still required to maximize their interfacial modification effect. Within 0–4 h, the plug-removal efficiency gradually increased as the acid system advanced. However, when the reaction time was extended beyond 5 h, the plug-removal efficiency decreased markedly. This decline was attributed to excessive acidification, which caused sparingly soluble salts or impurities in the rock framework to detach and migrate, thereby plugging narrow pore throats and inducing a secondary plugging.

At the beginning of the reaction, the chemical concentration is high, providing ample sites of contact on the surface of the blockage, resulting in a relatively fast dissolution or degradation rate. As the reaction proceeds, the active components are gradually consumed, the reaction rate decreases, and the system enters a slow-reaction phase. This phase should be appropriately extended to evaluate the chemical’s sustained effectiveness and the final unblocking result. − Therefore, to fully leverage the synergistic effects of the three chemical unblocking agents and achieve optimal unblocking results, the optimal reaction time for unblocking the plug was ultimately determined to be 4 h.

Final Plug-Removal Performance under Different Plug-Removal Schemes

Experimental results showed that the plug-removal efficiency varied significantly among different plug-removal schemes. In groups (a) and (b), the priority injection of depolymerization agents effectively dissolved the polymers, resulting in a pressure drop of approximately 0.15 MPa and a final unclogging rate of 46.94%. The relatively poor plug-removal performance of group (a) was attributed to the fact that incompletely decomposed polymers still encapsulated heavy components and clay minerals. After acid dissolution, the residual materials deteriorated, which adversely affected the subsequent oil-washing action of the surfactant and consequently reduced the overall plug-removal performance. Groups (c) and (d) adopted schemes in which the surfactant was injected first. In these schemes, some surfactant molecules not only contact the heavy components but also adsorb onto the polymer surface. Because of compatibility effects, the subsequently injected acid reacted with these adsorbed surfactant molecules, thereby compromising the overall plug-removal performance. As a result, the final plug-removal efficiency was only 13.97%, which was 67.85% lower than that of group (d). When the acid was injected at the final stage to dissolve residual inorganic impurities in the flow channels, as in groups (b) and (d), the plug-removal efficiency exceeded 80%, indicating a significant improvement in the overall plug-removal performance.

The above results indicate that group (b) achieved a higher permeability enhancement factor and exhibited a stronger plug removal capability for cores with more severe plugging, making it the most effective slug scheme. The pressure reduction was approximately 0.24 MPa, and both the plug-removal efficiency and permeability recovery exceeded 80%. The core permeability increased approximately 13.36 times.

The main component of composite plugs is a polymer profile control agent, whose network structure effectively encapsulates, adheres to, and retains heavy components and clay minerals; therefore, the depolymerization agent is the key component for removing the plug. If the acid solution is injected first, then the polymer that has not been completely decomposed will continue to encapsulate heavy components and clay minerals. Although the acid solution can dissolve some inorganic impurities, it tends to cause corrosion, degradation, or structural shrinkage of the residual polymer, thereby affecting the subsequent stripping and elution of heavy oil by the surfactant and leading to a decrease in the overall deblocking effectiveness. If surfactants are injected first, then some surfactant molecules will not only come into contact with heavy-phase components but also adsorb onto the polymer surface. Due to compatibility issues, the subsequent acid solution may react adversely with the residual system, thereby weakening the overall deblocking effect. −

By injecting the depolymerization agent first to disrupt the polymer network structure, the strength of the main blockage is reduced, and the encapsulated heavy oil components and clay minerals are released, thereby increasing the contact points for subsequent chemicals; followed by the interleaved injection of an oil-stripping agent, which, by reducing oil–water interfacial tension and through emulsification and dispersion, detaches heavy oil components adhering to the polymer surface, thereby improving the wettability and flowability of the permeation channels; finally, the acid solution is injected last to dissolve residual carbonates, clay minerals, and other inorganic impurities within the channels, further expanding the permeation channels and reducing secondary deposition. This synergistic approach of “depolymerization first, oil washing second, and acid dissolution last” not only prevents adverse degradation caused by direct contact between the acid solution and the polymer but also fully leverages the targeted effects of the depolymerization agent, oil washing agent, and acid solution on different plugging components, thereby significantly improving the overall removal efficiency of composite plugging materials.

Field Application

The S reservoir in the Liuzan Oilfield is characterized by medium porosity and medium permeability, with an average porosity of 19.5% and an average permeability of 265 × 10–3 μm2. The reservoir exhibits favorable physical properties. It is a layered, block-type lithologic structural reservoir located at a depth of 2,500–3,300 m, with an average formation temperature of 90 °C. The oil-bearing core consists primarily of quartz and feldspar, with an average clay mineral content of 5.9%. The mineral composition is relatively complex, dominated by illite-micasite interlayers and chlorite, accompanied by kaolinite and illite. After prolonged development, the reservoirs have all exhibited varying degrees of fluid deficiency and declining oil production capacity.

Following a previous general single-stage plug injection using 100 m3 of composite acid, daily fluid production increased from 2.1 m3 to 14.4 m3 but declined rapidly, while daily gas production dropped from 13 m3 before the treatment to 0 m3, indicating poor unplugging results; after adopting a composite unblocking system supplemented with antiswelling agents, iron ion stabilizers, and corrosion inhibitorswhich suppressed clay swelling, secondary precipitation, and residual fluid retentionwhile simultaneously controlling injection volume and reaction residence time, the fluid was promptly flushed out after treatment. This reduced the retention of residual chemicals and reaction byproducts in the wellbore throat, thereby lowering the risk of large-scale particle detachment and migration. In blocks L1 and L2, 28 wells were drilled in block L1, of which 26 were productive; in block L2, 74 wells were drilled, of which 66 were productive, resulting in a cumulative increase in oil production of 38,029.39 t.
