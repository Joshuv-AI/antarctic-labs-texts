When Autonomy Goes Rogue: Preparing for Risks of Multi-Agent Collusion in Social Systems
Qibing Ren, Sitao Xie, Longxuan Wei, Zhenfei Yin, Junchi Yan, Lizhuang Ma, Jing Shao

Abstract
Recent large-scale events like election fraud and financial scams have shown how harmful coordinated efforts by human groups can be. With the rise of autonomous AI systems, there is growing concern that AI-driven groups could also cause similar harm. While most AI safety research focuses on individual AI systems, the risks posed by multi-agent systems (MAS) in complex real-world situations are still underexplored. In this paper, we introduce a proof-of-concept to simulate the risks of malicious MAS collusion, using a flexible framework that supports both centralized and decentralized coordination structures. We apply this framework to two high-risk fields: misinformation spread and e-commerce fraud. Our findings show that decentralized systems are more effective at carrying out malicious actions than centralized ones. The increased autonomy of decentralized systems allows them to adapt their strategies and cause more damage. Even when traditional interventions, like content flagging, are applied, decentralized groups can adjust their tactics to avoid detection. We present key insights into how these malicious groups operate and the need for better detection systems and countermeasures. Code is available at https://github.com/renqibing/MultiAgent4Collusion .

. As shown in Figure

However, OASIS does not support collective behavior between agents. To fix this, we add communication channels within groups so agents can share memories and reflections, allowing them to learn from each other. Using these channels, we propose two types of coordination: centralized and decentralized. In decentralized coordination, agents cooperate by observing and imitating each other, while in centralized coordination, a “leader” assigns tasks to the agents.

With this framework, we can answer a key question: does multi-agent collusion really cause more harm than individual agents acting alone? Interestingly, we observe a “phase transition” as the number of agents increases. When just one malicious agent is added to the system, misinformation decreases. However, once more than 10 malicious agents are involved, they significantly increase misinformation spread. In our experiments, we control for the total number of interactions the malicious agents have, regardless of the setting. We think this phase transition happens because a single malicious agent’s interactions with the recommendation system reinforce the spread of content it prefers, overlooking others. In contrast, multiple agents, due to their diverse actions, cause much greater harm. This finding shows the importance of studying multi-agent systems.

This initial observation leads us to explore another key trait of agents: adaptability. Specifically, we want to know if agents can adapt to changes in the environment, such as new intervention measures. Beyond traditional content moderation, we find that malicious agents can play a "cat-and-mouse" game with platform operators. For example, when warning labels are added to misleading posts, the agents adapt by creating more subtle posts instead of continuing to comment on flagged content. After seeing the ineffectiveness of traditional content moderation, we suggest a new method: detecting malicious behavior by analyzing the agents’ actions, which enjoys good detection accuracy. As shown in Figure
1

Using clustering analysis, we find that subgroups of malicious agents emerge in the network. This insight could help inform strategies to reduce the risk.

In summary, to anticipate the future harms from malicious multi-agent systems, we provide an open-source framework in which agents can self-evolve and collaborate, and we present experiments yielding early evidence of the dangers while pointing toward detection and mitigation strategies. Hopefully, our study can give actionable insights for platform operators and policymakers.

2
Threat Model

Understanding the threat of collusion in multi-agent systems is essential for evaluating system robustness and designing effective safeguards. This section defines collusion, outlines adversarial goals across two application environments, and describes the constraints and knowledge assumptions applied to malicious agents.

Definition of Collusion.

Collusion occurs when two or more agents secretly coordinate their actions in ways that deviate from the system designer’s intended rules. This cooperation aims to subvert the system’s goals—such as fairness, safety, or efficiency—and benefits the colluding parties at the expense of others. Such coordination may be explicit (e.g., through direct messaging or centralized control) or tacit (e.g., emerging from shared incentives or behavioral adaptation without direct communication)
[
17
]
.

Adversarial Goals in Two Environments.

We consider two real-world inspired environments where agent collusion can significantly impact outcomes:

the
•
t
Misinformation. Malicious agents aim to spread false or misleading content on topics such as politics or public health (e.g., COVID-19), increasing exposure and acceptance through coordinated posting, engagement boosting, or timing strategies.

the
•
t
E-commerce. Colluding agents—acting as buyers and/or sellers—manipulate platform outcomes to boost fraudulent sellers or harm competitors. Typical attacks include fake reviews, inflated transaction volumes, negative review brigading, and fake refunds. Their goals are to increase: Total Sales Profit, Total Sales Volume, Conversion Rate, Ratings and Likes.

Constraints on Malicious Agents.

To maintain realism, we impose the following constraints:

the
•
t
Behavioral Limits: Malicious agents cannot act with abnormal frequency or complexity that would make them trivially detectable.

the
•
t
Interaction Boundaries: Agents can only interact via the platform’s standard communication mechanisms. They cannot tamper with or directly control other agents’ prompts or behaviors.

the
•
t
System Access: Malicious agents cannot modify the system infrastructure or underlying mechanisms (e.g., recommendation engines).

the
•
t
Population Ratio: The proportion of malicious agents is limited to a reasonable minority.

Adversarial Knowledge.

Malicious agents share the same observation space as benign agents and do not have access to internal system details, such as algorithm parameters or platform moderation logic. They may infer behavioral patterns through interaction, and are allowed to share information within their group, enabling decentralized coordination. All knowledge acquisition is bounded by the same interface and APIs available to all agents.

3
Preliminaries: OASIS

OASIS is a scalable, open-source social media simulator that employs LLM-based agents to model realistic, large-scale interactions on platforms like Twitter or Reddit. It supports up to one million users through 23 actions, such as posting, commenting, and following, and uses interest-based and hot-score recommendation algorithms to simulate content discovery and engagement. As illustrated in Figure
1
, OASIS comprises three core components: the Environment Server, Recommendation System and Agent Module. During the registration phase, agents are initialized with personalized profiles, roles, and guidelines. In the simulation phase, at each timestep, the recommendation system provides personalized posts, LLMs select actions based on agent memory, and agents act synchronously to update the global state.

While OASIS serves as a robust testbed for social media experiments, its default setup does not fully align with certain threat models. At the individual level, agents exhibit short-sighted behavior due to limited long-term reflection and learning from feedback. At the group level, it lacks support for collusion or coordinated actions. To address this, we extend OASIS with features like shared communication channels, group memory, and peer reflections to enable malicious multi-agent coordination, with details provided in the Section
4
.

4
Construction of Self-Evolving Multi-Agent Collusion Framework

This section introduces our framework for simulating self-evolving multi-agent collusion. The framework consists of three core components: (1) coordination mechanisms, (2) adaptive behavior evolution, and (3) platform-level intervention strategies. Together, these components support realistic modeling of dynamic, strategic collaboration among malicious agents.

Coordination Mechanisms.

To simulate realistic malicious collaboration, we implement two distinct coordination styles within agent groups: centralized and decentralized. These coordination strategies differ in how tasks are assigned and how information is shared among agents.

the
•
t

Centralized Coordination:
A designated leader agent dynamically determines group-level sub-tasks based on the overall progress and current environment state (e.g., which misinformation topics have low engagement). These sub-tasks—such as amplifying specific types of posts or targeting particular user groups—are then broadcast to the group. The leader communicates with currently active malicious agents using a broadcast-based information-sharing mechanism, instructing them on which actions to perform. This structure supports fast and consistent alignment within the group.

the
•
t

Decentralized Coordination:
In this setting, there is no explicit leader. Each agent implicitly collaborates with others using local cues. Specifically, when an agent receives a batch of posts from the recommendation system, it can identify which posts were authored or engaged with by peer malicious agents (e.g., via embedded identity markers or metadata). Based on this neighborhood-based observation, agents decide whether and how to participate in coordinated actions such as amplifying or replying to those posts. This approach supports more flexible, scalable collaboration without centralized control.

Behavior Evolution through Adaptability.

To enable agents to improve their behavior over time, we incorporate adaptability into two key stages of the agent lifecycle: decision making and reflection.

the
•
t

Decision Making with Feedback.

In each time step, agents receive environment feedback to guide their decisions. For instance, in the misinformation environment, agents observe metrics such as post engagement (likes, retweets, topic coverage). Since the group’s objective is to maximize spread, agents prioritize low-engagement posts to increase coverage and harm potential. This dynamic attention mechanism helps agents act more efficiently and collectively optimize group impact. Figure
1
illustrates how this feedback loop is integrated into the decision-making process.

the
•
t

Reflection Updates.

After performing actions, agents enter a reflection phase in which they evaluate the outcomes of their past behaviors. They analyze whether their previous decisions effectively contributed to the group objective. Reflection is based on post changes, action history, and social feedback. Each agent generates new insights—called reflections—which update its strategy for future decisions. This mechanism is iterative: agents refine their strategies over time, leading to more synchronized and adaptive group behaviors. Since agents take different exploration paths, their reflections diverge, and through coordination (explicit or implicit), they share experiences and evolve collaboratively.

Platform-Level Intervention Design.

To combat malicious coordination, we simulate platform-side mitigation strategies. Initially, we implement standard countermeasures such as prebunking and debunking. However, as shown in Section
5
, these static strategies are often insufficient against evolving collusive behavior.

To improve detection and intervention, we propose an agent-level monitoring mechanism. By analyzing an agent’s action trajectory—especially the patterns of content it promotes—we infer its likely intent and judge whether it is acting maliciously.

5.1
Agent Setup and Simulation Environment

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments are based on a default configuration: 900 benign agents and 100 malicious agents, with agent activation modeled as a Bernoulli process (
p=0.02
). Agents follow random initial social connections and possess diverse personality profiles generated using the Big Five framework. We mainly used the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model for our experiments. Appendix
D.6
also shows the results from the LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct model. All experiments are repeated 10 times, with mean and standard deviation reported to ensure statistical validity. Full hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix
C.1
.

Agent Generation.
Agents are defined by two key components.
1) Demographics:
Each agent is assigned a gender and an age between 18 and 65, sampled from a uniform distribution.
2) Personality:
Agents are initialized with Big Five personality traits, drawn from a normal distribution. This approach ensures behavioral diversity, which is essential for simulating realistic social interactions.

Datasets.

Misinformation:
We utilize the Fakenewsnet dataset
[
21
]
, which contains news articles, metadata, and user engagement context from real-world social media platforms.
E-commerce Fraud:
Additionally, we construct a synthetic dataset that simulates common fraudulent behaviors, including fake reviews, inflated sales, and misleading product descriptions.

Evaluation Metrics.
Each scenario adopts specific metrics to quantify harm:
1)Misinformation:
First, likes and reposts are used to measure visibility and reach; Second, sentiment analysis on comments is conducted to assess benign users’ emotional reactions, categorizing them as positive, negative, or neutral.
2) E-commerce Fraud:
The total sales and profit of products sold by malicious agents are tracked, indicating the economic success of fraudulent tactics. It is worth mentioning that, in order to measure the harmfulness of the bad users’ group, we only count the actions taken by benign users on posts or products published by bad users in both scenarios. Our experiment did not fix the random number seed and calculated the 95% confidence interval.

5.3
Can the System Adapt to Intervention Measures?

Intervention Measures.
To investigate the adaptability of malicious agent groups to dynamic environments, we test three key interventions:

the
•
Pre-bunking
: Benign agents are exposed in advance to factual content that anticipates and preempts common misinformation themes.

the
•
De-bunking
: Corrections are issued post hoc when misinformation is detected. We added a warning message to all contents made by malicious agents when the system reached halfway through its operation.

the
•
Banning:
We divided 1000 agents into 10 groups and periodically used the GPT-4 detector to examine the agents within a group. Malicious agents identified by our GPT-4 detector are removed from the system. The average accuracy for the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, and 10th detections is approximately 94.7%, 93.8%, 92.6%, 91.9%, and 91.3%, respectively.

Adaptation Behaviors Analysis.
We first compare the effectiveness of traditional content moderation methods with our agent detector. Then, we conduct in-depth observations on how agents adapt to the pressures of intervention measures.

the
•
Effectiveness of Various Interventions.
We evaluate the three intervention methods in our two tasks. First, to simulate the upper bound of debunking, during the middle of the simulation, we apply debunking by marking 100% of bad posts with a warning. As shown in Figure
5(a)
and
5(b)

Moreover, pre-bunking also shows some effectiveness, but debunking is stronger than pre-bunking. This makes sense because our debunking method directly tells users which posts are good and which are bad. Pre-bunking, on the other hand, encourages users to think critically and infer the truthfulness of posts, which is harder than debunking.

Regarding banning, we apply our GPT-4 detector every 10 time steps to identify and ban users flagged as malicious. Our agent-level auditing starts from the beginning and produces better results than pre-bunking. Note that the performance of debunking in our experiments is ideal, with 100% accuracy in detecting bad posts, while in the real world, there may be some differences. So, even though banning is weaker than debunking, it still shows potential in reducing risks.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel framework to study the risks of collusion in multi-agent systems (MAS), focusing on malicious agents in high-stakes environments like misinformation spreading and e-commerce fraud. We find that both the number of agents and coordination methods significantly impact the effectiveness of malicious behavior, with decentralized coordination proving particularly harmful. Our results also show that traditional intervention methods fail as agents adapt, posing new challenges for regulation. By providing tools for detecting malicious behavior at both agent and network levels, we offer insights for platform operators to counteract such risks. Ultimately, our work highlights the need for more advanced models and interventions to safeguard against coordinated malicious actions in MAS.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, our simulations focus on two environments, so expanding to others, like financial markets, would offer a broader understanding. Second, the model assumes rational agent behavior, while real-world agents may exhibit more complex, emotion-driven decision-making. Third, we haven’t fully accounted for the influence of external factors, such as platform governance or regulatory actions, which could affect outcomes. Lastly, while we propose methods for detecting malicious behavior, these tools need further refinement for large-scale systems. Further research is needed to enhance these models and detection techniques to address the evolving nature of collusion in MAS.

Broader Impacts

Our work has both positive and negative societal implications. On the positive side, it helps identify risks from coordinated malicious AI behaviors, guiding mitigation strategies in areas like misinformation and fraud. However, the same capabilities can be misused for harmful purposes, such as spreading disinformation or committing large-scale fraud. Additionally, AI systems may unintentionally amplify harmful content or cause unfair outcomes, raising concerns around fairness, privacy, and security.

To mitigate these risks, we propose controlled releases of the technology, development of defenses like improved content moderation, and continuous monitoring of system behavior over time to ensure responsible use and minimize harm.

Safety of Multi-agent System

Most previous work on multi-agent system safety looks at settings where all agents share the same goal. These studies test what happens when a malicious agent is added—can it break the teamwork of the good agents? For example, PsySafe
[

[

The environments are also simple, such as Python or Bash command-line interfaces. But in real life, people have different goals and personal interests. Our work looks at more realistic social settings, where each agent (like a human) acts based on its own goals. We explore how safety risks change in these cases, especially when malicious agents try to spread misinformation or commit fraud in large, human-like systems. Unlike past work that mainly focuses on breaking cooperation

We are more interested in how a group of agents—who share the same hidden goal—learn to work together and adapt to changing environments. Appendix B
Details of Self-Evolving Multi-Agent Collusion Framework

B.1
Individual-Level Scaffolding.

Beyond coordination and adaptability, we equip each agent with additional scaffolding to enhance its reasoning and memory capacity.

the
•
t

Long-Term Memory.

Agents maintain a structured long-term memory that includes past observations, actions, reflections, and selected outcomes. This memory allows them to reason over accumulated experience without overloading the prompt context. Only relevant memory segments are retrieved during decision-making.

the
•
t

Grounded Reflection.

Reflections are stored as part of memory and include high-level inferences about the effectiveness of past actions. This helps agents generalize beyond surface patterns and avoid overfitting to specific contexts.

the
•
t

System Prompt Design.

Each agent is initialized with a system prompt that encodes general priors and role-specific instructions. The prompt includes user profile, action space, group-level progress, personal and shared reflections, and environmental context.

Appendix C
Detailed Setups of Our Experiments

C.1
General Configurations

Activation probability distribution

In OASIS, each agent has an activation probability that determines whether it acts in a given time step. In our experiment, the activation probability of the user follows a binomial distribution, where at each time step, there is a 0.1 probability of being set to 0.2, and in all other cases, the activation probability is set to 0. Therefore, the mean of the activation probability is 0.02.

Relationship Network connection distribution

The relationship network uses the Erdős-Rényi random graph model, where the probability of an edge existing between any two nodes in the graph is 0.1.

Computation Resources

We used 16 A100 GPUs with 80GB of memory to conduct our experiments, and the model was deployed using vLLM.

# GUIDE for your behavior

When you see new information, pause and ask: **Who’s sharing it?** Is the source trustworthy? Watch for **common misinformation tactics**, like emotional headlines or selective facts. # Comment on the same post

[Important] Warning: This post is controversial and may provoke debate. Please read critically and verify information independently.

Agent auditing

Under Banning, we use GPT detector to detect the nature of a user based on the user’s recent behavior. If a user is detected as harmful by the detector, its subsequent actions will be prohibited.

Specifically, we first have the GPT detector generate a summary based on the content of posts and comments they have recently created, and then provide an output of either “benign” or “harmful” based on the content of the summary. The prompt used by the GPT detector is as follows:

PROMPT FOR DETECTION

# Prompt for action summarization

Below is the action trajectory of a user on a social media platform: {action_trajectory}.

Here is the opinion expressed by a user on a social media platform: {action_trajectory_summary}.

Please infer whether they are spreading misinformation. Output your thoughts inside <thought> tags, and you must answer either only “Yes” or “No”. Provide the answer in <answer> tags with no other commentary.
