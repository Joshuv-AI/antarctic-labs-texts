Complex oscillations of non-definite Sturm-Liouville problems, II
Angelo B. Mingarelli

Abstract




Abstract.

We correct and update a result of R.G.D. Richardson
[
13
]
dealing with the separation of zeros of the real and imaginary parts of non-real eigenfunctions of non-definite Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problems. We then extend it to the case where the weight function is allowed to be identically zero on a subinterval that excludes the end-points and study the behavior of the zeros of the real and imaginary parts when the end-points are included. Examples are given illustrating the sharpness of the results along with open questions.

Keywords:
Sturm-Liouville, eigenvalues, non-definite, complex ghosts, oscillations, zeros, separation theorem

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:
34B24, 34B09, 34C10

1.
Introduction

We consider the Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem with real piecewise continuous coefficients over an interval
[a,b]
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle-y^{\prime\prime}+q(x)y=\lambda r(x)y,

(1.2)

\displaystyle y(a)=y(b)=0.

in the non-definite case,
[
11
]
, i.e., this is essentially the case where the quadratic forms induced by the two operators on the left and on the right are sign-indefinite on their domains. The more general equation

-(p(x)y^{\prime})^{\prime}+q(x)y=\lambda r(x)y

where
p(x)
is of one sign may be reduced to (
1.1
) by means of a Liouville transformation, and so leads to no new results. Thus, we shall restrict ourselves to the formulation (
1.1
) in this paper.

Tacit assumptions and Notation

In the sequel we will always assume that the eigenvalue problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) is non-definite and has at least one pair of non-real eigenvalues, usually denoted by
\lambda,\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbf{C}
. The problem of the actual
existence
of non-real eigenvalues for problems of this type is difficult and very few results exist in this direction, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
. Given a non-real eigenvalue of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), an associated non-real eigenfunction will be denoted by
y=\varphi+i\psi
, where
\varphi,\psi
are its, normally unspecified, real and imaginary parts. A non-real eigenfunction will be called a complex ghost state or a
complex ghost
, for short
[
11
]
. Hereinafter, all solutions will be assumed non-trivial unless otherwise specified. In addition, since
{\rm Im}\ \lambda\neq 0
we will always assume that, unless otherwise specified,
{\rm Im}\ \lambda>0
. This is always possible as we can replace
\lambda
by
\bar{\lambda}
and
y
by
\bar{y}
in (
1.1
).

The following original result is essentially stated in Richardson
[
13
]
, see also
[
9
,
11
]
.

Theorem 1
.

[
[
13
]
, Theorem X]

Let
r
be continuous in
[a,b]
. If
r(x)
changes its sign precisely once in
(a,b)
then the roots of the real and imaginary parts of any complex ghost separate one another.

A discussion of Theorem
1
is necessary. First, it was understood at the time of Richardson’s paper (as it is now in classical Sturmian cases, such as in Sturm’s separation theorem) that the expression
separate one another
meant that between any two consecutive zeros of one solution there is always exactly one zero (of any other independent solution) that is distinct from either of the two original zeros. It is unlikely that Richardson had in mind the possibility that the weight function,
r(x)
, could be identically zero in a subinterval, however the latter possibility does change the result(s).

We will show below that Theorem
1
as stated is, in general, not true (see e.g., Example
1
and Figure
1
below). This was alluded to in
[
9
]
but without discussion. It is true if one excludes the endpoints as one of the two zeros in question, i.e., we are dealing only with interior zeros. However, counterexamples can be found in cases where the endpoints are included (see the examples at the end of this article). Indeed, we show in Remark
2
that if
r(x)
is identically zero in a right-neighborhood of
x=a
that includes the endpoint, then Theorem
1
is false. In fact, we go further and show in Lemma
1
below, that the statement in Theorem
1
cannot be true if the endpoints are included (if even the weight function never vanishes identically in any subinterval). As a result, Richardson’s Theorem X in
[
13
]
must be replaced by something like Theorem
2
. Indeed, in Theorem
2
we show that the single turning point or zero required in Theorem
1
may be replaced by an
interval of zeros
without affecting the conclusion.

In addition, we will find conditions under which given two consecutive zeros (one of which is an endpoint) of either the real (or imaginary) part of a complex ghost there is or there isn’t a zero of the imaginary (or real) part. In this sense, this paper continues a study of the zeros of the real and imaginary parts of such ghosts, research that began with
[
13
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
and continued with
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
and the results therein.

Remark 1
.

We note that Theorem
1
appears to be exceptional in the sense that it has no apparent equivalent in the two turning point case (with a weight function that doesn’t vanish identically in a subinterval). For example, in
[
7
]
there are examples showing that the interior zeros of the real and imaginary parts of non-real eigenfunctions may not separate one another.

2.
Main results

First, we show that Theorem
1
cannot be true if one or more end-points are included in its conclusion.

Lemma 1
.

There is no non-trivial complex ghost whose real and imaginary parts have zeros that separate one another on an interval that includes the endpoints of
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that the endpoint in question is
x=a
. Assuming the contrary, let
y=\varphi+\psi i
be a non-trivial complex ghost where
\varphi
satisfies
\varphi(a)=0
and
\varphi(x_{0})=0
where
x_{0}
is a zero of
\varphi
larger than
x=a
. Then, by hypothesis,
\psi(x)
has a unique zero
t_{0}
in
(a,x_{0})
. Now
\psi(a)=\psi(t_{0})=0
, thus, by assumption
\varphi(x_{1})=0
for some unique
x_{1}\in(a,t_{1})
. Since
\varphi(a)=\varphi(x_{1})=0
there must be a unique zero
t_{1}\in(a,x_{1})
of
\psi
. Continuing in this way we see that the now infinite sequence of zeros
x_{i},t_{i}
must accumulate at
x=a
. On the other hand the theorem of the mean then implies that both
\varphi^{\prime}(a)=0
and
\psi^{\prime}(a)=0
. Thus,
y^{\prime}(a)=0
and so
y
is the trivial solution, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
\lambda
be a non-real eigenvalue of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) and
y=\varphi+i\psi
be a corresponding eigenfunction. Then
\varphi,\psi
are linearly independent over
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary. Then
\psi=\mu\varphi
where
\mu\in\mathbf{C}\textbackslash{0}
. Since
y=(1+i\mu)\varphi
satisfies (
1.1
) we get that
-\varphi^{\prime\prime}+q\varphi=\lambda r\varphi
. Since the left side of the previous equation is real and
{\rm Im}\,\lambda\neq 0
we find that
r(x)\varphi(x)\equiv 0
on
[a,b]
. Since, by hypothesis, (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) is non-definite,
r(x)
cannot be identically zero and so there is an interval
J\subset[a,b]
on which
r(x)>0
, say. Thus,
\varphi(x)\equiv 0
on
J
. Hence
\psi(x)\equiv 0
on
J
so that
y\equiv 0
on
J
. This, however, violates the uniqueness of solutions of an initial value problem with initial conditions on a point of
J
. This contradiction proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Assume that, for some
c>a
,
r(x)>0
on
(a,c)
and
r
is piecewise continuous there. If
r(x)\equiv 0
on
[c,d]\subset(a,b)
and
r(x)<0
and continuous on
(d,b)
, then for any complex ghost
y
of (
1.1
),

(2.1)

G(x)\equiv\int_{a}^{x}r|y|^{2}\ dt,\quad\quad x\in[a,b],

is of one sign in
(a,b)
(i.e., either it’s always positive or always negative in
(a,b)
).

Proof.

Clearly,
G(x)>0
on
(a,d]
, by hypothesis, as an eigenfunction cannot vanish identically on an interval unless it is identically zero there. Assume, on the contrary, that there is a smallest point
x_{1}\in(a,b)
with
G(x_{1})=0
. Since
G(x)
must change its sign around
x_{1}
it follows that
x_{1}\in[d,b)
.

Next, observe that
G(x_{1})=G(b)=0
and
G\in C^{1}(d,b)
. Hence there exists
\eta\in(d,b)
such that
r(\eta)|y(\eta)|^{2}=0
, i.e.,
y(\eta)=0
. Consider the boundary problem (
1.1
) subject to the boundary conditions
y(\eta)=y(b)=0
. This new eigenvalue problem admits our complex ghost
y
as an eigenfunction corresponding to the same non-real eigenvalue. Thus, this problem must be non-definite on
[\eta,b]
, i.e.,
r(x)
must change its sign on this interval, but this is a contradiction as
r(x)<0
there. Hence no such
x_{1}
can exist. This completes the proof.
∎

c=d
).

Corollary 1
.

The same result holds if the other conditions are satisfied,
r(x)<0
on
(a,c)
,
r(x)\equiv 0
on

, and
r(x)>0
and continuous on

.

Proof.

Replace
\lambda
by
-\lambda
and
r
by
-r
in the lemma.

Corollary 2

(Theorem X,

is not identically zero in any right interval of

and

has a unique turning point in

, then

Proof.

This is the case where

Corollary 3
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma

, for any

, the function

This is an application of Lemma

\int_{a}^{b}r|y|^{2}\,dx=0.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that, for some
c>a
,
r(x)>0
on
(a,c)
,
r
is piecewise continuous there,
r(x)\equiv 0
on
[c,d]\subset(a,b)
and
r(x)<0
and continuous on
(d,b)
. Then the interior zeros of the real and imaginary part of a complex ghost separate one another.

Proof.

Let
y=\varphi+i\psi
be a complex ghost. Then, from (
1.1
), we find that (suppressing the variables for simplicity of exposition),

(2.2)

\displaystyle-\varphi^{\prime\prime}+(q-{\rm Re}\ \lambda)\varphi=-({\rm Im}\,\lambda)\ r\ \psi,

(2.3)

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime\prime}+(q-{\rm Re}\ \lambda)\psi=({\rm Im}\,\lambda)\ r\ \varphi,

where
{\rm Im}\,\lambda\neq 0
by hypothesis. For any two points
x_{1},x_{2}\in[a,b]
a simple Sturmian argument applied to (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) now yields,

(2.4)

\left[\varphi^{\prime}\psi-\varphi\psi^{\prime}\right]\bigg|_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}={\rm Im}\,\lambda\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}r|y|^{2}\,\ dx.

Since
y(a)=0
then, for any
x\in[a,b]
,

(2.5)

\varphi^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)-\varphi(x)\psi^{\prime}(x)={\rm Im}\,\lambda\int_{a}^{x}r|y|^{2}\,\ dt=({\rm Im}\,\lambda)\ G(x).

By Lemma
3
,
G(x)
is of one sign only on
(a,b)
.

Now let
x_{1}<x_{2}\in(a,b)
be two consecutive zeros of
\varphi(x)
where, without loss of generality, we may take it that
\varphi(x)>0
in
(x_{1},x_{2})
. (If
\varphi
is negative, we replace
\varphi
by
-\varphi
and
\psi
by
-\psi
in what follows.) Assume, if possible, that
\psi(x)\neq 0
in
[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

Case 1.
G(x)>0
in
(a,b)
.

Assume, if possible, that
\psi(x)>0
in
[x_{1},x_{2}]
. Using (
2.5
) we get, for
x_{1}\leq x\leq x_{2}
,

(2.6)

\left(\frac{\varphi}{\psi}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{{\rm Im}\,\lambda}{\psi^{2}(x)}\ G(x)>0.

Thus,
0=\varphi(x_{2})/\psi(x_{2})>\varphi(x)/\psi(x)
which is impossible. Hence,
\psi(x)>0
in
[x_{1},x_{2}]
cannot hold, i.e.,
\psi(x)
must vanish somewhere in
[x_{1},x_{2}]
. A similar argument holds in the case where
\psi(x)
is assumed to be negative in
[x_{1},x_{2}]
as from (
2.6
),
\varphi(x)/\psi(x)>\varphi(x_{1})/\psi(x_{1})=0
, which is also impossible.

Now we show that
\psi(x_{1})\psi(x_{2})\neq 0
. Assuming, if possible, that
\psi(x_{1})=0
, then
y(x_{1})=0
as well. Since
y(a)=y(x_{1})=0
and
y
is a non-real eigenfunction, (
1.1
) must be non-definite on
[a,x_{1}]
. Therefore, we must have that
x_{1}\in(d,b]
(see the hypotheses on
r(x)
). Once again the problem (
1.1
), subject to the boundary conditions
y(x_{1})=0,y(b)=0
, must be non-definite. But this is impossible since
r(x)<0
on
(d,b)
. This contradiction implies that
\psi(x_{1})\neq 0
. A similar argument applies to the case where
\psi(x_{2})=0
, and we leave this to the reader.

It follows from the preceding argument that
\psi(x)=0
somewhere in
(x_{1},x_{2})
, say, at
x=t_{1}
. We take it that
t_{1}
is the smallest such zero, so that
\psi(x)\neq 0
in
(t_{1},t_{2})
. However, since
\varphi(x)\neq 0
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, a modification of (
2.6
) using (
2.5
), gives

(2.7)

\left(\frac{\psi}{\varphi}\right)^{\prime}=-\frac{{\rm Im}\,\lambda}{\varphi^{2}(x)}\ G(x)<0,

from which we obtain a contradiction as before, as
\psi/\varphi
must be decreasing in
(t_{1},t_{2})
, yet
\psi
vanishes at the end-points. This contradiction shows that between any two consecutive zeros of
\varphi
, the function
\psi
has a unique zero.

Case 2. The case where
G(x)<0
follows from Case 1 upon replacing
\lambda
by
\bar{\lambda}
and
\psi
by
-\psi
, as the real part
\varphi
is invariant under this modification. Hence, similar arguments apply to show that between any two consecutive zeros (excluding the end-points) of
\psi
there is always a unique zero of
\varphi
and vice-versa.
∎

Remark 2
.

This extends and corrects Theorem
1
from a single turning point or zero to an interval of zeros. The condition that, for some
c>a
,
r(x)\neq 0
on
(a,c)
is necessary. This is because if
r
vanishes identically in some subinterval of
[a,c]
containing

\varphi,\psi
must be linearly dependent on account of (
2.4
) applied to said interval.

Remark 3
.

Since
y(a)=0
for our eigenfunctions we must have

. Thus, at least one (perhaps both) of
\varphi^{\prime}(a)
,
\psi^{\prime}(a)
is non-zero. We can assume that
\varphi^{\prime}(a)\neq 0
, otherwise, if
\varphi^{\prime}(a)=0
then
\psi^{\prime}(a)\neq 0

Lemma 4
.

The real and imaginary parts of any eigenfunction

Let

\psi(x_{0})=0

. If

4.
Sturm theory for non-real eigenfunctions

In this section the word "eigenvalue" refers to a non-real eigenvalue of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) and all questions here, unless otherwise specified, refer to the case where the weight function has a unique turning point around which
r(x)
changes sign. For example, we can ask:

1.

Under what conditions does there necessarily exist an eigenfunction whose real or imaginary part does not vanish in
(a,b)
?

Now, (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) always has a finite number of pairs of non-real eigenvalues by Lemma
9
, so this is a sensible question. In Example
2
there is a complex ghost whose real and imaginary part both have a zero in
(a,b)
. In this case, a simple calculation shows that
M=1
in Lemma
9
, and thus there can only be at most one pair of non-real eigenvalues. Since this eigenvalue is one of the only possible pairs of eigenvalues of the problem, the answer is negative in this case.

On the other hand, in Example
4
, for
q=\pi^{2}/4+2
we have an eigenvalue pair
\lambda\approx\pm 3.8741i
with an eigenfunction having a real part that is seemingly positive in
(-1,1)
. Once again,
M=1
in Lemma
9
and thus this is the only non-real pair.

In relation to 1) above, we can ask for a weaker result, that is,

2.

Can a non-real eigenfunction
y(x,\lambda)
vanish for some
x\in(a,b)
?

Generally speaking this cannot occur in the case of one turning point such as in Richardson
[
13
]
or, more generally, as in Theorem
2
. This is because of the separation of the interior zeros of the real and imaginary parts of the eigenfunction.

However, the possibility of a non-real eigenfunction vanishing in the interior of an interval may be true in the case of two turning points as the results in
[
7
]
show. There we constructed an example of a non-definite problem with two turning points in which a non-real eigenfunction vanished as a function of
x
inside
(a,b)
. The result is therefore likely true in the case of more than two turning points, but this has not been verified yet. In
[
9
]
it was shown that if the weight function has
n
turning points, then any non-real eigenfunction can vanish at most
n-1
times in
(a,b)
.

3.

Under what conditions can we infer the existence of an eigenfunction, corresponding to an eigenvalue of largest modulus, that does not vanish in
(a,b)
?

In the case of one turning point or as in Theorem
2
this is clear as eigenfunctions cannot vanish in the interior because of the separation property. In particular, see Example
4
where we have only one-non-real pair. This statement is then reminiscent of the Perron-Frobenius Theorem
[
6
]
in the finite dimensional (matrix) case. (See Corollary 3.2 in
[
12
]
for a related result.) Since our boundary value problem always has a finite number of pairs of non-real eigenvalues by Lemma
9
, this too is a ponderable question. To what extent is it true in cases where there are multiple turning points?
