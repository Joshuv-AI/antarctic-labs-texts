Thermodynamic consistency and structure-preservation in summation by parts methods for the moist compressible Euler equations
Kieran Ricardo, David Lee, Kenneth Duru

Abstract
Moist thermodynamics is a fundamental driver of atmospheric dynamics across all scales, making accurate modeling of these processes essential for reliable weather forecasts and climate change projections. However, atmospheric models often make a variety of inconsistent approximations in representing moist thermodynamics. These inconsistencies can introduce spurious sources and sinks of energy, potentially compromising the integrity of the models. Here, we present a thermodynamically consistent and structure preserving formulation of the moist compressible Euler equations. When discretised with a summation by parts method, our spatial discretisation conserves: mass, water, entropy, and energy. These properties are achieved by discretising a skew symmetric form of the moist compressible Euler equations, using entropy as a prognostic variable, and the summation-by-parts property of discrete derivative operators. Additionally, we derive a discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method with energy and tracer variance stable numerical fluxes, and experimentally verify our theoretical results through numerical simulations.

1
Introduction

Atmospheric models typically utilise multiple different, and often inconsistent, thermodynamic approximations throughout a single code. These thermodynamic inconsistencies introduce resolution independent spurious sources and sinks of energy and hence violate the first law of thermodynamics. For climate models these energy errors can be significant, and are believed to adversely affect the integrity of long-time climate simulations

Lauritzen et al. (2018)
;
Eldred et al. (2022)
.

To improve the energy budgets of global climate models, recent studies have explored the application of thermodynamic potentials to ensure thermodynamic consistency
Thuburn (2017)
;
Staniforth and White (2019)
;
Eldred et al. (2022)
. This approach focuses on approximating a single thermodynamic quantity—known as the thermodynamic potential—and deriving all other relevant quantities from it. The most commonly employed potentials in this context are internal energy and the Gibbs function. For a comprehensive overview of thermodynamic potentials we refer the reader to
Staniforth and White (2019)
and
Eldred et al. (2022)
.

The thermodynamic potential approach was first proposed in
Thuburn (2017)
, which introduces a thermodynamically consistent semi-Lagrangian model for a two-phase liquid-vapor system utilizing the Gibbs potential as a function of its natural variables: pressure and temperature. However, extending this method to include a third ice phase poses challenges, as the Gibbs potential does not uniquely define an equilibrium state at the triple point.

To address this issue,
Bowen and Thuburn (2022a)
;
Bowen and Thuburn (2022b)
explore an alternative approach that employs internal energy as the potential in a semi-Lagrangian discretization of the three-phase moist compressible Euler equations, effectively sidestepping the triple point ambiguity associated with the Gibbs potential. Similarly,
Guba et al. (2024)
presents a thermodynamically consistent method that also uses internal energy as the potential, implemented within a horizontally semi-Lagrangian and vertically spectral element discretization. While these methods ensure thermodynamic consistency and therefore discretize an energy-conserving set of equations, it is important to note that the discretizations themselves may not guarantee energy conservation or stability.

To the best of their knowledge, the authors are not aware of any mimetic or structure-preserving spatial discretizations for the moist compressible Euler equations, however it is noteworthy that significant progress has been made in developing such methods for the dry compressible Euler equations, as well as for related shallow water and thermal shallow water equations
McRae and Cotter (2014)
;
Lee et al. (2018)
;
Lee and Palha (2020)
;
Taylor and Fournier (2010)
;
Eldred et al. (2019)
;
Natale et al. (2016)
;
Cotter (2023)
. These advancements encompass a variety of approaches, including mixed finite element, continuous Galerkin, and discontinuous Galerkin methods. Although the specifics differ among these techniques and equations, they all maintain structure preservation by discretising the equations in a vector-invariant skew-symmetric form and utilizing summation-by-parts (SBP) operators.

Structure preservation may reduce model biases and more accurately represent physical processes, but it does not guarantee numerical stability for compressible flows with active tracers, such as the dry and moist compressible Euler equations and the thermal shallow water equations. We hypothesize that this limitation arises because energy is not a mathematical entropy for these equations. In this context, mathematical entropy refers to any conserved quantity that is a convex function of the prognostic variables; for instance, energy in the shallow water equations and physical entropy in the conservative dry Euler equations. Numerous studies
Waruszewski et al. (2022)
;
Ranocha (2020)
;
Ducros et al. (2000)
;
Morinishi (2010)
;
Sjögreen and Yee (2019)
;
Gassner et al. (2016)
;
Hennemann et al. (2021)
;
Pirozzoli (2010)
have demonstrated that discrete mathematical entropy stability is both necessary and often sufficient for ensuring numerical stability. These methods often utilize the conservative form of the equations and aim to conserve the physical entropy by employing techniques such as splitting derivative operators, SBP, and entropy stable numerical fluxes.

In
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024b)
, the authors introduce numerical methods for the thermal shallow water
and dry compressible Euler equations that are both mimetic and mathematical entropy stable. These studies identify tracer
variance as a form of mathematical entropy and propose a split-form, skew-symmetric formulation of the equations. The resulting formulations are discretized using SBP operators, ensuring both entropy and energy stability.

In this work, we extend this structure-preserving and mathematical entropy-stable approach to the moist compressible Euler equations. The result is a thermodynamically consistent skew-symmetric formulation that, when discretized using a carefully designed SBP scheme, maintains both structure preservation and mathematical entropy stability. We then develop a novel energy and mathematical entropy stable discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method (DG-SEM) for these equations.

The rest of the paper is as follows. In section 2 we derive an operator-split skew-symmetric formulation of the moist compressible Euler equations. We demonstrate that energy and mathematical entropy conservation can be proved using only integration by parts, and therefore any SBP discretisation of these equations will inherit discrete analogues of these conservation properties. In section 3 we outline the thermodynamic approximation we use in our numerical method. The discrete conservation and stability results are independent of the particular thermodyanmic approximation used but we include this for completeness. In section 4 we introduce DG-SEM and detail our novel DG-SEM discretisation. Following this, in section 5 we prove that our discrete method: conserves mass, water, and entropy; and is semi-discretely energy and (mathematical) entropy stable. These theoretical results are then verified via numerical experiments in section 6. Finally, our conclusions from this study are presented in section 7.

2
Continuous moist compressible Euler equations

In this section we derive a split-form skew-symmetric formulation of the vector invariant moist compressible Euler equations and subsequently demonstrate that the moist compressible Euler equations conserve energy and tracer variance. This provides a set of equations that can be discretised using any SBP operators to yield a semi-discretely energy and tracer-variance conserving scheme. While much of this analysis exists in the literature
Bannon (2003)
we include it here to aid the exposition, and the analysis performed in this paper.

2.1
Vector invariant pressure gradient formulation

For simplicity we consider the 2D moist compressible Euler equations in thermodynamic equilibrium on domain
\Omega
with periodic boundaries in the horizontal direction and wall/no-flux boundaries in the vertical. Our analysis also applies to the 3D moist compressible Euler equations and can be extended to include non-equilibrium thermodynamics
Bowen and Thuburn (2022b)
, noting that this introduces source terms into the tracer variance evolution equation at the continuous level. The vector invariant moist compressible Euler equations in pressure gradient form are

\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}+\nabla\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}+\Phi\right)+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p=0,

(1)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(2)

\frac{\partial\rho\eta}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\eta\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(3)

\frac{\partial\rho q_{w}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot q_{w}\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(4)

subject to the boundary condition

\rho\mathbf{v}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,

(5)

and the initial condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad{\rho}={\rho}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad{\eta}={\eta}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad{q_{w}}={q_{w}}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

(6)

Here our prognostic variables are: flow velocity
\mathbf{v}
, density of the water-air mixture
\rho
, entropy
\rho\eta
, and total water
\rho q_{w}
. Additionally,
\eta
is the specific entropy,
q_{w}
is the total water mass fraction,
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\nabla\crossproduct\mathbf{v}+f
is the absolute vorticity,
f
is the Coriolis parameter,
\Phi=gz
is the gravitational potential,
\mathbf{n}
is the unit normal of the boundary, and
p(\rho,\eta,q_{w})
is the pressure. These equations are closed with an approximate expression for the pressure, which if not chosen in a thermodynamically consistent manner can violate energy conservation.

2.2
Thermodynamics and energy conservation

In this section we follow a similar analysis to
Bannon (2003)
to derive a skew symmetric form of the moist compressible Euler equations and subsequently show that energy is conserved. The energy density
e
of the moist compressible Euler equations is defined as the sum of kinetic, potential, and internal energy densities

e=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\norm{\vb{v}}^{2}+\rho\Phi+\rho u,

(7)

where
u
is the specific internal energy. Energy conservation follows from the fundamental thermodynamic identities

\left(\frac{\delta u}{\delta\rho}\right)_{\eta,q_{w}}=\frac{p}{\rho^{2}},\;\left(\frac{\delta u}{\delta\eta}\right)_{\rho,q_{w}}=T,\left(\frac{\delta u}{\delta q_{w}}\right)_{\rho,\eta}=\mu_{w},

(8)

where
T
is the temperature and
\mu_{w}
is the chemical potential of water. This enables us to calculate the functional derivatives of energy w.r.t. the prognostic variables

\frac{\delta e}{\delta\mathbf{v}}=\rho\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{F},\;\frac{\delta e}{\delta\rho\eta}=T,\;\frac{\delta e}{\delta\rho q_{w}}=\mu_{w}\;,

(9)

\frac{\delta e}{\delta\rho}=\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}+\Phi+h-T\eta-\mu_{w}q_{w}:=G,

(10)

where
h=u+\frac{p}{\rho}
is the enthalpy. Using the identity
\nabla u=\frac{\delta u}{\delta\rho}\nabla\rho+\frac{\delta u}{\delta\eta}\nabla\eta+\frac{\delta u}{\delta q}\nabla q
we express the pressure gradient term as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p&=\nabla h-\nabla u+\frac{p}{\rho^{2}}\nabla\rho=\nabla h-T\nabla\eta-\mu_{w}\nabla q_{w}\\
&=\nabla\left(h-T\eta-q_{w}\mu_{w}\right)+\eta\nabla T+q_{w}\nabla\mu_{w}.\end{split}

(11)

Substituting (
11
) into the velocity equation (
1
) yields a skew-symmetric formulation of the moist compressible Euler equations

\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}+\nabla G+\eta\nabla T+q_{w}\nabla\mu_{w}=0,

(12)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(13)

\frac{\partial\rho\eta}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\eta\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(14)

\frac{\partial\rho q_{w}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot q_{w}\rho\mathbf{v}=0.

(15)

We now prove that the moist compressible Euler equations conserve energy by starting with the skew-symmetric form and using integration by parts.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the the moist compressible Euler equations (
12
)-(
15
) on the smooth spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with periodic and wall (
5
) boundary conditions. For continuous solutions
(\mathbf{v},\rho,\eta,q_{w}):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{5}
, at time
t\geq 0
let the total energy be denoted by
\mathcal{E}(t)=\int_{\Omega}{ed\Omega}
. The total energy is conserved, that is we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(16)

Proof.

We consider the time derivative of the total energy and apply the chain rule in time, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=\int_{\Omega}{\frac{\partial e}{\partial t}d\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{\left(\frac{\delta e}{\delta\mathbf{v}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+\frac{\delta e}{\delta\rho}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\delta e}{\delta(\rho\eta)}\frac{\partial\left(\rho\eta\right)}{\partial t}+\frac{\delta e}{\delta(\rho q_{w})}\frac{\partial\left(\rho q_{w}\right)}{\partial t}\right)d\Omega}.

(17)

Using the functional derivatives in (
9
) and (
10
)

\begin{split}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}&=\int_{\Omega}{\left(\mathbf{F}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+G\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+T\frac{\partial\left(\rho\eta\right)}{\partial t}+\mu_{w}\frac{\partial\left(\rho q_{w}\right)}{\partial t}\right)d\Omega}\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}{\left(\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}\right)+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla G+\eta\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla T+q_{w}\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\mu_{w}\right)d\Omega}\\
&-\int_{\Omega}{\left(G\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}+T\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mathbf{F}\right)+\mu_{w}\nabla\cdot\left(q_{w}\mathbf{F}\right)\right)d\Omega}.\end{split}

(18)

Noting that
\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}\right)=0
and collecting terms together we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=&-\int_{\Omega}{\left(\left(\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla G+G\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\right)+\left(\eta\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla T+T\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\mathbf{F}\right)\right)\right)d\Omega}\\
&-\int_{\Omega}{\left(q_{w}\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\mu_{w}+\mu_{w}\nabla\cdot\left(q_{w}\mathbf{F}\right)\right)d\Omega}.\end{split}

(19)

Applying integration by parts we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}&=-\oint_{\partial\Omega}{\left(\left(G+\eta T+q_{w}\mu_{w}\right)\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}dl}.\end{split}

(20)

The final step of the proof involves eliminating the boundary integral by imposing wall boundary conditions
\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
and cancellation of boundary terms across periodic boundaries, giving

\begin{split}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}&=0.\end{split}

(21)

The proof is complete.

∎

The proof only relies upon integration by parts and
\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}\right)=0
, implying that a well-designed SBP discretisation of (
12
)-(
15
) on collocated grids would hopefully conserve energy at the discrete level. Note that non-collocated methods can conserve energy by discretising the rotational term in the form
\mathbf{q}\crossproduct\mathbf{F}
, where
q=\frac{\mathbf{\omega}}{\rho}

McRae and Cotter (2014)
;
Lee et al. (2018)
.

2.3
Mathematical entropy and tracer variance

Entropy in the mathematical sense is any quantity that is both conserved and a convex function of the prognostic variables. Discrete entropy stability is closely tied to numerical stability, and many numerical methods achieve stability by ensuring discrete entropy stability
Waruszewski et al. (2022)
;
Ranocha (2020)
;
Ducros et al. (2000)
;
Morinishi (2010)
;
Sjögreen and Yee (2019)
;
Gassner et al. (2016)
;
Hennemann et al. (2021)
;
Pirozzoli (2010)
;
Chan (2018)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024b)
. For the equilibrium moist compressible Euler equations both tracer variances,
\tfrac{1}{2}\rho q_{w}^{2}
and
\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\eta^{2}
, are mathematical entropies. We prove conservation of the tracer variances in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider the the moist compressible Euler equations (
12
)-(
15
) on the smooth spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with periodic and wall (
5
) boundary conditions. For continuous solutions
(\mathbf{v},\rho,\eta,q_{w}):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{5}
, at time
t\geq 0
let the total entropy and water variances be denoted by
\mathcal{Y}(t)=\int_{\Omega}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\eta^{2}d\Omega}
and
\mathcal{Z}(t)=\int_{\Omega}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho q_{w}^{2}d\Omega}
. The total entropy and water variances are conserved, that is we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{Y}}{dt}=0,\quad\frac{d\mathcal{Z}}{dt}=0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(22)

Proof.

The proofs for entropy variance and water variance are identical so we consider
\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\gamma^{2}
with
\gamma\in\{\eta,q_{w}\}
,
{\Upsilon}(t)=\int_{\Omega}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\gamma^{2}d\Omega}
and

\frac{\partial\left(\rho\gamma\right)}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\rho\mathbf{v}\right)=0.

Consider the time derivative of
{\Upsilon}(t)
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\Upsilon}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\gamma^{2}d\Omega}&=\int_{\Omega}{\left(\gamma\frac{\partial\rho\gamma}{\partial t}-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right)d\Omega}\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}{\left(\gamma\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\right)d\Omega}.\end{split}

(23)

We apply the chain rule and the product rule, that is

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\gamma+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F},

(24)

and we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\Upsilon}{dt}&=-\int_{\Omega}{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\gamma+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\right)d\Omega}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\left(\gamma\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)+(\gamma\mathbf{F})\cdot\nabla\gamma\right)d\Omega}.\end{split}

(25)

We use integration by parts (divergence theorem) to convert the volume integrals to contour integrals around the boundary, and we have

\begin{split}\frac{d\Upsilon}{dt}&=-\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\Omega}{(\gamma^{2}\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}d\Gamma}.\end{split}

(26)

As before, the final step of the proof involves eliminating the contour integral on the boundary by imposing wall boundary conditions
\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
and cancellation of boundary terms across periodic boundaries, giving

\begin{split}\frac{d\Upsilon}{dt}&=0.\end{split}

(27)

The proof is complete.

∎

The key ingredients that are necessary in proving the continuous results, Theorem

2.1
, and Theorem
2.2
,
are integration by parts, the chain rule and the product rule. However, while SBP numerical derivative operators are designed to mimic integration by parts property at the discrete level, it is challenging for SBP operators to mimic the chain rule or product rule at the discrete level. To be able replicate the continuous analysis at the discrete level, the moist compressible Euler equations (
12
)-(
15
) at the continuous level must be rewritten into the so-called skew-symmetric split-form so that we can negate the use of the chain rule and product rule at the discrete level
Ricardo et al. (2024b)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
.

2.4
Skew-symmetric split-form and thermodynamic consistent formulation

Discrete derivative operators, in the SBP form, cannot discretely mimic the product rule or the chain rule
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024b)
. So, to discretely conserve the tracer variances we introduce the required splitting into the advection equations. Simultaneously conserving energy with SBP operators now requires adding corresponding splitting into the skew-symmetric velocity equation (
12
). For a vector field
\mathbf{F}
and scalar fields
\gamma
,
T
,
we consider the identities

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\gamma\mathbf{F}\right)=\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\gamma+\gamma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F},

(28)

\displaystyle\gamma\nabla T=\nabla\left(\gamma T\right)-T\nabla\gamma.

(29)

Our skew-symmetric split-form and thermodynamically consistent formulation of moist compressible Euler equations is obtained by replacing the corresponding terms in (
12
)-(
15
) with the averages of the left and right hand sides of (
28
)-(
29
). We have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}&+\boldsymbol{\omega}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}+\nabla G+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\eta\nabla T+\nabla\left(\eta T\right)-T\nabla\eta\right)\\
&+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q_{w}\nabla\mu_{w}+\nabla\left(q_{w}\mu_{w}\right)-\mu_{w}\nabla q_{w}\right)=0,\end{split}

(30)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\rho\mathbf{v}=0,

(31)

\frac{\partial\rho\eta}{\partial t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\eta\mathbf{F}+\eta\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\eta\right)=0,

(32)

\frac{\partial\rho q_{w}}{\partial t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\cdot q_{w}\mathbf{F}+q_{w}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla q_{w}\right)=0.

(33)

It is significantly noteworthy that, using our skew-symmetric split-form and thermodynamic consistent formulation of moist compressible Euler equations (
30
)–(
33
), we can now prove the continuous results, Theorem

2.1
and Theorem
2.2
, by applying the energy method and
employing integration by parts only, without the chain rule and the product rule.
In section 4, we will target the skew-symmetric split-form and thermodynamic consistent formulation of moist compressible Euler equations (
30
)–(
33
) with our discontinuous Galerkin discretisation. We will prove numerical stability by emulating the continuous results, Theorem

2.1
and Theorem
2.2
, at the discrete level.

Thermodynamics

This section presents the thermodynamic model we use to close the moist compressible Euler equations. We treat moist air as a mixture of dry air, water vapour, liquid water, and ice, and following
Eldred et al. (2022)
assume:

•

gaseous components (dry air and water vapour) are ideal gases,

•

condensates (liquid water and ice) are incompressible and have negligible volume,

•

constant specific heats

•

all components are in thermodynamic equilibrium.

These are used to derive the thermodynamic potentials, partition the water mass fraction
q_{w}
into the separate components
q_{v}
,
q_{l}
, and
q_{i}
, and then calculate the combined water chemical potential
\mu_{w}
.

3.1
Potentials

Here
u^{j}
,
\eta^{j}
,
g^{j}
,
R^{j}
,
C_{v}^{j}
, and
C_{p}^{j}
are the specific internal energy, specific entropy, Gibbs potential, ideal gas constant, and specific heats at constant volume and pressure of the components
j\in{d,v,l,i}
corresponding to dry air, water vapour, liquid water, and ice. The values for these constants can be found in appendix B. We use internal energy as our thermodynamic potential as in
Bowen and Thuburn (2022a)
;
Bowen and Thuburn (2022b)
, and with the above approximations these potentials are

u^{d}=C^{d}_{v}\exp\left(\frac{\eta^{d}}{C^{d}_{v}}\right)\left(q^{d}\rho\right)^{\frac{R^{d}}{C_{v}^{d}}},

(34)

u^{v}=C^{v}_{v}T_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\eta^{v}}{C^{v}_{v}}-\frac{C^{v}_{p}}{C^{v}_{v}}-\frac{L^{s}_{00}}{C^{v}_{v}T_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{q^{v}\rho}{\rho^{v}_{0}}\right)^{\frac{R^{v}}{C_{v}^{v}}}+L^{s}_{00},

(35)

u^{l}=C^{l}T_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\eta^{l}}{C^{l}}-1-\frac{L^{f}_{00}}{C^{l}T_{0}}\right)+L^{f}_{00},

(36)

u^{i}=C^{i}T_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\eta^{i}}{C^{i}}-1\right).

(37)

3.2
Thermodynamic equilibrium

The thermodynamic potentials, and hence the pressure gradient calculations, require the water species mass fractions. These mass fraction can be inferred from the prognostic variables under the thermodynamic equilibrium assumption. For a given specific entropy
\eta
, density
\rho
, and water mass fraction
q_{w}
, the second law of thermodynamics implies that the equilibrium values of
q^{j}
will minimise the specific internal energy. Mathematically the problem we solve is: given
\rho
,
q^{w}
,
q^{d}=1-q_{w}
, and
\eta=\sum_{j}{q^{j}\eta^{j}}
, minimize
u=\sum_{j}{q^{j}u^{j}}
subject to
q^{j}\geq 0
and
q^{w}=q^{v}+q^{l}+q^{i}
. Details can be found in appendix A.

4
Spatial discretisation

Here we present the details of the specific DG discretisation we use. Note that any SBP discretisation of equations (
30
)-(
33
) with suitable numerical fluxes would achieve the same conservation and stability results.

4.1
Spaces

We decompose the domain into quadrilateral elements
\Omega^{m}
. For each element we define an invertible map to the reference square
r(\mathbf{x};m):\Omega^{m}\rightarrow[-1,1]^{2}
, and approximate solutions by polynomials of order
p
in this reference square. We use a computationally efficient tensor product Lagrange polynomial basis with interpolation points collocated with Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) quadrature points. Using this basis the polynomials of order
p
on the reference square can be defined as

P_{p}:=\text{span}_{i,j=1}^{p+1}l_{i}(\xi)l_{j}(\eta)\text{,}

(38)

where
(\xi,\eta)\in[-1,1]^{2}
and
l_{i}
is the 1D Lagrange polynomial which interpolates the
i^{th}
GLL node. We define a discontinuous scalar space
S
as

S=\{\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega):\phi\left(r^{-1}\left(\xi,\eta;m\right)\right)\in P_{p},\forall m\}\text{.}

(39)

Note that within each element functions
\phi\in S
can be expressed as

\phi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{p+1}{\phi_{ijm}l_{i}(\xi)l_{j}(\eta)},\forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{m}\text{,}

(40)

where
\xi,\eta=r(\mathbf{x};m)
,
\phi_{ijm}=\phi(r^{-1}(\xi_{i},\eta_{j};m))
.

We then define a discontinuous vector-valued function space as a tensor product of
S
. Let
\mathbf{v}_{1}(x,y,z)
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}(x,y,z)
be any independent set of vectors which span the tangent space of the manifold at each point
(x,y,z)
, then the discontinuous vector space
V
containing the velocity
\mathbf{v}
can be defined as

V=\{\mathbf{w}:\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{i}\in S,i=1,2\}\text{.}

(41)

We note that
V
is independent of the particular choice of
\mathbf{v}_{i}
however two convenient choices are the contravariant and covariant basis vectors.

4.2
Operators

Before defining our discrete operators we first introduce the covariant vectors

\mathbf{g}_{1}=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial\xi},\quad\mathbf{g}_{2}=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial\eta}\text{,}

(42)

and the contravariant vectors

\mathbf{g}^{1}=\nabla\xi,\quad\mathbf{g}_{2}=\nabla\eta\text{.}

(43)

With these, the determinant of the Jacobian of
r(x;m)
is given by

J=|\mathbf{g}_{1}\times\mathbf{g}_{2}|\text{.}

(44)

Following
Taylor and Fournier (2010)
, we use the covariant and contravariant vectors to discretise the divergence, gradient, and curl as

\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{w}=\dfrac{1}{J}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial J\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{1}}{\partial\xi}+\dfrac{\partial J\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{2}}{\partial\eta}\bigg)\text{,}

(45)

\nabla_{d}\phi=\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi}\mathbf{g}^{1}+\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}\mathbf{g}^{2}\text{,}

(46)

\nabla_{d}\crossproduct\mathbf{w}=\dfrac{1}{J}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\partial\eta}-\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}}{\partial\xi}\bigg)\mathbf{k}\text{,}.

(47)

4.3
Integrals

We approximate integrals over the elements by using GLL

quadrature. This defines a discrete element inner product

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{p+1}{w_{i}w_{j}J_{ij}f_{ij}g_{ij}}\text{,}

(48)

where
w_{i}
are the 1D quadrature weights. This approximates the integral

\int_{\Omega^{m}}{fgd\Omega^{m}}\approx\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\text{.}

(49)

The discrete global inner product is defined simply as
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\sum_{m}{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}}
. Similarly for vectors

\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{p+1}{w_{i}w_{j}J_{ij}\mathbf{f}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{ij}}\text{.}

(50)

We also approximate element boundary integrals using GLL quadrature

\int_{\partial\Omega^{m}}{\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{n}dl}\approx\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\text{,}

(51)

where

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}:=\sum_{j}{w_{j}\big(|g_{1}|_{1j}\mathbf{f}_{1j}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{1j}+|g_{1}|_{(p+1)j}\mathbf{f}_{(p+1)j}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{(p+1)j}}\\
{+|g_{2}|_{j1}\mathbf{f}_{j1}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{j1}+|g_{2}|_{j(p+1)}\mathbf{f}_{j(p+1)}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{j(p+1)}\big)}\text{,}\end{split}

(52)

and
\mathbf{n}
is the unit normal of the element boundary
\partial\Omega^{m}
. Similarly, we use
\mathbf{t}
to denote the tangent vectors to the element boundaries, and we also use the boundary mean and jump notation

\{\!\{a\}\!\}=\tfrac{1}{2}(a^{+}+a^{-})\text{,}

(53)

[\![a]\!]=a^{+}-a^{-}\text{,}

(54)

where
+
and
-
superscripts are arbitrary and denote quantities on opposite sides of the element boundary.

With the discrete setup defined, we now present the following lemma which is crucial to our discrete stability and conservation proofs.

Lemma 4.1
.

The discrete spaces satisfy an element-wise SBP property.

\left\langle\phi,\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\nabla_{d}\phi,\mathbf{w}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}=\left\langle\phi\mathbf{w},\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\text{,}\;\forall\phi\in S,\mathbf{w}\in V\text{.}

(55)

Proof.

See
Taylor and Fournier (2010)
.

4.4
DG semi-discrete formulation

We use the strong form DG-SEM, which has shown to be equivalent to the weak form
Kopriva and Gassner (2010)
. Our discrete method is

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}&+\left\langle\mathbf{w},\omega\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}+\nabla_{d}G\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n},\hat{G}-G\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&+\left\langle\mathbf{w},\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{w}\nabla_{d}\mu_{w}+\nabla_{d}\left(\mu_{w}q_{w}\right)-\mu_{w}\nabla_{d}q_{w})\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n},\hat{q_{w}}\left(\hat{\mu}_{w}-\mu_{w}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&+\left\langle\mathbf{w},\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta\nabla_{d}T+\nabla_{d}\left(T\eta\right)-T\nabla_{d}\eta)\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n},\hat{\eta}\left(\hat{T}-T\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}=0\text{,}\;\forall\mathbf{w}\in V\text{,}\end{split}

(56)

\left\langle\phi,\rho_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\phi,\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\phi,\big(\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}=0\text{,}\;\forall\phi\in S\text{,}

(57)

\begin{split}\left\langle\phi,(\rho\eta)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}&+\left\langle\phi,\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla_{d}\eta+\nabla_{d}\cdot\left(\eta\mathbf{F}\right))\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&+\left\langle\phi,\big(\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\eta\mathbf{F}\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}=0\text{,}\;\forall\phi\in S\text{,}\end{split}

(58)

\begin{split}\left\langle\phi,(\rho q_{w})_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}&+\left\langle\phi,\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{w}\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla_{d}q_{w}+\nabla_{d}\cdot\left(q_{w}\mathbf{F}\right))\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&+\left\langle\phi,\big(\hat{q}_{w}\hat{\mathbf{F}}-q_{w}\mathbf{F}\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}=0\text{,}\;\forall\phi\in S\text{,}\end{split}

(59)

where the discrete vorticity is

\left\langle\phi,\omega\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}=\left\langle\phi\mathbf{k},\nabla_{d}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\phi,f\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\phi,\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}-\mathbf{v}\right)\cdot\mathbf{t}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\;\forall\phi\in S\text{,}

(60)

and the
\hat{\;}
terms denote numerical fluxes. The numerical fluxes extend those in
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024c)
, and are defined for the scalars
\alpha,\beta
as

\hat{G}=\{\!\{G\}\!\}+\alpha[\![\mathbf{F}]\!]\cdot{\mathbf{n}^{+}}\text{.}

(61)

\hat{\mathbf{F}}=\{\!\{\mathbf{F}\}\!\}\text{,}

(62)

\hat{\mathbf{v}}=\{\!\{\mathbf{v}\}\!\}\text{,}

(63)

\hat{T}=\{\!\{T\}\!\}\text{,}

(64)

\hat{\mu}_{w}=\{\!\{\mu_{w}\}\!\}\text{,}

(65)

\hat{q}_{w}=\{\!\{q_{w}\}\!\}+\beta[\![q_{w}]\!]\text{sign}{\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right)}\text{,}

(66)

\hat{\eta}=\{\!\{\eta\}\!\}+\beta[\![\eta]\!]\text{sign}{\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right)}\text{.}

(67)

We impose no-flux/wall boundary conditions
\mathbf{F}=0
at the top and bottom boundaries by setting the numerical fluxes:
\hat{\mathbf{F}}=0
,
\hat{G}=G+\gamma\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}
,
\hat{T}=T
,
\hat{\mu}_{w}=\mu_{w}
.

As detailed in the subsequent section, our method is energy conserving for
\alpha=\gamma=0
and energy stable for
\alpha\geq 0
,
\gamma\geq 0
. Similarly, it is tracer variance conserving for
\beta=0
and tracer variance stable for
\beta\geq 0
. We note that a choice of

corresponds to choosing upwind values of
\eta
and

5
Semi-discrete conservation and numerical stability

In this section, we present and prove the conservation and stability properties of our spatial discretization (
56
)-(
67
). The main results of this paper are presented in this section, including energy and tracer variance conservation/stability, and conservation of mass, entropy, and water.

The semi-discrete analysis presented here closely mirrors the continuous analysis in Section 2. In the following proofs, we first employ SBP to demonstrate conservation within a single element, following the structure of the continuous proofs. Next, we utilize the numerical fluxes to establish conservation/stability over the whole domain, taking into account inter-element coupling and boundary conditions.

5.1
Conservation of mass, entropy, and water

Let
\mathcal{M}^{m}
,
\mathcal{S}^{m}
, and
\mathcal{W}^{m}
be the total mass, entropy, and water mass in cell
m
, and
\mathcal{M}=\sum_{m}{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
,
\mathcal{S}=\sum_{m}{\mathcal{S}^{m}}
, and
\mathcal{W}=\sum_{m}{\mathcal{W}^{m}}
be the total mass, entropy, and water mass over the entire domain.

Theorem 5.1
.

The semi-discrete method (
56
)-(
67
) conserves mass, entropy and water, that is
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{W}_{t}=0
.

Proof.

Substituting
\phi=1
into (
57
) and using SBP as in (
55
) yields

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}^{m}_{t}&=-\left\langle 1,\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle 1,\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&=\left\langle\nabla_{d}1,\mathbf{F}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle 1,\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&=-\left\langle 1,\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}.\end{split}

(68)

At the top and bottom boundaries
\hat{\mathbf{F}}=0
, and at all other element boundaries contributions from neighbouring elements cancel by the continuity of
\hat{\mathbf{F}}
giving

\mathcal{M}_{t}=-\sum_{m}{\left\langle 1,\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}}=0.

(69)

Similarly, substituting
\phi=1
in (
58
) and applying SBP twice gives

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{t}&=-\left\langle 1,\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla_{d}\eta+\nabla_{d}\cdot\left(\eta\mathbf{F}\right))\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle 1,\big(\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\eta\mathbf{F}\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\eta,\nabla_{d}\cdot\mathbf{F}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathbf{F},\nabla_{d}\eta\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle 1,\big(\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\eta\mathbf{F}\big)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&=-\left\langle 1,\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}.\end{split}

(70)

As before

\mathcal{S}_{t}=-\sum_{m}{\left\langle 1,\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}}=0.

(71)

The same reasoning also shows
\mathcal{W}_{t}=0
.
∎

5.2
Energy stability

Let
\mathcal{E}^{m}=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\norm{\vb{v}}^{2}+\rho u+\rho\Phi\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}
be the discrete energy contained in element
m
, and
\mathcal{E}=\sum_{m}{\mathcal{E}^{m}}
be the total discrete energy over the domain
\Omega

Theorem 5.2
.

The semi-discrete method (
56
)-(
67
) is energy stable
\mathcal{E}_{t}\leq 0
for
\alpha,\gamma\geq 0
, and energy conserving
\mathcal{E}_{t}=0
for
\alpha=\gamma=0
.

Proof.

Following the continuous analysis

\mathcal{E}^{m}_{t}=\left\langle\mathbf{F},\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle G,\rho_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle T,\left(\rho\eta\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\mu_{w},\left(\rho q_{w}\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}},

(72)

where
\boldsymbol{F}
,
G
,
T
,
\mu_{w}
are the variational derivatives
of the energy with respect to
\mathbf{v}
,
\rho
,
\rho\eta
,
\rho q_{w}
as given in (
9
)-(
10
).

Substituting
G
,
T
, and
\mu_{w}
as test functions into equations (
57
)-(
59
) and applying SBP as in (
55
)

\left\langle G,\rho_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}=\left\langle\nabla_{d}G,\mathbf{F}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle G,\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}},

(73)

\left\langle T,\left(\rho\eta\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}=\left\langle\mathbf{F},\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\eta\nabla_{d}T+\nabla_{d}\left(\eta T\right)-T\nabla_{d}\eta\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}-\left\langle T,\hat{\eta}\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}},

(74)

\begin{split}\left\langle\mu_{w},\left(q_{w}\eta\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}&=\left\langle\mathbf{F},\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q_{w}\nabla_{d}\mu_{w}+\nabla_{d}\left(q_{w}\mu_{w}\right)-\mu_{w}\nabla_{d}q_{w}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\mu_{w},\hat{q}_{w}\hat{\mathbf{F}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}.\end{split}

(75)

Next we substitute
\mathbf{F}
as test function in (
56
)

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathbf{F},\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}=&-\left\langle\mathbf{F},\nabla_{d}G\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\mathbf{F},\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta\nabla_{d}T+\nabla_{d}\left(T\eta\right)-T\nabla_{d}\eta)\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\mathbf{F},\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{w}\nabla_{d}\mu_{w}+\nabla_{d}\left(\mu_{w}q_{w}\right)-\mu_{w}\nabla_{d}q_{w})\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n},\left(\hat{G}-G\right)+\hat{\eta}\left(\hat{T}-T\right)+\hat{q_{w}}\left(\hat{\mu}_{w}-\mu_{w}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}},\end{split}

(76)

where we have used
\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\omega\mathbf{k}\crossproduct\mathbf{v}\right)=0
which holds point-wise for collocated methods. Therefore the volume terms cancel and the element energy evolution equation can be reduced to a numerical energy flux through the element boundary

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{m}_{t}=&\left\langle\mathbf{F},\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle G,\rho_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle T,\left(\rho\eta\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}+\left\langle\mu_{w},\left(\rho q_{w}\right)_{t}\right\rangle_{\Omega^{m}}\\
=&-\left\langle\left(G\hat{\mathbf{F}}+\left(\hat{G}-G\right)\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\hat{\eta}\left(T\hat{\mathbf{F}}+\left(\hat{T}-T\right)\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}\\
&-\left\langle\hat{q}_{w}\left(\mu_{w}\hat{\mathbf{F}}+\left(\hat{\mu}_{w}-\mu_{w}\right)\mathbf{F}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega^{m}}.\end{split}

(77)

Next, we show that the contributions from the no-flux boundaries and the element interfaces are each
\leq 0
.

At the no-flux boundaries
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
:
\hat{\mathbf{F}}=0
,
\hat{T}=T
,
\hat{\mu}_{w}=\mu_{w}
, and
\hat{G}=G+\gamma\left(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right)
therefore the contribution from this boundary to (
77

-\left\langle\gamma\left(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}\leq 0
.

Finally, we show that the numerical energy flux jump is
\leq 0
at the element interfaces. Considering the 3 components of the energy flux separately and using
[\![ab]\!]=\{\!\{a\}\!\}[\![b]\!]+[\![a]\!]\{\!\{b\}\!\}
together with (58) yields

-\hat{q}_{w}[\![\mu_{w}\hat{\mathbf{F}}+\hat{\mu}_{w}\mathbf{F}-\mu_{w}\mathbf{F}]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}=-\hat{q}_{w}[\![\mu_{w}\{\!\{F\}\!\}+\{\!\{\mu_{w}\}\!\}\mathbf{F}-\mu_{w}\mathbf{F}]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}=0,

(78)

-\hat{\eta}[\![T\hat{F}+\hat{T}\mathbf{F}-T\mathbf{F}]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}=0,

(79)

-[\![G\hat{F}+\hat{G}\mathbf{F}-G\mathbf{F}]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}=-\alpha\left([\![F]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+}\right)^{2}\leq 0.

(80)

Therefore

\mathcal{E}_{t}=0

\alpha=\gamma=0

Therefore there are no transitions between vapour only, vapour-liquid, vapour-ice, and vapour-liquid ice states which introduce discontinuities in the temperature and chemical potential gradients and reduce the order of accuracy of the model.

Stability

To demonstrate the importance of discrete tracer variance stability for ensuring numerical stability, we run test case 1 with
\Delta\rho=\frac{1}{5}
using two different configurations: our standard DG method (
56
)-(
67
) (Method 1), and an alternate method (Method 2) that is energy stable but not tracer variance stable. Method 2 is identical to our method except that it excludes the term splitting in the volume integrals of (
56
), (
58
), and (
59
).

We run both simulations with a resolution of
8\times 8
elements, no upwinding or kinetic energy dissipation at the element interfaces
\alpha=\beta=0
, and with a boundary condition penalty of
\gamma=0.5
. Figure
8
shows the energy and water variance conservation errors for the two methods. While both methods are energy stable with similar energy conservation errors, they have distinctly different water variance conservation errors. At around
600\text{s}
the water variance of Method 2 begins increasing exponentially until the model crashes at approximately

7
Conclusion

In this work we developed a general framework for constructing numerical methods of the moist compressible Euler equations with excellent conservation and stability properties using only SBP operators. We then constructed a novel DG-SEM discretisation for the equilibrium moist compressible Euler equations using this framework and proved that our method discretely conserves mass, water, and entropy; and is semi-discretely energy and tracer variance stable/conserving. These theoretical results were then verified with numerical experiments.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first energy conserving/stable discretisation of the moist compressible Euler equations. Additionally, our approach is tracer variance stable, enabling our method to be numerically stable without applying artificial viscosity. We achieve these results by discretising the operator-split skew-symmetric formulation
Ricardo et al. (2024a)
;
Ricardo et al. (2024b)
with SBP operators and ensuring thermodynamic consistency through the use of the internal energy potential.

This approach has the potential to improve the energy budget of global climate models and therefore more faithfully model climate dynamics over long time scales. Future work will focus on including this approach in global atmospheric model with non-equilibrium physics.

Appendix A
Thermodynamic equilibrium

This section presents the approach used to solve for the equilibrium water mass fractions. First, the entropy and Gibbs functions are formulated as functions of temperature, density, and mass fractions. Next, we show how given entropy, density and the water mass fraction, the temperature and internal energy can be calculated. Finally, our optimisation method for finding the equilibrium mass fractions is presented.

A.1
Entropy and Gibbs functions

For each component the temperature can be derived from the internal energy components (
34
)-(
37
) through the relationship
T=\frac{\delta u^{j}}{\delta\eta^{j}}
. Re-arranging gives entropy as a function of temperature, density, and mass fractions:

\eta^{d}=C_{v}^{d}\log\left(T\right)-R^{d}\log\left(q^{d}\rho\right),

(84)

\eta^{v}=C_{p}^{v}+\frac{L^{s}_{00}}{T_{0}}+C_{v}^{v}\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)-R^{v}\log\left(\frac{q^{v}\rho}{\rho^{v}_{0}}\right),

(85)

\eta^{l}=C^{l}+\frac{L^{f}_{00}}{T_{0}}+C^{l}\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right),

(86)

\eta^{i}=C^{i}+C^{i}\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right).

(87)

The Gibbs functions, defined as
g^{j}:=u^{j}+\frac{p^{j}}{q^{j}\rho}-\eta^{j}T
, can then be expressed as

g^{v}=u^{v}+\frac{p^{v}}{q^{v}\rho}-\eta^{v}T=-C_{v}^{v}T\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)+R^{v}T\log\left(\frac{q^{v}\rho}{\rho^{v}_{0}}\right)+L^{s}_{00}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right),

(88)

g^{l}=u^{l}-\eta^{l}T=-C^{l}T\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)+L^{f}_{00}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right),

(89)

g^{i}=u^{i}-\eta^{i}T=-C^{i}T\log\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right).

(90)

A.2
Combined thermodynamic potential

In thermodynamic equilibrium all components have the same temperature
T
. Using (
84
)-(
87
) the total entropy can then be expressed as

\eta=\sum_{j}{q^{j}\eta^{j}}=C^{*}_{v}\log{\left(T\right)}-R^{*}\log{\left(\rho\right)}-\sum_{j\in\{d,v\}}{q^{j}R^{j}\log{\left(q^j\right)}}+\sum_{j}{q^{j}\eta^{j}_{r}},

(91)

where
C_{v}^{*}=\sum_{j}{q^{j}C^{j}_{v}}
,
R^{*}=q^{d}R^{d}+q^{v}R^{v}
, and reference specific entropies
\eta^{j}_{r}
are given by
\eta^{j}_{r}=\eta^{j}\left(T=\rho=q^{j}=1\right)
. This enables us to solve for
T
, and hence
u=C_{v}^{*}T+q^{v}L^{s}_{00}+q^{l}L^{f}_{00}
, as a function of total density, specific entropy, and water mass fractions

\begin{split}u(\rho,\eta,q^{d},q^{v},q^{l},q^{i})&=C_{v}^{*}\left[\exp\left(\eta-\sum_{j}{q^{j}\eta^{j}_{r}}\right)\rho^{R^{*}}\left(q^{d}\right)^{R^{d}q^{d}}\left(q^{v}\right)^{R^{v}q^{v}}\right]^{\frac{1}{C_{v}^{*}}}\\
&+q^{v}L^{s}_{00}+q^{l}L^{f}_{00}.\end{split}

(92)

We also note that the partial derivatives of
u
w.r.t. to the water mass fractions is simply the Gibbs function of that component

\frac{\delta u}{\delta q^{j}}=g^{j}.

(93)

A.3
Optimisation

Here we present our method for finding the equilibrium mass fractions by minimising the specific internal energy. Specifically, we solve the following convex problem:

\text{argmin}_{q^{l},q^{i}}\;u(\rho,\eta,q^{w}-q^{l}-q^{i},q^{l},q^{i}),

(94)

subject to

q^{l},q^{i}\geq 0,\quad q^{w}-q^{l}-q^{i}\geq 0,

(95)

where we have eliminated
q^{v}
through the constraint
q^{w}=q^{v}+q^{l}+q^{i}
. The partial derivatives of
u
are then

\frac{\partial u}{\partial q^{l}}=g^{l}-g^{v},

(96)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial q^{i}}=g^{i}-g^{v}.

(97)

We then solve this problem by:

1.

Check whether the triple point
q^{v},q^{l},q^{i}>0
is the solution. This is algebraically simple to verify and presented below.

2.

Check whether vapour only
q^{l}=q^{i}=0
is the solution.

3.

Newton solve for
g^{l}-g^{v}=0
assuming
q^{i}=0
. Check whether this is the solution.

4.

Newton solve for
g^{i}-g^{v}=0
assuming
q^{l}=0
. Check whether this is the solution.

The
q^{w}-q^{l}-q^{i}\geq 0
constraint is enforced by limiting
q^{v}
to a minimum value of
10^{-15}
at each Newton iteration. Limiting
q^{v}
may prevent the true local minima from being found and therefore introduce energy conservation errors, but the small limiting value ensures that this error is negligible. We did not find it necessary to explicitly enforce the
q^{l},q^{i}\geq 0
constraints.

A.3.1
Triple point

Water and ice co-existence
\implies g^{i}=g^{l}=0\implies T=T_{0}=0^{\circ}C
. If water vapour is present it implies
g^{v}=0\implies q^{v}=\frac{\rho^{v}_{0}}{\rho}
.
q^{i}
and
q^{l}
can then be solved from the entropy equations A.3-A.4 and
q_{v}=q^{v}+q^{l}+q^{i}
.
