Middle meningeal artery embolization of meningioma en plaque misdiagnosed as subdural hematoma: illustrative cases

ABSTRACT

Meningioma en plaque (MEP) is a subtype of meningioma characterized by aggressive local growth, firm adherence to adjacent cortex, and frequent presentation with seizures or progressive focal deficits.1,2 The appearance of MEP on contrasted images is fairly typical; however, on noncontrast CT scans, it may appear as an isodense or hyperdense extra-axial lentiform lesion, mimicking a subacute or chronic subdural hematoma (SDH).3–6

Historically, symptomatic lesions presumed to represent SDH were managed surgically, allowing prompt recognition of an underlying tumor at the time of evacuation.7,8 However, middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization has emerged as an increasingly common treatment for nonacute SDH, most often as an adjunct to surgery but increasingly as a stand-alone or temporizing measure.7,9 Because this treatment pathway may be pursued without direct neurosurgical evaluation or tissue diagnosis, radiographic misidentification of MEP as SDH may delay definitive oncological management while exposing the lesion to an intervention that may complicate subsequent resection.9–12 This evolving practice introduces a new diagnostic pitfall: patients with unrecognized MEP may undergo embolization instead of definitive tumor resection.

Herein, we present 2 illustrative cases of patients with MEP, misdiagnosed as SDHs and managed with MMA embolization, leading to delay in care and complicating their ultimate resection.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

An 86-year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus presented after a mechanical fall associated with transient right leg weakness. Noncontrast head CT demonstrated an isodense left frontal convexity extra-axial collection measuring 3.2 cm in maximal thickness with 1.4 cm of rightward midline shift, initially interpreted as a subacute SDH, although an extra-axial mass was included in the differential diagnosis. Given his minimal symptoms and advanced age, he underwent MMA embolization for presumed chronic SDH. During angiography, however, a prominent left frontal tumor blush was identified, and embolization of the lesion was performed using Onyx. The embolization was performed at an outside institution, where Onyx was injected through a prominent MMA branch to occlude the main arterial feeders of the tumor; the true degree of intratumoral penetration could not be determined from the available records. The earliest available CT study for this patient, obtained after embolization, demonstrated the left frontal extra-axial lesion with an associated Onyx cast (Fig. 1A). The diagnostic preembolization CT and angiographic studies were performed externally and were not available for inclusion. Subsequent MRI demonstrated a large left frontal dural-based mass with falcine extension, superior sagittal sinus invasion, vasogenic edema, and significant mass effect, consistent with MEP (Fig. 1B). Because of the patient’s age and frailty, he was initially managed conservatively. Over the following 18 months, he developed progressive seizures, cognitive decline, right-sided weakness, and worsening gait instability despite medical management. Repeat imaging demonstrated persistent tumor burden and continued rightward midline shift despite prior embolization (Fig. 1C).

Given his continued neurological deterioration, the patient underwent left frontal craniotomy for subtotal tumor resection. Intraoperatively, the lesion was highly vascular and densely adherent to the underlying cortex and falx, requiring aggressive internal debulking while leaving residual tumor along the superior sagittal sinus and cortical interface to avoid neurological injury. The estimated blood loss was 1 L, requiring transfusion of 3 units of packed red blood cells and 1 unit of fresh-frozen plasma. Pathological analysis confirmed atypical meningioma, CNS WHO grade 2.

Postoperatively, the patient improved clinically, with gradual recovery of cognition and motor strength during rehabilitation and no further seizures. Follow-up MRI demonstrated the expected postoperative changes with residual parafalcine tumor and improved midline shift (Fig. 1D).

Case 2

A 66-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, anxiety, depression, morbid obesity, and uterine cancer with resection initially presented after being found unresponsive and was admitted with a hypertensive emergency and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Head CT demonstrated a presumed right frontal subacute SDH (Fig. 2A and B) measuring 8 mm in thickness with mild mass effect and 3 mm of midline shift, as well as underlying right frontal edema. She returned rapidly to her neurological baseline; the acute event was not witnessed, and no definite seizure was diagnosed. The patient was managed conservatively, with antiseizure medication started as a precaution given the unclear nature of the event. MRI was recommended to evaluate the underlying edema and exclude metastatic disease but could not be obtained because of body habitus limitations. Three months later, the patient was readmitted with generalized tonic-clonic seizures and altered mental status. Repeat noncontrast CT at re-presentation showed no significant interval change in the chronicity of the collection, with persistent right frontal dural thickening and associated vasogenic edema. She subsequently underwent MMA embolization for presumed chronic SDH, performed using Optima/Optiblock detachable coils (1.5 mm × 8 cm and two 1 mm × 3 cm) and Onyx 18. Preembolization angiography demonstrated a prominent vascular blush over the right frontal convexity, initially interpreted as vascularized subdural membrane but retrospectively recognized as tumor vascularity, creating diagnostic momentum toward embolization (Fig. 2C and D). Postembolization radiographs demonstrated the resulting Onyx cast (Fig. 2E and F). Despite embolization, the lesion continued to enlarge, with progressive seizures and increasing right frontal mass effect on serial CT imaging. MRI with contrast performed 8 months after initial presentation demonstrated diffuse right frontal pachymeningeal enhancement with worsening vasogenic edema and 5 mm of leftward midline shift, raising concern for a dural-based neoplasm rather than an SDH (Fig. 3). The patient underwent right frontal biopsy confirming meningioma, followed by craniotomy for maximal safe resection (Fig. 4). Resection was complicated by a postoperative right frontal infarct due to significant invasion of the frontal cortex and involvement of distal middle cerebral artery branches. Final pathological analysis confirmed a CNS WHO grade 2 MEP. At the 18-month follow-up, the patient had partial neurological recovery and was ambulatory, but she had persistent left-sided weakness (3–4/5). Her imaging remains negative for recurrence, with the plan to proceed with radiation therapy if recurrence is demonstrated on interval imaging. Discussion

Observations

MEP is an uncommon growth pattern, accounting for approximately 2%–9% of all meningiomas, most commonly involving the convexity, sphenoid wing, and foramen magnum. Compared with globoid meningiomas, MEPs are characterized by diffuse dural infiltration and higher rates of brain invasion and frequently present with atypical histological features, making surgical management particularly challenging.1–3,13

Patients with MEP often present with progressive focal neurological deficits, seizures, headaches, or cognitive decline, depending on lesion location and degree of cortical involvement.2,14 Seizures may lead to falls or trauma, which can obscure the underlying diagnosis when initial imaging suggests subdural hemorrhage or intracerebral hemorrhage. Several reports have described meningiomas mimicking SDH, especially when hemorrhage or atypical noncontrast CT appearance masks the extra-axial tumor.3–6,15 Although hyperostosis on CT is a classic imaging clue suggestive of MEP, this feature is inconsistently present, and definitive diagnosis often requires contrast-enhanced MRI, which better demonstrates dural enhancement, sinus invasion, and pial involvement.1,14

Historically, symptomatic patients presenting with presumed subdural collections underwent surgical evacuation, allowing direct recognition of the underlying tumor intraoperatively. However, the treatment paradigm for chronic and subacute SDHs has evolved significantly with the introduction of MMA embolization. Recent studies have demonstrated that MMA embolization, alone or combined with surgery, reduces recurrence rates and is increasingly being adopted as a less invasive alternative, particularly in frail or anticoagulated patients.7–9

This shift has important implications for patients harboring occult dural tumors. In meningioma management, embolization is generally used preoperatively for large hypervascular lesions, primarily to reduce operative blood loss and facilitate resection.10,12 Stand-alone embolization of meningiomas has been reported in select frail patients, with variable radiographic responses. More recently, tumor involution after stand-alone embolization has been described in isolated reports, especially in small-convexity meningiomas supplied predominantly by the external carotid circulation.16,17 However, these responses are not universal and depend heavily on the tumor’s vascular architecture.12

As MMA embolization becomes increasingly incorporated into treatment algorithms for subdural collections, the likelihood of occult meningiomas being embolized under the presumption of chronic SDH may also rise. This is particularly relevant in urgent clinical settings, where treatment decisions may be based on initial imaging findings before full lesion characterization with contrast-enhanced studies, potentially delaying recognition of an underlying dural neoplasm.

Potential Biological Consequences of Embolization in MEP

In the 2 cases presented, delayed recognition of the underlying MEP was associated with progressive neurological deterioration and increasingly difficult resection. While delay in definitive diagnosis likely contributed to tumor progression, embolization itself may also have altered tumor vascular physiology in a manner that increased cortical vascular recruitment and operative complexity.

The vascular supply of meningiomas frequently arises from the external carotid system via the MMA, but pial contributions from the internal carotid or cerebral circulation are common in large, invasive, or high-grade lesions.10,12 These pial feeders are clinically significant because they correlate with brain edema, cortical invasion, and more difficult resection planes.2,12

Earlier angiographic studies demonstrated that partial embolization of meningiomas can lead to compensatory recruitment of pial arterial supply, particularly in tumors with mixed vascularization.11 This phenomenon has been corroborated by perfusion MRI studies demonstrating redistribution of tumor perfusion following embolization, suggesting that devascularization of external carotid feeders may trigger increased perfusion from intracranial arterial sources.11,18,19

Additionally, histopathological studies of embolized meningiomas have shown ischemic necrosis, inflammatory change, and vascular remodeling, which may alter the tumor-brain interface.20 However, embolization itself may also induce tumor infarction, edema, and acute neurological deterioration, particularly in large hypervascular lesions, which is why some authors advocate immediate resection after embolization in selected cases.21 When embolization is performed as a stand-alone intervention—particularly in invasive MEP lesions with preexisting pial supply—the interval before surgery may allow for vascular adaptation and recruitment of cortical blood supply, potentially increasing tumor adherence at the brain interface.18,19

This possibility may be especially relevant in MEP, where diffuse dural spread, sinus invasion, and cortical adherence are already common, and where the margin between tumor and brain is frequently poorly defined.1–3 In that setting, embolization intended to treat a presumed subdural collection may alter tumor vascular dynamics in a way that could increase tumor adherence and operative complexity.

It is equally possible that the marked cortical adherence observed intraoperatively reflects the intrinsic invasive biology of these high-grade (CNS WHO grade 2) MEPs rather than a direct effect of embolization, and these alternatives cannot be distinguished from the present cases. Although causation cannot be established from 2 cases alone, these observations suggest that stand-alone MMA embolization in unrecognized MEP may have unintended biological consequences, beyond simply delaying definitive treatment.

Lessons

These cases underscore the importance of avoiding reflexive embolization of presumed subdural collections without definitive contrast imaging when clinical or radiographic features are atypical. Particular caution is warranted in patients presenting with new-onset seizures, focal deficits disproportionate to the apparent hematoma size, unusual extra-axial morphology, or progressive mass effect, as these features may indicate an underlying dural neoplasm rather than isolated chronic subdural hemorrhage. In such settings, ­contrast-enhanced MRI should be obtained before or immediately after embolization, as it remains the best modality for identifying dural enhancement, pial invasion, and sinus involvement, which are characteristic of MEP. When MRI cannot be obtained—as in our second patient, in whom body habitus precluded MRI—a contrast-enhanced CT study should be considered as an alternative, particularly because vasogenic edema and dural enhancement are not expected features of an uncomplicated chronic SDH and should raise suspicion for an underlying neoplasm.

Our cases also raise the possibility that embolization of an unrecognized MEP may induce recruitment of cortical blood supply, potentially increasing tumor adherence and surgical complexity. While this mechanistic hypothesis requires validation in larger studies, it provides a biologically plausible explanation for the challenging operative findings observed in these patients. Importantly, the principal lesson of these cases is not that meningiomas or subdural collections should not be embolized—embolization remains a valuable tool, including as an adjunct to meningioma resection—but rather that an underlying neoplasm should be excluded with contrast-enhanced imaging before embolizing an atypical or radiologically suspicious presumed subdural collection.

As MMA embolization becomes increasingly widespread in the management of SDHs, awareness of this potential pitfall is essential. Careful imaging review and early recognition of atypical presentations may prevent delays in diagnosis and avoid embolization-related changes that could complicate definitive surgical treatment. Atypical extra-axial hyperdense collections on noncontrast CT should prompt contrast-enhanced MRI prior to MMA embolization to exclude neoplastic mimics that invade cortical and vascular structures.
