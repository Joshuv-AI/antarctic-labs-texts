Effect of oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention on oral frailty in older adults
Authors: Min Gao, Qian Ding, Xue Wang, Jingjie Xu, Sujuan Sun, Xiaoyan Pan, Zhaolan Guan, Chen Yang, Ya Tang, Shanshan Chen, Lingling Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the intervention effect of oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention on oral frailty in older adults.MethodsNinety-six older adults with oral frailty treated in our hospital from August 2023 to April 2024 were randomly assigned to an observation group or a control group. The control group received routine oral care and health guidance, and the observation group added oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention. After 3 months of intervention, the two groups were compared in oral function (chewing, swallowing), psychological status (SAS, SDS scores), oral health status, and self-efficacy before and after intervention.ResultsBefore the intervention, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in oral functional indicators, SAS and SDS scores, Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) scores, or Self-Efficacy Scale for Oral Health (SESS) scores (P > 0.05). After the intervention, the observation group showed superior chewing function, swallowing function, and SESS scores compared to the control group (P < 0.05). The SAS, SDS, and OHIP-14 scores of the observation group were significantly lower than those of the control group (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe combined oral functional exercise and psychological intervention effectively improved oral function, alleviated anxiety and depression, and enhanced oral health–related quality of life and self-efficacy among older adults with oral frailty. This intervention provides a feasible approach for managing oral frailty in older adults. Future multicenter studies and long-term follow-up data are needed to further validate its generalizability and sustainability, providing stronger evidence for clinical practice.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2100054200. https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html. Background

With the intensification of population aging in China, oral health issues among the older adults have increasingly garnered attention [1, 2]. As a novel concept in frailty research [3], oral frailty (OF) refers to the gradual decline of oral function with aging, characterized by impaired physiological oral functions and reduced quality of life [4, 5]. Common manifestations include diminished oral functions (e.g., chewing and swallowing abilities), tooth loss, and poor oral hygiene [6]. OF in the older adults not only affects nutritional intake but may also lead to psychological issues such as anxiety and depression [7], thereby compromising physical and mental health and increasing the risk of geriatric frailty [8]. Notably, OF is reversible in the early stages of physical frailty [9]. Early implementation of effective OF interventions can improve oral health in older adults and prevent progression to irreversible stages [4, 10]. Studies have shown that appropriate oral functional exercises can enhance oral muscle strength [11], improve oral functions, and promote oral health [12, 13], while psychological interventions play a positive role in alleviating negative emotions and improving treatment compliance among older adults OF patients [14]. However, current domestic research on the combined effects of oral functional exercise and psychological interventions on OF in the older adults remains limited, primarily focusing on descriptive analyses of current conditions [15, 16]. This study aims to investigate the efficacy of oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention in addressing oral frailty among the older adults, with the goal of enhancing oral health in this population and providing evidence-based references for clinical interventions.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Patients diagnosed with oral frailty at the stomatology outpatient department of our hospital from August 2023 to April 2024 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ≥ 65 years old; (2) met the diagnostic criteria for oral frailty (assessed using the Oral Frailty Index-8 [OFI-8] [13], with a score ≥ 4 points); (3) clear consciousness and ability to cooperate with the study; (4) residency in the local area for more than 1 year. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Severe cognitive impairment or mental illness; (2) severe dysfunction of major organs (e.g., heart, lung, kidney); (3) recent history of oral surgery or undergoing other oral treatments.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. PJ2023-139-001). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The sample size was estimated a priori using the formula for comparing means between two independent groups. Parameters were set as α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power (1–β) = 0.85, and an anticipated dropout rate of 10%. Based on pilot data showing approximately an 8% between-group difference in chewing efficiency (Cohen’s d = 0.65, medium effect size), 43 participants per group were required; accounting for attrition, 48 per group (96 total) were enrolled.

All 96 eligible elderly patients were assigned to the observation group (oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention) or the control group (routine oral care) using a computer-generated random number table. The random sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant recruitment or intervention implementation, and the grouping results were sealed in opaque envelopes. When a participant was enrolled, the researcher opened the envelope in sequence to confirm the group assignment—this ensured that neither the researchers nor the participants could predict the grouping, avoiding selection bias. We did not prospectively decide the group for any participant, nor did we use data from patients receiving existing treatments; all participants were prospectively recruited and assigned to groups based on the random sequence.

The two groups showed no significant differences in gender, age, or other baseline characteristics (Table 1).

Study method

The control group received routine oral care and health guidance, including oral hygiene instructions (e.g., proper toothbrushing techniques, rinsing with water or diluted saline after meals). Reminders for regular follow-up visits and oral examinations to address issues promptly. Distribution of oral health educational materials and explanations of oral care knowledge (e.g., balanced diet, smoking cessation, and alcohol moderation).

In addition to the control group’s regimen, the observation group underwent oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention, with details as follows. The intervention team consisted of two Associate Chief Physicians from geriatric dentistry (responsible for diagnosing OF, assessing patients, and overseeing the design of oral functional exercise protocols), one Psychologist (tasked with psychological interventions, including selecting assessment tools, designing and implementing counseling plans, and tracking psychological status changes), one Associate Chief Nurse (overseeing quality control management, collaborating on study design and training, and supervising implementation), two Senior Nurses (handling data collection, organization, and entry, as well as coordinating expert consultations and pilot studies), and five Nurses (executing interventions such as guiding patients in oral exercises, assisting the psychologist in counseling activities, and monitoring patient responses); after formation, multiple training and discussion sessions were held to ensure all members understood their roles and the intervention protocols, laying the groundwork for smooth implementation.

Intervention protocol

For the development of the intervention protocol, the team conducted an in-depth analysis of the literatures and preliminarily designed an oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention protocol, integrating the characteristics of oral frailty in the older adults, existing research findings, hospital resources, and patient care workflows. Five experts in stomatology, geriatrics, and psychology were invited to review the draft protocol, with feedback focusing on scientific rigor, feasibility, and safety—for example, experts suggested tailoring psychological counseling content to better align with the daily lives and emotional needs of old people, leading to corresponding revisions of the protocol; additionally, a pilot study involving 8 older adults with oral frailty meeting the inclusion criteria was conducted, during which patient acceptance, adverse reactions, and implementation processes were closely monitored, and adjustments were made based on observations (e.g., some participants experienced choking or coughing during swallowing exercises, prompting refinement of training methods and guidance protocols).

The combined intervention was not developed strictly according to a single theoretical framework such as the biopsychosocial or health belief model. Instead, it was empirically constructed based on the clinical needs of older adults with oral frailty. Specifically, the program originated from observed dual needs in this population—declining oral function (chewing and swallowing) and associated psychological distress (anxiety, depression)—and was refined through literature review, expert consultation, and pilot testing. This needs-oriented and evidence-based development process ensured the intervention’s clinical practicality and scientific validity.

The finalized protocol

Oral functional exercises

(1) Chewing Training: Patients sit or stand and chew sugar-free gum for 3–5 min, twice daily (morning and evening). Alternate chewing sides to strengthen bilateral masticatory muscles. Avoid chewing during coughing episodes. Masseter Muscle Training: Clench teeth tightly for 5–10 s, relax, and repeat 10 times per set. Perform 3 sets daily. (2) Swallowing Training: Patients sit upright, perform 5 dry swallows, followed by slow swallowing of 5 mL water, repeated 5 times per set. Complete 3 sets daily. (3) Tongue Muscle Training: Extend the tongue maximally and move it left/right and up/down 10–15 times per set. Perform 3 sets daily. Press the tongue against the hard palate for 5–10 s, relax, and repeat 10–15 times per set. Perform 3 sets daily. (4) Lip Muscle Training: Perform whistling, cheek puffing, and lip pursing. Hold each action for 5–10 s, repeat 10–15 times per set. Complete 3 sets daily.

Psychological intervention

The psychological intervention was designed based on established therapeutic models, primarily cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and positive psychology. It aimed to correct maladaptive cognitions such as “functional decline is uncontrollable” and to enhance self-efficacy and motivation through positive reinforcement and emotional support.

Psychological assessments: Conducted at baseline, mid-intervention (1.5 months), and post-intervention. Counseling Sessions: Biweekly 30-minute one-on-one sessions focused on active listening, emotional support, and fostering positive attitudes. Relaxation Training: Guided deep breathing exercises and progressive muscle relaxation.

Implementation of the intervention protocol

Educational materials preparation

Nurses created instructional videos demonstrating oral functional exercises, detailing key techniques, durations, frequencies, and precautions. Illustrated manuals with step-by-step exercise diagrams were distributed to facilitate independent practice.

Training and supervision

Upon enrollment, nurses provided face-to-face guidance to patients and caregivers on exercise protocols. Videos and manuals were shared until mastery was achieved. A dedicated WeChat group (including patients, caregivers, and healthcare staff) was established for daily check-ins. Patients uploaded exercise videos daily, with nurses providing feedback. Non-compliance within 24 h triggered SMS/phone reminders.

Biweekly visits

Nurses and psychologists conducted scheduled visits to assess psychological status and needs, fostering trust through empathetic communication. Nurses educated patients about oral frailty to reduce fear/anxiety. Psychologists encouraged emotional expression, offered counseling, and guided relaxation techniques (e.g., deep breathing exercises: 10–15 cycles of slow abdominal breathing, 2–3 sets daily; progressive muscle relaxation). Family members were encouraged to provide emotional support. Group sharing sessions were organized to promote social engagement and confidence.

Incentive system

No financial compensation was provided to participants. Small non-monetary gifts such as toothbrushes and toothpaste (valued below 20 RMB each) were given to participants who completed daily oral exercise check-ins on schedule. These items served only as minor motivational tokens and did not influence participant autonomy or study objectivity. Missed check-ins required make-up sessions (one missed session = one make-up).

Intervention duration

The 3-month intervention was completed without adverse events (e.g., aspiration, choking).

Quality control

Staff training

All team members underwent standardized training on intervention protocols, assessment tools, and operational guidelines. Competency assessments were conducted; only qualified staff participated in the study.

Bias control

Randomized allocation ensured baseline comparability between groups. The principle of blinding was applied during data collection and analysis to minimize both researcher and participant bias. To reduce potential assessment bias, all evaluators responsible for functional tests and psychological scale scoring were blinded to group allocation throughout the data collection and analysis process.

Outcome measures

Oral functional indicators

Chewing Function: Assessed using the sieving method. Patients rinsed their mouths to remove debris, then chewed 2.0 g of peeled peanuts for 20 s while seated. Swallowing was prohibited during the test. Afterward, patients rinsed repeatedly with water, and the expelled residue was collected, filtered, dried, and weighed. Higher values indicate better chewing efficiency.

Swallowing Function: Evaluated using the Water Swallowing Test [17]. Patients drank 30 mL of warm water while seated. Time to complete the task and presence of choking were recorded. Grading: Grade I: Completed in one attempt without choking. Grade II: Completed in two attempts without choking. Grade III: Completed in one attempt with choking. Grade IV: Completed in ≥ 2 attempts with choking. Grade V: Unable to complete due to repeated choking.

Psychological status assessed

Using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [18] and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) [19]. Evaluated at baseline, mid-intervention (1.5 months), and post-intervention. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety/depression.

Oral health-related quality of life

Measured with the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) [20]. Higher scores reflect poorer oral health-related quality of life.

Oral health self-efficacy assessed

using the Self-Efficacy Scale for Oral Health (SESS) [21]. Higher scores indicate stronger self-efficacy in oral care.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 software. Categorical variables: expressed as frequency and percentage; analysed using chi-square tests or rank-sum tests. Continuous data: expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s); Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Multiple comparisons between groups employed analysis of variance (ANOVA); Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-normally distributed data across multiple groups. Statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Values are presented as mean ± SD (95% confidence interval, CI) or n (%).

Results

The primary outcomes of the study, comparing oral function, psychological status, oral health-related quality of life, and self-efficacy between the observation group (oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention) and the control group (routine oral care) before and after 3 months of intervention, are as follows:

Oral functional indicators

Chewing Function: Before intervention, there was no significant difference in chewing efficiency between the two groups (observation group: 47.2% ± 4.3%; control group: 47.4% ± 3.4%; t = 0.291, P = 0.772). After intervention, the observation group showed a significantly higher chewing efficiency than the control group (85.1% ± 3.5% vs. 77.5% ± 3.2%; t = 11.193, P < 0.001), indicating a greater improvement in chewing function in the observation group. Psychological status

Anxiety (SAS) and Depression (SDS) Scores: Baseline SAS and SDS scores were comparable between the groups (SAS: observation 51.8 ± 3.6 vs. control 52.7 ± 3.5, P = 0.252; SDS: observation 52.8 ± 3.9 vs. control 52.0 ± 3.9, P = 0.317). Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of time for both scales (P < 0.001), reflecting significant reductions in both anxiety and depression scores across all participants over the 3-month period. A significant main effect of group was observed for SAS (F = 15.168, P < 0.001), indicating lower overall anxiety scores in the observation group. The group main effect for SDS was not significant (F = 1.618, P = 0.210). The interaction effect for SAS was not significant (F = 1.314, P = 0.272). Post-intervention comparisons confirmed that the observation group achieved significantly lower scores than the control group for both SAS (29.6 ± 2.5 vs. 31.6 ± 3.0, P = 0.001) and SDS (30.2 ± 2.4 vs. 33.2 ± 2.2, P < 0.001). Overall, the combined intervention of oral functional exercise and psychological intervention resulted in statistically significant improvements in oral function, psychological well-being, oral health-related quality of life, and self-efficacy compared to routine care alone.

Discussion

Oral frailty in the older adults results from multiple factors, including physiological decline, oral diseases, and psychological influences [22]. Studies have reported a prevalence of OF ranging from 8.4% to 44.7% in older adults, with a significant increase with advancing age [23]. Early-stage OF is reversible, and timely interventions can mitigate its progression [24]. The oral functional exercise regimen in this study—including chewing gum exercises, swallowing training, and tongue muscle exercises—enhanced oral muscle strength, coordination, and salivary secretion, thereby improving nutrient intake and digestion [25]. Swallowing training reduced the risk of dysphagia and aspiration, while tongue exercises strengthened lingual flexibility and propulsion during swallowing [26, 27]. These interventions also stimulated neuromuscular adaptation, delaying structural oral decline and improving overall oral function. Consistent with prior research [13, 14, 28–30], our results demonstrated significantly superior post-intervention chewing and swallowing function in the observation group compared to the control group (P < 0.05), highlighting the efficacy of targeted oral exercises in managing OF.

Age-related functional decline and OF-related challenges often trigger anxiety and depression in older adults, exacerbating physical frailty through neuroendocrine pathways [31]. Chronic negative emotions impair both psychological resilience and systemic health, accelerating frailty progression [32]. Our findings revealed no baseline differences in SAS and SDS scores between groups. Post-intervention, however, the observation group exhibited significantly lower anxiety and depression scores (P < 0.05). Many old people initially expressed fears about deteriorating health and social isolation due to oral discomfort, which diminished dietary enjoyment and social engagement. The integrated psychological intervention—featuring biweekly one-on-one counseling, relaxation training (e.g., deep breathing), and regular group support sessions—provided emotional outlets, corrected maladaptive cognitive patterns, and fostered peer-supported coping strategies. These measures enhanced self-efficacy and reduced feelings of loneliness, effectively alleviating psychological distress.

The observation group exhibited significantly lower OHIP-14 scores and higher SESS scores post-intervention compared to the control group (P < 0.05 for both). Oral functional exercises improved chewing and swallowing abilities [33], enabling old people to diversify food choices, meet nutritional needs, and enhance dietary satisfaction. The combination of oral exercises and psychological interventions yielded synergistic effects [34]. Anxiety and depression stemming from oral issues were alleviated through psychological counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy, and emotional support, fostering positive attitudes and increasing adherence to oral health practices [35, 36]. The intervention may support improvement in core functional and psychological domains related to oral frailty, thereby potentially slowing its progression; however, its long-term impact on overall frailty status requires further validation in studies integrating dental health maintenance and multidisciplinary management.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, the randomized design ensured comparability between groups and minimized selection bias. Second, a multidisciplinary intervention team—comprising dentists, nurses, and psychological counselors—provided integrated oral and psychological management, addressing both physical and emotional aspects of oral frailty. Third, the intervention components were highly practical: oral function exercises were simple and easily adopted by older adults, and the psychological counseling protocol was standardized and feasible for clinical implementation. Finally, the WeChat-based digital monitoring system enabled daily exercise tracking and communication with minimal resource demands, enhancing adherence and supporting scalability in community settings.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The short duration of the intervention (3 months) and the absence of long-term follow-up are important limitations of this study. As a result, the maintenance of exercise adherence and psychological benefits beyond the supervised period remains uncertain. A follow-up plan has been established to evaluate outcomes at 6 and 12months post-intervention using WeChat-based remote monitoring and periodic clinical assessments.

The difference in contact intensity between the intervention and control groups may have introduced attention bias, as the intervention group received more personalized guidance and digital follow-up. This limitation has been acknowledged, and future studies will consider attention-matched controls to minimize such bias.

The present study used the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8) only at baseline to identify participants with oral frailty, without reassessment after the 3-month intervention. Although key OFI-8 components—chewing and swallowing functions—showed significant improvement, the absence of post-intervention OFI-8 assessment limits direct confirmation of overall oral frailty improvement. To address this limitation, future studies will include both baseline and post-intervention OFI-8 assessments, as well as 6 and 12 month follow-ups, to directly evaluate sustained improvements in overall oral frailty.

Although the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) are widely used and validated in Chinese populations, their validity in older adults with multiple comorbidities may be limited, as somatic symptoms could inflate scores. This potential bias has been acknowledged, and future studies will consider geriatric-specific or clinician-administered tools to enhance accuracy.

Furthermore, future studies will incorporate strategies to enhance long-term sustainability, including periodic reinforcement sessions, caregiver-assisted training, and integration of oral function exercises into community oral health programs. The trial was retrospectively registered, and although outcome assessors were blinded, complete blinding of participants and implementers was not feasible, which may have introduced attention bias. The single-center, urban sample limits generalizability to other populations, particularly those without smartphone access or living in resource-constrained regions. Lastly, the small sample size and short duration preclude conclusions about long-term outcomes or population-specific subgroup effects.

Conclusion

Oral functional exercise combined with psychological intervention may improve components associated with oral frailty in old people, mitigates anxiety and depression, and enhances oral health-related quality of life as well as self-efficacy. This integrated intervention thus constitutes a clinically feasible strategy for managing oral frailty. Nevertheless, the generalizability of this study is constrained by its single-center design and the homogeneity of the sample population. Future research should extend to encompass older adult cohorts from diverse geographical regions and with varying living conditions, thereby strengthening the evidence base and providing insights for the development of more comprehensive preventive and therapeutic strategies targeting oral frailty.
