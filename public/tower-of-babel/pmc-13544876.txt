Familial Anti-N-Methyl-D-Aspartate Receptor Encephalitis—A Tale of Two Sisters
Authors: Praveen Kumar Yada, Sudheer Pachipala, Vyshnavi Jamalapuram, Anuja Patil, Sita Jayalakshmi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis is one of the most severe autoimmune neurologic disorders associated with antibodies against the GluN1 subunit of NMDAR. First described by Dalmau and colleagues as a paraneoplastic phenomenon in young women with ovarian teratoma,[1] it often manifests with prominent psychiatric symptoms that are difficult to differentiate from those of a primary psychiatric illness. Other clinical symptoms include seizures, movement disorders, dysautonomia, and central hypoventilation. Although ovarian teratomas and viral infections have been associated, many patients have no identifiable trigger, with likely genetic predisposition as a potential cause. Immunogenetics not only plays a role in preferential neuronal antigen presentation but also selectively alters immune tolerance. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified a strong association at the IFIH1 and HLA-DQB1*05:02 loci with anti-NMDAR encephalitis.[2] Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I alleles influence CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell-mediated responses that are responsible for paraneoplastic and intracellular antibody-related syndromes, and class II alleles monitor CD4+ helper T-cell mediated B-cell activation in cell surface antibody-related syndromes. The possible genetic variability and expression make associating specific HLA subtypes to the clinical syndromes a challenging concept. The familial occurrence of similar syndromes gives a rare chance at elucidating these pathways. The familial occurrence of the same autoimmune encephalitis among siblings[3456] strengthens the potential predisposition for antigen-specific susceptibility rather than a general autoimmune background. Here, we describe a case of NMDAR encephalitis in a young girl after a devastating outcome in her elder sister.

A 19-year-old girl with no significant medical history was admitted to the hospital following the sudden onset of behavioral abnormalities, left arm dystonia, and generalized tonic–clonic seizure. Her mother reported that she had burning micturition with vomiting in the previous week and was treated symptomatically. Approximately 6 months prior to this, her 20-year-old elder sister had suddenly developed altered behavior with the inability to recognize her parents or do routine chores and was prescribed antidepressants after a psychiatry consultation. There was preceding viral prodrome. She developed seizures with altered sensorium, which evolved to refractory status epilepticus. She was intubated and managed in the intensive care unit at a local facility. Evaluation, including brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and meningitis panel, was negative. A cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) autoimmune panel revealed anti-NMDAR antibody positivity. She had received early intravenous pulse methylprednisone and rituximab; however, her symptoms worsened, and she succumbed to illness after 40 days of hospitalization.

The younger sibling, at presentation, was afebrile, irritable, and could not follow complex commands. Her brain MRI was unremarkable, whereas electroencephalogram showed right hemispherical slowing. Her CSF analysis revealed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal proteins (10 cells). With a strong suspicion of autoimmune encephalitis, a CSF autoimmune panel was sent, and she was started on pulse methylprednisolone along with rituximab. Brain positron emission tomography/computed tomography showed hypermetabolism involving the right insula and right frontotemporal region [Figure 1]. Autoimmune encephalitis panel test showed positive cell based assay for anti-NMDA R antibody. Pelvic ultrasound and MRI were negative for ovarian teratoma. Meanwhile, she became progressively agitated and developed severe mutilating dyskinesias. She had new-onset multifocal seizures despite the optimal doses of levetiracetam and lacosamide. She developed neologism, which evolved to mutism with severe dysautonomia in the form of diaphoresis, tachyarrhythmias, and orthostasis. Immunotherapy was intensified with the addition of mycophenolate to the regimen.

Gradually, her dyskinesia resolved, and she could communicate with her family and was shifted for rehabilitation. However, by day 70 of the episode, she had recurrence of multiple episodes of left focal seizures, left upper limb dyskinesia, irrelevant talking, and refusal to eat. With the recurrence of symptoms despite existing immunotherapies and the failure of symptomatic treatments including multiple antiseizure medications, tocilizumab (8 mg/kg) infusion was initiated and maintained for the next 5 months. Her dyskinesia and seizures improved over the next 2 weeks, and she was able to return to the baseline level of performance. At the 2-year follow-up, she remains symptom-free, has resumed college, and has been selected at campus placement.

The clinical course in both the sisters was typical and tested positive for anti-NMDAR antibodies. In view of the familial occurrence, HLA testing was performed in the younger one and showed HLA-DRB1*1502, suggesting the possibility of genetic predisposition. The interplay between predisposing genes and environmental factors may contribute to the loss of self-tolerance, resulting in autoimmune diseases. A GWAS identified a strong association at the IFIH1 and HLA-DQB1*05:02 loci with anti-NMDAR encephalitis.[2] Studies have also identified non-HLA genes; including LRRK1, which is related to B-cell development, and NR1H3, which is involved in the inhibitory response to inflammation; as candidate genes among anti-NMDAR encephalitis cases compared with the healthy population.[7] The interplay between HLA alleles bypassing immune tolerance and non-HLA loci (IFIH1) influencing innate immune responses could explain the differential susceptibility, phenotypic variation, and treatment implications. Understanding the immunogenetic steps could translate into targeted treatment regimens in specific syndromes (innate vs B-cell directed therapies). Familial occurrence of Autoimmune encephalitis is rare. In particular, NMDAR encephalitis, which often leads to devastating outcomes, is a potentially treatable condition, and the prompt initiation of immunotherapy is crucial. A shared genetic background among siblings may not be alone responsible for these syndromes; a common environmental trigger, shared microbiome, and immune priming from childhood could all be potentially contributary. Sibling concordance suggests a common predisposing trigger against a permissive HLA background, resulting in a similar course of AE. Furthermore, The pathogenesis of these antibody mediated syndromes can be better characterized by assessing the CSF cytokine profiles, immune cell typing during acute phase. Further HLA subgroup analysis and peptide level segregation can provide novel insights into genetic or environmental modulation in occurrence and phenotypic variation among these syndromes. Finding similar HLA subtypes across different ethnicities, as has been described for anti-LGI1 encephalitis,[9] could further strengthen the genetic association underlying specific syndromes with age- or sex-wise phenotypes, rather than a chance population occurrence. Although a single case study may not be sufficient to link an HLA allele (HLA-DRB1*1502 in this case) to the specific syndrome, it does provide ground for screening these critical HLA alleles in wider population-based studies to identify the culprit checkpoint failures mediating the autoimmune syndromes.
