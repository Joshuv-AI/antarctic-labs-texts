Time-lag in the interacting effects of habitat amount and fragmentation on genetic diversity in red squirrels (Sciurus vulgaris)
Authors: Danielle J. Clake, Victorine Demiralp, Aurélie Coulon, Cécile H. Albert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextGenetic diversity is important for adaptation and persistence of populations. It is influenced by many scale-dependent factors including demographic history and interactions with surrounding landscape, including the availability of habitat.ObjectivesWe aim to determine the independent and interactive effects of habitat amount and habitat fragmentation on red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) genetic diversity. We also assess the spatial and temporal scales at which habitat amount and fragmentation have the strongest impacts on genetic diversity.MethodsWe deployed over 1100 hair tubes across five 16 × 16 km study areas in the south of France to gather red squirrel hair samples, and genotyped 443 individuals at 16 microsatellite loci. Using moving windows, we calculated the proportion and number of patches of forest habitat around sample locations across time periods from 1900 to 2019 and within distances between 4 and 18 km. We fit mixed-effects models correlating genetic diversity to these metrics.ResultsWe found a significant positive interaction between the amount of forest habitat and the number of patches when modeling genetic diversity. We present evidence that genetic diversity is most closely related to landscape conditions roughly 40 generations (40 years) prior to sampling, and at broad spatial scales (12 km radius or larger).ConclusionsOur research provides additional insight into the effects of habitat fragmentation per se (i.e. independently of habitat amount) on biodiversity, in addition to the scales of effect. Understanding the scales at which habitat loss and fragmentation impact genetic diversity is important for predicting and mitigating future biodiversity impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10980-026-02374-3.

Introduction

The loss and fragmentation of habitat are major factors impacting biodiversity today. While habitat loss is widely acknowledged to have adverse effects, there is an ongoing debate around the effects of habitat fragmentation when it is considered independently of habitat loss (i.e. habitat fragmentation per se; Fahrig 2017). Habitat fragmentation has been defined in many different ways. It is often considered to be the splitting of habitat areas into multiple smaller, isolated habitat patches with a smaller total area of habitat (e.g. Didham 2010; Schlaepfer et al. 2018). Habitat fragmentation per se has also been defined as strictly referring to the spatial pattern in the landscape—where fragmented landscapes have a greater number of smaller patches, but where habitat amount is either kept consistent or else accounted for independently (Fahrig 2017). This difference in definition has, in part, led to discrepancies in the reported effects of habitat fragmentation on biodiversity (Fletcher et al. 2018; Fahrig et al. 2019; Gonçalves-Souza et al. 2025). To further complicate matters, even when a consistent definition is used, the direction and magnitude of impacts of habitat fragmentation appear to be dependent on many different factors. These can include the study organism, location, type of habitat, the quality of the non-habitat matrix between areas of habitat, the study scale, and the response variable used (reviewed in Keyghobadi 2007; Rivera-Ortíz et al. 2015; Schlaepfer et al. 2018).

Genetic diversity is important for the persistence of populations, and has been linked to various aspects of fitness ranging from size and growth rate to reproductive success and survival (reviewed in DeWoody et al. 2021). Genetic diversity is also important for the conservation of species, both to guard against deleterious inbreeding effects, and as a potential reservoir for future adaptive potential (Hedrick & Kalinowski 2000; Moritz 2002; Bouzat 2010; Harrisson et al. 2014). This key facet of biodiversity is particularly at risk mainly due to human-induced disturbances and land-use change (Shaw et al. 2025). Despite its importance, the effects of landscape characteristics on genetic diversity have long been understudied (Storfer et al. 2010; DiLeo and Wagner 2016). Part of the hesitancy to examine genetic diversity in this context may be due to the complicated nature of the relationship between genetic diversity and the landscape.

Genetic diversity can be determined by many different factors, such as past and present population size, gene flow, selection, and life history and reproductive strategies (Frankham 1996; Ellegren and Galtier 2016). All of these factors can be impacted by the present and historical landscape context over which they occur. For example, population size can be influenced by the amount of habitat available to support individuals in a landscape, including past changes in habitat amount that could lead to scenarios such as population bottlenecks and genetic drift (e.g. Andersen et al. 2004; Keyghobadi 2007; Stevens et al. 2018; Pinto et al. 2024). In addition, the arrangement of habitat can impact how gene flow occurs within or between populations, and can determine whether groups of individuals function as one uniform population (e.g. if habitat is found in one large patch or in multiple patches each reachable from the others), or are split into multiple distinct populations with limited gene flow between them (e.g. if habitat is split into many dispersed and rarely reachable patches). The interaction between the amount and arrangement of habitat can also cause increased losses in genetic diversity, due to the compounding effects of increased genetic drift and reduced gene flow when habitat amount is decreased (Keyghobadi 2007). A better understanding of the relationships between these aspects of landscape structure and genetic diversity, as well as the mechanisms behind these relationships, can be helpful for land management for better conservation of this facet of biodiversity.

Spatial and temporal scale have been important considerations in landscape genetic studies since the discipline was first defined (Manel et al. 2003). There is general agreement that the choice of scale should be biologically meaningful based on the species and process of interest (Anderson et al. 2010), however the scale at which different phenomena operate is not always clear (Jackson & Fahrig 2014; Miguet et al. 2016). This is especially true for the genetic characteristics of populations, which integrate the interaction between organisms and their surrounding environment across multiple generations and therefore can be influenced by a broad range of variables across both space and time. While spatial (e.g. Rima et al. 2010; Capurucho et al. 2013; Jackson and Fahrig 2014; Haran et al. 2017; Kierepka et al. 2020; Wishingrad and Thomson 2023) and temporal (e.g. Keyghobadi et al. 2005; Holzhauer et al. 2006; Miller et al. 2013; Flavenot et al. 2015; Nowakowski et al. 2015; Draheim et al. 2018; Reinula et al. 2021) scales have been investigated previously in landscape genetic studies, many of these were focused on genetic differentiation, examined either spatial or temporal scales independently, and/or were based on simulations. In this study, we assess the impacts of both spatial and temporal scales of habitat effects on genetic diversity using real data from hundreds of Eurasian red squirrels (Sciurus vulgaris) across several study areas in the south of France.

The red squirrel is a widespread and relatively common species and can be found across Eurasia. It is commonly found in forest habitat, with an observed preference for older-grown coniferous dominated areas (Andren and Delin 1994; Delin and Andren 1999; Rima et al. 2010). While it is not currently considered to be a species of concern (Gazzard 2023), there have been noted population declines in many parts of the red squirrel range, including the United Kingdom, Finland and Russia (Ogden et al. 2005; Selonen et al. 2010; Turkia et al. 2018a, b). These declines have been attributed to a variety of causes, including competition with introduced grey squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) and Pallas squirrels (Callosciurus erythraeus), squirrel poxvirus, the impacts of anthropogenic pressures including road mortality and predation pressure from domestic animals in urban areas, and the broad pressures from changing climates and habitat loss (Gurnell and Pepper 1993; Barratt et al. 1999; Sainsbury et al. 2000; Simpson et al. 2013; Bertolino et al. 2014; Dozières et al. 2015; Turkia et al. 2018b; Fingland et al. 2022). Several characteristics of red squirrels make this species particularly interesting for evaluating the effects of landscape structure on genetic diversity. Red squirrels are broadly distributed yet associated with a specific type of habitat (forest), allowing for comparison between different landscapes with a range of urbanization and other characteristics. They have a relatively short generation time (~ 1 year) and a mean life expectancy of roughly 3 years after surviving to 6 months of age (Lurz et al. 2005), which also make them interesting for studying the temporal scale of landscape effects within the constraints of available landscape data. These characteristics, combined with the cumulated pressure that they face from human activities, make them a worthwhile study species for evaluating the repercussions of habitat loss and fragmentation.

In this study we aim to answer two main interrelated research questions: (1) How is genetic diversity influenced by the amount and arrangement of habitat? And (2) Does the strength of this influence vary with spatial and temporal scales? To do this we test three hypotheses: H1A) Genetic diversity is influenced by the amount of habitat available to support populations (habitat amount is expected to be associated with the size of past and present populations). H1B) Genetic diversity is affected by the arrangement of habitat after accounting for habitat amount (habitat arrangement is expected to impact the population dynamics of squirrels in a landscape and how easily they are able to interact, exchange genes, and function as a linked population). And H2) Genetic diversity is influenced by many generations across both time and space. As such it will be more closely related to past landscape conditions (amount and/or arrangement) than to present, and to a spatial scale encompassing multiple generations of dispersal. An improved understanding of the effect of habitat amount and fragmentation on genetic diversity will be helpful in assessing general risks to, and impacts on, biodiversity.

Methods

Study area and sample collection

For this study we sampled five 16 × 16 km study areas in the south of France (Fig. 1), chosen to represent a variety of habitat amounts and arrangements (Supplemental Figs. S1, S2). Major barriers to gene flow (large rivers, highways) were absent in all of them. Study areas also showed different patterns in how the amount and arrangement of habitat changed over time (Supplemental Fig. S3). In each study area, we installed between 178 and 289 hair collector tubes dispersed across the landscape, with a total of 1162 hair-tubes across all study areas. Target hair-tube locations were in an evenly spaced grid (600 m) across each landscape, but the actual installation locations were based on the availability of habitat and accessibility for researchers. Hair-tubes consisted of a piece of PVC tube roughly 30 cm long and with a diameter of 10 cm, installed in trees at a height reachable for researchers (Chiron et al. 2018). Two pieces of Pelton® tape were installed inside the top of each tube to catch hair from squirrels entering the tube. Based on preliminary tests, we found that Pelton® tape was more efficient than the traditionally used double-sided tape (Chiron et al. 2018) at collecting hairs with the bulb and that it was easier to retrieve hairs from this type of tape without breaking them and losing the bulbs. A walnut and peanut butter were also used to attract squirrels into the hair-tube. The first hair-tubes were installed starting in 2020 with deployment continuing in different landscapes until 2024. Hair-tubes were checked every 2–3 months, with samples collected between September 2020 and February 2024. All hair samples were examined under a microscope to identify which species they came from (Teerink 2004). We added 29 genetic samples to these analyses from road-kill cadavers collected opportunistically from the study area, as well as 11 samples collected during live trapping as part of a parallel study.

Sample genotyping

A total of 764 samples were sent to Microsynth Ecogenics (Balgach, Switzerland) for DNA extraction and genotyping analysis. Sample DNA was extracted using Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue kits. Samples were genotyped at 16 microsatellite loci (Clake et al. 2026). PCR amplification was conducted in a 10 μl reaction volume with 5 μl Qiagen 2 × HotstarTaq Master Mix, 1–5 ng of genomic DNA, 0.3 μM of each forward and reverse primer, and double distilled water. PCR conditions involved a 15 min denaturation step at 95 °C before the first cycle, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 56 °C (for multiplex assays 1 and 3) or 52 °C (for multiplex assay 2) for 90 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 30 min. Fragment analysis was performed using the GeneScan™ 500 LIZ size standard (Applied Biosystems), on a 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Allele calling was done using GeneMarker software (V2.6.4). A subset of 47 samples was run in triplicate to check genotype reliability. We removed individual samples where data were missing from more than one locus after assessment of the data run in triplicate suggested that these complete or nearly-complete samples had high data reliability (Supplemental Appendix).

Data analysis

Genetic data

We checked whether microsatellite data met assumptions of Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) using the pegas package in R (Paradis 2010). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between alleles was assessed using the genepop package (Rousset 2008), and frequencies of null alleles were estimated with PopGenReport (Adamack and Gruber 2014) based on the method of Chakraborty et al. (1994).

We used a moving window approach to calculate both genetic diversity and landscape variables within different portions of our study areas. As genetic diversity has traditionally been considered as a population-level statistic, it can be challenging to calculate in studies that use continuous individual sampling (Chambers et al. 2023). This continuous sampling, however, can help provide greater resolution in identifying landscape effects in landscape genetic and landscape genomic studies. A genetic neighbourhood approach to calculating genetic diversity has been described previously (e.g. Shirk and Cushman 2011; Bishop et al. 2023) to help overcome this limitation.

Our moving window analysis consisted of a grid of points spaced 1 km apart within each study area. This resulted in a total of 1280 grid points or “windows” (256 per study area). Genetic diversity was estimated for groups of individuals located within a given distance of each point of the grid. To determine which distance should be used we assessed genetic spatial autocorrelation for each study area as an estimate of genetic neighbourhood diameter (Shirk and Cushman 2011). We did this using the PopGenReport package, with 100 repetitions and the approach of Smouse and Peakall (1999). Three of the study areas (G, E, Mtp) did not have a large enough sample size across all distance categories to be reliably interpreted. In the study area Aix, for which we had the most data and therefore the clearest pattern of spatial autocorrelation, there was positive spatial autocorrelation within 4 km (Supplemental Fig. S4). We therefore used a range of 4 km as the distance around each point within which to draw individuals to calculate genetic diversity. We did not modify our diameter for each point based on a cost-distance approach as done in previous studies (Shirk and Cushman 2011) since our objective was to test the effects of landscape variables on genetic diversity. We therefore avoided using landscape variables while calculating genetic diversity.

We calculated two measures of genetic diversity: rarefied allelic richness (AR) was calculated using the R package hierfstat (Goutet and Jombart 2022) with a minimum allele cut-off of 10, and expected heterozygosity (HE) was calculated using the R adegenet package (Jombart 2008). Grid points with fewer than five individuals were considered to be under-sampled and were expected to have less accurate genetic diversity estimates, so were removed from further analyses.

Land cover data

We selected two landscape metrics to use in our analyses: the proportion of each landscape that was covered by forest (PLAND) and the number of forest patches in each landscape (NP). We used PLAND as a measure of habitat amount, and NP as a measure of habitat arrangement/fragmentation. In our study design NP is equivalent to patch density since we compare between windows of the same total area. We chose this metric because we would expect groups of squirrels to function differently when they are found, for example, in one single patch of habitat than when they are found in an equivalent area spread over multiple patches, which are less likely to function as a single population.

We used HILDA Global Land Use Change data (Winkler et al. 2020) in our initial analyses. This data is available for each year between 1899 and 2019 at 0.01° resolution (approximately 1 km in our study area), allowing us to test the influence of land cover at different time points. We selected years at 20-year intervals between 1899 and 2019, then assessed additional 10-year intervals around the year with the best fitting model to see whether the pattern was consistent. We fit one additional model using the average values for landscape metrics calculated across all years to determine whether a specific year had a stronger fit than average conditions through time. Land cover categories used in HILDA data include ocean, urban, cropland, pasture/rangeland, forest, unmanaged grass/shrubland, spares/no vegetation, and water. For these analyses we considered all “forest” pixels as suitable habitat for red squirrels.

While the HILDA data provides excellent temporal resolution, the 1 km spatial resolution could be considered overly coarse for our study organism. Indeed, red squirrels have estimated mean home range sizes ranging from 2.73 to 4.5 ha in coniferous areas, and 4.4–6.4 in deciduous areas (Wauters & Dhondt 1992) and mean dispersal distances between roughly 400–500 m up to 1–3 km (Wauters et al. 2010; Fey et al. 2016; Hämäläinen et al. 2019), a distance which may in part be dependent on the habitat type (Hämäläinen et al. 2019). We therefore used an additional land cover data set to check whether the spatial resolution impacted our key findings. We ran a second set of analyses using CORINE land cover data (Copernicus Land Monitoring Service 2021), which is available for the years 1990, 2000, 2006, 2012, and 2018 with 100 m resolution. CORINE data has a finer thematic resolution than HILDA data, with forest habitat split into three categories: broad-leaved, coniferous, and mixed forest. We combined these into one uniform “forest” category for analyses to be consistent with the thematic resolution of the HILDA data. Maps showing forest land cover in our study areas based on both HILDA and CORINE data can be found in Supplemental Information (Figs. S1, S2).

For each land cover dataset, we used the landscapemetrics R package (Hesselbarth et al. 2019) to calculate the PLAND and NP of forest surrounding each point on the 1 km grids used above in calculating allelic richness. We calculated the PLAND and NP within 4 km of each point, and then by buffer distances increasing by 2 km up to 18 km. At the larger distances in this range, both for PLAND and NP, we began to see very high correlations between metrics calculated at different spatial and temporal scales (Supplemental Figs. S5, S6), and thus did not incorporate distances greater than 18 km into our analyses. The NP was calculated using rook’s case (pixels connected to 4 neighbours when defining a patch) for both HILDA and CORINE data. Since HILDA data was aggregated at a 1 km scale, we felt it less likely that pixels connected at corners would have continuous habitat across those corners. We ran an additional set of analyses with the queen’s case (8 neighbours) for the CORINE data to check sensitivity of our results to this parameter.

Statistical analysis

We fit linear mixed effects models using the lme4 package in R to estimate the relationship between genetic diversity and landscape metrics. We included X and Y spatial coordinates as fixed effects in all models to account for spatial autocorrelation between individual points and moving windows, and study areas as a random effect on the intercept in all models to account for potential differences among the landscapes. We ran all combinations of models, starting with the null model (AR or HE ~ X + Y + (1|Study Area)), and adding NP and PLAND separately, then together in the same models both with and without an interaction term. We used the MuMIN package (Bartoń 2024) to calculate AICC for each model, as well as model-averaged coefficients of variables found in the top model set (ΔAICc of < 2 from lowest AICc model) for each combination of year and buffer distance. The MuMIN package was also used to calculate confidence intervals for coefficient estimates.

Finally, in order to test for effects of non-independence of our data points (since our moving windows are overlapping and therefore both individuals and landscape features could be included in multiple windows), we ran spatially-buffered leave-one-out (LOO) cross validation (le Rest et al. 2014; Roberts et al. 2017) for the top models. We fit models using a subset of points, then assessed the root mean square error (RMSE) of model-predicted vs. actual values of genetic diversity. Using a subset of points allowed us to check the sensitivity of the model to sample size, and the spatially-buffered LOO approach tested whether predictions were strongly influenced by the availability of spatially proximate points. In this case we removed all points within 6 km of a selected window before fitting a model with that subset of data, and then checked for major differences between the model-predicted and actual values for the selected window.

Results

Data distributions

Genetic data

Of the 764 samples genotyped, we removed 278 that were missing data at more than one locus. We removed 43 additional samples that were determined to be duplicates of the same individual, resulting in a working sample size of 443 for all remaining analyses (Aix = 206; C = 119; E = 28; G = 46; Mtp = 38). The spatial distribution of theses samples across our study areas resulted in differing ranges in the number of individuals found within moving windows, with the largest range found in Aix (Supplemental Fig. S7). After removing windows with less than five individuals, we had a total of 926 “windows” in our analyses (254 in Aix, 246 in C, 102 in E, 184 in G, and 140 in Mtp) and a mean of 18.9 (± 13.5) individuals per window (Supplemental Fig. S7).

Most loci were found to be out of HWE when calculated across all populations (Supplemental Table S1), fitting the expectation that mating would not be completely random across all of our distinct study areas. When calculated for each population separately, there was only one locus that was out of HWE across all populations (SV_947104_At532, Supplemental Table S2). This same locus had a high estimated frequency of null alleles (Supplemental Table S3) and was excluded from further analyses. There was significant LD for specific pairs of loci within most landscapes, however no locus pairs displayed significant LD across all study areas (Supplemental Fig. S8).

The AR calculated within a 4 km buffer around moving windows ranged from 1.6 to 3.4 (Supplemental Fig. S9). HE ranged from 0.25 to 0.52. There was a high correlation between the two genetic diversity metrics (Pearson’s correlation = 0.68; t = 28.09, df = 924, p < 0.01; Supplemental Fig. S10).

Land cover data

When calculated using HILDA land cover data, the range in the number of patches of forest (NP) in moving-window grid landscapes varied between 0 and 7 (for the smallest buffer distance) to 2–46 (for the largest buffer distance), and the range of proportion of forest (PLAND) was between 0–54 and 0–93% (Supplemental Fig. S5-S6). Study areas E and G had the highest proportion of forest on average across all years and moving windows, and Mtp had the lowest (Supplemental Fig. S3). Study areas E and G also had the greatest changes in the mean proportion of forest over the years, with the highest amounts at the beginning and end of our study period, and fluctuations in the intervening years (Supplemental Fig. S3). The proportion of forest stayed more consistent in the remaining study areas across years, with slight increases from 1960 onwards in Aix and C (Supplemental Fig. S3). In most study areas the mean number of patches of forest decreased in the middle of the study period, with differing patterns at the beginning and end (Supplemental Fig. S3).

There were correlations between different buffer distances and between different years for each landscape metric, with the highest correlations generally occurring at larger buffer distances (especially for PLAND), in addition to higher correlations between proximate years/distances (Supplemental Figs. S5, S6). The correlation between NP and PLAND also varied between different years and buffer distances, and ranged from − 0.28 to 0.74 (Supplemental Fig. S11). All correlation values greater than 0.6 were found in data from 1940 (Supplemental Fig. S11).

When comparing the HILDA land cover data to the CORINE data (based on the data from 1990 for both sources), there was quite a strong correlation in the PLAND in both datasets, with the NP in landscapes differing more between the two data sources (Supplemental Fig. S12).

Model results

HILDA land cover

For the majority of year and distance combinations, models including land cover variables (NP and PLAND) outperformed the null model for AR (40 out of 56; Fig. 2). The full model, incorporating both NP and PLAND as well as an interaction between the two, was most commonly the best-fitting model (28 out of 40; Fig. 2). In all cases where the interaction between NP and PLAND was in the top AIC model set, the interaction was significant and positive (Fig. 2). The strongest correlation between AR and habitat metrics was found when using land cover data from 1980, and when calculating habitat metrics within a buffer distance of 14 km (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table S4). The model fit using land cover data from 1980 and a buffer distance of 14 km also outperformed all models fit using land cover variables averaged across years (Supplemental Fig. S13). Predictions based on the best-fitting model illustrate that when there is less forest habitat in the landscape, AR tends to be higher when that habitat is concentrated in fewer patches, but that when there is a higher proportion of habitat in the landscape, AR tends to be higher when the habitat is found in a greater number of patches (Fig. 3). The model intercept varied between study areas (random effect variance = 0.039; standard deviation = 0.197 for best-fitting model), suggesting that not all variation in genetic diversity between study areas was captured in our model variables. Discussion

In this study, we assessed the impacts of habitat amount and fragmentation on genetic diversity in populations of red squirrels. We were motivated, in part, by previous reviews which found mixed results when assessing patterns of genetic diversity as it corresponds to habitat fragmentation (Keyghobadi 2007; Schlaepfer et al. 2018). Results varied to the extent that, in one review, roughly half of the studies assessed showed evidence of lower genetic diversity in populations found in more highly fragmented landscapes, while in the remaining studies there was either no significant effect of fragmentation, or the opposite trend of increased diversity in fragmented landscapes (Keyghobadi 2007).

These inconsistent findings have been attributed to different situational explanations, ranging from differences in habitat type and/or matrix quality (Levy et al. 2013), to characteristics of the study species such as body size, dispersal ability, life history, and degree of habitat specialization (Brouat et al. 2003; Meyer et al. 2009; Rivera-Ortiz et al. 2015; Khimoun et al. 2016; Lino et al. 2019). Many prior studies of habitat fragmentation and genetic diversity did not account for or distinguish between the effects of habitat amount and habitat fragmentation per se (Schlaepfer et al. 2018), which could also explain the discrepancies found. Our study helps to further address the potential sources of inconsistencies by showing that even within a single species and large sampling area the relationship between genetic diversity and the landscape must be understood through the interaction between habitat amount and arrangement, and through an assessment of spatial and temporal scales.

Effects of habitat amount and fragmentation

One of the key findings of our study was that, in this system, we can not adequately understand the impacts of habitat fragmentation per se without examining its interaction with the amount of habitat. While previous studies and reviews have also suggested that the effect size of habitat fragmentation per se may depend on the amount of habitat (e.g. Villard & Metzger 2014; Hanski 2015), we found that not only the effect size, but also the direction of effect of fragmentation differed depending on the habitat amount. When there was less habitat in landscapes, genetic diversity tended to be higher when that habitat was found in fewer patches. However, in landscapes with more habitat, there was higher genetic diversity when the habitat was arranged in a greater number of patches (Fig. 3C). This interaction between habitat amount and fragmentation has also been found in previous research where fragmentation effects were similarly modulated by habitat amount when looking at the presence of woodlarks in habitat patches (Bosco et al. 2021). The pattern of presence/absence in that case was explained by a preference for heterogeneous habitat arrangements and spatiotemporal variability once there was a sufficient amount of habitat (Bosco et al. 2021). This need for a sufficient amount of habitat combined with positive effects of heterogeneity could also help explain the pattern that we observed for genetic diversity, along with several other potential explanations.

For a landscape with a given amount of habitat, having that habitat split into more patches would result in a decreased mean patch size compared to a landscape with an equivalent amount of habitat split into fewer patches. In landscapes that have a smaller amount of habitat, a decrease in mean patch size could result in some very small patches that could support only a small number of individuals (i.e. a patch size effect; Fletcher et al. 2024; but see also Riva and Fahrig 2023).If these small patches with few individuals are also isolated in the landscape, it would result in small, disconnected populations. Previous literature has shown that having smaller, more isolated populations can expose them to the effects of genetic drift, and therefore a loss of genetic diversity (e.g. Brown et al. 2004; Dhuyvetter et al. 2005), including in red squirrels (Wauters et al. 1994). Much of this previous literature identified “fragmented habitats” as having both decreased amount and increased fragmentation of habitats without distinguishing between the two (Schlaepfer et al. 2018). In our study, moving windows with low amount of habitat and greater number of patches more readily fit the characteristics of these “fragmented habitats” as historically defined.

In the HILDA data used in this study, each pixel represents roughly 1 km2 (100 ha). Previous studies have found squirrels permanently inhabiting all woods over 30 ha, with the smallest inhabited habitat patches estimated to be 0.8 ha to 3.19 ha (Verboom & Van Apeldorn 1990; Rima et al. 2010). It is unlikely, however, that a single “forest” pixel of 1 km2 corresponds to complete cover by contiguous forest on the ground (see Supplemental Figs. S1, S2, and Supplemental Fig. S19 for examples of patch size distributions at various combinations of PLAND and NP). We found similar results when using the CORINE data in analysis, however, which has a 100 m2 (0.01 ha) area per pixel. At this resolution, the land cover data would be more likely to capture patches of forest that would not be sufficient to support large squirrel populations.

When there was more habitat in the landscape, we found a trend of higher global genetic diversity when that habitat was split into a greater number of patches. This pattern could be the result of several processes. First, with a greater amount of habitat in the landscape, there would be a larger mean patch size for a given number of patches when compared to landscapes with a smaller amount of habitat. If these larger patches support larger populations as expected (whether as one larger patch or many patches of larger size), it could decrease the potential impacts of genetic drift (Ellegren & Galtier 2016). This could therefore mitigate some of the negative effects of fragmentation per se described above for landscapes with less habitat.

Furthermore, having multiple populations may help buffer against stochastic loss of any single population such that if one population is lost, other populations could remain unaffected and persist. For example, having a population split into multiple patches may have benefits in reducing pathogen or parasite transmission between them (Taylor & Merriam 1996). In these cases, having some populations persist and maintain their genetic diversity could result in a higher level of genetic diversity when considered across the entire landscape.

Another potential explanation proposed by Fahrig (2017) to explain observed increases in species richness with increasing habitat fragmentation, is that when habitat is split across multiple patches there is likely to be some level of heterogeneity between patches (e.g. two forest patches occurring in close geographic proximity but at different elevations could have different light and temperature regimes). If having populations split between different habitat patches also increases the effective distance between them (e.g. if the non-habitat matrix between them is hostile enough to inhibit movement and/or survival) then it could allow for those populations to become increasingly genetically differentiated through a combination of local adaptations and genetic drift, without these effects being overwhelmed by gene flow (Willi et al. 2007; Willi & Hoffmann 2012). This could be thought of as similar to the effects of genetic isolation by distance but with an influence of spatial arrangement and matrix effects in addition to geographic distance. When unique genes are retained in each population it could therefore increase the genetic diversity across all populations cumulatively (Templeton et al. 1990). Contrastingly, when populations are clustered within the same habitat patch, genotypes could expand from one population to others more easily, potentially resulting in the fixation of certain genotypes across the entire group of populations (Toma et al. 2015).

The strength of these effects will of course be influenced by the quality of habitat or matrix between populations (Fletcher et al. 2024). In our case we expect the matrix quality to differ between our study areas. Study areas C, E and G are more rural landscapes where the matrix is composed of land cover that is more similar to squirrel habitat, and theoretically less resistant than in study areas Aix and Mtp, which include more urban areas and features that may be more restrictive for squirrel movement. These matrix effects could also interact with other features of our different study areas, such as the structural distance between patches, which is likely higher in landscapes with lower habitat amount than in landscape with higher habitat amount. By incorporating study area as a random effect in our models we attempted to account for some of these broad-scale differences, however the specifics of potential interactions between habitat fragmentation impacts and matrix effects on population genetic patterns are outside the scope of this paper and will be investigated further in a subsequent study.

While definitive identification of the processes behind the patterns that we found remains the topic for future study, here we show the importance of simultaneously considering both the amount and arrangement of habitat. Our results also provide important context for the ongoing debate about the effects of fragmentation (e.g. Fletcher et al. 2018; Fahrig et al. 2019; Gonçalves-Souza et al. 2025), and show that the effects of habitat fragmentation cannot be properly understood without consideration of the amount of habitat available, and the interaction between these two factors.

Scales of response

Genetic response time

Multiple studies have found that current patterns of genetic differentiation and diversity are more closely related to past landscape events or conditions than to current (Keyghobadi et al. 2005; Holzhauer et al. 2006; Miller et al. 2013; Reinula et al. 2021). Many of the studies associating genetic patterns to historical landscapes, however, used broad and/or limited numbers of temporal categories (e.g. Keyghobadi et al. 2005; Schlaepfer et al. 2018; Reinula et al. 2021). In our study we aimed to refine this time scale further by testing the potential influence of historical landscapes using a higher temporal resolution.

The strongest relationship between genetic diversity in red squirrel populations and the amount and arrangement of forest habitat was found using land cover data from 40 to 50 years ago (Fig. 2), corresponding to 40–50 generations in red squirrels. Our findings are in line with a previous review, which found that time lags in landscape genetic studies were most commonly estimated at 20–50 years (Epps & Keyghobadi 2015). This time scale was thought to possibly be a function of the availability of landscape data from that time period, with limited data available from prior years (Epps & Keyghobadi 2015). Our results suggest that this lag may not be strictly constrained by available data, however, and that 50 years may be a biologically relevant time span for these populations of red squirrels.

Caution must be used, however, when making inferences about the processes behind patterns linked to this specific time scale. Landscapes are rarely static (Supplemental Fig. S3), but are shaped by processes continuously occurring over a long period of time (Schlaepfer et al. 2018). The historical and contemporary processes behind landscape genetic patterns can therefore also be very complex, with patterns not only influenced by a specific period, but likely more or less continuously impacted by events leading as far back as the Pleistocene and last glacial maximum periods (Ellegren & Galtier 2016; Epps & Keyghobadi 2015).

In addition to landscape effects, characteristics of the population itself will influence spatial genetic patterns. For example, the source and size of a starting population can influence how quickly they are impacted by landscape changes (Schlaepfer et al. 2018). Time lags in genetic patterns may also be dependent on species-specific characteristics and life history traits, such as generation time/overlap, dispersal ability, and mating and reproductive strategy (Epps & Keyghobadi 2015; Gargiulo et al. 2025), and can also vary even between closely related species in the same landscape (e.g. Flavenot et al. 2015), or the same species in different regions (Levy et al. 2013).

Time lags in genetic response may also be dependent on the type of molecular marker examined, and on the genomic resolution afforded by the genetic dataset. Microsatellites, for example, have a high mutation rate and therefore are expected to reflect more recent processes and impacts than other markers with a lower mutation rate such as mitochondrial DNA (Selkoe & Toonen 2006; Wang 2011).

Despite their relatively high mutation rates, however, microsatellites may lack the statistical power to detect recent genetic changes due to the relatively small numbers of markers that tend to be used (McCartney-Melstad et al. 2018). While SNPs, on average, have a slower mutation rate than microsatellites, they tend to be characterized in much greater numbers which can allow for greater statistical power to detect more recent genetic changes and more subtle genetic structure (Sunde et al. 2020). While spatial genetic structure and differentiation are in turn more likely to reflect more recent landscape patterns than genetic diversity (Keyghobadi et al. 2005), applying higher resolution markers to a study such as ours could result in association of genetic diversity with more recent land cover changes.

Even when considering the same genetic response and marker type, selection pressure can also change the time lags between different loci (Gargiulo et al. 2025). Different measures can have different response times. For example, allelic richness is expected to respond more quickly than heterozygosity to reductions in population size that can occur with habitat loss and/or fragmentation, despite both being measures of genetic diversity (Allendorf 1986; Hill et al. 2023). This was not the case in our study, with both HE and allelic richness being most correlated with the 1980 landscape.

Spatial scales

Similar to the processes behind temporal scales, there are many factors that can determine the spatial scale of influence of landscape genetic studies (Miguet et al. 2016). The relevant spatial scale is likely to be dependent on the species in question, the type of habitat, and even the choice of response variable (Jackson & Fahrig 2014; Kierepka et al. 2020). There may also be multiple scales of effect linked to different processes affecting the same response variable (Rima et al. 2010). In studies of genetic diversity we expect the spatial scale to be relatively large, since genetic diversity can be impacted by many generations of individuals and population changes, and by the interactions between adaptation, gene flow, and genetic drift. This expectation, also predicted in a previous review (Miguet et al. 2016), is supported by a simulation study which found that the spatial scale of effect was larger for genetic diversity than for measures of presence/absence or abundance (Jackson & Fahrig 2014).

In the case of our study, we also found genetic diversity to be associated with landscape variables at broader geographic scales. While the most strongly supported scale of effect varied slightly depending on the land cover data (HILDA vs. Corine) and response metric (AR vs. HE), it was always a broad spatial scale, where landscape metrics were calculated within a 12 km radius or greater. The difference between models could be due to variation in the actual biological scale of effect. However, due to the high correlation between land cover variables at this scale (see Supplemental Figs. S5, S6), it is difficult to definitively isolate a specific distance that is likely to act as the scale of effect. Instead, we can assert that the scale of effect is likely broad, with a radius of 12 km or more.

This is similar to other studies which also found genetic diversity to be associated with land cover at broad, regional scales. For example, one study found genetic diversity in the black manakin (Xenopipo atronitens; a bird species found in the Amazon) to be strongly associated with the amount of habitat within 15 km (Capurucho et al. 2013). A study in eastern chipmunks (Tamias striatus) also found that genetic diversity was correlated with regional scale landscape arrangement, calculated within 23 km2 study cells with an additional 1.6 km buffer (Kierepka et al. 2020). These findings are not, however, universal, with other studies in white-footed mice (Peromyscus leucopus) finding that genetic diversity was most strongly correlated with landscape variables within 0.5 and 5 km radii (Taylor & Hoffman 2014), supporting the assertion that the scale of effect can be dependent on many different factors, including species’ biology.

Conclusions

The potential processes behind the genetic diversity patterns and associated scales of effect estimated in this study are likely numerous and complex. Without a thorough understanding of processes, caution is warranted in using these specific scales of effect to make predictions in other scenarios. This research nevertheless provides insights that can be used in a broader context to further understanding of the temporal and spatial scales between landscape change and associated patterns of genetic diversity. Our findings indicate that the impacts of land cover and habitat changes can show a long time-lag and be long-lasting and that they can affect populations at broad spatial scales, especially when considering measures such as genetic diversity. Our study further reinforces the importance of considering the interaction between the amount of habitat and its arrangement in studies of habitat fragmentation and not only their relative strength or simple direction of effect. Regardless of (and perhaps because of) the complexity of landscape effects, it remains important to consider the potentially far-reaching impacts of landscape changes and management occurring today, and to be mindful of the nuances behind habitat loss and fragmentation effects.
