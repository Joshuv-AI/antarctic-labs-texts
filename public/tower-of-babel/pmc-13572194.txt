Sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium alleviates deoxynivalenol-induced intestinal injury in neonatal chicken through modulation of ULK1/LC3-mediated autophagy
Authors: Wenxuan Dong, Jieyu Sun, Hao Zheng, Jinxi Li, Shansong Gao, Lele Hou, Jihong Dong, Fu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deoxynivalenol (DON) disrupts intestinal epithelial integrity through oxidative stress and dysregulated autophagy. This study evaluates the efficacy of sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium (S-COS-Se) in protecting chicken embryos and intestinal epithelial cells from DON-induced intestinal injury. Five hundred 9-day-old embryonated eggs were allocated into 10 groups: Control, Na₂SeO₃, chitosan sulfate, chitosan-selenium, S-COS-Se, DON, and 4 combination groups. Additionally, intestinal epithelial cells were treated with DON alone or combined with various selenium formulations for 24 h. S-COS-Se co-treatment alleviated DON-induced cytotoxicity and restored cell viability while preserving epithelial morphology. S-COS-Se modulated autophagy through PI3K restoration, ULK1 suppression, and LC3-II/LC3-I ratio normalization with reduced p62 accumulation. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed intact mitochondrial cristae and reduced autophagic vacuoles. S-COS-Se attenuated oxidative stress by decreasing levels of malondialdehyde, hydroxyl free radical, and hydrogen peroxide, while enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities. It also restored immune homeostasis through elevated immunoglobulins and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, exhibiting superior efficacy over other selenium compounds. These findings demonstrate that S-COS-Se confers broad protection against DON toxicity by the integrated modulation of ULK1/LC3-mediated autophagy, antioxidant defense, and immune homeostasis. This novel organoselenium compound has potential as a feed additive in poultry diets to protect against mycotoxin-induced intestinal injury.

Introduction

Mycotoxins, secondary metabolites produced by fungi, represent the most prevalent natural contaminants in global food and feed supplies, posing severe threats to human health and animal production (Munoz-Solano et al., 2024). According to estimates by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), approximately 25% of agricultural commodities worldwide are contaminated with mycotoxins. Of these, deoxynivalenol (DON) emerged as the most prominent food safety hazard due to its high prevalence and potent toxicity (Hu, et al., 2024; Nazhand, et al., 2020). DON, which is primarily synthesized by Fusarium graminearum and Fusarium culmorum, extensively contaminates cereal crops including wheat, maize, and barley (Tu, et al., 2023). A 2023 investigation revealed alarming contamination rates of 71% in Asian samples and 78% in North American samples (Jiang, et al., 2024). Owing to its remarkable thermal stability and chemical resistance, DON cannot be completely eliminated during conventional food processing, allowing it to persist and bioaccumulate throughout the food chain. Epidemiological data indicate that 60–70% of wheat globally and 96.4% of feed samples in certain regions of China are contaminated with DON, with dietary exposure levels in a substantial proportion of the population exceeding the maximum limits established by the Codex Alimentarius Commission (Kolawole, et al., 2019; Polak-Sliwinska and Paszczyk, 2021). DON exhibits a broad toxicological profile, encompassing cytotoxicity, immunotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, neurotoxicity, and potential carcinogenicity, leading to its classification as a Group 3 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (Hu, et al., 2024; Jiang, et al., 2024). As the primary physical and functional barrier mediating material exchange between the organism and the external environment, the intestine becomes the initial target organ upon DON exposure. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that DON disrupts intestinal epithelial tight junctions, increases mucosal permeability, and induces inflammatory responses, consequently compromising intestinal barrier integrity (Ge, et al., 2022; Xue, et al., 2022). However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying DON-induced intestinal injury, particularly those involving critical cellular survival pathways such as autophagy, remain largely elusive. Therefore, determining the pathological mechanisms of DON-mediated intestinal damage and exploring effective nutritional intervention holds significant theoretical and practical implications for safeguarding poultry intestinal health and mitigating food safety risks.

Selenium is an essential trace element for both humans and animals, playing pivotal roles in thyroid hormone synthesis, immune regulation, and antioxidant defense (Pieczynska and Grajeta, 2015). However, Se deficiency remains a global concern, leading to limited growth, reproductive disorders, and compromised immunity (Hatfield, et al., 2014). To address this, organic Se sources have gained preference over inorganic forms such as sodium selenite (Na₂SeO₃), owing to their superior bioavailability, lower toxicity, and reduced environmental impact (Chen, et al., 2017; Hou, et al., 2020). Among organic Se formulations, selenium chitosan (SC) has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in improving animal performance, modulating immune responses, and alleviating oxidative stress (Lochi, et al., 2023; Wang, et al., 2024). Chitosan (CTS), a natural polycationic alkaline polysaccharide derived from chitin deacetylation, exhibits growth-promoting, antibacterial, and immunomodulatory properties (Guan and Feng, 2022). Chitooligosaccharide selenium (COS-Se), formed by integrating Se with low-molecular-weight chitooligosaccharide (COS), surpasses CTS in solubility and bioactivity. COS-Se can be further modified by introducing a sulfate group (SO₄²⁻) to generate sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium (S-COS-Se), a novel composite organoselenium compound that synergistically combines the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and microbiota-modulating benefits of both Se and sulfated chitosan (Zheng, et al., 2025). However, whether this multifunctional agent can effectively counteract DON-induced intestinal injury through the regulation of autophagy remains to be determined. Therefore, this study systematically investigated the protective effects of inorganic selenium (Na₂SeO₃), chitosan sulfate (CS), chitosan-selenium (COS-Se), and sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium (S-COS-Se) against DON-induced intestinal injury in chicken embryos. Ten experimental groups were established: Control, Na₂SeO₃, CS, COS-Se, S-COS-Se, DON, DON+Na₂SeO₃, DON+CS, DON+COS-Se, and DON+S-COS-Se. We hypothesized that S-COS-Se, by virtue of its superior bioavailability and synergistic antioxidant–anti-inflammatory properties, would alleviate DON toxicity via the modulation of ULK1/LC3-mediated autophagy.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Deoxynivalenol (DON, 50 mg, purity ≥ 99%) was purchased from Pribolab Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Sodium selenite (Na₂SeO₃), CS, chitosan-selenium (COS-Se), and sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium (S-COS-Se) were prepared and characterized as described in our previous studies (Zheng, et al., 2025). The selenium content in S-COS-Se ranged from 30 to 40 mg/g, and the sulfate content was 200–250 mg/g. Collagenase I, hyaluronidase, DMEM/F12 medium, CCK-8 cell proliferation assay kit, DNA molecular weight marker, 100× penicillin/streptomycin solution, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Total RNA Extractor (Trizol) was purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits for mTOR, ULK1, LC3-I, LC3-II, P62, PI3K, inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, TGF-β), immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA, SIgA), NF-κB, and oxidative stress markers (SOD, MDA, GSH-Px, GSSG, GST, GSH, GPx, POD, CAT, T-AOC, OFR, H₂O₂) were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) or Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Experimental animals

Fertilized Hy-Line Brown chicken eggs were obtained from Qingdao Shengdongfang Breeding Farm (Shandong, China). All eggs were uniformly sized, cleaned, and disinfected before incubation.

Experimental design and treatments

All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Qingdao Agricultural University (Approval No. QAUDY-2024-0501). A total of 500 9-day-old embryonated eggs were randomly allocated into 10 groups (n = 50 per group, with 5 replicates of 10 eggs each): (1) Control group (sterile distilled water); (2) Na₂SeO₃ group (25 μmol/L Se); (3) CS group (chitosan sulfate); (4) COS-Se group (chitosan-selenium, 25 μmol/L Se); (5) S-COS-Se group (sulfated chitooligosaccharide selenium, 25 μmol/L Se); (6) DON group (10 μg/50 g egg weight); (7) DON + Na₂SeO₃ group; (8) DON + CS group; (9) DON + COS-Se group; and (10) DON + S-COS-Se group. The DON and Se compounds were dissolved in sterile distilled water and administered via the chorioallantoic membrane route at 9 days of embryonic age (E9). A small window was made on the air cell side of the shell, and the solution was injected directly into the CAM vasculature. The injection volume was 100 μL (0.1 mL) per egg. After injection, the eggs were sealed with paraffin wax and returned to the incubator (37.5 ± 0.5°C, 55–60% relative humidity, automatic turning every 2 h) until hatching at 21 days (E21). The chicks were then euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the jejunum and ileum were dissected, weighed, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analysis. The intestinal index was calculated as: Intestinal index = (Intestinal weight/Body weight) × 100%.

Cell isolation and culture

Intestinal epithelial cells (IEC) were isolated from 15-day-old chicken embryos (E15). Briefly, embryos were disinfected with 75% ethanol and decapitated, and the small intestines were aseptically excised, washed 3 times with ice-cold PBS, and minced into approximately 1 mm³ fragments after removal of mesentery and fat. The tissue was digested with equal volumes of 2 mg/mL collagenase I and 2 mg/mL hyaluronidase at 37°C with gentle shaking for 1 h, filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer, and centrifuged at 1,000 g/min for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, seeded into culture flasks, and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 1 h to allow differential adherence of fibroblasts. Non-adherent IEC were then collected, re-centrifuged, and resuspended in DMEM/F12 containing 2.5% FBS, adjusted to 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and seeded into 6-well plates. After 24 h of treatment with the respective agents, Control, Na₂SeO₃ (5 μmol/L Se), CS, COS-Se (5 μmol/L Se), S-COS-Se (5 μmol/L Se), DON (2 μg/mL), DON + Na₂SeO₃, DON + CS, DON + COS-Se, or DON + S-COS-Se, cells were harvested for further analysis.

Measurement of cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, purified IEC were seeded into 96-well plates (100 μL/well, central positions) and pre-incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. The respective treatments were then added, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. The medium was removed, cells were washed twice with fresh medium, and 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added to each well. After incubation for the specified time, cell morphology was examined under an inverted microscope, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (MK3, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

ELISA analysis

The expression levels of autophagy-related proteins (ULK1, mTOR, LC3-I, LC3-II, P62, PI3K) in IEC, chorioallantoic fluid, plasma, and intestinal tissues were determined using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, samples and standards were added to pre-coated 96-well plates in triplicate. After incubation at 37°C for 60 min, wells were washed 5 times, and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibody was added to the samples. Following color development and termination with substrate and stop solutions, respectively, optical density was measured at 450 nm within 15 min. Standard curves were generated using Excel software, and sample concentrations were calculated accordingly.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from IEC and intestinal tissues using Trizol reagent following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and integrity were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (100 V, 30 min) and visualization of 28S and 18S rRNA bands under UV light. Reverse transcription was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit under the following conditions: 25°C for 10 min (primer annealing), 50°C for 30 min (reverse transcription), and 85°C for 5 min (enzyme inactivation). The qRT-PCR was conducted using a 7500 Real-Time PCR System with SYBR Green detection. The 20 μL reaction mixture was subjected to thermal cycling: 95°C for 5 min (initial denaturation), followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Gene-specific primer sequences for β-actin (internal control), mTOR, PI3K, LC3, and ULK1 are listed in Table S1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Measurement of oxidative stress parameters

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), the total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), glutathione S-transferase (GST), reduced glutathione (GSH), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), hydroxyl free radical (OFR), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in cell supernatants, plasma, and tissue homogenates were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute or Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from IEC and intestinal tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (AR0051, Boster, Wuhan, China). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against P62 (1:1000 dilution) and β-actin (1:5000 dilution). Following 3 washes with TBST (5 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. After 5 additional washes, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and quantified by densitometry (Chu, et al., 2018). Relative protein expression was normalized to β-actin.

Transmission electron microscopy

Intestinal tissue samples (E12) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C for 4 h, washed 3 times with PBS (pH 7.4), and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO₄) at 4°C for 2 h. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), samples were infiltrated with epoxy resin and acetone mixtures (1:1 and 2:1) for 2 h each, followed by pure resin overnight. Tissues were embedded in epoxy resin and polymerized at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (50–70 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, collected on 200-mesh copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate (30 min) and lead citrate (5–10 min). Mitochondrial morphology and autophagic vesicles were examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi H-7650, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least 3 times. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM as indicated. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 6.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among groups, followed by post-hoc tests (LSD or Tukey) where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Effects of S-COS-Se on DON-induced cytotoxicity in IEC

To evaluate the protective effects of different selenium compounds against DON-induced cytotoxicity, IEC were treated with DON (2 μg/mL) alone or in combination with Na₂SeO₃, CS, COS-Se, or S-COS-Se (5 μmol/L Se) for 24 h. CCK-8 assays revealed that DON exposure significantly reduced cell viability compared to the control group (P < 0.01), whereas co-treatment with all 4 selenium compounds significantly alleviated this cytotoxicity (P < 0.01), with S-COS-Se exhibiting the most pronounced protective effect (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01 vs. other selenium groups) (Fig. 1A). Morphologically, control IEC displayed typical cobblestone-like morphology with dense cell-to-cell contact, whereas DON-exposed cells showed severe damage, including reduced cell number, increased intercellular spacing, cellular shrinkage, and vacuolation. Notably, S-COS-Se treatment best preserved cellular integrity with minimal pathological alterations (Fig. 1B). S-COS-Se modulates DON-induced autophagy in IEC

To investigate whether S-COS-Se alleviates DON toxicity through the regulation of autophagy, the expression levels of key autophagy-related proteins and genes were examined. ELISA analysis showed that DON exposure significantly upregulated mTOR, ULK1, and the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, while downregulating PI3K compared to the control group (P < 0.01). S-COS-Se co-treatment partially reversed these alterations, with elevated PI3K expression reaching statistical significance (P < 0.01) relative to the DON group, with ULK1 and LC3-II/LC3-I levels showing a decreasing trend without statistical significance (Fig. 2A–F). Consistently, qPCR results demonstrated that DON significantly increased ULK1 mRNA expression, which was partially suppressed by S-COS-Se without reaching statistical significance, whereas no significant changes were observed in mTOR, LC3, or PI3K mRNA levels among groups (Fig. 2G–J). S-COS-Se ameliorates DON-induced oxidative stress in IEC

To assess the protective effects of S-COS-Se against DON-induced oxidative damage, the levels of oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities were measured. As shown in Fig. 3A–C, DON exposure significantly elevated the contents of MDA, OFR, and H₂O₂ compared to the control group (P < 0.01), indicating severe lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species accumulation. Co-treatment with S-COS-Se markedly suppressed these increases (P < 0.01), with its efficacy surpassing that of Na₂SeO₃, CS, and COS-Se (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Conversely, DON significantly reduced T-AOC and GSH/GSSG ratio relative to the control group (P < 0.01), reflecting compromised antioxidant defense. S-COS-Se effectively restored these parameters to near-normal levels (P < 0.01 vs. DON group), whereas the other selenium compounds showed relatively modest effects (Fig. 3D, E). These results demonstrate that S-COS-Se confers superior protection against DON-induced oxidative stress by attenuating lipid peroxidation and promoting endogenous antioxidant capacity in IEC. Effects of S-COS-Se on intestinal development in neonatal chicks

To evaluate the impact of S-COS-Se on intestinal growth and development, body weight, intestinal weight, and intestinal index were measured at hatching (E21). As shown in Fig. 4, no significant differences in body weight were observed among the 10 experimental groups (P > 0.05), indicating that neither DON nor selenium affected overall embryonic growth (Fig. 4A). However, DON exposure significantly reduced both intestinal weights compared to the control group (P < 0.05), suggesting impaired intestinal development. Notably, co-treatment with Na₂SeO₃, CS, COS-Se, and S-COS-Se all showed ameliorative trends in these parameters, though none reached statistical significance (Fig. 4B and C). S-COS-Se restores intestinal immune homeostasis in neonatal chicks

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of S-COS-Se, humoral immunity indices, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and anti-inflammatory/regulatory factors were quantified in plasma at hatching (E21). As shown in Fig. 5A–D, DON exposure significantly reduced the levels of IgG, IgA, and SIgA compared to the control group (P < 0.01), indicating compromised mucosal humoral immunity. S-COS-Se co-treatment significantly restored these immunoglobulin levels to near-normal values (P < 0.01 vs. DON group), whereas Na₂SeO₃, CS, and COS-Se showed relatively modest effects (Fig. 5A–D). Conversely, DON significantly upregulated the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-κB relative to the control group (P < 0.01), reflecting overt inflammatory activation. S-COS-Se effectively suppressed IL-6 and NF-κB increases (P < 0.01) and showed a downward trend for IL-1β without reaching statistical significance (Fig. 5E–H). Furthermore, DON exposure elevated the anti-inflammatory and regulatory factors TGF-β and IL-2, potentially as a compensatory response to inflammation, and these increases were significantly attenuated by S-COS-Se treatment (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) (Fig. 5I–K). S-COS-Se alleviates systemic oxidative stress in neonatal chicks

To evaluate the systemic antioxidant capacity of S-COS-Se, oxidative stress markers and antioxidant parameters were measured in plasma at hatching (E21). As shown in Fig. 6A–C, DON exposure significantly elevated the levels of MDA, OFR, and H₂O₂ compared to the control group (P < 0.01), indicating severe systemic oxidative damage. S-COS-Se co-treatment markedly suppressed these increases (P < 0.01), with its efficacy surpassing that of Na₂SeO₃, CS, and COS-Se (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Conversely, DON significantly reduced the activities of antioxidant enzymes, including GPX, GST, POD, SOD, and CAT, relative to the control group (P < 0.01). S-COS-Se effectively restored GPX, GST, POD, and SOD activities (P < 0.01), exhibiting superior antioxidant capacity compared to the other selenium compounds (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) (Fig. 6D–H). Furthermore, DON exposure significantly decreased T-AOC and GSH/GSSG ratio, reflecting compromised non-enzymatic antioxidant defense and disrupted redox balance, and these parameters were significantly ameliorated by S-COS-Se treatment (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6I, J). S-COS-Se modulates intestinal autophagy in neonatal chicks

To validate whether the autophagy-regulatory effects of S-COS-Se observed in vitro extend to the intestinal tissue, autophagy-related protein expression and ultrastructural morphology were examined in the intestine at hatching (E21). As shown in Fig. 7A–D, ELISA analysis revealed that DON exposure significantly upregulated ULK1 and downregulated PI3K compared to the control group (P < 0.01), whereas the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio exhibited an upward trend without statistical significance, consistent with the in vitro findings. S-COS-Se co-treatment markedly reversed the alterations in ULK1 and PI3K (P < 0.01), with its efficacy surpassing that of Na₂SeO₃, CS, and COS-Se (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Additionally, S-COS-Se tended to normalize the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio toward control levels. Notably, no significant changes in mTOR expression were detected among groups. Western blotting further demonstrated elevated p62 accumulation in the DON group, indicating impaired autophagic flux, and this effect was significantly attenuated by S-COS-Se treatment (Fig. 7E). Ultrastructural examination by transmission electron microscopy revealed that DON-exposed IEC exhibited damaged mitochondria with disrupted cristae, whereas S-COS-Se treatment preserved mitochondrial integrity with intact cristae (Fig. 7F). Discussion

DON represents the most prevalent mycotoxin threat in global cereal-based food and feed supplies. Recent investigations revealing an 85.8% DON contamination rate in wheat products across Asia and North America, which exceeds the FAO/WHO provisional maximum tolerable daily intake of 1 µg/kg body weight, underscore the severity of this global food safety hazard (Jiang, et al., 2024; Xu, et al., 2024). The gastrointestinal tract serves as the primary interface between the organism and the external environment, rendering IEC the initial targets of dietary DON exposure. Such exposure disrupts tight junctions, increases mucosal permeability, and induces inflammatory responses to compromise intestinal barrier integrity (Ge, et al., 2022; Xue, et al., 2022). Previous mechanistic investigations have predominantly focused on DON-induced ribotoxic stress, MAPK phosphorylation, and apoptotic signaling pathways. However, the precise regulatory networks governing intestinal cell survival under DON challenge remain unclear. Autophagy has emerged as a pivotal cellular adaptive mechanism in response to mycotoxin exposure. Early studies have established that autophagy protects IEC against DON toxicity by alleviating oxidative stress via IKK signaling pathway activation (Tang, et al., 2015). Subsequent investigations revealed that DON exposure induces cytotoxicity and apoptosis through autophagy activation via PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway inhibition in porcine IEC (Gu, et al., 2019). Recent advances further demonstrate that DON triggers autophagy through ROS-dependent AMPK-mTOR pathway activation in hepatocytes, which suggests cell type-specific autophagic responses (Chen, et al., 2023). However, although existing studies predominantly focus on inflammatory cascades and barrier disruption, autophagy modulation as a therapeutic target has received limited attention. The present study addresses this gap by investigating the protective efficacy of structurally optimized selenium formulations against DON-induced intestinal injury through autophagy-related cell survival pathways.

Selenium, an essential trace element for both humans and animals participating in thyroid hormone synthesis, immune regulation, and antioxidant defense, remains a global concern when deficient, leading to limited growth, reproductive disorders, and compromised immunity (Hatfield, et al., 2014). To address this nutritional challenge, organic selenium sources have gained preference over inorganic forms owing to their superior bioavailability, lower toxicity, and reduced environmental impact (Żarczyńska, et al., 2026). Previously, we established that S-COS-Se exerts comprehensive protective effects against cadmium-induced spleen injury through simultaneous modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory response, mitochondrial homeostasis, pyroptosis signaling, and immune microenvironment balance (Zheng, et al., 2025). The present study extends these findings by demonstrating that S-COS-Se exhibits superior protective efficacy compared with inorganic selenium, sodium selenite, CS, and unmodified chitosan oligosaccharide selenium against DON-induced intestinal toxicity. The sulfate groups enhance water solubility and electrostatic interaction with biological membranes, facilitate free radical scavenging, and potentially exert prebiotic effects (Xu, et al., 2022). The low molecular weight chitooligosaccharide backbone ensures efficient absorption within organisms, enabling stronger biological activities than high molecular weight chitosan counterparts (Liaqat and Eltem, 2018). The organic selenium moiety provides essential trace element functionality with superior bioavailability and safety profile compared with inorganic salts (Żarczyńska, et al., 2026). The parallel effectiveness of S-COS-Se against cadmium-induced immunotoxicity and DON-induced intestinal injury indicates that this structurally optimized selenium formulation possesses broad-spectrum protective capacity against diverse environmental toxins despite distinct toxicological profiles and target organs.

Oxidative stress and autophagy crosstalk constitutes a fundamental regulatory axis governing cellular homeostasis under environmental toxin challenge (Filomeni, et al., 2015). Deoxynivalenol exposure disrupts intestinal epithelial redox balance and triggers excessive autophagy activation through the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway inhibition, resulting in elevated ULK1 expression, increased LC3-II to LC3-I ratio, and p62 accumulation indicative of autophagic flux impairment (Kowalska, et al., 2022). The present study demonstrates that S-COS-Se co-treatment restores PI3K expression, suppresses ULK1 overactivation, and normalizes the LC3-II to LC3-I ratio with concomitant p62 reduction, indicating restoration of functional autophagic flux. These molecular events prevent excessive autophagy-induced cell death and maintain intestinal epithelial barrier function against DON toxicity. Previous investigations have established that selenium deficiency exacerbates autophagy dysfunction in multiple tissues, whereas organic selenium supplementation restores autophagy homeostasis through Nrf2 pathway activation and the upregulation of mTOR signaling (Lian, et al., 2023; Wang, et al., 2023). Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating that structurally optimized S-COS-Se exhibits superior capacity to modulate autophagy initiation compared with inorganic selenium and unmodified chitosan derivatives. The mechanistic contrast in how S-COS-Se protects against cadmium toxicity versus DON toxicity illustrates the adaptive versatility of this organoselenium formulation. Our previous investigation established that cadmium exposure primarily triggers pyroptotic cell death characterized by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and gasdermin E-mediated pore formation in splenic tissue (Zheng, et al., 2025). Pyroptosis represents an inflammatory form of programmed cell death fundamentally distinct from autophagy-dependent cell survival pathways (Filomeni, et al., 2015). S-COS-Se exhibits the remarkable capacity to modulate both cell death modalities contextually by inhibiting pyroptosis against cadmium and restoring autophagy homeostasis against DON. This dual protective capacity of S-COS-Se against mechanistically distinct toxin-induced cell death pathways demonstrates its broad-spectrum therapeutic potential. Furthermore, its ability to directly counteract DON-induced intestinal epithelial injury through ribotoxic stress and oxidative damage, rather than merely aggravating pre-existing conditions, emphasizes its relevance for primary prevention in feed safety management.

The hierarchical organization of biological systems demands integrated protection across cellular tissue and organismal levels for effective toxin defense (Li, et al., 2025). Our study demonstrates that S-COS-Se confers comprehensive cross-hierarchical protection against DON toxicity. At the cellular level, this is evidenced by restored IEC viability and normalized oxidative stress markers. At the tissue level, improved intestinal development indices and balanced immunoglobulin and inflammatory profiles are observed, whereas enhanced plasma antioxidant capacity and immune homeostasis occur at the systemic level. This cross-level consistency distinguishes S-COS-Se from inorganic selenium and single modified polysaccharides, which typically exhibit limited efficacy across biological scales (Li, et al., 2025; Wang, et al., 2017). Comparative evaluation reveals a clear potency gradient: S-COS-Se is the most effective, followed by COS-Se, then CS, and finally sodium selenite. This is attributable to the triple structural integration of sulfate-enhanced solubility, low molecular weight chitooligosaccharide facilitated absorption, and organic selenium-mediated antioxidant defense. Inorganic sodium selenite exhibits bioaccumulation risks that restrict dietary application (Guan and Feng, 2022). Chitosan sulfate lacks selenium essential for selenoprotein synthesis, and unmodified chitosan oligosaccharide selenium exhibits diminished membrane interaction capacity, limiting cellular uptake (Guan and Feng, 2022). The practical application of S-COS-Se as a poultry feed additive addresses the urgent need for effective DON mitigation, given that DON contaminates approximately 25 percent of global agricultural commodities, with particularly high prevalence in cereal-based poultry feeds (Adugna, et al., 2024; Fang, et al., 2022). The broad-spectrum protective capacity against both heavy metal and mycotoxin insults positions S-COS-Se as a next-generation feed additive for complex environmental contaminant scenarios in intensive animal production. Species-specific metabolic differences relative to mammals, together with the potential involvement of gut microbiota modulation, represent important directions for future mechanistic investigation and for optimizing practical implementation.

While this study provides compelling evidence for the protective effects of S-COS-Se against DON-induced intestinal injury, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the chorioallantoic membrane injection route employed here bypasses the gastrointestinal tract, which may not fully replicate the natural oral exposure to DON through contaminated feed. Future studies using dietary incorporation or oral gavage in post-hatch chicks would enhance the translational relevance of these findings for practical poultry feed applications. Second, while the protective effects of S-COS-Se have been clearly demonstrated, the baseline selenium status of the embryonated eggs was not determined in this study, and its potential impact on the observed outcomes warrants further clarification. Third, the mechanisms by which S-COS-Se modulates distinct cell death pathways, including pyroptosis in the case of cadmium versus autophagy in the case of DON, remain to be directly compared within a unified framework. Future studies employing integrated multi-omics approaches and side-by-side comparative experiments are warranted to establish a cohesive mechanistic model.

Conclusion

In summary, S-COS-Se exhibits comprehensive protective efficacy against DON-induced intestinal injury through integrated modulation of autophagy, oxidative stress, and immune homeostasis across cellular tissue and systemic levels. The superior potency of S-COS-Se compared with other selenium formulations underscores the critical importance of structural optimization in selenium design. The broad-spectrum protective capacity of this organoselenium compound against diverse environmental stressors positions S-COS-Se as a potential candidate for comprehensive feed safety intervention strategies. The practical application as a poultry feed additive addresses the urgent need for effective mitigation strategies against mycotoxin-contaminated cereal-based feeds.
