Hybrid CNN BiLSTM architecture for smart grid cyberattack detection using smart meter data
Authors: Fakir Mashuque Alamgir, Sawrav Das, Abdullah Rakib Akand, Anika Fariha, Md Rafidul Islam, Md Zakirul Islam Sarker, Nikosi Zuberi, Mithila Arman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReal-time cyber-attack intrusion detection faces serious challenges in the smart grid communications infrastructure, as intrusion tactics become more advanced. Traditional rule-driven detection methods are unable to adapt to diverse attack patterns in modern power networks. In this work, a supervised deep learning framework is developed, using a CNN for spatial feature extraction, a BiLSTM for temporal dependency modeling, and an Extra Trees ensemble classifier to produce robust decisions, to achieve real-time intrusion detection on high-frequency smart meter data. The CNN layer extracts hierarchical spatial features from 128-dimensional multi-modal meter measurements (e.g., voltage, current, frequency harmonics), and the BiLSTM component captures temporal dynamics by processing whole sequences of meter data in both forward and backward directions to capture attack-evolution patterns that unidirectional models miss. Attention mechanisms dynamically weight the relevance of temporal features and enhance both prediction accuracy and interpretability. The Extra Trees ensemble provides a robust, low-variance decision output as an alternative to a standard Softmax layer. The architecture addresses several challenges: class imbalance (2.49:1 ratio), high dimensionality and noisy sensor data from heterogeneous sources, the requirement for real-time (millisecond-level) inference, and the need for explainability. The model was evaluated on 72,073 labeled power-grid logs from the Mississippi State University Power Grid Testbed, including False Data Injection Attacks, denial-of-service, replay, and man-in-the-middle attacks versus normal operation. With stratified five-fold cross-validation, the model achieves accuracy of 92.17% (± 0.27%), precision of 90.58% (± 0.49%), recall of 81.24% (± 0.86%), F1-score of 85.66% (± 0.20%), and ROC-AUC of 95.60% (± 0.14%), with an inference latency of only 12 ms, which is suitable for utility-scale deployment. A comprehensive comparative study against nine imbalance-handling strategies (no handling, class weighting, SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTETomek, SMOTEENN, random over/under-sampling) confirms the chosen weighted-learning strategy as a Pareto-optimal choice for this dataset. Paired McNemar’s tests with Holm correction demonstrate that the proposed model’s improvements over the tested baselines are statistically significant (p<0.001). Ablation studies validate that bidirectional processing increases accuracy by 20.12 pp over a unidirectional CNN-LSTM, and that the Extra Trees head boosts precision by 10.09 pp over the standalone Extra Trees baseline. This work contributes to hybrid deep learning for cyber-physical systems and provides deployment guidance in terms of computational cost and a human-in-the-loop framework. Introduction

Motivation and problem statement

The global transition toward Smart Grids (SGs) represents one of the most significant transformations in modern critical infrastructure, driven by the imperatives of renewable energy integration, demand-side management, and enhanced operational efficiency. As of 2024, the global smart grid security market has exceeded $10 billion, reflecting the critical importance of protecting this infrastructure1. Smart Grids achieve operational goals through deep integration of advanced Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) with traditional power systems, enabling real-time bidirectional communication among generation, transmission, distribution, and consumption nodes. This technological convergence generates unprecedented operational benefits: distributed renewable energy can be seamlessly integrated, demand can be actively managed to stabilize the grid, and system operators obtain millisecond-resolution visibility into grid states.

However, this cyber-physical integration creates a critical vulnerability landscape. While traditional power grids operated in relative isolation, modern Smart Grids present extensive attack surfaces spanning multiple communication protocols. Sophisticated adversaries have demonstrated persistent capability to compromise power grid security, with threats evolving from simple disruptions to complex, coordinated campaigns. Recent surveys by Zibaeirad et al.2 and Ahmadi et al.3 highlight that the complicated structure and heterogeneous components of smart grids impose significant challenges in combating advanced cyber attacks. The 2015 Ukraine power grid attack and subsequent incidents have underscored the necessity of robust defense mechanisms4.

The technical challenge of smart grid intrusion detection is fundamentally multidimensional. Kabir et al.5 note that the challenge is amplified by the complexity of cyber-physical power system (CPPS) data, characterized by high dimensionality, temporal patterns, and operational noise. Additionally, the natural statistical structure of grid data presents formidable machine learning challenges: benign normal operation vastly outnumbers malicious intrusion events, resulting in severe class imbalance. Under such conditions, standard classifiers often achieve superficially high accuracy through majority-class bias while failing to detect critical attacks6.

This work addresses cyber-attack intrusion detection in smart grid cyber-physical systems, specifically focusing on malicious activities (False Data Injection Attacks, Denial of Service, replay attacks) that compromise grid security. To avoid terminological ambiguity, throughout this manuscript we consistently use the terms intrusion detection and cyber-attack detection to refer to our target task. Physical faults (equipment failures, line faults, short circuits) represent a complementary but distinct problem and are not considered here. Our dataset includes attack scenarios labeled as the “Attack” class versus benign operations labeled as the “Natural” class.

Existing approaches and limitations

Traditional rule-based intrusion detection systems have proven inadequate for smart grid protection due to their inability to generalize to novel or polymorphic attacks. Consequently, research has shifted toward data-driven approaches. Early work employed conventional algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests. While these methods offer interpretability, they struggle with the raw, high-velocity time-series data characteristic of modern grids. Gupta et al.7 and Tufail et al.8 have shown that while machine learning offers promise, handling high-dimensional and imbalanced data remains a persistent hurdle.

Recent research has advocated deep learning approaches. Gunduz9 demonstrated that Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) effectively extract local spatial patterns from grid data. Similarly, Alsaiari et al.10 introduced hybrid CNN-LSTM models to capture temporal dependencies. However, standard LSTM layers process sequences unidirectionally, potentially missing contextual information from future observations valuable for retrospective analysis. Furthermore, Nemade et al.11 emphasize that holistic cyber defense strategies require integrating multiple advanced methodologies rather than relying on single-model architectures. Building on this call for integrated approaches, recent work has also highlighted overfitting as a persistent concern in deep intrusion detection models: Agarwal et al.12 show that generative augmentation via CTGAN can materially reduce overfitting in power-system IDS, while Agarwal and his team13 present a hybrid attack/cascading-failure framework that reinforces the need to couple detection with explicit generalization-aware training regimes.

A specific gap remains in effectively combining bidirectional temporal modeling with robust ensemble classification. Most existing hybrid models rely on Softmax output layers, which may lack the decision robustness of ensemble forests. Additionally, many studies apply standard loss functions without explicit class weighting, leading to biased performance14. Liu et al.15 further argue that resilient detection must be coupled with control mechanisms, requiring detection systems with high precision to avoid triggering unnecessary and potentially destabilizing automated responses.

Research contributions

This research addresses the identified gap through development and rigorous evaluation of a Hybrid CNN-BiLSTM+Extra Trees architecture. We acknowledge that hybrid pipelines combining a CNN feature extractor, a recurrent temporal layer, and an ensemble decision head have appeared previously in general network-intrusion-detection literature; the contribution of this work is therefore not the bare existence of such a pipeline. Rather, the novelty of this work is the joint package of: (a) specialization of the architecture to co-located PMU measurements and Snort/relay/control-panel security indicators on a single 128-dimensional joint feature vector, with the BiLSTM specifically capturing post-disturbance recovery context that unidirectional CNN-LSTM baselines cannot see (“Hybrid CNN-BiLSTM+extra trees architecture”, ablation in “Performance comparison against ensemble methods”); (b) empirical demonstration that, for the moderate (2.49:1) imbalance regime of CPPS testbed data, inverse-frequency class weighting Pareto-dominates eight resampling alternatives including SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTETomek, and SMOTEENN (“Imbalance-handling comparative study”)—a result that runs counter to the field-wide default of “always SMOTE” and that to our knowledge has not previously been established head-to-head on this dataset; (c) paired-sample statistical validation against thirteen baselines using McNemar’s test with Holm–Bonferroni correction (“Statistical validation via paired McNemar’s test”), with disjoint 95% cross-validated confidence intervals; and (d) a multi-method explainability analysis (built-in/permutation/SHAP global importance, PD/ICE feature-effect curves, and LIME local explanations of true-positive, false-positive, and false-negative samples) that closes the gap between aggregate metrics and operator-facing evidence (“Explainability analysis”). Literature review

The integration of advanced information and communication technologies (ICT) into traditional power networks has transformed them into Smart Grids (SGs), enabling real-time monitoring, bidirectional communication, and automated control. However, this cyber-physical convergence introduces significant vulnerabilities, exposing critical infrastructure to sophisticated cyber threats such as False Data Injection Attacks (FDIA), Denial of Service (DoS), and Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks. Consequently, the development of robust Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) for Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) has become a focal point of contemporary research. Recent literature has progressively shifted from traditional rule-based and statistical methods toward data-driven Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) approaches capable of modeling the complex, non-linear dynamics of modern power grids.

Machine learning approaches in smart grid security

Early data-driven approaches primarily utilized conventional machine learning algorithms to classify grid states. Tightiz and Yang19 demonstrated the efficacy of Support Vector Machines (SVM) in identifying data integrity attacks, noting their robustness in high-dimensional feature spaces but highlighting scalability issues with massive real-time datasets. Talati et al.20 conducted a comparative analysis of supervised learning algorithms, finding that Random Forests (RF) and Decision Trees (DT) offered superior interpretability for distinguishing between natural faults and malicious cyber-attacks. However, these traditional models often struggle with the raw, high-velocity time-series data characteristic of Phasor Measurement Units (PMUs).

To address feature engineering challenges, Li et al.21 proposed a Multivariate Ensemble Classification (MEC) framework, integrating Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), LightGBM, and Extreme Learning Machines (ELM). Their work emphasized the superior performance of gradient-boosting frameworks in handling tabular logs and discrete security events. Similarly, Aribisala22 explored hybrid ML architectures, specifically combining Random Forest with SVM (RF-SVM), to improve packet filtering accuracy in smart grid networks. While these ensemble methods achieve high accuracy, they often lack mechanisms to explicitly model the temporal dependencies inherent in grid physics, potentially missing sophisticated “low-and-slow” attacks that evolve over time.

Deep learning and temporal modeling

The limitation of static ML models led researchers to explore Deep Learning (DL) architectures capable of automated feature extraction and temporal modeling. Gunduz9 proposed a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based system for energy theft detection, demonstrating that CNNs could effectively extract local spatial patterns from consumption data that rule-based systems missed. Yu et al.23 advanced this domain by utilizing Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) to tune Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), achieving higher convergence rates and overcoming local minima traps common in high-dimensional CPS datasets.

Given the sequential nature of power system measurements, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their variants have gained prominence. Alsaiari et al.10 introduced a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model specifically for SCADA systems, proving that LSTM’s memory cells could capture long-range dependencies in time-series data better than standard RNNs. Mukherjee et al.24 extended this to detecting “blind” cyber-attacks, where attackers actively mask their presence, showing that non-linear DL models could infer hidden attack states from subtle temporal anomalies.

Hybrid deep learning architectures

Recent state-of-the-art research has increasingly converged on hybrid architectures that synergize the strengths of multiple deep learning paradigms. A prevailing trend is the combination of CNNs for spatial feature extraction with LSTMs for temporal modeling. Srinivasu and Ahmed25 proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM model for DNP3 protocol intrusion detection, reporting 99.50% accuracy by first reducing dimensionality with CNN layers before sequence modeling. Ahmadi et al.3 provided a comprehensive review validating that such hybrid CNN-RNN architectures consistently outperform standalone models by simultaneously capturing local invariances and global temporal trends.

However, standard LSTMs process data unidirectionally, potentially missing context from future system states (e.g., post-disturbance recovery patterns) that are valuable for retrospective anomaly detection. Nemade et al.11 addressed this by proposing a “Holistic Cyber Defence Strategy” (HCDI) incorporating attention mechanisms and deep ensembles, though their focus remained largely on graph-based specifications rather than bidirectional time-series modeling. Al-Qirim et al.26 explored Digital Twin technologies combined with LSTMs, creating a virtual replica of the grid to validate ML predictions, yet noted the high computational cost of such simulations for real-time inference.

Ensemble and advanced hybrid techniques

To further enhance classification robustness and reduce false alarms, researchers have begun integrating Deep Learning feature extractors with Ensemble classifiers. Hu27 developed an ensemble feature learning framework using autoencoders and Random Forests, explicitly targeting the noise-contained nature of cyber-physical data. Rao and Verma28 and Zhang et al.29 investigated the use of Bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTMs) in non-grid domains, highlighting that bidirectional processing significantly improves recall by considering both past and future context.

Despite these advancements, a gap remains in effectively combining Bidirectional temporal modeling (BiLSTM) with Ensemble decision trees (Extra Trees) for Smart Grid security. Most existing hybrid models16,30–33 rely on standard Softmax output layers, which may not possess the decision robustness of ensemble forests. Furthermore, while class imbalance is a pervasive issue in intrusion detection, many studies34–38 apply standard loss functions rather than weighted learning strategies, leading to biased performance in minority attack classes.

Overfitting and generalization in power-system IDS

Overfitting is a particularly acute concern for smart grid IDS because (i) labeled attack samples are scarce relative to benign operation, (ii) training datasets are almost exclusively drawn from lab testbeds with limited topological diversity, and (iii) deep models have capacity far exceeding typical CPS feature dimensionality. Shahriar et al.17 address this by synthesizing minority-class samples via CTGAN, reporting measurable reductions in the train–test generalization gap for power-system IDS. Singh and Govindarasu18 couple attack detection with cascading-failure prediction in a single hybrid framework and note that joint-task regularization further constrains model capacity. These findings motivate the empirical overfitting diagnostics reported in “Empirical overfitting and generalization diagnostics” of this work, including train–validation loss tracking, cross-validation variance analysis, and train-test metric gaps.

State-of-the-art methods and recent advances

Recent advancements have further refined intrusion detection methodologies. Lee39 implemented Bidirectional LSTM with attention mechanisms for power grid security, achieving substantial improvements in attack detection sensitivity. Patel et al.40 proposed ensemble learning frameworks specifically designed for robust cyber-attack detection, emphasizing variance reduction through aggregation. Gupta et al.7 conducted comparative analysis of machine learning algorithms across multiple datasets, identifying ensemble methods as consistently superior to single-model approaches.

Feature selection and engineering remain critical preprocessing steps. Wilson et al.41 investigated feature selection methodologies for intrusion detection in cyber-physical systems, demonstrating that dimensionality reduction can improve both computational efficiency and model interpretability. Zhao et al.42 provided a comprehensive survey of deep learning-based false data injection attack detection, synthesizing literature across multiple domains and identifying common design patterns for effective detection architectures.

Recent work has also addressed resilience and recovery. Liu et al.15 examined resilient control and detection of cyber-attacks in smart grids, proposing adaptive mechanisms enabling systems to recover from successful intrusions. These approaches complement detection systems by ensuring continued operation despite security breaches—a critical consideration for critical infrastructure protection.

Extra trees ensemble classification component

The flattened BiLSTM output is passed to an Extra Trees classifier for final decision-making. Extra Trees (Extremely Randomized Trees) is an ensemble learning algorithm that aggregates predictions from M independent decision trees. Unlike Random Forest, Extra Trees does not use bootstrap sampling by default; each tree is fit on the entire training set, with stochasticity introduced through randomized split-feature subsets and randomly drawn split thresholds at each internal node. Random feature subsets and random thresholds collectively decorrelate the trees, so that aggregation reduces variance without the variance-bias trade-off introduced by bootstrap resampling. Extra Trees was selected over Softmax for several operational reasons. First, ensemble aggregation reduces prediction variance compared to single-model Softmax outputs, critical for security systems where decision stability is paramount. Second, Extra Trees provides inherent interpretability through feature importance scores, facilitating operator understanding. Third, the random threshold selection and feature subsetting reduce overfitting risk relative to a single decision tree fit to the same features. Fourth, for real-time inference, Extra Trees offers computational advantages: once trained, tree traversal requires only operations per prediction, comparable to neural network forward passes but with deterministic latency. Our ablation study (“Performance comparison against ensemble methods”) demonstrates that adding Extra Trees to CNN-BiLSTM features improves precision by 10.09 pp over standalone Extra Trees while maintaining 12 ms inference latency suitable for real-time deployment.

Statistical validation via paired McNemar’s test

We conducted paired McNemar’s tests on test-set predictions for the most informative comparisons involving the proposed model to verify that the observed gains are not attributable to sampling variability. McNemar’s test is the standard paired hypothesis test for comparing two classifiers on a shared test set, as it conditions on the discordant cells of the 2× 2 contingency table (one model right while the other is wrong). We apply the continuity-corrected statistic and the Holm–Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate across all pairwise comparisons reported in Table 5. The table covers the proposed model against the six most informative competing classifiers: Logistic Regression, Linear SVM, LightGBM, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, and Gaussian NB. The remaining baselines in Table 3 are distinguished from the proposed model by even wider effective margins and are not separately tabulated here.

Two patterns deserve emphasis. First, for every comparison in which the proposed Hybrid CNN-BiLSTM + ET is Model B (i.e., compared against Logistic Regression, Linear SVM, LightGBM, AdaBoost, and Gradient Boosting), the column is at least an order of magnitude larger than the corresponding : 3456 vs. 185 against Logistic Regression, 3458 vs. 182 against Linear SVM, 2230 vs. 193 against LightGBM, 3462 vs. 171 against AdaBoost, and 3079 vs. 183 against Gradient Boosting. This means that on the shared test set the proposed model correctly classifies thousands of samples that each baseline misclassifies, while the reverse case occurs only in the low hundreds. Second, in the single comparison where the proposed model is Model A (against Gaussian NB), the asymmetry is even more extreme (9587 vs. 551), confirming that Gaussian NB is a strictly inferior classifier on this dataset. After Holm correction, all p-values remain below 0.001, providing strong statistical evidence that the improvements of the proposed model over the tested baselines are not attributable to sampling variability.

The integrated hybrid approach achieves superior ROC-AUC of 95.62% versus 94.96%, demonstrating that combining both components produces optimal overall discrimination.

XGBoost achieved 86.97% accuracy and 92.72% ROC-AUC with excellent 86.97% precision, but significantly lower 64.08% recall. This represents failure to identify approximately 36% of actual attacks—3,696 undetected intrusions in the test set. This critical deficiency in attack detection sensitivity makes XGBoost unsuitable for security applications. The proposed model improved over XGBoost by +5.23 pp accuracy, +3.73 pp precision, and critically, +16.99 pp recall, recovering approximately 1,748 previously missed attacks and reducing undetected intrusions from 36% to 19%.

LightGBM achieved only 82.07% accuracy with notably poor 43.07% recall, missing nearly 57% of attacks (5,857 undetected intrusions) despite superior 88.28% precision. The proposed model improved by + 10.13 pp accuracy, + 2.42 pp precision, and dramatically +38.00 pp recall, nearly doubling attack detection sensitivity. This large recall improvement underscores the critical value of deep feature learning for intrusion detection.

Performance comparison against neural network methods

Neural network baselines (MLP, Hybrid CNN-LSTM) achieved average accuracy of 77.91% with average recall of 50.14%, substantially inferior to the proposed architecture.

MLP (Multilayer Perceptron) achieved only 83.75% accuracy, 74.48% precision, and 65.78% recall, substantially underperforming the proposed hybrid architecture. The 8.45 pp accuracy improvement over MLP (from 83.75% to 92.20%) combined with + 16.22 pp precision improvement and + 15.29 pp recall improvement demonstrates the value of combining CNN feature extraction and BiLSTM temporal modeling versus shallow feedforward networks. The simple feedforward architecture lacks mechanisms for capturing local feature patterns (CNN advantage) or temporal dependencies (BiLSTM advantage), resulting in inferior performance.

Hybrid CNN-LSTM variant using unidirectional LSTM achieved only 72.08% accuracy with 77.42% precision and 34.49% recall, substantially underperforming the proposed bidirectional architecture. The 20.12 pp accuracy improvement (92.20% vs. 72.08%) demonstrates that bidirectional context processing through BiLSTM provides substantial advantages over unidirectional LSTM. We note that both models received the same hyperparameter tuning budget (five-fold stratified CV on the training set with identical learning-rate, dropout, and batch-size grids), so the gap reflects the architectural difference rather than asymmetric tuning. The backward LSTM processing in BiLSTM incorporates future context information, enabling superior interpretation of current observations within their temporal context. This finding validates the architectural choice of bidirectional recurrence for temporal pattern recognition in cyber-physical systems.

Performance comparison against distance-based methods

KNN achieved 86.33% accuracy, 78.92% precision, and 71.30% recall with 91.67% ROC-AUC. While respectable, the proposed model improved by + 5.87 pp accuracy, + 11.78 pp precision, and + 9.77 pp recall. KNN’s performance degradation likely stems from the curse of dimensionality in the 128-dimensional feature space, where distance metrics become increasingly unreliable as dimensionality increases. The proposed model’s learned feature representations from CNN and BiLSTM substantially outperform distance-based similarity in the original feature space.

Critical performance deficiencies in linear classifiers

Linear classifiers and probabilistic models exhibited severely compromised performance, particularly in recall, rendering them unsuitable for security applications.

Logistic Regression achieved only 71.69% accuracy with catastrophic 4.02% recall—identifying only 4 of every 100 actual attacks. This represents detection of merely 414 attacks out of 10,289 test-set attacks, missing 9875 intrusive events (96% of attacks). The proposed model’s 81.07% recall represents a 20-fold improvement over Logistic Regression’s 4.02%, detecting approximately 8000 additional attacks compared to the linear approach. The proposed model’s 78.10 pp F1-Score advantage over Logistic Regression reflects fundamental inadequacy of linear decision boundaries for security applications.

Ridge Classifier achieved 71.59% accuracy with even worse 2.40% recall, detecting a minuscule 246 attacks while missing 10,043 instances (97.6% miss rate). This catastrophic failure of L2-regularized linear classification underscores the non-linear nature of the decision boundary separating attacks from normal operations.

Perceptron achieved only 66.06% accuracy with 34.05% recall, missing approximately 6800 attacks. The simple linear thresholding’s fundamental inadequacy for intrusion detection is evident in this severe performance degradation.

These dramatic findings provide compelling evidence that complex non-linear decision boundaries learned by the proposed hybrid architecture are essential for capturing the feature manifold distinguishing attacks from normal operations in cyber-physical systems. The 20–30 pp accuracy advantages over linear approaches with 77–78 pp F1-Score advantages constitute evidence that intrusion detection in smart grids involves inherently non-linear decision boundaries (Fig. 9).

Explainability analysis

Deployment of an intrusion-detection model in a utility-scale smart grid setting requires that security analysts understand why the model flags a particular sample, not merely that it does. We therefore complement the aggregate performance metrics of the preceding subsections with a multi-method explainability analysis of the trained Extra Trees classifier. Three families of techniques are applied: (i) global feature attribution via built-in impurity importance, permutation importance, and SHAP values; (ii) feature-effect visualization via Partial Dependence and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curves; and (iii) local explanations via SHAP and LIME on individual test samples spanning correctly and incorrectly classified cases (Figs. 10, 11, 12).

The separation is largest in the high-recall regime (recall > 0.8), which is precisely the operating region of interest for a security IDS, where a small increase in recall must not be purchased at a large cost in precision.

Feature-effect analysis

Global importance scores quantify which features matter, but not how they affect the prediction. We therefore plot Partial Dependence (PD) curves, overlaid with Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) traces, for the six highest-importance features, in Fig. 16. The near-flat PD curves confirm that no single feature is monotonically decisive: the classifier relies on high-order interactions between the voltage and current channels across buses, consistent with the architectural motivation for CNN-based feature extraction in “Hybrid CNN-BiLSTM+extra trees architecture”.

Partial dependence (red dashed) and individual conditional expectation (blue) curves for the top six features, evaluated on the Extra Trees classifier for the Attack class. The bimodal ICE distribution (one band near 0, one band near 1) indicates that the sample population separates into two sub-populations whose classification is locally insensitive to the feature value, with the decision being driven by the interaction with other predictors.

Limitations, dataset bias, and domain-shift risks

While the reported metrics are encouraging, we explicitly moderate our claims of real-world deployability. The headline numbers in this paper are an upper bound on what the proposed architecture can deliver under the data-collection conditions of the Mississippi State testbed, and they should not be read as a forecast of operational performance on a live utility network without targeted re-validation. Specifically:

Single-testbed validation: All evaluation is performed on the Mississippi State University Power Grid Testbed49. Testbed datasets capture a narrow range of topologies, loads, and communication-protocol configurations compared to operational utility networks. Consequently, the reported 92.20% accuracy should be read as a ceiling on what is achievable in the testbed regime, not as a prediction of deployment performance.

Dataset bias: The 2.49:1 attack-to-benign ratio is an artifact of the data-collection protocol, in which attack scenarios were actively triggered. Operational networks exhibit vastly more benign-than-attack behavior (ratios often >1000:1), which shifts the precision–recall trade-off and changes the practical meaning of the reported metrics. The 95.62% ROC-AUC is threshold-independent and is more portable across base-rate shifts than the fixed-threshold accuracy/F1 numbers.

Domain-shift risks: Three classes of domain shift are likely in cross-utility deployment: (i) sensor-level shift—different PMU manufacturers produce measurements with different noise characteristics and sampling jitter; (ii) protocol-level shift—the Snort rule set that generates discrete security indicators varies across operators; (iii) attack-distribution shift—adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures evolve continuously, so a 2026-trained model may degrade on 2028 traffic. Our deployment recommendation is therefore to retrain quarterly and to treat any cross-utility transfer as requiring at minimum a domain-adaptation step, which we leave to future work on federated and transfer-learning extensions of the architecture.

Lack of cross-dataset validation: We do not currently report performance on independent datasets (e.g., ICS-Flow, SWaT). This is a limitation; external benchmarking is planned but out of scope here due to differences in feature modalities and labeling conventions. Consequently, the results here should be interpreted as within-domain evidence of architectural efficacy, not as a generalization claim across all smart-grid deployments.

Scope of imbalance-handling conclusions: The imbalance-handling study in “Imbalance-handling comparative study” is specific to the 2.49:1 ratio. For severely imbalanced () operational data, we recommend repeating this comparative study rather than extrapolating our conclusions.

Conclusion

The proposed Hybrid CNN-BiLSTM+Extra Trees architecture represents a principled and effective approach to intrusion detection in smart grid cyber-physical systems, achieving strong performance through strategic combination of complementary learning paradigms: convolutional neural networks for automated feature extraction from 128-dimensional sensor data, bidirectional long short-term memory networks for temporal dependency modeling, and Extra Trees ensemble learning for robust probabilistic classification. At the default decision threshold, the model attained performance metrics of 92.20% accuracy, 90.70% precision, 81.07% recall, 85.62% F1-Score, and 95.62% ROC-AUC on a single-run held-out test split, and mean cross-validated values of 92.17%, 90.58%, 81.24%, 85.66%, and 95.60% respectively, substantially exceeding thirteen baseline and comparative methods spanning traditional machine learning, ensemble approaches, neural networks, and online learning paradigms. Improvements over the tested baselines are statistically significant at p<0.001 after Holm correction via paired McNemar’s testing (“Statistical validation via paired McNemar’s test”), and the 95% confidence interval for the hybrid’s cross-validated accuracy ([0.9170, 0.9232]) is disjoint from that of the closest competitor. The comprehensive empirical evaluation across five complementary metrics on a preprocessed dataset of 72,073 samples with class imbalance (2.49:1 attack-to-benign ratio) demonstrates consistent superiority, with particularly dramatic improvements over linear classifiers (20–30 percentage-point accuracy advantages, 77–78 percentage-point F1-Score advantages, and a 20-fold improvement in recall over Logistic Regression).

The research provides compelling evidence that intrusion detection in cyber-physical systems requires non-linear decision boundaries, that bidirectional temporal context processing substantially enhances pattern recognition compared to unidirectional approaches (20.12 pp accuracy improvement), and that learned feature representations substantially enhance ensemble classification (2.30 pp accuracy improvement, 10.09 pp precision improvement over the standalone Extra Trees baseline). A nine-way imbalance-handling comparative study (’‘namerefsec:imbalancespscomparison”) supports inverse-frequency class weighting as Pareto-optimal for the 2.49:1 regime, outperforming SMOTE, ADASYN, Borderline-SMOTE, SMOTETomek, SMOTEENN, and random over/under-sampling on the joint criterion of precision, accuracy, training cost, and CV–test stability. Empirical overfitting diagnostics (“Empirical overfitting and generalization diagnostics‘’)—train–validation loss curves, cross-validation variance, and train–test gap analysis—confirm that the regularization stack operates as intended and that reported metrics do not rely on memorization.

The architecture’s balanced performance across metrics indicates well-calibrated decision boundaries suitable for operational deployment. The 95.62% ROC-AUC provides flexibility for operators to adjust detection thresholds according to organizational risk tolerance without sacrificing underlying discrimination capacity; for example, at a recall-favoring threshold (Fig. 9) the same trained model achieves substantially higher attack-detection sensitivity at the cost of additional false alarms, illustrating the operational headroom available without retraining.

We explicitly caveat these claims with the limitations analysis in “Limitations, dataset bias, and domain-shift risks”: single-testbed validation, testbed-induced base-rate bias, and domain-shift risks across sensors, protocols, and adversary populations. Future work should address dataset generalization across multiple smart grids via external benchmarking (e.g., ICS-Flow, SWaT), attack-type-stratified performance analysis identifying systematic failure modes, explainability enhancement enabling security analysts to understand detection reasoning, real-time deployment optimization for resource-constrained environments, adversarial robustness evaluation against deliberately crafted evasion attacks, and federated learning extensions enabling privacy-preserving collaborative training across utilities. The research contributes substantive advances in hybrid architecture design for security-critical pattern recognition, establishes the necessity of sophisticated non-linear and ensemble methods for cyber-physical system protection, and provides practical guidance for smart-grid operators deploying intrusion detection systems in increasingly connected and vulnerable infrastructure.
