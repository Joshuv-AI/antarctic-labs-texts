Cerebral protection in extended arch repair for acute type A aortic dissection
Authors: Lauren A Gillinov, Mikolaj Berezowski, Alexandra E Sperry, James Lao, Daniel Bazianos, Michael A Catalano, Kendall M Lawrence, Wilson Y Szeto, Nimesh D Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the association between cerebral perfusion strategy and neurologic outcomes following extended arch repair for acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD).MethodsWe identified patients who underwent extended arch repair for ATAAD at our institution between 2002 and 2024. Patients were stratified by cerebral perfusion strategyL antergrade certebral perfusion (ACP) or retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP). Outcomes included stroke and a composite of 30-day mortality, stroke, and temporary neurologic dysfunction. Multivariable analysis was used to evaluate the association between cerebral perfusion strategy and the composite outcome.ResultsA total of 206 patients met the study inclusion criteria (median age, 58.7 years; 25.2% female). ACP was the most common cerebral perfusion strategy (58.7%; n = 121), followed by combined ACP and RCP (32.0%; n = 66) and then RCP (9.2%; n = 19). The median circulatory arrest time was longer in cases using combined ACP and RCP (62 minutes) compared to ACP (38 minutes) or RCP (34 minutes) (P < .001). Permanent stroke occurred in 22 patients (10.7%) and did not differ by cerebral perfusion strategy. The composite outcome occurred in 53 patients (25.7%), with the highest frequency in the combined group, although the difference was not significant on pairwise comparison. On adjusted analysis, cerebral perfusion strategy was not an independent predictor of the composite outcome.ConclusionsDespite prolonged circulatory arrest times, cases using combined ACP and RCP were not associated with an increased rate of stroke. The combined approach may help extend the safe duration of circulatory arrest in extended arch repair for ATAAD.Key Words: cerebral perfusion, circulatory arrest, acute type A aortic dissection

Central Message

Despite prolonged circulatory arrest, cases using ACP and RCP were not associated with higher stroke rates. This approach may help extend the safe duration of circulatory arrest.

Perspective

There is ongoing debate regarding the optimal cerebral protection strategies for operations requiring circulatory arrest. In this retrospective study at a high-volume institution, neurologic outcomes were similar across cerebral perfusion strategies. A combined approach using ACP and RCP was used preferentially in cases with prolonged circulatory arrest.

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a cardiovascular emergency requiring immediate surgical intervention. Despite advances in surgical technique and perioperative care, stroke remains a devastating complication.1, 2, 3 Cerebral protection strategies have improved neurologic outcomes in cases requiring circulatory arrest, yet the optimal approach to neuroprotection remains uncertain.

A growing body of evidence supports the use of extended arch repair in selected patients with ATAAD.4, 5, 6, 7 While this approach offers potential long-term benefits, the procedures are complex and require extended periods of circulatory arrest, heightening the risk of cerebral ischemia.8 Identifying the most effective cerebral protection practices in these cases is critical to minimize neurologic risk. To address this, we evaluated the association between cerebral perfusion strategy and postoperative stroke following extended arch repair for ATAAD.

Methods

This study was deemed exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (Protocol 855137) on July 16, 2024. The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study Design and Patient Selection

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing ATAAD repair at the University of Pennsylvania. Our institutional type A aortic dissection (TAAD) database was queried for patients who underwent emergent extended arch repair for ATAAD between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2024. Extended arch repair was defined as replacement of the ascending aorta and aortic arch with reimplantation of 1 or more supra-aortic vessels. Hemiarch procedures were not included. All patients in the final cohort underwent a period of circulatory arrest with cerebral perfusion. The cohort was stratified into 3 groups based on cerebral perfusion strategy: isolated antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP), isolated retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP), and combined ACP and RCP. The study period was divided into four 5-year increments to assess trends in cerebral protection strategies over time.

Cerebral Perfusion Techniques

Our group has described our approaches to establishing cerebral perfusion in previous work.9,10 Typically, isolated ACP is performed via the right axillary artery at a rate of 7 to 10 mL/kg/minute, and isolated RCP is performed via the snared superior vena cava at a rate of 250 to 350 mL/minutes, titrated up to a maximum central venous pressure of 25 mmHg. In combined ACP and RCP cases, RCP is usually initiated first, and once the arch is opened, the innominate and carotid arteries are directly cannulated for ACP using balloon-tipped cannulas.

Variables and Outcomes

Data on preoperative characteristics, operative details, and perioperative outcomes were collected from our institutional TAAD database and review of medical records. The primary outcome was permanent postoperative stroke. Permanent stroke was defined as neurologic dysfunction persisting until discharge or death, with radiographic evidence of cerebral or spinal cord infarction. Stroke was classified as dissection-related or procedure-related, as previously characterized by our group.9 The secondary outcome was a composite defined as the occurrence of ≥1 of the following events within 30 days: mortality, stroke, or temporary neurologic dysfunction. Temporary neurologic dysfunction was defined as any neurologic deficit attributable to cerebral or spinal cord ischemia that resolved prior to discharge or death. Perioperative outcomes were assessed up to and including postoperative day 30.

Statistical Analysis

The use of cerebral perfusion strategies was compared over time and across intraoperative temperatures. Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range); categorical variables, as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons used the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, with the Dunn test and Fisher exact test for post hoc pairwise analyses. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of the composite outcome. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included age, prior stroke, cerebral perfusion strategy, degree of hypothermia, aortic cannulation, circulatory arrest time, and cross-clamp time. The reference groups were RCP (for cerebral perfusion strategy) and deep hypothermia (for degree of hypothermia). Missing data were assessed for all variables and were low (<5%) for each variable. Descriptive statistics were calculated using all available data, and complete cases were included for multivariable analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 (Bonferroni-adjusted P < .0167 for pairwise comparisons). Analyses were performed with STATA 18.5 (StataCorp).

Perioperative Outcomes

Thirty-day outcomes are presented in Table 3. In the overall cohort, 30-day mortality was 9.7% (n = 20), and permanent stroke occurred in 10.7% (n = 22); neither outcome differed by cerebral perfusion strategy. Temporary neurologic dysfunction that resolved by discharge occurred in 8.7% of patients (n = 18) at a similar rate across the 3 groups. The composite outcome of 30-day mortality, stroke, or temporary neurologic dysfunction occurred in 25.7% of the patients (n = 53) and differed significantly by cerebral perfusion strategy (P = .046), with the highest incidence in the ACP + RCP group (36.4%, n = 24). Discussion

In this 2-decade, single-center experience of extended arch repair for ATAAD, permanent stroke rates did not differ by cerebral perfusion strategy, and cerebral perfusion strategy was not an independent predictor of the composite outcome of 30-day mortality, stroke, or temporary neurologic dysfunction. DHCA, first described by Griepp and colleagues in 1975,11,12 was the traditional approach to cerebral protection in aortic arch surgery. But although DHCA may provide acceptable cerebral protection for up to 30 minutes, longer durations are associated with increased risk of neurologic injury and mortality.13 DHCA is also associated with systemic complications, including coagulopathy, endothelial dysfunction, and multisystem organ dysfunction.14,15 Consequently, once cerebral perfusion techniques were developed, practice patterns have shifted from use of DHCA alone to more moderate degrees of hypothermia combined with cerebral perfusion.16 This approach extends the safe duration of circulatory arrest while reducing the systemic complications of profound hypothermia.17, 18, 19 Major guidelines recommend cerebral perfusion over isolated DHCA during circulatory arrest20,21 because it is associated with lower rates of neurologic injury and mortality.22, 23, 24 For extended arch repair, expert consensus supports the use of ACP with either MHCA or DHCA.20

Although randomized data comparing ACP and RCP are lacking, ACP has emerged as the preferred cerebral perfusion strategy owing to its physiologic antegrade flow and presumed superior cerebral oxygenation.8,17,22 Nonetheless, multiple studies have failed to demonstrate superior outcomes with ACP.25, 26, 27, 28 In line with widespread trends, our institutional experience reflects increasing use of ACP over time (alone or in combination with RCP), while RCP alone continues to be used infrequently. In our study, ACP with MHCA was the most frequently used cerebral protection strategy overall, in 43.0% of cases.

Extended arch repair for ATAAD is inherently complex and carries a high risk of neurologic complications owing to prolonged periods of circulatory arrest and arch manipulation. Prior studies report permanent neurologic injury rates between 7% and 18%.29,30 Our observed stroke rate of 10.7% aligns with those findings and did not differ significantly by cerebral perfusion strategy.

The composite outcome of 30-day mortality, permanent stroke, and temporary neurologic dysfunction occurred in 25.7% of our cohort. Although the incidence of the composite outcome was highest among patients in the ACP + RCP group (36.4%), pairwise comparisons showed no significant differences across the 3 groups. Additionally, cerebral perfusion strategy was not independently associated with the composite outcome on adjusted analysis.

In our study, combined ACP and RCP was associated with significantly longer circulatory arrest times (62 minutes vs 38 minutes with ACP vs 34 minutes with RCP), suggesting its selective use in more complex cases. Prior studies have examined cerebral perfusion techniques in the setting of prolonged circulatory arrest. In the ARCH International database, stroke and mortality rates remained stable for ischemic periods up to 80 minutes with ACP,31 whereas Tanaka and colleagues32 reported a linear increase in stroke rate with longer circulatory arrest times with RCP. An analysis comparing ACP and RCP for circulatory arrest times between 40 and 80 minutes found similar stroke rates but a higher incidence of temporary neurologic dysfunction with RCP.33 These findings suggest that ACP may be superior to isolated RCP for prolonged circulatory arrest, although direct comparative data remain limited.

In the present study, combined ACP and RCP was used in 32% of cases. Reports on this approach are limited, although it was the predominant cerebral perfusion strategy for aggressive arch replacement in a study by Yang and colleagues,30 and Estrera and colleagues34 reported on its use in cases with prolonged circulatory arrest. While the combined approach adds complexity, our present findings suggest that it may offer additional neuroprotection in selected high-risk cases. There is a theoretical basis for its use: ACP provides physiologic antegrade flow and oxygen delivery, prolonging the safe interval of circulatory arrest, while RCP, although not providing substantial perfusion, maintains cerebral hypothermia and flushes air and embolic debris from cerebral vessels.35 By targeting the 2 major mechanisms of neurologic injury after circulatory arrest—hypoperfusion and embolism—the combined strategy may provide synergistic neuroprotection in complex aortic arch surgery.

This study was limited by its retrospective nature and single-center design. Procedures were performed at a single, high-volume aortic center by a small number of surgeons. Cerebral perfusion strategy likely was influenced by individual surgeon preference, which we did not account for. Although the 3 cerebral perfusion groups were similar with respect to measured preoperative characteristics, residual confounding from differences in operative factors may have influenced outcomes.

In conclusion, complex aortic arch repair for ATAAD carries unique neurologic risk owing to the required period of circulatory arrest. In this study, stroke rates did not differ by cerebral perfusion strategy. Notably, circulatory arrest times were significantly longer in the ACP + RCP group, suggesting that this approach may mitigate the neurologic risk associated with prolonged ischemic periods. These findings may support the selective use of a combined cerebral perfusion strategy in complex extended arch repair for ATAAD.
