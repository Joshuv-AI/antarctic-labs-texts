The Functional Machine Calculus III: Choice Early Announcement
Willem Heijltjes e

Abstract
The Functional Machine Calculus (Heijltjes 2022) is an extension of the lambda-calculus that preserves confluent reduction and typed termination, while enabling both call-by-name and call-by-value reduction behaviour and encoding the computational effects of mutable higher-order store, input/output, and probabilistic computation. In this note the calculus is extended to capture exception handling and loop constructs.

1
Introduction

The Functional Machine Calculus (FMC)
[
Heijltjes-2022
,
Barrett-Heijltjes-McCusker-2023
]
is a new approach to combining the
\lambda
-calculus—as the foundation of functional programming—with computational effects. It takes a view of the
\lambda
-calculus as an instruction language for an abstract machine with a single stack in the style of Krivine
[
Krivine-2007
]
, where
application
is
push
,
abstraction
is
pop
, and
variable
is
execute
. To accommodate effects, the calculus introduces the following two extensions
[
Heijltjes-2022
]
.

Locations

Multiple stacks on the machine, each named by a
location
, allow the encoding of various effects via push and pop actions:
mutable higher-order store
, as stacks of depth at most one;
input/output
, as pop-only respectively push-only streams; and
probabilities
and
non-determinism
as probabilistically respectively non-deterministically generated streams.

Sequencing

The introduction of
sequential composition
and its unit, imperative
skip
, gives control over evaluation behaviour away from strict call–by–name, and allows the encoding of Plotkin’s call–by–value
\lambda
-calculus
[
Plotkin-1975
]
, Moggi’s computational metalanguage
[
Moggi-1991
]
, and Levy’s call–by–push–value
[
Levy-2003
]
.

Encoding effects into the generalized operators of the calculus, rather than introducing primitives, means that two key properties of the
\lambda
-calculus are preserved.

Confluence

Reduction in the FMC is confluent in the presence of effects. This is a consequence of the separation of operational behaviour, which governs the machine, from local reduction behaviour, which is the interaction of consecutive push and pop actions. Reduction equivalence for state then implements the algebraic laws of Plotkin and Power
[
Plotkin-Power-2002
]
.

Types

The FMC can be simply typed, which conveys strong normalization and termination of the machine. This gives a solution to the problem of typing higher-order store:
Landin’s Knot

[
Landin-1964
]
, which encodes recursion via higher-order store, cannot be typed (in its full generality).

This paper introduces a third extension to the FMC,
choice
, to include a wider range of computational behaviours:
constants
,
conditionals
,
data constructors
,
exception handling
, and
loops
. These have in common that, semantically, they are modelled by
sums
or
coproducts
: for example, the Booleans are given by the type
1+1
, the error monad is given by the functor
TX=E+X
for a set of exceptions
E
, and loops are modelled by taking a map in
A\to A+B
to one in
A\to B
(looping on
A
, exiting on
B
)
[
Bloom-Esik-1993
]
. Together, these will be referred to as
choice
constructs.

The aim is sixfold. First and second, to preserve
confluence
and
types
: the resulting calculus should support a natural, confluent reduction relation, and a notion of simple types that guarantees termination of the machine and strong normalization of reduction (in the absence of loops). Third,
minimality
: choice constructs should be captured with as few syntactic operators as possible, avoiding any overlap in functionality and minimizing the interactions or reductions governing the semantics of the calculus. Fourth,
operational semantics
: the calculus should continue to be an instruction language for a simple and natural abstract machine. Fifth,
seamless integration
: different effects should combine seamlessly, without requiring lifting operations. Finally, the FMC has a natural
first-order restriction
, where function arguments are restricted to be (first-order) values, not arbitrary terms. The sixth aim is to preserve this restriction, which ensures that choice constructs are independent of the calculus being first-order or higher-order.

The approach has been to reconsider the notion of
choice
from first principles, with the aim of capturing coproducts and the constructs that they model in a simple and natural way, satisfying the six criteria above. This led to three (mostly) standard syntactic constructions, which however interact with the stack in subtle ways to give new and unexpected reduction behaviours. The resulting calculus is in some ways highly familiar, yet simultaneously in other ways novel and surprising.

In contrast with stateful effects, confluence and type safety are expected for exception handling. The main results are to integrate exceptions seamlessly with stateful effects, to support natural operational and denotational semantics, and to capture a wide range of behaviours with an elegant, minimal syntax. This note will discuss the background literature, introduce the selected
choice
constructs from operational considerations, formally define the calculus and its type system, and demonstrate how it captures existing formulations. For simplicity of exposition, the calculus will omit the
locations
modification, and feature only
sequencing
and
choice
. Proofs are incomplete at the time of writing, and hence omitted, leaving the intended theorems as conjectures.

2
Background

The study of effects in the
\lambda
-calculus is broad and varied. The overarching problem is that effects are
sequential
when modelled through global updates, while the
\lambda
-calculus is fundamentally
denotational
, governed by an equational theory. The contradiction manifests concretely as the loss of
confluence
, so that the choice of reduction strategy becomes salient, and syntactic control over evaluation behaviour becomes necessary. Semantic reasoning about programs, a main strength of the
\lambda
-calculus, is severely impaired—and much research has the objective of restoring it. To quote Filinski in 2011:

Yet few would confidently claim that programs with computational effects are by now as well understood, and as thoroughly supported by formal reasoning techniques, as types and terms in purely functional settings. — Filinski
[
Filinski-2011
]

Since Landin’s seminal work
[
Landin-1964
,
Landin-1965
,
Landin-1966
]
most practical functional programming languages employ a call–by–value (
cbv
) semantics, as formalized by Plotkin
[
Plotkin-1975
]
, where call–by–name (
cbn
) behaviour is forced locally with
thunks
. However, for reasoning about programs a
cbn
-semantics is desirable. The inefficiency of
cbn
may be overcome by
graph reduction

[
Wadsworth-1971
]
(or
lazy evaluation
, or
call–by–need

[
Turner-1985
,
PeytonJones-1987
,
Ariola-Felleisen-Maraist-Odersky-Wadler-1995
]
), but the imposed notion of sequentiality is opaque, and a semantic approach to effects becomes more urgent.

Among various proposals, such as taming destructive updates through
interference control

[
Reynolds-1978
,
OHearn-Power-Takeyama-Tennent-1999
]
or
uniqueness types

[
Smetsers-Barendsen-vanEekelen-Plasmeijer-1993
]
, and explictly typing effects in
type and effect
systems
[
Gifford-Lucassen-1986
,
Jouvelot-Gifford-1991
,
Talpin-Jouvelot-1994
,
Nielson-Nielson-1999
]
, Moggi’s account of effects as monads
[
Moggi-1991
]
became prominent. It casts sequentiality as composition in the Kleisli category of a monad
[
MacLane
]
, which may then be presented as sequential programming through syntactic sugar, such as Haskell’s
do
-notation.

However, monads do not compose, raising the issue of how to combine multiple effects. Haskell 98 resorts to cumbersome stacks of
monad transformers

[
Liang-Hudak-Jones-1995
]
(their limitations are discussed in
[
Kiselyov-Sabry-Swords-2013
]
). One solution is to take sums of monads
[
Luth-Ghani-2002
]
; another, added in Haskell 2010, is to weaken monads to
applicative
(or
monoidal
) functors
[
McBride-Paterson-2008
]
, which do compose. Both however need intrusive
lifting
functions to access different effects, suggesting that the problem is not merely with compositionality, but instead with explicit composition as such.

Levy’s
call–by–push–value
(
cbpv
)
[
Levy-2003
]
unifies effects in a single monad (not unlike the IO monad in Haskell
[
PeytonJones-Wadler-1993
]
) formed by an adjunction separating
values
and
computations
. It arose in the context of two closely related alternatives. Power, Robinson, and Thielecke sought to connect Hasegawa’s
\kappa
-calculus
[
Hasegawa-1995
]
with a semantics in
premonoidal
categories
[
Power-Robinson-1997
,
Power-Thielecke-1999
]
, which impose sequentiality on composition in a monoidal category by weakening its naturality laws—a semantics later applied to
cbpv

[
Levy-Power-Thielecke-2003
]
. This was mirrored by Hughes’ Arrows for Haskell
[
Hughes-2000
,
Lindley-Wadler-Yallop-2010
]
, which generalise Kleisli composition of monads in a similar way
[
Atkey-2011
]
. The similarities between
cbpv
and
\kappa
-calculus are striking: both feature sequentiality through an operational semantics in a stack machine, a decomposition of computations and values, and an embedding of
cbn
- and
cbv
-
\lambda
-calculus. It was
cbpv
that gained traction, being extended
[
Egger-Mogelberg-Simpson-2014
]
and rediscovered
[
Ehrhard-Guerrieri-2016
]
through linear logic and forming the basis of
effect handlers

[
Plotkin-Pretnar-2013
]
.

Effect handlers

[
Plotkin-Pretnar-2013
,
Kammar-Lindley-Oury-2013
,
Wu-Schrijvers-2015
,
Marsik-Amblard-deGroote-2021
]
combine
cbpv
with
algebraic effects

[
Plotkin-Power-2002
,
Hyland-Plotkin-Power-2006
,
Hyland-Levy-Plotkin-Power-2007
]
, a view of effects as algebraic theories that enables equational reasoning with effect operators, though not reduction. This is implemented by restricting
continuations
to enforce
sequentiality
(a restriction that is sometimes relaxed), generalising
exception handlers

[
Benton-Kennedy-2001
]
.

Continuations as a means of controlling sequential behaviour date back to ALGOL60 and Landin’s J-operator
[
Landin-1965
]
used to model its
go to
construct (Reynolds provides an overview of this early history
[
Reynolds-1993
]
), subsequently developed in the context of the
cbv
-
\lambda
-calculus
[
Felleisen-Friedman-1986
,
Felleisen-Friedman-Kohlbecker-Duba-1987
]
. Three developments then gave continuations a central place in programming language theory and practice: the discovery of a Curry–Howard relation with classical logic
[
Griffin-1990
]
, soon after expressed in Parigot’s
\lambda\mu
-calculus
[
Parigot-1992
,
Streicher-Reus-1998
]
; their usefulness in compilers
[
Appel-1992
]
; and the charactarization of the continuation monad as universal among effect monads
[
Filinski-1994
,
Filinski-1996
]
.

The distinction between continuations and
exception handling
is wafer-thin in a higher-order language, and exceptions in
\lambda
-calculus are frequently studied through continuations
[
DeGroote-1995
,
Ong-Stewart-1997
,
Bierman-1998
,
Crolard-1999
,
Kameyama-Sato-2002
,
Lebresne-2009
,
VanBakel-2019
]
. Subtle differences in reduction arise depending on the choice of semantics: in classical logic and
\lambda\mu
-calculus, or in intuitionistic coproducts as the
exceptions-as-values
approach generally models them
[
Wadler-1985
,
Spivey-1990
,
PeytonJones-Reid-Henderson-Hoare-Marlow-1999
]
, or in intuitionistic continuations as do the exception handlers
[
Benton-Kennedy-2001
,
Levy-2006-Exceptions
]
that inspired effect handlers
[
Plotkin-Pretnar-2013
]
.

The Functional Machine Calculus itself
[
Heijltjes-2022
,
Barrett-Heijltjes-McCusker-2023
]
originates in a confluent probabilistic
\lambda
-calculus
[
DalLago-Guerrieri-Heijltjes-2020
]
, and while developed independently, many individual aspects are familiar.
Sequencing
, which locates sequentiality in the operational semantics of a stack machine, appeared as
\kappa
-calculus
[
Hasegawa-1995
,
Power-Thielecke-1999
]
, in compiler theory
[
Douence-Fradet-1998
]
, and in practice as
concatenative programming

[
Herzberg-Reichert-2009
,
Pestov-Ehrenberg-Groff-2010
,
Mihelic-Steingargner-Novitzka-2021
]
.
Locations
are akin to the
channels
of process calculi
[
Hoare-1978
,
Milner-1980
,
Milner-Parrow-Walker-1992
]
, and their encoding of mutable strore is that of Haskell’s
MVar
mutable variables for shared-memory concurrency
[
PeytonJones-Gordon-Finne-1996
]
. A close overall relative are
action calculi

[
Mifsud-Milner-Power-1995
,
Milner-1996
]
. The FMC as a whole is nevertheless novel and surprising, as the introduction sets out.

The
choice
extension in this note uses standard syntactic constructions for exception handling, with subtly novel reduction behaviour due to the interaction with the stack. It takes a semantics in intuitionistic sums, and avoids swerving into continuations-territory by the design requirements of
simple types
and
first-order restriction
. Key inspirations are compiler optimizations through sequent calculus and sequentially-used continuations
[
Downen-Maurer-Ariola-PeytonJones-2016
,
Maurer-Downen-Ariola-PeytonJones-2017
]
, from which the terminology of
jumps
and
joins
is adopted. The
loop
construct is believed to be a new syntactic interpretation of the standard semantics for iteration
[
Bloom-Esik-1993
]
, dual to recursion
[
Simpson-Plotkin-2000
]
.

3
Development

The FMC extends the
\lambda
-calculus with
sequential composition

({\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}})
and
skip

({\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}})

signifies

The FMC introduces this concept into the Krivine Machine (which traditionally halts with an abstraction and empty stack, or a variable), and it is a key feature underlying many aspects of the calculus, including the design of the type system, the semantics, and the termination and normalization proofs
[
Heijltjes-2022
,
Barrett-Heijltjes-McCusker-2023

The central idea of this paper is to generalise

{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\in\mathcal{J}

{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\top\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\bot\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
with the conditional
\mathsf{if}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~\mathsf{then}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~\mathsf{else}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
given by two joins as
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\,{;}\,\top\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,\bot\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
(which associates as
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}(B\,{;}\,\top\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M)\,{;}\,\bot\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
, and assumes that both
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
exit with
skip
). Our
join
is then also a standard
case switch
:

\mathsf{switch}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~(\mathsf{case}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j_{1}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}:~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N_{1}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\dots\mathsf{case}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j_{n}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}:~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N_{n}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}})

becomes
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,j_{1}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N_{1}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\dots\,{;}\,j_{n}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N_{n}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. Such switches may have different
fall-through
policies (whether after a matching case also subsequent cases are considered), often with a
break
or
return

break
is another jump.

A subtle but key aspect is the difference between returning a constant as a value on the stack, and evaluating it as a jump. A standard function returning a Boolean should be interpreted in the former way, pushing
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\top\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
or
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\bot\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
onto the stack and exiting with
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
, and indeed terms are generally expected to exit successfully. To
evaluate
a constant, triggering subsequent join constructs, is the domain of conditionals, case switches, and exception handling.

or exits on other jumps. This is implemented by the reduction

{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~;~\mathsf{if}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~\mathsf{then}~(\mathsf{do}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~\mathsf{while}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}})~\mathsf{else}~{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~.

As before, control flow through
break
or
return
keywords may be modelled by viewing these as jumps, and positioning corresponding
join
constructs outside the loop.

On an abstract machine, it is standard to manage sequencing with a
continuation stack
, where
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
pushes
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
, and
skip
pops. The FMC, in the formulation used here, introduces this into the Krivine Machine. The generalization to jumps is captured on the machine by modifying the continuation stack to hold
conditional terms
:
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,j\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
pushes
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
, while
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
inspects the head
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}i\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and pops
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
if
i=j
, discarding it otherwise. A loop
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M^{j}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
pushes
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}j\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M^{j}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and continues as
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. The three constructs thus represent natural instructions on the machine, with
j
representing a forward jump and
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M^{j}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
a controlled backward jump.

Types for the FMC indicate the input and output stacks of successful machine evaluation: a term of type
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{m}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
expects an input stack of terms typed
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
through
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and produces a return stack with types
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{m}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. Semantically, terms are morphisms in a category where objects are type vectors
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}={\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{m}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
(assuming a single stack for simplicity; with multiple stacks, objects are families of type vectors).
With jumps, return types become sums of jump-indexed type vectors, each written
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
, rendering types as

{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\sigma\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}(\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8})\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}~.

For example, using
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
for the empty stack the type for Booleans becomes
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\top{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}1.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\bot\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. As this illustrates, jumps (as terms) are the
injections
of the coproduct.

A join
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,j\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
composes on the
j
-indexed summand; for example for a Boolean
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
we have
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\,{;}\,\top\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\star{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}1.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\bot\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. Codiagonals (the natural maps
A+A\to A
) are given implicitly by two joins with the same return type
and
jump. For example, if
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
are both typed
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
then the conditional
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}B\,{;}\,\top\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,\bot\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
will have the type
{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
as well.

The types for
loops
follow the standard semantics of iteration. Since
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M^{j}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
iterates on
j
and exits on other jumps, the return stack of
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
for the jump
j
must match its input stack, and this summand is removed for
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M^{j}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
. For example, if
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\star\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}
and
B
is a Boolean, then
{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,B\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\top{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\tau.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\bot\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}

The order of input types follows that of the abstractions, so that the type for the above identity is ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma\tau\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau\sigma\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ where ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}x\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\sigma\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ and ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}y\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, and in general identity types are ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\leftharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ where ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\leftharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ is the reverse vector to ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$. Further notation includes: concatenation of type vectors by juxtaposition ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\sigma\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, and the empty vector as ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}1\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$. The disjoint union of choice types as families is written ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\sigma\kern 0.4pt}\kern-1.0pt{}_{I}{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\kern-1.0pt{}_{J}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ where $I\cap J=\varnothing$, and ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ denotes the singleton family consisting of the vector ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ indexed by $j$. A choice type may then be written ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}{\kern 2.0pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}\kern 2.0pt}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}j_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ where the jumps are disjoint.
\par Semantically, types ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\leftharpoonup}{\sigma\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 2.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Rightarrow$}}}}\kern 2.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\kern-1.0pt{}_{J}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ are function spaces ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\sigma\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\rightarrow{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\kern-1.0pt{}_{J}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, stack types ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 1.0pt{\dots}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ are products ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\times\dots\times{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\tau_{n}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, and choice types $\{{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{j}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\mid j\in J\}$ are indexed sums $\sum_{j\in J}({\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}{}_{j}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}})$. \emph{Locations} are indexed products~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Barrett-Heijltjes-McCusker-2023}{}{}]}, giving the duality between state and exceptions observed in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Dumas-Duval-Fousse-Reynaud-2012}{}{}]}.

A \emph{stack typing judgement} $\vphantom{(}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\vdash}~S\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{{}:{}}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ assigns a stack the type vector ${\vphantom{)}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\kern 0.4pt\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{\tau\kern 0.4pt}\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$. The \emph{typing rules} for the calculus are given in Figure~\ref{fig:types}.

\par The careful design of the choice constructions for the FMC has subtle implications, which manifest more clearly in the context of the stack machine, the operational semantics of the calculus. We consider these here.
\par First, we consider data constructors. Standard case constructs such as are found in ML or Haskell are a direct interpretation of the disjunction elimination rule of natural deduction,
$$\mathsf{case}~M~\mathsf{of}~(\mathsf{inl}~x~\to~N~;~\mathsf{inr}~y~\to~P)$$generalised to indexed sums (data types with constructors) and multiple parameters. As does ML~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Appel-MacQueen-Miler-Tofte-1988}{}{}]}, the present approach unifies data constructors and exceptions, and renders the above as follows.
$${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M~\,{;}\,~\mathsf{inl}\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle x\rangle.\,N~\,{;}\,~\mathsf{inr}\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle y\rangle.\,P\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$$Instead of parameterizing the constructors as $\mathsf{inl}~x$ and $\mathsf{inl}~y$, the two joins use a regular \emph{pop}, ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle x\rangle.\,N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ or ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle y\rangle.\,P\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, to collect the return value from ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$. This is possible due to the nature of the FMC as a stack-based calculus, where ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ leaves its return value on the stack: an application to a constructor $\mathsf{inl}\,Q$ translates direcly as ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}[Q].\,\mathsf{inl}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$, pushing ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}Q\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ to the stack and exiting with $\mathsf{inl}$. Construction and deconstruction generalize to any number of arguments, simplyfying the syntax from a \emph{sequence} of cases each with a \emph{sequence} of parameters, to the single constructor ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}M\,{;}\,j\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$. The correctness of the interpretation is guaranteed by typing, which ensures that ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ does not exit with $\mathsf{inr}$ which would incorrectly trigger ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}P\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ in the example above, or in the untyped case by strictly jumps into \emph{constants/constructors} and \emph{exceptions}, and maintaining that constants and constructors are returned on the stack.
\par The fall-through aspect of joins makes them a potential solution to the problem of \emph{extensible data types}~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Swierstra-2008}{}{}]} (adding further constructors to an existing data type). However, the present exposition does not include \emph{inductive} types, or \emph{recursion} to compute with them, so at the moment this is only an interesting future direction.
\par Secondly, we will look at exception handling mechanisms. Though there are many variations, the underlying principle is the same: a computation may \emph{raise} or \emph{throw} an exception, which is then \emph{caught} by some \emph{handler}. First, we discuss the formulation by De Groote~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{DeGroote-1995}{}{}]}, for it raises an important issue. It considers exception handling in ML, and proceeds to give a variation on it via classical logic; we will refer to the original ML variant as \emph{coproduct-style}, and that of classical logic, \emph{continuation-style}. The syntax is as follows, slightly simplified and using $M,N$ for terms and $e$ for exceptions.
$$\mathsf{raise}~M\qquad\qquad M~\mathsf{handle}~(e\,x)\Rightarrow N$$The key distinction between coproduct-style and continuation-style exception handling is found in the rule for handling a return value (that is, not an exception), where continuation-style requires that $e$ is not free in $V$, but coproduct-style does not:

$$M~\mathsf{handle}~(e\,x)\Rightarrow N\quad\mapsto\quad\mathsf{let}~e\,x=N~\mathsf{in}~M$$By contrast, coproduct-style handling treats exceptions as \emph{constructors} or \emph{constants}. Handling may then be interpreted by a case construct, as follows, where the variable $y$ matches every other case than the exception $e$, returning it unchanged.
$$M~\mathsf{handle}~(e\,x)\Rightarrow N\quad\mapsto\quad\mathsf{case}~M~\mathsf{of}~(e\,x\to N~;~y\to y)$$Types for the two encodings clarify the difference: for continuation style, in $\mathsf{let}~e\,x=N~\mathsf{in}~M$ we expect $e:A\to B$ for $x:A$, $N:B$, and $M:B$, giving the continuation type $(A\to B)\to B$ to $\lambda e.M$. The two styles are thus related by the standard higher-order encoding of a coproduct $A+B$ as $(A\to C)\to(B\to C)\to C$.
\par Exception handling in the literature generally takes one these two forms: interpretations in $\lambda\mu$-calculus~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Ong-Stewart-1997,Bierman-1998,Crolard-1999,VanBakel-2019}{}{}]} are naturally continuation-style, while the exceptions-as-values approach~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Wadler-1985,Spivey-1990,PeytonJones-Reid-Henderson-Hoare-Marlow-1999}{}{}]}, including the exception monad $TX=E+X$, is in coproduct style. It is clear that effect handlers should be considered as continuation-style, and a handler understood as a binding construct for effect operations. A tentative interpretation is the following, where operators $\mathsf{op}$ are variables and a handler $H$ is a \emph{prefix} of push- and pop-actions, binding in $N$.
$$\begin{array}[]{r@{~=~}ll}\mathsf{op}\,V\,(x.M)&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}[V].\,\mathsf{op}~\,{;}\,~\langle x\rangle.\,M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\\
N~\mathsf{handle}~H~\mathsf{to}~x.M&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}H.\,N\,\,{;}\,\,\langle x\rangle.\,M\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\\
(\mathsf{op}\,x\,k\to N)+H&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}[\langle x\rangle.\,\{\star/k\}N].\,\langle\mathsf{op}\rangle.\,H\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\end{array}$$Note that the continuation ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}k\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ in ${\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}$ is discarded, and replaced with \emph{skip}, and correspondingly the continuation $K$ in $\mathsf{op}\,V\,K$ is composed sequentially, rather than passed as an argument. This interprets handlers directly in their \emph{generic} form~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Plotkin-Power-2003,Plotkin-Pretnar-2013}{}{}]}. The interpretation is correct for handlers satisfying the requisite law.
$$\mathsf{op}~V~(x.M)~;~K\quad=\quad\mathsf{op}~V~(x.(M;K))$$\emph{Shallow handlers}~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Hillerstrom-Lindley-2018}{}{}]} superficially appear closer to coproduct-style, since handlers become \emph{affine} (used at most once). However, for subtle reasons, reductions are not fully compatible with the expected interpretation in \emph{choice} constructs. This would be as below, where an effect operator is a \emph{jump} and a handler $H$ is a postfix sequence of \emph{joins}. The discrepancy needs further work to fully understand.
$$\begin{array}[]{r@{~=~}ll}\mathsf{op}\,V&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}[V].\,\mathsf{op}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\\
N~\mathsf{handle}~H&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}N\,\,{;}\,\,H\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\\
H+(\mathsf{op}\,x\,k\to N)&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}H\,\,{;}\,\,\mathsf{op}\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle x\rangle.\,\{\star/k\}N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\\
\mathsf{return}~x\to N&{\vphantom{(}\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\star\kern 1.0pt{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\raise 0.51663pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}{\mathbin{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}\kern 1.0pt\color[rgb]{0.8,0,0}\langle x\rangle.\,N\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}\end{array}$$\par\par\par\par\lx@frontmatter@fallback\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Conclusion}
\par This note has given an overview of current progress on the \emph{choice} extension for the Functional Machine Calculus. At present the design is complete, proofs are expected, and a start has been made on a close comparison with previous work. These are promising: the calculus is expected to fulfil the six aims given in the introduction (\emph{confluence}, \emph{types}, \emph{minimality}, \emph{operational semantics}, \emph{seamless integration}, and \emph{first-order restriction}), and the discussion in the previous section is starting to give an interesting and revealing perspective on past work. Clearly, this is only a starting point, and there is much more to investigate.
