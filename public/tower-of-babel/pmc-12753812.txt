An acceleration-only modal circle-fitting method for in-situ fatigue damage detection
Authors: Da Som Kang, Seung-Hoon Bae, Seunghun Baek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study proposes a novel modal circle-fitting method to estimate natural frequency and damping ratio from output-only acceleration. Validated through vibration-fatigue testing on an aerospace bracket, the method successfully detects initial, non-visible fatigue damage through key indicators: a decrease in natural frequency under random vibration and the emergence of a ‘split-peak’ pattern under sinusoidal excitation.

Introduction

The operational lifecycle of modern aerospace structures is defined by exposure to demanding and highly variable loading environments, including cyclic stresses from operational vibrations, aerodynamic forces, and transient shocks. Over time, this repetitive loading can initiate and propagate fatigue damage, a progressive material degradation process that begins with microscopic cracks and can culminate in catastrophic structural failure if left undetected1. A particularly challenging form of this damage is Widespread Fatigue Damage (WFD), where multiple small cracks form and grow concurrently, often evading conventional inspection techniques until they coalesce and compromise the structural integrity of a component2. The potential for such failures has historically led regulatory bodies and industry standards to mandate conservative, schedule-based inspection and maintenance regimes3. While effective at ensuring safety, these time-based approaches are inherently inefficient, often leading to unnecessary component replacements, extended aircraft downtime, and substantial Maintenance, Repair, and Overhaul (MRO) costs4.

In response to these economic and operational pressures, the aerospace industry is increasingly shifting towards Condition-Based Maintenance (CBM) paradigms, which are enabled by advanced Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) technologies5. SHM aims to replace scheduled inspections with continuous, real-time assessment of a structure’s health, integrating sensor networks with diagnostic algorithms to detect, locate, and characterize damage as it occurs. This approach promises not only to enhance safety by providing early warnings of impending failure but also to significantly reduce Direct Operating Costs (DOC) by optimizing maintenance schedules and extending component life6. The success of any SHM strategy, however, is fundamentally dependent on the robustness, reliability, and practical viability of its underlying damage detection methodologies.

Among the various SHM modalities, Vibration-Based Damage Detection (VBDD) has emerged as a leading candidate for global airframe monitoring. The core principle of VBDD is that structural damage—such as a crack, delamination, or loosened connection—alters the physical properties of the system, namely its mass, stiffness, and damping. These physical changes, in turn, induce measurable changes in the structure’s dynamic or modal response, such as its natural frequencies, damping ratios, and mode shapes7. Early VBDD research predominantly focused on tracking shifts in natural frequencies as the primary damage indicator8. While conceptually simple, this approach suffers from significant limitations. Natural frequency shifts are typically sensitive only to substantial, widespread damage and often fail to detect incipient or localized cracks, which produce only subtle changes in global stiffness. Furthermore, these small, damage-induced frequency shifts can be easily masked by environmental and operational variations, such as changes in temperature, humidity, and payload, which can produce frequency shifts of a similar or even greater magnitude, leading to high rates of false-positive or false-negative detections9.

A critical review of the existing literature reveals three persistent and interconnected research gaps that have hindered the transition of VBDD from a laboratory concept to a flight-certified engineering tool. First, much of the foundational research on VBDD has relied on overly simplified test geometries, such as uniform cantilever beams10,11. While these idealized models have proven useful for validating fundamental principles, their simplistic nature fails to capture the complex dynamic behaviors and localized failure modes inherent in real aerospace components12. Actual structures often incorporate geometric discontinuities—bends, holes, and bolted joints—that serve as stress concentrators and introduce critical phenomena such as fretting fatigue, which are entirely absent in simple beam models10,12. This discrepancy significantly limits the direct applicability of such studies to real-world applications and underscores the need for validation on more realistic, complex geometries.

Second, there is a notable scarcity of material-specific vibration-fatigue data, which is essential for developing robust structural health monitoring systems. While the static mechanical properties and traditional S–N fatigue behavior of many advanced aerospace materials are well-documented, little is known about how their modal parameters change under vibration-induced fatigue. This study addresses this gap by focusing on PH 15-7Mo, a precipitation-hardening stainless steel widely valued in aerospace applications. Although its high strength and temperature performance are well known, the lack of data on its dynamic response evolution under prolonged vibrational loading is a key barrier to enabling effective VBDD strategies for this material.

Finally, the methodological divide between Experimental Modal Analysis (EMA) and Operational Modal Analysis (OMA) continues to constrain the practical application of VBDD13–15. EMA techniques can yield a complete set of absolute modal parameters but their reliance on measured input forces renders them impractical for in-service monitoring where excitation is ambient and unmeasured13,14. Conversely, OMA is designed for such output-only conditions but traditionally cannot supply the absolute stiffness and damping values (i.e., scaled mode shapes) that are indispensable for quantitative damage assessment 14,15. This long-standing challenge highlights the need for novel methods that can bridge the EMA-OMA gap by robustly estimating absolute modal parameters from response-only data under realistic operational conditions.

This study introduces a comprehensive framework designed to systematically address these three challenges. The core of this framework is a novel analytical methodology that bridges the EMA-OMA gap by enabling the robust estimation of absolute modal parameters from acceleration-only measurements under realistic base-excitation conditions. This method is validated not on a simplified beam, but on a complex PH 15-7Mo bolted bracket assembly that replicates the geometric and interface complexities of real aerospace structures. By integrating this methodological innovation with realistic component-level testing and material-specific fatigue characterization, this work delivers a holistic strategy for detecting and tracking the evolution of vibration-induced fatigue. The findings demonstrate the identification of new, highly sensitive damage indicators that provide a direct path toward the development of practical, high-fidelity SHM systems for next-generation aerospace platforms.

Theoretical framework

The accurate identification of modal parameters from vibration response data is the cornerstone of VBDD. This section develops the theoretical framework for a novel method that enables the extraction of these parameters under base excitation using only acceleration measurements, thereby overcoming a primary limitation of traditional modal analysis techniques in operational settings.

The modal circle-fit method is a well-established technique in EMA for extracting modal parameters from measured Frequency Response Functions (FRFs)16. The method is based on the geometric property that, for a lightly damped system with well-separated modes, the Nyquist plot (a plot of the imaginary part versus the real part of the FRF) forms a near-perfect circle in the vicinity of a natural frequency17. By fitting a circle to the measured FRF data points around a resonance peak, one can accurately estimate the modal frequency (from the point of maximum angular velocity on the circle) and the damping ratio (from the circle’s diameter and frequency spacing).

A critical limitation of this powerful method is its reliance on a true FRF, which is defined as the ratio of the system’s output response to the input force in the frequency domain, H(ω) = X(ω)/F(ω). The calculation of an FRF therefore requires simultaneous measurement of both the input force (e.g., from a force transducer on an impact hammer or shaker) and the output response (e.g., from an accelerometer). This requirement restricts the classical circle-fit method to controlled laboratory settings (EMA) and makes it inapplicable to in-service SHM scenarios (OMA), where the input forces from operational or ambient sources are unknown and unmeasured.

The time-domain acceleration signals are converted to their complex frequency spectra, Ax​(ω) and Ay​(ω), using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Here, Z is a mode-dependent scaling constant that incorporates the mode shape component (ϕhj​) and the modal participation factor (γj). While its absolute determination is challenging, it is not strictly necessary for damage detection. Our approach is based on tracking changes from a healthy baseline. The practical calibration of Z can be achieved in two primary ways. First, a baseline measurement can be taken when the component is in a known healthy state. The circle-fit from this initial test defines the healthy signature, implicitly capturing the parameter Z. The SHM system then simply monitors for deviations from this initial state, making the drift itself the damage indicator. In addition, a model-based approach can be used where a high-fidelity Finite Element (FE) model, as used in our study, provides accurate estimates of the mode shapes and participation factors. These values allow for an analytical estimation of Z to establish a robust baseline for health monitoring. Therefore, the method’s utility lies not in calculating the absolute value of Z, but in tracking its evolution from a well-defined initial state.

Equation (13) is particularly powerful for damage detection. The coordinates of the circle’s center are (XZ, − Z/(4ξj​rj​)). This mathematical structure provides a basis for decoupling different types of damage signatures. Horizontal coordinate (XZ) is directly proportional to the scaling factor Z, which in turn is a function of the mode shape (ϕhj​). Therefore, the horizontal coordinate serves as a direct indicator of changes in the structure’s dynamic deflection shape. Any damage that alters how the structure physically deforms under excitation will cause a shift in this coordinate. While the vertical coordinate (− Z/(4 ξj​rj​)) is more complex, as it is a function of the mode shape (via Z), the damping ratio (ξj​), and the natural frequency (via rj​). It represents a combined measure of the system’s energy dissipation (damping) and stiffness properties.

A change that primarily affects the system’s energy dissipation (i.e., a change in the damping ratio ξj​) will cause a predominantly vertical shift in the circle’s center. In contrast, a change that alters the structure’s stiffness distribution or boundary conditions will modify the mode shape (ϕhj​), causing the scaling factor Z to change, which in turn results in both horizontal and vertical shifts of the circle’s center. This ability to distinguish between changes in damping and changes in modal scaling provides a much richer diagnostic capability than traditional methods that track only a single parameter like frequency.

Experimental set-up

A rigorous experimental program was designed to validate the proposed acceleration-only identification method and to characterize the evolution of vibration-fatigue damage in a realistic aerospace component. The program integrated a complex test article, advanced material characterization, and high-fidelity finite element modeling to ensure the relevance and reliability of the findings.

Results

The experimental program yielded a rich dataset that revealed distinct and highly sensitive indicators of fatigue damage progression under different loading conditions. The application of the proposed acceleration-only circle-fit method, in conjunction with physical observation and non-destructive inspection, allowed for a detailed characterization of the damage evolution from initial degradation to final failure.

Damage characterization under random base excitation

Three replicate tests were conducted under the MIL-STD-810G random vibration profile, with failure occurring after 38, 49, and 36 min, respectively, when visible cracks initiated at the A-side bend of the bracket. Throughout each test, the dynamic response was monitored at one-minute intervals to capture the evolution of the system’s modal properties.

The first natural frequency, a conventional metric in vibration-based damage detection (VBDD), displayed a pronounced and progressive decrease across all specimens (Fig. 4). Total frequency losses of 25.5%, 27.7%, and 29.9% were recorded, with an unexpectedly large portion of this reduction—54.9%, 59.1%, and 50.0%, respectively—occurring within the first 10 min of excitation. This rapid initial degradation underscores a substantial stiffness loss long before macroscopic cracks became apparent, revealing the limitations of relying solely on frequency shifts as a damage proxy.Fig. 4Time history of 1st Natural frequency in Random vibration test.

Time history of 1st Natural frequency in Random vibration test.

To extend beyond frequency-based indicators, the transfer function between the proof mass and jig base was evaluated using the proposed acceleration-only circle-fitting approach. The analysis yielded a strikingly consistent outcome: despite substantial frequency degradation, the estimated damping ratio remained essentially constant at ~ 3% throughout all three experiments (Fig. 5). This stability decouples stiffness reduction from dissipative mechanisms, indicating that early-stage degradation was not governed by plastic deformation or viscoelastic effects, but by boundary condition changes and local interface phenomena. The modal circle-center trajectory in the Nyquist plane emerged as the primary damage indicator predicted by the theoretical framework (Eq. (11)). As depicted in Fig. 6, the circle center followed a reproducible evolutionary path across the three tests, comprising three distinct phases: an initial period of rapid drift during the first 10 min, a subsequent quasi-stable phase, and a final abrupt shift coinciding with the visual emergence of a crack. This trajectory provided a richer, more descriptive characterization of the evolving damage state than frequency shifts alone, establishing the circle-center metric as a more sensitive diagnostic parameter.Fig. 5Time history of Damping Ratio estimated from circle fitting in Random vibration test.Fig. 6Displacement of circle center in circle fitting.

Time history of Damping Ratio estimated from circle fitting in Random vibration test.

Displacement of circle center in circle fitting.

High-speed videography provided direct physical evidence to interpret these modal trends. Operational deflection shapes (ODS) were recorded at one-minute intervals and quantified using the Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC). As illustrated in Fig. 7, the ODS data mirrored the three-phase evolution observed in the circle-center plots: an initial period of marked change, a stable plateau, and a terminal disruption. Visual inspection of the ODS videos (Fig. 8) confirmed that during the early degradation stage, micro-slip and relative motion occurred at the bolted interface between the bracket and proof mass, characteristic of fretting wear. This fretting altered the effective boundary conditions of the assembly, driving the observed modal drift. Once the joint settled into a new locked configuration, the system entered a quasi-rigid phase, consistent with the observed plateau. The final abrupt transition corresponded to the onset of a macro-crack, which fundamentally reconfigured the bracket’s flexibility and mode shape.Fig. 7MAC of random vibration test.Fig. 8Visually inspected of ODS.

MAC of random vibration test.

Visually inspected of ODS.

Together, these results demonstrate that under broadband random excitation, the proposed method not only captures stiffness loss but also resolves subtle boundary-condition changes arising from fretting wear. By doing so, it provides a sensitive and physically interpretable precursor metric to fatigue failure in assembled structures—an insight that conventional VBDD approaches often fail to deliver.

In this experiment, the primary early-stage damage mechanism was fretting wear at the bolted joints. This fretting process alters the local stiffness and effective boundary conditions of the assembly, causing a distinct change in the mode shape even before a macroscopic crack forms. The circle-center drift directly tracks this evolution, acting as a clear precursor to more severe structural damage. This phenomenon is not unique to our bracket geometry but is common in numerous aerospace structures, such as riveted panels and component mounts, where joint integrity is critical.

Concurrently, the drift is also a direct indicator of fatigue damage initiation and propagation within the material itself. A growing fatigue crack inherently reduces the local structural stiffness, which in turn alters the overall dynamic response and mode shape of the component. This physical change also contributes to the progressive movement of the modal circle’s center. Therefore, the strength of this indicator lies in its ability to capture the cumulative effects of damage. An initial, rapid drift may signal the onset of fretting and joint degradation, while a subsequent, accelerating drift can signify the formation and growth of a fatigue crack. By monitoring this single, rich parameter, we can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the structure’s health, from initial wear to impending failure.

Damage characterization under sinusoidal sweep excitation

Three replicate tests were conducted using the sinusoidal sweep profile, with tests lasting 60, 29, and 33 sweeps, respectively, until a visible crack formed near the B-side bend. This loading regime produced a different set of damage indicators, highlighting the importance of understanding the excitation environment.

The sinusoidal sweep tests revealed a progressive deterioration of the system’s global properties, mirroring the trends observed under random excitation. The first resonance frequency decreased substantially, with total reductions of 38%, 25%, and 23% across the three runs (Fig. 9). Unlike the random vibration case, however, the damping ratio exhibited a modest but systematic increase, rising from an initial value of approximately 3% to nearly 4% as the specimens approached failure (Fig. 10). This divergence between the two test regimes highlights that the pathway to failure is excitation-dependent, with harmonic loading amplifying dissipative mechanisms that remain largely hidden under broadband excitation.Fig. 9Time history of 1st natural frequency in sinusoidal sweep test.Fig. 10Time history of damping ratio in sinusoidal sweep test.

Time history of 1st natural frequency in sinusoidal sweep test.

Time history of damping ratio in sinusoidal sweep test.

The most striking observation from the sweep tests was the evolution of the transmissibility function near the first resonance. Figure 11 illustrates a critical finding of this study: the emergence of a split-peak in the transmissibility function as a key damage indicator under sinusoidal excitation. Specifically, Fig, 11(a) shows the response at the 7th sweep cycle, which serves as a baseline for the relatively undamaged state. It exhibits a single, sharp, and symmetric resonance peak, which is characteristic of a linear system.Fig. 11Transmissibility of sine sweep test.

Transmissibility of sine sweep test.

In contrast, Fig. 11(b) shows the response after 14 sweeps. Here, the single peak has clearly bifurcated into two distinct lobes. This ‘split-peak’ pattern is a robust indicator of nonlinearity introduced into the system. This phenomenon is attributed to the ‘breathing’ behavior of a fatigue crack, which alters the system’s stiffness depending on the direction of displacement. The transition from a single peak to a split peak thus marks a fundamental shift from simple stiffness degradation to the formation of a localized, nonlinear crack.

Our approach is based on a "quasi-static" assumption, which is a standard practice in the field of vibration-based structural health monitoring. The core idea is that the timescale over which damage evolves (in our case, minutes) is significantly longer than the timescale of the system’s dynamic response (milliseconds). Therefore, for a short segment of measurement data (e.g., data from a single minute of the test), we can assume the structure’s physical properties are momentarily constant. In this brief window, the system is treated as a Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) system, and we use the circle-fitting method to extract its equivalent linear modal parameters (natural frequency, damping ratio, and mode shape indicators like the circle’s center). By repeating this process for consecutive data segments over the entire duration of the test, we create a time-history of these “linearized” parameters. This allows us to effectively track the evolution of the nonlinear damage process by observing the changes in a sequence of simplified, linear models.

This phenomenon, consistently observed across all specimens (Fig. 12), provides a clear fingerprint of localized nonlinearity. Its physical origin is attributed to the opening-and-closing, or “breathing,” behavior of a fatigue crack subjected to harmonic excitation. The emergence of this split peak therefore marks a fundamental transition in the damage state—from diffuse, global stiffness loss to the formation of a discrete, localized crack.Fig. 12Transmissibility of sine sweep test over time domain.

Transmissibility of sine sweep test over time domain.

This convergence of dynamic testing and CT evidence establishes the split-peak phenomenon as a powerful early-warning indicator of fatigue cracking. Crucially, it demonstrates sensitivity to subsurface damage that is invisible to conventional inspection methods, providing a diagnostic window well in advance of surface crack emergence. The findings elevate transmissibility peak-splitting from a qualitative curiosity to a practical and actionable metric for structural health monitoring, offering a new pathway to detect incipient cracks before they compromise structural integrity.

Discussion

The results of this study provide significant new insights into the detection of vibration-induced fatigue in complex aerospace structures. By combining a novel analytical method with a realistic experimental program, this work not only validates a new SHM technique but also elucidates the distinct damage signatures that manifest under different operational loading conditions.

A central finding of this research is that the nature of the vibrational excitation fundamentally influences the dominant damage mechanisms and, consequently, the most sensitive indicators for SHM. The two loading regimes investigated—broadband random and narrowband sinusoidal sweep—produced markedly different evolutionary patterns in the measured modal parameters. Under broadband random vibration, the primary early-stage damage mechanism was identified as fretting wear at the bolted interfaces. This was evidenced by the unique combination of a rapid decrease in natural frequency (stiffness loss) while the system’s damping remained remarkably constant. The proposed acceleration-only circle-fit method proved exceptionally sensitive to this mechanism, not through damping changes, but through the pronounced drift of the modal circle’s center, which directly tracked the evolution of the mode shape as the boundary conditions were altered by the fretting process. This suggests that for structures with complex joints subjected to operational random vibration, monitoring parameters related to mode shape or modal scaling may be far more effective than tracking frequency or damping alone.

In contrast, the sinusoidal sweep excitation, which concentrates energy at a single, high-amplitude frequency, appeared to drive the growth of a discrete, localized crack more efficiently. The dominant damage signature in this case was not a change in a single modal parameter but a qualitative change in the system’s response behavior: the emergence of a split-peak in the transmissibility function. This nonlinear phenomenon, indicative of a “breathing” crack, served as a powerful and unambiguous precursor to failure. The slight increase in damping observed near failure in these tests is also consistent with the introduction of new energy dissipation mechanisms associated with crack-face friction and localized plasticity.

These distinct signatures have profound implications for designing robust SHM systems. An effective system must be capable of recognizing multiple types of indicators, as the most sensitive feature may depend on the specific loading environment the component experiences in service.

The findings of this research have direct and significant implications for the design of next-generation, on-board SHM systems. The validation of a force-free identification method is a critical enabling step, as it aligns with the practical constraints of in-flight monitoring where only response sensors are feasible. Furthermore, the identification of two distinct, highly sensitive damage indicators offers a more sophisticated diagnostic toolkit. An SHM algorithm could be designed to monitor for both the gradual drift of the modal circle center (indicative of wear and degradation at joints) and the sudden appearance of peak-splitting (indicative of a discrete crack). The correlation of the peak-splitting signature with subsurface damage confirmed by NDI is particularly powerful; it establishes this vibrational feature as an actionable trigger for focused inspection, embodying the core principle of CBM.

While this study provides a significant step forward, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The experimental investigation was focused on a single bracket geometry and mounting configuration. The stiffness estimation for the sinusoidal sweeps relied on a SDOF assumption, which may be less accurate once significant modal interaction (i.e., peak-splitting) occurs.

In addition, the theoretical basis of the proposed method—the modal circle-fit derived from an acceleration-based transfer function—is rooted in the principles of structural dynamics and is material-agnostic. Therefore, the framework remains valid for composite structures. The key difference lies in how damage manifests and how it would be interpreted by our method. For example, delamination and widespread matrix cracking cause a reduction in local stiffness, which would lead to a decrease in natural frequency, similar to what is observed in metallic fatigue. The proposed circle-fitting method would effectively capture this shift. However, unlike the given metallic test case where damping remained stable during fretting, composite damage often involves significant energy dissipation through mechanisms like inter-laminar friction in a delaminated region. Therefore, it is expected that a more pronounced and earlier increase in the estimated damping ratio. Also, the primary challenges for composites are their inherent anisotropy and heterogeneity, which lead to more complex mode shapes, and their sensitivity to environmental factors like temperature and moisture, which could mask subtle changes from incipient damage.

In conclusion, by synergistically combining methodological innovation, experimental realism, and non-destructive validation, this work demonstrates a significant advancement in the state-of-the-art of VBDD. The identified damage signatures and the analytical tools developed to track them represent a tangible step toward the realization of practical, high-fidelity, and reliable SHM systems capable of enhancing the safety and operational efficiency of future aerospace platforms.
