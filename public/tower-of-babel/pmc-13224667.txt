Molecular subtyping of colorectal cancer based on inositol metabolism identifies PLCG2 as a key prognostic biomarker
Authors: Dandan Hong, Jialin Dai, Xinyi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInositol metabolism, a crucial regulatory mechanism in cellular signaling, plays a pivotal role in colorectal cancer (CRC) tumorigenesis and progression. While multiple inositol-metabolizing enzymes have been implicated in CRC development, a systematic characterization of inositol metabolism-associated gene signatures and molecular subtyping based on these regulators remains unexplored.MethodsTranscriptomic profiles and clinical data of CRC patients were obtained from TCGA and GEO databases. Integrated transcriptomic analysis enabled the stratification of CRC into distinct inositol metabolism-associated molecular subtypes via unsupervised consensus clustering. An inositol metabolism-related prognostic signature was constructed using LASSO-Cox regression. The functional role of the key identified gene was validated through in vitro experiments.ResultsCRC was stratified into two distinct subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) exhibiting significant heterogeneity in clinical outcomes, tumor microenvironment features, and therapeutic vulnerabilities. The Cluster 1 subtype demonstrated strong associations with microsatellite stability (MSS), cell cycle processes, MYC signaling, and better patient prognosis. In contrast, the Cluster 2 subtype was closely linked to microsatellite instability (MSI), epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and TGF-β signaling. Additionally, a low Inositol Score was associated with increased sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic and targeted agents. Notably, phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2) was identified as a key discriminator between subtypes and a top prognostic biomarker. PLCG2 was upregulated in CRC tissues, and its knockdown significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation and migration in vitro.ConclusionsThis study delineates novel inositol metabolism-based molecular subtypes in CRC, proposes PLCG2 as a promising prognostic biomarker and functional oncogene, and provides a conceptual framework for targeting inositol metabolism in clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04789-3.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a formidable global health challenge, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. According to the latest GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, approximately 1.9 million new CRC cases and 900,000 deaths occurred globally, underscoring its significant epidemiological burden [1]. Furthermore, recent comprehensive analyses have emphasized the potential for reducing global CRC incidence through targeted interventions [2]. Notably, the incidence of early-onset CRC (diagnosed in individuals under 50 years) has been steadily rising in many regions, adding complexity to screening paradigms and raising concerns about evolving etiological factors. Currently, a widespread lack of screening awareness among younger populations often leads to delayed diagnosis, which significantly complicates clinical management [3]. Despite advances in screening, surgical techniques, and systemic therapies, a substantial proportion of patients with advanced or metastatic disease experience treatment failure, highlighting the persistent and critical need for a deeper molecular understanding of CRC heterogeneity and novel therapeutic vulnerabilities [4, 5].

The clinical management of CRC, particularly metastatic CRC (mCRC), is increasingly guided by molecular profiling, with established biomarkers such as microsatellite instability (MSI) status, RAS, and BRAF mutations informing therapeutic decisions. However, significant clinical dilemmas persist [6]. First, intrinsic and acquired resistance to conventional chemotherapies and targeted agents remains a major obstacle. Second, CRC exhibits profound intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity, which is not fully captured by current classification systems like the Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS) [7, 8]. This heterogeneity translates into variable clinical outcomes and differential treatment responses among patients with seemingly similar disease stages. Therefore, deciphering the nuanced molecular layers that drive this heterogeneity is imperative for developing more precise prognostic tools and identifying new actionable targets [9].

Inositol metabolism, centered on the versatile signaling molecule inositol and its phosphorylated derivatives, constitutes a fundamental regulatory network in cellular physiology. It governs a wide array of critical processes, including growth factor signaling, cell cycle progression, cytoskeletal organization, and lipid biosynthesis [10, 11]. The phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt and phospholipase C (PLC) pathways, both central to inositol phosphate signaling, are frequently dysregulated in numerous malignancies, contributing to oncogenic transformation, proliferation, survival, and metastasis [12]. For instance, PLCγ1 has been implicated in driving tumor progression in various cancers [13]. In the context of CRC, several inositol-metabolizing enzymes have been sporadically reported to be altered. However, the existing research landscape is largely fragmented, focusing on individual pathway components [14, 15]. A systematic, integrated evaluation of the complete inositol metabolism network—and its potential to define coherent molecular subtypes with distinct biological and clinical features—remains conspicuously unexplored. This gap in knowledge represents a significant opportunity to uncover new layers of CRC biology [16].

Building upon this rationale, the present study was designed to comprehensively characterize the transcriptional landscape of inositol metabolism-associated genes in CRC. We hypothesized that this pathway activity could stratify CRC into biologically and clinically relevant subgroups. Through this work, we pinpoint phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2) as a critical molecular discriminator and functional oncoprotein. This study moves beyond a view to propose a novel, metabolism-aware molecular taxonomy for CRC. The findings not only offer fresh insights into the mechanistic role of inositol signaling in CRC progression but also provide a translatable framework for prognostic prediction and the development of subtype-specific therapeutic strategies, ultimately aiming to bridge the gap between molecular discovery and clinical application.

Methods

Datasets

Transcriptomic profiles with matched clinical annotations for colorectal cancer (CRC) were obtained from public genomic archives: The Cancer Genome Atlas Colon Adenocarcinoma and Rectum Adenocarcinoma cohorts (TCGA-COAD, TCGA-READ) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession numbers GSE39582, GSE17538, GSE14333, GSE38832). The TCGA cohort served as the discovery set for molecular classification and model development, while the integrated GEO datasets constituted the external validation set. Genes involved in inositol metabolism were curated from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), specifically from the gene ontology term “GOBP_INOSITOL_PHOSPHATE_METABOLIC_PROCESS”, the KEGG pathway “KEGG_INOSITOL_PHOSPHATE_METABOLISM”, and the Reactome pathway “REACTOME_INOSITOL_PHOSPHATE_METABOLISM”. Samples lacking essential survival information (overall survival time and status) were excluded. Clinical covariates, including demographic data, tumor stage, and survival endpoints, were collected for integrated analysis. To mitigate non-biological technical variation across different datasets, the “ComBat” algorithm from the ‘sva’ R package was applied for batch effect adjustment. Somatic mutation data for CRC samples were downloaded from the TCGA portal for subsequent genomic alteration analysis.

Consensus clustering analysis

To establish molecular subtypes based on inositol metabolism gene expression, consensus clustering was performed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the consensus matrix heatmap, cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, and the relative change in the area under the CDF curve. The resulting clusters were designated as Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Inositol Metabolism subtypes 1 and 2). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, executed with the “survival” and “survminer” packages, was used to compare prognostic differences between the subtypes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 were identified using the “limma” package.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

Biological pathway activities across the defined subtypes were interrogated using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). Gene sets from the Hallmark and KEGG collections in MSigDB were employed. Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was subsequently used to quantify the enrichment scores of specific metabolic and signaling pathways, as well as to estimate the relative abundance of tumor microenvironment cell types using established gene signatures.

Characterization of the Tumor Microenvironment (TME)

The TME landscape of the IM subtypes was profiled using a multi-algorithm approach. The ESTIMATE algorithm was used to compute stromal, immune, and estimate scores, reflecting the non-tumor cellular components. Immune cell infiltration patterns were deconvoluted using the CIBERSORT tool. These results were complemented by the immune cell scores derived from ssGSEA to provide a comprehensive view of the TME heterogeneity.

Construction and assessment of a prognostic signature

A prognostic model was developed from inositol metabolism-related DEGs. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, implemented via the “glmnet” package, was applied to prevent overfitting and select the most predictive genes. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was then built using these genes. An Inositol Score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: Inositol Score = Σ (Coefficient_i * Expression_i). Patients were stratified into high-score and low-score groups based on the median cutoff. The model’s prognostic performance was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier survival curves and time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis using the “timeROC” package.

Analysis of genomic alterations, drug sensitivity, and clinical correlation

Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated for each sample based on the somatic mutation data. Differences in TMB and the landscape of frequently mutated genes (e.g., APC, TP53, KRAS) between the IM subtypes and Inositol Score groups were analyzed. The predicted sensitivity to common chemotherapeutic and targeted agents was inferred by calculating the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) using the “pRRophetic” R package based on publicly available pharmacogenomic databases. Associations between the molecular classifications and clinicopathological variables were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Participants

This study enrolled a total of 70 patients with colorectal cancer (CRC), including 42 patients with colon cancer and 28 patients with rectal cancer. All patients underwent radical surgical resection at the Affiliated Hospital with Jiangnan University between January 2024 and December 2024. The patients’ ages ranged from 18 to 80 years. All included participants were confirmed by pathological diagnosis and had no history of other malignant tumors. Clinical pathological data and/or surgical tissue samples from the patients were utilized in this study. All participants were informed of the study details and provided written informed consent.

Cell culture and transfection

Human CRC cell lines (HCT116, SW480, LoVo, RKO, DLD-1) and a normal colonic epithelial cell line (NCM460) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. All cell lines mentioned were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). For gene knockdown, cells were transfected with a specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting PLCG2 or a non-targeting control siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. Transfection efficiency was validated 48 h post-transfection by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The used cell lines were authenticated before being used for experimentation and free from mycoplasma contamination.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent. Complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7500 system with SYBR Green master mix. The relative expression of PLCG2 was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences were as follows: PLCG2 forward, 5’- GCAGTGGCAAGGTTGAAGTT-3’, and reverse, 5’-AACACAGTCATCACCGTCCC-3’; GAPDH forward, 5’-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3’, and reverse, 5’-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3’.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple groups). Non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) were applied where appropriate. Survival analyses utilized Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s or Spearman’s methods. All in vitro experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.1) or GraphPad Prism, with significance set at *p* < 0.05, **p* < 0.01, ***p* < 0.001.

Results

Molecular stratification based on inositol metabolism phenotypes

The overall design of this study is summarized in Fig. 1A. Inositol metabolism-related genes effectively distinguished between normal individuals and cancer patients within the TCGA COAD and READ databases (Fig. 1B). Unsupervised consensus clustering of colorectal cancer (CRC) samples from the TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ cohorts, based on the expression profiles of inositol metabolism-associated genes, robustly identified two distinct molecular subgroups, designated as Inositol Metabolism subtype 1 (Cluster 1) and Inositol Metabolism subtype 2 (Cluster 2) (k=2; Fig. 1C, Figure S1). The clinical characteristics of patients in each cluster are detailed in Table S1 (All CRC patients' clinical characteristics). Patients for each cluster are summarized in Table S2 (All CRC patients' inositol cluster distribution). The stability of this classification was confirmed by consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area analyses (Figure S1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) both demonstrated clear spatial separation between the Cluster 1 and Cluster 1 subgroups, validating the transcriptional distinctness of the identified clusters (Fig. 1D-E, Figure S1C-D). Survival analysis revealed a significant prognostic divergence between the subtypes. Patients classified as Cluster 1 exhibited markedly more favorable overall survival outcomes compared to those in the Cluster 2 subgroup (p = 0.021) (Fig. 1F).

Molecular and clinicopathological characteristics of inositol metabolism subtypes

Differential expression analysis identified a core set of inositol metabolism-related genes that were differentially expressed between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 subtypes (Fig. 2A). The differentially expressed genes identified between the two PAL subtypes are presented in Table S3 (DEGs between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2). Phenotypically, Cluster 1 were significantly enriched for HNF4A, and stemness-related genes like LGR5. In contrast, Cluster 2 displayed strong associations with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signatures, CDX2 expression, and microsatellite instability (MSI) status (Fig. 2B-C, Figure S2A-B). In the TCGA dataset, the ssGSEA score of Cluster 2 was slightly higher than that of Cluster 1, while the scores of the two clusters were relatively similar in the GEO dataset (Fig. 2D, Figure S2C). At the pathway level, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) revealed that Cluster 1 was linked to processes including SUMOylation, mRNA methylation, and basal transcription factor activity. Conversely, Cluster 2 was predominantly associated with pathways involving neutrophil activation, PD-1 signaling, and cell adhesion molecules (Fig.2E).

The tumor immune microenvironment of inositol metabolism subtypes

A comprehensive profiling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) was performed using integrated computational methods. Analysis of immune cell infiltration via the CIBERSORT algorithm revealed that Cluster 1 harbored significantly higher proportions of plasma cells and resting CD4⁺ memory T cells. In comparison, Cluster 2 tumors were characterized by elevated infiltrates of CD8⁺ T cells, M1 macrophages, and neutrophils (Fig. 3A, Figure S3A). This finding was corroborated by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), which showed significantly higher enrichment scores for the majority of immune cell types in the Cluster 2 subtype (Fig. 3B, Figure S3B). Consistent with this immunologically active state, the Cluster 2 subgroup demonstrated significantly higher Stromal, Immune, and ESTIMATE scores compared to Cluster 1 (Fig. 3C, Figure S3C). Correlation network analysis further linked inositol metabolism to key immune regulators, such as effector memory CD4⁺ T cells, central memory CD4⁺ T cells, and monocytes; these associations suggest a complex relationship between Inositol Metabolism and specific TME niches (Fig. 3D, Figure S3D). Furthermore, the expression of key immune checkpoint molecules, including LAG3, PD-L1, and co-stimulatory/inhibitory markers such as CD14, CD8A, PTPRC, and LILRB2, was significantly elevated in IM2 tumors (Fig. 3E, Figure S3E).

Development and validation of an inositol metabolism prognostic signature

A prognostic signature termed the Inositol Score was constructed using LASSO-Cox regression in the TCGA training cohort (Fig. 4A-B). Table S4 (The gene model constituting inositol score) presents the gene model used to calculate the inositol score. Patients were stratified into high- and low-score groups based on the median cutoff. Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that a high Inositol Score was a powerful predictor of poor overall survival in both the training (TCGA) and validation (combined GEO) cohorts (Fig. 4C-E, Figure S4A-C). We next compared the Inositol scores between surviving and deceased patients using the Wilcoxon test. The results revealed that deceased patients exhibited significantly higher Inositol scores than surviving patients in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 4D, Figure S4E). Notably, the Inositol Score was significantly higher in patients who died from CRC compared to survivors. An alluvial diagram illustrated the strong interrelationship between the Inositol Metabolism subtypes, Inositol Score groups, and patient survival outcomes, showing a predominant flow from the Cluster 1 subtype to a low Inositol Score and favorable prognosis, and from Cluster 2 to high Inositol Score and poor prognosis (Fig. 4G, Figure S4D). Survival analysis and corresponding ROC curves demonstrated that patients with a high inositol score had significantly worse outcomes; this finding was consistent across both independent cohorts (Fig. 4H-I, Figure S4F).

Genomic alterations and therapeutic implications of the prognostic signature

Cellular stemness potential, as measured by the CSC index, was significantly and inversely correlated with prognostic signature values (Fig. 6A). Analysis of tumor mutation burden (TMB) revealed a positive correlation with the Inositol Score. The mutational landscape differed between high- and low-score groups. In the colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) cohort, genes like MUC16 and SYNE1 were more frequently mutated in the high-score group (Fig. 6B-C). In the rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) cohort, the high-score group had a lower mutation frequency of canonical drivers like APC and TP53, but a higher frequency of KRAS and TTN mutations (Fig. 6D-E). Critically, drug sensitivity analysis predicted that patients in the low Inositol Score group would exhibit significantly increased susceptibility to a wide array of common chemotherapeutic agents and targeted therapies (Fig. 6F), suggesting the score’s potential utility in guiding treatment selection.

PLCG2 as a key functional oncogene in CRC

PLCG2 was identified as a top-ranking gene within the prognostic signature. Its expression was significantly elevated in human CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal mucosa (Fig. 7A). This overexpression was confirmed in vitro across a panel of CRC cell lines versus a normal colonic epithelial cell line (Fig. 7B). For subsequent functional experiments, we selected the two cell lines with the highest relative expression levels. In these models, we modulated PLCG2 expression using shRNA knockdown and overexpression plasmids, respectively, with validation by RT-qPCR (Fig. 7C). Functional validation using shRNA-mediated knockdown of PLCG2 in RKO and DLD-1 cell lines demonstrated that knocking-down PLCG2 led to a significant suppression of both cellular proliferation and migratory capacity (Fig. 7D-F). These results establish PLCG2 as a functional driver of CRC progression.

Discussion

Our study provides a systematic, transcriptome-based characterization of the inositol metabolism network in CRC, revealing its profound impact on tumor stratification and prognosis. We demonstrate that the transcriptional activity of this pathway delineates two molecular subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) with distinct clinicopathological features, immune microenvironment compositions, and patient outcomes. Furthermore, we developed and validated a novel prognostic signature—the Inositol Score—derived from this network, which offers superior predictive power over traditional staging and harbors potential for therapeutic guidance. A central finding emerging from this analytical framework is the identification and functional validation of PLCG2 as a key oncogenic driver in CRC.

The current molecular classification of CRC integrates multiple biological dimensions but does not explicitly capture metabolic reprogramming as a primary stratifier. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that the transcriptional program of inositol metabolism is sufficient to define biologically meaningful subgroups. The Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 subtypes recapitulate and extend known CRC biology: Cluster 1 tumors, associated with better prognosis, exhibit features of differentiated epithelium and stem-like signatures. In contrast, Cluster 2 tumors are aggressive, enriched with EMT, MSI, and a profoundly immunogenic microenvironment characterized by CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and high immune checkpoint expression. This aligns with the understanding that specific metabolic states can sculpt the TME. The strong link between the Cluster 2 phenotype, immune activation, and poorer survival suggests that the associated immune response may be dysfunctional, a hypothesis supported by the concurrent enrichment of neutrophil and PD-1 signaling pathways. Therefore, our inositol metabolism classification adds a crucial metabolic layer to existing frameworks, proposing that inositol metabolism is a key regulator bridging intracellular signaling with the immune landscape. Based on the central role of inositol metabolism in phospholipid signaling, we hypothesize that key metabolic genes (such as PLCG2) mediate these effects by hydrolyzing phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate the second messengers IP3 and DAG. This leads to intracellular calcium release and protein kinase C (PKC) activation. These signaling cascades not only directly regulate tumor cell proliferation and migration but also actively reshape the TME by modulating cytokine secretion and immune cell recruitment [17]. Notably, the dual regulatory role of such key molecules in driving intrinsic tumor progression while concurrently modulating the immune microenvironment has been increasingly recognized as a critical mechanism in cancer [18].

Within the inositol metabolism network, PLCG2 emerged as a critical node. While the role of PLCG1 in cancer is more extensively documented, the function of PLCG2, particularly in solid tumors like CRC, remains less clear [19–22]. Our findings position PLCG2 as a significant contributor to CRC progression [23]. Its consistent overexpression in CRC tissues and cell lines, correlation with poor prognosis, and functional validation through knockdown experiments collectively establish its oncogenic role. The suppression of proliferation and migration upon PLCG2 silencing in RKO and DLD-1 cells provides direct mechanistic evidence that PLCG2 drives malignant phenotypes. PLCG2 hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), key second messengers that modulate calcium signaling and protein kinase C (PKC) activation, respectively. This places PLCG2 upstream of multiple cascades influencing cell growth, motility, and survival. Its identification here as a top prognostic gene and functional driver highlights it as a promising therapeutic target worthy of further investigation, potentially through the development of isoform-specific PLC inhibitors. Although clinical drugs directly targeting PLCG2 are currently lacking, existing PLC inhibitors like U73122 have shown efficacy in suppressing PLCG2 phosphorylation and downstream inflammatory responses in specific models [24]. Furthermore, in hematological malignancies, activating mutations in PLCG2 are well-documented drivers of resistance to BTK inhibitors, suggesting that targeting the BTK-PLCG2 axis could overcome targeted therapy resistance [25]. In the context of CRC, recent evidence suggests that downregulating PLCG2 can enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by promoting CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [23]. Therefore, combinatorial strategies pairing PLCG2 intervention with established immunotherapies or targeted agents hold significant promise for clinical translation.

The translation of molecular insights into clinical tools is paramount. The Inositol Score, derived from a minimal gene set, demonstrated robust and independent prognostic value across multiple validation cohorts. Its integration into a nomogram with TNM staging enhanced predictive accuracy for long-term survival, offering a potentially simple yet powerful tool for risk stratification in the clinic. More importantly, the score harbors therapeutic implications. The predicted differential drug sensitivity between high- and low-score groups is highly suggestive. The increased susceptibility of low-score tumors to various chemotherapies and targeted agents implies that the Inositol Score could serve as a biomarker for de novo treatment resistance, guiding more personalized initial therapy. Conversely, the high-score/IM2 group, with its immune-rich yet likely dysfunctional TME and distinct mutational profile (e.g., different frequencies of APC, TP53, KRAS mutations), may benefit from alternative strategies, such as immunotherapies tailored to overcome specific immunosuppressive mechanisms or agents targeting EMT and metabolic pathways. Beyond guiding medical interventions, our findings hold notable translational implications for clinical nursing practice, particularly within the evolving paradigm of precision nursing. The proposed Inositol Score provides a quantifiable metric that can assist oncology nurses in early patient risk stratification. Patients classified into the high-risk subgroup (Cluster 2 or high Inositol Score) typically face poorer prognoses and may benefit from intensified clinical monitoring, early psychological interventions, and customized supportive care. Furthermore, because the prognostic signature predicts differential sensitivities to various chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, it empowers nursing staff to anticipate specific treatment-related adverse events. By integrating such molecular prognostic tools into routine nursing assessments, caregivers can deliver proactive symptom management and highly targeted patient education, thereby improving treatment adherence and the overall quality of life for CRC patients.

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations that chart a course for future research. First, the clinical samples used for validation in this study were collected from a single center (Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University) over a short time span in 2024. This may introduce selection bias; thus, large-scale, multi-center prospective cohorts are required to further validate our findings. Second, the drug sensitivity analysis was based solely on computational predictions. In future studies, we plan to utilize candidate drugs such as Pazopanib and Gefitinib to conduct empirical drug treatment experiments in CRC cell models with varying Inositol Scores. Third, regarding the functional validation of PLCG2, our mechanistic exploration remains phenotypic. We aim to conduct Co-Immunoprecipitation coupled with Mass Spectrometry (Co-IP-MS) to identify downstream interacting proteins of PLCG2 to further elucidate its molecular network. Additionally, due to resource constraints, we were unable to perform phenotypic rescue experiments following PLCG2 knockdown, which are necessary in future rigorous mechanistic designs to completely rule out off-target effects. The classification and signature are based solely on transcriptional data. Integrating multi-omics layers—such as metabolomics to directly measure inositol phosphate species, proteomics to assess protein activity, and phosphoproteomics to map signaling flux—would provide a more dynamic and causal understanding of the network’s operation. While we established a causal role for PLCG2 in vitro, the in vivo contribution of PLCG2 and other key genes in the signature to tumor growth, metastasis, and TME remodeling requires validation using genetically engineered mouse models and patient-derived xenografts. Moreover, the drug sensitivity predictions are computational and must be empirically validated in preclinical models and ultimately in clinical trials. Prospective cohort studies are needed to definitively establish the utility of the Inositol Score in predicting response to standard-of-care and experimental therapies. Finally, the mechanistic link between specific inositol metabolism gene expression patterns and the establishment of an immune-hot but pro-tumorigenic TME remain to be fully dissected.

Conclusion

In summary, this work systematically defines the landscape of inositol metabolism in CRC, establishing it as a key determinant of tumor biology and patient outcome. We propose a novel IM-based classification and a clinically applicable prognostic signature, both of which illuminate the interplay between metabolic signaling and the tumor immune microenvironment. The functional oncogene PLCG2 is highlighted as a central player and potential therapeutic target. By bridging a specific metabolic pathway with broader tumor phenotypes, this study provides a framework for developing more precise prognostic tools and metabolism-aware therapeutic strategies for colorectal cancer patients.
