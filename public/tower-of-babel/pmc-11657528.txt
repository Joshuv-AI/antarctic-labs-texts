A Method for the Determination of Rate Coefficients for the Formation of Protonated Monomers and Proton Bound Dimers of Volatile Organic Compounds in Air at Ambient Pressure by Ion Mobility Spectrometry
Authors: Oliver Hecht, Bert Ungethüm, Andreas Walte, Elie Lattouf, Osmo Anttalainen, Tapio Kotiaho, Hanna Hakulinen, Gary Eiceman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRate coefficients (k) for the reactions of hydrated protons and a sample vapor (M) to form protonated monomers (MH+(H2O)n) and proton bound dimers (M2H+) were determined simultaneously by using ion mobility spectrometry with a tandem drift tube at ambient pressure. In this method, sample vapors were introduced to a first drift region with stepwise reactions: M + H+(H2O)nMH+ (H2O)y + (n – y) H2O and M + MH+ (H2O)y M2H+ + yH2O. Ions with a drift time between 9 and 18 ms in the first drift region were subsequently mobility analyzed in purified air with the second drift region using a synchronized second ion gate with boxcar averaging. Slope for plots of ion abundance against drift time was fitted by successive approximation between peaks of H+(H2O)n and MH+(H2O)y to obtain k2 until computed and experimental slopes matched within a maximal deviation of 0.01. Values for k1 were obtained from the experimental baseline slope when adjusted for k2. Rate coefficients for triethyl phosphate and phenylacetate were determined using this method with k1 values of 1.14 × 10–9 ± 1.87 × 10–10 cm3/s and 0.81 × 10–9 ± 9.26 × 10–11 cm3/s and k2 values of 1.05 × 10–9 ± 4.13 × 10–10 cm3/s and 1.07 × 10–9 ± 1.86 × 10–10 cm3/s, respectively. Relative error for this method was determined using mobility spectra generated with a COMSOL model and artificial rate coefficients. Rate coefficients were then extracted from the modeled mobility spectra and compared to the original artificial values. Relative error of this method was 10% and should be generally applicable for individual substances with reactions forming protonated monomers and proton bound dimers.

Introduction

Gas phase reactions between hydrated protons (H+(H2O)n) and volatile organic molecules (M) at ambient pressure are the initial steps for chemical measurements in methods based on atmospheric pressure ionization (API) mass spectrometry (MS)1,2 and ion mobility spectrometry (IMS).3 Such reactions arise in the ion source or reaction region of API MS and IMS instruments through displacement of waters of hydration by M yielding a protonated monomer (MH+(H2O)y) as shown in eq 1:

The characteristics of these reactions define the metrics of measurements by API methods, including slopes of response curves, detection limits, and linear ranges, and have been explored over decades. Examples include the exploration of the influence of ion source temperature on response with API-MS4 and the influence of the hydration level (n in eq 1) on rate coefficients with selected ion flow tube MS.5 Understandings for ion molecule reactions given in eq 1 can be significant in several aspects of ionization chemistry and chemical instrumentation including: 1) interpretation of reaction speeds and mechanisms,6,7 2) prediction of response in API-based devices,4,7,8 and 3) understanding of chemical structure and reactivity.4,8,10

Such reactions have shared foundations with gas phase reactions occurring in reduced pressures4,8 with several key distinctives. These include the energies of ions, which are near thermal energies at ambient pressure (∼1030 mbar), and the level of hydration in H+(H2O)n where n can range from 2 to 4 or more.9 In contrast, hydrated protons in methods such as proton transfer reaction (PTR) MS are based on H3O+, and ion energies can be significantly above thermal energies11,12 and are usually performed at reduced pressure (<1 mbar). Impacts of these differences can be significant for example with response factors in IMS where reactivities of H+(H2O)n decrease as levels of hydration increase.13 Although API MS and IMS methods are more than 50 years old, quantitative treatment of such reactions is limited to a few investigations, although a general quantitative model to predict and evaluate such reactions has described recently for single substances6 and for binary mixtures.7 Such rate coefficients are also important in the emerging concept of gas ion distillation which involves separation of volatile substances in mixtures based on differences in ionization kinetics.14

Another distinctive for reactions shown in eq 1 is that the reactant ion, H+(H2O)n, is a dynamic distribution of species where n is established by moisture and temperature inside the ion source or reaction region.15 In practice, all hydrate clusters are used in the ionization step for a measurement by API MS and IMS since the isolation of single hydrated species is technically impractical, considering the simplicity of technology of such ion sources. As a consequence, absolute rate coefficients for reactions in eq 1 are unrealistic to obtain for API MS and IMS ambient pressure and the use of conditional rate coefficients, i.e., accurate only at a particular moisture and temperature of the supporting gas atmosphere, has been suggested.7

A remarkable situation today is that rate coefficients for the formation of protonated monomers or proton bound dimers (eq 2) are largely unavailable apart from a few exceptions in journals or reference literature.10,16−18

This may arise from the technical difficulties of obtaining rate coefficients at ambient pressure or from difficulties with multiple reactant ions. In one description of measuring rate coefficients at ambient pressure with ions near thermal energies,16 the formation of proton bound dimers as shown in eq 2 was evaluated using a single stage IMS drift tube. In this method, vapors of M were introduced throughout the drift region, and rate coefficients were extracted using slopes of baselines in mobility spectra between peaks for MH+(H2O)n and M2H+. Rate coefficients (cm3/s) were reported as 0.25 × 10–9 for methylisobutylketone, 0.86 × 10–10 for dimethyl phosphate, and 0.47 × 10–10 for dimethyl methyl phosphonate (DMMP). In yet another study,10 values of k for the formation of proton bound dimers for cyclic ketones (eq 2) ranged from 0.5 to 4.5 × 10–10 cm3/s, and increases in moisture were seen to cause significant losses in reactivity and decreased values for k. Other studies based on IMS drift tubes include17 exchange or cross reactions (eq 3) between dimethylpyridine and several reagent ions (RH+):

where rate coefficients (cm3/s) were 1.17 × 10–9 for H3O+, 0.90 × 10–9 for acetone·H+, and 0.68 × 10–9 for trimethyl amine·H+.

Only a few reports exist for rate coefficients for the formation of protonated monomer, in the rare instances where proton bound dimers do not form, and these include 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one (sulcatone) and 2,6-ditert-butylpyridine (2,6-DTBP).7 An alternative to reactions in the drift region involved reactions in a field free region with field switching ion gates and a pulsed nonradioactive ion source18 to determine k values for protonated monomer of DMMP as 1.35 × 10–9 cm3/s and formation of proton bound dimer as 1.09 × 10–9 cm3/s. Apart from this last reference, all studies cited above were based on IMS drift tubes with a single mobility stage in which rate coefficients for concurrent reactions (eqs 1 and 2) cannot be obtained simultaneously.

The difficulty in obtaining rate coefficients simultaneously with concurrent reactions in a single stage drift tube with IMS and equipped with a Faraday plate detector arises, because MH+(H2O)y is produced and consumed at the same time as shown in eq 4:

As a consequence, M2H+ can be formed not only from MH+(H2O)y generated in the reaction region but also from MH+(H2O)y generated in the drift region (Figure 1). Such reactions alter the baseline slopes between H+(H2O)n and MH+(H2O)y with further reactions of MH+(H2O)y to the proton bound dimer and the overlapping of signals at the faraday plate produced from MH+(H2O)y and M2H+. The baseline slope between protonated monomer and proton bound dimer (Figure 1) is also affected by simultaneous reactions where MH+(H2O)n is produced throughout the drift region making the slope unreliable to obtain k2 (i.e., eq 2). As a consequence, where all ions are detected by a faraday plate, there is no capability to extract the abundances of ions associated with these reactions and provide corrections to the slopes from the combination of reactions.

Tandem ion mobility spectrometers with unidirectional flow can provide analysis of ion abundances throughout the drift time of a mobility spectrum, such as shown in Figure 1 by sampling ion population for analysis in a second stage drift tube using boxcar averaging. In this work, we introduce and demonstrate the concept and the methods to simultaneously determine rate coefficients k1 and k2 with an ion mobility spectrometer using a tandem drift tube embodiment with a Faraday detector at the end of each drift region. Plots of ion abundances for mobility resolved protonated monomer and proton bound dimer obtained throughout the first stage of a drift tube include baselines between each ion pair, and rate coefficients can be extracted simultaneously with linked values for [M] and [MH+(H2O)y]. The objectives of this work were to develop and demonstrate this concept and method using triethyl phosphate (TEP) and pentyl acetate as example substances.

Experimental Section

Models and Mathematics

To determine the rate coefficients k1 and k2 from mobility spectra using IMS measurements, protonated monomer and proton bound dimers, formed in the drift region, arriving at the detector between the reactant ion peak and the monomer ion peak, must be isolated. The separation of MH+(H2O)y and M2H+ arriving at the same time at the detector can be arranged using a second mobility separation stage for the further mobility separation of ions (Figure 2). As a result, the slope between the reactant ion peak and the monomer ion peak can be separated into two slopes, one produced by the formation of M2H+ arriving at the detector (called [M2H+]obs in the following) and one produced by the formation of MH+(H2O)y, arriving in the same time (called [MH+(H2O)y]obs in the following).These slopes between the reactant ion peak (tRIP) and the monomer ion peak (tMono) were used to determine k2. In the following calculations, back reactions for eqs 1 and 2 are not included as a simple approximation, owing to anticipated small coefficients from such reactions. The amount of neutrals [M] in the drift region is much higher than the amount of ions traveling through the drift region. Therefore, [M] can be seen as constant. So, the ionization reactions can be seen as pseudo first order reactions.

Determination of k2

Successive approximation was used to determine k2. The amount of formed M2H+ was calculated out of the measured [MH+(H2O)y]obs for each time step. The resulting curve is compared to the [M2H+]obs curve until a fit was reached. The rate coefficient k2 is initially set to 1 × 10–11 cm3/s and modified during successive approximation. An algorithm implemented in Octave is going stepwise (timesteps of the data points) through the slopes of [MH+(H2O)y]obs and [M2H+]obs for drift times from td = tRIP to td = tMono. At td = tRIP, observed values for protonated monomer [MH+(H2O)y]obs and proton bound dimer [M2H+]obs are zero. Computational Modeling

With the shown algorithm, it should be possible to extract rate coefficients for monomer and dimer formation out of a kinetic IMS experiment. To test this hypothesis, COMSOL Multiphysics was used to simulate a kinetic experiment. Subsequently, the spectra generated from the simulation were used to recalculate the rate coefficients. A single drift tube geometry was simulated including electrostatic, flow, chemical reactions, and transport mechanisms of ions (see details in Supporting Information, Details of the COMSOL model). An ion package including reactant, monomer, and dimer ions is traveling through this drift tube. Neutrals M is present all through the drift tube. Ion molecule reactions for protonated monomer (eq 1) and proton bound dimer (eq 2) formation occur all the way. Different rate coefficients for monomer k1 and dimer formation k2 were set (Table S1). The previous algorithm shown was used to calculate the rate coefficient out of the mobility spectra resulting from those simulations to demonstrate the reliability of the shown method.

Instrumentation

Tandem Ion Mobility Spectrometer

A tandem IMS drift tube was built to measure simultaneously the rate coefficients for the formation of the protonated monomer and proton bound dimer (Figure 2). The dual stage ion mobility separation allows to cut a section or increment of a first stage mobility spectrum and perform a second mobility separation. Cutting a section out of the baseline slope between the H+(H2O)n peak and the MH+(H2O)y peak of a kinetic experiment, the mobility separation of the second mobility stage will give the ion composition of the section. The used tandem drift tube in total has a length of 130.14 mm where the first drift region (B) has a length of 60.6 mm and the second (C) 60.24 mm. The field inside the drift tube is driven by a voltage of 4500 V. The first part of the drift tube is the reaction region (A) with an ion source (95 MBq of 63Ni). Bradbury Nielson ion gate is placed between the reaction region and first drift region and between the first and second drift regions. This second ion gate was attached to a transimpedance amplifier with a gain of 1.1 × 109 V/A. When the gate blocks the transmission of ions, one-half of the grid wires have an elevated potential, and one has a lower potential. The positively charged ions will hit the grid wires with lower potential. The amplifier for measuring the ion current is connected to those wires and operates at elevated potential. The amplified ion current is then converted to a digital signal which is transmitted via an optocoupler to the microcontroller at ground potential. This allows it to be used as the second gate and as the first detector. The second ion gate, when synchronized to the first ion gate was used to mobility isolate, and analyze, ions from the first drift region. A Faraday Plate at the end of the second drift region was attached to a transimpedance amplifier with a gain of 1.1 × 109 V/A. Drift gas was ambient air purified in a molecular sieve filter and controlled with a model 1014VD membrane pump (Thomas, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany) and introduced at two locations, the end of the second drift region (C) and at the end of the first drift region immediately before the second ion gate (B). All gas flow was withdrawn at the beginning of the reaction region, creating a unidirectional flow in the IMS driven in a closed loop. The drift tube and the drift gas were heated at 60 °C. Spectra were acquired by using Winmuster (Airsense Analytics). The moisture amount in the drift tube is ∼5 ppmv while injecting it goes up to ∼60 ppmv (measured with A2-TX-100 moisture sensor from Michell Instruments)

An additional single drift tube IMS was used to confirm the response with the rate coefficients determined using the tandem drift tube. This drift tube was a commercial IMS tube (model GDA-P, Airsense Analytics, Schwerin, Germany) which was modified for confirmation measurements. It was modified in a way that the sample could be introduced via a syringe pump into the reaction region (Figure S6). The ion source was 95 MBq of 63Ni, and the ion gate was a Bradbury Nielson design. Drift gas was ambient air purified in a molecular sieve filter and controlled with a model 1014VD membrane pump (Thomas, Fürstenfeldbruck, Germany) circulating in a closed loop. The ion gate opening time was 245 μs. The IMS was heated to 60 °C. Spectra were acquired by using Winmuster. The moisture amount in this experiment is similar to that of the tandem IMS approach. Because of the high moisture content in the sample, the moisture content in the drift tube goes up to ∼60 ppmv during the injection.

Chemicals and Reagents

Chemicals were chosen based on the knowledge that a good baseline separation between their monomer and reactant ion peak is possible. This allows one to transfer multiple sections from the first to second stage of the tandem IMS between the reactant ion peak and the monomer ion peak. Triethyl phosphate from Sigma-Aldrich with a purity of 99.8% was used (protonated monomer K0 = 1.57 cm2/(V s); proton bound dimer K0 = 1.1 cm2/(V s) measured with GDA-FR Airsense Analytics), and pentyl acetate from Alfa Aesar with a purity of 99% was used (protonated monomer K0= 1.55 cm2/(V s); proton bound dimer K0 = 1.34 cm2/(V s) measured with GDA-FR Airsense Analytics).

Procedures

Measurements of Rate Coefficient

Five microliters of triethyl phosphate were vaporized into gastight sealed bags (StudioCook roasting bags, Bilthoven, Netherlands) filled with purified air. Headspace volumes of 216 mL were diluted into a fresh bag. The same procedure was performed for pentyl acetate where 3 mL were vaporized and 126 mL were diluted into the second bag. Vapor concentrations were determined using a piD-TECH eVx silver analyzer (AMETEK MOCON, Ringsted, Denmark) as shown in Table 1. A vapor sample was delivered using a 50 mL Fortuna Optima gastight syringe (Poulten & Graf GmbH, Wertheim, Germany) and a syringe pump (Cole-Parmer 78–8100C, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) into the drift tube at 0.2–0.4 mL·min–1 providing adjustable final concentrations in the drift gas from 5 to 8 ppbv. Sample vapors were introduced into the first drift region and the reaction region by adding a sample to the gas flow entering the tube at the end of the first drift region. This leads to the formation of monomer and dimer ions in the reaction region as well as in the first drift region. This creates a spectrum with elevated baselines between the peaks, measured with the first detector (second ion gate). After 20 min of continuous injection, a steady state was observed as the slopes and peak heights were unchanged and drift times for tRIP, tMono, and tDimer were obtained from spectra from the first detector. The second ion gate of the tandem IMS was used to transfer ions with an ion gate open time of 500 μs to the second drift region. The gate opening time was selected as long as possible to neglect the discrimination effect of the slower ions. It is assumed that the transmission is identical for the monomer and dimer ions.

Results and Discussion

The baseline slope between protonated monomer and proton bound dimer (Figure 3A) arises from reactions between sample neutrals and ions injected into the drift region; other ions are seen as symmetrical peaks from the injection of the ions formed in the reaction region. This is seen as a symmetrical peak for reactant ions (6.8 ms, Figure 3B) from the initial injection pulse. While the baseline in Figure 3C should have a positive slope, a slightly negative slope arose from the formation of proton bound dimers (eq 2) simultaneously within ion flow toward the detector. Experimental Findings with a Tandem Drift Tube for Ion Mobility Spectrometry

A tandem drift tube can provide abundances for individual ions throughout the drift time,20,21 so that slopes of spectral baselines of the kinetic experiment can be separated into two slopes using the second tandem IMS stage, resulting in mobility resolved abundances of each ion. This should provide direct access to the individual rate coefficients. This is shown in Figure 4 from tandem IMS characterization of triethyl phosphate introduced at 5.1 to 6.1 ppbv into the first drift region of the tandem drift tube.

Ion mobility spectrum from the first detector of the tandem-IMS (A), peak height of RIP (B), monomer peak (C), and dimer peak (D) from the second stage mobility spectrum from the second detector at each increment. Vertical error bars presented in B, C, and D are determined based on ten measurements. Horizontal error bars are based on the gate open time of 0.5 ms.

The plot in Figure 4A was obtained from the second ion gate used as a detector with the reactant ion, protonated monomer, and proton bound dimer seen at drift times of 9.5, 12.5, and 17.5 ms, respectively. These findings compare favorably to the COMSOL simulations in Figure 3A with symmetrical peaks and elevated baselines with positive slopes. Plots in Figure 4B,D were obtained from mobility spectra which were taken from 18 0.5 ms wide mobility isolated steps between 9 and 18 ms. Some intensity for the reactant ions was observed in the first two 0.5 ms increments due to the width of the mobility peaks and the second ion gate. This feature has no consequence on the slope. The ion abundances from the second drift region arise from full baseline separation of the protonated monomer and proton bound dimer and are accurate experimental measures of abundances of individual ions, in Figure 4C,D. In summary, findings from the tandem drift tube with boxcar integration over the mobility drift times of the first drift region favorably match the quantitative patterns of ion abundances from simulation (Figure 3). Although the ion abundances can be independently and accurately determined, the influence of simultaneous reaction of eq 2 altered the values for [M] and [MH+(H2O)n] negating the direct extraction of k1 or k2 from the baseline slopes.

Comparison of Experimental and Simulated Ion Intensities

Putting the determined values for k1 and k2 into differential equations and solving them for a neutral concentration [M] of 0 to 40 ppbv will result in reactant, monomer and dimer ion abundances shown in Figure 7. The intensities of different ion species (Q) are shown normalized to the maximum reactant ion intensity (Qmax). Experimental data were obtained by injecting triethyl phosphate into the reaction region of an single drift tube IMS (Figure S6) so that the concentration inside the reaction region was varied from 0 to 31 ppbv (Table S2), which provides the mobility spectra for six concentrations. The area under the different peaks is the amount of formed ions Q. Qmax is the area under the reactant ion peak when no sample is introduced. In Figure 7, the peak area of the reactant ion peak (black error bars), the monomer peak (blue error bars), and the dimer (red error bars) are shown, normalized to Qmax. Comparing the results from the simulation (black line, reactant ions; blue line, monomer ions; red line, dimer ions) and the measurements shows a favorable match. Within a 10% error to the max value, the real measurements are following the simulations. The deviation may be explained by experimental parameters that are missing from the basic simulation, such as the losses at the ion gate and the diffusion losses and the rough estimation of the reactant ion density. Also, the missing backward reaction can cause a difference between simulation and reality.

Conclusions

A method to simultaneously determine the rate coefficients for displacement reactions forming a protonated monomer and proton bound dimer from displacement reactions of volatile organic compounds with hydrated protons was developed using empirical measurements of ion mobility spectra at ambient pressure. This was achieved using a tandem drift tube, where ion abundances of multiple processes could be stabilized and quantitatively determined using the second mobility stage. The method of successive approximation of k2 and baseline corrections to determine k1 was tested with arbitrarily chosen rate coefficients to generate mobility spectra and matched back-extracted k values within 90% or greater. This method provides a first ever technique to obtain rate coefficients for protonated monomers when subsequent reactions to proton bound dimers occur. The values obtained for k1 here cannot be compared to any literature values under comparable conditions although reports for k1 with compounds not forming proton dimers7 were within the same range of these values. The values for k2 were also never reported for these substances under these conditions. While those values reported in this study are about an order of magnitude faster than some previously reported,10,16 differences can be attributed to levels of ion hydration, differences in substances, and lack of correction for formation of monomer in the previous work.

Consequently, the method is supported by findings from computational modeling and by reference to rate coefficients obtained at ambient pressure under similar, though not identical conditions. The methods involve comparatively uncomplicated technology, although the method also required determination of vapor concentrations separately. The method should be applicable for determination of rate coefficients for any volatile organic compound with the corresponding gas phase ion molecule reactions. This will include a large number of compounds whose chemical properties (e.g., dipole moments) allow formation of protonated monomers and proton bound dimers, which are stable over to 20+ ms in air at ambient pressure.
