Development of a novel high-performance portable barrier based on full-scale field tests and simulation optimization
Authors: Fulin Wang, Guowei Xiang, Quanqi Cheng, Yu Zhang, Xiugui Fang, Xiuchen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Existing portable barriers often sustain severe damage or catastrophic failure when subjected to vehicle collisions at high protection levels. As the number of road reconstruction and expansion projects grows, the safety performance of portable barriers becomes crucial for safeguarding the well-being of personnel in construction work zones. Moreover, numerical analysis has been extensively employed in simulating and evaluating roadside safety hardware over the past decade. However, in contrast to simple material tests, the actual damage modes and deformation mechanisms of barriers can differ substantially from simulated outcomes.​ Therefore, two sets of full-scale impact tests (at 80 km/h and 60 km/h) were initially carried out. In accordance with the design specifications for portable barriers outlined in international standards, the impact resistance of a newly designed portable concrete barrier (NPCB-2) at its most unfavorable installation position was investigated. Finite element (FE) models were established using LS-DYNA and validated against the crash test data to ensure the reliability of barrier safety performance evaluation.​ Subsequently, the validated FE models were utilized to assess the effects of reinforcement spacing, connection thickness, and barrier segment length on actual impact performance. Additionally, additional reinforcing bars were incorporated to further enhance the bonding strength between rectangular steel tubes and steel rebars, leading to the proposal of a modified portable barrier configuration (NPCB-4). Numerical simulations verified that the impact resistance performance of the modified barrier, including the impact process, stress distribution, acceleration, angular displacement, and energy absorption, meets the requirements of relevant specifications. The combined experimental-simulation approach adopted in this barrier design offers a valuable reference for the development of novel barrier configurations in the future.​

Introduction

Portable concrete barriers are used to isolate the construction work area and normal driving lane area, and is more and more widely used in the engineering project with the gradual increase of reconstruction and maintenance projects. At present, the impact resistance performance of the barrier used to isolate the construction area and the normal driving lane is seriously insufficient, which does not play a good role in intercepting the vehicle impact situation, and causes different degrees of injury to the driver and passengers after the impact.

Furthermore, existing research on the impact resistance of portable concrete barriers is constrained by two key factors: the high cost of full-scale vehicle impact tests and the prolonged production cycle of precast barriers. These limitations collectively result in a scarcity of experimental data, hindering in-depth investigations into barrier performance optimization. Portable concrete barrier impact tests typically involve only small passenger cars or small trucks, and the available data for collisions with medium to large vehicles is extremely limited. Fang et al.1 conducted a systematic study on the dynamic response and displacement deformation behavior of portable concrete barrier (PCB) systems under two structures (independent and box girder reinforced) under vehicle impact by combining experiments with numerical simulations, and analyzed the impact of shortening the total length of the barrier section on the box girder reinforced PCB. Dobrovolny et al.2 developed a new type of low-profile portable concrete barrier structure to address the issue of visual obstruction in highway construction areas. They verified a low-profile PCB system with a barrier height of only 635 mm under the TL-3 test grade through finite component and full-scale real vehicle crash tests. Bielenberg et al.3 conducted in-depth research on reinforcement measures that can enhance the safety performance of unanchored PCB through simulation and real vehicle impact tests. The study found that increasing the mass of barrier sections, increasing the friction coefficient between the barrier and the ground, and reducing the intervals between barrier sections can all reduce the maximum lateral displacement of the barrier system. However, changing the interaction (connection mode) between the barrier sections is the most effective way to reduce the lateral displacement of the barrier. Yang et al.4 proposed a novel form of movable median concrete barrier and systematically optimized its design parameters based on finite element simulation.

Furthermore, in existing studies, finite element models are often validated using a limited number of indicators, such as vehicle trajectories at different time in comparative tests5,6 This has led to insufficient accuracy in finite element simulation models, making it difficult to apply them to subsequent structural optimization analyses.

Therefore, a set of relatively comprehensive barrier anti-impact performance improvement scheme is established. A preliminary design of a portable concrete barrier is proposed, which can provide a higher level of safety protection, and can play a better role in protecting the safety of the occupants during the impact process. More specifically, the higher crashworthiness level SB was selected as the design basis for portable barrier design by comparing the design protection energies specified in different national standards7–9, as shown in Table 1, where a 10,000 kg bus impacted the portable barrier system at a speed of 80 km/h and at an angle of 20°. The full-scale experimental investigation was implemented to determine the deflection and safety performance of portable barrier system. Unfortunately, the NPCB-2 which was originally designed failed to contain and redirect the vehicle. Therefore, the numerical analysis was conducted to first validate the test results based on the experimental results. Based on the verified FE models, the weak-connection reinforcement was further analyzed and the configuration of connection end, longitudinal reinforcement and hoop reinforcement were mainly assessed and optimized. Moreover, a modified portable barrier, NPCB-4, was proposed. The impact process, stress distribution, concrete deformation and impact resistance evaluation of the NPCB-4 under vehicular collision were revealed by comprehensively numerical analysis.

Overall, the research further calibrates the FE numerical model through full-scale testing, which provides a reference for subsequent material modeling and simulation of different vehicle types. It is hoped that it could provide guidance for the safety performance of portable barriers with different connection methods.

Materials and methods

Preparation of barrier

A 72-m long portable concrete barrier (PCB) system, consisting of thirty-six 2.0 m long PCBs, named NPCB-2, was initially designed based on the traditional F and New Jersey barrier shape. Each barrier segment had a height of 1200 mm, which was attached with rectangular steel tubes and vertical I-beam pin to adjacent PCBs, and the initial barrier design profiles are shown in Fig. 1. Moreover, the internal configuration of the barrier was composed of 12 mm diameter longitudinal reinforcement and 16 mm hoop reinforcement, and the concrete was chosen to meet the C40 strength requirement.

Fig. 1The initial barrier design profile.

The initial barrier design profile.

The barrier was fabricated by the Dongying-Qingzhou Highway Project Department (Shandong Province, China). As illustrated in Fig. 2, its manufacturing process comprises three sequential steps:​.

Step Ⅰ: First, concrete forms, reinforcing bars, and connectors are fabricated respectively. Following prefabrication, the inner surfaces of the forms, reinforcing bars, and steel plates must be cleaned to ensure surface smoothness and cleanliness.​.

Step Ⅱ: Next, the reinforcing bars are tied and welded. For sections requiring welding, double-sided arc welding is applied to the joints; the remaining joints are secured via tying. Subsequently, rectangular steel tubes are welded to the ends of the reinforcing bars. To further reinforce the structurally weakest regions, full welding is implemented on four single-sided vertical connectors (connecting the long sides of rectangular tubes to circumferential reinforcing bars) and two horizontal connectors (connecting the short sides of rectangular tubes to longitudinal reinforcing bars).​.

Step Ⅲ: Finally, concrete pouring, formwork removal, and curing are completed in stages. Additionally, bridge automatic curing equipment is employed to enhance and sustain concrete performance, with adjustments based on temperature and humidity variations during the actual construction process.​.

The Material Testing System (MTS) electronic universal testing machine was used to conduct the tensile test on connection and steel bars, and compressive test on concrete. It was worth mentioning that it was necessary to change the clamps of the testing machine when measuring different components.

Concrete testing

C40 grade was chosen to design the concrete barrier. Consequently, the research used it as a barrier infill and panel. The standard test specimen of 150 × 150 × 150 mm size was used to find the compressive strength at 28 days of curing according to Chinese code GB/T 50,081–201914. Furthermore, the compression rate of concrete specimen was 2 mm/min and the average of the concrete’s compressive strength was 47.4 MPa.

Experimental setup and test procedure.

Impact tests were conducted using the full-scale vehicle testing system at the Xuchang Actual Vehicle Crash Test Base (Henan Province, China). Figure 4 presents the schematic diagram and detailed photographs of this test system. The vehicle testing system comprises a gravity tower, test vehicles, an acceleration track, and the test object, where the kinetic energy of the vehicle on the acceleration track is derived from the gravitational potential energy of the gravity tower15. Specifically, the test vehicle was positioned on a traction track aligned with the centerline of the acceleration track, and the traction trolley on the traction track was connected to the chassis of the test vehicle. Once the traction trolley reached the end of the track, brake pads decoupled the wire rope from the trolley. Thus, the speed of the vehicle at the moment of this decoupling can be considered the test speed of the runaway vehicle.

The test vehicle was made by Chinese Yutong company with a mass of 10.32 t, and its striking section was approximate rectangular with the dimensions of 2400 × 3140 mm. To minimize experimental costs, secondhand vehicles were used as collision vehicles. Notably, these vehicles maintained overall structural integrity while satisfying the requirements of relevant specifications.​.

Fig. 4Schematic plan of the barrier impact test and related facilities.

Schematic plan of the barrier impact test and related facilities.

The accelerometer and sensors were installed at the center of gravity of the vehicle to measure the impact acceleration and impact velocity. Moreover, three high-speed cameras were arranged in corresponding positions to record the lateral deformation of the barrier and the vehicle’s trajectory, one of which was located above the top of the impact point, one was located in a safe position parallel to the barrier length direction, and the last one was placed at the end parallel to the barrier9. Furthermore, the velocimeter was installed at the end of the acceleration track to ensure the velocity of the test vehicle.

The NPCB-2 barriers were accurately installed on the ground of the test site in accordance with the test conditions, as shown in Fig. 5 (b). In order to facilitate observation of the location of the point of impact, the barriers were sequentially numbered and the road safety sign was affixed to the impact points. According to the relevant specifications of barrier9, during a collision, it is necessary to ensure that the impact point of the barrier is located at 1/3 of the overall length of the barrier to avoid the end boundary effect.

Portable concrete barrier models

The finite element model of the portable barrier system consisted of 36 segments with a length of 2 m and a total length of 72 m. Each barrier segment was composed of concrete, connection (vertical I-beam and rectangular steel tube), and reinforcement (longitudinal reinforcement and hoop reinforcement).

The barrier concrete was modeled using eight-node full integration brick elements. And the continuous surface cap model (MAT_159 CSCM) was used to simulate the dynamic behavior of concrete under vehicle impacts16. The CSCM model could accurately capture the confinement effects and post-peak softening in the inelastic concrete behavior of concrete17. Based on material certification tests, the concrete compressive strength and aggregate size were determined to be 48.6 MPa and 10 mm respectively.

The adjacent barrier was connected using the connection key, as shown in Fig. 5. The connection key was made of 5 mm, 7 mm and 11 mm thick steel plates welded together to form the rectangular steel tubes, I-beam webs and flanges respectively. A four node Hughes-Liu shell element was used to create the connection key and the piecewise linear plasticity material model (MAT_24) was utilized to better show the effect of steel strain rate, as shown in Fig. 3 (b). Table 1 listed steel material properties determined based on true test certifications. Furthermore, the strain rate parameters Cs and Ps were separately defined as 40 and 518. Hughes-Liu beam elements were used for the steel reinforcement due to its efficient compatibility with concrete model. The plastic kinematic material model (MAT_3) was utilized to simulate the properties of the longitudinal reinforcement and hoop rebars. According to the material certification from the material test, the yield stress of reinforcing bars of different sizes is completely in accordance with Table 3.

Moreover, the Constrained Lagrange In Solid formulation of LS-DYNA was employed to embed the reinforcement in concrete using constraint-based contact19. The the Contact Automatic Single Surface command was used to establish contact between the concrete barrier and the steel connection, and the static friction coefficient and dynamic friction coefficient between the concrete and the steel connection (include rectangular steel tube and I-beam) were set to 0.6 and 0.45 respectively. And the contact between the concrete barrier and the vehicle was modeled using Contact Automatic Surface to Surface command, which was applicable to the penalty contact algorithm between the shell and solid element. Furthermore, the ground was modeled as a rigid plane while setting the coefficient of kinetic friction between the barrier system and the ground to 0.2. As shown in Fig. 5 (a), only the friction coefficient with the ground was applied to the bottom of the barrier, and no other additional constraints were applied. The gravity acceleration was applied to the vehicle through the Load body Z command.

Furthermore, the penalty stiffness scale factor for the contact between concrete and steel connectors is 1.0, and the contact ratio between vehicles and the guardrail system is set to 0.8.

The mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted to find the most acceptable mesh size that stroke a balance between computational accuracy and cost reduction. Therefore, numerous simulations of material scaling tests with different mesh sizes were implemented, and the optimal material properties and mesh sizes were determined in conjunction with actual material property tests before the numerical simulations. Moreover, a stiffness-based hourglass control method was utilized for all components to mitigate and avoid the hourglass deformation20. The final optimal grid size for concrete was determined to be 40 mm, for steel connectors to be 10 mm, and for reinforcing bars to be 10 mm.

Vehicle models.

The vehicle model was developed by the Road Traffic Safety Research Center of the Ministry of Public Security (MPS) and modified for road safety applications by the Vehicle Research Center of Tongji University. The adjusted vehicle model was fully in accordance with the SB level test conditions defined in the JTG B05-01 specification.

The total weight of the passenger car was 10 t, and the vehicle model contained more than 91,000 shell cells, 8 spring cells, 56 beam cells, 88,000 nodes, and 51 other elemental units. Moreover, the vehicle model was validated against an experimental test by many scholars with good correlation between the experimental and simulated results21. And the parameters of the vehicle model were almost identical to the test vehicle.

Experiment and numerical results

Experiment description and results

During the full-scale NPCB-2 crash test, the 10 t bus struck the connection between segments 12 and 13 (called measurement point No. 12.5, MP-12.5) of the portable barrier system. The measured impact velocity was 79.8 km/h and the impact angle was 19.6°. Thus, the deviation requirement was in accordance with the specification.

The dynamic process of vehicle collision with barrier was shown in Fig. 6. At the moment of impact, the vehicle’s front wheel began to contact the barrier. Subsequently, the right front wheel began to ride up the barrier surface and the lateral displacement of the barrier at the point of impact developed. The rear of the vehicle started to contact the barrier at 0.44 s. Then the connection at MP-12.5 pulled apart at 0.60 s and the connection at MP-14.5 between segments 14 and 15 pulled off at 0.65 s. The vehicle managed to steer with an exit angle of 35° until 0.74 s, but this was accompanied by the almost total collapse of the barrier between segments 13 and 18. The vehicle finally stopped at the edge of the test site.

Fig. 6Comparisons of dynamic process of vehicle collision with barrier (test and simulation).

Comparisons of dynamic process of vehicle collision with barrier (test and simulation).

The final damage pattern was shown in Fig. 7 (a) and (b), which was deemed unacceptable according the current criteria. The connected joints between the I-beam and the rectangular steel tube failed at MP-12.5 which caused a continuous collapse of the subsequent barrier. More specially, the large deflection and collapsed between segments 13 and 18 developed. Segment 13 was 4.87 m behind the installation at MP-12.5 end. Moreover, large cracks emerged at MP-10.5 (between Segments 10 and 11), accompanied by concrete spalling on the barrier surface. At this location, the longitudinal reinforcement at the connection was pulled apart, and the internal rectangular steel tubes of the barrier underwent significant deformation. Additionally, at MP-12.5, the failed welds at the connection joints between the rectangular steel tubes and steel reinforcement were found to be predominantly 5.2 mm in size—grossly inconsistent with the intended 8 mm design specification.

Fig. 7Comparisons of final damage pattern between test and simulation (80 km/h).

Comparisons of final damage pattern between test and simulation (80 km/h).

The configuration of the barrier connection is slightly weak and the construction weld could not meet the specified size, which leads to the failure of the test and fails to meet the requirements of SB level design criteria. In order to further verify the effectiveness of the barrier connecting structure, another set of full-scale impact tests were carried out at a test speed of 60 km/h, and the test results are shown in Fig. 8. The connecting joint between the I-beam and the rectangular steel tube also failed at MP-12.5, resulting in the continued collapse of the subsequent barrier. Specifically, at MP-12.5, the joint between segments 12 and 13 was broken, the longitudinal bars were pulled out, and the rectangular steel tube detached from the pre-embedded concrete.

Fig. 8Final damage pattern of NPCB-2 (60 km/h).

Final damage pattern of NPCB-2 (60 km/h).

Two sets of full-scale impact tests proved the weak characteristics of the original design structure, and therefore, corresponding improvements to the connecting joints were required to enable the portable barrier NPCB to meet the safety requirements of the standard evaluation.

Validation of simulation results.

The dynamic process of vehicle collision and final damage pattern was compared to determine the similarity between the experiment and simulation of NPCB-2. It could be seen from these pictures that the vehicle post-impact behavior matches reasonably well with that of the full-scale experimental tests. As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the moments of impact and steering of the simulated vehicle were slightly slower than the corresponding moment of the tests. However, the deformation of the vehicle and the barrier was almost the same in the comparison between the simulation and the experiment, and even the lateral deformation of the barrier after being broken by the impact was nearly the same. In addition, as shown in Fig. 7(b) and Fig. 8, the tests and simulations presented a similar phenomenon at the crushed barrier segments 12 and 13, where small-scale concrete spalling occurred on the surface of the barrier, the steel tubes were pulled off, and the internal hoop and longitudinal bars were pulled out and left bare.

Comparisons between changes in occupant impact velocity, Euler angular displacements and resultant acceleration of the test and simulation were shown in Fig. 9, all of which comply with the standards for occupant risk assessment in the specification. It can be seen that although the test failed, the error between the simulated peak acceleration and the actual one was only 3.6%, and the velocity and Euler angles of the vehicles showed a surprising similarity. Moreover, it was worth mentioning that the acceleration output of the vehicle test was filtered and processed with a cutoff frequency of 60 Hz, which could effectively reduce noise and interference at the measurement point. While some differences existed between the test and the simulation, the research team felt that relatively accurate barrier deflections and safety performance could still be estimated from the simulations for the provided vehicle and concrete models. Therefore, the validated finite element model could be used in the subsequent simulation study.

Numerical optimization

Modified barrier design

Minor structural defects in the impact connection keys and improper construction of the welds contributed to the failure of the real vehicle impact test. Through the analysis of the damage pattern of the barrier during the impact process and after the impact, it was found that the rebar arrangement at the crucial joint was too sparse, and the strength of the rectangular steel pipe and I-beam steel was slightly lacking. In addition, due to the characteristics of the portable barrier itself, the bottom of the barrier did not have any anchoring facilities with the ground, thus it was particularly important to rely on the barrier’s own weight to bring the ability to resist lateral deformation.

Barrier segment length

Given the critical role of individual barrier segment length, simulations of impact-induced deformation were conducted for barrier segments with lengths of 2 m, 3 m, and 4 m, respectively, under the original design configuration. As illustrated in Fig. 10, increasing the length of barrier segments-without additional structural reinforcement-was notably beneficial for SB-level impact scenarios. As segment length increased, the lateral displacement of the barrier during vehicle impact decreased, facilitating smooth vehicle redirection and substantially reducing damage to the vehicle body.​.

Specifically, the vehicle transitioned from a state of severe damage and immobilization to one with nearly no body damage, exiting at an angle that met or exceeded the required specifications. Notably, a single 4 m-long barrier segment (including reinforcement steel, concrete, and connection components) reached an approximate total weight of 5 tons. Exceeding this weight threshold would hinder subsequent installation and maintenance operations.

Fig. 10Comparison of final damage pattern of impacts with different barrier segment lengths.

Comparison of final damage pattern of impacts with different barrier segment lengths.

Rebar spacing and strength at connection

Based on the optimized length of a single barrier segment, the strength of the connection should also be reinforced. The analysis method was consistent as above, thus the thickness of the I-beam web and flange were enhanced to 10 mm and 13 mm respectively, and the wall thickness of the rectangular steel tube was increased from 5 mm to 10 mm. Moreover, the steel rebar at the connection between the barrier segments should also be locally strengthened. The simulation comparison was conducted by taking the distance of 100 mm and 150 mm to the left and right of the hoop reinforcement at the location of the adjacent rectangular steel tube, respectively.

The impact force-time curves showed nearly identical characteristics, merely the peak impact force and final deformation was observed slight difference. Therefore, the G100-2 type configuration would be adopted for further reinforcement depending on the minimal deformation and impact forces.

Modified barrier simulation

The original final damage pattern revealed a poor synergy between the longitudinal reinforcement, hoop reinforcement and the rectangular steel tube. Therefore, in addition to the above reinforcement measures, four reinforcing rebars with a diameter of 20 mm were added at the location of the hoop reinforcements in the reinforced connection and welded around the rectangular steel tube to further improve the strength of the connection structure, and the detailed connection structure was shown in Fig. 13.

Finite element model of the modified PCB system consisted of eighteen 4-m long, PCB segment comprising the total system length of 72 m, which was defined as NPCB-4. The thicknesses of its I-beam webs and rectangular steel tubes were increased to 13 mm and 10 mm, respectively, and the hoop reinforcement was locally densely arranged at the ends.

Overall simulation behavior

A critical requirement of the safety performances during the impact is that the impacted vehicle could continue to move forward along the same direction of the barrier, and it remain upright during and after the crash. Figure 14 illustrates top and front views of the impacting vehicle at different stages of impact. And the point of impact located at MP-6.5. It could be observed that the modified NPCB-4 showed acceptable performance under the high-level impact conditions. The maximum lateral displacement of the barrier was merely 835 mm and the vehicle body remained intact, realizing the entire resistance process of impact, redirecting, and driving away more smoothly.

Portable barriers were often used in reconstruction and expansion engineering projects, and the allowable deformation should be determined based on the actual maximum deformation required. Higher impact level simulations will be further explored subsequently.

Barrier and vehicle damage

Figure 15 shows the damage mode of the modified NPCB-4 subjected to vehicle impact. The rear surface of the barrier experienced more severe damage than the front surface. Localized concrete spalling was observed on the surfaces of segments 5, 6, and 8 and large area of cracks and concrete spalling ocurred on the connection between segments 6 and 7. This can be attributed to the huge impact force achieved through the back-slap effect of the vehicle rear collision. Furthermore, no significant stresses in the connections between the barriers before the rear collision. However, after the collision, there were different degrees of damage, and due to the good energy dissipation characteristics of the connection structure, the impact energy was also dissipated in several surrounding connection areas. It was worth mentioning that the I-beam and rectangular steel tube in the barrier segment of the connection merely deformed, not even yield, could play a good role in the key connection. During the process of vehicle impact to steering to driving away, NJPC-4 could produce displacement and deformation to consume the huge kinetic energy from the vehicle, and at the same time, it can effectively block the SB level impact, which can meet the safety requirements of high collision avoidance level.

Acceleration risk

The acceleration values were evaluated to determine the risk to occupant as shown in Fig. 17(a). The accelerations were attached to the location at the center of gravity of the simulated vehicle and the 60 Hz SAE filter was used as a filter for the raw results in the LS-DYNA. Increased oscillations first and then decreased were shown in the numerical acceleration. The maximum peak acceleration also occurred during the vehicle back-slap phase. Although the coordinate system would not be so perfect in the actual experiment because the accelerometer is always in motion, the maximum acceleration was shown to be only 3.69 g, which was far below the 20 g limit stipulated in the specification. In other words, the NPCB-4 structural configuration, when subjected to an impact energy of 280 kJ, could provide good protection for both occupant acceleration and the vehicle body itself.

Energy absorption

The time histories of the kinetic energy and internal energy of the whole system was shown in Fig. 18 (a). As shown in Fig. 18 (a), the kinetic energy of the whole system gradually decreased with time, while the internal energy continued to increase. The internal energy reached 73.6 kJ until 0.12 s, which was related to the first impact of the vehicle. After that, the internal energy remained nearly constant until the second collision from the rear end of the vehicle (0.42s), at which the internal energy reached 169 kJ. In addition, Fig. 18(b) demonstrated the energy absorption curves of different components of the barrier (concrete, steel reinforcement and the connection) with time during the vehicle impact. It could be seen that the energy consumed by the deigned connection accounts for approximately 11% until 0.7 s. These values were significantly lower than the energy consumed by the concrete itself. This was due to the fact that a larger area of concrete was damaged, spalling and peeling off, dissipating most of the energy.

Conclusions

This study developed a high-performance portable barrier (NCPB-2) capable of withstanding high-level impact conditions. Two sets of full-scale vehicle impact tests were performed to validate the performance of the barrier system. Numerical models of the portable barrier system were developed and validated against crash test data. The validated finite element (FE) model was then employed to reinforce the weak components of the initially designed barrier structure, enabling the optimized barrier configuration (NPCB-4) to meet high crashworthiness requirements. Furthermore, the safety performance of the NPCB-4 system was investigated. The following conclusions were drawn:

Full-scale crash tests of medium-sized buses impacting portable barriers under high protection levels (Class A and Class SB) were established. Notably, the NCPB-2 barrier failed to contain and redirect the impacting vehicle, indicating a need for structural configuration optimization.The accuracy of the simulation model was effectively improved by comparing positional changes of the vehicle and barrier during actual crash tests, barrier system failure modes, and variations in velocity, angular displacement, and acceleration during collision. This offers a reference for further optimization of barrier safety performance and subsequent scholarly research.The validated FE model was used to reinforce the original barrier system. Results indicated that increasing the length of barrier segments effectively reduced the maximum lateral displacement of the system. Additionally, barrier safety performance was enhanced by optimizing reinforcement arrangement at barrier segment joints (i.e., adjusting stirrup spacing and locally densifying reinforcement) and improving the strength of steel connectors. This study is expected to provide insights for subsequent research on local reinforcement of barrier structures.A modified portable barrier “NPCB-4” was proposed and its safety performance in terms of impact process, stress distribution, resultant acceleration, angular displacement, and energy absorption was evaluated. Results demonstrated that NPCB-4 could smoothly redirect simulated vehicles, dissipate vehicle kinetic energy, and avoid fatal injuries to occupants, while meeting the safety requirements of higher protection levels. Future work will involve comprehensive impact tests to confirm its effectiveness, with consideration of factors including material variations and structural defects.A new barrier design idea was proposed. The validated FE model was used to reinforce the original barrier system. Results indicated that increasing the length of barrier segments effectively reduced the maximum lateral displacement of the system. Additionally, barrier safety performance was enhanced by optimizing reinforcement arrangement at barrier segment joints (i.e., adjusting stirrup spacing and locally densifying reinforcement) and improving the strength of steel connectors. This study is expected to provide insights for subsequent research on local reinforcement of barrier structures.
