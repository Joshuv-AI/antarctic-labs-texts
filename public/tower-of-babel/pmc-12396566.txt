Social Work Faculty Perceptions on the Need for Inclusive LGBTQIA + Policies in Schools of Social Work
Authors: Daniel Jacobson López, Yan Yuan, Andrea Joseph-McCatty, Helen Petracchi, Mary Hawk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLiterature has found that LGBTQIA + faculty members experience discrimination both within the classroom and the larger academic environment, including decreased faculty collaboration, student support, and opportunities for promotion and tenure. However, few researchers have examined the experiences of LGBQ faculty within social work programs. Given this paucity in the literature, we examined the perceptions of faculty members in US social work schools regarding inclusive LGBTIA + policies, and how these policies influence the environments for and experiences of LGBQ faculty members.MethodsThis primary data collection consisted of a survey provided to social work faculty from the Baccalaureate Social Work Program Directors and the Master of Social Work Program Directors in 2017, consisting of 193 respondents. A multi-level path analysis with latent factors was conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM) in R.ResultsWe found that higher support was significantly associated with less phobia and fewer incidences; likewise, more intervention was significantly correlated with less phobia and fewer incidences. Additionally, more inclusive LGBTQIA + policies were associated with more interventions. Interestingly, the presence of more policies was linked with higher levels of reported phobia and more incidences.ConclusionThe results encourage a need for active interventions to combat homophobia and racism within schools of social work.Policy ImplicationsAdditionally, the results support a need for inclusive LGBTQIA + policies with social work programs to support and create safe environments for LGBTQIA + faculty.

In the United States, 4.5% of adults, (nearly 15 million people) identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, or transgender (LGBTQIA +) (Casey et al., 2019). Ample evidence demonstrates that LGBTQIA + individuals experience multiple forms of discrimination, harassment, and violence because of their sexual orientation and/or gender identity (Corrales, 2017; Flores & Park, 2018; Ryan et al., 2009). In a national survey of adults in the United States, LGBTQIA + participants reported high rates of discrimination including slurs (57%), microaggressions (53%), sexual harassment (51%), and violence (51%) related to their sexual orientation or gender identity (Casey et al., 2019). Discrimination related to sexual orientation and gender identity frequently occurs in the workplace (Arabsheibani et al., 2007; Bilimoria & Stewart, 2009; Herek, 2009; Mays & Cochran, 2001; Ragins & Cornwell, 2001) with thorough documentation demonstrating that hostile work environments affect the performance and morale of gay and lesbian employees (Badgett & Frank, 2007; Bilimoria & Stewart, 2009). Workplace discrimination also manifests as bias against LGBTQIA + employees in hiring practices and promotion practices (Mays & Cochran, 2001), which negatively affects salary (Arabsheibani et al., 2007).

Effects of Inclusive LGBTQIA + Protections in the Workplace

One factor that has contributed to the negative experiences for LGBTQIA + persons in the workplace has been the historical lack of federal protections and inconsistencies in organizational policies and practices to protect LGBTQIA + employees in the United States (Webster et al., 2018). Furthermore, the available resources and support in the workplace or organization have been found to either exacerbate or mitigate anti-LGBTQIA + sentiment in the workplace (Webster et al., 2018). Where they do exist, LGBTQIA + inclusive policies have been found to have a positive effect on LGBTQIA + persons. Not only do inclusive LGBTQIA + policies benefit LGBTQIA + employees, but research has also shown that workplaces with LGBTQIA + protective policies have numerous benefits for all employers including competitive stock market relations, lower employer turnover, better employee recruitment, and less stressful workplace environments (Li & Nagar, 2013; Metcalf & Rolfe, 2011; Pichler et al., 2018; Ragins & Cornwell, 2001).

LGBTQIA + Policies Within Higher Education

Existing research also suggests the same lack of LGBTQIA + protective policies evidenced in other workplace environments has been found within institutions of higher education. As a result, supportive and safe environments for LGBTQIA + students vary widely across educational institutions in the United States (Biaggio et al., 2003; Bazarsky & Sanlo, 2012). Research exploring the degree to which students and faculty in higher education support inclusive LGBTQIA + policies has been inconclusive among students and faculty (Atteberry-Ash et al., 2018; Jenkins et al., 2009; Lewis, 2003). In schools with inclusive LGBTQIA + policies, students reported experiencing less discrimination compared to schools without inclusive LGBTQIA + policies (Atteberry-Ash et al., 2018). There is some evidence suggesting that support of inclusive LGBTQIA + policies may be associated with individuals’ proximity to or personal relationships with LGBTQIA + persons (Woodford et al., 2013).

Experiences of LGBTQIA + Faculty Members Within Higher Education

Understanding the presence of LGBTQIA + protective policies in higher education is critical given the impact of policy on the experiences of LGBTQIA + persons. Faculty members who identify as LGBTQIA + experience discrimination both within the classroom and the larger academic environment resulting in decreased faculty collaboration, student support, and opportunities for promotion and tenure (LaSala et al., 2008; Misawa, 2015). LGBTQIA + faculty may also receive less favorable reactions from students. Indeed, Anderson and Kanner (2011) found that college students were more likely to perceive lesbian and gay faculty as being biased and possessing a greater political agenda than their heterosexual counterparts, even when the same syllabi were used by both groups. LGBTQIA + faculty also experience anti-LGBTQIA + discrimination by heterosexual and cisgender faculty members. Research has found that anti-LGBTQIA + sentiment often results in LGBTQIA + faculty members not being invited to collaborate with their heterosexual and/or cisgender colleagues on other academic research projects, consequently hindering their potential to advance their careers within their respective academic institutions (Anderson, 2011; LaSala et al., 2008; Misawa, 2015). Further, LGBTQIA + faculty whose research focused on sexual minority populations often reported feelings of isolation, tokenism, and/or role encapsulation (Misawa, 2015).

Research has indicated that LGBTQIA + faculty who also belong to racial and ethnic minority groups may encounter additional barriers due to the intersectionality of their sexual orientation and race and/or ethnicity. Misawa (2015) found that university faculty who identified as gay men of color encountered discrimination from students and other faculty due to both their sexual orientation and their race/ethnicity. This study also revealed that gay Latino and Black male faculty received homophobic evaluations, were excluded from professional collaborations with heterosexual faculty, were socially isolated from heterosexual faculty and that their professional opinions were often dismissed by senior faculty and administration. Their positionality and the intersectionality of their identities may exacerbate the increased marginalization of LGBTQIA + faculty of color within higher education, as these individuals likely experience discrimination related to race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or all three.

Experiences of LGBTQIA + Faculty Within Social Work Programs

Ben-Ari (2001) examined attitudes toward homosexuality in the departments of social work, education, and psychology in five main universities in Israel. They found that social work departments generally held less homophobic attitudes compared to education departments but held higher homophobic attitudes when compared to psychology departments. Other research has examined support of LGBTQIA + content in the department’s curriculum. For example, Frederiksen-Goldsen and colleagues (2011) examined the degree to which social work faculty supported the inclusion of LGBTQIA + content in US- and Canadian-based curricula. They found that social work faculty were more supportive of including LGB content in the curriculum compared to the inclusion of transgender content. Their findings revealed that faculty felt that LGB content, including the topic of homophobia, was more important than transgender content and the topic of transphobia. Lastly, their study found that there were more resources available to teach on LGB content than on transgender content. Other research has focused on social work faculty’s perceptions of transgender individuals. One study examined the perceptions of transgender colleagues by social work faculty at Christian colleges and universities. Their study found that 50% of the social work faculty felt social work education programs should continue the employment and tenure of qualified faculty after they come out as transgender (de Jong, 2017). Despite this finding, Woodford, and colleagues (2012) found that the vast majority of social work faculty (98%) acknowledged that discrimination against transgender individuals does exist.

Conceptual Frameworks

Organizational Support Theory

In our effort to contribute to the literature by examining the relationship between inclusive LGBTQIA + policies and the workplace experiences of LGBTQIA + social work faculty members, we draw on organizational support theory (OST). OST concerns the extent to which an employee perceives that the organization values both their work and well-being (Kurtessis et al., 2017). This phenomenon is also described as perceived organizational support (POS). Antecedents of POS include notions of fairness, practices from the human resources department, and support from one’s supervisor (Kurtessis et al., 2017). Examples of organizational support include but are not limited to non-discrimination policies, same-sex domestic partner benefits, inclusive diversity training, and employee resources groups (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001; Pichler & Ruggs, 2018). A study conducted by Pichler et al. (2017) demonstrates that POS mediates the association between supportive policies and dispositions about the workplace among heterosexual, cisgender, and LGBTQIA + faculty as it reflects inherent concern for employee worker well-being. POS is relevant for discussing the workplace experiences of LGBTQIA + faculty being that their sexual minority identity and positionality place them at increased risk for discrimination in organizations that espouse heterosexist and homophobic policies and practices (Pichler & Ruggs, 2018; Ragins & Cornwell, 2001). Consequently, LGBTQIA + faculty who feel marginalized in the workplace may be less likely to perceive organizational concern for their well-being which can contribute to workplace stress (Pichler & Ruggs, 2018) and the intention to leave their place of employment. In contrast, employees who perceive organizational support are more likely to demonstrate what Amhed describe as “employee engagement, job satisfaction, organizational commitment, organizational citizenship behavior” and are moderately less likely to have “turnover intentions” (p. 868). While POS research has consistently documented a positive relationship between POS and employee workplace satisfaction for faculty, we also offer that employee positionality uniquely contextualizes our ability to interpret workplace well-being.

Positionality

Inherent in our existence are the social constructions of identity markers such as race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality. These components contribute to our positionality which Martin and Gunten (2002) suggest are “relational, complex, and fluid positions” (p.46). Complicating these identities is the notion of power. That is, how much power one holds or loses relative to their identity and the spaces one occupies (for example, home versus the workplace). Sánchez (2010) further describes positionality stating that one’s values and personal experiences in relation to their identities in the world inform their views of society implicitly and explicitly. Since identity markers are inherent in personhood and are a part of one’s lived experiences in the workplace (Misawa, 2015), the way employees perceive and experience the workplace environment will be inextricably connected to their positionality.

Thus, in this study, we contextualize our findings by using perceived organizational support to compare and contrast the workplace experiences of heterosexual and LGBQ social work faculty. Likewise, we draw on the concept of positionality to highlight the power dynamics in the workplace that are often mediated by identity markers such as race, ethnicity, gender, and sexuality.

Current Focus

As we seek to better understand the experiences of LGBTQIA + faculty within higher education, this study sought to examine the experiences of LGBTQIA + within social work programs. While the current literature has identified various forms of discrimination experienced among LGBTQIA + faculty, few researchers have examined the experiences of LGBTQIA + faculty members or the effects of inclusive LGBTQIA + policies within social work programs.

We examined the gaps in the literature by exploring the perceptions of faculty members in US-based schools of social work regarding LGBTQIA + non-discrimination policies and the extent to which these policies influence the environments and experiences of LGBQ faculty members. Understanding factors that are conducive to creating safe and inclusive environments for LGBTQIA + individuals will help to enhance the overall culture of social work programs. We hope that identifying these factors will lead to establishing more safe and inclusive environments for LGBTQIA + persons within academia.

Methods

Sample and Procedure

To explore these research questions, we conducted a web-based survey of social work faculty using US listservs from the Association of Baccalaureate Social Work Program Directors and the MSW Program Directors. Data were collected from May to August 2017 and the initial Qualtrics-based survey was distributed in May with reminders sent in June, July, and August 2017 to recruit as many participants as possible. All responses were recorded anonymously to ensure the protection of participants.

The survey was adapted with permission from the authors from a study conducted by Craig et al. (2015) and the Council on Social Work Education, which focused on the experiences of LGBTQIA + baccalaureate and graduate students in the United States and Canada. The survey included questions across three domains: individual, institutional, and program. Individual questions assessed demographic characteristics including race, and sexual and gender identification in keeping with Craig et al. (2015). Race was categorized as follows: white non-Latino, Black non-Latino, Asian/Pacific Islander, Multi-Racial, Black Latino, white Latino, and Other. The extent to which respondents experienced or were aware of discrimination related to sexual and gender identity was also explored. In addition, the survey assessed policies and characteristics at the institutional level, such as the presence of non-discrimination policies or gender-neutral bathrooms, and at the program level, such as LGBTQIA + topics in the formal curricula.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess the overall experience of faculty in social work programs within the United States (e.g., the percentage of schools with non-discrimination policies and/or the mean rate of school’s friendliness). Given the small and unbalanced sample sizes of non-heterosexual people (please see Table 1) and no self-identified transgender people, we collapsed multiple sexual orientation categories into binomial variables (LGBQ and heterosexual) for the purpose of meaningful analysis. We investigated the differential experiences across multiple domains (e.g. attitudes and beliefs or homophobic experiences) comparing heterosexual faculty with their LGBQ counterparts, using chi-square and t-tests for categorical and continuous variables respectively.

A path analysis with latent factors was conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM) in R. Specifically, our model includes five factors: “policies” indicates whether the school has non-discrimination policies (measured by Q71_1, Q71_2, Q70_1, Q70_2); “intervention” shows actions of handling and intervening related topics (measured by Q59_1, Q59_2, Q72_1, Q72_2, Q73_1, Q73_2); “phobia” refers to perceived transphobic or homophobic attitudes (measured by Q74_1, Q74_2, Q75_1, Q75_2); “support” evaluates how friendly or supportive the school environment is of LGBTQIA + issues (measured by Q76_1, Q76_2, Q63_1, Q63_2); “incidences” refers to witnessed transphobic or homophobic incidences (measured by Q185, Q187, Q193, Q196). Regarding the structural model, we proposed significant negative paths from constructs of policies, support, and interventions to (1) perceived phobia and (2) actual incidences. In addition, the following indices were used to evaluate the model performance: nonsignificant chi-square, CFI > 0.95, SRMR < 0.08, and RMSEA < 0.06 indicates satisfactory model fit. Finally, modifications were performed using the LaGrange Multiplier method, by which covariances among variables were added iteratively to improve the model performance.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 211 social work faculty members responded to the survey. A final sample of 193 participants was included in the analyses. Eighteen participants were dropped due to the following reasons: non-US schools (n = 10), did not complete the entire survey (n = 2), and completed only demographics (n = 6). Of the 193 respondents, the majority of the respondents were White, non-Latino (84%), identified their sex as female (79%), identified their gender as women (78%), were between 50 and 59 years old (33%), and identified as heterosexual (59%). All of the respondents identified as LGBQ. No participants self-identified as transgender. Forty-one percent of respondents self-identified as lesbian, gay, or bisexual. Of this group, 49% were lesbian.

Descriptive Statistics (Frequency, t-Tests, and Chi-Square Test Results)

As seen in Table 2 and Fig. 1, we present overall perceptions of varied LGBTQIA + related topics among social work programs, such as awareness of LGBTQIA + faculty and students, knowledge of non-discrimination policies, perceptions of phobia as well as gender identity competence in their programs. It is critical to note that the perceived phobia (over 80% among students) is alarmingly high, whereas the perceived intervention probabilities were low (approximately 50%).

While contrasting LGBQ faculty with their heterosexual counterparts’ experiences, LGBQ faculty reported fewer positive perceptions of their department’s acceptance of LGBTQIA + persons and their handling and attention toward LGBTQIA + education and curriculum (Tables 3 and 4). Further, significant differences were found between LGBQ faculty and heterosexual faculty regarding perceived homophobia or transphobia (Table 4). Regarding the belief in combating homophobia and transphobia, LGBQ faculty were less likely to endorse a positive answer as compared to their heterosexual counterparts. Taken together, our results indicated that LGBQ persons had less confidence than their heterosexual colleagues in their department’s ability to handle LGBTQIA + topics.

Path Analysis

Path analysis results indicated that all five factors were well-identified as evidenced by significant path parameters (see Table 5 and Fig. 2). In terms of the structural model, higher support was significantly associated with less phobia (b = − 0.70, z = − 4.36, p < 0.001) and fewer incidences (b = − 0.70, z = − 3.60, p < 0.001); likewise, more intervention was significantly correlated with less phobia (b = − 0.67, z = − 4.65, p < 0.001) and fewer incidences (b = − 0.72, z = − 6.62, p < 0.001). Further, our models found that policies and interventions were positively correlated (b = 0.44, z = 5.07, p < 0.001), in other words, having more policies was associated with more interventions. Interestingly, the presence of more policies was linked with higher levels of reported phobia (b = 0.66, z = 3.40, p < 0.001) and more incidences (b = 0.72, z = 2.83, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study sought to explore the experiences of LGBQ faculty in social work programs and the effects of inclusive LGBTQIA + policies on the environment for LGBTQIA + individuals at US-based social work programs. The results indicated that LGBQ faculty were less likely to have a favorable perception of how their respective program handled LGBTQIA + issues compared to their heterosexual counterparts, consistent with organizational support theory. There are a few possible explanations for why LGBQ faculty may have lower perceptions of the handling of LGBTQIA + topics compared to their heterosexual and/or cisgender colleagues. Consistent with the theory of positionality (Martin & Gunten, 2002), the lived experiences of LGBQ individuals may be notably different than their heterosexual counterparts. Sánchez (2010) allows us to understand how LGBQ faculty’s experiences in relation to their identities as a minoritized group informs their perceptions of how social work programs respond to LGBTQIA + issues. As such, their positionality may make them more aware and critical of non-inclusive environments. Additionally, the need to adequately address LGBTQIA + issues may be different for those belonging to the LGBTQIA + community compared to those who are not members of the LGBTQIA + community.

Additionally, our findings suggest policies and interventions are positively related. That is schools with policies that protect individuals based on both sexual orientation and gender identity were associated with faculty who were more likely to intervene in homophobic or transphobic incidents. There are a couple of explanations for this finding. One explanation may be that the existence of LGBTQIA + inclusive policies encourages individuals to intervene in anti-LGBTQIA + situations and/or the policies contain negative repercussions for those who do not intervene to combat homophobia or transphobia. Another possibility may be that additional LGBTQIA + inclusive policies may create an environment where LGBTQIA + individuals feel that they will be protected by the university, and therefore more likely to combat homophobia and transphobia, which further is supported by Organization Support Theory. These findings necessitate inclusive policies that protect on the basis of sexual orientation and gender identity, which may result in faculty being more likely to intervene in combating incidences of homophobia and transphobia, creating a safer environment for LGBTQIA + persons.

Interestingly, we found that the presence of non-discrimination policies was linked with higher levels of phobia and more incidences, which may suggest the limitations of LGBTQIA + policies on affecting the culture of the department. However, it may also be the case that the presence of policies elevates awareness and recognition of homophobic and transphobic incidences, thus producing more reports of such incidences. Inclusive LGBTQIA + policies may encourage and consequently have more individuals to report incidents of homophobia and transphobia, thus resulting in higher reports of incidents, rather than the presence of policies being ineffective or causing greater incidents of homophobia and transphobia.

Lastly, we found that programs with higher levels of support had higher levels of intervention and lower levels of homophobia and transphobia. Thus, programs that were more supportive of LGBTQIA + faculty had more faculty intervening to combat homophobia and transphobia and lower levels of LGBTQIA + bias. It may also be that such inclusive LGBTQIA + policies help to reduce homophobia and transphobia within these programs or programs that are more accepting of LGBTQIA + persons are more likely to be supportive of LGBTQIA + individuals. It is evident that support creates a safer environment for LGBTQIA + persons.

Implications

Our findings lend themselves to several implications for inclusive LGBTQIA + policy and practice within social work programs. Our findings highlight the need for active intervention by faculty to combat homophobia and transphobia within social work programs. This is especially important given that social work students experience anti-LGBTQIA + attitudes in their social work programs (Craig et al., 2017). The data suggested that higher levels of intervention were associated with less phobia and fewer incidences. This finding speaks directly to the positive effects that social work faculty members can have on the culture of the environment, by actively intervening to address and end anti-LGBTQIA + actions from occurring on campus. When faculty actively stand up against anti-LGBTQIA + discrimination, both faculty and students are aware that they are in a culture where such discrimination will not be tolerated and where individuals may be less likely to engage in anti-LGBTQIA + discrimination. As a result, faculty and staff members may be less likely to engage in homophobic and transphobic words and/or actions if they work in a program with a culture that does not condone such behavior. However, policies are often not sufficient to fully affect or create the culture of an environment. While policies can infer the culture of the workplace, it does not represent the actual said values of the workplace (Webster et al., 2018). Research has indicated that when policies are inconsistent or not enforced, they often do not hold significance (Dwertmann et al., 2016; Webster et al., 2018). As such, future research should explore whether pro-LGBTQIA + policies are enforced in the program’s administration and among faculty members.

A culture will not become more inclusive unless the values and beliefs of the policies are implemented and accepted by individuals (Clair et al., 2005; Webster et al., 2018). Campuses have generally not been supportive climates for LGBTQIA + faculty, due to anti-gay, anti-trans, and sexist environments (Garvey & Rankin, 2018). Additionally, with the increase of anti-diversity, equity, and inclusion policies across college campuses, there are now additional barriers to creating safe and inclusive workplace environments for LGBTQIA + faculty. Our findings underscore the need for social work programs to encourage and implement inclusive LGBTQIA + practices that create and sustain an inclusive culture that corresponds with the values of the profession.

Additionally, our findings provide implications for social work programs to integrate policies that encourage practices that support LGBTQIA + individuals within their departments both in policy and practice. Prior research has demonstrated that university administration can be instrumental in supporting LGBTQIA + inclusive programming (Dentato et al., 2014).

Inclusive policies and practices could foster an environment of belonging and consequently help to prevent employment-based discrimination which could lead to litigation. To enhance the environment for LGBTQIA + faculty, one suggestion would be to conduct focus groups with LGBTQIA + faculty regarding their needs, wants, and suggestions for improvement in the treatment of LGBTQIA + faculty and students in their department. Social work programs may also benefit from general LGBTQIA + educational sessions that provide an overview of LGBTQIA + terminology, history, health disparities, and anti-LGBTQIA + discrimination in the US and abroad. Additional consideration should be given to transgender topics and transphobia, given previous research that has not viewed such topics as important compared to LGB content (Frederiksen-Goldsen et al., 2011). Such training and education for social work faculty could be beneficial in various ways: helping to reduce anti-LGBTQIA + attitudes and beliefs among social work faculty and helping them to be more likely to recognize anti-LGBTQIA + attitudes, and better support their LGBTQIA + colleagues.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations which are important to note for the interpretations of our findings. One limitation was the lack of variability among respondents. The vast majority of respondents identified their sex and gender as female (79% and 78%, respectively). We did not have a large sample size of heterosexual cisgender male participants. Additionally, the majority of the respondents were White, non-Latino (84%). Given our sample size and the limited diversity in respondents, our findings may not be generalizable or representative of many social work educators. In addition, the survey sample was derived from a listserv which only included social work educators who participated in this listserv; as such, it is not exhaustive of all social work educators. Additionally, this study did not include individuals who identified as transgender. While this study sought to include the experiences of transgender individuals, we did not have self-identified transgender participants from whom we could learn about their experiences in their respective social work programs. As such, their experiences were not captured in our study. Lastly, we recognize that this self-reported data may be biased; faculty may report themselves as being more or less LGBTQIA + friendly than they are in actuality.

Conclusion

Social work departments must continue to advance the culture for LGBTQIA + individuals within their departments. Research has demonstrated the various ways in which LGBTQIA + faculty are subjected to various forms of homophobia and transphobia, particularly LGBTQIA + faculty of color, including departments of social work (Anderson & Kanner, 2011; Ben-Ari, 2001; Fredriksen-Goldsen et al., 2011; LaSala et al., 2008). Research has found hostile work environments cause dissatisfaction in academia and may cause faculty to leave their appointments (Rosser, 2004; Sanderson et al., 2000). While pro-LGBTQIA + policies can increase workplace morale and increase benefits for LGBTQIA + employees (Atteberry-Ash et al., 2018; Pichler et al., 2017), the existence of policies does not equate to a culture of LGBTQIA + acceptance or protection. Additionally, our findings highlight the need for both inclusive LGBTQIA + policies and a school administration that actively encourages and supports LGBTQIA + faculty and students, intervention to combat homophobia and transphobia, and for all members of social work programs to actively engage in combating homophobia and transphobia.
