Llama-Mimi: Speech Language Models with Interleaved Semantic and Acoustic Tokens
Issa Sugiura, Shuhei Kurita, Yusuke Oda, Ryuichiro Higashinaka

Abstract
We propose Llama-Mimi, a speech language model that uses a unified tokenizer and a single Transformer decoder to jointly model sequences of interleaved semantic and acoustic tokens. Comprehensive evaluation shows that Llama-Mimi achieves state-of-the-art performance in acoustic consistency and possesses the ability to preserve speaker identity. Our analysis further demonstrates that increasing the number of quantizers improves acoustic fidelity but degrades linguistic performance, highlighting the inherent challenge of maintaining long-term coherence. We additionally introduce an LLM-as-a-Judge-based evaluation to assess the spoken content quality of generated outputs. Our models, code, and speech samples are publicly available. 1 1 1 https://speed1313.github.io/llama-mimi

Introduction

Speech Language Models (SLMs) approach speech generation as a language modeling task by first converting waveforms into sequences of discrete tokens and then predicting these tokens autoregressively
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Early work established the feasibility of modeling speech with discrete tokens
[
1
]
, and later advances improved audio quality and long-term consistency through hierarchical tokenization strategies
[
2
]
.

These developments demonstrated the potential of speech language models, but often at the cost of increased architectural complexity, such as multi-stage pipelines and specialized tokenizers
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
For example, AudioLM
[
2
]
first generates semantic tokens using a single decoder, and then a cascade of models predicts the remaining acoustic tokens conditioned on these semantic tokens.
In contrast, Moshi
[
5
]
employs an RQ-Transformer
[
7
]
with a dual-transformer design: a backbone transformer models the temporal sequence across frames, while a depth transformer handles multiple tokens within each frame.
Although effective, such designs complicate training and deployment, requiring careful hyperparameter tuning across multiple models.

Unlike these complex pipelines, the natural language processing (NLP) community has demonstrated that large language models with a single Transformer decoder can achieve remarkable performance
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
and have also provided valuable insights such as scaling laws and training stability
[
11
,
12
]
.
These results motivate us to explore a simple, single-decoder approach for speech.

In this work, we introduce Llama-Mimi, a speech language model built on a unified tokenizer
[
5
]
. By interleaving semantic and acoustic tokens in a single sequence, our model adopts a simple single-decoder Transformer architecture.
We comprehensively assess our models across both acoustic and semantic dimensions using likelihood-based and generation-based methods.
Through extensive evaluation, we demonstrate that Llama-Mimi achieves state-of-the-art performance in acoustic consistency and maintains high speaker similarity.
We also highlight the trade-off between linguistic coherence and acoustic quality.
We further introduce an LLM-as-a-Judge
[
13
]
based evaluation to assess the spoken content quality of generated outputs, providing a more reliable alternative to previously used perplexity-based methods.

Related Work

Speech language models have been rapidly advancing
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
GSLM
[
1
]
pioneered speech generation by quantizing self-supervised speech representations
[
14
]
into discrete tokens via k-means and training a Transformer decoder with next-token prediction. However, the acoustic quality remains limited, as these representations are highly correlated with phonetic content but weakly correlated with speaker identity and gender
[
15
]
. pGSLM
[
16
]
extended this framework by incorporating phonetic and prosodic tokens in a multi-stream Transformer.
AudioLM
[
2
]
improved audio quality and long-term consistency by hierarchically generating semantic and acoustic tokens from a neural audio codec
[
17
,
18
]
in a coarse-to-fine manner with multiple LMs.
TWIST
[
3
]
further demonstrated that initializing a speech model with a pretrained text LM improves performance and speeds up convergence.
MusicGen
[
19
]
studied performance variations by delaying multi-stream token sequences.
More recently, several models have enhanced pre-trained LLMs by incorporating discrete audio tokens, enabling them to handle diverse audio-text tasks such as ASR and TTS with a single Transformer decoder
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
Spirit-LM
[
23
]
interleaved word-level text and speech tokens, supporting joint generation across modalities.
Moshi
[
5
]
modeled full-duplex dialogue as speech-to-speech generation over multi-stream tokens using an RQ-Transformer
[
7
]
.

Various generation strategies have been proposed to handle the multi-stream of semantic and acoustic tokens
[
4
]
.
The coarse-to-fine approach first generates the full sequence of semantic tokens, and then produces acoustic tokens conditioned on them.
[
2
]
. While effective, this approach is less suitable for streaming scenarios.
The temporal-plus-depth approach
[
24
,
25
,
5
]
employs a nested structure: a backbone Transformer models dependencies across frames, while a depth Transformer predicts multiple tokens within each frame. Since the backbone Transformer takes only aggregated frame-level information as input, the model cannot attend to all tokens within each frame, potentially limiting its overall performance.
The interleaved coarse-and-fine approach
[
23
]
aligns semantic and acoustic tokens along the temporal axis and interleaves them at each frame, enabling joint processing of both information within a single modeling. Our method follows this strategy. Spirit-LM
[
23
]
is an existing example that interleaves multiple tokens, though it relies on different tokenizers for obtaining semantic, pitch, and style tokens. Our work further leverages unified tokenization strategy as well as interleaved generation, allowing us to manage only a single model stack for the entire processing.

3
Llama-Mimi

Figure
1
provides an overview of our approach.
We build upon Mimi
[
5
]
, a neural audio codec that converts audio waveforms into residual vector quantizers
[
17
,
18
]
. Mimi follows the unified tokenizer approach of
[
26
]
, in which the first-level quantizers are semantically distilled from WavLM
[
27
]
.
Given a single-channel audio waveform
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, we first represent
\mathbf{x}
as a sequence of discrete tokens
\mathbf{h}
using Mimi:

\mathbf{h}=(y^{1}_{1},y^{2}_{1},\dots,y^{Q}_{1},y^{1}_{2},\dots,y^{Q}_{T^{\prime}}),

where
Q
is the number of quantizers,
T^{\prime}
(
T^{\prime}\ll T
) the number of frames, and
y^{q}_{t}
the token from the
q
-th quantizer at time
t
. Tokens within each frame are ordered from the first to the
Q
-th quantizer.
We model this sequence directly with a Transformer decoder.
We use Llama 3
[
28
]
as our backbone Transformer decoder.
By predicting semantic tokens first and conditioning subsequent acoustic tokens on them within each frame, the model is expected to generate linguistically coherent speech with fine-grained acoustic detail.
To enable Llama 3 to process audio tokens, we extend its vocabulary with audio tokens and special tokens
<audio>
and
</audio>
. Training then follows the standard next-token prediction objective
[
8
]
.
At inference, the model autoregressively generates tokens as in conventional LLMs. The generated tokens are sequentially decoded into audio frames by Mimi, and the process terminates once the
</audio>
token is produced. During sampling, tokens are selected from the combined text and audio vocabulary. While this doesn’t strictly enforce Mimi’s RVQ order (
1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow Q
), empirically the model naturally follows this sequence, enabling generation without token constraints.

4
Experimental Setup

Model.

We employ the 1.3B and 8B variants of Llama 3
[
28
]
as our backbone Transformer decoder architectures, hereafter referring to them as Llama-Mimi-1.3B and Llama-Mimi-8B.
To balance sequence length and reconstruction quality, we use
Q=4
codebooks at a frame rate of 12.5Hz, yielding 50 tokens per second.
Following
[
3
]
, we initialize our models with pretrained Llama 3 checkpoints while keeping Mimi’s parameters frozen.

Datasets.

We train our models on publicly available audio corpora, including Libri-Light
[
29
]
, The People’s Speech
[
30
]
, VoxPopuli
[
31
]
, and Emilia
[
32
]
.
Restricting to the English subsets, the combined training data amounts to approximately 240k hours of audio.
All samples are truncated to a maximum of 20 seconds.
We have confirmed that the truncated dataset preserves a broad duration distribution, spanning from 1 to 20 seconds, which enables the model to learn to generate audio of diverse lengths.

Hyperparameters.

We train our models with the AdamW optimizer
[
33
]
.
The global batch size is set to 1,024, and the maximum sequence length is set to 1,024 tokens.
The learning rate follows the Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule
[
34
]
over 100k steps: it warms up to
3\times 10^{-4}
in 1.5k steps, stays constant for 80% of training, and linearly decays to
3\times 10^{-5}
in the final 20%.
Our models are trained on 32 NVIDIA H200 GPUs with FSDP
[
35
]
, and training Llama-Mimi-1.3B takes approximately 48 hours.

]
and TWIST-1.3B
[
3
]
perform strongly on semantic tasks. Flow-SLM-1B-ext
[
6
]
, which is based on conditional flow matching (CFM), excels at acoustic tasks. Moshi
[
5
]
uses Mimi as the audio tokenizer and follows a temporal-plus-depth approach.
Since Moshi’s checkpoint after the audio-only pretraining stage has not been publicly released, we report its scores from
[
5
]
.

5.1
Likelihood-based evaluation

We evaluate both acoustic and semantic knowledge using likelihood-based benchmarks.
For acoustic tasks, we use SALMon
[
36
]
, which is divided into two categories: acoustic consistency and acoustic-semantic alignment. Acoustic consistency task evaluates whether the model assigns higher likelihood to a natural sample compared with one where an acoustic property (e.g., speaker identity or sentiment) changes unnaturally within an utterance. Acoustic-semantic alignment task evaluates whether the model assigns higher scores to samples where acoustic signals match semantic factors (e.g., sentiment, background sounds), compared with mismatched and thus unnatural ones.
For semantic tasks, we use sWUGGY
[
37
]
, sBLIMP
[
37
]
, and sTopic-StoryCloze
[
3
]
.
sWUGGY evaluates whether the model assigns higher probability to real words over pseudo-words (e.g., “oscillation” vs. “odenacia”), while sBLIMP evaluates whether the model prefers grammatically correct sentences (e.g., “The sweater isn’t folding” vs. “The sweaters isn’t folding”).
sTopic-StoryCloze measures continuation coherence given a spoken prompt.
Following
[
2
]
, which computes perplexity only over semantic tokens, we restrict the perplexity calculation in Llama-Mimi to semantic tokens for all tasks except SALMon. In our experiments, we found that excluding acoustic tokens leads to better performance on all benchmarks except SALMon.

Results.

Table
1
shows the results.
Our models achieve the best performance on the SALMon acoustic consistency task.
We attribute their strong acoustic capability to our modeling approach, which enables the model to attend to all past tokens and capture fine-grained representations of subtle acoustic variations.
For semantic tasks, our models perform slightly worse than the SSL-based TWIST model, likely because the longer sequence lengths hinder effective capture of global information.
We also find that model size has minimal effect on acoustic tasks but improves the performance on semantic tasks.

5.2
Generation-based evaluation

We evaluate our models in a speech generation setting, where a short audio prompt is given and the model produces a continuation. The generated audio is then assessed for speaker consistency and spoken content quality. We use the LibriSpeech test-clean set
[
29
]
(2,620 examples), truncating each sample to the first three seconds as the prompt and generating continuations of up to 20 seconds. The sampling parameters are set to temperature 0.8 and top-k 30.

Speaker consistency.

To measure whether the continuation preserves the speaker identity, we extract embeddings with pyannote’s WavLM-based model
[
38
]
and compute cosine similarity between the prompt and continuation.

Spoken content quality.

[
1
]
propose evaluating spoken content quality by computing perplexity on transcriptions of generated speech. However, perplexity is unstable, as it is highly sensitive to sequence length and sampling
[
3
]
. To address this, we adopt an LLM-as-a-Judge approach
[
13
]
. We first transcribe both the prompt and continuation with Whisper Turbo
[
39
]
, then ask GPT-4o (
gpt-4o-2024-11-20
)
[
10
]
to rate the continuation from 1 (worst) to 10 (best). The temperature is set to
0
to ensure deterministic scoring. The evaluation prompt is:

Given the following prompt and completion, rate the quality of the completion on a scale from 1 to 10, where 10 is the best possible completion. Consider relevance, coherence, fluency, and informativeness. Output only the score as an integer.

Prompt: {prefix}

Completion: {suffix}

For comparison with LLM-as-a-Judge based evaluation, we also report perplexity using GPT-2
[
8
]
on transcriptions.

Results.

We compare against TWIST-1.3B and GSLM. Flow-SLM-1B-ext and Moshi are excluded, as audio-only pretrained versions are not available.
Table
3
shows the results.
For speaker similarity, our models achieve higher scores than baselines, demonstrating stronger preservation of speaker identity.
For spoken content quality, as expected, perplexity is unreliable: shorter continuations are penalized, and even the ground-truth reference receives the worst score.
In contrast, the LLM-as-a-Judge scores are more consistent: larger models achieve higher scores, and the ground-truth reference obtains the best score, indicating that the evaluation is reasonable.
Under the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, Llama-Mimi-1.3B attains scores between GSLM-151M and TWIST-1.3B, consistent with the likelihood-based ranking.

Effect of quantizer number

We evaluate the effect of the quantizer number against audio quality, speaker similarity, and spoken content quality.
We evaluate audio quality using Audiobox-Aesthetics
[

As in Section
5.2

Q\in\{2,4,8\}
).

6
Conclusion

We introduced Llama-Mimi, a speech language model that uses a unified tokenizer and a single Transformer decoder. Our model achieved state-of-the-art performance in acoustic consistency and possesses the ability to preserve speaker identity.
We also revealed a trade-off between audio fidelity and long-term coherence. We also introduced an LLM-as-a-Judge-based spoken content quality evaluation framework that provides more reliable and realistic assessments than perplexity-based metrics.
