Analysis of Plant-Based and Herbal Therapies in Periodontal Treatment: Clinical Applications, Mechanistic Insights, and Efficacy
Authors: Hiroj Bagde, Mallapragada Siddharth, Ashwini Dhopte, Vikas Singh, Mohammad Khursheed Alam, Sheetal Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Periodontitis, a widespread chronic inflammatory disease, is primarily treated with scaling and root planing (SRP). However, plant-based adjunctive therapies, known for their anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant effects, are emerging as valuable complements to SRP. This review presents findings from 24 studies on agents such as Edelweiss stem cells, pomegranate, neem, chamomile, and curcumin, which showed enhanced clinical outcomes in probing pocket depth (PPD) and clinical attachment levels (CAL) when used with SRP. These therapies offer safe and biocompatible alternatives to synthetic agents, potentially reducing antibiotic dependence and addressing antimicrobial resistance. The localized application of these agents maximizes periodontal benefits with minimal systemic exposure.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease of the supporting tooth structures, remains a major global oral health concern, often leading to tooth loss if untreated. Scaling and root planing (SRP) has long been the gold standard for nonsurgical management, but its limited ability to address underlying inflammation has driven interest in adjunctive therapies.[1] Among these, plant-based agents, such as Edelweiss stem cells, pomegranate extracts, Azadirachta indica (neem), Matricaria chamomilla (chamomile), lemongrass, curcumin, and aloe vera, have gained attention for their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties.[23] These natural agents can enhance clinical parameters like probing depth reduction and clinical attachment gain by targeting key periodontal pathogens and modulating inflammatory responses, offering advantages over chemical agents such as chlorhexidine, which carry risks like taste alteration, staining, and disruption of the oral microbiome.[45]

Evidence from multiple studies suggests that plant-derived adjuncts may provide multifaceted benefits in periodontal therapy by improving microbial control, reducing inflammation, and supporting tissue repair.[35] For example, neem exhibits potent antibacterial effects, chamomile aids gingival healing, and lemongrass demonstrates activity against Porphyromonas gingivalis, while curcumin and aloe vera contribute to antioxidant defense and tissue regeneration.[24]

REVIEW

A review of 24 studies highlights recurring challenges in the use of plant-based adjuncts in periodontal therapy, including determining their comparative effectiveness against conventional treatments, understanding their microbiological and biochemical impacts, assessing long-term efficacy, and addressing safety concerns.[123] While agents, such as Edelweiss stem cells, pomegranate extracts, neem, chamomile, lemongrass, curcumin, and aloe vera, show promise in enhancing clinical attachment levels (CAL) and reducing probing pocket depth (PPD) when combined with scaling and root planing (SRP), variability in dosage, delivery methods, and treatment durations limits the establishment of standardized protocols. Additionally, limited long-term data and a lack of direct comparisons between different herbal agents or against synthetic drugs remain significant research gaps.[45]

Solutions identified in these studies indicate that plant-based therapies can effectively complement SRP through their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties.[26] Herbal agents, such as curcumin, aged garlic extract, and lycopene, have demonstrated the ability to modulate inflammatory pathways and reduce oxidative stress, while neem, chamomile, lemongrass, and propolis have shown potent antimicrobial activity against key periodontal pathogens without the adverse effects of synthetic agents. Localized drug delivery systems, including gels, chips, and postbiotic-based formulations, enhance treatment efficacy by concentrating active compounds within periodontal pockets, reducing systemic exposure, and improving patient compliance.[57]

Overall, these findings suggest that plant-based adjuncts are viable, safe, and patient-preferred alternatives to synthetic antimicrobials, with the potential to reduce antibiotic dependency and mitigate antimicrobial resistance.[38] [Table 1].

Incorporating plant-based therapies, such as curcumin, neem, chamomile, pomegranate, and green tea, into periodontal care offers notable patient-centric benefits, including reduced side effects, improved tolerability, and alignment with the growing preference for natural and holistic treatment options.[56] Compared to conventional agents like chlorhexidine, which may cause tooth staining, taste alterations, and mucosal irritation, these herbal treatments are generally biocompatible and well tolerated, supporting better adherence and long-term compliance in chronic periodontitis management. Advanced delivery systems—such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and hydrogels—further enhance the bioavailability and targeted release of herbal agents, enabling sustained therapeutic concentrations in periodontal pockets and improving outcomes such as probing pocket depth reduction and clinical attachment gain.[7]

Future research on plant-based therapies in periodontal treatment should address several key gaps, including conducting long-term studies to evaluate sustained benefits, implementing larger multicenter trials for broader population representation, and performing direct head-to-head comparisons between different herbal agents to identify the most effective options.[567] Mechanistic investigations are needed to clarify molecular pathways underlying their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial effects, alongside broader microbiome analyses to assess their influence on both pathogenic and beneficial oral bacteria.[69]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, addressing these research gaps through well-designed, long-term studies, larger sample sizes, comparative analyses, and mechanistic exploration is essential for establishing plant-based therapies as reliable adjuncts in periodontal care.
