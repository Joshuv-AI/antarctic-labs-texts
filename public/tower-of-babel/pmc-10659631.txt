Trends in gender disparity in the field of Helicobacter pylori research from 2000 to 2020: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Huiqin Shi, Shu Huang, Zhenju Tan, Xinyue Ma, Han Zhang, Wei Zhang, Lei Shi, Xiaolin Zhong, Muhan Lü, Xia Chen, Xiaowei Tang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Many studies have investigated gender disparity in scientific publications, but this has been poorly studied in the field of digestive diseases. This study aimed to determine the gender difference of first and senior authors in publications related to Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) during the past 20 years. Data were derived from original articles published in the main journals of digestive diseases (Journal of Hepatology, Gut, Gastroenterology, American Journal of Gastroenterology, Endoscopy, Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, Alimentary Pharmacology and Therapeutics, Digestive Endoscopy, Journal of Gastroenterology, Helicobacter, and Gastric Cancer) in 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020. These original articles were classified according to the gender and nationality of the first and senior (last listed) authors. Linear-by-linear association test was used to analyze the proportion of women authors over time. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to explain the factors impacting authorship difference of first and senior authors. A total of 561 original articles on H. pylori were collected for this study, accounting for 10.70% in 2000 to 7.60% in 2020 among all articles. In these original articles, the percentage of women first authors increased from 14.60% in 2000 to 45.0% in 2020 (P < .001). The percentage of women senior authors increased from 5.60% in 2000 to 18.80% in 2020 (P < .001). Women first authors were more likely to perform research with women senior authors (18.42%) than with men senior authors (10.23%, P < .001). The proportion of women first authors from Oceania were higher than that from North and South America (P = .004), whereas there was no statistical difference regarding women senior authors. In the past 2 decades, although the percentage of women authors among both first and senior authors in the field of H. pylori research has increased significantly, women are still a minority in original research.

1. Introduction

Over the last few decades, great progress in gender equality has been made in the medical field, including the increasing percentage of women medical students.[1] But this disparity has not disappeared completely. The field of gastroenterology, an important part of medicine, it is also faced with this problem. It has been reported that the proportion of women trainees entering gastroenterology increased from 37% in 2012 to 40% in 2020.[2] However, the data from the Association of American Medical Colleges showed that among all practicing gastroenterologists in 2017, only 17.6% were women.[3] Another previous study found that women researchers were underrepresented in the field of microbiology, especially women corresponding authors, who accounted for just 23.4% of submissions.[4]

In general, publication in high-quality medical journals is regarded as an important metric of academic productivity and has an effect on academic promotion.[5] However, women published fewer articles in high-quality medical journals than men.[6] As the intersectional field of gastroenterology and microbiology, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) related studies may also have gender differences among its researchers, but there were few reports in this area. Thus, we aimed to determine the gender difference of first and senior authors in publications related to H. pylori research during the past 20 years. We hypothesized that there were also significant gender differences in the field of H. pylori, and the number and quality of original articles published by women first and senior authors were lower than that of men first and senior authors.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and study population

This was a cross-sectional study on gender differences between first and senior authors in the field of H. pylori research from 2000 to 2020. The reporting of this study followed the Reporting guidelines for Enhanced Observational Epidemiological Studies (STROBE) statement. Given that this study was based on published original researches, it was not suitable for ethics approval. The primary study population of this study were first author and senior author of original research on H. pylori, because these 2 positions are considered the most important. The first author is the junior scholar, while the last author is usually considered the senior scholar of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: First, the types of articles included in this study were only original articles, which could better reflect the research quality and contribution to the field. Meta-analyses, case reports, systematic reviews and other types of articles were excluded. Second, we selected the top 11 journals in the field of digestive diseases. These journals must have been published for at least 20 years, and original researches on H. pylori must be published. Third, original articles that did not provide the names of the first and senior authors, as well as articles with only 1 author, were initially excluded. Finally, authors whose gender could not be determined, as well as transgender and intersex individuals, were also excluded.

2.2. Data resources and collection

We selected the main journals in the field of H. pylori research with the highest impact factors (IFs > 5) according to the Clarivate 2020 Journal Citation Reports,[7] including the Journal of Hepatology, Gut, Gastroenterology, American Journal of Gastroenterology, Endoscopy, Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, Alimentary Pharmacology and Therapeutics, Digestive Endoscopy, Journal of Gastroenterology, Helicobacter, and Gastric Cancer. Data collection was performed by 3 members of the research team.

Using “Helicobacter pylori” or “H. pylori” as keywords for article title or abstract, all original articles that met the above criteria published in 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and from January to December 2020 were collected using the Medline database, respectively. We recorded the following information: title of original articles, abstract, first and last names of the first and senior authors, country and affiliations of the first and senior authors, and journal’s IF.

2.3. Gender confirmation and data cleansing

The gender of the first and senior authors was identified based on the authors’ first name using the Genderize website (https://genderize.io/). A probability of 90% was used as the threshold for determining likely gender. Data with a probability ≥ 90% could be included directly. Meanwhile, in data with gender probability < 90%, the authorship was identified by visiting the institute’s website or using the Bing search engine and the Google search engine.[8–11] If the gender could not be determined using the above-mentioned methods, the particular author was excluded.

Then, we categorized the collected data. Among them, the countries of authors were divided into 4 categories based on continent: Europe, Asia, North and South America, and Oceania.[12] Paper IF was divided into 2 categories according to data features: IF < 10.00 and IF ≥ 10.00.

2.4. Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics V.26.0 (International business machines, Armonk, NY) was used for all analyses in this study, and all analyses were two-tailed, with the P value set at .05. All graphics were drawn by GraphPad Prism 8.

The percentages of women first or senior authors was calculated respectively on the basis of different categories. Linear-by-linear association test was used to analyze the trend of woman proportion over time. Chi-square analysis was performed to determine the gender relationship between first and senior authors as well as the link between gender and other single variables. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to explain the factors impacting authorship difference of first and senior authors. Among them, the exposure factors of regression analysis were meaningful single factor variables.

3. Results

3.1. Included articles

A total of 7398 original articles from 11 journals were collected, among which 561 (7.60%) were related to H. pylori. Each article contained 2 or more authors, and a total of 1122 authors were identified, including 561 first authors and 561 senior authors. Of these 561 publications, the gender of the first and senior authors could not be identified in 11 (2.0%) and 7 (1.2%) original articles, respectively. The affiliation of the senior author was unknown in 1 (0.2%) article. Finally, 550 (98.0%) original articles were included for the analysis of the first author and 554 (98.8%) for the analysis of the senior author (Fig. 1; Table 1).

3.2. Time trend

Overall, studies on H. pylori have declined in the past 20 years, especially from 2005 (9.5%) to 2010 (5.1%), but the proportion in 2020 has increased by 1.5% compared with that in 2015 (Fig. 2). In comparison to men first authors, women first authors published significantly fewer original researches (n = 153/550, 27.8%, P < .001) in the articles reviewed. Similarly, the percentage of articles published by women senior authors was only 12.5% (n = 69/554, P < .001), which was much lower than that of men (Table 2). There was a statistically significant difference in the percentage of women first and senior authors between years, and the proportion increased over time (P < .001). The percentage of women first and senior authors increased from 14.6% to 45.0% and from 5.6% to 18.8%, respectively, over the past 20 years (Fig. 3).

3.3. Impact factor

Most H. pylori articles published by both women and men authors focused on low IF (<10.00), with 78.5% (n = 432/550) for first authors and 78.5% (n = 435/554) for senior authors. In addition, the proportions of both women first (85.6%) and senior (88.4%) authors were slightly higher than those of men first (75.8%, P = .012) and senior (77.1%, P = .033) authors in the low-IF group, but the opposite was true in the high-IF group. The difference was statistically significant (Tables 3 and 4).

3.4. Authors’ region

Of all the H. pylori studies included, most authors were from Asia (n = 496), followed to a lesser extent by Europe (n = 341) and North and South America (n = 221),) and finally Oceania (n = 43). Among the different regions, the gender composition of the first authors was different. Specifically, the proportion of women first authors from Oceania was the highest (61.9%), whereas that in Asia was the lowest (24.5%, P = .004) (Table 5). Moreover, the percentage of women first authors has increased slowly over time, but there was no significant difference in the proportion of women senior authors in all continents (P = .10, Figs. 4 and 5).

3.5. Author association by gender

Regarding author association, 18.4% (n = 28/152) of women first authors were more likely to collaborate with women senior authors. By contrast, the percentage of men first author was 10.2% (n = 40/391) when the senior author was woman. Similarly, 89.9% of men first authors published with men senior authors (Table 6). The significant correlation between the first authors and senior authors indicated that authors of the same gender were more likely to publish original research together than with the opposite gender (P < .01; odds ratio [OR] = 1.98; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.173–3.348).

3.6. Multivariable analysis

For the first author, the exposure factors were year of publication, journal IFs, the nationality of the first author and the gender of the senior author, and the result was the gender of the first author. For senior authors, exposure factors were year of publication, journal IFs and the gender of the first author, with the result being gender of senior author. Firstly, the multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that the authors’ region might have an impact on the gender ratio of first authors rather than that of senior authors. To be specific, women from Oceania were more likely to publish as first authors in the field of H. pylori research, in contrast to first authors from North and South America (P = .022; OR = 3.194; CI, 1.178–8.662). However, there was no difference related to region in senior authors. Secondly, the percentage of women serving as first (P = .002; OR = 2.750; CI, 1.455–5.198) or senior authors (P = .002; OR = 3.814; CI, 1.638–8.883) has gradually increased since 2010. Thirdly, the IF of journals might have no connection with gender-based differences, whether first author or senior author. Moreover, the gender of the senior authors were not affected by the gender of the first authors (Tables 7 and 8).

4. Discussion

We observed that substantial gender difference exists, especially senior authors, in the field of H. pylori research. Although the proportion of women first (14.6% in 2000 to 45.0% in 2020) and senior authors (5.6% in 2000 to 18.8% in 2020) has risen significantly in the past 20 years, there were fewer than a fifth of women senior authors in 2020. Moreover, through our study, we found that women authors not only had lower publication productivity but also had more difficulty publishing original research in journals with high IF. These findings were consistent with our hypothesis. Furthermore, there was a meaningful finding in our study that women first authors were more likely to publish with women senior authors.

Notably, gender difference was not specific to the field of H. pylori research. Some research have shown that gender differences existed in many areas, including neurosurgery, emergency medicine, psychiatry, radiology, and so on.[13–16] Bernardi et al[13] reported that women’ participation had been improving in different areas of surgery, with the proportion of women first authors increasing from 21.4% (2000) to 35.0% (2017) and that of women senior authors increasing from 5.0% (2000) to 18.6% (2017). In an original article of emergency medicine, 30% of first authors and 22% senior authors were determined as women in 2018.[14] A study suggested that, in 2008, 43.5% of first authors and 30.0% of senior authors were women, and the proportions increased to 49.5% and 35.7%, respectively, in 2018.[15] A 35-year analysis in radiology indicated that 21.98% of first authors and 13.81% of senior authors were women.[16] These were consistent with the results of our study.

The most important reason that may influence the publications of women authors is gender discrimination. The findings of Budden et al indicated that studies submitted by men authors were considered to be of higher quality if the gender of the author was known in peer reviews. Double-blind reviews promoted the growth of women first-author papers.[17,18] Moreover, as the decision maker of the publication of the article, the editor’s idea can play an important role in the review of the article. A study of women authors in infectious diseases journals showed that the higher the percentage of women editors in journals, the higher the percentage of women first and last authors. However, women editors are still underrepresented.[19] Inherent sexism in academia caused women authors to spend longer time publishing in the first author position than men authors, which hindered the transition from first authorship to senior authorship in women, exacerbating the lack of senior women authors in the medical academic filed.[20]

Excessive family responsibilities may also cause women to leave the medical academic field. A number of reports have showed that it was difficult for women to get help from their partners in family life; thus, they take on more housework.[21] Jolly S et al found that women who had children would spend more time and energy raising the children than their men contemporaries, leading them to devote far less time to research.[22]

Furthermore, this study showed that women authors from Oceania were more likely to appear as first authors in original articles in the field of H. pylori research. However, this finding differs from that of the study of Farooq et al,[23] which showed that women first/senior authors were more likely to come from North America. The possible reason for the high proportion of women authors from Oceania is related to the fact that H. pylori was discovered in that region in 1980 by Robin Warren, a pathologist at the Royal Perth Hospital in Western Australia, for which he, along with his colleague, Barry J. Marshall, won the Noble Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005.[24] Furthermore, the number of women entering the field of gastroenterology at the beginning of the 20th century significantly increased.[25] Since then, the discovery, which is as a major step forward in the field of digestive diseases, might have inspired many women to participate in H. pylori research.

An interesting finding of our study was that women first authors were more likely to publish with women senior authors. One study published on Gastrointestinal Endoscopy supported this idea, in which women who attended academic conferences were better able to understand and perhaps be inspired by what the women speakers shared.[26] However, women were later to get into the field of gastroenterology, and the negative effects of sexism made it harder for women to be senior authors, leading to a lack of guidance from women senior authors for women first authors.[10,27] Therefore, it is necessary to alleviate gender imbalance in order to improve the representativeness of women senior authors.

There are some clear strengths in our study. Mainly, gender inequality in the field of H. pylori research was investigated for the first time. The findings provide strong evidence that women authors are in a weak position, and such findings are expected to improve publication of women authors in this field. Two recent studies have confirmed that women surgeons performed better surgery and had a lower incidence of adverse reactions after surgery than men surgeons.[28,29] So we can speculated that women gastroenterologists might do a better job than men, have more favorable patient outcomes. Therefore, our study is likely to encourage more women to engage in work related to H. pylori and help them get more academic opportunities.

Several limitations of this study are noteworthy. First, the data of our study are incomplete and small. We only selected the top 11 representative journals with the highest IF in the field of digestive diseases in 5 time nodes over the past 20 years (2000–2020). Second, our study is based on the analysis of authors’ name. Although we used the same method of determining authors’ gender used by other similar articles, the possibility of gender misjudgment cannot be eliminated.[8,9,11,30–32] Because of cultural differences, the names of Western authors are more suited to the Genderize tool for the confirmation of gender.[33] However, other ways, such as social media sites, Google Chrome, and institutional websites, were used to determine the gender of non-Western names in order to improve accuracy. Third, only the first and senior authors were included and analyzed in this study, because these positions were the key to promotion.[13] Because of the design of our study, we cannot further explore the impact of article type, education background, career planning, and other possible factors on the gender gap of the authors we analyzed.

More studies are needed to focus on the field of H. pylori in the future. By further expanding the sample size, and exploring the possible reasons of gender imbalance from more perspectives, we hope to find better solutions and effectively improve the status of women authors.

5. Conclusion

Currently, only few studies have investigated gender difference in the field of H. pylori research, and our study provides preliminary information on the subject. This study suggested that articles published by women first or senior author were increasing in the last 20 years. However, women authors are still underrepresented, especially women senior authors. Greater attention should be devoted to this area to clarify the causes of gender disparity and find solutions to close the woman-man gap. In general, there is still a long way to go to solve the dilemma of women authors in the field of digestive diseases. To improve the status of women researchers in the field of H. pylori research, the academic area should give more support and opportunities for women authors in the future.
