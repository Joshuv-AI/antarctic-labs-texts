Predicting the Dark Matter - Baryon Abundance Ratio Preprint: LCTP-24-20
Abhishek Banerjee ID, Dawid Brzeminski ID, Anson Hook ID

Abstract
We discuss relaxation solutions to the dark matter - baryon coincidence problem in the context of QCD axion dark matter. In relaxation solutions, a moduli dynamically adjusts the mass of dark matter and baryons until their energy densities are 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) the same. Because the QCD axion is heavily connected to QCD, scanning the QCD axion mass inherently also scans the proton mass. In the context of relaxation solutions, this implies that the ratio of dark matter to baryon abundances ( Ω DM / Ω B \Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B} ) is a ratio of beta functions showing that these models can only accommodate discrete values of Ω DM / Ω B \Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B} thereby “predicting” the ratio of the dark matter to baryon abundances. The original composite axion model has only a single integer degree of freedom N N , the size of the gauge group, and we show that when N = 8 N=8 the observed value of Ω DM / Ω B = 5.36 \Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}=5.36 is reproduced to within its percent level error bars. Novel tests of this model include more precise measurements of Ω DM / Ω B \Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B} , a better lattice determination of the dependence of the proton mass on the high energy QCD gauge coupling, as well as more traditional tests such as fifth force experiments.

Introduction

There is a remarkable cosmological coincidence between the relic cold dark matter (DM) energy density (
\Omega_{\rm DM}
) and the relic baryon energy density (
\Omega_{\rm B}
).
The current measured value of the ratio is

\displaystyle r_{\rm obs}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\rm DM}}{\Omega_{\rm B}}=5.36\pm 0.05\,,

(1.1)

where each of these quantities are measured at a sub-percent level
Aghanim et al. 2020
.
The QCD confinement scale
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
is generated by dimensional transmutation and is dictated by a theory parameter that at high energy (UV) happens to be
\sim 0.1
.
Dimensional transmutation makes the baryon mass, and thus
\Omega_{\rm B}
, exponentially sensitive to the
\mathcal{O}(1)
UV theory parameter and is the explanation for why the proton mass is eighteen orders of magnitude lighter than the Planck scale.
On the other hand, the DM mass and number density depends on its theory and production mechanism, each possessing its own strong sensitivity to their own theory parameters, which are largely unrelated to QCD.
All of these unrelated parameters conspiring to produce a result that is only a factor of
5.36
different makes this puzzle astonishing.
Several solutions have been proposed to address this coincidence, which mostly try to relate either the number densities or the masses of the baryons and the DM, leaving the other to be within an
\mathcal{O}(1)
factor of each other
Hodges 1993
;
Berezhiani et al. 1996
;
Nussinov 1985
;
Gelmini et al. 1987
;
Barr et al. 1990
;
Barr 1991
;
Kaplan 1992
;
Kaplan et al. 2009
;
Foot and Volkas 2003
;
An et al. 2010
;
Farina 2015
;
Garcia Garcia et al. 2015
;
Lonsdale and Volkas 2018
;
Ibe et al. 2019
;
Murgui and Zurek 2022
;
Bai and Schwaller 2014
;
Newstead and TerBeek 2014
;
Ritter and Volkas 2023
;
Rosa and Silva 2023
.
Recently a relaxation solution was proposed to address the coincidence problem
Brzeminski and Hook 2023
, where the energy densities of both the sectors are cosmologically scanned, and relaxed to its observed value.

To see how it works, let us consider a scenario where both the baryon mass (
m_{\rm B}
) and the DM mass (
m_{\rm DM}
) depend on the expectation value of a scalar field,
\phi
, as

\displaystyle m_{\rm B}

\displaystyle\to m_{\rm B}(\phi)=m_{\rm B}(0)\exp{\left(c_{B}\phi/f_{\phi}\right)}

(1.2)

\displaystyle m_{\rm DM}

\displaystyle\to m_{\rm DM}(\phi)=m_{\rm DM}(0)\exp{\left(c_{D}\phi/f_{\phi}\right)}\,,

where
c_{B}
and
c_{D}
are some coefficients that depend on how these couplings are generated, and
f_{\phi}
is the coupling. Through out the text, we denote
\phi
as the scanner field.
If the universe contains non-relativistic baryons and DM, then the finite density potential of
\phi
is

\displaystyle V(\phi)=m_{\rm B}(\phi)\,n_{\rm B}+m_{\rm DM}(\phi)\,n_{\rm DM}\,.

(1.3)

At the minima of the potential
\partial V(\phi)/\partial\phi=0
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\rho_{\rm DM}}{\rho_{\rm B}}\right|_{\phi_{\rm min}}\!\!\!\!\simeq-\frac{c_{B}}{c_{D}}\,.

(1.4)

Thus, the ratio of the relic densities is obtained by the dynamics of a field
\phi
which is set by the ratio of
c_{D}
and
c_{B}
. As long as
c_{D}
and
c_{B}
are naturally of order the same, then the baryon and DM energy densities are relaxed to similar values.

As the problem is solved by relaxation, the cosmology of the scanner field is important. We start with a situation where the QCD confinement scale is higher than
\gtrsim\,{\rm GeV}
.
Thus initially the baryon contribution to the
\phi
potential is dominant over that of DM, and dictates the dynamics of
\phi
.
At the very beginning, the scanner remains effectively frozen as
m_{\phi}^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(\phi)=c_{B}^{2}\rho_{\rm B}/f_{\phi}^{2}\ll H^{2}
, where
H
denotes the Hubble scale.
Eventually, its mass becomes of order Hubble and it starts to slowly roll down the potential.
During the rolling phase,
\phi
changes the mass of baryons as
m_{\rm B}\propto R^{-1}
, where
R(t)
is the scale factor.
Thus the baryon mass is scanned from an initial higher value to
\,{\rm GeV}
during the relaxation phase.
During the scanning, the DM mass also changes
m_{\rm DM}\propto R^{-c_{D}/c_{B}}
.
If
c_{B}
and
c_{D}
have opposite signs, the DM contribution eventually becomes relevant, forcing the scanner to oscillate around its minimum,
\phi_{\rm min}
.

There are several crucial requirements for the scanning mechanism to work.
First, the
\phi
dependence of
m_{\rm B}
and
m_{\rm DM}
,
c_{B}
and
c_{D}
, need to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
the same and of opposite sign for the scanner to balance the two energy densities against each other

1

In this construction, DM needs to be produced by the end of the relaxation.
Initially we start with large baryon contribution to the
\phi
potential which decreases as
\rho_{\rm B}\propto 1/R^{4}
during the rolling phase.
Thus, the altering behaviour of the masses is only needed close to the end of the relaxation when the DM contribution to
V(\phi)
is important.
.
Second, we also require the baryons (DM) to be non-relativistic at the beginning (end) of the relaxation.
Finally, we require that other contributions to the potential of
\phi
be suppressed during the relaxation process, as they generically would drive
\phi
to a different minimum.
The strongest constraint on this model will come from requiring that the Standard Model (SM) thermal corrections to the
\phi
potential be small.
We will discuss the dynamics of the scanner in more detail in Section
III.3
.

In this work, we carefully consider the requirement that
c_{B}\sim c_{D}
. This requirement suggests that dark matter and QCD are connected to each other. Along these lines, there is an extremely well motivated and natural dark matter candidate to consider, the QCD axion.
The QCD axion is a well motivated particle that was originally proposed to solve the strong CP problem
Peccei and Quinn 1977a
;
Peccei and Quinn 1977b
;
Weinberg 1978
;
Wilczek 1978
;
Kim 1979

Shifman et al. 1980

There are two important ways in which the QCD axion is connected to QCD.
The first is that the QCD axion mass obeys
m_{a}\propto\Lambda_{\rm QCD}^{3/2}/f_{a}
(c.f. (
3.33
)).
Thus any scanning of the QCD scale
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
also changes the QCD axion mass.
This particular connection has the wrong sign (
c_{D}=3c_{B}/2
are the same sign) as for the scanner field to find the minima, the
\phi
dependence of the DM and the baryons mass needs to have opposite behaviour.

As a result, we consider the second manner in which the QCD axion and QCD are connected. The minimal QCD axion only has a single free parameter,
f_{a}
, and
f_{a}
is connected to the QCD scale as its form,
aG\tilde{G}/f_{a}
, directly couples the axion to QCD. The QCD axion is the complex phase of a field and couples to
G\tilde{G}
. Its scalar counterpart, often called the saxion in supersymmetric models, controls
f_{a}
and at the same time couples to the CP-even counterpart of
G\tilde{G}
, namely
G^{2}
. Thus any change in
f_{a}
simultaneously changes
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
.

Let us make the previous considerations more concrete by considering an explicit model of the QCD axion. One of the most appealing models of the QCD axion are composite axions models.
In models of composite axions, the role of axion is played by the “pion” of a confining group
G
.
Similar to the pion decay constant for
SU(3)
, the axion decay constant is given by the confinement scale of
G

Kim 1985
;
Choi and Kim 1985
;
Kaplan 1985
.
A composite axion requires the confinement of fermions that are charged under both
SU(3)
and
G
.
Confinement of colored fermions removes the colored fermions from the spectrum and changes the beta function of QCD giving

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\Lambda_{\rm QCD}(\phi)\propto\left(f_{a}(\phi)\right)^{Q_{N}/\beta_{3}}\equiv\Lambda_{\rm QCD}(0)e^{c_{B}\phi/f}\,,

(1.5)

where
Q_{N}
is given by the representation of the colored fermions under
G
and
SU(3)
and
\beta_{3}
is the three light fermions approximation of the 1-loop QCD beta function at low energies. Amusingly enough, this second connection between the QCD axion and QCD is almost too predictive.

To see how this connection might be “too” predictive, let us be more quantitative.
If the
\phi
dependence of
f_{a}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{a}(\phi)=f_{a}(0)\exp{\left(c_{N}\phi/f_{\phi}\right)}\,,

(1.6)

then the ratio of the energy densities can be written as,

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\rm DM}}{\rho_{\rm B}}=-\frac{c_{B}}{3c_{B}/2-c_{N}}=\frac{2}{2r-3}\,,

(1.7)

where
r=c_{N}/c_{B}
and
c_{D}=3c_{B}/2-c_{N}
.
Thus the cosmological coincidence is solved for
r\approx 1.687\pm 0.003
. In the composite axion model discussed above,
r=\beta_{3}/Q_{N}
, which is fixed by the ratio of the
\beta
functions of both the gauge groups.
In the original composite axion model with
G=SU(N)
, a vector-like fermion which is in the fundamental representations of both the
SU(3)
and the
SU(N)
confines giving
r=27/2N
depending only on the integer
N
. The observed abundance has been measured at the percent level and must be reproduced with only this integer degree of freedom,
N
. This lack of freedom is what we mean when we say that the model is “too” predictive. Amazingly though, the choice of
N=8
exactly reproduces the observed abundance and in principle could be excluded just by a better measurement of
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}
. In contrast, just being a little off, e.g.
N=7
, gives
\frac{\Omega_{\rm DM}}{\Omega_{\rm B}}=2.33
, very much off from the observed value.

While it is amazing that we are able to reproduce the observed value of
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}
to within its percent level error bars, it is worth mentioning two important caveats. The first caveat is that the standard measurement of
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}
was made assuming standard cosmology. Many BSM scenarios allow for new effects such as
\Delta N_{\rm eff}\neq 0
, which would increase the error bars. As such, it is really more reasonable to take the error bars to be more on the level of 10% as opposed to 1%. Additionally, there is currently an
\mathcal{O}(10\%)
disagreement between early and late time measurements of Hubble (see Ref.
Kamionkowski and Riess 2023
for a review). As such, it is plausible that whatever resolves this Hubble tension, will also change
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}
by
\mathcal{O}(10\%)
.
The second caveat, is that when calculating the predicted value of
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{\rm B}
, we used 1-loop RG evolution and, more importantly, the 3 flavor approximation for the IR beta function of QCD. These approximations introduce uncertainties larger than 1% into our theoretical prediction.

We show that fulfilling the minimal requirements to address the coincidence problem leads to a highly predictive range of the QCD axion parameter space.
Our model has only two free parameters; the duration of the relaxation phase and the time when it ends.
As the final baryon mass is
\,{\rm GeV}
, the initial baryon mass determines the duration of the relaxation which we vary between
5\,{\rm GeV}
to
1.5\,{\rm TeV}
.
As discussed before, relaxation must be completed before the SM thermal corrections dominate the
\phi
potential.
The effective field theory requirement of
f_{a}\lesssim f_{\phi}
and this condition, limits the axion decay constant to
f_{a}\lesssim 3\times 10^{11}\,{\rm GeV}

However, astrophysical and cosmological observations impose a stronger model-dependent bound of

Raffelt 2008
;
Iwamoto 1984a
;
Buschmann et al. 2022
. Thus, the allowed parameter space is

\displaystyle 10^{8}\,{\rm GeV}\lesssim f_{a}\lesssim 3\times 10^{11}\,{\rm GeV}\,,

(1.8)

addressing the coincidence problem and accommodating a composite axion DM.

Cosmological Timeline

In this section we describe cosmological set up that we are considering.
The cosmological history is illustrated in Fig.
3
and consists of several major phases; the end of inflation, the creation of the baryon asymmetry, the relaxation phase, the creation of dark matter, the generation of the scanner potential, and reheating.

Very briefly, our cosmological history starts with the end of inflation where the inflaton decays into a light reheaton as well as having a small branching ratio into a dark “scanner” sector. The reheaton is the dominant energy density of the universe and eventually reheats the SM. The scanner sector will be responsible for the mass of
\phi
.

After the end of inflation, comes the formation of the baryon asymmetry and eventually dark matter. We will take Affleck-Dine baryogenesis and use the misalignment mechanism to produce dark matter.

Afterwards, relaxation occurs.
\rho_{\rm B}/\rho_{\rm DM}
is dynamically adjusted to its observed value.
The last step of relaxation is when the scanner sector confines and gives a potential for
\phi
. This potential fixes in place the value of
\rho_{\rm B}/\rho_{\rm DM}
.

Finally, the reheaton decays and the SM is reheated and standard cosmology occurs.
In what follows, we give a more detailed description of these epochs in chronological order.

III.1
End of Inflation

After the end of inflation, the inflaton mostly decays into reheaton fields
\Phi_{E}
whose self interactions are small enough that they do not thermalize, and whose mass is small enough that they are produced relativistically. The bath of
\Phi_{E}
particles drives the expansion of the Universe. Eventually while still relativistic,
\Phi_{E}
will decay into Standard Model particles and reheat the universe.

The requirement that something other than the Standard Model (SM) dominates the energy density of the universe comes from two considerations. Firstly, it is difficult to obtain non-relativistic protons when the SM is hot. Secondly, as discussed in more detail at the end of Sec.
III.4
, when the SM plasma is hot, the thermal loop induced
\phi
potential can easily be more dominant than the small non-relativistic baryonic contribution.
In that case, either the scanner does not successfully scan the energy densities, or the thermal contributions draw
\phi
to a minima where
\rho_{\rm B}/\rho_{\rm DM}\approx 0
; thus spoiling the relaxation.

To successfully obtain big bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) and a post BBN universe, we require
\Phi_{E}
to dump its entropy to the SM before few
\,{\rm MeV}
temperature.
We consider a
\Phi_{E}
interaction with the SM Higgs
|H|
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset\kappa_{E}|H|^{2}\Phi_{E}\,,

(3.1)

where
\kappa_{E}
is the interaction strength.
With a boost factor of
\gamma_{E}=10^{3}
at decay, and
\kappa_{E}\simeq 10^{-7}\,{\rm GeV}
, we obtain a decay width of

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Phi_{E}}\simeq\frac{\kappa_{E}^{2}}{8\pi M_{\Phi_{E}}\gamma_{E}}\simeq H(T_{\rm rh}\simeq 8.4\,{\rm MeV})\,,

(3.2)

for
M_{\Phi_{E}}=10\,{\rm TeV}
.
H(T)
is the Hubble scale at temperature
T
.
Thus
\Phi_{E}
decays to the SM, and the universe is reheated at
T_{\rm rh}\simeq 8.4\,{\rm MeV}
, where we have defined the reheat temperature to be the SM temperature at which
\rho_{\Phi_{E}}=\rho_{\rm SM}
.

III.2
Generating Baryon Asymmetry – Afleck Dine Mechanism

Sometime after the inflaton decays to the reheaton, baryogenesis happens.
We consider the production of the baryon asymmetry via the Afleck-Dine (AD) mechanism
Affleck and Dine 1985
.

To that end, we give the simplest AD model that we know of, where the asymmetry is generated due to the dynamics of a complex scalar field
\Phi
(AD field) carrying baryon number.
We show that a symmetry preserving decay, a small symmetry breaking mass term and a generic initial condition i.e.
{\rm Re}(\Phi),\,{\rm Im}(\Phi)\neq 0
is sufficient to have successful baryogenesis
Lloyd-Stubbs and McDonald 2021
;
Mohapatra and Okada 2021
.
The physical interpretation of this particular AD model is that the misalignment mechanism produces a coherent scalar field that initially has no asymmetry and afterwards oscillates between its particle and anti-particle states, transitioning back and forth from dominantly particles to dominantly anti-particle.
If the lifetime is long compared to the oscillation time scale, then its baryon number conserving decay occurs equally when it is a particle versus anti-particle and the produced asymmetry is small.
If the lifetime is fast compared to the oscillation time scale, then it decays before any excess is formed and again no asymmetry is produced.
The sweet spot for maximal baryogenesis is when the decay width is of order the oscillation time scale so that it has time to oscillate into dominantly particles and then decays before oscillating into anti-particles.

Consider a complex scalar

III.4

Before discussing the evolution of the QCD axion in our case, let us first outline the usual scenario.

IV.1
Temperature of the SM

In this section, we comment on the temperature of the SM sector.
At the end of inflation, the inflaton predominantly decays to
\Phi_{E}
.
Even if the SM is not populated by the inflaton decay, it does acquire a temperature due to the early decay of reheaton.
At the same time, at the end of baryogenesis, the AD field decays to the SM, and heats up the SM plasma.
The temperature of the SM can be obtained by solving the evolution equation as,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\gamma}+4H\rho_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Phi_{E}}\rho_{\Phi_{E}}+\Gamma_{\Phi}\rho_{\rm AD}\,,

(4.1)

where,
\rho_{\Phi_{E}}
is the energy density of the reheaton field, and
\rho_{\gamma}
denotes the energy density of the SM relativistic degrees of freedoms.
The general expression for
\rho_{\gamma}
can be written as,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{\gamma}(R)=\frac{1}{R^{4}}\int^{R}\frac{dR^{\prime}}{HR^{\prime}}\,R^{\prime 4}\left[\Gamma_{\Phi_{E}}\rho_{\Phi_{E}}+\Gamma_{\Phi}\rho_{\rm AD}\right]\,.

(4.2)

We solve the above equation in two regimes.
If the reheaton dominates in the above equation, then the SM temperature can be obtained as,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{\gamma}\simeq\frac{1}{3}\frac{\Gamma_{\Phi_{E}}\rho_{\Phi_{E}}}{H}\propto R^{-1}\,,\,T_{\gamma}\sim\rho_{\gamma}^{1/4}\propto R^{-1/4}\,,

(4.3)

where we use
\rho_{\Phi_{E}}\propto R^{-4}
, and
\Gamma_{\Phi_{E}}\propto R
.
The SM temperature is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!T_{\gamma}(t_{f})\sim 0.07\,{\rm GeV}\left(\frac{2.5\times 10^{-7}}{t_{f}/t_{\rm rh}}\right)^{1/8},

(4.4)

at the end of the relaxation, which goes upto

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!T_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})\sim 0.37\,{\rm GeV}\left(\frac{m_{\rm B}(t_{\rm in})}{\,{\rm TeV}}\frac{5\times 10^{-4}}{(t_{f}/t_{\rm rh})^{1/2}}\right)^{1/4}\!\!,

(4.5)

when the relaxation begins.
Thus, the baryons are non relativistic at the beginning of the relaxation if the SM gets temperature only from early decays.
At the end of the relaxation, pions are relativistic for

\displaystyle\frac{t_{f}}{t_{\rm rh}}\lesssim 4\times 10^{-10}\,,

(4.6)

and in that case the pion induced potential is much larger than
\rho_{\rm B}
.
Even the non relativistic pions can displace
\phi
from its special minima.
The pion contribution to
V(\phi)
can be written as,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!V_{\pi}(t_{f})=\left(\frac{m_{\pi}T_{\gamma}(t_{f})}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}T_{\gamma}(t_{f})\,e^{-m_{\pi}/T_{\gamma}(t_{f})}\,.

(4.7)

Requiring the pion contribution to be sub-dominant compare to that of the baryon, and dark matter, i.e.
V^{\prime}_{\pi}(t_{f})\lesssim(\rho_{\rm B})^{\prime}(t_{f})
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{t_{f}}{t_{\rm rh}}\lesssim 2\times 10^{-6}\,.

(4.8)

The above condition constrains the time when the relaxation needs to end irrespective of the initial baryon mass.

To calculate the SM temperature from the decay of the AD field, we note that,

\rho_{\rm AD}\propto R^{-3}
for
t_{\rm beg}\lesssim t\lesssim t_{\rm end}
. Also, the AD field decays when
H(t_{\rm end})=\Gamma_{\Phi}
.
Thus we get,

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{\Phi}}{R^{4}}\int^{R_{\rm end}}_{R_{\rm beg}}\frac{dR^{\prime}}{H}R^{\prime 3}\rho_{\rm AD}(R^{\prime})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm end})\left(\frac{R_{\rm end}}{R}\right)^{4}\,,

for,
t_{\rm beg}\ll t_{\rm end}
. So, the SM temperature obtained from the decay of the AD scales as
T_{\gamma}\propto R^{-1}
.
Thus, if baryogenesis ends sufficiently early i.e.
t_{\rm end}\ll t_{\rm in}
, the SM gets enough time cool down and the baryons become non relativistic when the relaxation starts.
For
\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm beg})=10^{30}\,{\rm GeV}^{4}
, we find the temperature of the SM at the end of baryogenesis as

\displaystyle T_{\rm SM}(t_{\rm end})\sim 13\,{\rm TeV}\,,

(4.10)

for
t_{\rm beg}/t_{\rm end}=10^{-8}
. We use
g_{*}(T)=106.7
in the above estimate.
We want to know the temperature of the SM at the beginning of the relaxation.
Using Eq. (
4.9
) we obtain

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})=\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm beg})\left(\frac{R_{\rm beg}}{R_{\rm end}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{R_{\rm end}}{R_{\rm in}}\right)^{4}\,,

(4.11)

and,

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})\simeq\left(2T_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})\right)^{4}\,,

(4.12)

where we use
g_{*}\sim 50
as the SM relativistic degrees
of freedoms after the QCD confinement.
Thus we obtain
T_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})\simeq\,{\rm GeV}
for
t_{\rm end}/t_{\rm in}=10^{-8}
.

For the relaxation to proceed as described in Section
III.3
, the baryons need to be non relativistic at the beginning of the relaxation.
Thus by requiring
T_{\gamma}(t_{\rm in})\lesssim m_{\rm B}(t_{\rm in})
, we get a constraint on the ratio of time between the end of baryogenesis and the beginning of relaxation as,

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\rm in}}{t_{\rm end}}\gtrsim 2.5\times 10^{2}\left(\frac{\,{\rm TeV}}{m_{\rm B}(t_{\rm in})}\right)^{2}\,,

(4.13)

for
\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm beg})=10^{30}\,{\rm GeV}^{4}
, and
t_{\rm beg}/t_{\rm end}=10^{-8}
.
Even when the baryons are non relativistic, the pions can still be relativistic, and can dominate the evolution of
\phi
at the beginning of the relaxation.
Requiring the pion contribution to the
\phi
potential is sub-dominant compare to that of the baryons at
t=t_{\rm in}
i.e.
m^{2}_{\pi}T_{\gamma}^{2}(t_{\rm in})/8\lesssim\rho_{\rm B}(t_{\rm in})
, using Eq. (
3.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{t_{\rm in}}{t_{\rm rh}}\lesssim 3.6\times 10^{-5}\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm beg})}{10^{30}\,{\rm GeV}^{4}}\right)\!\!\left(\frac{100\,{\rm TeV}}{m_{\Phi}}\right)^{3},

(4.14)

for
t_{\rm beg}/t_{\rm end}=10^{-8}
, which is a much weaker requirement than Eq. (
4.8
).
The above equation is conservative as the pions are relativistic only for

t_{\rm in}/t_{\rm rh}\lesssim 2\times 10^{-9}(\,{\rm GeV}/m_{\rm B}(t_{\rm in}))
.
If the pions are non-relativistic at the beginning of the relaxation, their contribution is exponentially small and thus can be safely neglected.
Thus the requirement of the pion contribution to the
\phi
potential needs to be subdominant compare to that of the baryons at the beginning of the relaxation does not lead to any new constraints.

We also require that the pions to be non relativistic at the end of relaxation i.e.
m_{\pi}(t_{f})\gtrsim T_{\gamma}(t_{f})
, and obtain a constraint as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{t_{f}}{t_{\rm rh}}\gtrsim 2\times 10^{-9}\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm AD}(t_{\rm beg})}{10^{30}\,{\rm GeV}^{4}}\right)^{1/2}\!\!\left(\frac{100\,{\rm TeV}}{m_{\Phi}}\right).

(4.15)

This is similar to the case discussed in Eq. (
4.6
); however the the SM temperature is dominated by the decay of the AD field here.
However, the above constraint is weaker as the relativistic pion contribution to the
\phi
potential is sub-dominant compare to that of the baryons at
t=t_{f}
as long as
t_{f}/t_{\rm rh}\lesssim 3.6\times 10^{-5}
.

V
Summary and conclusion

In this article, we explored the possibility that a new relaxation mechanism could explain the dark matter - baryon coincidence problem in the context of QCD axion dark matter.
The relaxation mechanism solving this problem requires a coupling to both dark matter and QCD.
The QCD axion is a dark matter candidate that is intrinsically tied to QCD. As such, it is natural that anything that modifies the QCD axion also modifies QCD.
In fact, this connection is so strong that it is a bit surprising that the model has enough flexibility to accommodate current data.

By scanning the axion decay constant,
f_{a}
, the axion and baryon masses are connected with only integer degrees of freedom, a ratio of beta functions. The integer degree of freedom is required to reproduce the observed baryon to dark matter ratio to within 1-10% depending on if the new physics modifies the cosmological fit.
The fact that there is a solution is amazing. Amusingly this entire approach can be excluded in several very non-standard ways.
A precision measurement of
\rho_{\rm B}/\rho_{\rm DM}
and a better understanding of how the proton mass depends on the UV QCD gauge coupling are just two of the odd ways in which such a theory could be tested.

Finally, there is still much to think about and improve on this approach.
Firstly, the cosmology we consider is complicated and it would be interesting if there were a simpler cosmology that works.
Secondly, an important assumption, present for all theories where finite density/temperature effects are important, is that the vacuum potential for
\phi
has been highly suppressed.
It would be interesting if there was a convincing solution to this puzzle.

Appendix A
Composite QCD axion

In this section, we briefly introduce the composite axion models
Kim 1985
;
Choi and Kim 1985
;
Kaplan 1985
.
In these models, the axion is not a fundamental particle, rather the composite pion-like object of some gauge group
G
.
Because the axion is a composite, its decay constant is given by the confinement scale of the gauge group
G
, similar to the case of the pion decay constant being related to
\Lambda_{\rm QCD}

Witten 1980
.
As the axion decay constant is generated by the physics of dimensional transmutation, the composite axions are more protected against the quality operators compare to that of the DFSZ and/or KSVZ models
Randall 1992
;
Dobrescu 1997
.
To highlight our purpose, in the following we provide one example of composite axions.
For simplicity, we take
G=SU(N)
.

Following
Kim 1985
, we consider massless vector-like fermions
\psi
and
\xi
that transform under
SU(N)
and
SU(3)
respectively as,

\displaystyle\psi=(N,3)\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\,\xi=(N,1)\,.

(A.1)

We assume that
SU(N)
confines at a scale
\Lambda_{N}\gg\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
.
As long as
SU(N)
confines with chiral symmetry breaking, it will break the flavor symmetries down to the diagonal via
\left<\bar{\psi}_{L,i}\psi_{R,j}\right>=\left<\bar{\xi}_{L,i}\xi_{R,j}\right>=\Lambda_{N}\,\delta_{ij}
.
In the limit of QCD coupling goes to zero (
g_{s}\to 0
), the global symmetry is
SU(4)_{L}\times SU(4)_{R}\times U(1)_{V}
, which is spontaneously broken down to
SU(4)_{L+R}\times U(1)_{V}
by the fermion condensates at the scale
\Lambda_{N}
. However, a small but non zero
g_{s}
, forces the condensate to preserve color, and the 15 pseudo-Nambu Goldstone Bosons transform as
1\oplus 3\oplus\bar{3}\oplus 8
representations of
SU(3)_{C}
.
The color singlet combination of

\displaystyle a\sim\left(\bar{\psi}\gamma^{5}\psi-3\bar{\xi}\gamma^{5}\xi\right)\,,

(A.2)

is identified with the axion.

When QCD is turned on (i.e.
g_{s}\neq 0
), the
SU(4)_{L+R}
is broken explicitly to the gauged
SU(3)_{C}
and a global
U(1)
. This gauging gives a mass
\sim g_{s}\Lambda_{N}/4\pi
to all of the non-axion massless particles.
Meanwhile the axion gets a tiny mass from QCD instantons,
since the associated current

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{A}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{6}}\left(\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi-3\bar{\xi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\xi\right)\,,

(A.3)

is anomalous under QCD as

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{A}=\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\frac{N}{2\sqrt{6}}G\widetilde{G}\,.

(A.4)

The axion interaction with the SM can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\frac{a}{f_{a}}G\widetilde{G}\,,

(A.5)

with axion decay constant
f_{a}=\sqrt{6}\Lambda_{N}/N\,
.
Note that the simplest composite axion model considered here have domain walls, which can be circumvented by additional model building (see e.g.
Kim 1985
for a discussion).
In our case, we are considering axion DM production from the misalignment mechanism with a reheating temperature of
\sim\mathcal{O}(10\,{\rm MeV})
.
Therefore our conclusion does not require any additional model building regarding this.
