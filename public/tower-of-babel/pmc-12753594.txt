Survival outcomes and prognostic predictors after adrenalectomy for renal cell carcinoma adrenal metastasis
Authors: Wencong Han, Kun Peng, Zejin Ou, Shiwei Chen, Tai Kang, Xiaoteng Yu, Zheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate survival outcomes after adrenalectomy for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) adrenal metastasis and to identify risk factors influencing overall survival.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed all histologically confirmed cases of RCC adrenal metastasis managed at a tertiary center from January 2013 to December 2024. Overall survival (OS) was estimated with Kaplan–Meier analysis and compared with log-rank tests. Prognostic factors were examined using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 82 patients with RCC adrenal metastasis were included (median age 60.5 years; IQR 54.8–67.0). Metachronous disease was present in 52 patients (63.4%), and 8 (9.8%) had bilateral adrenal involvement. Seventy-one patients (86.6%) underwent adrenalectomy; seven with bilateral primary renal tumors were excluded owing to indeterminate timing and/or laterality, leaving 64 for survival analysis. After a median follow-up of 32.5 months (IQR 14.8–80.8), the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 96.6% (95% CI 92.2–100.0%), 87.1% (95% CI 77.8–97.6%), and 74.4% (95% CI 61.4–90.2%), respectively. Log-rank analysis showed poorer OS for synchronous presentation (P = 0.02), RCC T3/4 stage (P < 0.001), and primary tumor diameter > 6 cm (P = 0.001). In multivariable Cox analysis, increasing primary tumor diameter was the only independent predictor of reduced OS (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.02–1.75; P = 0.04).ConclusionsAdrenalectomy provides durable survival for well-selected patients with isolated or oligometastatic RCC adrenal metastasis. Prospective multicenter trials comparing surgery with non-surgical modalities are needed to refine management algorithms.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, Adrenal metastasis, Adrenalectomy, Overall survival

Introduction

Adrenal glands are common sites for metastasis from various malignancies, with a prevalence ranging from 0.7% to 7.5% of all adrenal tumors [3, 6, 11]. The most frequent primary tumors associated with adrenal metastasis include lung carcinoma, breast cancer, melanoma, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and gastrointestinal malignancies [20, 21]. Pain or symptoms of adrenal insufficiency typically occur only in cases of significant mass effect or destruction of the adrenal cortex [20, 26]. The increasing use of advanced imaging techniques and the development of radiomics and artificial intelligence-based imaging analysis have improved the sensitivity and accuracy of diagnosing adrenal metastasis [19, 29].

The kidney, located beneath the adrenal gland, is a common source of tumors that may directly invade or metastasize to the adrenal gland [5]. Adrenal metastasis is observed in approximately 6%-10% of all metastatic cases of RCC [5]. Concurrent ipsilateral adrenalectomy is no longer routinely performed during radical nephrectomy, and is only carried out when there is radiographic or intraoperative suspicion [1, 13]. While some studies suggest that adrenalectomy may offer survival benefits in patients with adrenal metastasis, the lack of high-quality evidence makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions [8, 21, 27]. The 2022 American Association of Endocrine Surgeons Guidelines for Adrenalectomy raise the question of whether adrenal resection improves survival outcomes compared to systemic therapy alone in the management of adrenal metastasis [30].

Previous studies have involved heterogeneous patient populations encompassing various primary malignancies, limiting their generalizability to specific tumor types [8, 21, 27]. In this study, we focused exclusively on adrenal metastasis from RCC and conducted a retrospective investigation of patients diagnosed with RCC adrenal metastasis at a single tertiary institution. We described the basic characteristics of RCC adrenal metastasis and reported the oncological outcomes of adrenalectomy in cases of isolated or oligometastatic disease.

Methods and patients

This retrospective cohort study reviewed all consecutive patients who underwent adrenalectomy or image-guided adrenal biopsy at our institution between January 2013 and December 2024 and were subsequently confirmed to have metastatic involvement of the adrenal gland on histopathology. Patients were excluded when the adrenal lesion arose from direct extension of the primary tumor or was diagnosed as lymphoma or sarcoma. Demographic data, oncologic feature of the primary and metastasis disease, and operative details were extracted from the electronic medical record.

Definitions

Metastasis identified within six months of nephrectomy, or resected during the RCC procedure, were defined as synchronous, whereas those detected more than six months after nephrectomy were classified as metachronous. Disease was classified as isolated (a single adrenal metastasis) or oligometastatic (other synchronous or prior metastasis, with ≤ 5 total metastatic sites). The surgical interval (SI) was calculated as the time from nephrectomy to adrenalectomy; for staged bilateral resections, SI referred to the interval between nephrectomy and the first adrenalectomy, while overall survival (OS) was measured from completion of the final adrenal resection. Tumor size was recorded as the greatest pathological diameter; for bilateral or multifocal disease, summed diameters were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R 4.4.2 software, and all data were visualized using the "ggplot2" R package. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as counts with corresponding percentages. Survival curves were generated with the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Factors associated with OS were examined with univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models implemented in the "survival" R package. The final multivariable model included two covariates chosen a priori for their clinical relevance and univariable p-values < 0.10. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 135 patients with histologically proven adrenal metastasis, RCC was the most common primary tumor (82 patients, 60.7%), followed by lung cancer (36, 26.7%), hepatobiliary cancers (4, 3.0%), urothelial carcinoma (4, 3.0%), gastrointestinal primaries (3, 2.2%), and single cases (0.7% each) of breast, endometrial, ovarian, prostate, thymic, and thyroid cancers.

The present analysis focuses on the 82 patients with RCC adrenal metastasis (Table 1). The median age at diagnosis was 60.5 years (IQR 54.8–67.0), and only three patients (3.7%) were symptomatic. Adrenal involvement was synchronous in 26 patients (31.7%), including 19 (23.2%) who underwent concomitant adrenalectomy during nephrectomy. The remaining 52 patients (63.4%) presented with metachronous disease after a median SI of 61.8 months (IQR 30.3–93.6). Bilateral metastasis occurred in eight patients (9.8%); the other 74 (90.2%) had unilateral disease, with contralateral adrenal metastasis in 30 cases (36.6%). Clear cell histology predominated (97.6%), with papillary RCC identified in the other two patients (2.4%).

Follow-up and survival

Three patients (3.7%) received CT-guided radiofrequency or thermal ablation, four (4.9%) began systemic therapy after biopsy alone, and four (4.9%) underwent cytoreductive surgery (Table 2). Seventy-one (86.6%) patients proceeded to adrenal metastasectomy. Seven with bilateral primary RCC were excluded from survival analyses because the timing and/or laterality of metastasis could not be classified, leaving 64 evaluable patients (Fig. 1).

After a median follow-up of 32.5 months (IQR 14.8–80.8), the median OS had not been reached. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 96.6% (95% CI 92.2–100.0%), 87.1% (95% CI 77.8–97.6%), and 74.4% (95% CI 61.4–90.2%), respectively. During follow-up, 12 of 64 patients (18.8%) died, all from progressive RCC; no non-cancer-related deaths occurred. Accordingly, OS equaled cancer-specific survival in this cohort. Twenty-one patients (32.8%) achieved a no evidence of disease state lasting a median of 29.1 months (IQR 14.0–57.5).

Kaplan–Meier curves (Fig. 2) demonstrated significantly longer OS for metachronous versus synchronous adrenal metastasis (P = 0.02). Survival also declined with increasing primary tumor stage (T > 2 vs ≤ 2; P < 0.01) and with a primary RCC diameter > 6 cm (P < 0.01). Age, sex, metastatic burden (isolated vs oligometastatic), surgical approach (open vs laparoscopic), type of nephrectomy (radical vs partial), use of adjuvant therapy after renal surgery, and contralateral adrenal disease did not influence OS. On univariable Cox analysis, synchronous presentation and larger RCC size were associated with poorer OS, whereas in multivariable modelling only RCC diameter retained independent prognostic significance (hazard ratio 1.33, 95% CI 1.02–1.75; P = 0.04) (Table 3).

Discussion

Adrenal metastases constitute the most frequent cause of malignant adrenal tumors [6, 11]. A recent population-based analysis from the United States showed that they represented 7.5% of all adrenal lesions, compared with 1.1% for pheochromocytoma and 0.3% for adrenocortical carcinoma[6]. In a Mayo Clinic series spanning more than two decades and comprising 579 patients with adrenal metastasis, lung primaries were the most frequent (39%), followed by genitourinary (28%) and gastrointestinal (14%) tumors; notably, adrenalectomy was associated with a lower mortality rate within that series[20]. Several investigators have further noted that patients with RCC survive longer than those harboring other primaries[7],Goujon, et al., 2020; [9, 12].

Our data reinforce this observation. Among selected patients with isolated or oligometastatic RCC, adrenalectomy delivered durable benefit, with actuarial 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 96.6%, 87.1%, 74.4%, respectively. These outcomes exceed the pooled estimates reported by Kong et al. (83.0%, 66.0%, and 58.8% at the same time points) and compare favorably with the 84 months median OS in a European multicenter cohort of 37 patients who underwent adrenalectomy for RCC adrenal metastasis[12, 21].

Consistent with prior studies, our analysis reveals that resection of metachronous adrenal metastasis is a significant prognostic factor and is associated with more favorable survival [8, 10, 21, 24]). Howell et al. reported that synchronous metastasis and a disease-free interval (DFI) < 12 months predicted worse outcomes[10]. A French multicenter cohort study involving 106 patients, 47.2% of whom had RCC histology, confirmed these observations, as multivariable Cox regression showed that the metastasis timing strongly affected recurrence-free survival (HR 18.60, p = 0.002), whereas a longer DFI independently improved CSS (HR 0.01, p = 0.009) [8].

Previous studies have also identified adrenal metastasis diameter as an adverse prognostic factor [8, 28, 32] Distinctly, our data underscore the prognostic value of relevance of primary tumor characteristics: in univariable analyses, advanced T stage and larger tumor diameter were associated with poorer survival. In the multivariable Cox model, however, primary RCC diameter remained the sole independent predictor of OS. Each additional centimeter in primary RCC size translated into a 33% increase in mortality risk, plausibly reflecting higher tumor burden and more aggressive biology. In line with prior evidence, smaller primaries display less intratumoral heterogeneity and genomic instability, whereas increasing tumor diameter is associated with worse outcomes [4, 16]. The difference from earlier reports may also stem from selection bias, as our cohort comprised only patients with RCC.

In our cohort, age, nephrectomy type (partial vs. radical), and WHO/ISUP grade were not associated with overall survival; however, given the limited number of events, modest effects cannot be excluded. Contemporary evidence supports the safety of partial nephrectomy in older patients with favorable oncologic and functional outcomes, whereas radical nephrectomy has been associated with a higher risk of major cardiovascular events, which may adversely influence overall survival [14, 15]. In parallel, accumulating data indicate that the percentage abundance of sarcomatoid differentiation carries independent prognostic information in clear cell RCC; in particular, a sarcomatoid component ≥ 20% has been linked to significantly worse cancer-specific and recurrence-free survival [17, 18]). In our series, 73.4% patients had WHO/ISUP grade ≤ 2, and sarcomatoid percentage was not uniformly quantified, precluding a robust assessment; nevertheless, this histologic feature likely contributes to outcome heterogeneity and warrants routine documentation where feasible.

Our study also found no association between surgical approach and survival. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies (2 279 patients, 26% with RCC primaries) that compared minimally invasive with open adrenalectomy for secondary adrenal tumors and found equivalent oncological outcomes [12]. Laparoscopic adrenalectomy, however, provided superior perioperative results, including less blood loss, fewer complications, faster recovery, and shorter hospital stay [22, 25]. Therefore, when tumor size, local invasiveness, and patient performance status permit, a minimally invasive approach should be preferred[23].

From a therapeutic standpoint, our findings support adrenalectomy as a valuable component of multidisciplinary care for isolated or oligometastatic RCC. Systemic therapy, radiation, and image-guided ablation have also been used for adrenal metastasis with encouraging results. In a meta-analysis of 1 006 patients (6.4% with renal primaries) treated with stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), 1-year local control reached 82%, with overall response of 54.6% and pooled 1- and 2-year OS rates of 66% and 42%, respectively [2]. Another systematic review of 538 patients of adrenal metastasis managed with percutaneous ablation (radiofrequency, cryoablation, or microwave) reported pooled 1- and 3-year OS rates of 80% and 46%, although no RCC specific subgroup analysis was available [33]. Evolving systemic therapy paradigms—cytokines, targeted agents, and immune-checkpoint inhibitors—over the 12-year study period may confound comparisons with historical cohorts [31]. Moreover, the small number of patients receiving non-surgical modalities in our series precluded a direct survival comparison with surgery. Prospective, multicenter trials comparing adrenalectomy with systemic therapy alone, or evaluating combined modality strategies, are therefore warranted.

The main strength of our study is its relatively large, histologically confirmed sample focused exclusively on RCC adrenal metastasis. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as patients undergoing adrenalectomy likely had better performance status and fewer comorbidities. Second, the limited number of outcome events resulted in a low events‑per‑variable ratio, such as tumor stage variables significant on log-rank testing, were excluded from the Cox models due to wide confidence intervals. Overall, effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. Third, disease-free survival could not be reliably ascertained, so OS was the sole endpoint. Finally, as mentioned earlier, the absence of a non-surgical control group prevents definitive conclusions about the relative benefit of adrenal metastasectomy.

Conclusion

Adrenalectomy provides durable survival for patients with isolated or oligometastatic RCC adrenal metastasis. Increasing primary RCC size may be associated with poorer outcomes. Given the retrospective design and potential selection bias, prospective multicenter trials comparing surgical and non-surgical strategies are required to establish optimal management of adrenal metastasis.
