Oblique Pyramid Microstructure-Patterned Flexible Sensors for Pressure and Visual Temperature Sensing
Authors: Shujuan Li, Xiyue Cui, Yuanyuan Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlexible tactile sensors have garnered considerable attention in diverse fields. Among them, the sensors integrated with multifunctional tactile sensing features can simultaneously detect various stimuli, such as pressure and temperature, and are thus suitable for practical applications. However, integrating multiple sensor modalities within a solitary pixel invariably encounters various limitations encompassing interplay among disparate sensors, intricate structural design demands, and the complexities and high costs associated with fabrication. Herein, we harness a visual sensing mechanism to synergize with electric sensors, thereby realizing a tactile sensor reliant on thermochromic microstructures for simultaneous pressure and temperature sensing. The thermal distribution could be easily displayed by the color change of the sensor, avoiding inference between the sensing units, which is beneficial for low-cost mass fabrication. A capacitor sensor with dual-scale oblique pyramid microstructures in its dielectric layer is used for the pressure sensing function, resulting in improved sensitivity and an extended measurement range. This innovative tactile sensor design offers insights into tactile sensing mechanisms, paving the way for cost-effective, high-performance, and multimodal sensor fabrication.Keywords: tactile sensor, integrated sensing, visualized sensing, oblique pyramid microstructure, thermochromic mechanism

Introduction

Tactile sensors have recently been extensively studied for potential application in various fields, e.g., health monitoring, smart skins for robots, and prosthetics.1−5 Multifunctional tactile sensing that mimics human skin and simultaneously detects multiple stimuli, i.e., pressure and temperature, is highly desired for practical applications.6−11 Pioneering works have been conducted to develop multifunctional tactile sensors through the integration of multiple sensors into one pixel layer-by-layer. The resulting combined sensor can offer a range of multifunctional sensing capabilities because each sensing layer is sensitive to distinct physical stimuli.10,11 However, such a design is generally confronted with limitations, such as interplay between different sensors, the requirement of a sophisticated structural design, and a complicated and high-cost fabrication process. To circumvent these challenges, alternative approaches have been proposed, primarily through the use of multisensitive materials. The electrical properties of sensors developed using such alternative approaches exhibit concurrent variations in response to alterations in pressure and temperature.7,8 Nonetheless, the sensing properties interact with each other, leading to difficulties in decoupling for independent sensing.

Visual sensing, which presents distinguishable evolution alongside variation of detected signals,12−14 provides an innovative solution to avoid the interference between different sensing modules. Inspired by animals, chameleons that change their color for communication, temperature sensing, and display could be implemented by color change instead of an electric signal. Various visualizing mechanisms have been proposed to reflect the local changes caused by physical stimuli by presenting interactive displays, i.e., color change and luminescence.15,16 The structure of the dual-mode sensor could be further simplified for noninterference multisensing and low-cost mass fabrication by incorporating such a mechanism with an electric pressure sensing module.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to implement pressure sensing, including capacitive, piezoresistive, and triboelectric sensing mechanisms.17−19 Among them, capacitive sensors are designed using a simple governing equation and require low power consumption, making them versatile in various applications. The exploration of microstructures has become a prominent direction in contemporary flexible tactile sensor research to enhance sensor sensitivity and resolution. These microstructures, often exemplified by pyramid geometries,17,20,21 ameliorate elastomer compressibility through their 3D configuration. The endeavor to augment the sensitivity remains an ongoing pursuit, spurring researchers to delve deeper into the design of these microstructures. In addition, the optimization is commonly limited to a reduced pressure range; thus, the integration of a high measurement range and sensitivity remains challenging.

In this study, we propose a tactile sensor based on thermochromic oblique pyramid microstructures for integrated pressure- and temperature-sensing applications. The microstructures are configured on bimodal scales to expand the measurement scope. Leveraging the oblique pyramid design, the sensor’s sensitivity is significantly increased beyond that of the conventional pyramid microstructures. To incorporate temperature-sensing capabilities, a visual sensing mechanism is introduced using thermochromic materials. This strategic approach mitigates interference between the pressure- and temperature-sensing components. Such a tactile sensor design provides new insights into the tactile sensing mechanism, paving a practical avenue for the fabrication of a high-performance multimodal sensor.

Results and Discussion

The pressure/temperature sensor is fabricated using a straightforward process, as depicted in Figures 1a and S1. This sensor configuration comprises two indium–tin oxide (ITO) film electrodes and a thermochromic dielectric layer sandwiched between them. The capacitive sensing mechanism is utilized for pressure sensing, and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is selected as the base of the dielectric layer owing to its excellent elastic properties and high flexibility. Figure 1b,c shows that the dielectric layer is engineered to obtain a dual-scale oblique pyramid microstructure (height: 600 and 800 μm) using a 3D-printed mold to enhance the sensing performance. Although the 3D printing process used for the mold has limited precision and produces an uneven surface, the PDMS microstructure successfully replicates the intended oblique pyramid structure. Moreover, to achieve a visually discernible temperature-sensing feature, PDMS is combined with thermochromic particles during the dielectric layer construction. Consequently, the fabricated sensor exhibits temperature fluctuations as observable color shifts (Figure 1d). The ITO electrodes and sandwiched dielectric layer are packaged to form a complete tactile sensor for integrated pressure and temperature sensing.

(c) To enhance the sensing performance, the dielectric layer is designed with a dual-scale oblique pyramid microstructure. The sensor’s visual temperature-sensing capability is derived from the thermochromic mechanism facilitated by the incorporation of thermochromic particles within the dielectric layer. These thermochromic particles, enclosed within capsules, encompass a composite composition that includes a weak acid (benzotriazole), crystal violet lactone, and a quaternary ammonium salt of a fatty acid dissolved in 1-dodecanol as a solvent. The underlying principal hinges on a reversible chemical reaction that responds to changes in temperature, eliciting a notable shift in color as depicted in Figure 2a. The composite mixture retains its solidity at low temperatures, facilitating the formation of a color complex through the reaction between the weak acid and the dye. This interaction ensures the opening of the lactone ring at the dye molecule core. However, as the temperature increases, the solvent enclosed within the thermochromic microcapsules gradually liquefies, prompting the dissociation of the ammonium salt to engage with the weak acid. Consequently, an increase in pH within the encapsulated capsule prompts the closure of the dye’s lactone ring, thereby inducing a discernible alteration in color. This dynamic interplay results in colorless capsules at elevated temperatures, allowing the hue of the external dye to predominate. Conversely, at lower temperatures, the observable color reflects a composite effect originating from the external and encapsulated dyes.

Visual temperature sensing of the sensor. (a) The visualized temperature sensing ability of the sensor is based on the thermochromic mechanism of the embedded thermochromic capsules. When the temperature increases, the solvent inside the capsule starts to melt, and the ammonium salt dissociates and reacts with the weak acid, leading to the color change of the encapsulated dye. (b) The thermochromic dielectric layer can quickly change its color in the area where temperature increases.

The thermochromic functionality inherent in the dielectric layer is visually depicted in Figure 2b, which shows a discernible response of the thermochromic dielectric layer to fluctuations in temperature. These fluctuations appear as distinct alterations in coloration within areas experiencing an elevation in temperature. This dynamic process is demonstrated by the observed transition from red to green in the contact region upon finger interaction, whereas the surrounding noncontact regions retain their original hue. This distinctive attribute facilitates temperature visualization based on the spatially distributed color manifestations across the dielectric layer. For characterization, the thermochromic film is fabricated with different thickness values, i.e., 400, 600, and 800 μm. Figure 3a shows the temperature–color correlation of the sensor’s response in the temperature range of 25–65 °C. As temperatures ascend, a transformative shift unfolds, traversing from a subdued reddish tone to a vibrant grassy green, culminating in a steady state of cerulean green. With an increment in dielectric layer thickness, the color saturation experiences a modest augmentation while maintaining a consistent variation trend. The response time for color change, typically ranging from 0.5 to 1.5 s, varies based on both the thickness of the dielectric layer and the heat applied. Because the heat transfer rate increases with a small volume of thermochromic PDMS and a high-temperature gradient, the response time becomes shorter. A comprehensive assessment of the colorimetric properties is conducted, and the RGB values corresponding to temperature-induced color shifts are elucidated in Figures 3b and S2. The transformation of these values reveals that the variation of the green component surpasses that of the red and blue components. The value of the green component undergoes a decrease as the temperature escalates. The distribution of the green component at 25 and 65 °C is depicted in Figure 3c, where an approximately 3.6-fold increase in the average value is observed. The thermochromic films with different PDMS-to-thermochromic particle weight ratios are also exploited and observed to display accordant color change patterns, as shown in Figure S3. This result confirms their good thermal sensitivity for visual temperature sensing.

The dielectric layer is designed with an oblique pyramid microstructure to bolster its sensing efficacy. The sensitivity of the sensor is quantitatively represented as S = (ΔC/C0)/P, where C0, ΔC, and P denote the initial capacitance, the alteration in capacitance, and the applied pressure, respectively. Sensitivity S is intrinsically correlated with alterations in the spacing between the two electrode plates, assuming a consistent permittivity for the dielectric layer and a constant electrode plate area. Figure 4a shows that within the framework of the conventional right pyramid microstructure, the imposed pressure manifests solely as a normal component and thus induces elastic deformation, which appears as the output. Conversely, in the oblique pyramid configuration, the applied pressure undergoes partitioning into discrete normal and shear components. When viewing the microstructure in an analogy to a cantilever, the presence of the shear force can engender significantly enhanced deformations. Consequently, the deformation magnitude achievable within the oblique pyramid microstructure exceeds that inherent to its conventional pyramid counterpart. Simulation models for both the right pyramid and oblique pyramid microstructures are developed using COMSOL Multiphysics. The 3D modeling is based on the actual dimensions of the microstructure. The analysis is conducted with the model’s bottom set as a fixed constraint condition, and pressure is applied at the upper end of the model. Figure S4 displays the displacement of the microstructures when a pressure of 100 Pa is applied to the tips of the three microstructures. Evidently, the deformation amplitude achieved by the oblique pyramid microstructure Δdop surpasses that of the conventional pyramid microstructure Δdrp as Δdop > Δdrp, and Δdop increases with the degree of inclination. Therefore, the sensitivity ascribed to the oblique microstructural arrangement supersedes that of the conventional microstructural configuration.

(a) When exposed to the same pressure, the electrodes’ spacing variation of the oblique microstructure-based sensor is greater compared with that of the right pyramid microstructure-based sensor, resulting in an increase of capacitance variation. (b) Sensitivity of a right pyramid microstructure-based sensor. (c) The sensitivity of an oblique pyramid microstructure-based sensor is greater than that of a right pyramid microstructure-based sensor, and the sensitivity increases with the incline of the microstructure.

An investigation of the sensor’s performance across various slant angles is undertaken to assess the heightened sensitivity facilitated by the oblique pyramid microstructure design. A digital push–pull force gauge (HP-200) and precision LCR digital bridge (TH2830) are used for characterization (Figure S5). The sensor prototypes are formulated to encompass two ITO electrodes (with a resistance of 6 Ω) and feature three distinctive microstructural configurations, namely, the conventional right pyramid, the oblique pyramid I with a 30° slant angle, and the oblique pyramid II with a 60° slant angle (Figure S6). The sensitivity of these sensors is comprehensively assessed within a pressure range spanning 0–200 kPa. Specifically, within the initial 0–25 kPa range, the sensor’s capacitance significantly increases with increasing pressure. This empirical trend yields sensitivity values of Srp = 0.84% kPa–1 for the right pyramid configuration (Figure 4b), SopI = 1.07% kPa–1 for oblique pyramid I, and SopII = 2.81% kPa–1 for oblique pyramid II (Figure 4c). Notably, this tiered sequence of results vividly illustrates the quantifiable enhancements engendered by the integration of slant microstructure designs and aligns with the simulation results. As the applied pressure is increased beyond a certain threshold, the microstructure undergoes compression, which degrades the sensitivity of the device. The dual-scale microstructure design emerges as an effective strategy for mitigating these inherent limitations, thereby empowering the sensor’s capacity to extend its sensing range up to 200 kPa. As summarized in Table S1, our sensor shows an outstanding sensitivity across a large measurement range, indicating the superiority of dual-scale oblique pyramid microstructure designs.

Experimental investigations are conducted to validate the influential factors and the sustained performance of the sensing mechanism. The oblique pyramid I microstructure is used as an illustrative example, and the microstructures are strategically arranged based on spacing distributions of 600, 700, and 800 μm (Figure S7). The resultant capacitance variations are documented in Figure 5a. Evidently, the closer the arrangement of microstructures, the higher the sensitivity exhibited by the flexible pressure sensor. Within the 0–25 kPa pressure range, the sensitivity is quantified as Sd600 = 1.07% kPa–1 > Sd700 = 0.70% kPa–1 > Sd800 = 0.61% kPa–1. This phenomenon is rooted in the proximal arrangement of microstructures, effectively diminishing the interelectrode gap. As a result, PDMS occupancy is enhanced, and a larger variation of relative permittivity is achieved. Thus, a reduced spacing between the microstructures results in high capacitance change rates, thereby fundamentally enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity. Moreover, a reduced electrode resistance is conducive to increasing the sensitivity of the sensor (Figure S8). To ascertain the reliability of the sensor, experiments are undertaken involving the application of varying pressures (17, 29, and 41 kPa) to the sensor (oblique pyramid II), with each measurement repeated 1000 times. The results displayed in Figure 5b illustrate a consistent trajectory in the rate of capacitance change across each applied pressure level, underscoring the commendable stability and reliability intrinsic to the sensor’s operational performance.

Characterization of the influential factors and sustained performance of the sensor. (a) With a closer arrangement of microstructures, the pressure sensor exhibits higher sensitivity. (b) The flexible pressure sensor exhibits good reliability over 1000 cycles at different pressure values of 17, 29, and 41 kPa.

Because of the optimized sensing efficiency of the sensor, it can be applied to a wide array of potential applications. One notable application is its potential for monitoring human physiological signals, i.e., tracking joint motion, which underscores its versatility within the domain of electronic skin. The sensor is affixed to the midjoint of the index finger (Figure 6a), enabling the observation of the finger’s flexion at different angles (0, 30, 60, and 90°). The real-time changes in the output capacitance of the sensor are documented. Notably, the sensor responds to increasing degrees of finger flexion with a corresponding augmentation of capacitance variation. The “stepwise” increase in capacitance alteration is indicative of the sensor’s capacity to discern distinct finger postures. Thus, this feature underscores the ability of the finger to recognize and differentiate various finger configurations. The sensor’s remarkable combination of elevated sensitivity and rapid response characteristics further endows it with utility in tasks, such as code reading. Figure 6b shows an example in which the letters “XMDX” are sequentially pressed on the sensor, following the Morse code letter comparison table (Figure S9). The flexible pressure sensor promptly translates the sequence of key presses into a distinctive password representation, manifested through changes in capacitance rates. This multifaceted operational scope positions the sensor as a versatile tool capable of addressing a range of applications, encompassing physiological monitoring and swift information encoding.

Potential applications of the flexible sensor enabled by the optimized pressure sensing efficiency. (a) The sensor could be used to sense the motion shown in the figures as the bending angle varies from 0, 30, and 60 to 90°. (b) The sensor could be used for code reading by changing the capacitance according to the pressed letters “XMDX” as in the Morse code letter comparison table.

The incorporation of a visual temperature-sensing capability enhances the sensor’s potential for integrated pressure and temperature detection applications. Figure 7a shows that the sensor is positioned on the wrist region of the human body, thereby facilitating pulse monitoring. In this configuration, real-time wrist pulse data are recorded through the detection of capacitance variations with a frequency of approximately 84 beats per minute. Furthermore, the sensor’s capacity extends to providing real-time feedback on the human body temperature, manifesting as a chromatic transition from red to green within the context of ambient room conditions. This attribute enables the sensor to effectively execute physiological temperature monitoring. In addition, the sensor’s functionality encompasses targeted thermal detection. For demonstration, the sensor is strategically positioned on the inner palm region to lift cups containing water at diverse temperatures, as exemplified in Figure 7b. Evidently, the sensor responds to the temperature differences by initiating corresponding color changes, thus effectively executing temperature detection. Simultaneously, the sensor records the motion sequences of lifting and lowering the cups, and this result corroborates its ability to achieve integrated feedback corresponding to the pressure and temperature parameters (Figure 7c). These results confirm that the flexible pressure sensor can seamlessly integrate pressure and temperature feedback. This unique and beneficial feature renders it an immensely promising candidate for a spectrum of applications, e.g., electronic skin, prosthetics, and beyond.

Conclusions

In this study, we develop a versatile tactile sensor capable of seamlessly integrating capacitive pressure and visual temperature-sensing functionalities. A temperature-sensing feature is integrated through the strategic use of a visual sensing mechanism based on the thermochromic attributes inherent in the design of the dielectric layer. The experimental results underscore the sensor’s remarkable ability to undergo chromatic changes in response to varying thermal distributions. Oblique pyramid microstructures, designed in the dielectric layer of the capacitive sensor, significantly elevate the sensor’s sensitivity compared to that of the conventional pyramid-based sensors. Moreover, dual-scale microstructural features are incorporated to expand the measurable range, facilitating measurements up to 200 kPa. The tactile sensor overcomes the challenge of cross-interference between pressure- and temperature-sensing components as well as showcases an exceptional and reliable sensing performance. Consequently, its applicability extends across a diverse array of fields including but not limited to electronic skin and prosthetic devices. The conceptual framework of this tactile sensing design provides fresh perspectives on the underlying mechanisms of tactile perception, promising exciting prospects for future sensing applications.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Sensors

The thermochromic dielectric layer was developed by using a mold-assisted methodology. Initially, PDMS was prepared in a weight ratio of 10:1 (base/curing agent) and subjected to vigorous stirring for 10 min. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to a 30 min vacuuming process to eliminate the trapped air bubbles. Following this deaeration step, PDMS was combined with thermochromic particles (color-changing temperature: 30–65 °C) at a weight ratio of 1:1.7 and stirred gently with a pestle for another 10 min. The resulting homogeneous mixture was then carefully poured into a mold and cured at 70 °C. This controlled thermal treatment process yielded a dielectric layer with the intended microstructure. Subsequently, the sensor was packaged after plasma treatment, and the components, including the ITO film and dielectric layer, were methodically assembled from the bottom to top.

Characterization of the Sensor and Its Sensing Performance

The morphology of the microstructure was analyzed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (SUPRA 55 SAPPHIRE) and a microscope (AM73915MZT, Dino-Lite). The pressure and capacitance calibration of the sensor was carried out using a force sensor (HP-20, Handpi) and an LCR bridge (TH2830, Tonghui). The temperature stimulus applied to demonstrate the temperature-sensing performance was controlled with a hot plate. A thermal imaging camera (E6-XT, FLIR) was used to detect the sensor’s temperature.
