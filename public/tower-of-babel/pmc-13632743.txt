ABSTRACT
Nuclear glycogen storage has been known for nearly 100 years. However, its role in diseases remains unclear. Here, we found nuclear glycogen storage in livers of mice and humans with argininosuccinic aciduria (ASA), an inborn error of metabolism. Nuclear glycogenolysis sustains histone acetylation and liver homeostasis. We show that hepatic nuclear glycogenolysis is regulated by nitric oxide (NO)–dependent control of the nuclear abundance of liver glycogen phosphorylase (PYGL), an enzyme involved in glycogen breakdown that translocates from the cytosol to the nucleus. In vivo, NO supplementation restored the nuclear content of PYGL by promoting its S-nitrosylation at cysteine-446 and its nuclear translocation. Next, we found that nuclear import of PYGL is mediated by HNRNPF, a regulator of RNA maturation that shuttles between the nucleus and cytosol, thus linking control of gene expression to RNA metabolism. Similar changes were detected in both rodent and human livers with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a global health concern with limited therapies, that shares with ASA a decrease in NO synthesis. In conclusion, our findings reveal a mechanism underlying reduced glycogenolysis and hepatic nuclear glycogen storage disorders, a new group of metabolic disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Glycogen is a branched polymer of glucose that supplies energy to cells. Defects in the synthesis or breakdown of glycogen are responsible for glycogen storage diseases (GSDs), a group of rare inborn errors of metabolism characterized by the accumulation of glycogen in the cytosol or lysosomes (1). The liver regulates glucose homeostasis, maintaining postprandial and fasting euglycemia, by breaking down glycogen. Consequently, the liver is one of the organs most affected in many GSDs. Glycogenolysis relies on specific enzymes for the catabolism of α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds. Terminal α-1,4 glycosidic bonds of glycogen are broken by phosphorylation mediated by glycogen phosphorylase that releases glucose-1-phosphate. Glycogen phosphorylase comprises three isoforms encoded by separate genes, with expression in the liver (PYGL), muscle (PYGM), and brain (PYGB) (2).

Secondary cytosolic glycogen storage has been reported in the livers with other inherited metabolic disorders, including the urea cycle disorders (UCDs), a group of diseases due to impaired clearance of toxic nitrogen (3). Among UCDs, cytosolic accumulation of glycogen has been especially observed in argininosuccinic aciduria (ASA) due to argininosuccinate lyase (ASL) deficiency. In contrast to other UCDs, ASA has multisystem involvement including neurocognitive impairment, systemic hypertension, and chronic liver disease (4). ASL deficiency results in reduced nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, owing to both decreased endogenous arginine synthesis and impaired ability to use extracellular arginine for NO production (5). Several clinical features of ASA are thought to be dependent on NO deficiency rather than impaired ureagenesis (5). Growing evidence suggests that chronic hepatocellular injury in ASA is dependent on glycogen storage in the cytosol of hepatocytes (6).

“Glycogenated nuclei” have been reported since the 1940s, but their role and relevance remains elusive. In hepatocytes, glycogenated nuclei appear as enlarged nuclei with peripherally arranged chromatin appearing similar to glycogen by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (7). Glycogenated nuclei were first reported in individuals with diabetes mellitus, obesity, and chronic liver diseases (7). More recently, intranuclear glycogen storage has been detected in lung cancer cells, and glycogen was found to be synthesized and degraded within the nucleus. Nuclear glycogenolysis was found to provide a source of acetyl–coenzyme A (CoA) that is required for histone acetylation and modulation of the epigenetic landscape (8). Mobilization of acetyl-CoA from glycogen requires the activities of glycolytic enzymes and pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex within the nucleus. Consistently, more than 200 metabolic enzymes have been identified within the nucleus, including glycolytic enzymes, all the subunits of the PDH complex, and PYGL (9). However, how the enzymes shuttle from the cytosol to the nucleus remains largely unknown. Furthermore, the role of impaired nuclear metabolic pathways in disease states remains to be elucidated. In the present study, we identified nuclear glycogen storage in both inherited and acquired metabolic liver disorders, and we identified a posttranslational modification (PTM) involved in nuclear translocation of PYGL and nuclear glycogenolysis.

RESULTS

Nuclear glycogen storage in the livers of mice and humans with ASA

In hepatocytes of AslNeo/Neo mice, a model for ASA due to partial deficiency of ASL deficiency (5), abnormal nuclear glycogen storage was detected by electron microscopy (EM) (Fig. 1A). Nuclear glycogen storage likely corresponds to the glycogenated nuclei visualized in about 10% of liver cells by H&E (Fig. 1B). Nuclear glycogen was confirmed by confocal immunofluorescence signal with an anti-glycogen antibody (8) on purified nuclei from AslNeo/Neo mouse livers, and the signal was abolished after treatment with α-amylase, an enzyme that digests glycogen (Fig. 1C). Nuclear glycogen storage was also detected by EM on liver specimens of patients with ASA (Fig. 1, D and E). Together, these data show nuclear glycogen storage in both mice and humans with ASA.

Impaired nuclear glycogenolysis is associated with epigenetic changes and altered hepatic gene expression

Consistent with previous studies (6, 10), livers of AslNeo/Neo mice showed reduced transcript and protein of PYGL, the rate-limiting enzyme of the glycogenolytic pathway (fig. S1, A and B). Western blot analysis of nuclear enriched fractions isolated from the livers of AslNeo/Neo mice and wild-type (WT) controls showed marked reduced PYGL, without significant changes in the liver-specific glycogen synthase isoform 2 (GYS2) (Fig. 2, A and B), suggesting reduced glycogen degradation but unaffected glycogen synthesis in the nuclei of AslNeo/Neo mouse hepatocytes. Notably, there was no gender effect, and the reduced nuclear PYGL protein was similarly observed in livers from male and female AslNeo/Neo mice (fig. S1C). The nuclear abundance of two other key enzymes in glycogen metabolism, the glycogen debranching enzyme (AGL) and the glycogen branching enzyme (GBE), were unaffected in AslNeo/Neo mouse livers (fig. S1D). Reduced flux through nuclear glycolysis in AslNeo/Neo livers was supported by reduced abundance of the nuclear glycolytic intermediates, such as glucose-6-phosphate (glucose-6P) and dihydroxyacetone-phosphate/glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (DHAP/GAP), and acetyl-CoA (Fig. 2C), in the face of no major changes in their total concentrations (fig. S1E). Acetyl-CoA produced in the nucleus is a donor of acetyl groups for histone acetyltransferases (HATs), and reduced nuclear acetyl-CoA results in decreased histone acetylation (11). By Western blot analysis in AslNeo/Neo mouse livers, we detected decreased histone acetylation, including acetylation of lysine-9 of histone H3 (H3K9Ac), lysine-5 of histone 4 (H4K5Ac), and lysine-12 of histone 4 (H4K12Ac) (Fig. 2, D and E). In contrast to livers, no changes in histone acetylation were detected on nuclear fractions isolated from brains and muscles of AslNeo/Neo mice (fig. S1F). In AslNeo/Neo mouse livers, HAT activity was unaffected, whereas histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity was mildly decreased (fig. S1, G and H); thus, global changes in HAT or HDAC activities are not responsible for the reduced histone acetylation. Because the detected histone modifications regulate chromatin accessibility and gene expression, digital RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed on AslNeo/Neo mouse livers and WT controls, and it detected 1853 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (BioStudies accession number: S-BSST2234) (table S1). A significant overlap (1549 out of 1853; 84%) was found between the DEGs and the H3K9Ac chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from livers of 8-week-old WT mice (Fig. 2f and table S2) (12), supporting the hypothesis that liver transcriptomic changes in AslNeo/Neo mice are largely driven by changes in histone acetylation. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis (13) on these 1549 genes revealed enrichment in pathways involved in progression of liver disease (table S3), including down-regulation of protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, proteins involved in lipid metabolism, and cancer-related processes, among others. Together, these results suggest that reduced histone acetylation affects the expression of genes involved in liver homeostasis. Next, we investigated the effects of increasing histone acetylation in AslNeo/Neo mice using either adeno-associated viral (AAV) vector delivering the PYGL gene under the control of a hepatocyte-specific promoter (AAV-PYGL) or treatment with suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA), a potent and reversible pan-inhibitor of HDAC. In WT mice, intravenous injection of AAV-PYGL resulted in reduced liver glycogen content and overexpression of PYGL in both the cytosolic and nuclear-enriched liver fractions (fig. S2, A and B). The AslNeo/Neo mice were either injected with AAV-PYGL or treated by intraperitoneal injections of SAHA, in combination with a standard therapy (ST), consisting of daily intraperitoneal injections of an ammonia scavenger drug (sodium benzoate) and l-arginine (l-Arg), starting on day 10 of life and lasting for 3 weeks. As control, AslNeo/Neo mice received only ST. Consistent with previous reports (5, 10), AslNeo/Neo mice treated with ST showed reduced survival (Fig. 2G), whereas both AAV-PYGL and SAHA treatments significantly improved survival (Fig. 2G) and increased histone acetylation in the livers of AslNeo/Neo mice (Fig. 2, H and I). Neither AAV-mediated delivery of PYGL nor SAHA affected mouse body weight (fig. S2C) or residual ASL protein levels (fig. S2D). As expected, AAV-PYGL increased nuclear PYGL in AslNeo/Neo livers (fig. S2E). Furthermore, the liver transcriptome of AslNeo/Neo mice was well separated from WT controls but could be partially restored after AAV-PYGL administration (fig. S2F).

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) is a nuclear receptor involved in lipid metabolism, and its expression is low in nonsteatotic liver compared to muscle and adipose tissue but increases in steatosis and steatohepatitis (14). Therefore, PPARγ up-regulation has been proposed as one of the mechanisms involved in the development of fatty liver disease (15). PPARγ expression was increased in livers of AslNeo/Neo mice, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on DEGs revealed a significant enrichment of PPARγ target genes (16) in AslNeo/Neo compared to WT controls [enrichment score of 0.681, false discovery rate (FDR) q value: 0.111] (Fig. 2J). Furthermore, AAV-PYGL reduced both hepatic PPARγ expression (Fig. 2K) and activation, as shown by GSEA in AslNeo/Neo livers (fig. S2, G and H). In summary, reduced nuclear glycogenolysis in ASL-deficient hepatocytes is associated with histone hypoacetylation and global changes in gene expression, including up-regulation of lipogenic PPARγ.

Nuclear glycogen storage is not detected in other inherited urea cycle or glycogen storage disorders

Livers of Spf-ash mice, a model of ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency (10), and two murine models of argininosuccinate synthetase 1 (ASS1) deficiency [the hypormorphic Ass1Fold (17) and the liver-specific ASS1 knockout (18)] were not found to display nuclear glycogen storage by EM (fig. S3A). Consistent with this finding, no changes in histone acetylation were detected (fig. S3, B and C). Livers of murine models of GSDs, including a liver-specific glucose-6-phosphatase alpha deficiency (L-G6pc−/−) (19), glycogen debranching enzyme deficiency (Agl−/−) (20), and α-1,4-glucosidase deficiency (Gaa−/−) (21), were also found to be devoid of nuclear glycogen, despite abundant glycogen storage in either the cytoplasm or lysosomes (fig. S4A). Consistent with this finding, histone acetylation was not reduced in L-G6pc−/− and Gaa−/− livers (fig. S4, B and C). Intriguingly, nuclear glycogen storage was absent in five out of six livers of Pygl−/− mice (22) but was detected in one Pygl−/− mouse (fig. S5A), while Western blotting on nuclear extracts showed no changes in histone acetylation (fig. S5, B and C). In the nuclei of Pygl−/− livers, GYS2 enzyme activity was reduced (fig. S5D), whereas total nuclear GYS2 was unaffected and phosphorylation of GYS2 at serine-641 was increased (fig. S5, E and F). This PTM negatively regulates GYS2 catalytic activity (23), thus suggesting a compensatory inhibition of nuclear GYS2 that prevents nuclear glycogen accumulation in the nuclei of Pygl−/− mouse hepatocytes. In contrast, histone acetylation was reduced in PYGL knockout (PYGL-KO) HepG2 cells, which did not show compensatory changes in phosphorylation of GYS2 at serine-641 (fig. S6, A and B). Consistently, PYGL-KO HepG2 cells displayed increased nuclear glycogen signal compared to WT cells (fig. S6, C and D). Moreover, AAV vector–mediated delivery of a mutant GYS2 harboring serine-641 mutated into alanine (S641A), which cannot be phosphorylated and has a higher activity (fig. S6E), and is fused to four nuclear localization signals (NLS) to enforce nuclear localization (fig. S6F, G and H), resulted in increased nuclear GYS2 and massive nuclear glycogenesis in Pygl−/− mice (fig. S6I). In contrast, glycogen storage was not observed by EM in control livers of WT mice receiving the AAV-NLS-GYS2(S641A) vector (fig. S6I). Together, these data suggest that nuclear glycogen storage is induced by an imbalance between glycogen synthesis and degradation in the nucleus.

NO regulates PYGL nuclear translocation through S-nitrosylation

Reduced synthesis of NO is a hallmark of ASA (5). To investigate whether reduced PYGL nuclear translocation was dependent on impaired NO production, AslNeo/Neo mice were treated with sodium nitrite, a NO supplement (24). Consistent with previous studies (5, 24), weight gain was unaffected (fig. S7A), but mouse survival was significantly increased by sodium nitrite (fig. S7B). PYGL mRNA and total protein remained unaffected in livers of AslNeo/Neo mice treated with sodium nitrite (fig. S7, C to E). However, NO supplementation increased PYGL abundance in nuclear enriched fractions (Fig. 3, A and B), suggesting NO involvement in nucleus-cytoplasmic shuttling of PYGL. Consistent with the enhanced nuclear glycogenolysis, histone acetylation increased in livers of AslNeo/Neo mice receiving NO supplementation (fig. S7F).

Protein S-nitrosylation is a reversible PTM occurring on cysteines, which dynamically regulates protein function, including protein activity, protein-protein interactions, and protein subcellular localization (25). S-Nitrosylation can regulate nuclear translocation of its target proteins (26–28). Notably, PYGL has been detected by site-specific S-nitroso-proteomic screenings (29, 30). Using a biotin-switch assay followed by immunoprecipitation, we confirmed S-nitrosylation of PYGL both in total extracts (Fig. 3C) and in nuclear isolated fractions (fig. S8A) from mouse livers. Dynamic S-nitrosylation of PYGL was confirmed by independent SNO-RAC method (resin-assisted capture of S-nitrosylated proteins) (31) in murine WT livers (fig. S8B). Furthermore, S-nitrosylation of PYGL was increased in livers of mice with deficiency in GSNO reductase (GSNOR) (fig. S8, B and C), a protein denitrosylase highly active in liver (32). In mouse livers, S-nitrosylation of PYGL was found to occur on cysteine-446, a residue conserved between mouse and human (30). Mutant PYGL harboring cysteine-446 mutated into alanine (C446A) showed unaffected protein stability and activity in vitro (fig. S8, D and E), but reduced nuclear abundance compared to cells transfected with the WT PYGL (Fig. 3, D to G). Consistently, PYGL (C446A) mutant delivered in vivo to livers of WT mice by AAV vectors retained protein stability and enzyme activity (fig. S8, F and G), but showed reduced nuclear translocation compared to the WT PYGL (Fig. 3, H and I). Consistent with the unaffected enzyme activity, the p.Cys446Ala variant in PYGL has not been reported in ClinVar as causative of GSD type VI due to PYGL deficiency. Moreover, PYGL (C446A) displayed reduced S-nitrosylation compared to the WT PYGL (Fig. 3, J and K), thus confirming cysteine-446 residue as a major site of S-nitrosylation. In summary, these data suggest that S-nitrosylation on cysteine-446 regulates PYGL nuclear translocation for nuclear glycogenolysis.

Nuclear import of PYGL is mediated by HNRNPF

Non–small cell lung cancers suppress nuclear glycogenolysis by preventing nuclear translocation of PYGB through down-regulation of malin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase (8). Expression of malin, encoded by Nhlcr1, was unaffected in livers of AslNeo/Neo mice (fig. S9A). Furthermore, by EM analysis, livers of malin-deficient mice (33) were devoid of nuclear glycogen storage, despite the accumulation of cytosolic glycogen (fig. S9B). In addition, nuclear abundance of PYGL was unaffected in malin-deficient livers (fig. S9C). Together, these data do not support the involvement of malin in regulating the nuclear entry of hepatic PYGL.

Proteins require nuclear transport receptors for nuclear import or export, such as the karyopherin-β superfamily proteins, which include importins and exportins (34). Importins recognize a cargo NLS, whereas exportins recognize a nuclear export signal (NES). Sequence features of classical NLS make them highly predictable by several available NLS prediction tools (35–37). Although a recently developed tool based on protein language models [NLSExplorer (37)] predicted a putative NLS in PYGL, this could not be confirmed by NLStradamus or cNLS Mapper (Fig. 4A). Nearly 50% of nuclear proteins of the human proteome lack a predictable classical NLS (38), suggesting additional nuclear import pathways, such as alternative uncharacterized NLS or cotransport into the nucleus via physical interactions with other proteins (39–42). Notably, a NES in PYGL was predicted by LocNES, a computational tool for mapping classical NES (Fig. 4A) (43). Therefore, based on predictions, PYGL is unlikely to be directly recognized by importin proteins for nuclear import. Hence, we hypothesize that PYGL is cotransported into the nucleus by proteins shuttling between the cytosol and the nucleus. Immunoprecipitation of mouse liver lysates with an anti-PYGL antibody followed by mass spectrometry analysis revealed 59 putative PYGL interactor proteins (Fig. 4B and table S4; dataset PXD070559). Among these interactors, the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein F (HNRNPF), a critical regulator of RNA maturation, was confirmed by coimmunoprecipitation to interact with PYGL, whereas other HNRNPs expressed in liver (HNRNPL and HNRNPC) were not immunoprecipitated with PYGL (Fig. 4C and fig. S10A).

HNRNPs are part of a large family of ubiquitously expressed RNA binding proteins that shuttle between the nucleus and the cytoplasm to transport mRNA out of the nucleus (44). Efficient nuclear localization of HNRNPF requires ongoing RNA polymerase II transcription (45), whose inhibition by actinomycin D reduced nuclear levels of both HNRNPF and PYGL (Fig. 4, D and E), without affecting total PYGL in hepatic Huh-7 cells (fig. S10B), suggesting that HNRNPF depletion affects PYGL nuclear import. Moreover, small interfering RNA–mediated down-regulation of HNRNPF reduced nuclear PYGL in Huh-7 cells (fig. S10C). Consistent with these data, HNRNPF knockout HepG2 cells showed reduced nuclear PYGL abundance (Fig. 4, F and G) without changes in the amounts of total PYGL protein (fig. S10D), nuclear accumulation of glycogen (Fig. 4, H and I), and decreased histone acetylation (Fig. 4, J and K). Modeling the interacting modalities between PYGL and HNRNPF by AlphaFold3 (46) with enriched multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) (47) provided putative binding modes between the two proteins (fig. S11 and table S5). Collectively, these data established physical and functional interaction of PYGL with HNRNPF that favors the nuclear import of PYGL.

Defective nuclear glycogenolysis in fatty liver disease

Glycogenated nuclei and nuclear inclusions have been reported in hepatocytes of patients with chronic liver disease, particularly diabetes mellitus and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a major worldwide health problem (48). Besides ASA, reduced synthesis of NO is the hallmark of MASLD (49). Therefore, we investigated nuclear glycogenolysis in two preclinical models of fatty liver disease. Using H&E and EM, we found that livers of rats fed for 2 weeks with a high-fat high-cholesterol (HFC) diet, an experimental model of early MASLD (50), showed morphological abnormalities consistent with steatosis (fig. S12A) and marked accumulation of nuclear glycogen compared to control rats receiving a standard diet (Fig. 5A). Moreover, Western blot analysis of nuclear extracts showed reduced PYGL but no changes in GYS2 abundance, consistent with the data in AslNeo/Neo mice (Fig. 5, B and C). Furthermore, reduced histone acetylation was observed in livers of rats under the HFC diet (Fig. 5, D and E). Notably, hepatic PYGL and GYS2 total protein amounts were unaffected in HFC-treated rats (fig. S12B), suggesting defective PYGL nuclear translocation.

Intranuclear material in MASLD has been distinguished as inclusions bound by the nuclear membrane, containing degenerative cell organelles and lysosomes (inclusions type 1) and inclusions with deposits of glycogen without any limiting membrane (inclusions type 2) (51). Membrane-bound intranuclear type 2 inclusions but not nuclear glycogen deposits were detected in leptin-deficient ob/ob mice (fig. S12C), a genetic model of obesity (52). These findings are consistent with “endonucleosis” that involves nuclear engulfment of cytoplasmic material during cellular stress in ob/ob livers (53). Moreover, nuclear abundance of the glycogen metabolizing enzymes PYGL and GYS2 (fig. S12, D and E) and histone acetylation (fig. S12, F and G) were all unaffected in ob/ob livers. In summary, reduced nuclear glycogenolysis appears to be an early event in the pathogenesis of a subset of MASLD. Protein S-nitrosylation (fig. S12H) and S-nitrosylation of PYGL were both reduced in livers of HFC-fed rats (Fig. 5, F and G), suggesting a shared pathogenetic mechanism between ASA and MASLD. Last, we investigated nuclear glycogen accumulation in liver biopsies of pediatric patients with MASLD and its advanced form [e.g., metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH)], which may progress into cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (54). Confocal immunofluorescence revealed increased nuclear glycogen storage in both MASLD and MASH livers compared to unrelated controls (Fig. 5, H and I, and fig. S12I). This suggests that nuclear glycogen storage is increased with the early onset of MASLD and remains elevated in MASH.

DISCUSSION

Nuclear glycogen storage in the liver has been known for almost a century, but its role is still poorly understood. In this work, we found nuclear glycogen in the livers of mice and humans affected by ASA. Nuclear glycogen storage was associated with reduced nuclear PYGL, impaired nuclear glycogenolysis, and global hypoacetylation of histones driving changes in gene expression. The nuclear abundance of PYGL was corrected by NO supplementation in ASA mice. Nuclear glycogen and reductions in S-nitrosylation of PYGL, PYGL nuclear import, and histone acetylation were also detected in MASLD, which shares decreased NO synthesis with ASA (49). Collectively, these findings suggest a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of fatty liver disease for disorders with nuclear glycogen storage.

PYGL catalytic activity is positively regulated by phosphorylation and glycosylation (55), whereas acetylation negatively regulates its activity (56). We found S-nitrosylation as an additional PTM operating on PYGL that, unlike the other PTMs, regulates the subcellular localization rather than the activity of the enzyme. The C446A mutant of PYGL was indeed found to have normal enzyme activity but inefficient nuclear translocation.

Metabolism has evolved to operate in separate subcellular compartments. Nuclear synthesis of acetyl-CoA is used for histone acetylation (57). Nuclear glycogen provides acetyl-CoA for histone acetylation and epigenetic reprogramming in lung cancer (8). Here, we found that hepatic nuclear glycogenolysis, regulated by NO-dependent control of PYGL abundance in the nucleus, sustains histone acetylation and liver homeostasis. In addition to PYGL-mediated glycogenolysis, acetyl-CoA can be produced by other enzymes that also translocate to the nucleus from the cytosol or the mitochondria, such as ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2), and PDH complex (58–63). However, how acetyl-CoA–producing enzymes are imported in the nucleus and the signals inducing their translocation are unknown. We found that nuclear translocation of PYGL requires HNRNPF, a nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling protein that plays a critical role in mRNA maturation (64–66). HNRNPs are also involved in several functions related to RNA processing and regulation, including alternative splicing and polyadenylation. The combined nuclear translocation of PYGL with HNRNPF delivers both the enzyme required to generate the substrate for epigenetic control of gene expression and the mRNA binding protein necessary for inducing increased mRNA expression and maturation. Via HNRNPF binding, the HIF-1α antisense long noncoding RNA recruits a protein complex formed by prolyl hydroxylase 3 and pyruvate kinase 2 enzymes and translocates to the nucleus (67). ACSS2 and PDH complex were found to regulate the acetylation of specific gene loci (68, 69) but further studies are needed to understand intranuclear localization of glycogenolysis and whether nuclear glycogen preferentially localizes into specific chromatin domains to provide hotspots of acetyl-CoA production in a locus-specific manner.

MASLD is a global health concern (70), and its pathogenesis is multifactorial but not yet fully elucidated (71). Nuclear glycogen storage, reduced PYGL, and decreased histone acetylation were found in early-onset patients and in a diet-induced rat model of MASLD, suggesting a shared pathogenic mechanism. However, nuclear glycogen storage was not detected in ob/ob mice, another commonly used animal model of MASLD. This discrepancy is in line with the heterogeneity of pathogenetic mechanisms underlying MASLD (72). NO is a key epigenetic regulator and dynamically controls gene expression via histone, DNA, and mRNA methylation (73–75). NO also regulates chromatin compaction by regulating histone PTMs including its acetylation (76). Therefore, the defective NO synthesis in AslNeo/Neo mice likely affects histone acetylation at multiple levels. Nevertheless, the reduced histone acetylation observed in cells deficient in nuclear PYGL (PYGL knockout and HNRNPF knockout cells), which have normal NO synthesis, supports the reduced nuclear glycogenolysis as a mechanism underlying histone hypoacetylation.

Both ASA and MASLD have impaired synthesis of NO. Notably, while NO reduction depends on reduced endogenous synthesis of arginine and impaired channeling of exogenous arginine toward NO synthase (NOS) in ASA (5), decreased NO availability is a direct consequence of reduced NOS in MASLD (49). Several studies showed that sodium nitrite can be recycled to form NO as an alternative to the classical arginine-NOS pathway. Based on these studies, the efficacy of sodium nitrite supplementation has been proposed as an intervention for ASA (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT0225277 and NCT03064048). NO-boosting treatments have been proposed in MASLD or metabolic syndrome, but the efficacy of these treatments is unclear (77, 78). The doses and formulation of the NO-donor might have been insufficient to correct the MASLD, but it is possible that only a subset of MASLD sharing the same pathogenesis involving defective S-nitrosylation and nuclear translocation of PYGL is responsive to NO-based therapies.

In conclusion, we identified nuclear glycogen storage as a unique subcellular compartment and regulatory pathway, distinct from lysosomal and cytosolic glycogen storage. Nuclear glycogen affects liver homeostasis by reducing histone acetylation and affecting gene expression. These findings illustrate the importance of studying rare genetic diseases that can inform the pathogenesis of more common disorders. Moreover, they set the stage for precision medicine strategies targeting nuclear glycogen metabolism in both genetic and nongenetic disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human samples

The study was conducted following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Frozen liver samples were obtained from patients diagnosed with ASA who underwent liver transplantation at Ospedale Pediatrico Bambino Gesù (Rome, Italy). Control liver samples were from patients who received liver transplantation for diseases unrelated to ASA. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ospedale Pediatrico Bambino Gesù (3020_OPBG_2022 and 3075_OPBG_2023). Written informed consent for biobanking and research use was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Mouse studies

All mouse procedures were performed in accordance with regulations and were authorized by the Italian Ministry of Health (protocol numbers: 413/2021-PR and C6AC4.7), the UK Home Office (protocol number: PP9223137), and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Connecticut Health Center (protocol number: AP 201495-0628). AslNeo/Neo mice (B6.129S7-Asltm1Brle/J) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed in ventilated cages under a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Mice were inbred and maintained on standard rodent chow (Harlan 2018, Teklab Diets, Madison, WI, USA; protein content, 18%) with water ad libitum. Genotyping was performed as previously described (5) using DNA extracted from finger clips. Because no sex differences had been previously reported in this mouse model, male and female mice were included for experiments. AslNeo/Neo mouse body weight was evaluated daily from 10 days of age. For all experiments, WT and AslNeo/Neo littermates were housed in the same cages. Pups and their mothers were fed the same diet. For all long-term experiments, AslNeo/Neo mice received a modified supportive treatment (79) consisting of daily intraperitoneal injections of sodium benzoate (0.5 g/kg per day; Sigma-Aldrich catalog no. B3420) and l-Arg (0.5 g/kg per day; Sigma-Aldrich catalog no. A5006) from day 10 to day 30. For experiments involving sodium nitrite supplementation, AslNeo/Neo mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of sodium nitrite (10 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich catalog no. 237213) from 10 days of age to the end of the experiment. For SAHA treatment, AslNeo/Neo mice received intraperitoneal injections of SAHA (50 mg/kg; Selleckchem catalog no. S1047) three times per week (Monday, Wednesday, and Friday), from day 10 of age to the end of the treatment. Treated animals were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at indicated time points, and tissues (liver, skeletal muscle, and brain) were harvested for analysis. The efficacy of Myc-tagged human PYGL gene transfer was assessed in WT mice through intravenous injection in the retro-orbital plexus of a dose of 1 × 1013 genome copies (GC)/kg of the AAV-PYGL vector or saline. Mice were sacrificed 4 weeks after injection, and livers were harvested for further analyses. For PYGL gene transfer in AslNeo/Neo mice, 7-day-old AslNeo/Neo mice received a single intravenous injection in the retro-orbital plexus of a dose of 1 × 1013 GC/kg of the AAV-PYGL vector, and mice were sacrificed on day 30. For enhanced nuclear synthesis of glycogen, WT and Pygl−/− mice were intravenously injected with a dose of 1 × 1013 GC/kg of the AAV-NLS-GYS2(S641A) vector or saline in the retro-orbital plexus and sacrificed 4 weeks after vector administration. For experiments evaluating PYGL nuclear translocation, 6-week-old WT mice were intravenously injected with a dose of 1 × 1013 GC/kg of either the AAV-PYGL or AAV-PYGL (C446A) vectors in the retro-orbital plexus, and mice were sacrificed 4 weeks post–vector injection. Liver samples from a variety of murine models of UCDs and GSDs (and their corresponding controls) were obtained from prior studies. These samples include livers from Ass1Fold mice (17), spf-ash mice (10), ASS1 LiKO (18), Gaa−/− mice (80), Agl−/− mice (20), L-G6pc−/− mice (19), Nhlrc1−/− mice (33), and Pygl−/− mice (22) and were used for comparative analyses. Liver samples from female rats treated with an HFC diet for 2 weeks (50) were obtained from University of Aarhus (Denmark). Livers from ob/ob mice and Gsnor−/− mice were obtained from the Sorrentino and Stamler laboratories, respectively. A summary of the major findings obtained in each mouse model used in this study is provided as table S6.

Electron microscopy

Frozen liver specimens were fixed in 1% glutaraldehyde in 0.2 M Hepes buffer. Small fragments were then postfixed in OsO4 and uranyl acetate. Samples were dehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions and propylene oxide and then embedded in epoxy resin and polymerized for 72 hours at 60°C. Thin sections of each sample were cut with a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome. Image acquisition was performed using an FEI Tecnai 12 electron microscope (FEI, Eindhoven, Netherlands) equipped with a Veletta CCD camera (Olympus, Lakewood, CO, USA) for digital image acquisition.

Immunofluorescence on purified nuclei

Nuclei from subcellular fractionation were resuspended in 4% paraformaldehyde and placed on glass coverslips. Prior to permeabilization, negative control slides were treated with 0.5% α-amylase in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 20 min to digest glycogen. Samples were then permeabilized for 20 min in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100. Blocking solution containing 5% donkey serum was applied for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4°C. Anti-lamin A (Abcam, catalog no.26300) was used at 1:1000 dilution, and anti-glycogen (IVS8B6) (81) was used at 1:50 dilution. The secondary antibodies anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 were used at 1:400 dilutions. Sections were mounted on glass slides with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) mounting reagent. Images were obtained using ZEISS LSM 700 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Nuclear and total metabolite measurements

Nuclear acetyl CoA concentrations were determined by using a fluorometric assay kit (Abcam catalog no. ab87546) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glucose-6P and DHAP/GAP concentrations were obtained by liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry using an API-3500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled with an ExionLC AC System (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) as previously reported (82).

Digital mRNA-seq and functional analysis of transcriptomic data

Total RNA was quantified using the Qubit 4.0 fluorimetric assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The libraries were prepared from 125 ng of total RNA using the NEGEDIA Digital mRNA-seq research-grade sequencing service (Next Generation Diagnostic srl), which included library preparation, quality assessment, and sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system using a single-end, 100-cycle strategy (Illumina Inc.). The raw data were analyzed by Next Generation Diagnostic srl proprietary NEGEDIA Digital mRNA-seq pipeline (v2.0), which involves a cleaning step by quality filtering and trimming, alignment to the reference genome, and counting by gene (83, 84). Raw expression data were normalized, analyzed by degsanalysis pipeline (v2.0) (85, 86), and visualized in a proprietary report (v1.0). FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were deposited in BioStudies with accession no. S-BSST2234. The list of 1853 deregulated genes between WT and AslNeo/Neo livers was crossed with the list of peaks obtained by the Experiment ENCSR000CEQ publicly available in the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) project (12). The list of peaks was cut to restrict the promoter region to a 3-kb region that includes the peaks located 2.5 kb upstream and 0.5 kb downstream of the transcription start site. The overlap with the list of deregulated genes was high: 84% (1549/1853) of the altered genes presents at least one acetylated H3K9Ac positive peak in the mouse liver. Data from RNA-seq were analyzed using the DAVID online tool (DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8) for the KEGG pathway analysis. The threshold for statistical significance for the KEGG pathway analysis was FDR ≤ 10%. GSEA was performed using GSEA software (www.broadinstitute.org/gsea). The Pparγ target gene set was previously reported (16). Heatmaps were generated using TIGEM Bioinformatic tools (https://bioinformatics.tigem.it/heatmap.php).

Adeno-associated viral vectors

Myc-tagged human PYGL and GYS2 cDNAs were purchased from OriGene Technologies Inc. (Rockville, MD, USA) and subcloned into a pAAV2/8.TBG.GFP plasmid (87). Serine-641 of GYS2 was mutagenized into alanine using the Quikchange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies catalog no. 200521). Primers used for mutagenesis were as follows: forward, 5′-tatcccaggccttccgcagtaccaccttctc-3′; reverse, 5′-gagaaggtggtactgcggaaggcctgggata-3′.

To enforce nuclear entry of mutagenized GYS2, four nuclear localization signals (4xNLS) from SV40 virus large T antigen (88) were added to the sequence through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the following primers: forward, 5′-ctccacaggtgtccaggcggccccaaaaaagaagagaaaggtagatccaaaaaagaagagaaaggtagatccaaaaaagaagagaaaggtagcgatcgccatgcttcgaggc-3′; reverse, 5′-aggttgattggatccaagctgccgtttaaaccttatcgtcgtc-3′. Primers used for mutagenesis of PYGL cysteine-446 into alanine were as follows: forward, 5′-ggatcaacatggcccatctcgctattgtcggttcccatgctgtg-3′; reverse, 5′-cacagcatgggaaccgacaatagcgagatgggccatgttgatcc-3′. The sequence was confirmed by DNA sequencing (Eurofins Genomics). AAV vectors were produced by Innovavector srl (Pozzuoli, Italy) by triple transfection in HEK293 cells and purified by CsCl2 ultracentrifugation. Viral titers were determined by real-time PCR quantification using TaqMan (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and dot-blot analysis as previously described (89). Average values between PCR quantification and dot-blot results were used to calculate the final titer of each preparation.

PYGL immunoprecipitation

Mouse liver lysates (500 μg) were incubated with either anti-PYGL or anti-Myc tag (ratio of 1 μg antibody:100 μg lysate) overnight at 4°C under rotation. As negative control, lysates were incubated with anti-rabbit immunoglobulin (IgG) isotope (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalog no. 31235). The following day, lysates were incubated with 40 μl of magnetic Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 hours at 4°C under gentle rotation. Using a DynaMag magnet (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the beads were then washed three times with 1 ml of PBS containing 0.02% of Tween-20 at 4°C for 5 min under rotation, and immunoprecipitated PYGL was eluted in Laemmli buffer (62.5 mM tris-HCl, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, and 20 mM dithiothreitol, pH 6.8), separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and analyzed by Western blot with antibodies of interest.

Immunofluorescence on human livers

Immunofluorescence was performed on 2-μm sections of formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded liver tissues from children with MASLD/MASH. The slices were dewaxed and rehydrated, then heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed by boiling with Dako Target Retrieval Solution (Dako-Agilent Technologies catalog no. K800021-2). The primary glycogen antibody was diluted 1:100 in PBS/bovine serum albumin (BSA) 1% and incubated overnight at 4°C (IVS8B6). The slices were then incubated with the secondary antibody, anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalog no. A-11017), diluted 1:500 in PBS/BSA 1%. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DRAQ5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalog no. 622251). Images were acquired using Olympus Fluoview FV3000 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Olympus, Lakewood, CO, USA). Dot/nuclei quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons of continuous variables between two and more experimental groups were performed using either one- or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc tests, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size. No formal randomization procedure was used, but assignment of mice to treatment groups was based on mouse identification numbers, and the investigators were not blinded. The number of replicates is reported in the figure legends. Data are expressed as means ± SEM. P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
