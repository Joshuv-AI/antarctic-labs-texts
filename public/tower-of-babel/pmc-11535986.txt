Impact of an emergency department closure on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survival and emergency medical service system in Ulsan, South Korea
Authors: Song Yi Park, Sun Hyu Kim, Byungho Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study evaluated the impact of an emergency department (ED) closure on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survival outcomes and the emergency medical service system in Ulsan, South Korea.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational cohort study from March 2017 to February 2021 and compared survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurological outcomes. We investigated transport time intervals (TTIs) in the total study population and subgroup covered by the closed ED. The analysis methods included propensity score matching (PSM), inverse propensity weighting (IPW), and logistic regression analysis.ResultsThe study included 692 and 920 patients before and after ED closure groups, respectively. After adjusting for covariates, the adjusted odds ratios and 95 % confidence interval for survival discharge and favorable neurological outcomes were 1.040 (0.749–1.444, p = 0.814) and 2.845 (1.563–5.177, p = 0.001), respectively. PSM showed values of 1.015 (0.727–1.416, p = 0.932) for discharge survival and 1.777 (1.074–2.941, p = 0.025) for neurological outcomes. IPW showed values of 0.980 (0.762–1.261, p = 0.875) for discharge survival and 1.782 (1.196–2.656, p = 0.005) for neurological outcomes. The TTI increased from 5.8 ± 5.0 to 6.7 ± 5.5 min (p < 0.001) in the total population and from 9.2 ± 7.9 to 16.2 ± 6.5 min (p < 0.001) in the subgroup.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that while the overall system may remain stable, subgroups could be disproportionately affected by an ED closure, underscoring the importance of the ongoing monitoring of survival outcomes and transport times.

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) refers to the sudden cessation of effective cardiac activity, impacting approximately 3.8 million individuals globally each year [1]. Despite extensive efforts, the worldwide survival rate for attempted resuscitations after OHCA remains around 8 %, with South Korea reporting a slightly higher rate from 9.0 % to 9.3 % [2,3]. Thus, OHCA remains a significant public health concern.

Numerous studies have explored factors associated with survival outcomes following OHCA [[4], [5], [6]]. A key concept in improving these outcomes is the “the chain of survival”, which emphasizes the importance of early access to the emergency medical services (EMS) [7,8]. While enhancing EMS response is a global priority, it is crucial to consider the specific challenges posed by regional variations in healthcare infrastructures and EMS systems.

The emergency medical center or emergency department (ED) plays a critical role in the chain of survival for OHCA patients, providing timely and advanced life support. EDs are vital community resources, and their abrupt closures are generally rare events. However, on March 1, 2019, an exceptional situation occurred in Ulsan, South Korea, when a specific area's ED was closed. Such a closure can have a cascading impact on the regional EMS system. Local ambulances may be required to transport patients over longer distances, leading to increased response time. Additionally, EMS teams from neighboring areas might be needed to cover the resulting ambulance vacancies, particularly in time-sensitive situations such as OHCA.

The closure of a single ED within an EMS system can therefore affect not only the immediate area but also the broader region. However, the reports on this topic seem to vary by region. An observational study in England found that the closure of EDs did not result in a statistically significant change in overall population mortality, although a slight increase in case fatality was observed, suggesting a potential impact on patient risk levels and hospital admissions [9]. A controlled longitudinal study in Finland reported that closing a geographically distant primary care ED did not increase the use of local primary care services or affect overall mortality rates [10].

Despite the potential implications, limited research exists on how ED closures affect the survival outcomes of OHCA patients. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the impact of one ED closure in Ulsan on the EMS system and survival outcomes of OHCA patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study analyzing adult nontraumatic OHCA patients in Ulsan, South Korea, from March 2017 to February 2021. The specific objective of this study was to compare survival outcomes, specifically survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurological outcomes of OHCA patients, along with regional EMS changes before and after the ED closure.

We delineated two distinct timeframes to investigate the effect of ED closure on survival outcomes. The first timeframe from March 2017 to February 2019 was the period before the closure of the ED (before ED closure group). The second timeframe from March 2019 to February 2021 was the period after ED closure (after ED closure group). We were not able to find previous research or specific guidance to aid in determining the timeframe for our study. Thus, we elected to focus on two years. This duration was considered sufficient to accumulate a substantial number of OHCA cases, considering the annual incidence of OHCA in Ulsan. This approach ensured adequate statistical power to detect meaningful differences in survival outcomes. A one-year period might be too brief to capture a representative sample of OHCA cases, potentially limiting our ability to draw robust conclusions. In contrast, extending the study to three years could introduce the risk of additional confounding variables, which might affect the results. Therefore, a two-year assessment was chosen as a balanced approach, aiming to comprehensively evaluate the impact of EMS system changes while minimizing the risks associated with shorter or longer study durations.

We systematically observed and compared EMS changes in the regional study area at two distinct time points, acknowledging that the last year of the study period coincided with the COVID-19 period. We also conducted subgroup analysis to explore variations by comparing changes in the EMS before and after closure. The subgroup was defined as OHCA patients who were transferred by the EMS team in southern Ulju County (Onsan-eup, Onyang-eup, and Seosaeng-myeon), which was the area covered by the closed ED.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ulsan University Hospital (IRB No. UUH-IRB-2023-09-025). The requirement to obtain written informed consent from all study patients was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study setting

This study was conducted in Ulsan, South Korea's eighth-largest city and industrial powerhouse. Situated on the east coast of the Korean peninsula, Ulsan covers an area of 1057.136 km2 and was home to over 1.1 million people in 2022. The city is divided into four urban districts (Buk, Dong, Jung, and Nam) and one rural county (Ulju) [11].

Ulsan's EMS system adheres to the structure of South Korea's National EMS systems. As of 2022, Ulsan boasted 30 fire stations, each equipped with more than one public ambulance and a central dispatch center. The EMS resuscitation protocol in Ulsan includes multiple dispatches, on-site CPR, patient transport to EDs, and continuous CPR maintenance within the ambulance. EMS personnel are obligated to sustain CPR until the return of spontaneous circulation, the presence of death signs, or the implementation of a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order. The declaration of death is within the purview of hospital ED physicians. EMS teams, typically consisting of two or three personnel with at least one emergency medical technician (EMT), predominantly hold certifications as registered nurses or level 1 or 2 EMTs, providing basic to intermediate-level EMT services. These services encompass basic life support, advanced airway management, intravenous establishment, and fluid/drug administration. Ambulances staffed with physicians are unavailable. Procedures, such as airway management, intravenous access, fluid and drug administration, and decisions regarding resuscitation withhold/withdrawal, are directly overseen by medical directors, mainly emergency physicians [12].

Namulsan Boram Hospital opened in the center of rural Ulju County in 1991 to provide medical services to residents of southern Ulju County, including Onsan-eup, Onyang-eup, and Seosaeng-myeon. It was the only general hospital in southern Ulju, with 13 specialists and 171 beds in seven departments (Fig. 1). In particular, it has a 24-h emergency medical center. A total of 58,000 residents in the region were covered by its medical benefits. Unfortunately, economic difficulties led to its conversion into a nursing hospital by the end of February 2019, resulting in the closure of the region's only emergency medical center. Participants and procedures

All individuals assessed by EMS personnel as experiencing OHCA within the study reference period were considered eligible for study inclusion. The exclusion criteria were: (1) resuscitation withholding or withdrawal based on the presence of signs of death or a DNR order; (2) suspected traumatic arrest, encompassing cases involving trauma, intoxication, or drowning; (3) age younger than 18 years.

The data for this study were gathered from both prehospital and hospital stages to ensure a comprehensive understanding, given the absence of an integrated cardiac arrest registration system in the study region. Prehospital data were collected from the Ulsan Fire Agency headquarters, which electronically compiles information on every EMS dispatch, and prehospital cardiac arrest patient care reports are made in the case of OHCA. Hospital data were sourced from the EDs of all 17 receiving hospitals within the region.

Confirmation of cardiac arrest was based on the absence of signs of circulation by EMS personnel. The patient variables included age, sex, witnessed status, arrest location, and comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, liver disease, renal failure, and malignancy. Bystander-related variables included bystander CPR and automated external defibrillator (AED) use. EMS-related variables covered initial rhythm at the scene, types of advanced airway management (no advanced airway, tracheal intubation, and I-gel/supraglottic airway), mechanical chest compression device use, epinephrine administration, and EMS processing times (response time, scene time, and transport time). The response time interval (RTI) was defined as the duration from EMS dispatch to arrival at the scene. The scene time interval (STI) was the time from EMS’ arrival at the scene to their departure, and the transport time interval (TTI) was the time from EMS’ departure from the scene to arrival at the ED.

Hospital variables encompassed both survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurological outcomes, assessed until the point of discharge for all patients. Neurological outcomes were evaluated using cerebral performance category (CPC) scales at hospital discharge, where a CPC score of 1 or 2 indicated a favorable outcome [13,14]. This meticulous data collection methodology adhered to the Utstein-style reporting guidelines for OHCA [15].

We set the primary outcome of this study to compare both survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurological outcomes before and after ED closure. Our secondary outcome focused on following and comparing regional EMS changes before and after ED closure.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. We employed Pearson's Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Student t-tests for continuous numerical variables to assess differences between the two groups. For the comparison of the two groups, we identified differences not only in sample size but also in covariates due to COVID-19 in the last year of the study period. Accordingly, we conducted propensity score matching to balance covariates and reduce bias when comparing groups, utilizing a logistic regression model that considered all relevant covariates associated with OHCA survival outcomes [16]. These covariates included age, sex, witnessed status, arrest location, comorbidities, bystander CPR, bystander AED use, initial rhythm, types of advanced airway management, mechanical chest compression, epinephrine administration, RTI, STI, and TTI. Each patient in the before ED closure group was matched to a patient in the after ED closure group using a 1:1 matching ratio to achieve a balanced comparison between the two groups. Given the higher number of patients in the after ED closure group, we employed the greedy, nearest-neighbor method without replacement, using a caliper of 0.01 in the propensity score for the matching process. For additional analyses aimed at achieving a balance between the two groups, we conducted inverse probability weighting (IPW) matching, both with and without trimming [17,18]. Stabilized weights derived from the propensity scores were utilized in this process. Weights were trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles in the trimming IPW approach to mitigate the influence of extremely small and large weights. We conducted a subgroup analysis of the area covered by the closed ED (southern Ulju). We set statistical significance at an alpha of 0.05 and calculated all p-values as two-sided. We conducted data manipulation and statistical analyses using SAS softer version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

During the study periods, Ulsan recorded a total of 183,478 EMS calls. Among these, 4,472 patients were identified by EMS personnel as having sustained an OHCA. Resuscitation was withheld for 2,074 patients due to signs of death or DNR orders, while it was initiated for the remaining 2,398 patients. From this group, 84 patients were excluded due to confirmed death during resuscitation (resuscitation withdrawal). Subsequently, 2,314 patients were transferred to EDs. Of these, 602 patients were excluded due to presumed traumatic arrest (305 in the Before ED closure group and 297 in the After ED closure group), and 45 patients were excluded because they were younger than 18 years of age. Additionally, 55 patients were excluded due to missing data. Ultimately, the study included a total of 1,612 patients. The study population consisted of 692 patients before ED closure and 920 patients after ED closure (Fig. 2). The subgroup was comprised of 63 patients before ED closure and 73 patients after ED closure. Changes in the regional EMS

A comparison of EMS changes before and after ED closure revealed significant increases in the use of I-gel/supraglottic airway usage (51.2 % versus 78.8 %, p < 0.001) and mechanical chest compressions (10.7 % versus 49.0 %, p < 0.001). Epinephrine administration also significantly increased (9.1 % versus 13.6 %, p = 0.006). Both STI (12.7 ± 5.3 min versus 14.5 ± 5.5 min, p < 0.001) and TTI (5.8 ± 5.0 min versus 6.7 ± 5.5 min, p < 0.001) were longer after ED closure (Fig. 3, Table 1). The favorable neurological outcomes rates in before and after ED closure groups of the total study population were 6.9 % (48/692) and 6.4 % (59/920) (p = 0.676), respectively. The aOR and 95 % CI for favorable neurological outcomes was 2.845 (1.563–5.177, p = 0.001). In propensity score and IPW matching, there was a significant impact of ED closure in favorable neurological outcomes (aOR: 1.777, 95 % CI: 1.074–2.941, p = 0.025) and 1.782 (1.196–2.656, p = 0.005) (Table 3).

In the subgroup, the survival to hospital discharge rates before and after the ED closure were 28.6 % (18/63) and 19.2 % (14/73), respectively (p = 0.198). The favorable neurological outcomes rates were 7.4 % (5/63) before and 5.5 % (4/73) after the closure (p = 0.733). Due to the limited sample size, a logistic regression model could not be derived.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of an ED closure on the EMS system and the survival outcomes of OHCA patients. We compared survival to hospital discharge rates and favorable neurological outcomes of adult nontraumatic OHCA patients before and after the closure of an ED in Ulsan, South Korea. Key findings include: 1) a significant increase in TTI across the total study population, with nearly a twofold increase observed in the affected subgroups, 2) while no significant difference was found in survival to hospital discharge, favorable neurological outcomes improved significantly after adjusting for confounding factors.

Research on the effects of ED closures is relatively scarce, likely due to challenges in data collection after such event. A U.S. study found that closing Boston Medical Center's ED lead to increased EMS turnaround times and decreased ED volume [19]. Another U.S. study reported that a hospital closure resulted in increased homeless patient visits to nearby EDs, with longer ED times [20]. Similarly, a Japanese study reported that after the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant accident, emergency transport times were longer in former evacuation zones [21]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to analysis survival outcomes in OHCA patients following an ED closure.

Our findings align with previous studies showing that ED closure significantly affects EMS transport times [19,21]. Specifically, the TTI for the subgroup nearly doubled after the ED closure (9.2 ± 7.9 min vs. 16.2 ± 6.5 min, p < 0.001), indicating that EMS personnel had to transport OHCA patients to more distant EDs. In contrast, the increase in TTI for the total study population was about 1 min (5.8 ± 5.0 min vs. 6.7 ± 5.5 min, p < 0.001), which we attribute primarily to the impact of COVID-19 rather than the ED closure itself. This is supported by a study that reported a similar 1-min delay in TTIs in Korea following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic [22].

The increase in STI for the total study population after the ED closure may be linked to the COVID-19 pandemic, which coincided with the closure. During this time, EMS personnel were advised to minimize direct patient contact, leading to a preference for mechanical compression over manual compression and I-gel use over tracheal intubation. Additionally, changes in guidelines for prehospital epinephrine administration from September 2020 resulted in increased epinephrine use at the scene, likely contributing to the increase in STIs.

In contrast, RTI remained stable before and after the ED closure, suggesting that EMS response times were unaffected by the closure and the COVID-19 pandemic. This stability may be attributed to the low incidence of OHCA in the Ulju area. Over two years following the ED closure, Ulsan recorded a total of 920 OHCA cases (1.2 cases per day), while the Ulju area had only 73 cases (0.1 cases per day). The low OHCA incidence in Ulju meant that the increased TTI did not necessitate additional coverage from other regions.

The closure of the ED in Ulsan did not significantly impact survival to hospital discharge, even after adjusting for confounding factors. This outcome can be attributed to three factors: First, the lower OHCA incidence in the Ulju area compared to the total study population meant that increased TTI had a limited effect on survival rates. This may have contributed to the stable survival rates across the entire study population before and after the closure. Second, while TTI is a significant factor, other variables also influence OHCA survival. Although we accounted for many prehospital variables known to affect survival [[23], [24], [25]], some unmeasured factors, such as post-resuscitation care and the quality of bystander chest compressions, may have impacted outcomes. Third, there may have been redistribution and absorption effects, with EMS teams and EDs at regional boundaries potentially redirecting patients. This could have been supported by the observed decrease in 10.13039/100004821OHCA incidence in southern Ulju. Indeed, the number of OHCA patients in Ulju decreased by 23.7 % from 38 to 29 in the first year after the closure. This decline seems unlikely to be a natural occurrence, given the overall increase in OHCA cases in Ulsan during the study period. Other studies have also noted increased patient volumes and longer lengths of stay at nearby EDs following an ED closure, leading to more patients leaving without being seen [26].

Although survival to hospital discharge did not show significant differences after the ED closure, neurological outcomes improved statistically, even after adjusting for confounding factors. This improvement might be related to the profile of patients who were able to reach the hospital after the closure, suggesting potential selection bias where more severe cases, less likely to survive extended transport, were excluded from the hospital discharge statistics.

Several limitations should be noted. As a retrospective observational study, it is subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounders. The reliance on existing records and the unique nature of the ED closure event further complicate replication in a prospective design. Additionally, the limited sample size, particularly in subgroups, may have affected the detection of significant differences. We included all cases in the Ulsan area but could not extend the study period to cover more cases from the Ulju area, introducing complexities such as confounding variables and local EMS adaptations. Internal and external changes within the EMS system, such as the introduction of prehospital epinephrine use in late 2019 and the COVID-19 pandemic, were not directly accounted for and may have influenced the results. Although we used statistical methods to adjust for these changes, caution is advised in interpreting the results due to their dynamic nature. Moreover, this study's population did not include trauma patients. While trauma patients might benefit significantly from rapid transport and in-hospital treatment, A total of 602 traumatic OHCA patients (305 in the Before ED closure group and 297 in the After ED closure group) were excluded from our analysis because they follow different trauma center transport guidelines compared to non-traumatic OHCA patients. Future research should explore the outcomes of traumatic cardiac arrests in the context of rapid transport to provide further insights. Finally, while this study's findings are based on the Ulsan region, they may be relevant to other similar EMS systems, particularly in the Asia-Pacific region. Generalizing these findings should be approached with caution, but the study can serve as a valuable reference for similar healthcare settings within the region.

Conclusions

In summary, our study revealed that the closure of an ED in Ulsan did not significantly affect the overall survival to hospital discharge among adult nontraumatic OHCA patients, nor did it substantially delay EMS transport time in the entire EMS system. However, we observed a statistically significant transport time increase in the subgroup, which was immediately covered by the closed ED, although the limited sample size in this subgroup precluded a comprehensive comparison of survival outcomes. The findings suggest that while the overall system may remain stable, subgroups could be disproportionately affected by changes in healthcare infrastructures. These observations highlight the importance of the continuous monitoring of survival outcomes and EMS transport times. Ongoing and detailed follow-up is vital to fully understand and address the long-term effects of an ED closure on community health and the effectiveness of the EMS system.
