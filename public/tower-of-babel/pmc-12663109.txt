Reciprocal control of metabolic and chromatin regulators improves rice tolerance to heat
Authors: Yaping Yue, Biao Liu, Qiutao Xu, Zhengting Chen, Jing Wang, Yu Zhao, Dao-Xiu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant metabolic activities are regulated to adapt to the fluctuating environment for optimized growth, while interplay between metabolic and chromatin pathways plays an essential role in environmental adaptation. However, how metabolic and chromatin regulators cooperate to control metabolite accumulation and gene expression required for stress tolerance remains unclear. Here, we show that the pyruvate kinase 1 (PK1) has a function to integrate stress signals for metabolic and epigenetic controls of heat tolerance in rice. Over-expression of PK1 enhances plant tolerance to heat, while its loss-of-function decreases the recovery rate from heat stress. Heat stress induces PK1 production, nuclear enrichment, lysine acetylation and activity for pyruvate accumulation, H3T11 phosphorylation (H3T11p) and H3K9 acetylation (H3K9ac), and gene expression. In addition, PK1 phosphorylates General control non-repressed protein 5 (GCN5) and stimulates its activity for H3K9ac. Conversely, under heat stress GCN5 enhances PK1 lysine acetylation and enhances its activity for H3T11p and pyruvate production. The PK1 and GCN5-controlled H3T11p and H3K9ac are required for heat stress-responsive gene expression. These results establish PK1 as key player linking metabolic and chromatin pathways and uncover a mutually stimulating mechanism between metabolic and chromatin regulators for stress tolerance in rice.

Introduction

High temperatures seriously decrease crop yield. It is estimated that each Celsius degree increase in global average temperature causes 3–8% reduction of crop yield1. Reinforcing crops tolerance to heat is an essential aspect to sustain food production in the global warming era. Studies in model plants have identified a range of molecular and cellular mechanisms to cope with heat stress, including protein folding/modifications, stress granule formation, chromatin modification and activation of heat stress-related genes as well as alteration of protein translation and metabolism2–4. Chromatin modifications, such as histone acetylation and methylation and histone variant H2A.Z deposition, play a critical role in heat stress-responsive gene expression5–7. Enzymes involved in chromatin dynamics, such as chromatin remodeling complexes, histone methyltransferases and demethylases, histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and deacetylases (HDACs), are key players in the process8–10. HAT and HDAC are not only involved in epigenetic regulation of stress-responsive gene expression but also control acetylation of many non-histone proteins of diverse cellular pathways11–15. For instance, rice GCN5 HAT acetylates the adaptor protein ADA2 affecting its stability and finally the HAT activity of the complex in high energy metabolism conditions14. Rice GCN5 together with the HDAC protein HDA705 controls the lysine acetylation level of the m6A RNA “reader” protein OsECT3 affecting its binding activity to m6A RNA targets under cold stress16. In addition, rice HDACs OsSRT1 could deacetylate glycolytic glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) affecting its moonlighting function as transcriptional regulator under oxidative stress12. Rice HDAC-mediated deacetylation of ribosomal proteins is required for an efficient translational elongation process17. Recent results showed that heat-stress causes a general decrease of the overall acetylation level in many proteins including metabolic enzymes, ribosomal proteins and SG marker proteins in rice, which is at least partly dependent on HDA714, a heat stress-responsive and cytoplasm-localized HDAC13,17. This suggests that change in acetylation levels of cellular proteins is a molecular response to heat stress, which may affect their functions in SG formation, metabolic activity, protein quality control, and translation for tolerance to heat stress.

In plants, metabolic activities are regulated to adapt to the fluctuating environment for optimized growth. For instance, glycolysis is induced by diverse biotic and abiotic stresses including heat stress in plants11–13,18–21. It is suggested that enhanced glycolysis is important to provide additional energy and scavengers of reactive oxygen species, such as pyruvate and NADH, for stress tolerance20. Activities of glycolytic and TCA enzymes are also detected in the nucleus, where they produce metabolic intermediates and exercise moonlight functions to control chromatin modification and gene activation22–25. Several glycolytic and TCA enzymes have been shown to accumulate in the nucleus of plant cells under stress12,26,27. For instance, GAPDH is enriched in the nucleus in rice cells under oxidative stress and has a function to upregulate glycolysis enzyme gene expression12. Pyruvate kinase (PK) that converts phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate is detected in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus and functions as a protein kinase that can phosphorylate histone H3 threonine 11 (H3T11) in yeast28 and mammalian cells29 and recently in Arabidopsis26. Pyruvate produced in the nucleus or imported from cytoplasm is converted by pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHC) to acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) that is required for histone acetylation in mammalian and plant cells30–32. Recent results showed that PDHC is enriched in the nucleus to regulate histone acetylation and gene transcription in response to ethylene signaling in Arabidopsis31. Apparently, there is interplay between primary metabolism and chromatin dynamics in controlling plant responses to the adverse environments22,33, while the precise mechanisms remain unclear.

In this paper we report that rice pyruvate kinase 1, PK1, is a key player in the metabolic and epigenetic control of plant tolerance to heat stress. First, PK1 activity is induced by heat and is responsible for heat-induced increases of pyruvate and downstream acetyl-CoA production. Second, heat enhances PK1 nuclear enrichment, H3T11 phosphorylation and heat-responsive gene activation. Third, PK1 could phosphorylate GCN5 and increase its histone acetyltransferase activity for H3K9ac. Conversely, GCN5 could acetylate PK1 and enhance its activity for both pyruvate/acetyl-CoA production and H3T11 phosphorylation. Collectively, the results establish PK1 as a key regulator at the nexus of metabolic and chromatin pathways conferring heat tolerance in rice and uncover mechanistic details of interplay between metabolic and chromatin regulators to control metabolite accumulation and gene expression during plant response to stress.

Results

PK1 is required for heat tolerance in rice

There are 10 PK genes in rice34 (Supplementary Fig. 1a), some of which (PK1: LOC_Os11g05110, PK2: LOC_Os07g08340, PK3: LOC_Os04g58110, PK4: LOC_Os12g05110, and PK5: LOC_Os11g10980) have been functionally characterized35–38. Among these genes, PK1 appears to be more ubiquitously expressed in different rice tissues/organs or developmental stages35 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). RT-qPCR analysis indicated that the expression levels of PK1 and PK3 are the highest among the 5 PK genes and that PK1 is induced by stresses including heat, salt, drought and cold (Supplementary Fig. 1c). This is consistent with previous results showing that PK1 is upregulated by ABA treatment39, suggesting that PK1 may play a role in rice stress response. To test the hypothesis, we produced two independent PK1 knockout (KO) lines (pk1#1 and pk1#2) using CRISPR technology (Supplementary Fig. 1d). The pk1 lines displayed a reduced plant height and seed setting rates (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f), as previously reported35,37. To confirm that the phenotypes were due to the KO of PK1, we retransformed the pk1 lines with FLAG-HA-tagged PK1 gene controlled by its own promoter (2.5 kb upstream of the start codon) (Supplementary Fig. 1g) and found that the mutant phenotypes were complemented (Supplementary Fig. 1h). To test whether PK1 was involved in plant tolerance to stress, we treated wild type, KO and complementation plants with cold (4 °C), salt (180 mM NaCl), PEG6000 (20%) and heat (42 °C) stress for 4 days before transferring to normal conditions for 7 days. Survey of plant survival rates revealed that pk1 plants displayed no clear difference from the wild type under cold, salt or PEG stress (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). However, under heat stress the pk1 plants showed a much-reduced survival rate, while the complementation lines behaved as the wild type plants (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). By comparison, pk2 (Os07g0181000) mutants36 displayed no difference from the wild type after heat treatment (Supplementary Figs. 2d, 3d). In addition, the pk1 plants showed a reduced plant height and a lower seed setting rate and produced seeds with an apparent aspect of chalkiness that is prone to be induced by heat35,37(Supplementary Fig. 1e, f, i). To further study the function of PK1 in heat stress tolerance, we over-expressed PK1 in transgenic rice plants (Supplementary Fig. 1j) and found that the OE plants displayed enhanced tolerance to heat (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3c). Nuclear enrichment of PK1 is accountable for rice tolerance to heat stress

Consistent with heat-induced PK1 transcription, the HA tagged-PK1 protein level in the complementation plants was augmented to about 2-3 folds after 4 h heat treatment (Fig. 1d). The tagged-PK1 protein was detected in both cytosolic and nuclear fractions and heat stress mainly enriched the PK1 level in the nuclear fraction (Fig. 1e). Transient expression in rice protoplasts and tobacco leaf epidermal cells further confirmed the cyto-nuclear localization of PK1 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). PK3 that is closely related to PK1 also showed a cyto-nuclear localization in the transient expression assays (Supplementary Fig. 4b). To confirm the heat-induced nuclear enrichment of PK1, we produced Ubipro::PK1-GFP transgenic rice plants and analyzed the GFP signal in root elongation zone cells under normal and heat stress conditions. The GFP signals were detected in both cytoplasm and the nucleus but clearly enhanced in the nucleus under heat stress (Fig. 1f). We further analyzed the cytosolic and nuclear fractions isolated from Ubipro::PK1-GFP transgenic plants by immunoblotting and observed an increase of PK1-GFP level in the nuclear fraction under heat stress (Fig. 1g). Under normal conditions, the pk1 mutations caused a decrease of PK enzymatic activity by 30–40% in total and nuclear fractions compared with wild type plants (Fig. 2a), consistent with the ubiquitous and high-level expression of PK1 in rice. The PK activities in total and nuclear fractions were clearly induced by heat stress in wild type plants, while the inductions did not occur in the pk1 mutants (Fig. 2a). Similarly, the pk1 mutations resulted in clear decreases of total and nuclear pyruvate levels under normal temperatures, which were elevated in the wild type but not pk1 plants under heat stress (Fig. 2b). Pyruvate can be converted into acetyl-CoA by pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH). Tests of acetyl-CoA levels in the plants under normal and heat conditions obtained similar results to pyruvate (Fig. 2c). We also compared PK activity and acetyl-CoA levels in the nuclear fraction isolated from wild type and PK1-overexpression (OE) plants and found higher levels in the OE plants compared to wild type (Fig. 2d). PK1 is required for heat-induced histone H3T11p

It is shown that PK can phosphorylate histone H3 threonine 11 (H3T11) in mammalian and yeast cells28,29,40,41 and recently in Arabidopsis26. Analysis of rice plants by immunoblotting revealed a clear decrease (>50%) of H3T11 phosphorylation (H3T11p) and, to a lesser extent, H3 serine 10 phosphorylation (H3S10p) in the pk1 mutants compared to wild type plants (Fig. 3a). By contrast, the mutations of the plastidic PK2 (pk2-1, pk2-2) had no clear effect on H3T11p (Fig. 3b). Pull-down and co-immuno-precipitation (Co-IP) assays indicated that PK1 could interact with histone H3 protein in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 3c, d). E. coli- produced PK1 protein had a H3T11 and H3S10 kinase activity in in vitro assays with phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) as phosphate donor (Fig. 3e). These results together indicated that PK1 was a bona feta histone H3T11 (as well as H3S10) kinase in rice. PK1 stimulates GCN5 activity to enhance H3K9ac under heat stress

To test whether the pk1 mutations affected other chromatin modifications, we analyzed histone lysine acetylation and found that H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac) was reduced in the pk1 mutants compared to wild type plants, while no clear changes were observed for the other analyzed histone lysine residues (H3K14, H3K23, H3K27, H4K5, and H4K16) in the mutants (Fig. 4a). We found that the overall H3K9ac in rice plants was also augmented by heat stress, but the pk1 mutation eliminated the heat-induced H3K9ac (Fig. 4b). These observations indicated that PK1 was required for both the basal and heat-induced H3K9ac. To study whether PK1-dependent H3K9ac involved H3T11p, we expressed FLAG-tagged H3 protein and a version of H3 with T11A point mutation (H3T11A) together with or without GFP-tagged PK1 in transfected tobacco leaf cells. Immunoblotting analysis of purified FLAG-tagged H3 proteins revealed an increase of H3K9ac in the presence of GFP-tagged PK1 (Fig. 4c), while the H3T11A mutant showed lower H3K9ac than wild type H3 and the presence of PK1-GFP had no effect on H3K9ac of the mutant protein (Fig. 4c). As GCN5 is a major histone acetyltransferase for nucleosomal H3K9ac in rice as well as in other organism42–46 and located in nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 4c), we tested whether PK1 could phosphorylate and stimulate GCN5 activity in rice. First, protein structural prediction suggested that PK1 and GCN5 could be interacting partners (Supplementary Fig. 5a), which was confirmed by SLC (split-luciferase complementation) in transient transfected tobacco leaf cells, in vitro pull-down, and in vivo Co-IP from rice cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b–e). Second, Phos-tag PAGE analysis indicated that FLAG-HA-tagged GCN5 protein produced in transgenic rice plants was phosphorylated (Fig. 5a), and mass spectrometry analysis identified at least 4 phosphorylation sites (T101, S119, T136 and T271) within GCN5 protein produced in transiently transfected plant cells (Supplementary Data 1). Third, GCN5 showed a lower phosphorylation level when expressed in pk1 protoplasts than in wild type (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, in vitro kinase assays confirmed that PK1 could phosphorylate GCN5 and the Thr to Ala point mutation at T271 that is localized in the HAT domain and is conserved in different species (Supplementary Fig. 6a) clearly reduced the GCN5 phosphorylation level by PK1 (Fig. 5c), indicating that T271 is one of the PK-dependent phosphorylation sites. In addition, immunoblotting with a Phospho-threonine antibody revealed that GCN5 threonine phosphorylation level was increased in rice plants under heat stress (Fig. 5d), consistent with the heat-induced nuclear enrichment of PK1 (Fig. 1e). To test whether PK1-mediated GCN5 phosphorylation enhanced its HAT activity at H3K9ac, we co-transfected tagged GCN5 (by mCherry), PK1 (by GFP) and histone H3 (by FLAG) vectors into tobacco leaf cells. After immuno-purification, we analyzed acetylation and/or phosphorylation levels of tagged H3 and/or GCN5 proteins by immunoblotting. When co-transfected with PK1-GFP, H3-FLAG showed enhanced level of H3T11p and H3K9ac but not H3K14ac (Fig. 5e), confirming the above observations (Figs. 3 and 4a). When co-transfected with GCN5-mCherry, H3-FLAG showed increased levels of H3K9ac and H3K14ac as expected, while H3T11p level was not clearly changed. Co-transfection with PK1-GFP and GCN5-mCherry together further increased H3K9ac and H3T11p levels of H3-FLAG, whereas GCN5-mCherry showed a higher phosphorylation level in the presence of PK1-GFP (Fig. 5e). Discussion

PK1 is a key player in rice tolerance to heat stress

In this work we provide evidence that pyruvate kinase plays a key role in rice tolerance to heat stress. Among the rice PK genes, PK1 is solely responsible for the heat-induced increases of PK activity, pyruvate production, and H3T11p. Likely, PK1 function in conferring heat stress-tolerance is mainly relied on its function in the nucleus, as heat stress induced the nuclear accumulation of PK1 and its activity for H3T11p. This is also supported by the observations that the increases of PK1 activity and pyruvate levels in the nucleus induced by heat stress were close or equivalent to those in the total cellular extracts (Fig. 2). The present results rather support that PK1-mediated H3T11p and gene activation confer plant tolerance to heat stress, while the increases of pyruvate level may also contribute to the process. Pyruvate is not only a source for energy production but also can be converted to acetyl-CoA (required for histone acetylation) and NADH+ (a scavenger of reactive oxygen species). Consistent with this is that pk1 mutations cause oxidative stress in rice plants39. The heat-induced nuclear enrichment of PK1 appears not at the detriment of the cytoplasmic pool, as similar cytoplasmic PK1 protein levels were observed before and after heat stress (Fig. 1e). Thus, a role of cytoplasmic PK1 in heat tolerance is not excluded, especially when considering that PK1 is found in heat-induced stress granules (SGs) in the cytoplasm13. The release of PK1 from SGs may also contribute to plant recovery from the stress by sustaining glycolysis. In addition, the induction of PK1 by other stresses (Supplementary Fig. 1c) suggests that PK1 may be generally involved in rice stress tolerance.

PK1 is the heat stress-responsive H3T11 kinase in rice

PK is shown to phosphorylate histone H3T11 in mammalian and yeast cells28,29,40,41, and recently in Arabidopsis26. In mammalian cells, the tumor-specific pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) mediates H3T11p upon EGF receptor activation and subsequent H3K9ac for EGF-induced gene expression, tumor cell proliferation, and tumorigenesis. The present data showing that PK1, but not PK2, is required for H3T11p indicates that not all rice PKs are involved in H3T11p. As the H3T11p could be still detected in pk1 plants by immunoblot; PK1 may not be the only H3T11 kinase in rice. However, the observation that heat-induced H3T11p is abolished in pk1 mutant indicates that PK1 is the heat-responsive kinase for H3T11p in rice. The requirement of PK1 for heat-induced H3T11p, gene activation and stress tolerance enrich the current view of chromatin dynamics for heat-stress responses in plants. It was shown in mammalian cells that H3T11p is required for the dissociation of a histone deacetylase (HDAC3) from the cell proliferation gene promoters to increase H3K9ac for gene activity29. Although it is not excluded that a similar mechanism may exist in plant cells, the present results support that PK1 enhances GCN5 HAT activity to promote H3K9ac in rice cells. Pyruvate is a key metabolite for chromatin modifications54. The requirement of PK1 for heat-induced pyruvate accumulation (Fig. 2b) implies a role of PK1 in metabolic control of chromatin modification for heat tolerance in rice. It is worth noting that the pk1 mutation reduces the overall H3T11p and H3K9ac under normal conditions (Figs. 3a and 4a), which suggests that PK1-mediated chromatin modifications may also affect rice gene expression for growth and development.

Mutual stimulation of PK1 and GCN5 for stress tolerance in rice

GCN5 has been shown to be a main HAT involved in stress-response in plants14,44,61. GCN5 functions with a set of adaptor proteins such as ADA2 to enhance its HAT activity to acetylate histones at several lysine residues in chromatin62–64, especially when acetyl-CoA becomes limited in plant cells14. The results that GCN5 is phosphorylated by PK1 (Fig. 5b, c) and that the phosphorylation is stimulated by heat stress and enhances GCN5 HAT activity at H3K9 (Fig. 5d, e), uncover an additional layer of regulation of GCN5 function in stress response in plants and support the conclusion that PK1-mediated H3T11p affects H3K9ac by stimulating GCN5. This is reminiscent of yeast protein kinase C1 (Pkc1) that phosphorylates the histone acetyltransferase Rtt109 and promotes its ability to acetylate H3K5665. Similarly, AMPK/Snf1 can phosphorylate the H3S10 (in yeast) and H2BS36 (in mammalian) which activate specific histone acetyltransferases (HAT), consequently enhancing histone acetylation66.

Our data show that under heat stress GCN5 in turn acetylates and enhances PK1 activity to phosphorylate H3T11. Co-regulation by acetylation and phosphorylation has been reported to control cell-division cycle (CDC)-6 protein activity required for S-phase progression67. GCN5 specifically acetylates CDC6 at three lysine residues flanking its cyclin-docking motif, and this modification is crucial for the subsequent phosphorylation of the protein by Cyclin A-CDKs at a specific residue close to the acetylation site. It was shown that histone H3 acetylation and phosphorylation have synergistic effects in response to epidermal growth factor stimulation68. Crosstalk between histone H3 phosphorylation and acetylation may generate a histone code for gene transcription69,70.

Previous results showed that rice PK1 is acetylated at multiple lysine residues and heat stress reduced the overall acetylation levels of whole cellular PK1, while several PK1 lysine residues displayed higher acetylation under the stress13. Thus, acetylation at different lysine residues of PK1 may respond differently to heat stress and may be controlled by different mechanisms, as observed in mammalian PKM271–73. The heat-enhanced acetylation of nuclear PK1 (Fig. 7a) indicates that PK1 acetylation is regulated differently between the cytoplasm and the nucleus in response to heat stress. Previous data showed that under heat stress, cytoplasmic PK1 is de-acetylated by HDA714 and is assembled into stress granules13. The present results indicate that nuclear PK1 acetylation is induced by heat stress, which is at least partly dependent on GCN5. The effects of lysine acetylation on protein function are diverse, which is likely related to position of acetylated lysine residues within the target proteins10,74. For instance, acetylation of PKM2 at K305 by PCAF HAT leads to PKM2 degradation by autophagy, while acetylation of PKM2 at K433 by p300 HAT promotes PKM2 nuclear translocation and stimulates its activity to phosphorylate H3T11 and regulates the transcription of senescence-associated genes71.

The effects of point mutations at three lysine residues (K297, K385, and K503 acetylated by GCN5 in vitro) on PK1 activity for both H3T11p and pyruvate production (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 7c) together with that of GCN5 RNAi on heat-induced PK activities for pyruvate accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b) and H3T11p (Fig. 6b), support the assumption that GCN5-mediated PK1 acetylation in the nucleus stimulates the PK1 activity for pyruvate production and chromatin modifications to confer tolerance for heat stress. Pyruvate accumulation can be converted to acetyl-CoA by PDH in the nucleus, which in turn supports HAT activity for histone acetylation and chromatin remodeling30,31,75. Collectively, the results reveal a reciprocal control between PK1 and GCN5 for chromatin modification, gene expression and metabolism to confer tolerance to heat stress in rice (Fig. 9).Fig. 9Interplay between PK1 and GCN5 conferring rice tolerance to heat stress.Under normal conditions, besides its glycolytic activity to convert phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate, PK1 also phosphorylates histone H3T11 and enhances H3K9 acetylation mainly through phosphorylating and stimulating GCN5 to support plant growth. Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

In this study, the rice variety Zhonghua11 (Oryza sativa ssp. Japonica) was used to produce the transgenic plants. Tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) plants used for transient expression were grown in soil for six weeks at 20 °C ± 2 °C, 8 h photoperiod, and 100 µmol quanta/ (m2 s) illumination. For phenotyping, rice plants were hydroponically cultivated in liquid Yoshida’s solution76. Various stress treatments were applied to seedlings at the early developmental stage13. For heat stress, 14-day-old seedlings grown hydroponically under a 25 °C/14 h light and 10 h dark cycles were transferred to a 42 °C chamber for 4 or 6 days. For cold stress, seedlings were incubated at 4 °C in a growth chamber for 4 days. For salt and osmotic stresses, NaCl (180 mM) and PEG6000 (20%) were added to the nutrient solution for 4 days, respectively. For the controls, seedlings were grown under normal conditions at 25 °C/14 h light and 25 °C/10 h dark. After stress treatments, seedlings were returned to normal conditions for 7 days before surveying survival rates based on the percentage of viable seedlings. The survival rates (live plants/total plants ×100%) after recovery were surveyed in 3 biological replicates (n = 40 for each replicate). To identify rice thermotolerance at the reproductive stage, rice plants were grown in the Wuhan area during the summer rice growing season and the phenotypes were assayed in 2022 and 2023, respectively.

Rice transgenic plants and vector construction

For the pk1 mutants, two independent transgenic lines (in ZH11) were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system described in77. CRIPSR-P (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) was used for sgRNA design and two sgRNA targets were selected for OsPK1: Ospk1#1, CCCTTGAAGCGAATGGCACTGTT; Ospk1#2: CCCTGCATTGTTCTCAGATTCAT. The Gibson Assembly method (Pme I digestion) was applied to assemble the sgRNA oligos into the CRIPSR system. The pk2, ada2 and GCN5 RNAi plants used in this study were characterized in previous study14,36,43, respectively. The PK1 overexpression lines were generated by fusing FLAG or GFP tag to the C-terminus of the coding sequences, driven by the ubiquitin promoter. The pk1 complementation lines were obtained by the transformation of pk1 mutants with the vector proPK1::OsPK1 genome-3×FLAG-2×HA. The GCN5 OE lines OXGCN5#3 was generated by transforming a Ubipro::GCN5-CDS-2×FLAG-2×HA construct into ZH11.

Total protein and Histone extraction for immunoblotting

To extract total proteins, the rice seedlings grown under heat stress and normal conditions were first collected and ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen and then homogenized in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40). The suspension was then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Add one third of the volume of 4×SDS loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 40% glycerol, 5% SDS, 0.025% [W/V] bromophenol blue, 20% [V/V] β-mercaptoethanol) was added to the supernatant and subsequently heated at 95 °C for 10 min. The extracted total proteins were then used for immunoblotting analysis, using anti-Actin (1:4000, Sangon, D191048-0100) and anti-HA (1:5000, ABclonal, AE036) antibodies.

The EpiQuik Total Histone Extraction Kit (Epigentek USA, OP-0006-100) was used to extract total histone from 14-day-old seedlings of wild type (WT), pk1, pk2 and GCN5 RNAi plants. The extracted histone proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting with the following antibodies: anti-H3K9ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-352), anti-H3K14ac (1:1000, Abcam, ab52946), anti-H3K23ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-355), anti-H3K27ac (1:1000, Abcam, ab4729), anti-H3 (1:1000, Abcam, ab1791), anti-H3Kac (1:1000, Millipore, 17-615), anti-H4 (1:1000, Abcam, ab177840), anti-H4K5ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-327), anti-H4K16ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-329), anti-H4Kac (1:1000, Millipore, 06-598), anti-H3T11p (1:2000, Abcam, ab133457), anti-H3S10p (1:2000, ABclonal, AP0840), and peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:10000, Abbkine, A21020). Immunoblotting results were quantified by ImageJ (v1.8.0_172).

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractionation

For the nucleo-cytoplasmic fractionation, 14-day-old pk1 complementation seedlings or Ubipro::PK1-GFP transgenic plants (hydroponic culture in 1× nutrient solution) were transferred to 42 °C for 4 h or in normal conditions and collected after transfer. Samples were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in lysis buffer (0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM PMSF, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) containing one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 11697498001). The homogenates were then filtered through two layers of Miracloth (Millipore, 475855), followed by centrifugation at 1200 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting pellet was washed in washing buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 % Triton X-100, 0.1 mM PMSF, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail) and kept as the nuclear fraction. The supernatant was centrifuged again at 16,000 g for 15 min to remove cellular debris at 4 °C and was kept as the cytoplasmic fraction. The separated fractions were detected by immunoblotting using anti-H3 (1:1000, Abcam, ab1791) and anti-Actin (1:4000, Sangon, D191048-0100) as loading control.

Subcellular localization assays

For PK1 subcellular localization in Ubipro::PK1-GFP transgenic plants. 1 cm of rice root tips were collected and pressed into temporary slides for observation. For PK1, PK3 and GCN5 subcellular localization in rice protoplasts or tobacco leaf cells. plasmids containing 35Spro::RFP-FIB2, 35Spro::PK1-GFP, 35Spro::PK3-GFP and 35Spro::GCN5-CFP fragments were transformed by the PEG method into rice shoot cell protoplasts isolated from 14-day-old wild type seedlings as described previously12 or infiltrated into the abaxial epidermis of 6-week-old tobacco (N. benthamiana) leaves. After 12 or 72 h of transfection, Fluorescence signals were captured with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200).

PK Activity Assays and Pyruvate and Acetyl-CoA Measurement

The PK activities were determined using commercial kits (Jining Biotechnology, JN78500)13. His-tagged PK1 or PK1-3KR proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells and purified by Ni-NTA agarose beads (ABclonal, AS045), then 12 μg protein was used to test the PK activity. For rice plants, 0.1 g of 14-day-old green tissues or nuclei fractions extracted from 1 g seedling were homogenized in 1 mL or 2 mL of enzyme extraction buffer and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 minutes. The supernatants were collected to test the PK activity, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 340 nm using a multimode microplate reader (Spark, TECAN).

Pyruvate and acetyl-CoA content between samples were measured as follows: 0.1 g of 14-day-old green tissues or nuclei fractions extracted from 1 g of compared seedling samples in 1 or 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.0), as described in ref. 78. The suspensions were vortexed and then placed at 25 °C for 20 min before being centrifuged at 16,873 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for pyruvate and acetyl-CoA content measurements using the Plant Pyruvate ELISA Kit (Shanghai Jining Biotechnology, JN594008) or the Plant acetyl-CoA assay ELISA kit (Ji Ning Biotech, JN709212), respectively.

In vitro and in vivo PK protein kinase assays

In vitro PK protein kinase assays were performed with SUMO-His-tagged PK1 on GST-tagged H3, GST-tagged GCN5, GST-tagged GCN5T271A. The recombinant proteins were produced in E.coli BL21 and purified with GST agarose (ABclonal, AS044) and Ni-NTA agarose (ABclonal, AS045) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then the assay was performed by incubating 1.2 mg of OsPK1-SUMO-6×His with 300 µg of the substrate OsH3-GST or OsGCN5-GST/GCN5T271A-GST in 60 µL kinase reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM DTT, 1% glycerol, 5 mM FBP, 5 mM PEP) for 1 h at 25 °C. The mixtures were supplemented with 20 µL 4×SDS loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 40% glycerol, 5% SDS, 0.025% [W/V] bromophenol blue, 20% [V/V] β-mercaptoethanol) before being heated at 95 °C for 10 min and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-GST (1:5000, Abcam, ab19256), anti-His (1:4000, Abcam, ab9108), anti-H3S10p (1:2000, ABclonal, AP0840), anti-H3T11p (1:2000, Abcam, ab133457) and anti-Phos-threonine (1:1000, ABclonal, AP1422) antibodies.

For the protein kinase assays in vivo, FLAG-tagged histone H3 and H3-T11A vectors were transformed into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaf epidermal cells, either alone or in combination with PK1-GFP or PK13KR-GFP. After 72 h of transfection, the tobacco leaves were collected and ground into powder using liquid nitrogen. Then the proteins were extracted with lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP40) and purified with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, M8823). After three washes with TBST buffer, the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected with anti-GFP (1:1000, Abcam, ab290), anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165), anti-H3K9ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-352), anti-H3T11p (1:2000, Abcam, ab133457) antibodies.

In vivo GCN5 phosphorylation assays

Immunoblot analysis of the phosphorylation of GCN5 in Ubipro::GCN5-2×FLAG-2×HA transgenic rice plants under normal or heat stress conditions. GCN5 proteins were precipitated from nuclear extracts using anti-FLAG M2 beads (Sigma M8823). The CIAP (Takara 2250 A) treatments were conducted by adding CIAP with their reaction buffer to the precipitated proteins as described by the manufacturer and then detected by Phos-tag gel with anti-HA (1:5000, ABclonal, AE036) antibody or separated by SDS-PAGE with an anti-Phos-threonine antibody (1:1000, ABclonal, AP1422).

For GCN5 phosphorylation assays in rice protoplasts, 35Spro::GCN5-FLAG construct was transfected into rice protoplasts isolated from wild type (WT) and pk1 mutants for 14 h, then the proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, M8823) at 4 °C for 5 h. After three washes with washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA), the immunoprecipitated proteins were treated with CIAP (Takara 2250 A) with their reaction buffer as described by the manufacturer and then separated by SDS-PAGE with anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165) and anti-Phos-threonine (1:1000, ABclonal, AP1422) antibodies.

In vitro histone acetyltransferase activity assays

For in vitro histone acetyl transferase activity assays, vectors of GCN5-mCherry, PK1-GFP and H3-3×FLAG were transformed into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaf epidermal cells. After 72 h of transfection, the transfected tobacco leaves were collected and ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Then the H3 and GCN5 proteins were extracted with lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) and purified with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, M8823) or anti-mCherry M2 magnetic beads (ABMagic, MA111-25T). After three washes with TBST buffer, the immunoprecipitated H3 protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected with anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165), anti-H3T11p (1:2000, Abcam, ab133457), anti-H3K9ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-352) and anti-H3K14ac (1:1000, Abcam, ab52946) antibodies. The purified GCN5 Protein was analyzed by anti-mCherry (1:2000, ABclonal, AE171) and anti-Phos-threonine (1:1000, ABclonal, AP1422) antibodies.

Phylogenetic tree construction and protein structural modeling

The PK proteins from rice were aligned with ClustalX (v2.0) in this study. The MEGA (v7.0) program was used to conduct phylogenetic analysis and the Neighbor-Joining method was used to estimate evolutionary distances.

Protein sequences of rice PK1-PK5 were obtained from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (https://rice.uga.edu/). Homologues of rice GCN5 in other species were identified by blasting the protein sequences in the Universal Protein Database (https://www.uniprot.org/). For sequence alignment, the protein sequences of PK or GCN5 homologues were aligned using BioEdit (v.7.0.9) software with the default ClustalW Multiple alignment settings. The output file was imported into GeneDoc (v.2.7.0.0) for visualization. The heatmap was generated with TBtools (v0.6).

For computational modeling of the protein interaction between GCN5 and PK1, the 3-D structures of GCN5 and PK1 complex were modeled through AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com).

The model with the highest score was selected for further analysis. Interacting amino acids were analyzed by PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/pistart.html). Structural figures were generated by PyMOL (Version 2.5.2).

Protein-protein interaction assay

For the split-luciferase complementation assays, the cDNA of GCN5 was cloned in-frame upstream of the sequence encoding the N-terminal half of firefly Luciferase (NLuc), while that of PK1 was cloned in-frame downstream of the sequence encoding the C-terminal half of firefly Luciferase (CLuc). Subsequently, the vectors of 35Spro::GCN5-NLuc and 35Spro::PK1-CLuc were infiltrated into 6-week-old tobacco (N. benthamiana) leaves. After 48 h transfection, tobacco leaves were infiltrated with 1 mM luciferin (Gold Biotechnology, 115144-35-9). Luciferase bioluminescence images were taken with the Chemi-Image System (Tanon 5200Multi, China).

For in vitro pull-down assay, His-tagged PK1 and GST-tagged H3 or GCN5 were constructed and expressed in the Escherichia coli cells. GST or H3-GST/GCN5-GST coupled GST beads (GE Healthcare, 17–5132-01) were used to pull down PK1-6×His and an anti-His (1:4000, Abcam, ab9108) antibody were applied to detect PK1 protein.

For co-immunoprecipitation assay in rice protoplasts or pk1 complementation seedlings, 35Spro::GCN5-FLAG construct was transfected into rice protoplasts with 35Spro::PK1-GFP or 35Spro::GFP alone. then the proteins were extracted from rice protoplasts or transgenic plants using lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with anti-GFP M2 magnetic beads (AlpalifeBio, KTSM1334) or anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, M8823) for 5 h at 4 °C. After three washes with washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA), the co-immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and detected with anti-GFP (1:1000, Abcam, ab290), anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165), anti-GCN5 (1:3000, Abcam, ab153903), anti-HA (1:5000, ABclonal, AE036) and anti-H3 (1:1000, Abcam, ab1791) antibodies.

In vitro KAT/ HAT activity assays

For in vitro acetylation reactions, PK1, PK1K297R, PK1K385R, PK1K503R and PK13KR was cloned into the pET28a vector (Novagen), and GCN5 was cloned into the pGEX-6P vector (GE Healthcare). The recombinant proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells and purified by Ni-NTA agarose beads (ABclonal, AS045) or GST agarose beads (ABclonal, AS044). All in vitro KAT/HAT assays were incubated in KAT buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 2 mM TSA and 1 mM PMSF) with various combinations of recombinant proteins and acetyl-CoA as indicated at 25 °C for 4 h. The reaction was terminated by adding 4×SDS loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 40% glycerol, 5% SDS, 0.025% [W/V] bromophenol blue, 20% [V/V] β-mercaptoethanol), and the samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis and immunoblotted with anti-His (1:4000, Abcam, ab9108) and anti-acetyl-lysine (1:4000, Abcam, ab21623) antibodies.

In vivo PK1 acetylation assays

The PK1-FLAG protein was immunoprecipitated from 14-day-old seedlings of PK1 overexpression plants. For heat stress treatment, rice plants were transferred to 42 °C for 4 h. Nuclei were extracted from the plant materials as described above. The supernatant was then incubated with anti-FLAG M2 beads (Sigma M8823) overnight. After three washes with TBST buffer, the beads were suspended in 1× SDS loading buffer (62.5 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 1.25% SDS, 0.00625% [W/V] bromophenol blue, 5% [V/V] β-mercaptoethanol) heated at 95 °C for 10 min. The extracted proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165) and anti-acetyl-lysine (1:4000, Abcam, ab21623) antibodies.

For PK1 acetylation assays in rice protoplasts, 35Spro::PK1-GFP or 35Spro::PK13KR-GFP construct was transfected into rice protoplasts isolated from wild type (WT) and GCN5 RNAi plants for 14 h, then the proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with anti-GFP M2 magnetic beads (AlpalifeBio, KTSM1334) at 4 °C for 5 h. After three washes with washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA), the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE with anti-GFP (1:1000, Abcam, ab290) and anti-acetyl-lysine (1:4000, Abcam, ab21623) antibodies.

Nuclear protein fractionation from the rice protoplasts

The nuclear protein fractionation from the rice protoplasts was conducted as described by previously reported79. 35Spro:PK1-GFP was transfected into rice protoplasts isolated from wild type (WT) and GCN5 RNAi plants for 14 h. Subsequently, the protoplasts were collected and mixed with lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40). Then homogenized with a pellet pestle for 1 min, repeat 5–10 times, followed by centrifuge at 300 g, 4 °C for 5 min to remove cell debris. Transfer the supernatant to a new 1.5 mL tube and collect the nuclei at 1000 g, 4 °C for 10 min, then the PK1 protein was extracted as described above.

Phosphorylation mass spectrometry identification

For phosphorylation mass spectrometry identification of GCN5, 35Spro::GCN5-FLAG construct was transfected into rice protoplasts isolated from two-week-old wild-type (WT) plants at a concentration of 20 μg per 100 μL and incubated for 14 h, then the proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, M8823) at 4 °C for 5 h. After three washes with washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA), the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by 8% SDS-PAGE gel for identifying phosphorylation sites. One biological replicate (n = 1) was performed for this analysis.

Mass Spectrometric analysis was performed by AIMSMASS Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). protein sample were denatured and reduced with reaction buffer 1 (1.25 mM NH₄HCO₃, 10 mM DTT) at 50 °C for 30 min, and alkylated with reaction buffer 2 (2.25 mM NH₄HCO₃, 50 mM IAA) at 25 °C for 30 min. Then, trypsin was added and incubated at 37 °C while shaking for digestion. TFA was added to terminate the digestion. The supernatant was obtained by centrifuging at 16,000 g and desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore). Finally, the samples were drained for mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive HF-X spectrometer with an Easy-nLC 1000 HPLC system and a C18 column (1.8 mm, 0.15 × 100 mm), and equipped with mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B: 80% acetonitrile. The gradient was: 0-20 min, 5–13% B; 20–54 min, 13–26% B; 54–61 min, 26–45% B; 61–62 min, 45–80% B; 62–65 min, 80% B, at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The scan range was 120–2000 m/z in DDA mode using FTMS at a mass resolution of 30,000 and the ten most intense precursor ions were selected for MS/MS. The MS/MS was acquired using higher-energy collision dissociation at 35% collision energy at a mass resolution of 15,000.

The resulting MS/MS data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer (v3.1) with the uniprot-Oryza sativa subsp. japonica (Rice).fasta. Database searching was performed with trypsin (≤2 missed cleavages), carbamidomethyl-C as fixed modification, Oxidation-M, Acetyl-K and Phospho-S/T as dynamic modification. 10 ppm precursor and 0.02 Da fragment tolerances, and q-value validation. The search results were screened and filtered with Perseus, applying a 1% False Discovery Rate (FDR) threshold for both protein and peptide identifications. The mass spectrometry data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchage database under the accession code PXD066361.

Chip-seq and data analysis

Two grams of 2-week-old rice seedlings were cross-linked in 1% (w/v) formaldehyde for 30 min and used for chromatin extraction. Chromatin was fragmented to approximately 150 bp by sonication and then incubated overnight with antibody-coated beads (Invitrogen 10001D). After extensive washing, immunoprecipitated chromatin was de-cross-linked and purified for sequencing. Unimmunoprecipitated chromatin was used as input. Anti-H3K9ac (Millipore, 07-352) and anti-H3T11p (Millipore, 623684) antibodies were used. DNA purified from chromatin immunoprecipitation was used to construct sequencing libraries following the protocol provided by the Illumina TruSeq ChIP Sample Prep Set A and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000. FastP (v0.232) was used to filter out low-quality reads. Clean reads were then mapped to the rice genome (MSU7.0, http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/) with Bowtie2 (version 2.5.4) using default parameters. SAMtools (version 1.9) was used to remove potential PCR duplicates. MACS (version 2.2.7.1) was used to calculate histone modification peaks. Differential peaks were calculated using HOMER (v4.11) with a threshold for fold-change>2 and P < 0.05. Peak annotation was performed using HOMER (v4.11) annotatePeaks tool. String database (v2.0) was used to conduct gene ontology analysis. Fingerprint plot was performed using plot Fingerprint from deepTools (v3.5.4), with 50,000 genome bins and normalization to 1x sequencing depth. Scatter plots, and box plots were generated in R (v3.5).

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from seedlings with Transzol reagent (Cook Gene, KKR009) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by reverse transcription (1 µg of RNA per reaction) using Vazyme mix (R232). For quantitative PCR (qPCR), 0.2 µL of each primer and 3 µL cDNA template were added to one reaction, together with 5 µL of AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q511) and 1.6 µL of ddH2O. The qPCR was performed using the gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Information and a real-time PCR7500 system (Applied Biosystems).

CUT&Tag qPCR

CUT&Tag experiments were performed as described80 with minor modifications. About 3 × 105 seedling nuclei were harvested, washed with wash buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine) and immobilized to concanavalin A-coated beads (Vazyme, TD903) with incubation at room temperature for 10 min. The bead-bound nuclei were incubated in 200 µL of primary antibody buffer (wash buffer with 1% BSA, 2 mM EDTA and 0.05% digitonin for gentle permeabilization of the nuclear membrane) with a 1:50 dilution of anti-H3K9ac (1:1000, Millipore, 07-352), anti-H3T11p (1:2000, Abcam, ab133457), anti-FLAG (1:1000, Sigma, F3165), IgG (ABclonal, AC005) and IgG (ABclonal, AC011) antibodies at 4 °C by rotating overnight. The next day, the primary antibody buffer was removed, and the cells were washed with 800 µL of dig-wash buffer (wash buffer with 1% BSA and 0.05% digitonin) three times. After washing, the antibody-incubated nuclei were resuspended in 200 µL of dig-wash buffer with a 1:100 dilution of secondary antibody (Vazyme, TD903) and incubated at room temperature with gentle rotation for 1 h. To eliminate binding antibodies, the nuclei were washed three times with 800 µL of dig-wash buffer. After a brief wash with dig-wash buffer, the nuclei were resuspended in 200 µL of dig-300 buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1% BSA and 0.01% digitonin) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h with gentle rotation. pA-Tn5-bound nuclei were washed with 800 µL of dig-300 buffer three times and then tagmentated at 37 °C for 1 h. 15 mM EDTA, 500 μg mL−1 proteinase K and 0.1% SDS were added after tagmentation to terminate tagmentation and degrade protein. Genomic DNA was extracted and purified with DNA Extract Beads Pro (Vazyme, TD903). For qPCR, 0.2 µL of each primer and 3 µL cDNA template were added to one reaction, together with 5 µL of AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q511) and 1.6 µL of ddH2O. The qPCR was performed using the gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Information and a real-time PCR7500 system (Applied Biosystems).

Statistical analyses

The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with error bars. Statistical analyses were performed by using GraphPad Prism Software (Version 8.0.1). Significance of differences between two groups was analyzed by Student’s unpaired and two-tailed t tests with Welch’s correction. For multiple comparisons, in case of equal standard deviations, one-way ANOVA (nonparametric) tests were performed with Tukey’s multiple comparisons. The values represented as means ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Detailed statistical information can be found in the figure legends.

The CUT&Tag data comprised two biological replicates for each genotype. The biological replicates of all other experiments presented in this study are indicated in the respective figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Samples were randomly allocated into experimental groups according to different mutants. No sample was excluded. Blinding was not relevant to our study.
