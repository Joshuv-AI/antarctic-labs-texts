Helping teens quit: A systematic review and network meta-analysis of smoking cessation interventions
Authors: Kansak Boonpattharatthiti, Kanchana Mueangfa, Natchaya Aiumtanaporn, Chanchanok Koomsri, Junpen Chantara, Teerapon Dhippayom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAdolescent smoking remains a significant public health concern. Effective smoking cessation interventions tailored for adolescents are essential, yet evidence comparing different strategies remains limited. We aimed to compare the effectiveness of various smoking cessation interventions for adolescents using a network meta-analysis (NMA) approach.Study designSystematic review and network meta-analysis.MethodsWe searched PubMed, EMBASE, CENTRAL, CINAHL, and PsycINFO from their inception to February 28, 2024. Randomized controlled trials comparing smoking cessation interventions for adolescents were included if they reported biochemical verification of smoking abstinence. Two pairs of researchers independently conducted the study selection. NMA was performed using a random-effects model in a frequentist framework. The quality of the included studies was assessed using Cochrane's Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. (PROSPERO CRD420251001010).ResultsTwelve studies were included. No intervention demonstrated clear superiority over usual care or other comparators. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)-based, CBT combined with nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), and Non-NRT interventions showed higher short-term abstinence (≤6 months) compared with usual care (UC), with relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 2.31 (95%CI: 1.59–3.35), 2.44 (95%CI: 1.16–5.16), and 1.57 (95%CI: 1.09–2.28), respectively. However, these findings were based on very low-certainty evidence and were not sustained at longer follow-up.ConclusionsThe evidence base for adolescent smoking cessation is limited and heterogeneous. While some interventions may offer short-term benefits, there is no robust evidence of sustained effectiveness beyond six months. High-quality trials are needed to establish effective long-term strategies.

Introduction

Tobacco use is a leading cause of preventable death, contributing to over 8 million deaths annually, including 1.2 million from secondhand smoke [1]. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable, with many starting before age 18, increasing their risk of long-term nicotine dependence. Their developing brains, especially in areas controlling impulse and decision-making, heighten susceptibility to addiction, increasing vulnerability to substance use and making quitting more challenging compared to adults [2]. Early intervention is crucial to prevent lifelong dependence and associated health risks, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory disorders, and cancer [3].

Adolescents represent a special population that requires tailored smoking cessation interventions. While behavioral therapy, pharmacotherapy, and combination approaches are effective for smoking cessation in the general population, evidence supporting their use in adolescents is limited [4]. Pharmacological treatments, such as nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and bupropion, have shown efficacy in adults but are not widely recommended for adolescents due to a lack of FDA approval, limited evidence of effectiveness, and insufficient research on their use in younger populations [5]. Behavioral interventions, including motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and peer support programs, have been explored as alternative strategies for adolescent smoking cessation [6,7].

Despite various efforts to help adolescents quit smoking, there is no comprehensive comparative analysis evaluating the effectiveness of different smoking cessation interventions in this population. Although previous meta-analyses have evaluated specific approaches, the evidence remains fragmented and inconsistent [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]], making it difficult for clinicians and policymakers to identify the most effective strategies for helping adolescent smokers quit. Network meta-analysis (NMA) addresses this gap by enabling the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions through the integration of direct and indirect evidence, even in the absence of head-to-head trials. This approach is particularly valuable in the context of sparse evidence, as it improves the precision of comparative estimates and allows for the ranking of interventions. Therefore, this study aims to compare the effectiveness of various smoking cessation interventions for adolescents using NMA approach, offering a broader perspective on what works best to improve smoking cessation rates in this population.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the efficacy of different smoking cessation interventions for adolescents, using the approach recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [11] and reporting the results of the NMA in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for NMA [12]. The protocol for this study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251001010).

Search strategies

We searched the following bibliographic databases from the inception of each database to 28 February 2024: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. We searched for grey literature using EBSCO Open Dissertations. Furthermore, we employed both backward and forward citation searching in Scopus to ensure comprehensive coverage. Backward citation searching involved screening the reference lists of included studies to identify relevant prior research, while forward citation searching involved tracking studies that cited the included studies to identify more recent related research. This approach helped minimize the risk of missing relevant literature. The search terms included three domains: smoking cessation, adolescents, and randomized controlled trials. The full strategic search terms used in all databases can be found in Appendix 1.

Study selection

Four researchers (K.M., N.A., C.K., and J.C.) were paired and independently screened the titles and abstracts of half of all database search results. Subsequently, the full texts of studies that passed the title and abstract screening were assessed against the eligibility criteria by the same pairs of researchers. Any discrepancies between pairs of researchers were resolved by reaching a consensus with T.D.

Eligibility criteria

To be included, studies must be conducted in adolescent smokers and compare either a pharmacological or a non-pharmacological intervention for smoking cessation. They must also use a valid biochemical method to confirm abstinence and follow a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. Studies were excluded if they were conducted in pregnant adolescents. We did not impose restrictions on the language of publication or the period of publication.

Data extraction

Two researchers (K.M. and N.A.) independently extracted the data using a pre-specified data extraction form. The extracted data included study characteristics (such as study design, study setting, country); patient characteristics (such as age, gender, ethnicity, cigarette consumption per day); detail of intervention and comparator; and outcomes (point prevalence abstinence with biochemical verification). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with T.D.

Quality assessment

Two researchers (K.M. and N.A.) used the Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [13] to assess the methodological quality of included studies, which evaluates bias in the following domains: 1) randomization process 2) deviations from intended interventions 3) missing outcome data 4) measurement of the outcome 5) selection of the reported result. Risk of bias was assessed for each domain, and an overall risk of bias was rated as low, some concerns or high risk of bias. Any discrepancy was discussed, and consensus was reached with a third reviewer (T.D.). We used the Risk-of-Bias VISualization (robvis) tool to create traffic light plots of the domain-level judgments for the smoking abstinence outcome [14].

Classification of smoking cessation interventions

Smoking cessation interventions were classified according to their primary active components, as reported in the included studies, with the aim of grouping interventions that share similar mechanisms of action. The classification was developed through iterative discussion within the review team to ensure consistency and clinical plausibility and was further reviewed and confirmed by a clinician with expertise in psychological interventions. Interventions sharing core therapeutic components were grouped together, while combination interventions were retained as separate nodes only when each component was considered to contribute a distinct and meaningful therapeutic effect; otherwise, they were collapsed into broader categories. Based on this collaborative evaluation, the interventions were categorized into seven treatment nodes as follows: cognitive behavioral therapy based (CBT-based), motivational interviewing (MI), nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), non-nicotine replacement therapy (Non-NRT), cognitive behavioral therapy and nicotine replacement therapy (CBT + NRT), nicotine replacement therapy and non-nicotine replacement therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy (NRT + NonNRT + CBT), and usual care (UC).

Network geometry

We drew a network geometry to examine the relationships between the treatment nodes. In the network geometry, the solid line represents a direct comparison between trials, allowing us to visualize the evidence structure and identify potential gaps in the data.

Data analysis

We conducted a standard pairwise meta-analysis using a random effect model for direct comparisons. We assessed the statistical heterogeneity between trials using the Chi-squared test and I2 statistics when there are two or more trials included in direct comparisons. We evaluated clinical heterogeneity by examining variations in population characteristics and interventions across studies.

We performed a network meta-analysis to assess smoking cessation abstinence using the risk ratio (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) within a random-effects model in the frequentist framework. We summarized the pooled estimates of all treatment comparisons in a league table. Analyses were conducted using the intention-to-treat principle, whereby participants who dropped out or were lost to follow-up were considered not to have achieved smoking abstinence. In cases where multiple point prevalence abstinence values were reported, such as 7-day or 30-day point prevalence abstinence, we used the longest reported point prevalence abstinence from each study for the main analysis to better reflect the sustained effectiveness of the interventions.

To assess global network inconsistency, we applied a consistency–inconsistency model to measure the disagreement between direct and indirect effects. We explored transitivity by examining the distribution of clinical and methodological variables (including age, gender, cigarette consumption per day) that could influence the outcomes of interest. We assessed publication bias using a comparison-adjusted funnel plot and Egger's test to detect small study effects. Additionally, we used the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) method to rank smoking cessation interventions hierarchically and estimate the probability of the best treatment option. Higher SUCRA values indicated better treatment ranks. We considered a p-value of <0.05 statistically significant. We performed all analyses using STATA 17.0 (College Station, Texas, United States).

For the sensitivity analysis, we excluded studies with small sample sizes (defined as those below the 25th percentile of all included studies) and additionally conducted a post hoc analysis excluding studies that reported 30-day point-prevalence abstinence [15]. We also conducted subgroup analyses based on: 1) the duration of reported outcome using six months period as the cut-off point, 2) the format of intervention delivery (group or individual), and 3) intervention settings (school-based or non-school-based). These subgroup analyses were conducted post hoc to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and should be interpreted cautiously.

Certainty of evidence assessment

We assessed the certainty of evidence using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) approach, which considers six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. The overall assessment across domains was summarized to determine the overall certainty of evidence, which was classified as very low, low, moderate, or high [16].

Results

Search results

A search result yielded 6773 articles after duplicates were removed. Following the title and abstract screening, a total of 6631 articles were excluded due to irrelevance, leaving 142 articles for full-text review. After the full-text review, 133 articles were excluded for reasons outlined in Fig. 1. A total of 9 studies from database search met our inclusion criteria. Other searching techniques based on these 9 studies yielded 825 articles after duplicates were removed. Of these, three articles were included. A list of excluded studies and the reasons for exclusion can be found in Appendix 2. Characteristics of smoking cessation intervention

Ten trials deliver the intervention in an individual basis [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21],[23], [24], [25], [26],28], while only two trials use a group-based approach [22,27]. The intervention characteristics varies across the included trials. For example, MI was implemented in four trials [18,19,23,26], with differences in session length, delivery methods, and additional support. Brown et al. used two 45-min sessions, relapse prevention materials, and follow-ups [18]. Colby et al. incorporated MI techniques such as goal setting, ambivalence exploration, and personalized feedback [19]. Lipkus et al. combined MI with self-help booklets, a motivational video, and three counseling calls [23]. Roddy et al. included weekly 10–15-min MI-based counseling sessions, delivered individually alongside placebo patches to maintain blinding [26]. CBT also varied across four studies [17,21,25,27]. Abdul Halim et al. adapted the Quit4Life program, integrating peer support, school counselors, and community involvement [17]. Hanson et al. provided CBT, covering topics such as identifying smoking triggers and managing relapse risks [21]. Pbert et al. used the Craving to Quit (C2Q) app, a mindfulness-based smoking cessation program adapted for adolescents [25]. Rubinstein et al. implemented an eight-week structured program focused on coping strategies and quit planning [27]. More details on each intervention are available in Appendix 3.

Quality of included trials

Among the 12 trials, seven were classified as having some concerns risk of bias [[17], [18], [19],23,25,26,28], whereas five trials were classified as having a low risk of bias [[20], [21], [22],24,27]. Most of the concerns arose from the randomization process in the included trials. These concerns were primarily due to insufficient reporting of sequence generation and allocation concealment, as well as baseline imbalances between intervention groups in some trials. These limitations raise the possibility of selection bias, which may have influenced the estimated treatment effects. A more detailed assessment of the risk of bias across different domains can be found in Appendix 4.

Point prevalence abstinent

The 12 included trials reported point prevalence abstinence included a total of 2428 participants. A total of 7 direct comparison pairs were identified in the network of point prevalence abstinence (Fig. 2). When compared with UC, only NRT had point estimates lower than one (RR = 0.76; 95%CI: 0.22-2.67), suggesting potential ineffectiveness, while others had RRs greater than one, indicating a tendency to be more effective than UC. However, none of these differences were statistically significant. Interventions with RR above one included: CBT + NRT (1.49; 95%CI: 0.40-5.54), CBT-based (1.58; 95%CI: 0.76-3.30), MI (1.44; 95%CI: 0.70-2.97), NRT + NonNRT + CBT (1.14; 95%CI: 0.22-5.85), and NonNRT (1.30; 95%CI: 0.61-2.78). It should be noted that these comparisons were based on evidence with very low certainty, and the pooled estimates contained measurement heterogeneity due to the combination of 30-day and 7-day point prevalence abstinence outcomes, so they should be interpreted with caution (Table 2 and Appendix 5). The SUCRA analysis ranked CBT-based (69.4%), CBT + NRT (62.5%), and MI (61.9%) among the top three interventions with the highest probabilities of being more effective than others (Table 3 and Appendix 5). Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analysis, excluding small sample size studies and excluding trails reporting 30-day point prevalence abstinence, revealed trends and effect sizes similar to those in the main analysis and confirmed that none of the comparisons were significantly different (Appendix 7). Although no significant differences were found between interventions overall, subgroup analyses based on reporting period showed that CBT-based, CBT + NRT and Non-NRT significantly improved smoking cessation abstinent at less than 6 months post-intervention compare to UC, with RRs of 2.31 (95% CI: 1.59, 3.35), 2.44 (95%CI: 1.16, 5.16), and 1.57 (95% CI: 1.09, 2.28), respectively. However, trials that measured abstinence after 6 months found no significant differences between interventions. Additionally, the format of intervention delivery and the intervention setting demonstrated comparable effect estimates across different subgroups, with none of the comparisons statistically significant (Appendix 8).

Assessment of heterogeneity, consistency, transitivity, and publication bias

The heterogeneity of the included studies was moderate (I2 ranged from 33.6% to 61.8%). There was no inconsistency in the effect estimate between direct and indirect evidence, as indicated by a non-significant global inconsistency test (Appendix 5). Transitivity across comparisons was assessed by comparing the distribution of effect modifiers. While there was some overlap in these characteristics (Appendix 9), variability across studies was observed, and the plausibility of the transitivity assumption should therefore be interpreted with caution. The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, and the non-significant Egger's test (p = 0.296) suggested no publication bias (Appendix 10).

Discussion

Our study provides a comparative analysis of smoking cessation interventions in adolescents using an NMA approach. The strength of this study lies in its ability to simultaneously compare various pharmacological, non-pharmacological, and combined interventions within a single analytical framework. While no significant differences were observed among interventions in terms of efficacy or safety outcomes, CBT-based interventions and NonNRT showed a potential trend toward higher smoking abstinence rates, particularly in studies with follow-ups of less than six months. These findings should be interpreted with caution. Short-term abstinence in adolescents is not a reliable indicator of sustained cessation, and the observed effects were based on evidence of very low certainty. Importantly, no interventions demonstrated sustained effectiveness at follow-up periods beyond 6 months. This lack of evidence for long-term benefit represents the more critical and policy-relevant finding of this review, highlighting a substantial gap in the current evidence base, emphasizing the need for further high-quality studies to confirm the observed trends.

Several non-pharmacological interventions have been used to support smoking cessation in adolescents, including CBT, MI, peer-support programs, and financial incentives. Behavioral interventions, such as cognitive education, video counseling, and motivational interviewing, have been shown to significantly improve smoking cessation rates in adults [29]. However, while some studies have reported that behavioral-based interventions significantly improve smoking cessation in adolescents, they used different approaches than ours. For example, Peirson et al. included trials without biochemical verification [8], while Choi et al. lumped all behavioral interventions together [6]. Unlike these studies, our analysis aimed to determine which specific type of intervention is most effective for smoking cessation. Instead of the lumping approach used in previous research, we applied a splitting approach, considering key domains that contribute to an intervention's potential effects. Although our findings did not show significant effects for any intervention, they suggest a potential short-term benefit of CBT-based interventions, particularly in studies with follow-ups of less than six months.

Our findings align with the recent Cochrane review, which found no clear evidence supporting the effectiveness of pharmacological interventions for adolescent smoking cessation [7]. While pharmacotherapy has demonstrated efficacy in adults, its effects in adolescents remain uncertain. A previous meta-analysis suggested that pharmacotherapy improved abstinence rates in adolescents, but its effectiveness varied by medication, with NRT showing no significant benefit over placebo [10]. Although NRT is widely used in adult populations, adolescents may struggle with consistent use due to factors such as lower motivation to quit, social dynamics, and developmental characteristics [30].

Findings from a previous network meta-analysis on current smokers suggest that monotherapies such as varenicline, bupropion, and NRT have similar effects, with no significant differences between them [31]. However, a subgroup analysis from a meta-analysis in adolescents by Myung et al. found that bupropion significantly improved smoking cessation compared to placebo [10]. While our primary analysis did not show significant benefits of non-NRT pharmacotherapy, subgroup analysis of studies with treatment durations shorter than six months indicated that non-NRT interventions significantly increased smoking abstinence. The difference between our findings and previous studies may be due to variations in how treatment groups were classified. Unlike a study by Myung et al. that grouped all comparators together [10], our study categorized interventions separately, allowing for more detailed comparisons. A plausible explanation for bupropion's potential benefits is its antidepressant properties, which may be particularly helpful for adolescents facing mood-related challenges during smoking cessation [32,33].

The beneficial effects observed in CBT and non-NRT interventions did not extend beyond six months. This aligns with findings from a previous meta-analysis, which reported that the effects of smoking cessation medications diminish within the first year [34]. Several factors may contribute to this decline, including reduced motivation, increased exposure to smoking triggers (such as stress, peer influence, and environmental cues), and lower adherence to treatment over time. These challenges highlight the need for long-term strategies to support sustained smoking abstinence among adolescents.

Nearly half of adolescent smokers are in the precontemplation stage, indicating they have no immediate intention to quit [35]. They have less favorable characteristics for cessation than individuals at more advanced stages of readiness, which may explain why interventions effective in adults are less successful in adolescents. Future research should focus on developing tailored interventions that account for the unique psychological and behavioral characteristics of adolescent smokers, ensuring sustained abstinence over time.

Our study has several limitations. Despite efforts to classify interventions based on their key components, the limited number of available trials resulted in small sample sizes for each treatment comparison. This may have reduced statistical power and contributed to the lack of significant findings. In addition, there was substantial heterogeneity in study populations and settings. These differences may represent important effect modifiers and raise concerns regarding the transitivity assumption. As such, indirect comparisons and relative treatment effects should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when generalizing findings across different adolescent populations and settings. Moreover, given the increasing prevalence of e-cigarette use among adolescents, the applicability of our findings to contemporary populations may be limited. Therefore, the generalizability of these results to current patterns of nicotine use should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the majority of included studies had some concerns regarding risk of bias, which affect the certainty of the evidence. Further research with rigorous study designs and larger sample sizes is needed to provide more conclusive evidence on the most effective smoking cessation strategies for adolescents.

Conclusions

None of the interventions demonstrated robust or consistent superiority in promoting smoking cessation among adolescents across the network. Although some approaches, particularly CBT-based interventions, showed potential short-term benefits, these findings were primarily derived from subgroup analyses and were supported by very low-certainty evidence. Future studies should focus on high-quality trials to confirm the benefits of CBT, make it more adaptable for real-world use, and develop strategies that support long-term smoking cessation.
