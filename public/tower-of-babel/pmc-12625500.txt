Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis reveals key genes and core metabolic pathways involved in phosphorus stress response in quinoa flower spikes
Authors: Qianchao Wang, Qingwen Jiang, Junna Liu, Ping Zhang, Li Li, Hanxue Li, Peng Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuinoa is native to the Andes Mountains, where the ecological environment is relatively harsh, making it highly resilient in adapting to its environment. In the flower spike stage, phosphorus (P) is vital for plant metabolism and signaling. Here, we analyzed the flower spike response to low and high P stress.ResultsA total of 1891 metabolites were identified among the three subgroups: control (CB, CY), high P (HB, HY), and low P (LB, LY), where Y represents Dianyuli 1, and B represents Dianyuli 3. There were 290 and 164 differentially expressed metabolites (DAMs) in LB vs. CB and LY vs. CY, respectively, and 144 and 248 DAMs in HB vs. CB and HY vs. CY, respectively. A total of 53,202 genes were annotated using transcriptome analysis, with 2,818 and 3,628 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in LB vs. CB and LY vs. CY, respectively, and 263 and 3,702 DEGs in HB vs. CB and HY vs. CY, respectively. The main molecular mechanisms of quinoa floral spikes were different under low and high P conditions, with the main pathways under low P stress being purine, starch, and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis, and flavonoid biosynthesis. The primary pathways involved in high P stress were pyrimidine, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, and phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis. The synthesis pathways indicated that sugars, organic acids, flavonoids, nucleotides, and their derivatives were involved in the P stress response mechanisms of quinoa flower spikes. Joint metabolite and gene analysis identified the candidate genes involved in these mechanisms, explaining the pattern of biosynthesis and accumulation of these metabolites.ConclusionsOur findings will contribute to the development of quinoa lines that are low P stress tolerant, thus accelerating the cultivation and industrialization of quinoa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07649-6.Keywords: Quinoa, Low and high p stress, Metabolites, Gene expression analysis

Introduction

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is an annual dicotyledonous plant of the genus Chenopodium in the family Amaranthaceae. Quinoa is a food crop native to South America that has a notably high nutritional value [1]. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations recognizes quinoa as the only plant that can satisfy all the basic nutritional needs of the human body and consequently recommends quinoa as the most suitable whole food for humans [2, 3]. Quinoa also has salinity, drought, cold, barren, and other physiological resistance characteristics. As such, quinoa has been rapidly promoted and developed as a nutritional agricultural crop [4].

Phosphorus (P) is one of the three major nutrient elements required for plant growth, and is an important cellular constituent and essential element [5]. P is an important component of energy molecules such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP); plays an important role in the regulation of photosynthesis, respiration, cell division, cell signaling, and gene expression in plants; and is critical for plant growth, development, and yield formation [6, 7]. In plant flowering spikes, the critical period of plant nutritional growth to reproductive growth, P content is crucial. P determines flower bud differentiation, pollen development, and the transition from reproductive to nutritional growth. P further promotes plant flowering, seed growth and development, and nutrient accumulation, which are essential for seed growth and development [8–10].

Appropriate P content ensures proper plant growth, flowering, development, and nutrient accumulation. However, under low P stress, plant growth is retarded, yield is reduced, bud differentiation is reduced, fewer flower buds appear, and “false flowers” appear, affecting the number and quality of the produced fruits [5, 11]. Excessive P fertilizer promotes plant respiration, leading to premature flowering and maturation, small seeds, and low yield. In addition, it increases the agricultural production cost, causes soil condensation, and leads to eutrophication pollution in water bodies [12, 13]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying plant flowering spikes in response to different P stressors can improve P-use efficiency.

P metabolism is regulated by various factors, such as plant hormones, soil microorganisms, and the activity of certain enzymes. Gene expression in the underlying metabolic pathways varies under different P conditions. Plant response mechanisms to abiotic stress can be determined using transcriptome analysis [14]. Several studies have analyzed plant responses to P stress in quinoa seedlings, ryegrass, and barley [15–17]. The metabolome is the basis and direct embodiment of the phenotype of an organism and can be used to qualitatively and quantitatively study the metabolites of organisms. Liu et al. [18] studied the metabolic responses of tomato leaves and roots in response to nitrogen (N), P, and potassium (K) deficiencies. Plant responses to adverse conditions are complex and diverse, and comprehensive mechanism evaluation using a single metabolic or transcriptional marker is challenging. Joint transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses can comprehensively resolve the biomolecular functions and regulatory mechanisms [19, 20].

In our previous study, we analyzed the responses of quinoa seedlings to low and high P stress. We proposed that quinoa seedlings respond to different P environments by regulating changes in metabolites and genes in the glycolysis, glycerolipid, and glycerophospholipid pathways [17]. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), five core genes were identified that were highly positively and negatively correlated with P levels [21]. Nevertheless, the flower spike period, which is critical for grain yield, remains insufficiently understood in terms of quinoa flower spikes under low and high P stress.

In this study, we used Dianyuli 1 and Dianyuli 3 as experimental materials and employed ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) metabolomics alongside transcriptomics to jointly analyze differential genes and metabolites, explore key metabolic pathways, and elucidate the molecular mechanism of quinoa flower spike response to low and high P stress, providing a theoretical reference for breeding excellent quinoa varieties.

Materials and methods

Materials and sample preparation

Genotypes Dianyuli 1 and Dianyuli 3 (both varieties have been identified and possess variety specificity, consistency, and stability) were planted in the Modern Agricultural Education and Research Base of Yunnan Agricultural University, Xundian County, Kunming (E 102°41′, N 25°20′, Kunming, China). Uniform and consistent seeds were selected and evenly sown in pots (117 cm × 39 cm × 65 cm). The growing media was soil, and the N, P, and K in the soil matrix were 2.75 g/kg, 0.56 g/kg, and 1.18 g/kg (Norminkoda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Wuhan, China), respectively. To establish different levels of P fertilizer, P2O5 dosages of 0 kg/hm2, 450.0 kg/hm2, and 562.5 kg/hm2 were applied, and 112.5 kg/hm2 of CH4N2O and K2O were supplemented to each substrate. The application of P fertilizer was conducted in two phases. Initially, it was incorporated into the soil medium prior to sowing at different quantities. Subsequently, a second application was performed during the quinoa seedling stage.

Conventional cultivation and management techniques were followed in the early stages (average temperature: 25.6 ℃; sunshine duration: approximately 10 h; sowing depth: 2–3 cm) with approximately 10 seedlings in each flowerpot. Quinoa flower spikes under low and high P stress were selected, and three subgroups were formed: control (CB, CY), high P (HB, HY), and low P (LB, LY), where Y represents Dianyuli 1 and B represents Dianyuli 3. Flower spikes were sampled, collected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 ℃ (Table 1; Fig. 1). These samples were used for reference transcriptome and metabolomic determination, using a total of 18 samples. Three biological and three technical replicates were used for data detection and analysis.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of metabolites

After vacuum freeze-drying, the flower spike samples were ground using a grinder (MM400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. The ground samples were extracted with 1200 µl of pre-cooled 70% methanol aqueous internal standard extract at −20 ℃, and the experimental data were collected using UPLC-MS/MS (ExionLC™ AD, https://sciex.com.cn/). Based on the Metware Database, the metabolites were characterized and quantified using secondary mass spectrometry information and triple quadrupole mass spectrometry in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, in which the same metabolite was corrected by integrating the peaks of the mass spectra from different samples [22]. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing with sample extracts, and the reproducibility of the analytical process was monitored by inserting one QC sample for every 10 samples that were analyzed. The reproducibility of metabolite extraction and detection was determined by analyzing the total ion flow plots (TICs) of different QC samples analyzed by mass spectrometry using an overlapping display [23, 24]. The dimensionality of the data was simplified and reduced using multivariate statistical analysis to establish a digital model. R software (www.r-project. org/) was used to calculate the prcomp function and analyze the differences between the samples [22, 25].

Principal Component Analysis (PCA), a method for statistical unsupervised identification of multidimensional data, uses the built-in statistical prcomp function of the R software to perform unit variance scaling on the data, and utilizes an Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) approach to extract components in the independent variable X and dependent variable Y for variance screening [24, 26]. Based on the results of OPLS-DA, the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) of the OPLS-DA model of multivariate analysis was obtained and further screened for differential metabolites by combining the P-Value and Fold Change (FC) [26]. Metabolites with a multiplicity of differences of ≥ 2-fold in the control and experimental groups or ≤ 0.5 were identified as having significant differences. The relative contents of the detected differential metabolites were normalized using the z-score, followed by K-means cluster analysis to analyze the changes in the relative contents of metabolites in the different subgroups. Screening was calibrated and annotated in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes Genomes (KEGG) database, and significance was determined by the P-value of the hypergeometric test [27].

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

RNA extraction, RNA detection, library construction, on-board sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Wuhan Metwel Biotechnology Co. Ltd [28] (Wuhan, China, www.metware.cn). After library construction, preliminary quantification was performed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, followed by insert size detection of the library using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and accurate quantification using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The libraries were then used for machine sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform. After filtering the raw data, checking the sequencing error rate, and examining the GC content distribution, clean reads were obtained for subsequent analysis. Clean Data were compared with the reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/?Term=Chenopodium+quinoa+Willd). Genes were annotated using HISATv2.1.0 software [29] and the Bowtie2 method [30], and the new genes were compared using KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), Gene Ontology (GO, https://geneontology.org/), Non-Redundant Protein Database (NR, https://ftp.ncbi.nih.gov/blast/db), Swiss-Prot (http://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/?query=*&fil=reviewed%3Ayes), trEMBL (https://www.uniprot.org/), and EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG, https://ftp.ncbi.nih.gov/pub/COG/KOG/kyva) databases using BLAST software. Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKM) was used as an indicator of gene expression level. Differential expression analysis between the sample groups was initially conducted using DESeq2. Subsequently, the hypothesis testing probability (P-value) was adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to obtain the False Discovery Rate (FDR). The final selection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was based on the criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 [31].

Combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis

The results of the differential metabolite and gene analyses were mapped onto the KEGG pathway maps to elucidate the relationship between genes and metabolites. Correlation analysis (CCA) was performed on the genes and metabolites detected in each differential subgroup, and the Pearson correlation coefficients of the genes and metabolites were calculated using the cor program in R [32, 33]. The differential multiplicity of gene metabolites with Pearson correlation coefficients > 0.8 in each differential subgroup was demonstrated using a nine-quadrant plot. All differential genes and metabolites were selected to establish an O2PLS model. The variables exhibiting higher correlations and weights in different data groups were identified using loading diagrams, and the important variables affecting other histological features were screened out [34].

Transcriptomic data verification via RT-qPCR

RNA extracted from LY, LB, HY, HB, CY, and CB was used for RT-qPCR. Three technical replicates were performed for each biological replicate. Beacon Design 7.9 was used for the primer design for the genes LOC110685458, LOC110725246, LOC110733547, LOC110706846, LOC110731693, and LOC110732367. TUB-6 was used as an internal reference gene. Target genes were amplified using PerfectStart SYBR qPCR Supermix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and a StepOnePlus qPCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, California, USA). The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to analyze the normalized expression of each sample [35].

Determination of the biomass/agronomic/morphophysiological data

Plant height: Select plants that can represent the overall height of each treatment, randomly select 3 plants from the middle as samples for hanging observation, spread the distance from the base to the top of the leaf tip, measure 3 times for each plant, calculate the average value, and then hang and fix the plants for measurement in the later stage. Stem diameter: Simultaneously with measuring plant height, select the same position on the ground of the plant, use a vernier caliper (1183–150 A) to measure the diameter of the main stem, measure 3 times for each plant, and calculate the average value. The first time measuring the diameter of the middle position of the plant and hanging the rope, and later measuring according to the position of the hanging rope. Leaf area (cm2): Simultaneously measured with plant height, take leaves from the same position in the upper middle of the quinoa plant and use the TPYX-A crop leaf morphology measuring instrument (TPYX-A, Zhejiang, China), https://www.tpyn.net)Measure, take the average of multiple measurements. Dry weight determination: Oven drying method is used. The oven temperature is 110 ° C for 30 min of withering, and 80 ° C for constant weight drying. Take out the paper bag and materials, cool to room temperature, and weigh the dry weight. Biomass: After drying to a constant weight, weigh and measure the biomass separately.

The P content was determined by Norminkoda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Total Antioxidants (T-AOC), Proline (Pro), Peroxidase (POD), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and Catalase (CAT) were detected using a kit at Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (http://www.njjcbio.com).

Results

The effects of different phosphorus levels on agronomic traits indicators during the flowering and panicle stage of quinoa

Different phosphorus levels have a certain impact on the phenotype of quinoa during the booting stage. As the phosphorus level increases, the overall growth and development of quinoa improves. However, as the phosphorus application exceeds a certain level, the overall growth of quinoa plants shows a downward trend. As shown in Table 2, when the phosphorus application rate is 0 kg/hm2, quinoa plants are relatively short between the two varieties; Specifically, the number of leaves is small and the area is small, the development of plant height is significantly limited, and the main stem is thicker and thinner. When the phosphorus application rate is 450 kg/hm2, the number and area of seedling leaves are large, the leaves grow vigorously, the plant height increases significantly, and the main stem thickness increases significantly; When the phosphorus application rate was 562.5 kg/hm2, the number and area of leaves, plant height, and main stem diameter were significantly higher than those without phosphorus application, but significantly lower than those when the phosphorus application rate was 450 kg/hm2. Under different phosphorus levels, the dry matter quality of quinoa also varies. The biomass of Dianyuli 1 and Dianyuli 3 is highest when the phosphorus application rate is 450 kg/hm2, and lowest when no phosphorus fertilizer is applied.

Metabolomic data quality control of quinoa flower spike

In this study, 18 flower spike samples were selected and divided into six groups for metabolic analysis. Each group had three biological replicates. Metabolite quantification was performed using triple quadrupole mass spectrometry in the MRM mode (Fig. S1), with accurate quantification and high reproducibility. Overlapping display analysis of the TIC diagram and analysis of different QC samples determined that the reproducibility of metabolite extraction and detection was good, with high instrumental stability, which provides an important guarantee for data reproducibility and reliability (Fig. 2 A). The coefficient of variation (CV) value distribution diagram shows that the percentage of substances with CV values < 0.3 compared with those of the QC samples was > 75%, which reflects good experimental data stability (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, PCA was performed, and PC1 and PC2 explained > 45% of the variance, which was mainly differentiated by PC1 (Fig. 2 C). The results showed significant differences between the low and high P treatments and the control. Correlation plots demonstrated good biological reproducibility and a strong correlation within the sample groups, indicating that the differentially expressed metabolites (DAMs) obtained were reliable (Fig. 2D). The OPLS-DA model showed that Q2 > 0.9, R2Y = 1, and R2X > 0.5 in all six subgroups, confirming that the predictive ability of the OPLS-DA model was valid and reliable (Fig. S2).

Differential metabolite analysis and enrichment

We detected 1891 metabolites, including amino acids and their derivatives (222, 11.74%), phenolic acids (224, 11.85%), flavonoids (346, 18.3%), and alkaloids (232, 12.27%), which accounted for the largest proportion of metabolites (Fig. 3A, Table S1). DAMs with VIP > 1 and FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 were selected. We identified 144 DAMs in HB vs. CB (63 up-regulated and 81 down-regulated), 248 in HY vs. CY (166 up-regulated and 82 down-regulated), 369 in HB vs. LB (160 up-regulated, 209 down-regulated), 148 in HY vs. LY (136 up-regulated, 12 down-regulated), 290 in LB vs. CB (143 up-regulated, 147 down-regulated), and 164 in LY vs. CY (36 up-regulated, 128 down-regulated) (Fig. 3B). The highest numbers of DAMs, flavonoids, and alkaloids were found in these six groups. The Venn diagrams show 30 DAMs for LB vs. CB and HB vs. CB, with 260 and 114 DAMs unique to each; 77 DAMs for HY vs. CY and LY vs. CY, with 171 and 87 DAMs unique to each; 72 DAMs for HB vs. LB and HY vs. LY, with 297 and 76 DAMs unique to each; 297 and 76 DAMs for HY vs. CY, HB vs. CB, LY vs. 297, 76; HY vs. CY, HB vs. CB, LY vs. CY, and LB vs. CB shared seven DAMs, and each had 73, 86, 60, 140 unique DAMs (Fig. 3C). Seven common DAMs were evident in the four P treated groups, namely cinnamoyltyramine, N-trans-cinnamoyl-p-hydroxyphenylethylamine, N-(4-hydroxyphenethyl)cinnamamide, N-trans-feruloyl-4-O-methyl-feruloyl 4′-O-methyldopamine(2e)−3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-n-[2-(4-hydroxyphenyl) ethyl] prop-2-enimidic acid, p-coumaroyltyramine, 4-hydroxycinnamic acid p-hydroxyphenethylamine, all of which are alkaloids.

Pathways significantly enriched under low P stress included linoleic acid metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. Linoleic acid metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, α-linolenic acid metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and zeatin biosynthesis were significantly enriched in LB vs. CB. Flavonoid biosynthesis was the most significantly enriched pathway in LY vs. CY (Fig. 5).

Gene expression quantification and annotation

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on 18 samples, and a total of 138.98 Gb of clean data was obtained. The clean data of all samples reached 6 Gb, and the percentage of Q30 bases was ≥ 92%. After QC, the clean reads were compared with the reference genome, and the comparison efficiency was > 97%, indicating that the results were reliable and accurate. Expression density distribution graphs are used to represent the abundance of genes with changes in expression in different subgroup comparisons; the concentration interval of gene expression FPKM values in the experimental samples was 10− 2.5–102.5 (Fig. 6A). The PCA plot reveals good intra-group reproducibility and significant inter-group variability among the samples (Fig. 6B). The absolute value of r in the correlation heat map was close to 1, indicating a strong correlation between the samples (Fig. 6C). By combining the PCA plot with the clustering heat map, differences in gene expression in the different subgroup comparisons are evident (Fig. 6D).

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis of DEGs

Functional annotation was performed on the detected genes in the NR, Swiss-Prot, GO, TrEMBL, KOG, Pfam, and KEGG databases. A total of 56,983 genes were annotated in NR, 37,051 in Swiss-Prot, 45,817 in GO, 51,989 in KOG, 46,754 in Pfam, 30,609 in KEGG, and 56,154 in TrEMBL. Differential expression analysis between the sample groups was performed using DESeq2 to obtain a set of DEGs between the two biological conditions. The screening criteria for DEGs were |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. The total number of differentially expressed, up-regulated, and down-regulated genes in each group is shown in Fig. 7.

Statistical chart of DEGs. The horizontal and vertical axes represent the different comparison groups and the number of DEGs, respectively

The FPKM of the genes was centered and normalized. Subsequently, K-means clustering analysis was performed to study the gene expression patterns. Genes belonging to the same class showed similar trends under different experimental treatments, suggesting similar functions (Fig. 8 A). DEGs were categorized into nine clusters (Table S3): under low P stress, clusters 1, 4, 8, and 9 showed a gradual upward trend, and clusters 5, 6, and 7 showed a downward post-trend. Discussion

P is essential for plant growth and development and participates in key processes such as substance and energy metabolism, respiration, assimilate transport, and a series of enzymatic reactions within the plant [36]. The P required for plant growth is mainly obtained from the soil. Excessive or insufficient soil P content can affect plant photosynthesis, respiration, and biosynthesis [37]. Plant flower spikes affect seed formation and the reproductive processes. As an indispensable element in the flowering process of plants, P is directly involved in various aspects of the plant flowering spike stage, such as energy transfer, material conversion, assimilate formation, and transport, which are essential for the plant to form sufficient energy and material to support flower and seed formation and growth [38, 39]. Therefore, we investigated the response mechanism of quinoa flower spikes under low and high P stress, identified key pathways, and proposed some DAMs and core DEGs, which have important reference significance for quinoa production and provide a theoretical basis for the study of quinoa and other crops under P stress.

Research has shown that leaves are the most important organ for plants to carry out photosynthesis and exchange gases with the outside world. The size of leaf area affects photosynthetic efficiency and the accumulation of substances, which in turn affects the ability to collect, synthesize, and supply nutrients, and is of great significance for plant growth and development. Measuring the area of leaves is an important parameter for studying photosynthesis and material production and transformation [40]. Stem thickness is one of the important indicators for evaluating the growth status of plants, and its size is related to the growth rate, condition, and other factors of the plant [41]. Plant height as an intuitive appearance phenotype of plants, can quickly detect morphological changes in plants through growth analysis [42]. In this article, under low and high phosphorus stress, the plant height, main stem thickness, and leaf area of quinoa were significantly affected, and compared with the control, they all showed a significant decrease. It is speculated that the decrease in leaf area affects photosynthesis, leading to stunted growth of plants.

Biomass can represent the ability of plants to obtain nutrients under stress conditions, and the accumulation of plant substances varies greatly under different phosphorus levels. Research has shown that phosphorus stress has many adverse effects on plant growth, such as a significant decrease in biomass and yield [43]. In this study, increasing phosphorus fertilizer can promote the biomass accumulation of quinoa and aid in its growth. However, excessive phosphorus application can affect the increase in biomass, while not applying phosphorus fertilizer has the most severe impact and the least biomass accumulation. This is consistent with the above research results.Insufficient or excessive phosphorus application can affect the photosynthesis, metabolism, and cellular activity of plants, leading to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, osmotic stress, and ion toxicity, thereby limiting plant growth [44]. Therefore, under low phosphorus and high phosphorus stress, Pro, T-AOC, POD, CAT, and SOD contents were measured in the flower spikes of Chenopodium yunnan-1 and Chenopodium yunnan-3, respectively. Understand the response characteristics of physicochemical indicators of quinoa seeds under low and high phosphorus stress. Some antioxidant enzymes and metabolites in plants can eliminate some harmful substances and alleviate the toxic effects caused by stress. T-AOC can be used as one of the criteria to determine the strength of antioxidant activity [45]. In this study, under low and high phosphorus stress, T-AOC was significantly higher than the control, indicating that quinoa can reduce the damage caused by phosphorus stress by changing the content of T-AOC to eliminate free radicals. Research has shown that substances such as SOD, POD, Pro, CAT play a very important role in plants’ resistance to stress, and their levels can be used to reflect the intensity of external stress in plants [46]. The results of this study indicate that low phosphorus and high phosphorus stress treatments significantly affect the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT) in quinoa flower spikes. Under low and high phosphorus stress, these antioxidant enzymes convert excess reactive oxygen species and free radicals in the body into less toxic or harmless substances, balancing the content of reactive oxygen species in the body to protect the development of flower spikes.

Core metabolic pathways and key genes of quinoa flower spikes in response to low P stress

Under low P stress, plant assimilation is inhibited, thereby affecting flower formation [9, 47]. P deficiency in plants affects normal growth and development. If plants experience P deficiency or have an insufficient supply of P during the flowering spike stage, flower bud differentiation is restricted, inflorescence formation is reduced, and flowering quality is poor, which considerably affects yield. Plants in a high P environment become dwarfed, enter early senescence, have excessively strong plant respiration, and dry matter production is less than nutrient consumption during growth. Moreover, during the flowering stage, the acceleration of reproductive growth imbalance leads to a reduction in fruit seed production and a consequent decline in yield.

In this study, under low P stress, the DAMs were mainly sugars, nucleotides and their derivatives, and flavonoid metabolites. DAMs and DEGs in flower spikes were significantly enriched in glycolysis, urine metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. The sugar signaling pathway plays an important role in regulating plant responses to low P stress [48, 49]. We found that α-D-glucose-1P, glycerate-3P, and phosphoenolpyruvate contents in glycolysis were reduced under low P stress. In starch and sucrose metabolism, sucrose, D-glucose, D-glucose-6P, and D-fructose-6P contents were similarly reduced. In this study, the sugar signaling pathway was inhibited under low P stress, resulting in a decrease in carbohydrate content, which is consistent with the results of Malhotra and Ciereszko [9, 50]. Under low P stress, in the glycolysis pathway, phosphoglycerate kinase [EC: 2.7.2.3] (LOC110685458) was down-regulated, and the content of the downstream substance glycerate-3P was reduced. Glycerate-3P is an intermediate that connects glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism, and is an upstream substance in fatty acid metabolism. Therefore, a decrease in its content can affect fatty acid synthesis [51]. UDP-d-xylose levels were significantly reduced under low P stress. UDP-d-xylose is a sugar donor used for the synthesis of glycoproteins, polysaccharides, various metabolites, and oligosaccharides in plants [52]. Glycoprotein synthesis also requires UDP-d-xylose [53]. As sugar donors for polysaccharides, various metabolites, and oligosaccharide synthesis, a decrease in their content reduces the glucuronic acid glycation reaction, affecting the accumulation of carbohydrates and fatty acids [54]. Based on the transcriptome results, hexokinase [EC: 2.7.1.1] (LOC110688727 and LOC110686362) and D-glucose were positively regulated in starch and sucrose metabolism. D-glucose, a basic sugar source unit, undergoes metabolic disturbances and a reduction in content induced by P deficiency signals, consequently impeding soluble carbohydrate synthesis and transportation. The reduction in carbohydrate metabolites also leads to a decrease in downstream phosphoenolpyruvate content. Under low P stress, soluble sugars directly enter the respiratory pathway, providing plant cell support [55]. In this study, these carbohydrate metabolites served as basic energy donors for the growth and development of flower spikes, and their content decreased, affecting the accumulation of carbohydrates in flower spikes and subsequently affecting the development of quinoa grains.

In purine metabolism, L-glutamine is involved in ATP synthesis. The levels of L-glutamine, IMP, AMP, and ADP decreased under low P stress. IMP, ATP, and ADP contain phosphate, which impairs energy synthesis and decomposition under low P stress. Adenylate kinase [EC: 2.7.4.3] (LOC110711388) was positively correlated with regulated ADP and catalyzed the phosphate transfer reaction between ADP and ATP [56]. An insufficient energy supply weakens intracellular energy metabolism, thereby affecting normal cell function and survival. L-Phenylalanine plays an important role in various biosynthetic pathways and is an important precursor for the synthesis of secondary biomass, such as flavonoids and isoflavones [57]. Under low P stress, L-phenylalanine content decreased under the catalysis of tyrosine amino transfer [EC: 2.6.1.5], whereas the related regulatory gene novel.2716 was significantly expressed in both varieties. However, in LB vs. CB, it was positively correlated with expression in LY vs. CY, showing a negative correlation, which may be caused by varietal differences.

In flavonoid biosynthesis, under low P stress, eriodictyol, luteolin, dihydrokaempferol, and kaempferol all increased in both varieties. These flavonoid metabolites exhibit antioxidant properties that facilitate the elimination of oxygen negative ions and mitigate the toxicity associated with low P production [58, 59]. Therefore, the accumulation of flavonoid metabolites is a key factor in the ability of quinoa to manage low P stress.

Core metabolic pathways and key genes of quinoa flower spikes in response to high P stress

Excessive P promotes plant respiration, leading to early development of reproductive organs, premature flowering and maturation, and reduced yield. High P stress can also affect carbohydrate distribution and utilization during the flowering and panicle stages. P stress signals cause metabolic disorders, thereby hindering soluble carbohydrate synthesis and transportation [60, 61]. The D-glucose, D-ribulose-5P, and glycerate-3P levels in HB vs. CB and HY vs. CY decreased, indicating that high P stress significantly inhibited the anaerobic and aerobic respiration processes of quinoa inflorescences.

The downstream phosphorylethanolamine of glycerate-3P also decreased under high P stress. Phosphorylethanolamine is the most abundant component of plant cell membrane phospholipids, and a decrease in its content can affect cell membrane synthesis [62]. Under high P stress, the fumarate, oxalocate, citrate, and GABA contents decreased while 2-oxoglutarate increased alanine, aspartate, and glucose metabolism. These organic acids are important intermediates in energy metabolism and contribute to the maintenance of ion balance and cell pH stability. A decrease in content indicates that the intracellular balance is disrupted under high P stress [63, 64]. The L-glutamate and L-glutamine contents decreased under high P stress, whereas that of 2-oxoglutarate increased. 2-Oxoglutarate is a key component of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and L-glutamate may be produced via transamination, resulting in a decrease in L-glutamate content and an increase in 2-oxoglutarate content [65]. ATP supply impairment can also lead to a decrease in L-glutamate and L-glutamine content. Combining transcriptome results, deoxycytidine is a deoxyribonucleoside that is positively correlated with 5′-deoxynucleotidase [EC: 3.1.3.89] (LOC110701427, YGK1). Deoxycytidine increases under high P stress and can be used to alleviate high P stress-induced damage.

The types of flavonoid metabolites differed in quinoa inflorescences under high and low P stress. Pinocembrin, pinostrobin, philorizin, naringenin chalcone, dihydrokaempferol, naringen, quercetin, and myricetin, which are flavonoid precursors, were significantly altered under high P stress. Furthermore, cinnamic acid and tyrosine decreased phenylpropanoid biosynthesis under high P stress, likely attributable to a significant reduction in energy metabolites, such as organic acids, which in turn affects the accumulation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and weakens the antioxidant capacity of quinoa inflorescences, leading to an intracellular imbalance [64, 66]. Combining transcriptome results, the downstream pinostrobin of cinnamic acid was positively correlated with chalcone isomerase [EC: 5.5.1.6] (LOC110721033), and its content decreased under high P stress. Naringenin chalcone, which is downstream of tyrosine, was positively correlated with chalcone synthesis [EC: 2.3.1.74] (novel.690, CHS), and its content increased under high P stress. The downstream dihydrokaempferol of naringenin chalcone was also affected. Quercetin content decreased under flavonoid 3′-monooxygenase regulation [EC: 1.14.14.82], whereas downstream myricetin content decreased. Under high P stress, plant energy metabolism is restricted, leading to flavonoid synthesis and metabolic activity inhibition, decreasing flavonoid content [60, 67, 68].

Conclusion

We combined transcriptomics and metabolomics to investigate the response mechanisms of two quinoa varieties to low and high P stress. A total of 53,202 genes, including 11,291 DEGs, were annotated, and 1981 DAMs were detected. The response of flower spikes to low and high P stress was influenced by multiple factors. The main responses to low P stress from the perspective of soluble sugar accumulation included changes in energy substrates and flavonoid metabolism. The main response mechanisms of flower spikes under high P stress were organic acid accumulation, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid metabolism. Joint analysis showed that LOC110688727, LOC110686362, LOC110685458, LOC110725246, LOC110733547, LOC110682232, LOC110682806, LOC110682954, LOC110684542, novel.780, and LOC110701427 were closely related to quinoa spike responses under low and high P stress. The findings of this study will help cultivators develop quinoa strains that are P stress tolerant, thereby accelerating the quinoa breeding process and industrialization.
