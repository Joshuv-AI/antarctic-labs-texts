Abrocitinib Treatment in Diffuse Alopecia Areata: A Case Report and a Literature Review
Authors: Xiaojing Zhang, Jianbo Zhong, Liming Wu, Ping Yang, Xingyun Zhao, Yingzhi Luo, Yunmi Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlopecia areata is an autoimmune disease with diverse clinical manifestations. This case report describes a patient with diffuse alopecia areata who responded well to oral abrocitinib, resulting in complete hair regrowth. The patient achieved complete recovery without any observed adverse effects.Keywords: alopecia areata, inflammation disease, Janus kinase inhibitor, therapeutics

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune condition characterized by non‐scarring hair loss on the scalp and may also affect other body areas [1]. Current treatment modalities include intralesional corticosteroids, topical immunotherapies, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, and systemic corticosteroids [2]. However, conventional corticosteroid therapy and topical minoxidil are not always effective. In June 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved baricitinib, a non‐selective JAK inhibitor for the management of AA [3]. In contrast, abrocitinib, which is a selective JAK‐1 inhibitor approved for atopic dermatitis, has not been evaluated in any Phase 3 clinical trial for AA. Moreover, real‐world evidence supporting the use of abrocitinib for AA remains limited to case reports and a recent retrospective cohort study [4]. This case report described a patient with diffuse AA who was successfully treated with abrocitinib, adding to the emerging evidence for this JAK‐1‐selective inhibitor as a promising therapeutic option for AA. We also summarized the global experience with abrocitinib in treating AA in both adult and pediatric populations, highlighting its favorable safety profile.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 30‐year‐old female presented to our outpatient clinic in June 2023 with a one‐month history of hair loss. She had received one glucocorticoid injection and used oral prednisone for 1 week. The patient discontinued treatment due to concerns about steroid‐related adverse effects, such as weight gain. Additionally, no significant improvement was observed. Physical examination was unremarkable except for extensive hair loss on the scalp vertex and frontal area, a positive hair‐pull test, and a Severity of Alopecia Tool (SALT) score of 40 (Figure 1A). Dermoscopic examination of the affected area revealed vellus hair and empty follicular openings. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, and thyroid function tests, were within normal limits. The patient was diagnosed with diffuse alopecia areata.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Oral abrocitinib therapy was initiated after informed consent was obtained. Pretreatment screening, including the T‐SPOT TB (T‐SPOT) assay, coagulation profile, female tumor markers, autoantibody panel, viral hepatitis serology, syphilis antibody test, and HIV antibody test, yielded normal results. Treatment was initiated with 100 mg of abrocitinib once daily. After 3 months, hair growth increased and density visibly improved (Figure 1B). The dosage was then reduced to 100 mg every other day for the following 6 months, after which the patient eventually achieved complete hair regrowth (Figure 1C). Conclusion and Results

After 3 months of treatment, hair shedding decreased and regrowth increased (Figure 1B). Complete hair restoration was achieved by 6 months (Figure 1C). Six months after treatment, dermoscopy of the scalp showed a decline in empty follicular openings accompanied by regrowth of normal black terminal hairs. Throughout the treatment period, routine monitoring of blood counts, liver and renal functions, and coagulation profiles showed no abnormalities. The patient did not report any adverse effects, such as headache or nausea. The patient received abrocitinib for a total of 9 months. Due to logistical constraints, she was unable to attend in‐person follow‐up visits. Consequently, post‐treatment telephone follow‐ups were conducted every 6 months for 2 years. We acknowledge that telephone follow‐ups have inherent limitations compared to in‐person assessments, including the lack of objective dermoscopic evaluation. The patient reported sustained normal hair growth without hair loss recurrence. The absence of adverse effects, as noted above, remains appropriately highlighted as a key finding that supports treatment safety.

Discussion

In murine AA models, cytotoxic CD8+ NKG2D+ T cells infiltrate the follicular epithelium, which promotes the loss of immune privilege and stimulates the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐15, thereby establishing a self‐sustaining autoimmune loop [5]. JAK–STAT pathway inhibitors interfere with IFN‐γ signaling, which may help restore hair growth [6]. Although abrocitinib is not currently approved for AA, growing evidence supports its potential efficacy in this condition [7].

Several recent studies have reported positive outcomes with abrocitinib in AA:

Lin et al. treated 12 patients with severe diffuse AA with abrocitinib 100 mg/day for 12 weeks, resulting in complete regrowth in four patients [8].

Our case shares key similarities with reported cases. Our patient responded to abrocitinib at 100 mg/day initially, and further improvement was observed with continued treatment over time. However, unlike most previous studies that used fixed‐dose regimens, we provided a detailed response‐driven tapering strategy. We also acknowledge the limitations of the study, including the single‐patient data and limited follow‐up. In summary, based on our experience and a review of the literature, abrocitinib at an initial dose of 100 mg/day may be considered for adult and pediatric patients with AA who show an inadequate response to conventional therapies. In severe cases, the dose may be increased to 200 mg/day and tapered to a maintenance regimen once a response is achieved [9]. Treatment was generally well tolerated across studies [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Nevertheless, future large‐scale clinical trials are required to establish standardized treatment protocols and confirm the long‐term efficacy and safety profile of abrocitinib use in AA. Future trials should address optimal dosing, tapering criteria, and endpoints, such as changes in the SALT score and relapse rate.
