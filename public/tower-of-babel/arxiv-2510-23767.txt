Re-framing the classification of ideals in noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry
Timothy De Deyn, Sam K. Miller

Abstract
. We prove that, given the Balmer spectrum of any essentially small monoidal-triangulated category, one has a classification of semiprime thick ⊗ \otimes -ideals arising in terms of a “pseudo-Hochster-dual” of the noncommutative Balmer spectrum. This extends Balmer’s classification of radical thick ⊗ \otimes -ideals to noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry. To achieve this, we utilize the notion of support data for lattices and frames, under which the classification is a consequence of Stone duality. We also give a characterization for when the noncommutative Balmer spectrum behaves as it does in tensor-triangular geometry, that is, when it is a spectral space with quasi-compact opens given by complements of supports, and show that rigid centrally generated monoidal-triangulated categories satisfy this property.

Introduction

Given a mountain, a mountaineer’s burning desire is to summit it, and given a tensor-triangulated category, a tensor-triangular geometer’s burning desire is to classify its thick
\otimes
-ideals. Such classification questions have presented themselves for a few decades now in widely varying fields, in differing languages and with differing motivations. Classical classification results in stable homotopy theory
[
DHS88
,
HS98
]
, algebraic geometry
[
Hop87
,
Nee96
,
Tho97
]
, and modular representation theory
[
BCR97
,
FP07
]
were united under Balmer’s theory of tensor-triangular geometry
[
Bal05
]
. In this setting, one associates to an essentially small tensor-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
a spectral topological space, now christened the
Balmer spectrum

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
, equipped with, for each object
x\in\mathscr{K}
, its
support

\operatorname{supp}(x)
, a closed subset with quasi-compact complement. Together, this data determines the
universal support theory
of
\mathscr{K}
.

One of Balmer’s key insights
[
Bal05
, Theorem 4.10]
fundamentally connects
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
to the classification of thick
\otimes
-ideals: one has an order-preserving map between the
radical
thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
(a negligible assumption if for instance
\mathscr{K}
is
rigid
) and the so-called
Thomason subsets
of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
, that is, unions of closed subsets with quasi-compact complement. Moreover, such a so-called
classifying support
is unique - if a topological space with support similarly classifies the radical thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
, it must in fact be
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})

[
Bal05
,
BKS07
]
.

As the world is fundamentally noncommutative, one need not limit themselves to a symmetric tensor product. Nakano–Vashaw–Yakimov
[
NVY22
]
, building on Buan–Krause–Solberg’s approach
[
BKS07
]
, formalized the theory of
noncommutative
tensor-triangular geometry, the study of
monoidal
triangulated categories.
Here, one has a noncommutative Balmer spectrum
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
, as well as a
complete prime spectrum

\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
, again with associated supports on objects. Again,
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is the universal
noncommutative
support datum, and Miller showed analogously that
\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
is the universal
multiplicative
support datum
[
Mil25
]
. But life without a commutative tensor product can be vexing, and the question of classification of thick
\otimes
-ideals was left unresolved. The first answer came from Nakano–Vashaw–Yakimov in their pioneering paper. The authors determined that if a space classifies thick
\otimes
-ideals under certain hypotheses (including rigidity, extension to a “big” category, and Noetherianity of the classifying space), then indeed the space must be
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
. Other examples of when
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
classifies
semiprime
thick
\otimes
-ideals (the noncommutative analogue of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals, which again becomes automatic if
\mathscr{K}
is rigid) subsequently followed:

(a)

when
\mathscr{K}
has a thick generator;
[
NVY23
]

(b)

when
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})=\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
;
[
MR23
]

(c)

when
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian.
[
Row24
]

However, all hope for a classification theorem mirroring the commutative case was lost when Huang–Vashaw dropped a bombshell
[
HV25
]
: an example of a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
whose thick
\otimes
-ideals are parametrized by the
specialization-closed
subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
, which in this example is a set strictly larger than the collection of Thomason subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
. Suddenly, the picture of how classification should look, or if it was always possible, had become rather murky.

Back in time, a lattice-theoretic point of view was brewing, as first suggested by Buan–Krause–Solberg
[
BKS07
]
and later developed in an algebro-geometric context by Koch–Pitsch
[
KP17
]
. The key insight is that in the tensor-triangular settings, the Hochster dual
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}
of the Balmer spectrum is the spectral space corresponding to the the
coherent frame
of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals via Stone duality, an antiequivalence between certain topological spaces and frames
[
Sto37
]
. Classification of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals is therefore a consequence of the duality. Gratz–Stevenson
[
GS23
]
and Krause
[
Kra23
,
Kra24
]
extended the lattice theory point of view further, demonstrating its potential use in wider contexts, such as tensor-
exact
geometry and noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry (although
[
BKS07
]
already had sown the noncommutative seeds). This perspective has been adopted in practical contexts recently
[
Aok23
,
MR23
,
BTGLS26
]
.

In this paper, we further harness Stone duality to settle the following looming classification questions of noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry:

Question
.

(a)

When can we classify the semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals of an essentially small monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
, given its universal support
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}),\operatorname{supp})
?

(b)

When does classification behave as in the tensor-triangular setting? That is,
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is a spectral topological space with quasi-compact open sets given by the complements of supports, and we have an order-preserving bijection between the Thomason subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
and the semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
?

The answer to the first question is, in short,
always
! We denote by
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
the lattice of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
, and given a topological space
X
, we denote the lattice of open subsets of
X
by
\Omega(X)
. We view
[
GS23
, Theorem 6.4.5]
as a precursor and inspiration to this theorem, as it observes that the noncommutative Balmer spectrum is recovered from
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
under the hypothesis that all ideals are semiprime.

Theorem A

(
Theorem

4.9
)
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be an essentially small monoidal-triangulated category. We have a natural isomorphism of lattices

T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\cong\Omega(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}),

where
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
denotes the “pseudo-Hochster dual” of
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}),\operatorname{supp})
. That is,
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}=\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
as a set with supports
\operatorname{supp}(x)
forming an
open
base for the topology.

We note that this “pseudo-Hochster dual” is not necessarily the true Hochster dual, since
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
and
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
need not be spectral, but one can still obtain one topology from the other, so long as the support data is known. There are two key observations which lead to this result. First, even if the lattice of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
is not necessarily a coherent frame, it is still
spatial
, and therefore, by definition, identifies with the lattice of open sets of its space of points. Second, Gratz–Stevenson
[
GS23
]
define the notion of a “prime with respect to semiprimes;” equivalently being a meet-prime element of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
. We show that semiprimes detect primality, that is, such a meet-prime is equivalently a prime thick
\otimes
-ideal of
\mathscr{K}
.

Perhaps the moral of the story here is that when it comes to classifying ideals, Stone duality suggests support should be open, in which case classification is a natural byproduct. There are two pseudo-dual topologies (and Hochster dual if things play nice) on the Balmer spectrum (and sometimes more, for example, the patch topology).
Of course, when the Balmer spectrum is spectral, they are easily identified via Hochster duality, but that may not always be the case!

A
answers the first question, so far so good.
For the second, Huang–Vashaw’s example
[
HV25
, Example 7.7]
shows that the answer to the second question cannot be “always”.
Indeed, the frame of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals is spatial, but not always coherent.
Recall that a principal semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal is one generated by single elements, so of the form
\langle x\rangle_{s}
, the smallest semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal containing
x\in\mathscr{K}
.
In the commutative case these always form a compact generating sublattice of the lattice of semiprime (i.e., radical) thick
\otimes
-ideals.
In the noncommutative case, however, these may not form a sublattice of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
or may not consist of compact elements.
We denote the sub-poset of principal semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals by
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, and call it the
principal part
of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.
Reframing the second question lattice-theoretically, the equivalent question becomes:

Question
.

When is
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
a coherent frame, with its sublattice of compact elements precisely
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, the sub-poset of the principally generated semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals?

The following definitions are necessary to answer the latter question.

Definition
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be an essentially small monoidal-triangulated category.

(a)

We say
\mathscr{K}
is
principally closed
if for all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
, we have
\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle z\rangle_{s}
for some
z\in\mathscr{K}
.

(b)

We say
\mathscr{K}
has
compact detection
if for all
x\in\mathscr{K}
, there exists a
s_{x}\in\mathscr{K}
such that the following holds: for all semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{I}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
,
x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
.

Compact detection is a mild assumption; it holds for instance if
\mathscr{K}
is half-rigid. More generally, it holds when all ideals are semiprime, see
Corollary

5.17
. Moreover, it holds if all semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals are radical. Principal closure is more restrictive, but still holds for all the ones where Balmer classification is known, and (weakly)
centrally generated
categories; a fact shown in
Section

7.1
. The condition of central generation has come to the foregrounds of noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry
[
NP23
,
NVY25
]
, and Negron-Pevtsova showed the condition occurs for stable module categories of certain (non-co-commutative) Hopf algebras.

We show
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed if and only if
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a sublattice of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, and is precisely the poset of compact elements if and only if
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection. Consequently, we obtain the following theorem, thereby answering the second question.

Theorem B

(
Theorem

6.1
,
Corollary

6.4
, and
Corollary

6.5
)
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathscr{K}
has principal closure and compact detection;

(b)

The poset
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a bounded distributive lattice consisting of the compact elements of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
;

(c)

The noncommutative Balmer spectrum
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is a spectral space with quasi-compact opens given by complements of supports,

K^{\circ}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}))=\{\operatorname{supp}(x)^{c}\mid x\in\mathscr{K}\};

If any of these conditions hold, we have an order-preserving bijection with the Thomason subsets

\displaystyle T_{s}(\mathscr{K})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow}\rule{-1.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}\operatorname{Th}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})),

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}\quad

\displaystyle\longmapsto\bigcup_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\operatorname{supp}(x).

In particular, all previously known examples where Balmer’s classification holds noncommutatively satisfy principal closure and compact detection. We note that it is still possible for
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
to be spectral without the two conditions holding.
However, for this to occur, compact detection must fail; in particular,
\mathscr{K}
cannot be rigid, so an example of this phenomena would be rather exotic.

Theorem C

(
Corollary

6.8
)
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be an essentially small monoidal-triangulated category which is principally closed and has compact detection, and let
(X,\sigma)
be a weak support datum (in the sense of
[

]
) for

(a)

If

Finally, we work out some small examples in

To establish our results, our main mantra (for which we claim no originality) is that support theory and Balmer’s classification result in tensor-triangular geometry is a direct translation of Stone duality.
Therefore, understanding when these hold in the noncommutative setting really necessitates us to understand the lattice theory underpinning
\otimes
-ideals.
Our observation that

is in fact a spatial frame then allows us to exploit the machinery of Stone duality.
In fact, Krause laid out much of the groundwork for coherent frames

]

For a spatial frame, its universal support is its space of points

For a coherent frame (such as the lattice of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals of a tensor-triangulated category), one can rephrase the support theories in terms of support of its sublattice of compact elements.

Lattice preliminaries

We start by recalling some lattice theoretic concepts. Our aim is to translate some standard tensor-triangular notions to the context of bounded distributive lattices. This is not a new idea, see also
[
KP17
,
GS23
,
Kra24
]
.
For a comprehensive review of general lattice theory, we refer the reader to
[

]
; for spectral spaces, to
[

]

1.1.
Bounded distributive lattices and coherent frames

Recall a
lattice

\mathscr{L}
is a partially ordered set (poset) such that every non-empty subset of elements has least upper bound and greatest lower bound, i.e., it has non-empty finite joins and meets, denoted
\vee
and
\wedge
respectively.
The empty join and meet, if they exist, are called the
bottom
and
top
and are denoted
0
and
1
; a lattice with a bottom and top is called
bounded
.
If joins distribute over meets, equivalently meets distribute over joins, the lattice is called
distributive
.

When
\mathscr{L}
has arbitrary joins it is called
complete
(this is equivalent to having arbitrary meets).
Moreover, if additionally arbitrary joins distribute over finite meets the lattice is called a
frame
.
An element
x\in\mathscr{L}
is called
compact
(also called
finite
,
finitely presented
or
small
) if for all subsets
S\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
x\leq\vee S
, there exists a finite subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
x\leq\vee S^{\prime}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A frame
\mathscr{F}
is called
coherent
if the compact elements form a bounded (necessarily distributive) sublattice and generate
\mathscr{F}
under joins, i.e., any element of
\mathscr{F}
is join of compact elements.

Remark 1.2
.

When the compact elements generate under join, but they do not necessarily form a sublattice, the frame is called
algebraic
.

To any lattice one can associate another lattice, that of its ideals, which is a coherent frame when the starting lattice is bounded and distributive.
An
ideal

\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is a non-empty downward set that is closed under finite joins. That is, if
x\in\mathscr{I}
and
y\leq x
, then
y\in\mathscr{I}
, and if
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathscr{I}
, then
x^{\prime}\wedge y^{\prime}\in\mathscr{I}
. The set of ideals is denoted
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
; it is a bounded, complete lattice.
An ideal
\mathscr{I}
of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
is
principal
if it is generated by a single element
x\in\mathscr{L}
. That is,
\mathscr{I}=\{y\in\mathscr{L}\mid y\leq x\}
. We denote the ideal generated by
x
by
\downarrow\hskip-2.79999ptx
. The principal ideals are precisely the compact elements of the ideal lattice
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
of
\mathscr{L}
and they generate under joins, demonstrating that
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
is coherent.
In fact, any coherent frame is necessarily of this form.

Proposition 1.3

(see e.g.,
[
Joh82
, Corollary II.3.3]
)
.

There is an equivalence of categories

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{BDLat}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{CohFrm}}\hbox to0.0pt{ ,\hss}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Id}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{c}}

between the category of bounded distributive lattices and the category of coherent frames.
The functor
\mathscr{L}\mapsto\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
maps a bounded distributive lattice to its lattice of ideals, whilst
\mathscr{F}\mapsto\mathscr{F}^{c}
maps a coherent frame to its sublattice of compact elements.

1.2.
Stone duality

Stone duality gives an antiequivalence between spatial lattices and sober topological spaces.
Let us recall these.
A topological space is
sober
if every nonempty irreducible closed subset has a unique generic point.
A point of a frame
\mathscr{F}
is a frame morphism, i.e., an order preserving map preserving arbitrary joins and finite meets,
\mathscr{F}\to\mathbf{2}
, where
\mathbf{2}
denotes the two-element bounded lattice
\{0,1\}
.

Definition 1.4
.

A frame
\mathscr{F}
is called
spatial
when it has enough points in the sense that whenever
x\not\leq y
in
\mathscr{F}
there exists a point
p\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathbf{2}
with
p(x)=1
and
p(y)=0
.

In
Propositions

1.7
and
1.9
, we will observe that points and spatiality translate into primality conditions.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
Sto37
, Stone duality]
)
.

There is a duality

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{SFrm}^{\operatorname{op}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{Sob}}\hbox to0.0pt{ ,\hss}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{pt}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}

where
\Omega
maps a space to its lattice of open subsets and
\operatorname{pt}
maps a spatial frame
\mathscr{F}
to its set of points together with the open subsets given by
U_{x}:=\{p\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathbf{2}\mid p(x)=1\}
for
x\in\mathscr{F}
.

.

Really there is an adjunction

For the more algebraically inclined reader, let us make the link between points and primes. Recall an element

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a frame. Then the space of points
p\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathbf{2}
corresponds to the set of meet-prime elements
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{F}
via the bijection

\mathfrak{p}\mapsto\left(p_{\mathfrak{p}}\colon x\mapsto\begin{cases}1&x\not\leq\mathfrak{p}\\
0&x\leq\mathfrak{p}\end{cases}\right).

Under this bijection, for all
x\in\mathscr{F}
, the open set
U_{x}=\{p\mid p(x)=1\}\in\Omega(\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}))
corresponds to the set of meet-primes
\{\mathfrak{p}\mid x\not\leq\mathfrak{p}\}=:\operatorname{supp}(x)
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify the assignment is well-defined and injective. It remains to show every point is of this form. Let
p\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathsf{Frm}}(\mathscr{F},\mathbf{2})
be a point.
As
p
is a morphism of frames,
p^{-1}(0)
is a prime ideal and taking
a:=\vee p^{-1}(0)

p^{-1}(0)=\downarrow\hskip-2.79999pta
and thus

is meet-prime. Furthermore, by construction

p(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\not\leq a\\
0&x\leq a\end{cases}

therefore
a
is our sought after meet-prime.
∎

Remark 1.8
.

We note that the terminology “support” of an element in
\mathscr{F}
hearkens back to the classical algebro-geometric and topological notions of support. Indeed, given a frame
\mathscr{F}
and an element
\mathfrak{i}\in\mathscr{F}
, one can define a quotient frame
\mathscr{F}/{\downarrow\hskip-2.79999pt\mathfrak{i}}
as follows.
Define a congruence relation of
\mathscr{F}
by
a\equiv_{\mathfrak{i}}b
if and only if there exist
i,j\in\downarrow\hskip-2.79999pt\mathfrak{i}
with
a\vee i=b\vee j
.
Then the quotient frame
\mathscr{F}/{\downarrow\hskip-2.79999pt\mathfrak{i}}
is the set of equivalence classes and it is readily verified it remains a frame (see e.g.,
[
DP66
]
).
It follows that
\operatorname{supp}(x)
is simply the collection of meet-primes
\mathfrak{p}
for which
x
does not identify with
0
in
\mathscr{F}/{\downarrow\hskip-2.79999pt\mathfrak{p}}
.
Topologically,
\operatorname{supp}(x)=U_{x}
and
U_{x}
is the collection of all points
p
whose support contains
x
.

Proposition 1.9
.

A frame
\mathscr{F}
is spatial if and only if each element of
\mathscr{F}
can be expressed as a meet of meet-prime elements.

Proof.

If every element is a meet of primes,
x\not\leq y
implies that there exists a prime
\mathfrak{p}
with
x\not\leq\mathfrak{p}
and
y\leq\mathfrak{p}
; glancing at
Proposition

1.7
we see that the corresponding point separates
x
and
y
.
Conversely, if
\mathscr{F}
is spatial and suppose
y:=\wedge_{x\leq\mathfrak{p}}\mathfrak{p}\not\leq x
then there is a prime
\mathfrak{p}
with
x\leq\mathfrak{p}
and
y\not\leq\mathfrak{p}
which is clearly contradicting the definition of
y
.
∎

1.3.
Hochster duality

In
[
Hoc69
, Proposition 8]
Hochster proved that spectral spaces have a duality theory, now referred to as Hochster duality.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
X
be a spectral space.
The
Hochster dual
of
X
, denoted by
X^{\vee}
, has the same underlying set as
X
and has topology generated by taking the closed subsets of
X
with quasi-compact complement as a basis of open subsets.

This is a duality, meaning
(X^{\vee})^{\vee}=X
.
The open subsets in
X^{\vee}
are sometimes referred to as
Thomason subsets
of
X
(or alternatively
inverse-open
subsets).
By definition, a Thomason subset is a union of complements of quasi-compact opens of
X
.

1.4.
The spectrum of a lattice

To finish this section we recall the following construction; it has appeared before in e.g.,
[
BKS07
,
DST19
,
Kra24
]
.

Definition 1.11
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice.
The
spectrum of
\mathscr{L}
is defined as the space of points of its ideal lattice,

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}):=\operatorname{pt}(\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})).

By definition, the spectrum of a lattice is a spectral space.
In order to give a, perhaps, more familiar description of the spectrum let us recall prime ideals of a lattice
\mathscr{L}
: a proper ideal
\mathscr{P}\subset\mathscr{L}
is
prime
if it is meet-prime in the ideal lattice
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
.
Equivalently, if
\mathscr{I}\cap\mathscr{J}\in\mathscr{P}
, for ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, then
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
.

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice.
The spectrum
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
identifies with the subset of
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
consisting of prime ideals.
The collection

\operatorname{supp}(x):=\{\mathscr{P}\in\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})\mid x\not\in\mathscr{P}\},\quad x\in\mathscr{L},

forms a basis of
open
sets for the topology on
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
.

Proof.

Follows from
Proposition

1.7
.
∎

We note that from the point of view of Stone duality, it is more natural to specify the supports as open, rather than closed, as one who is familiar with the tensor-triangular setting may expect.
As a warning: this is the opposite convention of the spectrum as defined in
[
Kra24
]
(but is the same as in
[
DST19
]
).
We promise the reader this is not an arbitrary choice, and the discrepancy will be justified in the sequel.
However, in this setup the choice of open versus closed is of minimal importance: one obtains the perhaps more familiar closed basis by applying Hochster duality for spectral spaces.

Lemma 1.13
.

The Hochster dual
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
of the spectrum, is as a set identical to
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
but has the supports as a
closed
basis for the topology.

Proof.

This follows since
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee},\operatorname{supp})=(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}^{op}),\operatorname{supp}^{c})
, see e.g.,
[
Kra24
, Section 4]
.
∎

One can translate many of the usual properties of primes for commutative rings or tensor-triangulated categories into this setting.
The essential ingredient is prime lifting.

Lemma 1.14

(Prime Lifting)
.

Let
F
be a filter (that is,
F
is an nonempty upward set and
x,y\in F
implies
x\wedge y\in F
) in a bounded distributive lattice.
Suppose
\mathscr{I}
is an ideal maximal among those disjoint from
F
. Then
\mathscr{I}
is prime.

Proof.

This is
[
Joh82
, Theorem I.2.4]
.
∎

As in the tensor-triangulated setting, the supports (or complements of supports) of elements are precisely the quasi-compact opens. This is analogous to
[
Bal05
, Lemma 2.13, Proposition 2.14]
.

Proposition 1.15

(
[
DST19
, 3.1.7]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice.
We have equalities

K^{\circ}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee})=\{\operatorname{supp}(x)^{c}\mid x\in\mathscr{L}\}\text{ and }K^{\circ}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}))=\{\operatorname{supp}(x)\mid x\in\mathscr{L}\}.

In particular, the Thomason subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
, equivalently the open subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
, are unions of supports.

2.
Support theory for frames

In this section we spell out support theory for lattices and frames, largely following and building on
[
BKS07
,
Kra24
]
(see also
[
DST19
, Chapter 3]
). Our main purpose is to understand when these support theories are “classifying”. In particular, we provide an analogue of
[
NVY22
, Theorem 6.2.1]
and
[
Del10
, Theorem 3.1]
giving a characterization of when a support datum is classifying.

The main takeaway here is that support theory can be viewed as a translation of Stone duality: support theories approximate a frame, and the universal support is that which reconstructs the frame (in which case, the frame is spatial). For this reason it is more natural to work with
open
support data instead of closed, but we briefly remark on the closed support data at the end, see
Remark

2.11
.

2.1.
Support data for spatial frames

This subsection is mostly tautological, but we introduce these ideas to motivate future constructions which are less tautological, and connect them back to Stone duality.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a spatial frame.

(a)

An
open support datum
on
\mathscr{F}
is a tuple
(X,\sigma)
consisting of a spatial space
X
and a frame morphism
\sigma\colon\mathscr{L}\to\Omega(X)
, more explicitly
\sigma
is required to satisfy the following properties:

(a)

\sigma(0)=\emptyset
,
\sigma(1)=X
,

(b)

\sigma(\vee_{i}x_{i})=\cup_{i}\sigma(x_{i})
,

(c)

\sigma(x\wedge y)=\sigma(x)\cap\sigma(y)
.

(b)

A morphism of support data
(X,\sigma)\to(Y,\tau)
is a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
such that
\sigma=f^{-1}\circ\tau
.

(c)

An open support datum
(X,\sigma)
on
\mathscr{F}
is
classifying
if
\sigma
is an isomorphism.

Note that a morphism of support data is an isomorphism if and only if the underlying map of topological spaces is a homeomorphism. The following observations are simply reformulations of Stone duality.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a spatial frame.

(a)

The pair
(\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}),\operatorname{supp})
is classifying and is the final open support datum on
\mathscr{F}
.
Explicitly, given any open support datum
(X,\sigma)
, the unique map
f\colon(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}),\operatorname{supp})
is given by

f(x)=\{a\in\mathscr{F}\mid x\not\in\sigma(a)\}

and satisfies
\sigma(x)=f^{-1}(\operatorname{supp}(x))
for all
x\in\mathscr{L}
.

(b)

Let
f\colon(X,\sigma)\to(Y,\tau)
be a morphism of open support data on
\mathscr{F}
.
If both support data are classifying, then
f\colon X\to Y
is a homeomorphism.
In particular, an open support datum
(X,\sigma)
is classifying if and only if the canonical morphism
(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}),\operatorname{supp})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

(a)
: The classifying part is exactly Stone duality which states that there is a natural isomorphism
\mathscr{F}\cong\Omega(\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}))
which is given by
x\mapsto\operatorname{supp}(x)
.
That it is final comes from

\mathsf{SFrm}(\mathscr{F},\Omega(X))\cong\mathsf{Sob}(X,\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F})).

(b)
: By definition of a support datum being classifying we have

\Omega(Y)\cong\mathscr{F}\cong\Omega(X)

and this is simply given by
V\mapsto f^{-1}(V)
since
\sigma=f^{-1}\circ\tau
.
As both
X
and
Y
are spatial, it follows that
f
is a homeomorphism.
∎

The idea here is that a support datum is a space which tries to reconstruct
\mathscr{F}
, and the classifying support datum is the universal one that completely reconstructs
\mathscr{F}.

2.2.
Support data for coherent frames

The previous section did nothing more than translate Stone duality.
However, when our frame
\mathscr{F}
is generated by some bounded distributive sublattice
\mathscr{L}
, there is a reduction in data one obtains by simply phrasing everything in terms of
\mathscr{L}
.
The key observation is that if
X
is a topological space, restriction along
\mathscr{L}\subset\mathscr{F}
yields a natural isomorphism

\mathsf{Frm}(\mathscr{F},\Omega(X))\cong\mathsf{BDLat}(\mathscr{L},\Omega(X)).

In this section we will focus on the case
\mathscr{F}
is a coherent frame and
\mathscr{L}
is its sublattice of compact elements. As these are the main examples we have in mind.
Moreover, we will restrict to spectral spaces for our support data; this way we get nice criteria to detect classifying.
As any coherent frame is of the form
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
for some bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
, i.e., its compacts, by
Proposition

1.3
we can translate everything simply in terms of
\mathscr{L}
.

The notion of classifying support data for a bounded distributive lattice coincides with the abstract classifying support datum on an ideal lattice from
[
BKS07
]
, which generalizes the classical tensor-triangular notion of
[
Bal05
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded lattice.

(a)

An
open support datum
on
\mathscr{L}
is a tuple
(X,\sigma)
consisting of spectral space
X
and a (bounded) lattice morphism
\sigma\colon\mathscr{L}\to\Omega(X)
. Explicitly
\sigma
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

\sigma(0)=\emptyset
,
\sigma(1)=X
,

(b)

\sigma(x\vee y)=\sigma(x)\cup\sigma(y)
,

(c)

\sigma(x\wedge y)=\sigma(x)\cap\sigma(y)
.

(b)

A morphism of support data
(X,\sigma)\to(Y,\tau)
is a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
such that
\sigma=f^{-1}\circ\tau
.

(c)

An open support datum
(X,\sigma)
is
classifying
if the induced support datum on
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
is classifying.
Explicitly, this means we have an order-preserving bijection
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\to\Omega(X)
induced by the assignments
\mathscr{I}\mapsto\cup_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\sigma(x)
, with inverse induced by the assignment
U\mapsto\{x\in\mathscr{L}\mid\sigma(x)\subseteq U\}
.

As the ideal lattice
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
is determined by its compact part
\mathscr{L}
when
\mathscr{L}
is distributive, one loosely speaking gets a reduction in information.
The spectrum of a lattice is our salient example of classifying support data. In this case,
Proposition

2.2
translates to lattice-theoretic versions of key results in
[
Bal05
,
BKS07
]
. Note that
Item

(a)
is
[
Kra24
, Theorem 7 & Corollary 10]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice.

(a)

The pair
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
is classifying and is the final open support datum on
\mathscr{L}
.

(b)

Let
f\colon(X,\sigma)\to(Y,\tau)
be a morphism of open support data on
\mathscr{L}
.
If both support data are classifying, then
f\colon X\to Y
is a homeomorphism.
In particular, an open support datum
(X,\sigma)
is classifying if and only if the canonical morphism
(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
is an isomorphism.

Next, we give a lattice-theoretic analogue of
[
Del10
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
NVY22
, Theorem 6.2.1]
- in particular, we provide conditions for a support datum to be classifying, and hence homeomorphic to the spectrum. We do this in the “small” setting, see
Remark

2.9
for how this differs from the setting of “big” triangulated categories.

Definition 2.5
.

We say an open support datum
(X,\sigma)
on a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
satisfies

(a)

injectivity
if
\sigma
is an injective map of sets,

(b)

faithfulness
if
\sigma(x)=\emptyset
implies
x=0
,

(c)

realization
if the quasi-compact opens subsets
K^{\circ}(X)
lie in the image of
\sigma
,

(d)

Noetherian realization
if
\sigma
is surjective.

Proposition 2.6
.

An open support datum
(X,\sigma)
for a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
is classifying if and only if it injective and realizing.
In particular,
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
(uniquely) satisfies injectivity (hence faithfulness) and realization.

Proof.

Consider the order-preserving mapping

(1)

\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\to\Omega(X),\ \mathscr{I}\mapsto\bigcup_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\sigma(x).

By definition
(X,\sigma)
is classifying if and only if this map is a bijection.
But as both sides are coherent frames,
Equation

1
is a bijection if and only if it induces an isomorphisms on the compact parts, i.e.,
\sigma
induces a bijection

\mathscr{L}\to K^{\circ}(X),\ x\mapsto\sigma(x).

Clearly this is equivalent to
\sigma
being injective and realizing.
∎

Noetherian realization is strong as it implies
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
is Noetherian.
This is an analogue of the assumptions in
[
NVY22
, Theorem 6.2.1]
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Suppose an open support datum
(X,\sigma)
for a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
is injective and Noetherian realizing. Then
X
is Noetherian and
(X,\sigma)
is a classifying support datum.

Proof.

As Noetherian realizing is stronger than realizing,
(X,\sigma)
is classifying. To see Noetherianity of
X
use
[
BF11
, Proposition 7.13]
and the fact that by Noetherian realization, every open is quasi-compact.
∎

We warn that Noetherian realization only implies that
X
is Noetherian, but says nothing about the Noetherianity of
X^{\vee}
. In the sequel, we will reformulate a corresponding theorem for closed support data, with an analogous Noetherianity-detection statement for
X^{\vee}
, see
Definition

6.7
.

We can rephrase these results in terms of the universal map into the spectrum.
Observe that by Stone duality, a continuous map of spectral topological spaces
f\colon X\to Y
is a monomorphism (resp. epimorphism) if and only if the induced map on the coherent lattices of opens
f^{*}\colon\Omega(Y)\to\Omega(X)
is an epimorphism (resp. a monomorphism).
Moreover, a map of spectral spaces
f
is a monomorphism (resp. epimorphism) if and only if it is a continuous injective (resp. surjective) map if and only if
f^{*}
is surjective (resp. injective), see e.g.,
[
DST19
, Theorems 5.2.2 & 5.2.5]
.
Note, however, that epimorphisms of bounded distributive lattices are not necessarily surjective, although any surjective lattice homomorphism is an epimorphism (see
[
Mag
]
for an example of a non-surjective epimorphism). Therefore one cannot quite state the following purely in terms of the bounded distributive lattice using
Proposition

1.3
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be an open support datum for a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
.
Then,

(a)

the universal map
(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
is surjective if and only if
(X,\sigma)
is injective, i.e.,
\sigma\colon\mathscr{L}\to\Omega(X)
is injective.

(b)

the universal map
(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
is injective if and only if the map
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\to\Omega(X)
induced by
\sigma
is surjective.
In particular, the latter is the case if
(X,\sigma)
is realizing.

Proof.

If
f\colon(X,\sigma)\to(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}),\operatorname{supp})
is the support data induced by
\sigma
, then

f^{*}\colon\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\allowbreak=\Omega(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L}))\to\Omega(X)
is simply the map induced by
\sigma
.
The statements then follow from the above discussion.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

We require injectivity over faithfulness in contrast to
[
NVY22
, (5.1.1)]
or
[
Del10
, Theorem 3.1(S9)]
. Indeed, it is clear from the proof that we genuinely require
\sigma
to be an injective homomorphism.
However, the weaker condition of
\sigma(x)=\emptyset
implies
x=0
does not imply
\sigma
is an injective lattice homomorphism without additional assumptions. For an easy example, consider the three element poset
\mathbf{3}
and the homomorphism given by the assignment
0\mapsto 0
and
1,2\mapsto 2
.

In fact, given an essentially small tt-category
\mathscr{K}
, we may construct a support datum on on
\mathscr{K}
(which corresponds to a support datum as above), satisfying faithfulness and realization, but which is not necessarily isomorphic to the Balmer spectrum.
Let
X
be the subset of closed points of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
endowed with the subset topology and let
\sigma={\operatorname{supp}}|_{X}
be induced from restriction.
Then it is a routine verification that
(X,\sigma)
is a well-defined support datum satisfying faithfulness and realization for
\mathscr{K}
, but if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
has non-closed points, then
X\not\cong\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
.

The key point, which is certainly known to the experts, is that one needs extended support data on big categories, where the Rickard idempotents and (co)localization sequences live, to show faithfulness implies injectivity on the lattice; see
[
Del10
, Lemma 3.5]
and
[
NVY22
, Theorem 5.3.1]
.

Given the above remark, it is useful to note injectivity is equivalent to the key property of order-reflection shown in
[
BCR97
, Lemma 3.3]
,
[
Tho97
, Lemma 3.14]
, and
[
KP17
, Lemma 4.1.9]
.
This a key property for showing that their respective supports indeed are classifying and therefore are the universal support.

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose
(X,\sigma)
is an open support datum for a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
. Then
(X,\sigma)
is injective if and only if
\sigma
is order-reflecting, i.e., the following holds: for any
x,y\in\mathscr{L}
,
x\leq y
if and only if
\sigma(x)\subseteq\sigma(y)
.

Proof.

Note one implication is automatic as
\sigma
is order preserving.
(e.g.,
x\leq y
is equivalent to
x\vee y=y
, so
\sigma(y)=\sigma(x\vee y)=\sigma(x)\cup\sigma(y)
and
\sigma(x)\subseteq\sigma(y)
.)

First, suppose
(X,\sigma)
is injective. If
x\not\leq y
, then
y\neq x\vee y
.
Consequently, by assumption,
\sigma(y)\neq\sigma(x\vee y)=\sigma(x)\cup\sigma(y)
and hence
\sigma(x)\not\subseteq\sigma(y)
.
Conversely, if
(X,\sigma)
is not injective, there are
x\neq y
with
\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)
.
Clearly,
x\not\leq y
or
y\not\leq x
showing that
\sigma
does not reflect the order.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

In the abstract lattice-theoretic setting we consider in this section, it is natural to speak about open support data, as this is more in line with the language of Stone duality.
One can still speak of closed support data, changing the lattice of open subsets by the lattice of closed subsets in the definition. With this view, one can make sense of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
as the universal closed support datum, as in
[
Kra24
, Theorem 5]
, and state similar detection theorems for classifying support data along the lines of
Proposition

2.6
.

A closed support datum being classifying, as for example in
[
BKS07
]
, means that the closed support data yields an isomorphism
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\cong\Omega(X^{\vee})
, i.e., an isomorphism with the Thomason subsets of
X
. This requires that the closed support datum factors through the sublattice of closed subsets with quasi-compact complement, in which case, through Hochster duality, one can translate everything into open support data.

3.
A lattice-theoretic Cohen’s theorem

In
[
Bar25
]
Barthel proved a tensor triangular version of Cohen’s theorem
[
Coh50
]
, characterizing those tt-categories with weakly Noetherian spectrum whose spectrum is finite: these are exactly those for which every radical
\otimes
-ideal is finitely generated. We extend this result here, and in the context of open support data, the result describes Noetherianity on the nose.
Weakly-Noetherian spectral spaces were introduced in
[
BHS23
]
to expand the theory of stratification to big tt-categories with non-Noetherian Balmer spectra. Zou
[
Zou23
]
further showed that the notion is optimal - if a tt-category is stratified, its Balmer spectrum is weakly Noetherian.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a spectral space
X
, we say
X
is
inverse-
P
, for any property
P
, if
X^{\vee}
satisfies property
P
. For instance, if
X^{\vee}
is Noetherian, we say
X
is
inverse-Noetherian
(note
X
need not be Noetherian).

Definition 3.2

(
[
BHS23
, Definition 2.3]
)
.

Let
X
be a spectral space.

(a)

A subset
V\subseteq X
is
weakly visible
if it is the intersection of a Thomason subset and the complement of a Thomason subset (i.e., the intersection of an open set and a closed set in
X^{\vee}
).

(b)

A point
x\in X
is
weakly visible
if the set
\{x\}
is and
X
is
weakly Noetherian
if all its points are.

Remark 3.3
.

Again,
X
is spectral as in the previous definition.

(a)

Noetherian spectral spaces and
T_{1}
(equivalently Hausdorff, equivalently profinite) spectral spaces are weakly Noetherian, see
[
BHS23
, Remark 2.4]
.

(b)

By
[
Bar25
, Lemma 3]
,
X
is finite if and only if
X
is weakly Noetherian and inverse-Noetherian.

We translate Barthel’s results to the lattice-theoretic setting. The proofs are the same as in
[
Bar25
]
, replacing the tensor-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
with a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
, the lattice of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
with the ideal lattice
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
,
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
with
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
, and finitely generated ideals with compact elements of
\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})
, i.e., principal ideals (by (
Proposition

1.3
).

Proposition 3.4

(
[
Bar25
, Proposition 6]
)
.

For a bounded distributive lattice
\mathscr{L}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
is inverse-Noetherian, i.e.
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})
is Noetherian;

(b)

every ideal of
\mathscr{L}
is principal;

(c)

every prime ideal of
\mathscr{L}
is principal;

Corollary 3.5

(
[
Bar25
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a bounded distributive lattice. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
is weakly Noetherian and every ideal in
\mathscr{L}
is principal;

(b)

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
is weakly Noetherian and every prime ideal in
\mathscr{L}
is principal;

(c)

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
is finite.

The weak Noetherianity condition for
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{L})^{\vee}
is necessary: as Barthel notes, the stable homotopy category of finite
p
-local spectra
\operatorname{Sp}^{\text{fin}}_{(p)}
gives a counterexample (see
Definition

5.2
for how one translates this to a bounded distributive lattice).

4.
Noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry

We shift our focus to noncommutative tensor-triangular geometry, applying our lattice-theoretic machinery towards the classification of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals, i.e., the noncommutative analogue of Balmer’s classification of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals in tensor-triangular geometry
[
Bal05
, Theorem 4.10]
.

4.1.
Recollections

Notation 4.1
.

Throughout, we assume
\mathscr{K}
is an
essentially small monoidal-triangulated category
, in particular the tensor product is exact but not necessarily symmetric or braided.

See, e.g.,
[
EGNO15
]
for more on monoidal categories and
[
Nee01
,
HJR10
]
for more on triangulated categories.

Definition 4.2

(
[
NVY22
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category.

(a)

A
thick subcategory
of
\mathscr{K}
is a full triangulated subcategory of
\mathscr{K}
closed under direct summands. A thick subcategory
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
is a
thick
\otimes
-ideal
if in addition, for all
x\in\mathscr{I}
and
y\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes y\in\mathscr{K}
and
y\otimes x\in\mathscr{K}
.

(b)

A proper thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{P}\subset\mathscr{K}
is
prime
if for all thick
\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}
of
\mathscr{K}
,
\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. Equivalently
\mathscr{P}
is prime if for all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
x\in\mathscr{K}
or
y\in\mathscr{K}
. Finally,
\mathscr{P}
is
completely prime
if for all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes y\in\mathscr{P}
implies
x\in\mathscr{P}
or
y\in\mathscr{P}
. If
\mathscr{K}
is symmetric, every prime ideal is completely prime.

(c)

A thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{I}
is
semiprime
if it is an intersection of prime thick
\otimes
-ideals (with the convention that
\mathscr{K}
is the empty intersection). Equivalently,
\mathscr{I}
is semiprime if for all
x\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
implies
x\in\mathscr{I}
, or for any thick
\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{J}
of
\mathscr{K}
,
\mathscr{J}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
implies
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
.

(d)

A thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{I}
is
radical
if for all
x\in\mathscr{K}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
x^{\otimes n}\in\mathscr{I}
. When
\mathscr{K}
is symmetric, radical equals semiprime.

We write
T(\mathscr{K})
(resp.
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
and
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
) to denote the lattice of thick
\otimes
-ideals (resp. semiprime and radical thick
\otimes
-ideals). Given an element
x\in\mathscr{K}
, we write
\langle x\rangle
(resp.
\langle x\rangle_{s}
and
\langle x\rangle_{r}
) to denote the thick
\otimes
-ideal (resp. semiprime and radical thick
\otimes
-ideal) generated by
x
.

Remark 4.3
.

More concrete descriptions of the hulls are given as follows:

\langle x\rangle
is the intersection of all ideals containing
x
, and equivalently the collection of all objects which can be built from
x
in finitely many steps by taking cones, direct summands, and tensoring by objects of
\mathscr{K}
on the left or right. By definition,
\langle x\rangle_{s}
is the intersection of all prime ideals containing
x
, and by
[
Mil25
, Theorem 6.4]
,
\langle x\rangle_{r}
is equivalently the intersection of all completely prime ideals containing
x
(if any exist).

Definition 4.4
.

The
noncommutative Balmer spectrum

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
of a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
is, as a set, the collection of prime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
equipped with the topology defined by taking the supports
\operatorname{supp}(x):=\{\mathscr{P}\in\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})\mid x\not\in\mathscr{P}\}
as a basis of
closed
subsets.

The Balmer spectrum was introduced by Balmer for tensor-triangulated categories (i.e., symmetric monoidal-triangulated categories)
[
Bal05
]
, and extended to the noncommutative setting by Nakano–Vashaw–Yakimov
[
NVY22
]
.
The connection to lattices was first established in Buan–Krause–Solberg
[
BKS07
]
, expanded on by Kock–Pitsch
[
KP17
]
, and recently revisited by Gratz–Stevenson and Krause
[
GS23
,
Kra24
]
.

4.2.
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a spatial frame

We will predominantly focus on the lattice of semiprimes. Meets and joins have a simple description, demonstrating that
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a distributive lattice (in fact, as we shall see below, it is always a spatial frame).
By
[
GS23
, Lemma 6.4.2]
, for
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, we have
\mathscr{I}\wedge\mathscr{J}=\mathscr{I}\cap\mathscr{J}=\langle\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\rangle_{s}=\langle\mathscr{J}\otimes\mathscr{I}\rangle_{s}
.
Moreover,
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is complete with arbitrary meets corresponding to intersections, and arbitrary joins, for
\{\mathscr{J}_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, are given as
\vee_{i}\mathscr{J}_{i}=\langle\cup_{i}\mathscr{J}_{i}\rangle_{s}
.

The following definition was introduced in
[
GS23
]
.

Definition 4.5
.

We say a thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{P}
is
prime with respect to semiprimes
if for all
semiprime

\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}
,
\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
.

Of course, a prime thick
\otimes
-ideal is prime with respect to semiprimes. We claim that, in fact, any thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{P}
is prime with respect to semiprimes if and only if it is prime. The key is a semiprime analogue of
[
NVY22
, Lemma 3.1.2]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For every two collections
\mathscr{M},\mathscr{N}\subset\mathscr{K}
of objects, we have an inclusion

\langle\mathscr{M}\rangle_{s}\otimes\langle\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}.

Proof.

This follows essentially the same as the proof of
[
NVY22
, Lemma 3.1.2]
with one additional technicality. First, we show that
\langle\mathscr{M}\rangle_{s}\otimes\mathscr{N}\subseteq\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
. Let
\mathscr{I}
denote the full subcategory of objects
x
such that for all
n\in\mathscr{N}
and
y\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes y\otimes n\in\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
. Obviously,
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
.

We claim that
\mathscr{I}
is a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal. Verifying
\mathscr{I}
is thick, triangulated, and a
\otimes
-ideal follows identically as in
loc. cit.
To show
\mathscr{I}
is semiprime, let
x\in\mathscr{K}
and suppose
x\otimes z\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
for all
z\in\mathscr{K}
. Then equivalently,
x\otimes z\otimes x\otimes y\otimes n\in\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
for all
z,y\in\mathscr{K}
and
n\in\mathscr{N}
. In particular, setting
z=y\otimes n\otimes z^{\prime}
for any
z^{\prime}\in\mathscr{K}
yields

(x\otimes y\otimes n)\otimes z^{\prime}\otimes(x\otimes y\otimes n)\in\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}

for all
y,z^{\prime}\in\mathscr{K}
and
n\in\mathscr{N}
, therefore semiprimality implies
x\otimes y\otimes n\in\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
, hence
x\in\mathscr{I}
as desired. Because
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
and
\mathscr{I}
is a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal,
\langle\mathscr{M}\rangle_{s}\subseteq\mathscr{I}
as well. Therefore,
\langle\mathscr{M}\rangle_{s}\otimes\mathscr{N}\subseteq\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
.

Then by analogous arguments, we have
\mathscr{M}\otimes\langle\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
.
Lastly, replacing
\mathscr{N}
by
\langle\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
in the previous paragraph and using the last sentence we have
\langle\mathscr{M}\rangle_{s}\otimes\langle\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes\mathscr{N}\rangle_{s}
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a thick
\otimes
-ideal. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathscr{P}
is prime, i.e., for all thick
\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}
,
\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
;

(b)

\mathscr{P}
is meet-prime in
T(\mathscr{K})
;

(c)

For all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
,
x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
x\in\mathscr{P}
or
y\in\mathscr{P}
.

(d)

\mathscr{P}
is prime with respect to semiprimes, i.e., for all semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}
,
\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
;

(e)

\mathscr{P}
is meet-prime in
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.

Proof.

(a)

\Leftrightarrow

(c)
and
(d)

\Leftrightarrow

(e)
are
[
NVY22
, Theorem 3.2.2]
and
[
GS23
, Lemma 6.4.3]
respectively, and
(a)

\Leftrightarrow

(b)
is by definition.
Evidently
(a)
implies
(d)
, so it rests to show
(d)
implies
(c)
.
Suppose
\mathscr{P}
is prime and
x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. Since
\mathscr{P}
is semiprime we have using
Lemma

4.6
an inclusion

\langle x\rangle_{s}\otimes\langle y\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\rangle_{s}\subseteq\mathscr{P},

and primality implies
\langle x\rangle_{s}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\langle y\rangle_{s}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. Therefore
x\in\mathscr{P}
or
y\in\mathscr{P}
, as desired.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. The lattice
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals is a spatial frame.

Proof.

We first verify
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a frame; for this we must verify for all semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals,
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}_{i}
for
i\in I
, that

\mathscr{I}\cap\Big\langle\bigcup_{i}\mathscr{J}_{i}\Big\rangle_{s}=\Big\langle\bigcup_{i}(\mathscr{I}\cap\mathscr{J}_{i})\Big\rangle_{s}.

The left-hand side is identically the intersection of all prime thick
\otimes
-ideals containing either
\mathscr{I}
or containing all
\mathscr{J}_{i}
, and the right-hand side is the intersection of all prime thick
\otimes
-ideals containing, for each
i\in I
, either
\mathscr{I}
or
\mathscr{J}_{i}
. These two conditions on containment are equivalent,
hence equality holds. Now,
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is spatial from
Proposition

1.9
and
Proposition

4.7
, as every element is an intersection of prime ideals, which are equivalently the meet-primes of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. We have a natural isomorphism of lattices

T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow}\rule{-1.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}\Omega(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}),~\mathscr{I}\mapsto\bigcup_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\operatorname{supp}(x),

where
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
denotes the “pseudo-Hochster dual” of
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}),\operatorname{supp})
. That is,
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}=\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
as a set with supports
\operatorname{supp}(x)
forming an
open
base for the topology.

Proof.

Proposition

1.7
asserts
\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
identifies with the meet-primes of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
with the supports forming an open base for the topology, and
Proposition

4.7
asserts the meet-primes are precisely the prime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
.
Therefore,
\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K}))=\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
.
The statement then reduces to Stone duality, as
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is spatial by
Corollary

4.8
. Therefore, we have natural isomorphisms
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\cong\Omega(\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K})))=\Omega(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu})
, where the latter isomorphism is given by
Proposition

1.7
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

In
Theorem

4.9
, the space
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
is not the genuine Hochster dual of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
, since
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
need not be a spectral space.
However, one can still construct
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
from
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
and vice versa, but only when one also has the support data
\operatorname{supp}
for
\mathscr{K}
; for one these form a basis of closed sets and for the other a basis of open sets - in this sense the datum
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}),\operatorname{supp})
and
(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu},\operatorname{supp})
are “dual,” hence the terminology “pseudo-Hochster dual.” On the other hand, if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is spectral, then
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}=\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
; in this case we write
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}
.

Finally, it is routine to see that via the lattice isomorphism of
Theorem

4.9
, compact objects of the lattice
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
correspond to quasi-compact opens of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
.

4.3.
On group actions on monoidal-triangulated categories

To show that in general
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
need not be a coherent frame, we briefly remark on group actions on monoidal-triangulated categories, the corresponding crossed product categories, and Huang-Vashaw’s classification result
[
HV25
, Proposition 7.4]
. We note that such categories encompass stable module categories of the Hopf algebras considered by Benson–Witherspoon
[
BW14
]
and Bergh–Plavnik–Witherspoon
[
BPW24
]
which demonstrate particularly noncommutative behavior, such as the tensor product property not holding for
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
.

Here, semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals are parametrized by specialization-closed subsets, which can be a proper superset of Thomason subsets, as
[
HV25
, Example 7.7]
shows. We briefly review the relevant constructions and results.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
via monoidal-triangulated autoequivalences. We set the
crossed product category
of
\mathscr{K}
by
G
to be the direct sum

\mathscr{K}\rtimes G:=\bigoplus_{g\in G}\mathscr{K}

indexed by elements of
G
. For any element
x\in\mathscr{K}
, the corresponding object in
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
indexed by
g\in G
is denoted
x\boxtimes g
. We set

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{K}\rtimes G}(x\boxtimes g,y\boxtimes h):=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{K}}(x,y)&g=h,\\
0&g\neq h.\end{cases}

The monoidal product is given by the formula

(x\boxtimes g)\otimes(y\boxtimes h):=\left(x\otimes g(y)\right)\boxtimes gh.

We say a thick subcategory of
\mathscr{K}
is a
G
-ideal if it is a
G
-stable
\otimes
-ideal, and say
\mathscr{P}
is a
G
-prime
\otimes
-ideal if for all
G
-ideals
\mathscr{I},\mathscr{J}
,
\mathscr{I}\otimes\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
implies
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
or
\mathscr{J}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. We warn the reader that a
G
-prime
\otimes
-ideal may not necessarily be a prime
\otimes
-ideal!

Proposition 4.12

(
[
HV25
, Proposition 5.4]
)
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
.

(a)

The assignment
\mathscr{I}\mapsto\mathscr{I}\rtimes G:=\{i\boxtimes g\mid i\in\mathscr{I},g\in G\}\subseteq\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
is an order-preserving bijection between the
G
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and the thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
.

(b)

The bijection restricts to a homeomorphism
G
-
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})\cong\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)
.

Proposition 4.13

(
[
HV25
, Proposition 7.4]
)
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a half-rigid
\operatorname{Spc}
-Noetherian monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
. The
G
-Balmer support
\Phi_{G}
on
G
-
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
induces a bijection between
G
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and specialization-closed subsets of
G
-
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
.

Observation 4.14
.

If
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian, then every specialization-closed subset of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is a union of supports (c.f.
[
Row24
, Remark 3.6]
).
When
\mathscr{K}
is half-rigid the same holds true for

\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)
; even though the latter need not be Noetherian again.
Indeed, a specialization-closed subset of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)
is a union of closed subsets, and by the proof of
[
HV25
, Proposition 7.4]
any closed subset is a union of supports.
It follows that the specialization-closed subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)
correspond bijectively to open subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)^{\nu}
.
Consequently,
[
HV25
, Proposition 7.4]
is a special case of
Theorem

4.9
.

Furthermore, in
[
HV25
, Example 7.7]
the authors give an example of a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
with non-spectral Balmer spectrum, as the spectrum itself is not quasi-compact; hence, an example of a category
\mathscr{K}
with
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
a non-coherent frame.
From what we show below, see also
Remark

6.6
, this category must have two principal semiprime ideals whose intersection is not principally generated; consequently, the sub-poset of compact objects of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
does not form a sublattice. Hence neither
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
nor
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
are spectral topological spaces.

Unfortunately, it does not seem easy to describe the sets corresponding to the open subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}\rtimes G)^{\nu}
when
\mathscr{K}
is no longer Noetherian, since the arguments in
[
HV25
]
rely on this.

It would be interesting to know if there are conditions characterizing when open subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
correspond exactly to the specialization-closed subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
(and therefore, when the bijection given in
[
HV25
, Proposition 7.4]
holds). When
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is spectral with supports corresponding exactly to the complements of quasi-compact open subsets, this holds if and only if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian, but if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is non-spectral, it is evident that other scenarios arise.

We will revisit crossed product categories again in the sequel, after more terminology is introduced. Under mild hypotheses, we will see that some crossed product categories still remain well-behaved, see
Section

7.2

5.
Coherence and the principal part

We saw in
Theorem

4.9
that unlike in the symmetric case, is is
not
the Thomason subsets of the Balmer spectrum that classify the semiprime ideals.
The reason for this is exhibited in
4.14
:
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
can in general fail to be a coherent frame.
However, once it is a coherent frame (and the compact elements are what they should be), one recovers the ‘usual’ classification (c.f.
[
Bal05
, Theorem 4.10]
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. Then
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
is a spectral space if and only if
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is coherent.
Furthermore, if the supports
\operatorname{supp}(x)
are exactly the quasi-compact open subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
, we obtain an order-preserving bijection

T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\cong\operatorname{Th}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}))

between the semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and the Thomason subsets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
.

Proof.

As
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}
is precisely
\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
the first assertion follows from Stone duality.
For the second, if the supports are exactly the quasi-compact open subsets, then
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}
is the genuine Hochster dual of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
. The open subsets of the Hochster dual are exactly the Thomason subsets.
∎

Thus in order to obtain the more common classification in terms of Thomason subsets of the Balmer spectrum and support, we need to know when
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a coherent frame and when the supports exactly correspond to quasi-compact opens.
This latter condition, again by Stone duality, translates to when the finitely generated semiprime ideals are exactly the compacts elements in
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
. Therefore, we aim to answer the following question:

Question
.

When is
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
a coherent frame, with its sublattice of compact elements corresponding to the principally generated semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals
\langle x\rangle_{s}
?

5.1.
The principal part

To this end we need to understand the subposet of finitely generated, equivalently principal, semiprime ideals, which we call the principal part.

Definition 5.2
.

We let
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
denote the subposet of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
consisting of principal semiprime
\otimes
-ideals.
We call
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
the
principal part
of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.

In general this is only a join sublattice of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
(see
Lemma

5.5
below).
An alternative way of thinking about this join sublattice is as follows.

Remark 5.3

(c.f.
[
Kra24
, Section 6]
)
.

For
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
, we write
x\sim y
if and only if
\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle y\rangle_{s}
. This is an equivalence relation, and the set of equivalence relations
L_{s}(\mathscr{K}):=\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{K})/{\sim}
obtains a partial order induced via inclusions of the corresponding semiprime ideals. We call
L_{s}(\mathscr{K})
the
(semiprime) object poset
of
\mathscr{K}
.

Remark 5.4
.

(a)

As already alluded to,
L_{s}(\mathscr{K})\cong t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
by
[x]\mapsto\langle x\rangle_{s}
.

(b)

One can play a similar game by looking at the principal parts of the lattices
T(\mathscr{K})
or
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
and define principal parts
t(\mathscr{K})
or
t_{r}(\mathscr{K})
.
Alternatively, one can think of these in terms of objects up to equivalence relation using
\langle-\rangle
and
\langle-\rangle_{r}
instead of
\langle-\rangle_{s}
to define
L(\mathscr{K})
and
L_{r}(\mathscr{K})
as was done in
[
Kra24
,
Mil25
]
. Since our main goal is the classification of semiprime ideals, we will, for the most part, not consider non-semiprime ideals.

(c)

For radical thick
\otimes
-ideals, the situation behaves as it does in the symmetric case. Krause shows that for radical thick
\otimes
-ideals,
L_{r}(\mathscr{K})
is exactly the compact part of
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
, hence
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
is a coherent frame
[
Kra24
, Proposition 14]
. Miller concludes that
\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})=\operatorname{Spc}(L_{r}(\mathscr{K}))^{\vee}
, hence one has Balmer classification of radical thick
\otimes
-ideals in terms of
\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
, and
\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
is spectral. However,
\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
can be empty, see
[
Mil25
, Theorem 8.5]
- this corresponds to
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
having one element,
\mathscr{K}
.

In order to understand the principal part, we need to understand the joins and meets of principal semiprime
\otimes
-ideals.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
. Then in
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, we have an equality

\langle x\oplus y\rangle_{s}=\langle x\rangle_{s}\vee\langle y\rangle_{s}.

Moreover, we have an inclusion

\langle(x\otimes y)\oplus(y\otimes x)\rangle_{s}\leq\langle x\rangle_{s}\wedge\langle y\rangle_{s},

and equality holds for all elements in
\mathscr{K}
if and only if all semiprime ideals of
\mathscr{K}
are radical if and only if all primes of
\mathscr{K}
are completely prime.

Proof.

First, since
x,y\in\langle x\oplus y\rangle_{s}
by thickness,
\langle x\rangle_{s}\vee\langle y\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle x\oplus y\rangle_{s}
. On the other hand,
x\oplus y\in\langle x\rangle_{s}\vee\langle y\rangle_{s}
, hence
\langle x\oplus y\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle x\rangle_{s}\vee\langle y\rangle_{s}
, as desired.

For the latter statement, the inequality is obvious. If equality holds, then in particular,
\langle x\otimes x\rangle_{s}=\langle x\rangle_{s}\wedge\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle x\rangle_{s}
implying all semiprime ideals are radical. Conversely suppose all semiprime ideals are radical. Then if
z\in\langle x\rangle_{s}\wedge\langle y\rangle_{s}
, then
z\in\langle z\otimes z\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle x\otimes y\rangle_{s}
, and similarly for
y\otimes x
. Finally, for the last statement, if equality holds, then
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})=t_{r}(\mathscr{K})
, therefore
T_{r}(\mathscr{K})=T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
and
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})=\operatorname{Spc}^{cp}(\mathscr{K})
by
[
Mil25
, Proposition 6.2]
. Conversely if all primes are completely prime, then all semiprimes are radical since every semiprime is an intersection of radical primes.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

For radical thick
\otimes
-ideals, joins and meets mirror the symmetric case,
\langle x\rangle_{r}\vee\langle y\rangle_{r}=\langle x\oplus y\rangle_{r}
and
\langle x\rangle_{r}\wedge\langle y\rangle_{r}=\langle x\otimes y\rangle_{r}
, as suggested by the previous proposition. The proof is left as an easy exercise.

5.2.
Principal closure

Thus
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
has joins but not necessarily meets, since the intersection of two principal (or equivalently, finitely generated) semiprimes may not again be principal (as noted after
[
GS23
, Example 4.4.4]
for the non-tensor setting). Indeed,

\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\rangle_{s}

in the poset of semiprime ideals, and in general the right hand side may not be finitely generated. Therefore we consider the following class of monoidal-triangulated categories for which this property does hold.

Definition 5.7
.

We say
\mathscr{K}
is
principally closed
if for all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
,
\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle z\rangle_{s}
for some
z\in\mathscr{K}
.

This condition holds in the cases referenced in the intro where one has Balmer classification of thick
\otimes
-ideals.

Example 5.8
.

(a)

If
\mathscr{K}
is a tensor-triangulated category, or more generally the tensor product is (potentially non-functorially) commutative, then the primes equal the completely primes and
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed by
Lemma

5.5
.

(b)

More generally, if every semiprime ideal of
\mathscr{K}
is radical, then
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed also by
Lemma

5.5
.

(c)

If
\mathscr{K}
has a thick generator
g
then
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed. Indeed, in this case we have

\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle\langle x\rangle_{s}\otimes\langle y\rangle_{s}\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes g\otimes y\rangle_{s}.

(d)

If
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\vee}
is Noetherian, then
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed. Indeed, for any
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
the set

\{\operatorname{supp}(\langle x\otimes S\otimes y\rangle_{s})\mid S\subseteq\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{K})\text{ finite}\}

of quasi-compact open sets of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
satisfies the ascending chain condition.

Additionally, if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian, then
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed; this follows from
Corollary

6.4
in the sequel and results of Rowe
[
Row24
]
.

If
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed, then
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is well-behaved: it is distributive.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be principally closed. Then
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a bounded distributive lattice.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{K}
is principally closed,
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a bounded sublattice of the (spatial) frame
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.
Consequently it inherits distributivity from the latter.
∎

This fact does not hold in general, for the analogous poset of principal (not necessarily semiprime)
\otimes
-ideals
t(\mathscr{K})
, see
[
GS23
, Example 7.1.4]
.

5.3.
Compact elements in the lattice of semiprimes

Our desire, motivated by
Proposition

1.3
, is that
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is the sublattice of compact objects of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.
We determine exactly when this occurs.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
x\in\mathscr{K}
. We say
x
has compact detection
in
\mathscr{K}
if and only if there exists an element
s_{x}\in\mathscr{K}
for which the following holds: given any semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
, we have
x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x\subseteq\mathscr{I}
; equivalently,
\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x\rangle_{s}
.
We say the element
s_{x}

witnesses compact detection
for
x
.
We say
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection
if all elements of
\mathscr{K}
have compact detection.

Remark 5.11
.

Of course, instead of requiring a single element in the definition above it suffices to ask there exists a finite subset of elements
S_{x}
such that given any semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
, we have
x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
x\otimes S_{x}\otimes x\subseteq\mathscr{I}
.

Compact detection is a very mild assumption, as the following examples demonstrate.

Example 5.12
.

If
\mathscr{K}
is rigid (or half-rigid), then
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection by choosing
s_{x}=x^{*}
or
{}^{*}x
for every element
x\in\mathscr{K}
. If every semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal of
\mathscr{K}
is radical (e.g., if
\mathscr{K}
is symmetric), then
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection by choosing
s_{x}=1
for every element
x\in\mathscr{K}.

Additionally, if
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian, then
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection by
Corollary

6.4
below and results of Rowe
[
Row24
]
.

It turns out compact detection is the exact condition one requires for our wants to be satisfied.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category.
The compact elements of the lattice of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
are precisely the principal ideals
\langle x\rangle_{s}
if and only if
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.

We prove two lemmas first.

Lemma 5.14
.

Any non-principal ideal
\mathscr{I}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
cannot be compact.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathscr{I}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is compact.
As
\mathscr{I}=\vee_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\langle x\rangle_{s}
compactness gives a finite subset
A\subseteq\mathscr{I}
with
\mathscr{I}=\vee_{a\in A}\langle a\rangle_{s}=\langle\oplus_{a\in A}a\rangle_{s}
and so is principal.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Suppose
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.
For any thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{I}
with
x\in\langle\mathscr{I}\rangle_{s}
, there exists
y\in\mathscr{K}
witnessing compact detection for
x
such that
x\otimes y\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
.
Consequently, if
S\subseteq\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{K})
is a set of elements of
\mathscr{K}
with
x\in\langle S\rangle_{s}
, there exists a finite subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
x\in\langle S^{\prime}\rangle_{s}
.

Proof.

If
x\in\mathscr{I}
, any
y
witnessing compact detection for
x
satisfies
x\otimes y\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
. Thus, we may assume
x\notin\mathscr{I}
.
Let
x_{1}:=x
and pick an
a_{1}\in\mathscr{K}
witnessing compact detection for
x_{1}
, which exists by assumption, and put
x_{2}:=x_{1}\otimes a_{1}\otimes x_{1}
.
Then, inductively for
n>1
, define
x_{n+1}:=x_{n}\otimes a_{n}\otimes x_{n}
where
a_{n}
is chosen to witness compact detection for
x_{n}
.
By construction, for any semiprime
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{J}
,
x\in\mathscr{J}
if and only if
x_{n}\in\mathscr{J}
for any
n>1
. Thus as every
x_{i}
is of the form
x\otimes s_{i}\otimes x
, necessarily
s_{i}
witnesses compact detection for
x
.

The set
\mathscr{M}:=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots\}
is a nc-multiplicative in the sense of
[
NVY23
, Appendix A]
. Suppose for contradiction that
\mathscr{M}\cap\mathscr{I}=\emptyset
. Then by
[
NVY23
, Lemma A.1.1]
there exists a prime
\mathscr{P}\in\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
such that
\mathscr{P}\supseteq\mathscr{I}
and
\mathscr{M}\cap\mathscr{P}=\emptyset
.
However, since
x\in\langle\mathscr{I}\rangle_{s}
by assumption,
x\in\mathscr{P}
for all primes
\mathscr{P}\supseteq\mathscr{I}
(as the semiprime hull of an ideal is equivalently the intersection of all primes containing that ideal).
As
x\in\mathscr{M}
, this is a contradiction.
Thus, there exists some
x_{i}\in\mathscr{I}
as desired.

For the final statement, if
x\in\langle S\rangle_{s}
pick a
y
as above with
x\otimes y\otimes x\in\langle S\rangle
.
Since every element of
\langle S\rangle
can be built from elements of
S
in finitely many steps via tensoring on the left and right, taking summands, and taking cones we may take
S^{\prime}
to be the finite set of elements of
S
involved in the construction of
x\otimes y\otimes x
.
Thus
x\otimes y\otimes x\in\langle S^{\prime}\rangle
and consequently
x\in\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes y\otimes x\rangle_{s}\subseteq\langle S^{\prime}\rangle_{s}
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.13
.

By
Lemma

5.14
it suffices to show that every principal is compact if and only if
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.

Suppose every principal is compact. Fix
x\in\mathscr{K}
.
We have
x\in\langle x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes x\rangle_{s}=\vee_{y\in\mathscr{K}}\langle x\otimes y\otimes x\rangle
, so by assumption there exists a finite subset
S\subseteq\mathscr{K}
for which
\langle x\rangle_{s}=\vee_{y\in S}\langle x\otimes y\otimes x\rangle
.
Taking the sum of the elements in
S
gives an element witnessing compact detection for
x
. Hence,
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.

Conversely, suppose
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection. Pick
x\in\mathscr{K}
and suppose
\{\mathscr{I}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a collection of semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals with
\langle x\rangle_{s}\leq\vee_{i\in I}\mathscr{I}_{i}=\langle\cup_{i}\mathscr{I}_{i}\rangle_{s}
.
By
Lemma

5.15
there exist a finite subset
S\subset\cup_{i}\mathscr{I}_{i}
with
x\in\langle S\rangle_{s}
; clearly, this implies there is a finite subset
I^{\prime}\subset I
with
\langle x\rangle_{s}\leq\vee_{i\in I^{\prime}}\mathscr{I}_{i}
showing
\langle x\rangle_{s}
is compact.
∎

The following corollary can be observed directly from the proof of
Proposition

5.13
.

Corollary 5.16
.

If
\langle x\rangle_{s}
is a compact element of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, then
x\in\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.

Corollary 5.17
.

Suppose all ideals of
\mathscr{K}
are semiprime (e.g.,
\mathscr{K}
is rigid). Then
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection.

Proof.

By assumption
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})=T(\mathscr{K})
the lattice of thick
\otimes
-ideals.
Hence, by
Proposition

5.13
, it suffices to show that the compact elements of
T(\mathscr{K})
are precisely the principal ideals
\langle x\rangle
.
This is well-known.
The same argument as
Lemma

5.14
shows that any compact needs to be principal and the fact that principals are compact was already used in
Lemma

5.15
; i.e., any object in
\langle S\rangle
can be built from elements of
S
in finitely many steps via tensoring on the left and right, taking summands, and taking cones.
∎

Remark 5.18
.

(a)

The converse of
Corollary

5.17
does not hold;
\mathscr{K}
having compact detection does not imply all thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
are semiprime. This can easily be seen in the tensor-triangular setting: if
\mathscr{K}
is a tensor-triangulated category, then
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})=T_{r}(\mathscr{K})
and
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})=t_{r}(\mathscr{K})
.
Hence
\mathscr{K}
always has compact detection, but there are small tensor-triangulated categories with non-radical thick
\otimes
-ideals.

(b)

[
GS23
, Theorem 6.4.5]
can be viewed, after a bit of yoga, as a reformulation of
Theorem

4.9
with the assumption that all ideals are semiprime. It is certainly the processor.
The dual topology provided in
[
GS23
, Theorem 6.4.5]
is defined by declaring the closed sets to be generated by the quasi-compact open subsets of
\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
.
Since, in particular, their assumptions imply
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection, the quasi-open subsets are exactly the supports of objects
\operatorname{supp}(x)
.
Thus, this coincides exactly with the topology of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})^{\nu}
.

(c)

If follows immediately that when
\mathscr{K}
satisfies compact detection that
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is algebraic, as the principal ideals always generate under join. Of course, it could be algebraic without compact detection holding as well.

(d)

If
\mathscr{K}
satisfies compact detection and principal closure, then one can say more about the element giving the intersection; namely, for all
x,y\in\mathscr{K}
, if
\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}
is principal and hence compact
\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes\mathscr{K}\otimes y\rangle_{s}
implies there exists a
z\in\mathscr{K}
with
\langle x\rangle_{s}\cap\langle y\rangle_{s}=\langle x\otimes z\otimes y\rangle_{s}
(cf. the proof of
Proposition

5.13
).

6.
Control of the principals

We have everything we need to characterize when the lattice
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is coherent in such a way that the usual classification in terms of Thomason subsets of the noncommutative Balmer spectrum
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
goes through.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. The following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathscr{K}
is principally closed and has compact detection;

(b)

T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
is a coherent frame with sublattice of compact elements
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.

Proof.

The principals always generate under join, i.e., for any
\mathscr{I}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
one has
\mathscr{I}=\vee_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\langle x\rangle_{s}
.
Moreover,
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
are the compacts if and only if compact detection holds and these form a sublattice if and only if principal closure holds.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

One can also rephrase this in terms of the object poset.
The conditions of the theorem are equivalent to
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow}\rule{-1.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}\operatorname{Id}(L_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
under the map
\mathscr{I}\mapsto\{[x]\in L_{s}(\mathscr{K})\mid\langle x\rangle_{s}\subseteq\mathscr{I}\}
.

Remark 6.3
.

We can see how
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
can fail to satisfy the conditions of
Theorem

6.1
.
If principal closure fails to hold, then
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
fails to be a sublattice of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, but still can generate
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.
If compact detection fails on the other hand,
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
will contain non-compact elements of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
, the compact elements of
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
may or may not form a distributive lattice, but it is not clear they generate
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
.

In principle, it seems conceivable that
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})
could be a coherent frame whose compact elements are properly contained in
t_{s}(\mathscr{K})
- this cannot happen if
\mathscr{K}
has a commutative tensor product. It would be interesting to know if there are any interesting examples of essentially small monoidal-triangulated categories
\mathscr{K}
where this occurs. Such a category would necessarily have to be non-rigid, so our expectation is that examples may be rather pathological.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathscr{K}
is principally closed and has compact detection;

(b)

The noncommutative Balmer spectrum
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is a spectral space with quasi-compact opens given by complements of supports,

K^{\circ}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}))=\{\operatorname{supp}(x)^{c}\mid x\in\mathscr{K}\}.

Proof.

For
(a)
implies
(b)
, we have
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})=\operatorname{Id}(t_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
and therefore
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})=\operatorname{Spc}(t_{s}(\mathscr{K}))^{\vee}
, where the latter is the spectrum of the bounded distributive lattice as defined in
Section

1.4
, whose quasi-compacts are characterized by
Proposition

1.15
.

Conversely, suppose
(b)
.
Then, the Hochster dual of
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is exactly
\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K}))
.
As this is the (genuine) Hochster dual of a spectral space, this shows
\Omega(\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K})))
is a coherent frame with compact elements precisely the supports
\{\operatorname{supp}(x)\mid x\in\mathscr{K}\}
.
As
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})=\Omega(\operatorname{pt}(T_{s}(\mathscr{K})))
, with principals corresponding to the supports of elements, the principals are exactly the sublattice of compact objects, as desired.
∎

Furthermore, Balmer’s classification theorem holds in its original form.

Corollary 6.5

(c.f.
[
Bal05
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

If
\mathscr{K}
has principal closure and compact detection then we have an order-preserving bijection
T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\cong\operatorname{Th}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}))
induced by the following assignments:

\mathscr{I}\mapsto\bigcup_{x\in\mathscr{I}}\operatorname{supp}(x)\in\operatorname{Th}(\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K}))

Z\mapsto\{x\in\mathscr{K}\mid\operatorname{supp}(x)\subseteq Z\}\in T_{s}(\mathscr{K}).

Proof.

Follows directly from
Theorem

5.1
and
Corollary

6.4
.
∎

6.1.
A note on functoriality

Stone duality is an antiequivalence, hence for every homomorphism of coherent frames
f\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
one obtains a corresponding continuous homomorphism on the space of points
f^{*}:\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F}^{\prime})\to\operatorname{pt}(\mathscr{F})
, and vice versa. Similarly, given any morphism of bounded distributive lattices
l:\mathscr{L}\to\mathscr{L}^{\prime}
, one obtains a corresponding morphism on their ideal lattices
\operatorname{Id}{(l)}:\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L})\to\operatorname{Id}(\mathscr{L}^{\prime})
, and conversely if one has a compact-preserving morphism of coherent frames
f\colon\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}^{\prime}
, it restricts to a morphism on their compact elements,
f^{c}:\mathscr{F}^{c}\to(\mathscr{F}^{\prime})^{c}
, necessarily a morphism of bounded distributive lattices.

The reason why functoriality generally fails for
\operatorname{Spc}(-)^{\nu}
is clear: an exact monoidal functor
F\colon\mathscr{K}\to\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
may not yield a meet preserving map on lattices.
Any such functor induces induces a map of
posets

\hat{F}:T_{s}(\mathscr{K})\to T_{s}(\mathscr{K}^{\prime}),\ \mathscr{I}\mapsto\langle F(\mathscr{I})\rangle_{s}
which respects joins.
However without strong hypotheses, such as
T_{s}(\mathscr{K}^{\prime})=T_{r}(\mathscr{K}^{\prime})
(i.e.,
\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
satisfies the tensor product property) or
F
essentially surjective,
\hat{F}
may in general fail to preserve meets.
The same remains true for the restricted morphisms
\hat{F}:t_{s}(\mathscr{K})\to t_{s}(\mathscr{K}^{\prime})
, even when
\mathscr{K}
and
\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
are principally closure.
Hence for functoriality, it seems the lattice perspective offers little more than what was already deduced in
[
Mil25
, Section 5]
.

7.1.
(Weak) central generation implies principal closure

We consider the (weak)

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal-triangulated category.

(a)

We say

satisfies the

if all thick
\otimes
-ideals of

such that

(possibly non-functorially) for all objects

. If

satisfies the central generation hypothesis if its compact part

(b)

We say
\mathscr{K}

\mathscr{K}
for which all thick
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
are generated by collections of elements which centralize

We refer to

Of course, taking

for
\mathscr{K}
satisfying the central generation hypothesis shows it also satisfies weak central generation hypothesis. Again, if
\mathscr{T}
is a big monoidal-triangulated category, we say
\mathscr{T}
satisfies the weak central generation hypothesis if

does.

Proposition 7.3
.

Suppose

\otimes
-ideal of

Proof.

Let us first show that for any

central with respect to

\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle x^{\prime}\rangle_{s}

\langle x\rangle_{s}=\langle S\rangle_{s}

central with respect to

implies that there exists a finite subset

Now, we have for any

We note that if

Lemma 7.4
.

Every
G
-prime of
\mathscr{K}
is semiprime, i.e., an intersection of primes, if and only if the bijection between
G

\mathscr{K}
and
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
(see
[

, Proposition 5.4]
) restricts to a bijection between semiprime
G
-ideals and semiprime
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
.

Proof.

As we have a lattice isomorphism between the
G
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and the
\otimes
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
, it necessarily restricts to an isomorphism between those elements that are meets of meet-prime elements, i.e., the semiprime elements of the lattice. For
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
, these are precisely the semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals, so it suffices to show that every
G
-prime of
\mathscr{K}
is semiprime if and only if every semiprime element in the lattice of
G
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
is a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal. If every
G
-prime of
\mathscr{K}
is semiprime, then since every semiprime object in the lattice is an intersection of
G
-primes, every such object is also an intersection of honest primes, and so a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideals. Conversely, if there exists a
G
-prime of
\mathscr{K}
not a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal, then, as it is trivially a semiprime element of the lattice, there exists such an element not a semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal.
∎

Of course, the conditions in
Lemma

7.4
are trivially satisfied when all ideals of
\mathscr{K}
are semiprime, e.g., if
\mathscr{K}
is half-rigid.
[
HV25

prove a stronger condition under stronger hypotheses; the authors show every
G
-prime is an intersection of a
G
-orbit of a prime, assuming
\operatorname{Spc}(\mathscr{K})
is Noetherian and
\mathscr{K}
is half-rigid.

With the above property for
G
-primes,
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
automatically inherits compact detection from
\mathscr{K}
.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal triangulated category and let
G
be a group acting on
\mathscr{K}
via autoequivalences. If all
G
-primes of
\mathscr{K}
are semiprime and
\mathscr{K}
has compact detection, then
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
has compact detection.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathscr{K}
and
g\in G
, and suppose
s_{x}
witnesses compact detection for
x
. We claim
s_{x\boxtimes g}:=g^{-1}(s_{x})\boxtimes g^{-1}
witnesses compact detection for
x\boxtimes g
. Indeed, first note that

(x\boxtimes g)\otimes(g^{-1}(s_{x})\boxtimes g^{-1})\otimes(x\boxtimes g)\cong(x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x)\boxtimes g.

Moreover, by
Lemma

7.4
, any semiprime thick
\otimes
-ideal
\mathscr{J}=\mathscr{I}\rtimes G
of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
satisfies that
\mathscr{I}\subseteq\mathscr{K}
is semiprime as well. Therefore,
x\boxtimes g\in\mathscr{J}
if and only if
x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x\in\mathscr{I}
if and only if
(x\otimes s_{x}\otimes x)\boxtimes g\in\mathscr{J}
, as desired.
∎

[
HV25
, Example 7.7]
demonstrates that a crossed product category formed from a monoidal-triangulated category
\mathscr{K}
satisfying principal closure need not satisfy principal closure again. However, if
G
is finite, this can be salvaged.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a monoidal triangulated category and let
G
be a finite group acting on
\mathscr{K}
via autoequivalences. If all
G
-primes of
\mathscr{K}
are semiprime and
\mathscr{K}
satisfies principal closure, then so does
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
.

Proof.

In fact, the bijection between semiprime
G
-ideals of
\mathscr{K}
and semiprime ideals of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
from
Lemma

7.4
preserves principal ones.
Indeed, if
\langle x\rangle_{s}
is a
G
-ideal of
\mathscr{K}
, then
\langle x\rangle_{s}\rtimes G
is a principal semiprime ideal of
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
, with generator
x\boxtimes 1
. Conversely, if
\mathscr{I}\rtimes G
is semiprime and principally generated by
x_{1}\boxtimes g_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n}\boxtimes g_{n}
, then
\mathscr{I}
is principally generated by

x:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\bigoplus_{g\in G}g(x_{i}).

The result now follows since
(\mathscr{I}\rtimes G)\cap(\mathscr{J}\rtimes G)=(\mathscr{I}\cap\mathscr{J})\rtimes G
.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

The key obstruction preventing this statement from holding in the infinite group case is that
\mathscr{K}\rtimes G
may have principal semiprime ideals
\mathscr{I}\rtimes G
for which
\mathscr{I}
may not be a principal semiprime
G
-ideal of
\mathscr{K}
. Indeed, for any
x\in\mathscr{K}
, the semiprime ideal
\mathscr{I}=\langle g(x)\mid g\in G\rangle_{s}
may not be a principal semiprime, but
\mathscr{I}\rtimes G
is principal and semiprime, generated by
x\boxtimes 1
. In this case, one cannot use principal closure of
\mathscr{K}
to deduce anything about intersections with this principal semiprime.
