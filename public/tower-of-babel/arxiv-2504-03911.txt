Coxeter systems, left inversion sets, and higher dimensional cubes
Harrison Gimenez

Abstract
. Let ( W , S ) (W,S) be a Coxeter system. We investigate the equation w ⁡ ( Φ x ) = Φ y w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y} where w , x , y ∈ W w,x,y\in W and Φ x \Phi_{x} , Φ y \Phi_{y} denote the left inversion sets of x x and y y . We then define a commutative square diagram called a Coxeter square which describes the relationship between 4 non-identity elements of the Coxeter group W W and the equation w ⁡ ( Φ x ) = Φ y w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y} . Coxeter squares were first introduced by Dyer, Wang in [ 5 ] and [ 7 ] . Coxeter squares can be “glued" together by compatible edges to form commutative diagrams in the shape of higher dimensional cubes called Coxeter n n -cubes, which were first defined by Dyer in Example 12.5 of [ 5 ] . When | W | < ∞ |W|<\infty and | S | = n |S|=n , we show that Coxeter n n -cubes must exist within ( W , S ) (W,S) . We then prove results about Coxeter n n -cubes in the A n A_{n} Coxeter system. We establish an explicit bijection between Coxeter n n -cubes (modulo orientation) in A n A_{n} and binary trees with n + 1 n+1 leaves. We also show that an element x x of A n A_{n} appears as the edge of some Coxeter n n -cube if and only if x x is a bigrassmannian permutation.

1.
Introduction

Coxeter systems are interesting mathematical objects of study due to their relationship with group theory, representation theory, and combinatorics. The canonical root system
\Phi
associated to a Coxeter system
(W,S)
as defined in
[
9
]
encodes geometric and combinatorial information about the Coxeter group
W
. Given an element
x\in W
, we let
\Phi_{x}
denote the set of elements of
\Phi^{+}
that are sent into
\Phi^{-}
by the element
x^{-1}
. The set
\Phi_{x}
is called the left inversion set of
x
. This paper studies the equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
where
w,x,y\in W
. In other words, we are interested in determining when a left inversion set is transferred to a left inversion by an element of
W
. As motivation for studying left inversion sets, we note that left inversion sets relate to reduced expressions of elements, the weak right order, and the root poset of a Coxeter system. In the case when
x=s
,
y=s^{\prime}
where
s,s^{\prime}\in S
, the equation
w(\Phi_{s})=\Phi_{s^{\prime}}
becomes
w(\alpha_{s})=\alpha_{s^{\prime}}
where
\alpha_{s}
,
\alpha_{s^{\prime}}
denote the simple roots corresponding to
s
,
s^{\prime}
respectively. Thus, the specific case of
w(\Phi_{s})=\Phi_{s^{\prime}}
asks when
w
maps a simple root to a simple root. The transfer of simple roots is important in the fundamental “exchange condition" of Coxeter systems and for studying morphisms of Brink-Howlett groupoids as defined by Brink and Howlett in
[
2
]
. Morphisms of a Brink-Howlett groupoid are elements of
W
that map subsets of simple roots to subsets of simple roots. Studying the more general equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
may provide insights to when
w
maps a collection of left inversion sets to a collection of left inversion sets. Transferring collections of left inversion sets to collections of left inversion sets allows for a generalization of the Brink-Howlett groupoid to a wider class of groupoids (see Example 4 of
[
7
]
).

In Section
2
, we review some basic definitions and constructions related to the Coxeter system
(W,S)
. We define the canonical root system associated to
(W,S)
as in Humphreys
[
9
]
. We also state some propositions that connect left inversion sets to the reflection cocycle and weak right order of
(W,S)
.

In Section
3
, we define Brink-Howlett groupoids and establish their basic properties. We describe standard generators for Brink-Howlett groupoids, defined originally by Howlett and Deodhar.

In Section
4
, we prove some propositions regarding the equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
. We relate that equation to the equation
wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)
where
N(x)
denotes the reflection cocycle of
x
. In the specific case when
|W|<\infty
, the equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
implies that
w
is a right divisor of
w_{0}x^{-1}
where
w_{0}
denotes the longest element of
W
.

In Section
5
, we define Coxeter squares and Coxeter
n
-cubes. When
|W|<\infty
and
|S|=n
, we show that Coxeter
n
-cubes must exist within
(W,S)
. We then prove results about Coxeter
n
-cubes in the
A_{n}
Coxeter system. We establish an explicit bijection between Coxeter
n
-cubes (modulo orientation) in
A_{n}
and binary trees with
n+1
leaves. We also show that an element
x
of
A_{n}
appears as the edge of some Coxeter
n
-cube if and only if
x
is a bigrassmannian permutation.

2.
Background Material on Coxeter Systems

We let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of non-negative integers (so that
0\in\mathbb{N}
). We define
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\geq 2\}
. For a comprehensive background on Coxeter systems, we recommend both
[
1
]
and
[
9
]
.

2.1.
Coxeter systems

Let
S
be a set. We define a function
m:S\times S\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that the following hold:

(1)

Let
s,s^{\prime}\in S
. Then
m(s,s^{\prime})=1
if and only if
s=s^{\prime}
. If
s\neq s^{\prime}
, then
m(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\cup\{\infty\}
.

(2)

Let
s,s^{\prime}\in S
. Then
m(s,s^{\prime})=m(s^{\prime},s)
.

Such a function
m:S\times S\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying the above properties is called a
Coxeter matrix
. A
Coxeter System
is an ordered pair
(W,S)
where

(1)

S
is a set.

(2)

m:S\times S\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is a Coxeter matrix.

(3)

W
is a group such that
W=\langle{s\in S\mid\forall s,s^{\prime}\in S,\ (ss^{\prime})^{m(s,s^{\prime})}=1}\rangle_{group}
.

If
m(s,s^{\prime})=\infty
, then we omit the relation
(ss^{\prime})^{\infty}=1
from the above presentation of the group
W
.
W
and
S
are referred to as the
Coxeter group
and the
Coxeter generators
respectively. We also define
T:=\{wsw^{-1}\mid s\in S,\ w\in W\}
.
T
is called the
set of reflections of
(W,S)
.

2.2.
Standard parabolic subgroups

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
J\subseteq S
. Define
W_{J}
to be the subgroup of
W
generated by
J
. The subgroup
W_{J}
is called a
standard parabolic subgroup of
W
.
(W_{J},J)
is a Coxeter system with the following presentation

W_{J}:=\langle{s\in J\mid\forall s,s^{\prime}\in J,\ (ss^{\prime})^{m(s,s^{\prime})}=1}\rangle_{group}

and
(W_{J},J)
is called a
standard parabolic subsystem of
(W,S)
.

2.3.
The length function

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
w\in W
. We define the
length function

\ell:W\to\mathbb{N}
so that:

\ell(w):=\textnormal{min}\{{n\mid w=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n},\ s_{i}\in S\textrm{ for all }i}\}

Furthermore,
\ell(w)=0
if and only if
w=1
, where
1
denotes the identity of the group
W
. If
J\subseteq S
, we let
\ell_{J}:W_{J}\to\mathbb{N}
denote the length function associated to the standard parabolic subsystem
(W_{J},J)
of
(W,S)
. If
w\in W_{J}
, then
\ell_{J}(w)=\ell(w)
by Theorem 5.5 part (b) of
[
9
]
.

2.4.
The canonical root system

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Define the set of
simple roots

\Pi:=\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S\}
where
\alpha_{s}
is treated as a formal symbol. Let
V
denote the real vector space with basis
\Pi
. Define a function
B:\Pi\times\Pi\to\mathbb{R}
in the following way:

B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s^{\prime}})=-\cos(\frac{\pi}{m(s,s^{\prime})})\textrm{ for all }s,s^{\prime}\in S

where if
m(s,s^{\prime})=\infty
, then
-\cos(\frac{\pi}{\infty}):=-1
. Note that
B(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s^{\prime}})=B(\alpha_{s^{\prime}},\alpha_{s})
since
m(s,s^{\prime})=m(s^{\prime},s)
. Since
\Pi
is a basis of
V
, we can extend
B(-,-)
via bilinearlity to become a symmetric bilinear form
B:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
. We will call
B:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}

the Coxeter form of
(W,S)
.

Given
s\in S
and
v\in V
, we define a map
s:V\to V
such that

s(v)=v-2B(v,\alpha_{s})\alpha_{s}

One can verify that the maps
\{s:V\to V\mid s\in S\}
satisfy the relations of the Coxeter system
(W,S)
(see Proposition 5.3 of
[
9
]
). There is a unique representation of
W
on the vector space
V
such that
s\in S\subseteq W
acts as the linear map defined above. This representation
W\to\textrm{End}(V)
is called
the canonical representation of
(W,S)
. The canonical representation is faithful (Corollary 5.4 of
[
9
]
).

Using the canonical representation
W\to\textrm{End}(V)
, we define
the root system

\Phi\subseteq V
such that

\Phi:=\{w(\alpha_{s})\mid s\in S,\ w\in W\}

Given some subset
H
of the vector space
V
, we define

\operatorname{cone}(H):=\{c_{1}h_{1}+c_{2}h_{2}+\dots+c_{k}h_{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N},\ c_{i}\geq 0,\ h_{i}\in H\ \forall i=1,2,\dots,k\}

where
\operatorname{cone}(\emptyset)=\{0\}
. The positive roots
\Phi^{+}\subseteq\Phi
and the negative roots
\Phi^{-}\subseteq\Phi
are defined as follows:

\Phi^{+}=\Phi\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Pi)

\Phi^{-}:=-(\Phi^{+})=\Phi\cap\big(-\operatorname{cone}(\Pi)\big)

Note that
\Pi\subseteq\Phi^{+}
. A general fact about
\Phi
is that
\Phi=\Phi^{+}\coprod\Phi^{-}
, where
\coprod
denotes disjoint union. If
J\subseteq S
, then we define
\Pi_{J}:=\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in J\}
,
\Phi_{J}:=\{w(\alpha_{s})\mid s\in J,\ w\in W_{J}\}
,
\Phi_{J}^{+}:=\Phi_{J}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Pi_{J})
, and
\Phi_{J}^{-}:=-(\Phi_{J}^{+})=\Phi_{J}\cap(-\operatorname{cone}(\Pi_{J}))
. We have
\Phi_{J}=\Phi_{J}^{+}\coprod\Phi_{J}^{-}
. Furthermore, we also have
\Phi_{J}=\Phi\cap\operatorname{span}(\Pi_{J})
.

If
t\in T
is a reflection, then we let
\alpha_{t}\in\Phi^{+}
denote the unique positive root associated to the reflection
t
(section 5.7 of
[
9
]
). The generalized exchange condition is one of the most important properties of the canonical representation:

Proposition 2.1

(Generalized Exchange Condition)
.

Let
w=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{r}
. Let
t\in T
such that
\ell(wt)<\ell(w)
. Then the following are true:

•

w(\alpha_{t})\in\Phi^{-}

•

There is an index
i
such that
wt=s_{1}\dots\widehat{s_{i}}\dots s_{r}
. If
\ell(w)=r
, then the index
i
is unique.

Proof.

Proposition 5.7 and Theorem 5.8 of
[
9
]
.
∎

2.5.
Left Inversion Sets

Given a Coxeter system
(W,S)
and an element
w\in W
, we define
the left inversion set of
w
:

\Phi_{w}:=\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\mid w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}\}

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
w\in W
. Then
\ell(w)=|\Phi_{w}|

Proof.

\ell(w)=\ell(w^{-1})
, and
\ell(w^{-1})
is equal to the number of positive roots sent into
\Phi^{-}
by the element
w^{-1}
(Proposition 5.6 part (b) of
[
9
]
). Hence,

\ell(w)=\ell(w^{-1})=|\Phi_{w}|

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
w\in W
. Let
w=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n}
with
\ell(w)=n
. Also let
\alpha_{i}\in\Pi
denote the simple root corresponding to
s_{i}\in S
. Then
\Phi_{w}=\{\beta_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
where

\beta_{i}:=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{i-1}(\alpha_{i})

and
\beta_{1}:=\alpha_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
w=s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n}
is a reduced expression, it follows that
w^{-1}=s_{n}\dots s_{2}s_{1}
is a reduced expression. The result now follows from Exercise 1 of Section 5.6 of
[
9
]
.
∎

2.6.
The weak right order

Given a Coxeter system
(W,S)
and elements
x,y\in W
, we define a relation
x\leq_{R}y
if and only if

y=xs_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n}\textrm{ and }\ell(xs_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{i})=\ell(x)+i\textrm{ for all }0\leq i\leq n

where
s_{i}\in S
for all
i
. The relation
\leq_{R}
is called
the weak right order of
(W,S)
. The weak right order is a partial order.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system and let
x,y\in W
. Then
x\leq_{R}y
if and only if
\Phi_{x}\subseteq\Phi_{y}
.

Proof.

The proposition is a well-known fact. See section 1.3 of
[
6
]
.
∎

2.7.
The reflection cocycle

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system with set of reflections
T=\{wsw^{-1}\mid s\in S,\ w\in W\}
. Let
N:W\to\mathcal{P}(T)
be defined by

w\longmapsto N(w):=\{t\in T\mid\ell(tw)<\ell(w)\}

We call
N:W\to\mathcal{P}(T)
the reflection cocycle of
(W,S)
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
x,y\in W
. Then:

N(xy)=N(x)\Delta xN(y)x^{-1}

where
\Delta
denotes the symmetric differences of sets. Also,

\ell(xy)=\ell(x)+\ell(y)\iff N(xy)=N(x)\coprod xN(y)x^{-1}

where
\coprod
denotes the disjoint union.

Proof.

See the beginning of the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
6
]
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
x,y\in W
. Then the following are true:

(1)

There is a bijective correspondence between the sets
N(x)
and
\Phi_{x}
given by
t\longmapsto\alpha_{t}
where
t\in N(x)
.

(2)

N(x)\subseteq N(y)\iff\Phi_{x}\subseteq\Phi_{y}\iff x\leq_{R}y

(3)

|N(x)|=|\Phi_{x}|=\ell(x)

Proof.

Part (1) follows immediately from
2.1
. Statements (2) and (3) are consequences of part (1) and the previous propositions.
∎

3.
Background Material on Brink-Howlett groupoids

3.1.
Definition of Brink-Howlett groupoids

Recall that a groupoid is a small category in which every morphism is invertible. Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
J\subseteq S
. Define the following:

\mathcal{O}({\Pi_{J}}):=\{\Pi_{K}\subseteq\Pi\mid K\subseteq S,\ w\in W,\ w(\Pi_{J})=\Pi_{K}\}

We let
G(\Pi_{J})
denote
the Brink-Howlett groupoid generated at the object
\mathcal{O}({\Pi_{J}})
. The objects of
G(\Pi_{J})
are the elements of the set
\mathcal{O}({\Pi_{J}})
. A morphism of
G(\Pi_{J})
is defined to be an ordered triple
(\Pi_{L},w,\Pi_{K})
where
\Pi_{K},\Pi_{L}\in\mathcal{O}({\Pi_{J}})
and
w\in W
such that
w(\Pi_{K})=\Pi_{L}
. If
(\Pi_{L^{\prime}},w^{\prime},\Pi_{K^{\prime}})
and
(\Pi_{L},w,\Pi_{K})
are morphisms of
G(\Pi_{J})
such that
K^{\prime}=L
, then we define the composition of morphisms as:

(\Pi_{L^{\prime}},w^{\prime},\Pi_{K^{\prime}})(\Pi_{L},w,\Pi_{K})=(\Pi_{L^{\prime}},w^{\prime}w,\Pi_{K})

It is trivial to check that
w^{\prime}w(\Pi_{K})=\Pi_{L^{\prime}}
. If
(\Pi_{L},w,\Pi_{K})
is a morphism, then
(\Pi_{K},w^{-1},\Pi_{L})
is the inverse morphism.

4.
The equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}

In this section, we establish some basic facts regarding the equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
. These facts will later be used to prove properties about Coxeter squares and Coxeter
n
-cubes in the next section.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have

w(\Phi_{x})\subseteq\Phi^{+}\iff\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)

In particular,
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
implies
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
.

Proof.

Note that
w(\Phi_{x})\subseteq\Phi^{+}
if and only if
\Phi_{w^{-1}}\cap\Phi_{x}=\emptyset
. But by Lemma 3.2 of
[
2
]
,
\Phi_{w^{-1}}\cap\Phi_{x}=\emptyset
if and only if
x
is a right divisor of
wx
, meaning that
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
. (In
[
2
]
, their notation differs from the notation that we use. In
[
2
]
, they use
N(w^{-1})
to mean
\Phi_{w}
).
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

We have

w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}\implies wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)

If
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
, then:

wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)\implies w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}

Proof.

The implication
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}\implies wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)
follows almost immediately from Proposition
2.1
and also from the conjugation formula

wt_{\alpha}w^{-1}=t_{w(\alpha)}\textrm{ where }\alpha\in\Phi

For the reverse direction, suppose
wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)
. Let

\Phi_{x}=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}

and

\Phi_{y}=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\}

Then we have

N(x)=\{t_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,t_{\alpha_{n}}\}

and

N(y)=\{t_{\beta_{1}},\dots,t_{\beta_{n}}\}

Thus,

wN(x)w^{-1}=\{wt_{\alpha_{1}}w^{-1},\dots,wt_{\alpha_{n}}w^{-1}\}

apply the conjugation formula to get:

wN(x)w^{-1}=\{t_{w(\alpha_{1})},\dots,t_{w(\alpha_{n})}\}

Thus, we get:

\{t_{w(\alpha_{1})},\dots,t_{w(\alpha_{n})}\}=\{t_{\beta_{1}},\dots,t_{\beta_{n}}\}

Let us assume without loss of generality that
t_{w(\alpha_{i})}=t_{\beta_{i}}
for each
i
. This implies that
w(\alpha_{i})=\pm\beta_{i}
for each
i
. Note that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are elements of
\Phi^{+}
for each
i
. Since
l(wx)=l(w)+l(x)
, we can apply Proposition
4.1
to conclude that
w(\alpha_{i})\in\Phi^{+}
for each
i
. But this then implies that
w(\alpha_{i})=\beta_{i}
for each
i
. Hence,
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
w,x,y,z\in W
. Suppose further that
yz=wx
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}

(2)

y(\Phi_{z})=\Phi_{w}

Remark 4.4
.

This was first proven as Lemma 12.3 of
[
5
]
(see both
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
). It was also proven as Lemma 3.4 part (a) in
[
7
]
. However, the proof that is given below is slightly different than the one given in
[
7
]
.

Proof.

Suppose
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
. Since
yz=wx
, we have
N(yz)=N(wx)
. Proposition
2.5
allows us to deduce:

N(y)\Delta yN(z)y^{-1}=N(w)\Delta wN(x)w^{-1}

By Proposition
4.2
, we have
wN(x)w^{-1}=N(y)
. Thus,
yN(z)y^{-1}=N(w)
. Because of
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
, Proposition
4.1
implies that
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
. But since
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
, Proposition
2.5
implies that
N(w)
and
wN(x)w^{-1}
are disjoint. Hence,
yN(z)y^{-1}
and
N(y)
are disjoint. Thus, by Proposition
2.5
, we deduce
\ell(yz)=\ell(y)+\ell(z)
. Both
\ell(yz)=\ell(y)+\ell(z)
and
yN(z)y^{-1}=N(w)
imply that
y(\Phi_{z})=\Phi_{w}
by Proposition
4.2
. This proves that (1) implies (2).

For the reverse direction, note that there is a symmetry of the assumption
yz=wx
and the two conditions
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
and
y(\Phi_{z})=\Phi_{w}
. To be specific, if we interchange the roles of
w
and
y
and the roles of
z
and
x
, we get the reverse implication. This proves the equivalence.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group. Let
w_{0}
denote the unique longest element of
W
. Let
x,y\in W
such that
\ell(x)=\ell(w_{0})-1
. If there exists a
w\in W\setminus\{1\}
such that
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
, then
x=y
.

Proof.

Let
w,x,y
satisfy the hypotheses of the proposition. Since
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
, we have
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
by Proposition
4.1
. Since
w\neq 1
,
\ell(w)>0
. Since
\ell(x)=\ell(w_{0})-1
where
w_{0}
is the longest element, we deduce
\ell(w)=1
. Thus,
w=s
for some
s\in S
such that
sx=w_{0}
. The equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
becomes:

s(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}

Since
s
is an involution, we can move
s
over to the other side:
\Phi_{x}=s(\Phi_{y})
. Also, note that
\ell(y)=\ell(x)=\ell(w_{0})-1
. We can now interchange the roles of
x
and
y
to conclude that
s
is the unique element of
S
such that
sy=w_{0}
. Since
sx=w_{0}=sy
, we conclude that
x=y
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group with longest element
w_{0}
. Suppose that

w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}

then
w
is a right divisor of
w_{0}x^{-1}
.

Proof.

The condition
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
implies that
\ell(wx)=\ell(w)+\ell(x)
. Let
g\in W
such that
\ell(w_{0})=\ell(g(wx))
. Such a
g
exists since we can always increase the length of an element by continually multiplying on the left by simple reflections until we reach the length of the longest element (page 16 of
[
9
]
). Note that since
\ell(w_{0})=\ell(g(wx))
, and because the longest element of a Coxeter group is unique if it exists, we must have that
w_{0}=g(wx)
. Note that both:

\ell(w_{0})=\ell(g(wx))=\ell(g)+\ell(wx)=\ell(g)+\ell(w)+\ell(x)

\ell(w_{0})=\ell((w_{0}x^{-1})x)=\ell(w_{0}x^{-1})+\ell(x)

Hence, we deduce that
\ell(w_{0}x^{-1})=\ell(g)+\ell(w)
. Furthermore,
(w_{0}x^{-1})x=w_{0}=g(wx)
, which implies
w_{0}x^{-1}=gw
. Both
w_{0}x^{-1}=gw
and
\ell(w_{0}x^{-1})=\ell(g)+\ell(w)
imply that
w
is a right divisor of
w_{0}x^{-1}
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
W
be a finite Coxeter group with longest element
w_{0}
. Then:

(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}

Furthermore, if
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
, then
(w_{0}y^{-1}wxw_{0})(\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}})=\Phi_{w_{0}y^{-1}w_{0}}
.

Proof.

First, observe that

\ell(w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0})=\ell(w_{0}(x^{-1}w_{0}))=\ell(w_{0})-\ell(x^{-1}w_{0})

=\ell(w_{0})-(\ell(w_{0})-\ell(x^{-1}))=\ell(x^{-1})=\ell(x)

Thus, we get
\ell(w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0})=\ell(x)
, which implies that
|\Phi_{x}|=\ell(x)=\ell(w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0})=|\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}|
by Proposition
2.6
.
More precisely,
\Phi_{x}
and
\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
are finite sets of the same cardinality. Thus,
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})
and
\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
are both finite sets of the same cardinality. Therefore, to show that these sets are equal, it suffice to show only one containment. I will show that
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})\subseteq\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
, thus proving that
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
.

Let
\beta\in\Phi_{x}
(note
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
). Then
x^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{-}
. But then we have that
w_{0}x^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
, since the longest element
w_{0}
takes all of the negative roots into
\Phi^{+}
. Hence,
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})\subseteq\Phi^{+}
. All that is left to do is prove that applying
(w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0})^{-1}
to
w_{0}x^{-1}(\beta)
gives us a negative root. But this calculation is trivial to verify using the fact that
w_{0}^{-1}=w_{0}
and that
w_{0}
sends all positive roots into
\Phi^{-}
. Thus,
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})\subseteq\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
, so we conclude that
(w_{0}x^{-1})(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
.

If
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
, then by making the substitutions
\Phi_{x}=(xw_{0})\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}}
and
\Phi_{y}=(yw_{0})\Phi_{w_{0}y^{-1}w_{0}}
, one quickly gets
(w_{0}y^{-1}wxw_{0})(\Phi_{w_{0}x^{-1}w_{0}})=\Phi_{w_{0}y^{-1}w_{0}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
|S|=n
. If
k>n
, then
\textnormal{Cubes}_{{k}}({W,S})=0
.

Proof.

Note that if
(w,x,y,z)\in(W\setminus\{1\})^{4}
is a Coxeter square, then the equation
w(\Phi_{x})=\Phi_{y}
implies
w^{-1}(\Phi_{y})\subseteq\Phi^{+}
, which gives
\Phi_{w}\cap\Phi_{y}=\emptyset
. This shows that if
w
and
y
are the terminal edges of a Coxeter square, then
\Phi_{w}
and
\Phi_{y}
must be disjoint.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
denote the terminal edges of a Coxeter
k
-cube. Note that if
i\neq j
, then
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
will be the terminal edges of some Coxeter square. Hence,
\Phi_{x_{i}}
and
\Phi_{x_{j}}
will be disjoint when
i\neq j
. Thus, the left inversion sets
\Phi_{x_{i}}
for
i=1,2,\dots,k
are all pairwise disjoint. But note that each edge of a Coxeter
k
-cube is a non-identity element. Hence,
\Phi_{x_{i}}
must contain at least one simple root for each
i=1,2,\dots,k
. If
k>n=|S|
, then at least one distinct pair
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
will have the same simple root in their left inversion sets. This contradicts disjointness of the left inversion sets. Thus,
k\leq n
.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. If

are the terminal edges of a Coxeter
n
-cube in
(W,S)
, then

\Phi_{\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}=\coprod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{x_{i}}

where
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
denotes the join of the
x_{i}
’s in the weak right order. Furthermore, if
|W|<\infty
and
|S|=n
, then
w_{0}=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
where

is the longest element of

. Hence,

\Phi^{+}=\coprod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{x_{i}}

Note that if

-cube. Indeed, by Proposition

,

x_{n-1}
and
x_{n}
. This process can be viewed geometrically as “collapsing" the Coxeter
n
-cube along the Coxeter square that contains
x_{n-1}
and
x_{n}
. In the case when

, the “collapsing" of the cube can be visualized in the following diagram:

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. Let
J,K\subseteq S
be orthogonal subsets of simple reflections such that
|W_{J}|<\infty
and
|W_{K}|<\infty
. Then

\textnormal{Cubes}_{{|J|+|K|}}({W_{J\cup K},J\cup K})=\textnormal{Cubes}_{{|J|}}({W_{J},J})\textnormal{Cubes}_{{|K|}}({W_{K},K})

In other words, all of the Coxeter
(|J|+|K|)
-cubes that occur in
(W_{J\cup K},J\cup K)
are product cubes.

Proof.

Let
Z
be a Coxeter
(|J|+|K|)
-cube in
(W_{J\cup K},J\cup K)
. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\dots,\dots,z_{|J|+|K|}
be the terminal edges of
Z
. It suffice to show that for each
i
,
z_{i}\in W_{J}
or
z_{i}\in W_{K}
. Once we have shown this, Proposition
5.4
then implies that exactly
|J|
of the
z_{i}
’s lie in
W_{J}
and the other
|K|
of the
z_{i}
’s lie in
W_{K}
. Hence,
Z=\textnormal{Prod}(X,Y)
where
X
is a
|J|
-cube in
W_{J}
and
Y
is a
|K|
-cube in
W_{K}
.

Since
J
and
K
are orthogonal subsets of simple reflections, it follows that
W_{J\cup K}=W_{J}\times W_{K}
. Hence,
|W_{J\cup K}|=|W_{J}||W_{K}|<\infty
. Therefore, we can apply Proposition
5.9
to the Coxeter system
(W_{J\cup K},J\cup K)
to deduce that

\Phi^{+}_{J\cup K}=\coprod_{i=1}^{|J|+|K|}\Phi_{z_{i}}

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there existed a
j
such that
z_{j}\notin W_{J}
and
z_{j}\notin W_{K}
. Since
W_{J\cup K}=W_{J}\times W_{K}
, this would imply that
z_{j}=ww^{\prime}
where
w\in W_{J}\setminus\{1\}
and
w^{\prime}\in W_{K}\setminus\{1\}
. Note that since
W_{J\cup K}=W_{J}\times W_{K}
, we would have that

\Phi_{z_{j}}=\Phi_{ww^{\prime}}=\Phi_{w}\coprod\Phi_{w^{\prime}}

Hence, we get:

\Phi^{+}_{J\cup K}=\Phi_{z_{j}}\coprod(\coprod_{i\neq j}\Phi_{z_{i}})

\Phi^{+}_{J\cup K}=(\Phi_{w}\coprod\Phi_{w^{\prime}})\coprod(\coprod_{i\neq j}\Phi_{z_{i}})

Note that there are
|J|+|K|+1
non-empty left inversion sets on the right-hand side in the above equation. But we know that each left inversion set above has to contain at least one simple reflection from
\Pi_{J\cup K}
. Since
|J|+|K|+1>|J|+|K|
, we deduce that there has to exist at least two left inversion sets in the above equation that contain the same simple root. This contradictions the pairwise disjointness of the above left inversion sets.
∎

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
X
be a Coxeter
n
-cube in
A_{n}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
be the terminal edges of
X
. Let
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
denote the highest root in the root poset of
A_{n}
. Then the following are true:

(1)

There exists a unique
i
such that
\beta\in\Phi_{x_{i}}
.

(2)

Let
i
be as in statement (1). Let
\alpha\in\Pi\cap\Phi_{x_{i}}
denote the unique simple root in
\Phi_{x_{i}}
. Then:

\Phi_{x_{i}}=\{\gamma\in\Phi^{+}\mid\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)\}

where

\operatorname{supp}(\gamma):=\{\rho\in\Pi\mid\exists c\in\mathbb{R}\textnormal{ with }c\neq 0\textnormal{ and }\gamma\in c\rho+\operatorname{span}(\Pi\setminus\{\rho\})\}

Proof.

The existence of such an
i
in part (1) follows from Proposition
5.9
. Similarly, the existence and uniqueness of a simple root
\alpha\in\Pi\cap\Phi_{x_{i}}
also follows from Proposition
5.9
. Let
\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
such that
\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)
. Note that the root poset of
A_{n}
is a “pyramid" shape. An explicit diagram of the root poset of
A_{4}
is given below:
\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{3}
\alpha_{4}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}
\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}
\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}

By analyzing the structure of the root poset of
A_{n}
, we see that

\beta=\gamma+\sum_{\rho\in\Pi\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)}\rho

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\gamma\notin\Phi_{x_{i}}
. This would imply that
x_{i}^{-1}(\gamma)\in\Phi^{+}
. Since
\alpha
is the unique simple root in
\Phi_{x_{i}}
, and because
\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)
, it follows that
x_{i}^{-1}(\rho)\in\Phi^{+}
for all
\rho\in\Pi\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)
. Hence:

x_{i}^{-1}(\beta)=x_{i}^{-1}\big(\gamma+\sum_{\rho\in\Pi\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)}\rho\big)=x_{i}^{-1}(\gamma)+\sum_{\rho\in\Pi\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)}x_{i}^{-1}(\rho)

where the right hand side is a sum of positive roots. Hence,
x_{i}^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
, which contradicts
\beta\in\Phi_{x_{i}}
.

If
\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
such that
\alpha\notin\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)
, then we could express
\gamma
as a linear combination of simple roots from the set
\Pi\setminus\{\alpha\}
. Since
\alpha
is the unique simple root that lies in
\Phi_{x_{i}}
, it would follow that
x_{i}^{-1}(\gamma)
would be a sum of positive roots, and thus
x_{i}^{-1}(\gamma)
would have to be a positive root. Hence,
\gamma\notin\Phi_{x_{i}}
. This establishes that

\Phi_{x_{i}}=\{\gamma\in\Phi^{+}\mid\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)\}

∎

Proposition 5.19
.

Consider the
A_{n}
Coxeter system. Let

. Define

\Phi_{\nu(\alpha_{s},\Pi_{J})}

(2)

\Phi_{\nu(\alpha_{s},\Pi_{J})}=\{\gamma\in\Phi^{+}\mid\rho\in\operatorname{supp}(\gamma)\}

\{\rho\}=\Phi_{\nu(\alpha_{s},\Pi_{J})}\cap\Pi

(3)

If
x

A_{n}

\Phi_{\nu(\alpha_{s},\Pi_{J})}

Statement (2) immediately follows from statement (1) and Proposition

For statement (3), let

be the unique element of
\Phi_{x}\cap\Pi
. Let

be the unique positive root of highest depth in the root poset of

Theorem 5.22
.

Proof.

Since
P
is a based rectangle partition,

must contain a highest based rectangle (a based rectangle that contains the positive root of maximum depth). But by Proposition
5.19
part (3), we know that this highest based rectangle is a left inversion set of some

,

or

Theorem 5.32
.

We have the following recursive relation:

|\textnormal{Edge}_{{n+1}}|=|\textnormal{Edge}_{{n}}|+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}

where
|\textnormal{Edge}_{{1}}|=1
. Hence:

|\textnormal{Edge}_{{n}}|=\binom{n+2}{3}=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{6}

If
s

, then the unique Coxeter

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\bullet$}}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\bullet$}}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}

Since

, we conclude that
\textnormal{Edge}_{{1}}=\{s\}
. Hence,
|\textnormal{Edge}_{{1}}|=1
. Clearly,

, so the explicit formula for
|\textnormal{Edge}_{{n}}|
in the latter half of the proposition holds true in the base case.

Let us now consider how to compute
|\textnormal{Edge}_{{n+1}}|
. By Theorem
5.22
, we know that the elements of
\textnormal{Edge}_{{n+1}}
correspond to based rectangles in the root poset
\Phi^{+}
of
A_{n+1}
. Hence, we just need to enumerate the number of based rectangles in the root poset

of

denote the Coxeter generators for the

. We just need to calculate the based rectangles in

that are not fully contained in
