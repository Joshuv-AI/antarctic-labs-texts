Asymptotic considerations in a Bayesian linear model with nonparametrically modelled time series innovations
Claudia Kirch 1,2 Alexander Meier 1 Renate Meyer 3 Yifu Tang 4

Abstract




Abstract
.

This paper considers a semiparametric approach within the general Bayesian linear model where the innovations consist of a stationary, mean zero Gaussian time series. While a parametric prior is specified for the linear model coefficients,
the autocovariance structure of the time series is modeled nonparametrically using
a Bernstein-Gamma process prior for the spectral density function, the Fourier transform of the autocovariance function.
When updating this joint prior
with Whittle’s likelihood,
a Bernstein-von-Mises result is established for the linear model coefficients showing the asymptotic equivalence of the corresponding estimators to those obtained from frequentist pseudo-maximum-likelihood estimation under the Whittle likelihood.
Local asymptotic normality of the likelihood is shown, demonstrating that the marginal posterior distribution of the linear model coefficients
shrinks at parametric rate towards the true value, and that the conditional posterior distribution of the spectral
density contracts in the sup-norm, even in the case of a partially misspecified
linear model.

Introduction

Bayesian nonparametric approaches to the analysis of stationary time series have received a lot of attention over the last two decades, both from an applied and theoretical perspective. The majority of these approaches aim at estimating the spectral density and are thus formulated in the frequency domain, utilizing the Whittle likelihood approximation and placing a nonparametric prior on the (log-) spectral density function, e.g.
Carter and Kohn (1997)
;
Choudhuri et al. (2004a)
;
Rousseau et al. (2012)
;
Chopin et al. (2013)
;
Cadonna et al. (2017)
;
Kirch et al. (2019)
;
Edwards et al. (2019)
;
Rao and Yang (2021)
;
Maturana-Russel and Meyer (2021)
. For Bayesian parametric methods, the Bernstein-von-Mises (BvM) theorem guarantees that frequentist maximum-likelihood and Bayesian inference procedures are asymptotically equal to the second order of approximation. But that is no longer true for Bayesian nonparametric models. One even needs to explicitly show posterior consistency, i.e. that the posterior distribution contracts around the true parameter value with increasing sample size.
Proofs of the consistency of the respective posterior distribution of the spectral density have been provided by
Choudhuri et al. (2004a)
,
Rousseau et al. (2012)
,
Kirch et al. (2019)
for zero-mean Gaussian time series and by
Tang et al. (2023)
for zero-mean non-Gaussian time series.

Arguably, (linear) regression models are among the most widely used statistical procedures where the standard assumption of independent innovations does not always hold in practical applications. More realistically, a mean-zero Gaussian time series can be used to model the correlated innovations in such a regression model, as for instance in
Carter and Kohn (1997)
;
Dey et al. (2018)
;
Jun et al. (2022)
;
Merkatas and Särkkä (2022)
. In such a semiparametric model, the aim is to estimate the regression coefficients jointly with the spectral density of the noise process, the Fourier transform of the autocovariance function. In this situation, the spectral density is of secondary interest and could be regarded as a nuisance parameter. However, a proper and adapt modeling of the autocorrelation structure has the potential to properly capture not only the first but also second-order properties. A parametric model for the autocorrelation such as the classical assumption of i.i.d. Gaussian noise, even though first-order consistent, would over- or underestimate the variability if the parametric model is misspecified. This is problematic not only in view of understanding the uncertainty associated with the obtained estimator but also for understanding the uncertainty associated with corresponding predicted values. This can be overcome by using a nonparametric modeling of the error structure.

In this paper we consider the semiparametric general linear model with time series errors and estimate both linear regression coefficients and autocorrelation structure in the frequency domain, using a Bernstein-Gamma process prior for the spectral density. The main focus is on establishing a Bernstein-von-Mises result for the marginal posterior distribution of the regression coefficients. In particular, we show that we achieve the same contraction rate as with a parametric model for the autocovariance and derive the corresponding asymptotic variance of the procedure based on the Whittle likelihood approximation. For the special case of a general linear model with design matrix consisting of a vector of ones and time series errors, i.e. estimating the mean of a stationary time series, we can show that the asymptotic variance is the same as that of the maximum likelihood estimator based on the true likelihood. However, we also provide a counterexample where the variance of the estimator under the true likelihood differs from that under the Whittle likelihood so that here, too, the variability of the estimator is not properly captured by the Bayesian procedure. This shows, that one needs to be careful with the interpretation of the Bayesian results and, if possible, check validity on a case-by-case basis.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we give a definition of the semipametric model including likelihood and prior specifications and state the Bernstein-von Mises theorem. Section
3
provides results of a comprehensive simulation study and a case study. The proof of the Bernstein-von Mises theorem is given in Section
4
by first stating a general semiparametric Bernstein-von Mises theorem, which can be applied to obtain the main result of this paper by establishing a LAN property and joint posterior contraction rates. Technical lemmas are given in Appendix
A
.

2
Semiparametric model and Bernstein-von-Mises theorem

In this section, we propose a Bayesian procedure for a linear model with time-dependent errors, where we do not use a parametric time series model. Instead, we use a Whittle-likelihood approximation of the time-dependent errors in combination with a Bernstein-Gamma process prior for the spectral density. We show consistency and parametric contraction rates for the linear regression coefficients and derive their asymptotic variance as well as a Bernstein-von-Mises theorem.

2.1
Linear model with nonparametric time series errors

Consider the following linear model

\displaystyle Z_{t}=\underline{x}_{t}^{T}\underline{\theta}+e_{t},\quad\text{for }t=1,\ldots,n,

Unlike in a classical setting, we do not assume independence of the innovations
\{e_{t}\}
, instead, we allow for time-dependency by modelling them as a stationary mean zero Gaussian time series
with covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{+}
. Equivalently, this model can be stated in vectorized notation

\displaystyle\underline{Z}_{n}=\bm{X}_{n}\underline{\theta}+\underline{e}_{n},\quad\underline{e}_{n}\sim N_{n}(\underline{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{n}),

(1)

with design matrix
\bm{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.

Corresponding methodology can also be developed for a multivariate regression model by replacing scalar calculations by corresponding matrix-vector calculations similarly to
Meier et al. (2020)
. For simplicity of presentation we concentrate on the univariate situation here.

We make the following assumptions on the regression part of the model:

Assumption 1
.

The design matrix
\bm{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is of full rank for
n
sufficiently large.
Furthermore, we assume that
0<\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{\max}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})<\infty
.

Assumption
1
implies that
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})\to\infty
and
\|\bm{X}_{n}\|=o(\exp(n^{\delta}))
for every
\delta>0
as
n\to\infty
. These implications are useful in the proof of the main result.

Often, in regression analysis, the design matrix
fulfils

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}\to\bm{V}\quad\text{for some matrix }\bm{V}\in\mathcal{S}_{r}^{+}.

(2)

In this case, Assumption
1
holds since by the continuity of eigenvalues,
\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}
is positive definite and has rank
r
for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{\max}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})\lesssim\lambda_{\max}(\bm{V})<\infty

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})\gtrsim\lambda_{\min}(\bm{V})>0.

Additionally, we make the following assumption on the time series:

Assumption 2
.

The true spectral density
f_{0}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly bounded away from 0.
The corresponding autocovariance function
\gamma_{0}(h)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}(\omega)\exp(ih\omega)d\omega
fulfils

\sum_{h\geqslant 0}|\gamma_{0}(h)|\,h^{a}<\infty

for some
1<a\leqslant 2
.

This is an assumption on the decay of the autocovariance function of the time series and implies that the spectral density is differentiable with
(a-1)
-Hölder-continuous derivative (see e.g. Remark 8.5 in
Serov (2017)
).
Under this assumption the Whittle likelihood below and the true likelihood are mutually contiguous. Contiguity is useful to prove posterior consistency of the spectral density. Using different proof techniques as e.g. in
Tang et al. (2023)
the assumption on the time series can be relaxed.

We now give some important examples of such linear models in the context of time series analysis:

Example 1

(Time series with unknown mean)
.

The simplest situation covered by this methodology deals with Bayesian inference about the mean of a time series with no parametric assumptions on the structure of the time series. In this case,
Z_{t}=\mu+e_{t}
, i.e.
\bm{X}_{n}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}

and
\theta=\mu
. Furthermore, we observe that
\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}=n
such that (
2
) and thus
Assumption
1
are clearly fulfilled.

Example 2

(Time series with unknown linear trend)
.

In the linear trend model
Z_{t}=\mu+\frac{bt}{n}+e_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,n
, we are interested in Bayesian inference for a linear trend in a time series.
In this case, it holds
\underline{\theta}=(\mu,b)^{T}
and

\bm{X}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\ldots&1\\
\frac{1}{n}&\frac{2}{n}&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}^{T},\quad\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}n&\frac{n+1}{2}\\
\frac{n+1}{2}&\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n}\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly,

\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}\to\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+},\quad n\to\infty,

such that (
2
) and thus
Assumption
1
are fulfilled.

Example 3

(Time series with unknown linear trend and seasonality)
.

A time series model with linear trend and seasonality can be written as
Z_{t}=\mu+b\frac{t}{n}+\sum_{l=1}^{p-1}s_{l}1_{\{t\in M_{l}\}}+e_{t}
, where
p
is the period,
M_{l}=\{l+i\cdot p:i=0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\underline{\theta}=(\mu,b,s_{1},\cdots,s_{p-1})^{T}
. The last seasonal component has been omitted due to identifiability with the intercept and for
p=1
the model coincides with Example
2
. Thus, the design matrix is given by
\bm{X}_{n}=(\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{p})
, where
\mathbf{y}_{0}^{T}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
,
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{T}=(1,2,\ldots,n)/n
and
\mathbf{y}_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,p-1
, are vectors with entries of
1
at the positions
l+i\cdot p
,
i\geqslant 0
, and
0
at all other positions. Some calculations yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}\to\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{p}&\ldots&\ldots&\frac{1}{p}\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{3}&\frac{1}{2p}&\ldots&\ldots&\frac{1}{2p}\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{p}&\frac{1}{2p}&\frac{1}{p}&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\frac{1}{p}&\frac{1}{2p}&0&\ldots&\ldots&\frac{1}{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}_{p+1}^{+},\quad n\to\infty,

which can be seen to be positive definite by calculating the determinant using a result for partitioned matrices given e.g. in

Bernstein (2018)
, Prop. 3.9.4. Consequently, (
2
) and thus
Assumption
1
are fulfilled.

2.2
Pseudo-Likelihood and Prior specification

Modelling the distribution of the Gaussian stationary time series errors nonparametrically requires specifying a nonparametric prior on the space of symmetric, positive semidefinite covariance matrices. Furthermore, the evaluation of the Gaussian likelihood subsequently requires the inversion of a high-dimensional covariance matrix which is computationally expensive. In the literature, this is often avoided by making use of the so-called Whittle likelihood, which is based on the observation that
a Fourier transform of the original time series observations leads to (in a certain sense) asymptotically independent complex Gaussians with variances equal to the spectral density evaluated at the Fourier frequencies. The corresponding approximate Whittle likelihood is misspecified but asymptotically contiguous to the true likelihood under the Assumption 2 as shown by
Choudhuri et al. (2004a)
.

More explicitly, Whittle’s likelihood
P_{W}^{n}
is formulated
in terms of the Fourier coefficients
\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the innovations
as obtained via the transformation
\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}=\bm{F}_{n}\underline{e}_{n}
,
where
\bm{F}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the (orthogonal) discrete
Fourier transformation matrix, defined in (
5
) below,
see Section 2.1 in
Kirch et al. (2019)
for more details.
Whittle’s likelihood for the observations
\underline{Z}_{n}
can then be written in terms
of the following Lebesgue density (see (8.3) in
Meier (2018)
for more details)

p_{W}^{n}(\underline{z}_{n}|\underline{\theta},f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}|\bm{D}_{n}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{\tilde{z}}_{n}-\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\underline{\theta}\right)^{T}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\left(\underline{\tilde{z}}_{n}-\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\underline{\theta}\right)\right)

(3)

with diagonal frequency domain covariance matrix
\bm{D}_{n}:=\bm{D}_{n}(f)
defined by

\bm{D}_{n}=\bm{D}_{n}(f):=2\pi\,\begin{cases}\text{diag}(f(0),f(\lambda_{1}),f(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,f(\lambda_{N}),f(\lambda_{N}),f(\lambda_{n/2}))&n\text{ even},\\
\text{diag}(f(0),f(\lambda_{1}),f(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,f(\lambda_{N}),f(\lambda_{N}))&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(4)

with Fourier frequencies
\lambda_{j}=\frac{2\pi j}{n}
, for
j=0,\ldots,N=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
,
the frequency domain design matrix
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}:=\bm{F}_{n}\bm{X}_{n}

and the frequency domain observations
\underline{\tilde{Z}}_{n}=\bm{F}_{n}\underline{Z}_{n}
, where

\displaystyle F_{n}=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{N},\mathbf{s}_{N},\mathbf{e}_{n/2})^{T},&n\text{ even},\\
(\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{N},\mathbf{s}_{N})^{T},&n\text{ odd},\end{cases}

(5)

where
\mathbf{c}_{j}=\sqrt{2}\Re\mathbf{e}_{j}
(real part),
\mathbf{s}_{j}=\sqrt{2}\Im\mathbf{e}_{j}
(imaginary part) and

\mathbf{e}_{j}=n^{-1/2}(e_{j},e_{j}^{2},\ldots,e_{j}^{n})^{T}
with
e_{j}=\exp(-2\pi ij/n)
.

The Bayesian model specification
is completed by specifying a prior for the linear model coefficient and innovation
covariance structure, which will be modeled in terms of the spectral density function
f
.
We consider an independent prior of the form
P(d\underline{\theta},df)=P(d\underline{\theta})P(df)
.
For the spectral density, we will employ a Bernstein-Gamma prior, a special case of the Bernstein-Hpd-matrix-Gamma prior, developed for multivariate time series in

Meier et al. (2020)
and used here for the univariate case. The main idea is to represent the spectral density by a positive linear combination of Bernstein polynomials (based on Weierstrass approximation theorem) and putting a prior on the positive weights and the degree of Bernstein polynomials. Note that suitably normalized, the Bernstein polynomials on the interval
[0,1]
are Beta densities.

For
0<\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\infty
, consider the set

\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\colon\|f^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\tau_{0}^{-1},\|f\|_{L}\leqslant\tau_{1}\right\}

(6)

with the Lipschitz norm
\|f\|_{L}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|\Delta f\|_{\infty}
,
where
\Delta f(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=|f(\omega_{1})-f(\omega_{2})|/|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|

for
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
.
Recall the univariate Gamma process
\operatorname{GP}(\alpha,\beta)
on
\mathcal{X}=[0,\pi]
with
\alpha,\beta\colon[0,\pi]\to(0,\infty)
(see e.g. (1.9) in
Meier (2018)
).
Let
I_{j,k}:=((j-1)\pi/k,j\pi/k]
for
j=1,\ldots,k
denote the equidistant partition of
[0,\pi]
of size
k

and denote by
b(\cdot|j,l)
the probability density function on
[0,1]

of the
\operatorname{Beta}(j,l)
distribution. Let
P(df)
denote
the Bernstein-Gamma prior for
f
defined by:

\displaystyle f(\omega)=f(\omega|\Phi,k):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Phi(I_{j,k})b(\omega/\pi|j,k-j+1),\quad 0\leqslant\omega\leqslant\pi,

\displaystyle\Phi\sim\operatorname{GP}(\alpha,\beta),\quad k\sim p(k).

The restriction of
P(df)
to
\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}
will be denoted by
P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}(df)
:

P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}(F):=\frac{P(F\cap\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}})}{P(\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}})},\quad F\subset\mathcal{C}^{1}\text{ measurable}.

(7)

The joint prior for
(\underline{\theta},f)
will be denoted
by
P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}(d\underline{\theta},df):=P(d\underline{\theta})P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}(df)
.
We will denote by
P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(d\underline{\theta},df|\underline{Z}_{n})
the pseudo posterior
for
(\underline{\theta},f)

obtained when updating
P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}
with Whittle’s likelihood
P_{W}^{n}
from (
3
).

We assume that the prior for
\underline{\theta}
is continuous, has moments up to a certain order, and its support includes the true parameter vector
\underline{\theta}_{0}
, i.e., more specifically:

Assumption 3
.

The prior
P(d\underline{\theta})
on
\underline{\theta}
is such that
\mathrm{E}\|\underline{\theta}\|^{\nu}<\infty
for some
\nu>0
. It possesses a Lebesgue density
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{r}

fulfilling
g(\underline{\theta})\leqslant C
(for some
C>0
) and is continuous and strictly positive in an open
neighbourhood of
\underline{\theta}_{0}
.

Furthermore, we need the following two assumptions about the hyperparameters of the nonparametric prior on the spectral density function
f
:

Assumption 4
.

For the Gamma process parameters
\alpha,\beta\colon[0,\pi]\to(0,\infty)
,
there exist positive constants
g_{0},g_{1}
such that
g_{0}\leqslant\alpha(x),\beta(x)\leqslant g_{1}

holds for all
x\in[0,\pi]\setminus N
, where
N
is a Lebesgue null set.

Assumption 5
.

There exist positive constants
A_{1},A_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
such that
the prior probability mass function
p(\cdot)
of the Bernstein polynomial degree
k
fulfils

A_{1}\exp(-\kappa_{1}j\log j)\leqslant p(j)\leqslant A_{2}\exp(-\kappa_{2}j)

for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption
4
is needed to ensure that any small neighbourhood of the true spectral density has positive prior probability. Assumption
5
ensures that the prior on
k
has full support on
\mathbb{N}
and that the decrease of tail probabilities is bounded.

2.3
Bernstein-von-Mises result

We are now ready to state a Bernstein-von-Mises result for the coefficient of the linear part of the model.

Theorem 1
.

Let (
1
) hold with fixed design matrix
\bm{X}_{n}
fulfilling Assumption
1

and stationary mean zero Gaussian innovations
\{e_{t}\}
with
spectral density
f_{0}
fulfilling Assumption
2
.
Denote the true distribution of
\underline{Z}_{n}
by
P_{0}^{n}
and let
0<\tau_{0}<\min_{\omega}f_{0}(\omega)\leqslant\max_{\omega}f_{0}(\omega)<\tau_{1}<\infty
. Furthermore, let the prior specification hold as in Section
2.2
fulfilling Assumptions
3
–
5
.
Then, it holds, as
n\to\infty
, in
P_{0}
-probability

\displaystyle\delta_{\operatorname{TV}}\left[P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(\underline{\theta}\in\cdot|\underline{Z}_{n}),N_{r}\left(\bm{V}_{W;n}\,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\underline{\tilde{Z}}_{n},\bm{V}_{W;n}\right)\right]\to 0,

\displaystyle\text{where }\bm{V}_{W;n}=\left(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1},\quad\bm{D}_{n,0}=\bm{D}_{n}(f_{0})

and

\delta_{\operatorname{TV}}
denotes the total variation
distance.

Because
\bm{V}_{W;n}
typically contracts like
1/n
by Assumptions
1
or by (
2
), the theorem shows that we have contraction at parametric rate (compare also Section
4.2.2
).

2.3.1
Asymptotic second-order correctness

The quantity
\bm{V}_{W;n}\,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\underline{\tilde{Z}}_{n}
in Theorem
1
is the pseudo maximum likelihood estimator under the Whittle likelihood with known spectral density, which is a special case of weighted least squares estimation. As a frequentist estimator this quantity is unbiased for
\underline{\theta}_{0}
and has a normal distribution with covariance
\bm{V}_{W;n}
under the Whittle likelihood. This shows that the posterior covariance matrix is asymptotically equivalent to the one of the pseudo maximum likelihood estimator under the Whittle likelihood.

In contrast, the covariance of the latter estimator under the true likelihood is given by

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{0;n}=\left(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\,\left(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1},

(8)

where
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}=\bm{F}_{n}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{F}_{n}^{T}
.

If
\bm{V}_{0;n}
and
\bm{V}_{W;n}
are asymptotically equivalent in the sense of

\displaystyle\|\bm{V}_{W;n}^{-1/2}\bm{V}_{0;n}\bm{V}_{W;n}^{-1/2}-\mbox{Id}\|_{F}=o(1),

(9)

then the total variation distance between two Gaussians with the same mean and
\bm{V}_{0;n}
respectively
\bm{V}_{W;n}
as covariance matrices converges to zero. Indeed, this is not only a sufficient but also a necessary condition because the minimum of
1
and the left hand side of (
9
)
upper and lower bounds the total variation distance (with appropriate factors) between such two multivariate Gaussians (see e.g.
Devroye et al. (2022)
).

Consequently, under (
9
),
we can replace the statement in Theorem
1
by a corresponding statement with
N_{r}\left(\bm{V}_{W;n}\,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\underline{\tilde{Z}}_{n},\bm{V}_{0;n}\right)
, i.e. the covariance matrix under the true likelihood instead of under the Whittle likelihood.
This shows second order correctness of the Bayesian procedure in the sense that the credibility sets are asymptotic confidence sets in this case. Otherwise any Bayesian uncertainty quantification of a Bayesian point estimate such as the posterior standard deviation will systematically over- or underestimate the true uncertainty.

In the one-parameter situation, i.e. when
\bm{X}_{n}
is actually a vector, under the boundedness assumption of Assumption
2
in combination with (
2
) the condition in (
9
) comes down to

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}(\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}-\bm{D}_{n,0})\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\left(\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}-\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\right)\bm{X}_{n}\right\|_{F}\to 0.

(10)

Standard results from time series analysis (or circulant matrices) show that the entries of
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}-\bm{D}_{n,0}
are uniformly bounded by
1/n
under suitable assumptions (see e.g. Theorem 4.9 in
Meier (2018)
), which is not strong enough to obtain the above results in general.

Indeed, Example
4
gives a counter-example, where asymptotic second-order correctness does indeed not hold, while Examples
1
and
2
give situations, where asymptotic second-order correctness does hold.

This shows that one has to check asymptotic second-order correctness on a case-by-case basis for the credibility sets to be asymptotic confidence sets.

We will now come back to our initial examples:

Example 1

(Time series with unknown mean (cont.))
.

Some simple calculations show that
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}=(\sqrt{n},0,\ldots,0)^{T}
and the first component of
\underline{\tilde{Z}}_{n}
is given by the sample mean
\bar{Z}_{n}
of the observations. Consequently, by Theorem
1
the posterior distribution of
\mu
is centered at the sample mean and multiplied with
\sqrt{n}
behaves asymptotically like a centered normal distribution with variance equal to
2\pi f_{0}(0)
. Thus, if one chooses the expectation of the posterior as estimator, then this estimator is asymptotically equal to the sample mean. It is well known, see e.g. Theorems 7.1.1 in
Brockwell and Davis (1991)
, that the sample mean of Gaussian time series, centered at the true expectation and multiplied by
\sqrt{n}
, is asymptotically normal with variance
2\pi f_{0}(0)
, indeed the matrix entry in the first row and first column of
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle n\,\left(\gamma(0)+2\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-l}{n}\,\gamma(l)\right)=n(2\pi f_{0}(0)+o(1)),

such that
\bm{V}_{0;n}
as in (
8
) multiplied with
n
coincides asymptotically with
2\pi f_{0}(0)
.

Consequently, the corresponding Bayesian inference is second-order correct in the above sense and the corresponding credibility sets are also asymptotical confidence intervals.

Example 2

(Time series with unknown linear trend (cont.))
.

In this case, the frequency domain signal is (in general) nonzero in all components, c.f. Example (c) in Section 8.1 in
Meier (2018)
. In particular, unlike in Example
1
, the asymptotic distribution now depends on all frequencies and not only frequency zero.
In the one-parameter model with a linear trend but no intercept, i.e. for
\bm{X}_{n}=(1/n,2/n,\ldots,1)^{T}
, we get as in Example (c) in Section 8.1 in
Meier (2018)
for
n
odd

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}((n+1)/\sqrt{2},1,\cot(\lambda_{1}/2),1,\cot(\lambda_{2}/2),\ldots,1,\cot(\lambda_{N}/2))

with
\lambda_{k}=2\pi k/n
and
N=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
as before. For
n
even an additional term of
1/\sqrt{4n}
appears at the end of the vector.

If the entries of
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}-\bm{D}_{n,0}
are uniformly bounded by
1/n
, which holds under suitable assumptions, see e.g. Theorem 4.9 in
Meier (2018)
, and by the boundedness assumptions on the spectral density, then (
10
) is fulfilled if
\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\|_{1}=o(n)
.

In the above case, clearly, the sum over all entries of
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}
except for the cotangent-terms is of order
O(\sqrt{n})=o(n)
. Concerning the sum over the absolute values of the remaining cotangent-terms, note, that by the proof of Lemma 4.4.2 in
Kirch (2006)

\displaystyle|\cot(\omega_{j}/2)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sin(\pi j/n)}=O\left(\max\left(\frac{n}{j},\frac{n}{n-j}\right)\right)

and the sum over these terms is bounded by
O(n\log n)
. Due to the
additional
1/\sqrt{n}
factor the sum over the cotangent-terms of
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}
is of order
O(\sqrt{n}\log n)=o(n)
. Consequently, the desired condition
\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\|_{1}=o(n)
holds.

While in the above two examples, the second-order correctness of the Bayesian procedure with misspecified Whittle-likelihood was shown, this is by no means guaranteed as the following example shows:

Example 4

(Time series with design matrix not yielding nominal frequentist coverage)
.

Here, we will give an example, where (
10
) does not hold. To do so, we consider a Gaussian AR(1) time series which allows us to calculate the matrices
\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}
and
\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}
, which makes checking (
10
) for specific design matrices very easy. In particular, this provides a counter example where second-order correctness does not hold and corresponding credibility sets are not asymptotic confidence sets.

To elaborate, let the noise
\{e_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be a causal Gaussian AR(1) process satisfying
e_{t}=\alpha_{0}e_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t}
,
\epsilon_{t}\sim N(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
with
0<|\alpha_{0}|<1
,
\sigma_{0}>0
and spectral density
f_{AR}
, hence
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}=\sigma_{0}^{2}(1-\alpha_{0}^{2})^{-1}\left(\alpha_{0}^{|i-j|}\right)_{i,j=1,\cdots,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, see e.g. Example 3.2.2 in combination with Theorem 3.2.1 in
Brockwell and Davis (1991)
. Furthermore, by e.g. Theorem 4.4.2 in
Brockwell and Davis (1991)
,

f_{AR}^{-1}=f_{MA}
is the spectral density of an MA(1) time series with moving average parameter
-\alpha_{0}
and variance
\sigma_{0}^{-2}
of the innovations, hence for
n\geqslant 4
,

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}=\sigma_{0}^{-2}\left(\begin{matrix}1+\alpha_{0}^{2}&-\alpha_{0}&0&0&\cdots&-\alpha_{0}\\
-\alpha_{0}&1+\alpha_{0}^{2}&-\alpha_{0}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-\alpha_{0}&1+\alpha_{0}^{2}&-\alpha_{0}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&-\alpha_{0}&1+\alpha_{0}^{2}&-\alpha_{0}\\
-\alpha_{0}&0&\cdots&0&-\alpha_{0}&1+\alpha_{0}^{2}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

which is the correspondent circulant matrix, see e.g. (4.5.5) in
Brockwell and Davis (1991)
noting that
m(h)
there is the autocovariance function of an MA(1) time series which is zero for
|h|>1
, such that the
\lambda_{j}
there coincide with
f_{MA}=f_{AR}^{-1}
at
\omega_{j}
for
n\geqslant 2
. To facilitate notation we further assume that
\sigma_{0}=1
in the following, where the results for general
\sigma_{0}
follow easily as this is just a multiplicative constant.

According to equation (15) of
Galbraith and Galbraith (1974)
, noting that
M_{n}
there is
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}
here,
\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}
only differ at the four corner entries. To be more specific, we have

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}=\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}+\bm{A}_{n},

(11)

where
\bm{A}_{n}=\alpha_{0}^{2}\bm{E}_{(1,1);n}-\alpha_{0}\bm{E}_{(1,n);n}-\alpha_{0}\bm{E}_{(n,1);n}+\alpha_{0}^{2}\bm{E}_{(n,n);n}
and
\bm{E}_{(i,j);n}
is an
n\times n
matrix whose only non-zero entry is the
(i,j)
-th entry with value 1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left[\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\right]\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}-\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}=\left[\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}-\bm{\mbox{Id}_{n}}\right]\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left[\bm{A}_{n}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\right]\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}=\bm{A}_{n}+\bm{A}_{n}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\bm{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\,\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}-\alpha_{0}^{n+2}}{1-\alpha_{0}^{2}}\left(\bm{E}_{(1,1);n}+\bm{E}_{(n,n);n}\right)-\frac{(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{0}^{3})(1-\alpha_{0}^{n})}{1-\alpha_{0}^{2}}\left(\bm{E}_{(1,n);n}+\bm{E}_{(n,1);n}\right).

This shows that again the only non-zero entries of the matrix

\left[\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\right]\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}-\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}
are located at the four corner entries.

Consequently, for a one-parameter regression design
\bm{X}_{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}
, it holds

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\left(\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}-\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\right)\bm{X}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha_{0}^{n}}{1-\alpha_{0}^{2}}\,\left(2\alpha_{0}^{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{n}^{2}\right)-2\alpha_{0}(1+\alpha_{0}^{2})\,x_{1}x_{n}\right).

Choosing, e.g.,
\bm{X}_{n}=(1,\cdots,1,1+\sqrt{n})^{T}
fulfills
\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}=2(n+\sqrt{n})
such that (
2
) and consequently Assumption
1
are satisfied.
However,
clearly (
10
) is not fulfilled as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\,\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\left(\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\,\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}-\bm{F}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n,0}^{-1}\bm{F}_{n}\right)\bm{X}_{n}\to\frac{2\alpha_{0}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{0}^{2}}\neq 0.

The obvious difference between the first two and the last example is the fact that there was a dominating term in
\bm{X}_{n}
in the latter, i.e. it does not fulfill a Noether-type condition as known from the asymptotic theory on linear rank statistics, see e.g.
Hájek (1968)
. We conjecture, that this may be key to distinguishing these two situations even though the exact time series structure given in the last example only yields a counter-example if the first or last term are dominating.

These considerations are also consistent with the fact that frequentist results concerning the asymptotic normality of time series regression often require much stronger assumptions on the
\bm{X}_{n}
than Assumption
1
, see e.g.
Amemiya (1973)
. Indeed, the design matrix in Example
4
satisfies Assumption
1
but fails to fulfill Assumption 2 of
Amemiya (1973)
, which is related to a Noether-condition in a non-triangular setting.

3
of Section

that contains the intercept but not the last dummy variable), i.e.,

where

if

is a multiple of
i
and 0 otherwise, for

and
i=2,\ldots 13
. The error process
\epsilon_{t}
is assumed to be a mean zero Gaussian time series with spectral density
f(\omega)
. We use a flat Normal prior with mean 0 and standard deviation of 10,000 for
\beta_{1}
, flat Normal priors with mean 0 and standard deviation 100 for
\beta_{i}
,
i=2,\ldots,13,
and the Bernstein-Gamma process prior for the error process. In the univariate case, a normalized version of the Bernstein-Hpd matrix-Gamma process prior is the Bernstein-Dirichlet process, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(\omega)=f(\omega|\Phi,k):=\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Phi^{\prime}(I_{j,k})b(\omega/\pi|j,k-j+1),\quad 0\leqslant\omega\leqslant\pi,

\displaystyle\Phi\sim\operatorname{DP}(M,G_{0}),\quad k\sim p(k),\quad\tau\sim\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)

where
\operatorname{DP}(M,G_{0})
denotes the Dirichlet process with concentration parameter
M
and base measure
G_{0}
. Thus, we use the Bernstein-Dirichlet prior for the spectral density of the error process, with hyperparameters set to
G_{0}=\mbox{Uniform}[0,1]
,
M=1
,
p(k)\propto\exp(-0.01k^{2})
, and
\alpha=\beta=0.001
.

shows the data overlaid by the posterior median and 90% posterior credible intervals for trend and seasonality.
Both increasing linear trend over time as well as the seasonality components are accurately estimated while taking the serially correlated errors into account when calculating the marginal posterior credible bands.

4
Proof of Theorem
1

The following theorem constitutes the main tool for the proof of Theorem
1
. It is due to
Ghosal and van der Vaart (2017)
.
A general semiparametric Bernstein-von-Mises theorem has also been presented in
Bickel and Kleijn (2012)
.
However, the authors there restrict their attention to the iid setting.
One possible route for an alternative proof of Theorem
1
could be
a generalization of their results (and the proof technique, which is closely related
to concentration of partially misspecified posterior distributions as discussed
in
Kleijn and Van der Vaart (2012)
) to the non-iid setting.

Theorem 2

(General semiparametric BvM)
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be random variables with likelihood
function having Lebesgue density
p^{n}(\underline{Z}_{n}|\underline{\theta},\eta)
, where
\underline{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}

and
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}
being some measurable space.
Let the prior on
(\underline{\theta},\eta)
be of the form
P(d\underline{\theta},d\eta)=P(d\underline{\theta})P(d\eta)
.
Assume that there exist sequences of subsets
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset\mathcal{H}
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}=\left\{\underline{\theta}\in\Theta\colon(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})^{T}\bm{S}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})\leqslant\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{S}_{n})}\right\}

(14)

with
\bm{S}_{n}
as in (b).
Additionally, let the following properties hold:

(a)

Prior Positivity: In an open neighbourhood of
\underline{\theta}_{0}
, the prior
P(d\underline{\theta})

possesses a continuous and strictly positive Lebesgue density.

(b)

Local Asymptotic Normality (LAN): There exists a tight sequence of random
variables
(\underline{G}_{n})
(with respect to
P_{0}^{n}
, where
P_{0}^{n}
denotes the distribution of
\underline{Z}_{n}
under
(\underline{\theta}_{0},\eta_{0})
), a sequence of spd matrices
(\bm{S}_{n})
with
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{S}_{n})\to\infty
such that

Conditional Nuisance Posterior Concentration: It holds

Proof.

d_{n,H}^{2}\big((\underline{\theta}_{0},f_{0}),(\underline{\theta},f)\big)\lesssim\|f-f_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{n}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})\right\|_{1}\lesssim\|f-f_{0}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\right\|\,\left\|\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0}\right\|,

because by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality it holds

for any vector
\underline{x}
of dimension

This implies (see e.g. Lemma B.32 in the appendix of

) for some positive constant

\log N(\xi\varepsilon_{n},\mathcal{A}_{n},d_{n,H})\leqslant\log N(c\xi^{2}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\|\bm{X}_{n}\|^{-1},\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)},\|\cdot\|)+\log N(c\xi^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2},\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(2)},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}).

(26)

An application of Proposition C.2 in
Ghosal and van der Vaart (2017)
reveals – recalling

from (

the first summand on the right hand side of (

is bounded from above by

r\log 3+r\rho_{1}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}-r\log(c\xi^{2}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2})+r\log(\|\bm{X}_{n}\|)\leqslant 2r\rho_{1}n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}

for
n
large enough by Assumption

and the fact that

grows polynomially in

.
By choosing

small enough, this can be bounded from above by

.

By Lemma B.4 in
Choudhuri et al. (2004a)
and because

falls polynomially in

\lesssim k_{n}\log(n)=\rho_{2}\,n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}

This concludes (

The existence of tests as required by Theorem 8.19 in

follows e.g. from Lemma 2 in

, such that all assumptions of that theorem are fulfilled showing that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)c}\times\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(2)}\right)\cup\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(1)}\times\mathcal{A}_{n}^{(2)c}\right),

and thus by prior independence

(27)

By Assumption

Concerning the second probability in (

Thus, for some

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}\right)}{P_{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}\big(B_{n}(\underline{\theta}_{0},f_{0},\bar{\varepsilon}_{n})\big)}\lesssim\exp\left(-\bar{c}n^{1-2s}/\log(n)+cn^{1-2\bar{s}}\right)=o\left(\exp(-2n\bar{\varepsilon}_{n}^{2})\right).

\displaystyle(\underline{\hat{\theta}}_{n}-\underline{\theta}_{0})^{T}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}(\underline{\hat{\theta}}_{n}-\underline{\theta}_{0})=\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T})^{2}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}.

\displaystyle\tau_{0}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n})^{-1}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{n}\preceq\tau_{1}(\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n})^{-1}

\displaystyle M_{n}^{-2}\lambda_{\min}^{-1}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})\|\tilde{\bm{X}}_{n}^{T}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{n}{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n})}\,\|\bm{W}_{n}(f)\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=O(1)(\|\bm{W}_{n}(f_{0})\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}\|^{2}+\|\bm{W}_{n}(f)\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}-\bm{W}_{n}(f_{0})\underline{\tilde{e}}_{n}\|^{2}).

It remains to prove

Lemma 8

Under the assumptions of Theorem

Theorem 9

as in (

Proof.

From Theorem
6

Thus, it follows from Corollary

large enough, yielding

P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}((\underline{\theta},f)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathcal{H}_{n}|\underline{Z}_{n})\to 1

in
P_{W;0}^{n}
-probability.
First, it holds

\displaystyle(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})^{T}\bm{S}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})\leqslant\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}\,(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})^{T}\bm{X}_{n}^{T}\bm{X}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0}),

such that

\displaystyle P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(\Theta_{n}\times\mathcal{H}_{n}|\underline{Z}_{n})\geqslant P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(\{\|\bm{X}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})\|^{2}<\tau_{0}\lambda_{\min}^{1/2}(\bm{S}_{n})\}\times\mathcal{H}_{n}|\underline{Z}_{n})

\displaystyle=P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathcal{H}_{n}|\underline{Z}_{n})-P_{W;\tau_{0},\tau_{1}}^{n}(\{\|\bm{X}_{n}(\underline{\theta}-\underline{\theta}_{0})\|\geqslant\sqrt{\tau_{0}}\lambda_{\min}^{1/4}(\bm{S}_{n})\}\times\mathcal{H}_{n}|\underline{Z}_{n})

\displaystyle\to 1

in
P_{W;0}^{n}
-probability by (
16
) and Lemma
8
.
∎

5
Discussion

In this paper,
a semiparametric Bernstein-von-Mises theorem for the parameter vector of a linear model with nonparametrically modelled time series errors has been derived where the pseudo-posterior distribution is based on the Whittle likelihood approximation.

The theorem shows that the pseudo-posterior is asymptotically Gaussian with mean equal to the pseudo-maximum likelihood estimator calculated under the Whittle likelihood and covariance matrix equal to the asymptotic covariance matrix of the pseudo-maximum likelihood estimator also calculated under the Whittle likelihood.

The theorem is crucial as Bayesian uncertainty quantification e.g. by means of credibility sets or posterior standard deviations will only report the true frequentist uncertainty if this covariance matrix asymptotically coincides in a suitable sense with the corresponding quantity under the exact Gaussian likelihood.

We discuss conditions under which this asymptotic equivalence holds and illustrate this in examples. However, we also provide a counter-example where these conditions are not met and where posterior inference based on the asymptotic Gaussian approximation under the Whittle likelihood results in a systematic over- or underestimation of the statistical uncertainty.

An important avenue for future research will be to obtain a better understanding of what assumptions are required to have not only first (in the sense of posterior consistency) but also second-order correctness (in the sense that the asymptotic covariance associated with a Bayesian point estimator coincides with the one present in the posterior).

Lemma 11
.

Let
\underline{\mu}_{0},\underline{\mu}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
,
where
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the cone of symmetric positive definite
matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
with
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{i})\geqslant\tau_{0}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{i})\leqslant\tau_{1}

for
i=0,1
and some positive constants
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
.
Assume that
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1/2}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1/2})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
Let
p_{i}
denote the density of the
N_{d}(\underline{\mu}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}_{i})
distribution for
i=0,1
.
Denote by

K:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\frac{p_{0}(\underline{z})}{p_{1}(\underline{z})}p_{0}(\underline{z})d\underline{z},\quad V:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\log\frac{p_{0}(\underline{z})}{p_{1}(\underline{z})}-K\right)^{2}p_{0}(\underline{z})d\underline{z}

the Kullback-Leibler divergence and associated variance term from
p_{0}
to
p_{1}
.
Then it holds

\displaystyle K\lesssim\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}

and

\displaystyle V\lesssim\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}

with proportionality constants only depending on
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
.

Proof.

Some calculations yield for
\underline{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle 2\log\frac{p_{0}(z)}{p_{1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\left[\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}|-\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}|+(\underline{z}-\underline{\mu}_{0})^{T}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)(\underline{z}-\underline{\mu}_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{z}-\underline{\mu}_{0})+(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})

\displaystyle=:l_{1}(\underline{z})+2\,l_{2}(\underline{z})+(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}).

(34)

Since
l_{1}(\underline{z})/2
is the log-likelihood-ratio between an
N_{d}(\underline{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
and an
N_{d}(\underline{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{1})
distribution, we conclude (see e.g. Lemma 7.5 in
Meier [2018]
) for the corresponding Kullback-Leibler divergence

\int l_{1}(\underline{z})p_{0}(\underline{z})dz\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1})^{2}}\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}.

By
\int l_{2}(\underline{z})p_{0}(\underline{z})d\underline{z}=0
and
(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})\lesssim\|\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}
, the first assertion follows.

For the second assertion, let
\underline{Z}\sim N_{d}(\underline{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
.
Then
V=\mathrm{Var}[\log\frac{p_{0}(\underline{Z}+\mu_{0})}{p_{1}(\underline{Z}+\mu_{0})}]
and from (
34
) this yields

4V=\mathrm{Var}\left[\underline{Z}^{T}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)\underline{Z}+2(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\underline{Z}\right]=:\mathrm{Var}\left[\eta_{1}+2\eta_{2}\right].

\mathrm{Var}[\eta_{1}]
is the KL variance term (up to a multiplicative constant)
between an
N_{d}(\underline{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
and an
N_{d}(\underline{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{1})

distribution, so that (see e.g. Lemma 7.5 in
Meier [2018]
)

\mathrm{Var}[\eta_{1}]\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1})^{2}}\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\eta_{2}]=\mathrm{Var}\left[(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\underline{Z}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\underline{Z}\underline{Z}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})\right]

\displaystyle=(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}(\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1})\leqslant\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\right)\|\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}\lesssim\|\underline{\mu}_{0}-\underline{\mu}_{1}\|^{2}.

The assertion follows by
|\mathrm{Cov}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})|\leqslant\max(\mathrm{Var}(\eta_{1}),\mathrm{Var}(\eta_{2}))
by an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.∎

The following result bounds the Hellinger distance between two normals.
A corresponding upper bound for multivariate normals can be found in Lemma 9.4 of
Meier [2018]
while in this situation the lower bound can be obtained similarly to Lemma 7.16 in
Meier et al. [2020]
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<\tau_{0}\leqslant\sigma_{i}^{2}\leqslant\tau_{1}<\infty
,
i=1,2
, for some positive constants
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
.
Let
p_{i}
denote the density of the
N(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
distribution for
i=1,2
.
Then it holds

\displaystyle|\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}|^{2}+1-\exp\left(-c|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|^{2}\right)\lesssim d_{H}^{2}(p_{1},p_{2})\lesssim|\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}|+|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|

with
c>0
and proportionality constants depending only on
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
.

Proof.

We start by noting the representation (see
Pardo [2005]
, p.51 and 46)

\displaystyle d_{H}^{2}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)\right).

(35)

For the first summand we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle 0\leqslant 1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\tau_{0}}}\,|\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}|\lesssim|\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}|,

where in the second to last step we used the mean value theorem to bound
|\sqrt{x_{1}}-\sqrt{x_{2}}|
(with
x_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
).
For the second term we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)\right)\lesssim 1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8\,\tau_{0}}\,(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}\right)\lesssim|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|,

because
the function
h_{c}
with
h_{c}(x):=1-\exp(-cx^{2})

has points of inflection at
x^{*}=\pm(2c)^{-1/2}
, where the maximal derivative
is attained, with value
h_{c}^{\prime}(x^{*})=\sqrt{2c}\exp(-1/2)
. This completes the proof of the upper bound.

Because
1-\sqrt{x}\geqslant(1-x)/2
we get the lower bound for the first summand in (
35
)

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\gtrsim|\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}|^{2},

where, in the last step, we used again the mean value theorem to bound
|\sqrt{x_{1}}-\sqrt{x_{2}}|
(with
x_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
) this time from below. The lower bound for the second summand in (
35
) follows straightforwardly from

\tau_{0}\leqslant\sigma_{i}^{2}\leqslant\tau_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Denote
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)

E

Let
\bm{W}_{n}(\cdot)=(w_{i,j;n}(\cdot))_{i=1,\cdots,r;j=1\cdots,n}

\displaystyle\|\bm{W}_{n}(f)\underline{\xi}_{n}-\bm{W}_{n}(f_{0})\underline{\xi}_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(Q_{i;n}(f)-Q_{i;n}(f_{0}))^{2},
