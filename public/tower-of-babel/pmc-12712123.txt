Differential response of plasma membrane versus chloroplast functions to freeze–thaw stress by Antarctic species, Deschampsia antarctica and Colobanthus quitensis, as explored through freeze-injury and post-thaw recovery
Authors: Kyungwon Min, Syahril Sulaiman, Jungeun Lee, Suyeon Seo, Rajeev Arora, Hyoungseok Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Main conclusionAntarctic plants employ distinct cold acclimation strategies: Deschampsia antarctica uses general membrane-chloroplast stabilization while Colobanthus quitensis relies on chloroplast-focused tolerance mechanisms.AbstractThe two native vascular plants of Antarctica, Deschampsia antarctica and Colobanthus quitensis, persist in one of the most extreme terrestrial environments on Earth, where episodic freeze–thaw cycles are frequent even during the growing season. Survival under such conditions necessitates not only tolerance to freezing alone but also effective recovery from freeze-induced injuries—a composite trait referred to as freeze–thaw stress tolerance (FTST). Yet, estimates of FTST of Antarctic plants have remained inconsistent across studies, largely due to methodological differences in freezing regimes and injury assessment metrics. Here, we employed a standardized, ice-nucleation-controlled freeze–thaw protocol and assessed FTST using two independent physiological indicators: electrolyte leakage (membrane integrity) and chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm; PSII function). We further validated the LT50 values—the temperature causing 50% injury—through post-thaw recovery (PTR) assays, and examined total soluble sugar dynamics as a metabolic indicator of recovery capacity. D. antarctica exhibited coordinated enhancements in both membrane and chloroplast resilience following cold acclimation, with LT50 values from both metrics closely aligned. In contrast, C. quitensis demonstrated a chloroplast-centered acclimation strategy, characterized by pronounced improvement in Fv/Fm-based LT50, while electrolyte-leakage based estimates remained largely unchanged. PTR results and sugar profiling supported the biological relevance of Fv/Fm as a more reliable FTST marker in C. quitensis. Together, these findings reveal distinct, species-specific acclimation frameworks to freeze–thaw stress; a global stabilization strategy in D. antarctica and a chloroplast-focused tolerance mechanism in C. quitensis, underscoring divergent evolutionary pathways for polar plant survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00425-025-04899-4.

Introduction

Antarctica is the coldest and windiest landmass on Earth, with mean air temperatures in the coastal zones of the Antarctic Peninsula and adjacent islands (Maritime Antarctica) rarely exceeding 0 to 5 °C and daily temperature fluctuations ranging from − 10 to 15 °C during the summer season. Under these frequent freeze–thaw conditions, only two vascular plant species—Antarctic hair grass Deschampsia antarctica Desv. (Poaceae) and Antarctic pearlwort Colobanthus quitensis (Kunth) Bartl. (Caryophyllaceae)—have successfully established natural populations (Lewis Smith 2003). This implies that these Antarctic plants must endure repeated freeze–thaw cycles, even during the growing season, necessitating sufficient freeze–thaw stress tolerance (FTST) for survival and growth. In fact, the true FTST of a plant is not only its ability to survive a frost episode but also its capacity to recover from freeze–thaw induced injuries (Palta et al. 1977a,b; Arora and Palta 1988, 1991; Chen and Arora 2014; Vyse et al. 2020; Min et al. 2024). During a frost event, plant cells experience freeze-induced dehydration (cell shrinkage) as ice forms in the extracellular space, followed by rehydration (cell expansion) upon thawing—a process collectively referred to as freeze–thaw stress. These sequential stressors can act synergistically to disrupt cellular homeostasis including structural and functional alterations in the plasma membrane manifested in electrolyte-leakage (EL) and decreased quantum yield efficiency of photosystem II (PSII) (manifested in reduced Fv/Fm measured by chlorophyll fluorescence) (Mittler 2002; Chen and Arora 2014; Min et al. 2014), among other metabolic disorders (Arora 2018). Hence, the extent of freeze–thaw injury in the plasma membrane or PSII can serve as a key indicator for assessing the FTST of plant tissues.

Plant survival from freezing stress encompasses two primary mechanisms: freezing avoidance and freezing tolerance—the latter herein referred to as FTST. Freezing avoidance means prevention of ice formation through freezing point depression and/or supercooling of cell sap due to its insulation from freeze-desiccation despite the presence of extracellular ice (Wisniewski and Arora 1992). Supercooling capacity can also be imparted to tissues due to thermal or ice barriers (Bertel et al. 2021). FTST, on the other hand, refers to the ability to survive extracellular ice formation and subsequent freeze-induced dehydration (Larcher 2003). Of these two survival mechanisms, FTST serves as the primary survival strategy for most cold-climate plants, as the freezing avoidance strategy is often limited to specific tissues or environmental conditions, such as xylem parenchyma and bud primordia of several temperate zone woody perennials (Squeo et al. 1991; Wisniewski et al. 2014; Neuner et al. 2019a, b; Ambroise et al. 2020). Importantly, such FTST can be enhanced via cold acclimation (CA), a process whereby plants acquire greater tolerance to freeze–thaw stress following exposure to low but non-freezing temperatures (Thomashow 2010). This involves a myriad of adjustments at physiological and biochemical levels, including accumulation of osmolytes (e.g. soluble sugars) (Guy 1990; Xin and Browse 2000). Several studies also reported that D. antarctica and C. quitensis possess the capacity for CA, with FTST improvement (Bravo et al. 2001; Gianoli et al. 2004; Chew et al. 2012; Reyes-Bahamonde 2013; López et al. 2023).

Numerous studies collectively demonstrate considerable variability in FTST assessments, with non-acclimated (NA) LT50 values (i.e., temperature causing 50% tissue injury) ranging from − 4.8 to − 8.1 °C for C. quitensis and − 12.0 to − 16.9 °C for D. antarctica; cold-acclimated values ranged from − 5.8 to − 15 °C and − 17.0 to − 26.6 °C, respectively (Bravo et al. 2001; Gianoli et al. 2004; Sierra-Almeida et al. 2018; López et al. 2023). Such discrepancies among studies may be attributed to several methodological factors, including differences in pre-treatment conditions (open field, greenhouse, and growth chamber growing environments), cooling rates (Arora 2018), ice-nucleation protocols (controlled vs. uncontrolled freezing), duration of freezing in the freeze-tolerance tests (Min et al. 2014; Arora and Chen 2025), physiological or chronological age (Lim et al. 2014), and physiological endpoints used for LT50 determination (electrolyte-leakage vs. photosynthetic assays). To address this variability in FTST (LT50) estimates and gain insights into species-specific CA-induced FTST mechanisms, a systematic comparative study employing standardized protocols and multiple physiological assessments under controlled environmental conditions is required.

In the present study, we aimed to estimate the FTST of D. antarctica and C. quitensis by determining LT50 values in NA and CA tissues, using both EL and chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm) as indicators of membrane and PSII injury, respectively. To validate the measured LT50 values truly reflect the boundary between sub-lethal and lethal stress, we conducted post-thaw recovery (PTR) tests, with the underlying hypothesis that successful recovery at temperatures milder than LT50 temperatures and irreversible damage at temperatures colder than LT50 would indicate the functional validity of the LT50 estimates (Chen and Arora 2014; Vyse et al. 2020; Min et al. 2024; Arora and Chen 2025). We also analyzed total soluble sugar (TSS) dynamics during the PTR periods to explore potential metabolic strategies contributing to FTST. This approach allowed us to provide the first physiologically validated quantification of true FTST in Antarctic vascular plants, while also revealing species-specific injury and recovery patterns that reflect distinct adaptive strategies to freeze–thaw stress.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Deschampsia antarctica Desv. (Poaceae) and Colobanthus quitensis (Kunth) Bartl. (Caryophyllaceae) were collected in the vicinity of the Korean King Sejong Antarctic Station (62° 14′ 29″ S; 58° 44′ 18″ W) on the Barton Peninsula of King George Island in January 2013. The collected plants were moved to the lab and cultivated hydroponically in a 0.5 × Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 2% sucrose. They were maintained throughout in the vegetative / juvenile phase under a 16-h photoperiod with average photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) of ~ 150 μmol m−2 s−1 at 16 °C, hereafter referred to as the non-acclimation (NA) treatment (Min et al. 2024). For cold acclimation (CA) treatment, the plants described above were subjected to a CA regimen comprising three different durations (i.e., 7-, 14-, and 30-days) at 4 °C, with a light intensity of ~ 75 μmol m−2 s−1 under a 16/8-h (D/N). For all physiological analyses (below), only green, photosynthetically active rosettes from C. quitensis and tillers from D. antarctica were used as the experimental materials; any yellowed or senescent leaf portions were carefully removed prior to experiments.

FTST (LT50) determination using electrolyte-leakage (EL) based temperature-controlled freeze–thaw protocol

The EL-based, temperature-controlled freeze–thaw test was conducted as detailed by Min et al. (2024). Briefly, a single rosette of C. quitensis and six to seven green tillers of D. antarctica were individually placed in a 2.5 × 20 cm test tube containing 100 μL of ddH2O. For each temperature treatment, five to six replicates per plant species were prepared and subjected to a temperature-controlled glycol bath (Model NCB-2600; RIKAKIKAI Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). To prevent artificial supercooling, ice nucleation (extra-cellular freezing) was ensured in all samples by dropping ice chips in each test tube at − 1 °C, allowing the tissue and glycol bath temperatures to reach equilibrium (~ 1-h). Tissues were subsequently cooled to various sub-zero temperatures at controlled rates: 0.5 °C/30 min down to − 4 °C, 1 °C/30 min from − 4 to − 12 °C, followed by 3 °C/30 min from − 12 to − 21 °C. The tubes were removed from the glycol bath at designated temperatures, and the tissues were thawed on ice overnight (~ 12-h). Unfrozen controls (UFCs) were kept at 0 °C throughout the freeze–thaw cycle. The following morning, samples were taken off the ice and gradually warmed at 4 °C for 1-h, followed by 1-h at ~ 20 °C (room temperature). Next, 5 mL ddH2O was added to each tube followed by vacuum infiltration two times (2 min each) at ~ 100 kPa and shaken for 1.5-h at 250 rpm in a shaker (Model VS-202D; Vision Scientific Co., Ltd, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Initial EL was measured for each sample with a conductivity meter (Twin Cond. B-173, HORIBA, Kyoto, Japan). Final EL was measured at room temperature after autoclaving samples. Percent injury, indicative of the FTST of samples under individual sub-zero temperatures, was used to calculate the LT50, defined as the temperature at which tissues reach 50% of their maximum injury, by generating a sigmoid curve fitting the Gompertz function, as per Lim et al. (1998). Data from three independent EL-based FTST experiments, each with 5–6 technical replicates, were pooled for these calculations, and the mean LT50 value along with standard error (SE) was presented.

FTST (LT50) determination using Fv/Fm-based temperature-controlled freeze–thaw protocol

To determine Fv/Fm based FTST (LT50), we slightly modified the protocol used by Stegner et al. (2020). The plant samples subjected to the freeze–thaw cycles, as detailed in the ion leakage-based FTST protocol, were used for this determination to ensure consistency in stress exposure conditions. Following the freeze–thaw cycle, injury was assessed by measuring dark-adapted chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm, the maximal quantum yield of PSII) using an Imaging-PAM (Heinz Walz GMbH, Effeltrich, Germany). Fv/Fm values for each temperature treatment were used to calculate the LT50 by fitting a sigmoidal curve to the data using the Boltzmann function. Data from three independent Fv/Fm based FTST experiments, each with 5–6 technical replicates, were pooled for these calculations, and the mean LT50 value along with SE was presented. LT50 values derived from EL and Fv/Fm methods were compared by plotting the two metrics against each other and fitting linear regression models using R software (v.4.5.1). Agreement with the 1:1 line was used to assess correspondence between methods, while deviations indicated species-specific differences in sensitivity of the two physiological responses.

Post-thaw recovery test

The experimental design for post-thaw recovery (PTR) was adapted from well-established protocols previously validated in various plant species, including onion scales (Palta et al. 1977a, b; Arora and Palta 1991), spinach (Chen and Arora 2014; Min et al. 2014), and Arabidopsis (Vyse et al. 2020); notably, the PTR protocol for C. quitensis (Min et al. 2024) is particularly relevant, as this species was also the focus of the present study. Guided by the LT50 values of NA and CA samples across both species, four temperature treatments (–6.0 °C, − 8.0 °C, − 10.0 °C, and − 12.0 °C) were selected to represent moderate to severe stress levels for subsequent characterization of the recovery processes. Tissues were subsequently frozen to the four selected temperatures and thawed according to the previously described procedure. The injury of stressed tissues was assessed by calculating the injury percentage via EL and measuring Fv/Fm right after thawing (RAT), with their corresponding UFCs (RATUFCs). A separate set of replicated freeze–thaw injured samples was subsequently subjected to a recovery treatment, consisting of 6-day exposure (6D-REC) to 4–5 °C under 50 μmol m−2 s−1 PAR with a 16/8-h (D/N), within the same test tubes in which the samples were initially frozen. The corresponding UFC was also subjected to the same recovery conditions, resulting in 6D-RECUFC. EL assays and Fv/Fm measurements were performed on both 6D-RECUFC and 6D-REC samples to evaluate and compare changes in injury following recovery, as described above. These tests were independently repeated three times, with each repetition consisting of five to six replicates per temperature. The percent injury data from EL assays and Fv/Fm measurements were pooled across all experiments to calculate the representative means with SE for each test, respectively, followed by paired t-test (α = 0.05) to determine significant differences between RAT and REC samples at each test temperature. Additional samples of RATUFC, RAT, 6D-RECUFC, and 6D-REC, each consisting of three biological replicates with five to six technical replicates per treatment per temperature, were immediately frozen at − 80 °C after collection for subsequent biochemical analysis.

Total soluble sugar analysis

Total soluble sugar content (TSS) was quantified in NA and 30-day CA (CA30d) samples of D. antarctica and C. quitensis, which had undergone freeze–thaw cycles at four sub-zero temperatures (− 6.0 °C, − 8.0 °C, − 10.0 °C, and − 12.0 °C) followed by a 6-day recovery period, as described earlier. For each condition (RAT and 6D-REC) of both NA and CA30d samples, three biological replicates with five to six technical replicates per treatment temperature were analyzed. Approximately 0.1–0.5 g of frozen plant tissue was weighed, homogenized using a mortar and pestle, and extracted with 10 mL of 80% ethanol. The extraction was carried out by incubating the samples in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 1800 g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and the extraction was repeated to ensure complete sugar recovery. To quantify the total soluble sugars, 1 mL of the pooled extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of 5% phenol solution and 2.5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid, vortexed, and left at room temperature for 10 min (Dubois et al. 1956). The mixture was then incubated in a water bath at 90 °C for 10 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer (Synergy MX, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). A standard curve was constructed using known concentrations of glucose (0.0 to 1.0 mg mL−1), and the sugar content of each sample was calculated by comparing the absorbance values to the standard curve (Masuko et al. 2005). The TSS was expressed on a dry weight basis. This experiment was independently conducted three times, each with five technical replicates for each temperature, treatment, and species. The data were pooled to calculate the representative treatment means with SE. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, followed by one-way ANOVA with treatment and temperature combined as a single factor. Mean differences were evaluated using Fisher’s LSD test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.05).

Comparison of LT50 values estimated by EL assay and Fv/Fm measurement across all treatments reveals distinct species-specific patterns (Table 1). D. antarctica exhibited remarkable consistency between the two metrics throughout CA periods, with differences of LT50 never exceeding 0.5 °C. Based on EL assays, CA progressively enhanced FTST from − 7.8 °C (NA) to − 9.3 °C (CA7d), − 9.6 °C (CA14d), and − 10.7 °C (CA30d), while Fv/Fm-based assessments showed similar improvements from − 7.8 °C (NA) to-8.9 °C (CA7d), − 9.8 °C (CA14d), and − 11.2 °C (CA30d). This translated to a uniform enhancement in FTST of about 3 °C at the end of 30d CA as assessed by the two cellular indicators, i.e., membrane integrity or chloroplast functions. In contrast, C. quitensis displayed distinct responses between the two metrics as CA progressed (Table 1). For this species, EL assays showed relatively minor improvements from − 7.5 °C (NA) to − 7.7 °C (CA7d), − 8.4 °C (CA14d), and − 8.6 °C (CA30d). In contrast, Fv/Fm assessments revealed much greater enhancement of FTST, i.e., from − 8.4 °C (NA) to − 10.3 °C (CA7d), − 10.7 °C (CA14d), and − 13.7 °C (CA30d). Hence, the discrepancy between EL and Fv/Fm assessments widened from 0.9 °C at NA to 5.1 °C at CA30d (Table 1), indicating that the Fv/Fm method detected greater CA responses than the EL method. For C. quitensis, the two physiological parameters (EL vs. Fv/Fm) showed different patterns for the recovery potential. EL-based recovery patterns showed unexpected recovery at temperatures colder than the estimated LT50 threshold—most notably, CA30d tissues recovered at − 10.0 °C despite an EL-based LT50 of − 8.6 °C. NA and CA7d tissues also recovered at − 8.0 °C when their LT50s were − 7.5 °C and − 7.7 °C, respectively (Table 2; note the shaded ✓ marks). This contradiction indicates that EL measurements underestimate true FTST (which includes ability to recover) in cold-acclimated C. quitensis. In contrast, Fv/Fm recovery patterns (Table 3) showed consistent correspondence with Fv/Fm-based LT50 estimates, i.e., all treatments recovered at temperatures warmer than their respective Fv/Fm-based LT50 values, including CA30d tissues that recovered across all tested temperatures, while failing to recover at temperatures below their corresponding LT50 thresholds. This strong correlation suggests Fv/Fm as a more reliable indicator of true FTST in C. quitensis.

Discussion

Standardized protocols for FTST (LT50) assessments

A key methodological consideration for measuring FTST in this study was the implementation of protocols that ensure freeze-initiation at the same temperature for all samples and equilibrium freezing. Inadvertent and inconsistent supercooling (in the absence of ice nucleation at a relatively warmer sub-zero temperature) can present a challenge for obtaining consistent injury patterns across samples, as it can cause samples to remain unfrozen until varying degrees of lower temperatures, leading, potentially, to variable injury patterns despite a similar freezing temperature (Fuller and Wisniewski 1998; Wisniewski and Fuller 1999). This inconsistency may arise potentially due to varying durations of extracellular freezing and / or freezing of supercooled cell sap intracellularly (Arora and Chen 2025). Among the major FTST (LT50) assessment studies on Antarctic vascular plants (Bravo et al. 2001; Gianoli et al. 2004; Sierra-Almeida et al. 2018; López et al. 2023), only Bravo et al. (2001) explicitly implemented ice-nucleation control using silver iodide as a nucleating agent. This approach involved applying silver iodide to samples before subjecting them to a cooling program and detecting ‘spontaneous’ ice-nucleation of tissues. This approach may be useful for understanding the supercooling capacity. However, this method may not guarantee synchronous and extracellular ice formation across samples. Moreover, the extended supercooling periods inherent in this approach could increase the risk of intracellular freezing if nucleation eventually occurred at much lower temperatures, potentially leading to erroneous FTST. The degree of supercooling can also be impacted by wetness versus dryness of the surface, a drier leaf supercooling to a greater extent than a wet leaf (Gusta et al. 2004). To address this limitation, we evaluated FTST using a temperature-controlled freeze–thaw protocol that regulated gradual and realistic cooling and thawing, while ensuring ice-nucleation (extracellular ice-formation) at − 1 °C by introducing an ice chip to all the samples simultaneously and allowing them to equilibrate until the dissipation of all exothermic heat before administering a further cooling program.

LT50 in this study was quantified by two independent physiological indicators simultaneously: EL and PSII quantum efficiency (Fv/Fm). As illustrated in Fig. 1A and C, EL-based LT50 was determined by fitting a Gompertz function to sigmoidal injury curves generated from EL data obtained after freeze–thaw cycles. In parallel, Fig. 1B and D show LT50 estimation based on Fv/Fm measurements across freezing temperatures, where a Boltzmann function was used to identify the mid-point of decline in quantum efficiency of PSII. EL-based LT50 estimation has been widely used to assess membrane stability under freeze–thaw stress across various plant species including C. quitensis (Lim et al. 1998; Chen and Arora 2014; Liu et al. 2020; Vyse et al. 2020; Rehm et al. 2021; Min et al. 2024), while the Fv/Fm-based approach is valuable for evaluating photosynthetic apparatus integrity (PSII efficiency) following freeze–thaw events (Su et al. 2015; Neuner et al. 2019b; Stegner et al. 2020) as well as post-thaw recovery (Vyse et al. 2020).

EL-based LT50 comparison reveals distinct membrane-level responses between D. antarctica and C. quitensis following cold acclimation

Among all FTST studies on Antarctic vascular plants to date, the only study, to the best of our knowledge, that serves as the most direct comparison with our results on EL-based LT50 is that of Bravo et al. (2001). In D. antarctica, these authors reported a marked improvement in FTST during CA, as LT50 values shifted from − 12 °C (NA tissues) to − 26.6 °C following 21 days of CA—an ~ 15 °C enhancement of FTST. In contrast, our study showed a substantially lower (~ 3.0 °C) shift of LT50 following 30 days of CA, i.e., from − 7.8 °C (NA) to − 10.7 °C (CA30d) (Table 1). This discrepancy may be attributed to differences between somewhat spontaneous, uncontrolled ice nucleation (Bravo et al. 2001) versus controlled ice nucleation at − 1.0 °C (in the present study). Consequently, the results of Bravo et al. (2001) may represent overestimated values in the context of true FTST (LT50) assessment, likely due to extended supercooling, which could potentially lead to intracellular freezing at much colder temperatures. Additionally, it is tempting to speculate that the exposure of plants to relatively low PPFD, i.e., 75 μmol m−2 s−1 in our study, precludes a possible confounding effect of light stress during the several days of cold acclimation treatment. Therefore, enhanced EL and lowered Fv/Fm values for cold-stressed tissues noted in the present study may predominantly be a reflection of true freezing (cold) stress. In support of this notion, Clemente-Moreno et al. (2019) noted a significant photooxidative injury in cold-acclimated D. antarctica plants that were exposed to 300 μmol m−2 s−1 PPFD.

Despite the discrepancy in absolute values, however, both studies (Bravo et al. 2001 and the present studies) reveal consistent species-specific patterns in CA responses. In D. antarctica, LT50 values declined progressively with increasing CA periods in both datasets. Bravo et al. (2001) noted a gradual lowering of LT50 (increase in FTST) from − 12.0 °C in NA tissues to − 21.5 °C, − 24.0 °C, or − 26.6 °C after 7, 14, or 21 days of CA, respectively. Similarly, the present study showed progressive enhancement of FTST from − 7.8 °C in NA tissues to − 9.3 °C, − 9.6 °C, or − 10.7 °C following 7, 14, or 30 days of CA, respectively (Table 1). This cross-study consistency suggests that D. antarctica improved membrane integrity following CA, reflected in reduced EL after freeze–thaw stress. A sizable body of literature has similarly demonstrated that CA tissues exhibit structural and functional resiliency of membrane, largely attributable to increased lipid unsaturation, which promotes greater membrane fluidity and stability under freezing conditions (Xin and Browse 2000; Barrero-Sicilia et al. 2017; Satyakam et al. 2022; Vu et al. 2022). Indeed, metabolomic analyses of cold-acclimated D. antarctica showed significant accumulation of metabolites associated with both membrane stabilization and maintenance of PSII function (Fv/Fm) (Clemente-Moreno et al. 2020). In contrast, C. quitensis exhibited only marginal changes in LT50 with CA across both studies. Bravo et al. (2001) reported a minor lowering of LT50 from − 4.8 °C in NA tissues to − 5.8 °C over 21 days of CA, closely matching the degree of cold acclimation (~ 1 °C) also observed in the present study, i.e., from − 7.5 °C in NA tissues to − 8.6 °C after 30 days of CA (Table 1). This consistently limited shift in LT50 suggests that membrane stabilization plays a relatively minor role in the CA responses by C. quitensis, highlighting a fundamentally different strategy from that of D. antarctica, as reflected also by Fig. 1E.

Chloroplast-specific cold acclimation strategy in C. quitensis

While C. quitensis exhibited only modest improvement in FTST when assessed by EL-based membrane-integrity criterion, our Fv/Fm-based LT50 assessments revealed markedly greater enhancement of FTST during CA. Specifically, LT50 values shifted from − 8.4 °C in NA tissues to − 13.7 °C after 30 days of CA, representing a 5.3 °C improvement in FTST (Table 1). Such a fivefold enhancement of FTST compared to only ~ 1 °C increase observed by EL-based LT50 measurements over the same 30d period of CA suggests that C. quitensis preferentially enhances photosynthetic apparatus stability during CA rather than the membrane integrity. This differential response becomes more apparent when compared to D. antarctica, where LT50 values derived from EL vs. Fv/Fm measurements, respectively, remained closely aligned throughout all CA stages (e.g., − 9.3 °C vs. − 8.9 °C, − 9.6 °C vs. − 9.8 °C, and − 10.7 °C vs. − 11.2 °C at CA7d, CA14d and CA30d, respectively) (Table 1). This indicated coordinated membrane-integrity and chloroplast-function responses to CA in D. antarctica. In contrast, for C. quitensis, the gap between membrane-integrity and chloroplast-function-based LT50 values widened with the duration of CA, reaching a 5.1 °C difference by CA30d, thereby highlighting a clear shift toward chloroplast-specific acclimation. It is also noteworthy that even at the NA state, C. quitensis exhibited about 1 °C higher FTST based on chloroplast functions (Fv/Fm LT50, − 8.4 °C) than that reflected by membrane integrity (EL LT50, − 7.5 °C); no such difference was noted for D. antarctica.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that reported substantial improvement in FTST of C. quitensis when assessed using photochemical methods. For instance, Reyes-Bahamonde (2013) documented an LT50 shift from − 7.0 to − 14.9 °C, while López et al. (2023) observed similar enhancements from − 8.1 to − 13.3 °C following CA. In both cases, the photochemical-based improvements far exceeded those estimated by EL assays (Bravo et al. 2001), as well as our own membrane-based results (Table 1), reinforcing the notion that chloroplast function—rather than plasma membrane stability—constitutes the primary target of CA in C. quitensis.

Post-thaw recovery validates LT50 estimates and reinforces species-specific FTST strategies

To validate the physiological relevance of our LT50 estimates and further investigate species-specific FTST mechanisms, we conducted post-thaw recovery (PTR) tests. The underlying principle is that tissues exposed to temperatures warmer than their LT50 should retain the potential for recovery, while those subjected to temperatures colder than LT50, typically, are irreparably injured (Chen and Arora 2014; Vyse et al. 2020; Min et al. 2024). Consistent with this thesis, D. antarctica tissues failed to recover either membrane integrity or PSII function when exposed to temperatures colder than their respective EL- or Fv/Fm-based LT50 values. In contrast, recovery was evident at temperatures warmer than the LT50 thresholds (Tables 2, 3), confirming that both LT50 metrics reliably reflect true FTST boundaries in this species, which includes not only freeze-tolerance right after thaw but also PTR potential.

In C. quitensis, however, PTR data revealed an intriguingly different pattern. Although the EL-based LT50 for NA and CA7d samples were − 7.5 °C and − 7.7 °C, respectively, both groups exhibited recovery of membrane integrity (i.e., reduced EL, and therefore, injury during post-thaw) even after exposure to − 8.0 °C—a temperature colder than their estimated lethal thresholds (Table 2). More interestingly, CA30d tissues, with an EL-based LT50 of − 8.6 °C, showed consistent recovery of membrane integrity even at − 10.0 °C, and partial, albeit statistically non-significant, recovery at − 12.0 °C (Table 2). This pattern challenges the assumption that EL-derived LT50 alone defines the functional limit of freeze–thaw survival in this species. In contrast, Fv/Fm-based LT50 values in C. quitensis closely matched conventionally expected PTR response, i.e., recovery occurred exclusively at temperatures warmer than the corresponding Fv/Fm-derived LT50 for each acclimation stage (Table 3). This alignment supports the biological validity of Fv/Fm as, perhaps, a more reliable FTST indicator in C. quitensis. Moreover, it reinforces the conclusion that this species employs a distinct CA strategy from D. antarctica—one that centers, preferentially, on the protection and functional preservation of the chloroplast, and less so on the stabilization of the plasma membrane.

A question may arise as to why Fv/Fm-based LT50 offers a more physiologically robust estimate of FTST in C. quitensis, and what physiological mechanisms may underlie its chloroplast-centered acclimation strategy. In terms of methodology, Fv/Fm-based assessments are less affected by anatomical traits such as leaf thickness and tissue rigidity, which can influence electrolyte diffusion and compromise the validity of EL measurements in species with dense mesophyll layers, such as C. quitensis (Boorse et al. 1998; Bannister 2007). The chlorophyll fluorescence approach, by contrast, directly reflects PSII functionality and could provide a more consistent and tissue-independent measure of physiological injury under freeze–thaw conditions (Neuner and Buchner 1999). Regarding the underlying mechanisms, although this study did not directly investigate chloroplast-level acclimation processes, previous works have shown that C. quitensis exhibits significantly greater NPQ capacity and more robust xanthophyll cycle activity following CA compared to D. antarctica (Bravo et al. 2007; Pérez-Torres et al. 2007). These photoprotective traits likely enhance PSII stability and facilitate more efficient dissipation of excess excitation energy under combined cold and high-light stress, thereby supporting the observed alignment between Fv/Fm-based LT50 and PTR response.

Sugar recovery patterns highlight differential acclimation strategies in D. antarctica and C. quitensis

Across both species, sugar concentrations sharply declined immediately at RAT, regardless of CA status (Fig. 2). This reduction is likely due to freeze-induced membrane damage, during which soluble sugars, like electrolytes, are lost through injured membrane transport proteins—a well-documented phenomenon in plants subjected to freeze–thaw stress (Palta et al. 1977a; Barbier et al. 1982; Arora 2018; Chowhan et al. 2024; Arora and Chen 2025). The decline in sugars in RAT samples could also be due to the export of sugars from the symplast to extracellular space, to potentially prevent adhesion of ice to cell wall polymers (Olien 1984). Below 0 °C, ice competes with cell wall polymers for a thin layer of intervening water molecules. At sub-zero temperatures under equilibrium freezing, the reduction in thickness and kinetic freedom of intervening water promotes ice adhesion with cell wall polymers (Takahashi et al. 2021), leading to lethal distortions of membrane integrity (Olien 1984). Olien (1984) hypothesized that plants exude sugars into extracellular space during freezing to relieve ice-adhesion stress. Experiments with winter oat and rye at − 3 °C confirmed such presence of extracellular sucrose, fructose, and glucose (Livingston and Henson 1998). The consistent decline across NA and CA30d samples suggests that neither species could prevent initial sugar leakage upon freezing. Reduced sugars in RAT samples relative to UFC, especially at the temperatures from which tissues were able to recover, could also be due to consumption of sugars by elevated respiration. Respiration was not measured in the present study; however, Vyse et al. (2020) noted significantly increased respiration in Arabidopsis leaves right after thaw (when frozen to sub-lethal temperatures) compared to unfrozen controls. Notably, in both Antarctic species used in the present study, sugar levels in 6D-RECUFC samples were consistently higher than in RATUFC samples. This reflects the effect of extended CA periods: NA-UFC samples experienced 6 days of CA (i.e., NA6D-REC) compared to no prior acclimation (NA-RAT), while CA30d-UFC samples underwent a total of 36 days of CA (CA30d 6D-REC) versus 30 days of CA (CA30dRAT). This pattern is consistent with numerous studies reporting that prolonged CA progressively increases soluble sugar content in plants (Guy 1990; Kamata and Uemura 2004; Vyse et al. 2020; Kitashova et al. 2023).

Sugar dynamics during PTR period further clarify species-specific FTST strategies by revealing distinct physiological priorities between D. antarctica and C. quitensis. In D. antarctica, sugar levels increased only in samples exposed to temperatures warmer than both EL- and Fv/Fm-based LT50 values (e.g., − 6.0 °C in NA; − 6.0 °C and − 8.0 °C in CA30d), while tissues exposed to colder temperatures (− 10.0 °C and − 12.0 °C) exhibited further sugar decline (Fig. 2A). This indicates that sugar re-accumulation—likely driven by resumed photosynthetic activity—occurs only within a narrow recovery window defined by both LT50 metrics. In contrast, C. quitensis exhibited sugar recovery at temperatures that would be, conventionally, deemed lethal by EL-based LT50 but remained within the sub-lethal range defined by Fv/Fm-based LT50. For example, NA tissues showed increased sugar levels at − 8.0 °C (a temperature colder than EL-based LT50 of − 7.5 °C but warmer than Fv/Fm-based LT50 of − 8.4 °C), and CA30d tissues accumulated sugars even at − 10 °C (a temperature colder than EL-based LT50 of − 8.6 °C but warmer than Fv/Fm-based LT50 of − 13.7 °C) (Fig. 2B). These results support the notion that Fv/Fm-based LT50 more robustly reflects the threshold for functional recovery in C. quitensis, and that this species possess greater capacity to restore chloroplast activity following freeze–thaw stress. These findings underscore the importance of chloroplast-targeted acclimation in shaping species-specific freezing resilience and may offer broader insights into plant survival strategies under extreme environment.

Conclusions

This study highlights species-specific strategies of FTST in two Antarctic vascular plants under a standardized, controlled-ice-nucleation protocol. D. antarctica exhibited coordinated acclimation of both membrane and chloroplast function, as evidenced by consistent shifts in LT50 values based on EL and Fv/Fm measurements. In contrast, C. quitensis showed a chloroplast-centered response, with a markedly greater enhancement in Fv/Fm-based LT50 and broader PTR capacity, despite minimal changes in membrane stability. The reliability of Fv/Fm as an FTST indicator in C. quitensis was supported by our observation that Fv/Fm-derived LT50 values consistently reflected actual recovery thresholds, whereas EL-based estimates underestimated survival capacity. The hypothesis that C. quitensis acquires freeze–thaw resilience through preferentially invoking functional preservation of the photosynthetic apparatus offers a mechanistic insight into its unique acclimation strategy under extreme Antarctic conditions.
