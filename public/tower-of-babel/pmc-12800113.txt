The stress–strain analysis of the entire construction process of underground diaphragm wall based on self-sensing FRP bar monitoring
Authors: Jian Li, Qilong Shi, Shanchang Xu, Kangxin Sun, Yue Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The diaphragm wall, as a key supporting structure in bridge anchorage projects, faces complex and variable stress conditions during construction. Therefore, ensuring structural safety and performance monitoring is crucial. Traditional rebar monitoring methods, due to poor corrosion resistance, insufficient real-time capabilities, and maintenance difficulties, cannot meet the high precision and reliability requirements of modern underground engineering. This study applies self-sensing technology to fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) materials by embedding optical fiber sensors within the FRP, enhancing real-time monitoring of stress and strain during the construction of diaphragm walls. This technology has been successfully implemented in the Shiziyang Bridge project, enabling real-time monitoring of stress and strain in the anchor diaphragm wall. The study adopts a quasi-distributed optical fiber monitoring scheme, combined with wireless transmission and a cloud platform for remote data acquisition and analysis. The results indicate that the self-sensing FRP bar shows excellent stress and strain monitoring capabilities at various stages of diaphragm wall construction. In stages 1–3, the stress curve transitions from tensile stress to alternating tensile and compressive stress. The shallow and mid-layers exhibit tensile stress, while the deep layers experience compressive stress. The maximum tensile stress recorded is 35.8 MPa, and the maximum compressive stress is -20.3 MPa, mainly due to pressure imbalance caused by soil excavation and the decreasing groundwater level. In stage 4, the upper stress gradually decreases, while the lower stress transitions from tensile to compressive, with the maximum tensile stress at 13.9 MPa and the maximum compressive stress at − 37.7 MPa. These changes are attributed to the completion of the liner and bottom slab construction, backfilling of the soil, and the increased self-weight of the upper structure. In stage 5, as construction progresses, the stress curve forms an M-shape, with compressive stress gradually decreasing. The maximum tensile stress is 2.3 MPa, and the maximum compressive stress is − 11.8 MPa, mainly influenced by the increasing tensile force applied by the stay cables. As of November 14, 2024, the monitoring data show that the tensile strain in the shallow layers remains unchanged, while the compressive strain in the middle and deep layers is gradually decreasing.

Introduction

Underground diaphragm walls have been extensively employed as essential retaining and waterproofing structures in deep excavations, such as bridge anchorages, metro stations, and large underground commercial complexes, owing to their outstanding impermeability, structural integrity, and adaptability to complex construction conditions1–3. However, during practical construction, the diaphragm walls frequently experience intricate stress variations induced by alternating processes such as excavation, bracing installation, and backfilling, resulting in complex stress–strain interactions between the wall structures and surrounding soil4–6. These interactions exhibit pronounced nonlinear and time-dependent characteristics, posing significant challenges for structural safety evaluation and construction management7.

Currently, the monitoring techniques for diaphragm walls primarily include point-based sensing devices (e.g., inclinometers, strain gauges, accelerometers) and discretely distributed fiber-optic sensors8–11. Nevertheless, these conventional methods commonly suffer from limitations such as poor real-time responsiveness, inadequate coverage in deep layers, restricted signal stability, and weak resistance to external interference12–17. Particularly, these methods struggle to achieve continuous, full-scale, and all-weather monitoring of the dynamic evolution of stress–strain states in the deep portions of walls interacting with heterogeneous soil, significantly impeding effective structural diagnosis and proactive risk-warning mechanisms18.

In recent years, advances in intelligent sensing and self-sensing materials have provided innovative solutions for addressing these limitations. Among these, Fiber-Reinforced Polymer (FRP) composites, characterized by excellent corrosion resistance, high strength-to-weight ratios, and superior compatibility with concrete, have emerged as ideal substrates for embedded sensing systems19–21. Embedded with miniature sensing units, self-sensing FRP bars enable real-time monitoring of multiple physical parameters, including stress, strain, and temperature, without compromising structural load-bearing performance22–24. These FRP-based sensing systems offer distributed monitoring capabilities, automated data acquisition, and remote transmission features, providing a promising alternative for comprehensive diaphragm wall monitoring25–30.

Compared to traditional surface-attached or shallowly embedded sensors, anchor-type FRP sensing bars provide continuous, in-depth monitoring across various depths within the diaphragm wall, effectively minimizing external disturbances and significantly improving monitoring accuracy and system reliability25,31. Additionally, the dual functionality of FRP bars in load-bearing and sensing eliminates the need for extra structural modifications, simplifying the installation process and enhancing practical field adaptability32–34.

For monitoring stress and strain along anchors or bolts, fiber Bragg grating (FBG) arrays or quasi-distributed FBG systems are preferable when sub-meter spatial resolution is required, whereas Brillouin scattering–based distributed fiber-optic sensing (DFOS) is more suitable for long-term monitoring where meter-level resolution suffices35. Embedded FBGs, introduced during pultrusion or consolidation, can maintain the structural integrity of FRP anchors while providing stable and reliable stress measurements. Although surface-mounted or grooved FBGs are feasible, the groove geometry and adhesive selection must be carefully optimized to avoid strength degradation36. In FRP anchors, the pull-out behavior is primarily governed by interfacial shear at the FRP–grout or grout–soil interface rather than by tensile rupture of the bar, and a critical bond length exists beyond which additional bonding offers negligible improvement in load capacity37. The interpretation of strain profiles should account for local effects—particularly near anchor plates or exposed ends—since they may distort axial force readings under low loads. Experimental studies have emphasized the importance of grout quality, bar geometry, and interfacial shear properties in defining the minimum bond length, while current design guidelines remain limited and often rely on pull-out testing and field monitoring data to validate anchorage performance38.

Considering the existing challenges in diaphragm wall monitoring, particularly the inadequate capture of deep structural responses, data acquisition delays, and ineffective early-warning mechanisms, there is an urgent need to develop intelligent monitoring systems featuring high spatial coverage, robust environmental adaptability, and precise sensing capabilities39.

Addressing these needs, this study proposes and develops an integrated monitoring system based on self-sensing FRP bars for capturing the full-process stress–strain evolution of diaphragm walls during bridge anchorage construction. This system, combining embedded smart FRP bars with anchor-type distributed deployment strategies, aims to realize high-precision, distributed, and continuous monitoring across all construction phases. This comprehensive monitoring provides reliable foundational data for structural health assessment and safety early-warning systems.

Performance testing of self-sensing FRP bar

The self-sensing FRP bars used in this study were made of GFRP (Glass Fiber Reinforced Polymer), and the tensile properties of the Self-sensing FRP bars were tested. The tensile mechanical property tests were conducted using a 1000 kN universal testing machine. During the test, a displacement gauge was placed on the free segment of the Self-sensing FRP bars to measure the deformation of the specimen under loading. The loading rate was set at 6 mm/min. Axial displacement and strain measured by the extensometer were transmitted to a computer through a data acquisition system for recording. The test results indicated that the tensile strength of Self-sensing FRP bars was 798.7 MPa, the elastic modulus was 50.5 GPa, and the ultimate strain was 1.8%. Corrosion resistance and durability testing of self-sensing FRP bar

To accurately simulate the corrosion environment experienced by GFRP bars embedded in concrete, accelerated durability tests were performed following the recommendations of the ACI 440.3R-04 standard. This guideline specifies a standardized procedure for evaluating the long-term durability of FRP materials in concrete environments by immersing specimens in an alkaline solution maintained at 60°C, which effectively replicates the highly alkaline pore solution of concrete. According to the studies conducted by Dejke V and Porter M L et al., immersion tests at a temperature of (60 ± 1) °C with durations of 3.65, 18, 36.5, 92, and 183 days correspond approximately to natural exposure periods of 1, 5, 10, 25, and 50 years, respectively40,41. This accelerated approach enables the evaluation of material degradation and performance retention within a shortened experimental timeframe, providing an effective means to assess the long-term corrosion resistance and durability of GFRP bars under simulated concrete environments.

GFRP exhibits excellent corrosion resistance and durability because it is composed of chemically inert polymer resins and chemically resistant glass fibers. The two components form a dense interfacial structure that effectively prevents the ingress of water, ions, and other corrosive agents. In addition, GFRP contains no metallic constituents and is thus immune to electrochemical corrosion, enabling it to maintain stable mechanical performance over long periods even under harsh conditions such as acidic, alkaline, saline, or marine environments.

The self-sensing FRP Bar fundamentally addresses the drawbacks of traditional externally attached sensors, such as low monitoring accuracy, high cost, susceptibility to damage, and poor durability. In addition to providing reinforcement for linings, it enables long-term monitoring of the stress state of the linings. By achieving complete coupling between the optical fiber and the monitored object, the self-sensing FRP Bar fully inherits the excellent sensing properties of optical fiber sensors, significantly enhancing the accuracy and durability of sensor performance. Furthermore, the self-sensing FRP Bar can be installed directly into the reinforcement mesh along with steel bars during construction, eliminating the need for additional construction steps. Compared to traditional monitoring methods that require secondary manual intervention, it greatly reduces labor and time costs. Design, installation, and application of the intelligent monitoring system for diaphragm walls

Design and installation of the intelligent monitoring system for diaphragm walls

A quasi-distributed optical fiber measurement scheme is adopted, in which multiple fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensing points are distributed along a single transmission line according to design requirements, forming a multi-point self-sensing FRP Bar with embedded quasi-distributed FBG arrays. By inscribing seven FBGs at equal intervals on a single optical fiber, localized distributed monitoring can be achieved, as shown in Fig. 9. High-precision monitoring at different depths of the diaphragm wall enables an accurate assessment of the overall stress state of the structure.Fig. 9Distribution of grating measurement points.

Distribution of grating measurement points.

To prevent slippage caused by insufficient bonding strength between the self-sensing FRP Bar and the concrete, the reinforcement is secured to the inner side of the diaphragm wall’s reinforcement cage using clamping straps. After binding, the reinforcement cage is hoisted into position. Two 40-m-long self-sensing FRP Bars are installed in each panel of the diaphragm wall reinforcement cage, as illustrated in Fig. 10.Fig. 10On-site installation of self-sensing FRP bar.

On-site installation of self-sensing FRP bar.

Temperature variations can affect the data from fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors, which may lead to inaccurate measurements during diaphragm wall monitoring. Therefore, it is necessary to eliminate the influence of temperature effects. Before performing effective measurements, the system must remain static for a period of time until the temperature of the diaphragm wall and the self-sensing FRP Bar stabilizes. The measurements taken at this stage represent the true values, free from temperature interference.

After determining the monitoring locations, the self-sensing FRP Bar, FBG demodulator, and cloud-based data acquisition system are sequentially installed to enable remote collection and analysis of monitoring data for the diaphragm wall. Considering that the diaphragm wall is a concealed underground structure, wireless transmission equipment is placed outside the anchorage for convenient data transmission. Application of intelligent monitoring

The Shiziyang Cross-River Passage is located in the core area of the Pearl River Estuary, connecting the eastern and western regions of Guangdong Province, with a total length of approximately 34.9 km. Its anchorage foundation adopts a circular diaphragm wall structure, featuring a wall thickness of 1.5 m, an outer diameter of 130 m, a foundation depth of 36 m, a top elevation of + 4.0 m, and a bottom elevation of − 32 m. The foundation embeds 8 m into the rock layer, with moderately weathered argillaceous sandstone serving as the bearing stratum. The anchorage dimensions are 110.6 m in length, 95.5 m in width, and 44.2 m in height. Given that the construction site is a coastal water-rich area and the anchorage is below the groundwater table, monitoring the deformation and stress changes of the diaphragm wall during anchorage excavation is critically important.

At the CX7 location of the diaphragm wall, one self-sensing FRP Bar was installed. Along its depth, the reinforcement was equipped with seven fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensing points spaced 6–7 m apart, forming an FBG array spanning 40 m, as shown in Fig. 7. This setup allows for high-precision monitoring of the diaphragm wall in this region. After the self-sensing FRP Bar was installed, it was secured to the reinforcement cage using clamping straps to prevent detachment during concrete pouring. This also ensures better cooperative deformation among the self-sensing FRP Bar, concrete, and steel reinforcement, as illustrated in Fig. 12. To enable subsequent monitoring, the optical fiber jumper of the self-sensing FRP Bar should be led out before concrete pouring and protected using PVC tubing to avoid fiber damage during later construction, as shown in Fig. 14.Fig. 14Optical fiber jumper protection device for self-sensing FRP bar.

Stress analysis of stages 1–5

Stage 1

The excavation of the diaphragm wall from the 1st to the 2nd layer is defined as the first construction stage. As the construction progresses, especially with the layered excavation of soil, the stress experienced by the diaphragm wall changes significantly. In Stage 1, the wall primarily experiences tensile stress. The reason for this is that, during the initial construction phase, the shallow soil on the inner side of the wall is extensively removed, while the outer side of the wall is subjected to substantial lateral pressure from the surrounding soil. Since less soil is excavated on the inner side, the pressure imbalance between the inner and outer sides of the wall is minimal, and the wall predominantly bears lateral pressure from the outer soil, leading to tensile stress in the self-sensing FRP Bar on the inner side. At this stage, due to the relatively shallow excavation, the wall deformation is small, and the tensile stress is not significant. As shown in Fig. 20, the stress curve during the excavation of the 2nd layer indicates that the stress range of the self-sensing FRP Bar in this stage is approximately 2.7–7.2 MPa.Fig. 20Stress curve during excavation of the 2nd layer in stage 1.

Stress curve during excavation of the 2nd layer in stage 1.

Stage 2

The excavation of the diaphragm wall from the 3rd to the 9th layer is defined as the second construction stage. During this stage, the stress state of the wall is complex, with the outer side primarily experiencing compressive stress and the inner side potentially experiencing tensile stress. The stress curves of the FRP Bar exhibit an alternation of tensile and compressive stresses. This complexity is attributed to several factors: First, as deeper soil layers are excavated, the outer side of the wall continues to experience significant lateral pressure from the remaining soil, while the inner side undergoes a significant reduction in pressure due to soil removal, resulting in a pronounced imbalance between inner and outer pressures. Second, with the hardening of the lining concrete in the upper part of the wall, the diaphragm wall and the lining form an integrated structure, increasing the overall stiffness, self-weight, and resistance to deformation. Third, during the construction process, the groundwater level is maintained at least 1 m below the excavation layer, leading to an increase in the effective stress in the soil on the outer side of the wall, which exerts greater lateral pressure on the wall. Lastly, before the lining concrete reaches the designed strength, support structures are used to ensure the flatness of the concrete within the formwork, which imposes additional pressure on both the formwork and the diaphragm wall. As a result of these combined factors, the stress curves in Stage 2 exhibit a distinct alternation of tensile and compressive stresses. According to the monitoring data from the self-sensing FRP Bar, the maximum compressive stress during the excavation of the 9th layer was recorded as 15 MPa, while the maximum tensile stress during the excavation of the 8th layer reached 35.8 MPa. Limitations and prospects

In this study, although the self-sensing FRP bars demonstrated excellent stress–strain monitoring capabilities during the construction of underground diaphragm walls, several limitations remain. First, the installation and placement of self-sensing FRP bars require high precision in construction; improper installation could affect the accuracy of the monitoring data. Second, the impact of temperature fluctuations on the fiber optic sensors requires further investigation to mitigate potential interference with data collection. Furthermore, the current monitoring system relies on fiber optic transmission and wireless communication technologies, which may face limitations in extreme environmental conditions, potentially leading to signal interference or equipment failure.

With the continuous advancement of related technologies, the application of self-sensing FRP bars is expected to further enhance monitoring accuracy and reliability. Future research could focus on optimizing the manufacturing processes of self-sensing FRP bars to improve their adaptability in complex construction environments, as well as exploring methods to reduce the cost and complexity of monitoring systems. Additionally, integrating big data and artificial intelligence technologies could enable more intelligent and automated monitoring systems, which would not only provide more real-time and precise stress state monitoring but also contribute to enhancing engineering safety and optimizing the construction process.
