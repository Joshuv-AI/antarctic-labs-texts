Effects of long-acting versus short-acting granulocyte colony stimulating factor after radiotherapy in gynecologic malignancies: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Xiaojing Chen, Ming Wu, Shuiqing Ma, Xianjie Tan, Sen Zhong, Lei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Little is known about the role of the protective effects of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) in patients after radiotherapy. The aim of the present study was to explore the prophylactic effects of long-acting granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) on febrile neutropenia (FN) and myelosuppression in chemotherapy patients with gynecologic malignancies after pelvic radiotherapy. Patients voluntarily participated in a study group (long-acting G-CSF for all chemotherapy cycles) and a control group (short-acting G-CSF) after they were educated about G-CSF utilization. The incidences of FN and myelosuppression, as well as adverse events, were compared between the two groups. A regression model was used to determine the risk factors for FN and myelosuppression. From January 6, 2019, to August 22, 2019, 61 patients were included in the final analysis, with 286 chemotherapy cycles. There were 14 (23.0%) and 57 (77.0%) patients in the study and control groups, respectively. The study group had significantly fewer complete blood count tests, fewer outpatient clinic visits, fewer short-acting G-CSF doses, and lower incidences of FN and myelosuppression per chemotherapy cycle. According to the binary regression model, the use of long-acting G-CSF was the only factor associated with a decreased incidence of myelosuppression but not FN. The major adverse event related to G-CSF was mild bone pain. In conclusion, long-acting G-CSF may effectively reduce the incidence of FN and myelosuppression with mild adverse effects during chemotherapy after radiotherapy. Introduction

Pegfilgrastim, a long-acting granulocyte colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), has received marketing authorization approval in many countries to reduce the incidence of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia on the basis of its efficacy, safety, and relative convenience [1–5]. Meta-analyses [6–8] and real-world studies [9] have confirmed the success (> 50%) of primary prophylaxis for febrile neutropenia (FN) produced by long-acting G-CSF. Therefore, many guidelines recommend long-acting G-CSF as a first-line treatment for severe myelosuppression and/or FN [10–13]. According to these guidelines, a previous history of radiotherapy is an important risk factor for FN. However, prevention of FN and severe myelosuppression are infrequently evaluated in gynecologic cancer patients receiving radiotherapy. In a phase II study, grade 3/4 hematologic toxicity occurred in 67% of patients with advanced or recurrent cervical cancer treated with irinotecan plus nedaplatin with recombinant human G-CSF support [14]. Another study has reported that grade 3/4 neutropenia occurs in 40% of cycles of carboplatin-based neoadjuvant treatment with recombinant human G-CSF support in locally advanced cervical cancer patients with bulky metastatic lymph nodes [15]. However, these previous studies did not provide more information about the prophylactic effects of FN in patients with common chemotherapy regimens after radiotherapy because of their study designs and limited sample sizes.

Our previous random controlled trials revealed that long-acting G-CSF reduces the incidence of FN and myelosuppression in newly diagnosed patients with epithelial ovarian cancer receiving chemotherapy [16]. The present prospective case‒control study evaluated the prophylactic effects of pegylated recombinant human G-CSF on FN and myelosuppression in chemotherapy patients with gynecologic malignancies after radiotherapy.

Methods

Study design and ethical issues

This was a prospective case‒controlled study in a single teaching hospital. All eligible patients voluntarily participated in the study group (long-acting G-CSF for all chemotherapy cycles) or control group (short-acting G-CSF for all chemotherapy cycles) after patient education. To guarantee the consistency of the prophylactic effects of G-CSF, patients were asked to adhere to the G-CSF regimens at the first selection. If a patient deviated from the primary G-CSF regimens by abandoning long-acting G-CSF in the study group or selecting long-acting G-CSF in the control group, the patient was excluded from the study.

The primary outcome was the incidence of grade 3/4 myelosuppression per chemotherapy cycle in each group. The following secondary outcomes were evaluated: (1) incidence of FN and myelosuppression of all grades; (2) clinical visits to the emergency room (ER), outpatient clinics, and all visits; and (3) adverse events associated with G-CSF. The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the study center (No. JS-1723) and was registered at clinicaltrials.gov on 04/01/2019 (No. NCT03793205). All patients provided informed consent before participation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Patient enrollment and sample size

All chemotherapy patients with gynecologic malignancies after radiotherapy at the study center from January 6, 2019, to August 22, 2019, were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) definite pathologic results of vulva, vaginal, cervical, or endometrial carcinoma; (2) definite radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy history for the same gynecologic disease; (3) platinum-based chemotherapy of 3 weeks per cycle for the same gynecologic disease; (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0–1; (5) age 18 years or older; (6) regular follow-up at the study center; (7) history of radiotherapy, without other high-risk factors for FN defined by the guidelines of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network; [11] (8) consent for participation; and (9) all the chemotherapy cycles fulfilled according to the schedule. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) failure to meet al.l the inclusion criteria; (2) noncompliance with the study protocols; (3) immunosuppressive diseases, such as organ transplantation or acquired immune deficiency syndrome; (4) hematologic disorders; (5) deviation from the primary selection of G-CSF regimens in the control group; or (6) received prior chemotherapy for previous cancer history.

Given that few data concerning the FN rates in chemotherapy patients after pelvic radiotherapy exist, the sample size was estimated by the incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia according to the report of Ferrandina et al. [15] Among locally advanced cervical cancer patients with bulky metastatic lymph nodes who received carboplatin-based neoadjuvant treatment with recombinant human G-CSF support, 40% experienced grade 3/4 neutropenia [15, 17]. Assuming that long-acting G-CSF would decrease the incidence to 20%, at least 82 chemotherapy cycles were required in the study and control groups to meet the criteria of a class I probability (bilateral α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.80, with the Z-pooled approximate normal distribution method.

Interventions

All patients underwent a platinum-based chemotherapy regimen of paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) and carboplatin (AUC = 5) or cisplatin (60 mg/m2, maximum dose of 100 mg) for 3 weeks per cycle according to the physician’s protocol. Dose reduction and the planned duration of therapy were discussed between patients and their physicians on the basis of severe treatment events and compromised quality of life. Patients were not excluded from the analysis if they did not complete the planned duration of therapy. Bevacizumab, a humanized vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-neutralizing monoclonal antibody, was used during treatment. For all patients, a complete blood count (CBC) was suggested to be checked at a frequency of twice per week. At least one visit to the outpatient clinic per chemotherapy cycle was mandatory for all participants to guarantee appropriate follow-up. Visits to the emergency department for myelosuppression were recorded.

An introduction and a booklet about the utilization, cost, and adverse effects of long- and short-acting G-CSF were provided to all patients before enrollment. Patients volunteered to accept long- or short-acting G-CSF. No matching was attempted between the two groups because of the limited study population. Patients in both groups had the right to drop out voluntarily or in accordance with the instructions from the physician, but the patients who dropped out of either group were not included in the analysis. For the study group, 6 mg of pegylated recombinant human G-CSF (PEG-rhG-CSF, Qilu Pharmaceutical®) was given to the patients 48 h after chemotherapy and was used for all cycles. Short-acting G-CSF can be utilized as a remedial treatment for the treatment of grade 3/4 myelosuppression. For the control group, prophylactic or therapeutic short-acting G-CSF was used for all cycles at the physician’s discretion. The prophylactic utilization of short-acting G-CSF followed the consensus guidance recommendations for long-acting G-CSF in 2017 [18].

Measures

A structured case report form was used to collect the epidemiologic and clinical information of each patient via an electronic case management system and self-reports by the patients. The data of all the CBC tests, visits to the ER and/or outpatient clinics, and adverse events of the G-CSF treatment were extracted by the electronic case management system, and the reports were collected from the patient if the tests were not registered in the system. Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v4.03 [19]. FN was defined as an oral temperature of > 38.3 °C, two consecutive readings of > 38.0 °C for 2 h and an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 0.5 × 109/L, or an ANC below 0.5 × 109/L. A visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 to 10 was used to evaluate the degree of bone pain associated with G-CSF. The history of radiotherapy and its regimen were collected from case reports. The involvement of the para-aortic lymphatic region in radiotherapy was evaluated. The history of radiotherapy was described as the duration from the end of radiotherapy to the beginning of chemotherapy being more than 1 year or less than 1 year.

Statistics

A comparison of the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of patients between the study and control groups was conducted. A comparison of the incidence of FN and myelosuppression between the study and control groups was conducted for each chemotherapy cycle according to the protocol design. Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted with parametric methods if assumptions of normal distribution were confirmed. A binary regression model was used to estimate the hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the effects of long-acting G-CSF on FN and myelosuppression, and it was adjusted for several factors, such as age, body mass index, ECOG performance status, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, history of radiotherapy, radiotherapy plans, complications, chemotherapy regimens (especially cisplatin versus carboplatin), chemotherapy cycles, and bevacizumab use. Nonnormally distributed variables and categorical data were compared via nonparametric tests. Unless otherwise stated, all analyses were performed with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and conducted with SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

The patient selection procedure is shown in a flowchart in Fig. 1. From January 6, 2019, to August 22, 2019, a total of 92 patients were admitted for chemotherapy with a history of pelvic radiotherapy, and 61 patients were included in the final analysis. There were 14 (23.0%) and 47 (77.0%) patients in the study and control groups, with 79 and 207 chemotherapy cycles, respectively. The patient characteristics are listed in Table 1, and detailed data are provided in the Supplemental Materials. Among the 61 patients, 3 (4.9%), 6 (9.8%), and 52 patients (85.2%) were diagnosed with vulvar, endometrial, and cervical cancer, respectively. Moreover, 35 (57.4%) and 26 (42%) patients had primary and recurrent disease, respectively, and 16 (26.2%) and 48 (78.7%) patients had a history of pelvic radiotherapy of more than 1 year and less than 1 year, respectively. Three patients had repeated pelvic radiotherapy, and five patients had a history of surgery, including one radical excision of the vulva, two laparoscopic hysterectomies, and two laparoscopic radical hysterectomies. All 61 patients received pelvic external-beam radiotherapy (ERBT), 18 (29.5%) patients received radiotherapy beyond the pelvic cavity, and 37 (60.7%) patients received brachytherapy. Concurrent weekly cisplatin chemotherapy was utilized in 43 (70.5%) patients. The median interval from the end of radiotherapy to the beginning of chemotherapy was 1.5 months (range of 1–60 months). No patient discontinued chemotherapy because of myelosuppression. On the basis of their chemotherapy regimens and previous history of pelvic radiotherapy, all patients had an FN risk > 20% and needed prophylactic medication with G-CSF according to current guidelines [11].

In total, 286 chemotherapy cycles were performed, with a median of 5 cycles (range of 2–9 cycles). Paclitaxel/carboplatin and paclitaxel/cisplatin were used in 48 (16.8%) and 238 (83.2%) cycles, respectively, and bevacizumab was used in 135 cycles (47.2%).

The study and control groups were well balanced with respect to most of the epidemiologic and clinical characteristics (Table 1). However, the study group had a higher mean of chemotherapy cycles (6.0 ± 1.5 versus 4.4 ± 1.4, p < 0.001) and fewer patients receiving brachytherapy (35.7% versus 27.7, p = 0.030) than the control group. Moreover, patients in the study and control groups had similar total ERBT doses and fractions.

Incidences and risk factors of FN and myelosuppression

The incidences of FN and myelosuppression per chemotherapy cycle in the two groups are listed in Table 2. In total, 15 patients (all in the control group) experienced 16 episodes of FN. Compared with patients in the control group, those in the study group had significantly fewer incidences of FN (0 [0/79] vs. 7.7% [16/207], p = 0.008), all grades of myelosuppression (31.6% [25/79] vs. 54.6% [113/207], p = 0.001), grade 3/4 myelosuppression (13.9% [11/79] vs. 29.0% [60/207], p = 0.008), and grade 4 myelosuppression (3.8% [3/79] vs. 13.0% [27/207], p = 0.023) per cycle.

According to the univariate analysis, only the use of long-acting G-CSF (p = 0.008) and age (p = 0.046) were risk factors for FN. Specifically, a history of radiotherapy (more than or less than 1 year) had no significant effect on the incidence of FN, total grade, or severe myelosuppression per cycle in either the study group or the control group (all p values > 0.05). For the control group, the number of chemotherapy cycles (within 3 cycles versus beyond 3 cycles) had no effect on the incidence of FN (11/140 [7.9%] versus 5/67 [7.5%], p = 0.921).

After adjusting for the abovementioned risk factors, a binary regression model revealed that increased age was the only independent risk factor for FN (odds ratio [OR] 1.104, 95% confidence interval [95% CI] 1.012–1.204, p = 0.026). According to the regression model, the use of short-acting G-CSF was the only independent factor associated with all grades of myelosuppression (OR 2.987, 95% CI 1.650–5.407, p < 0.001), grade 3/4 myelosuppression (OR 2.757, 95% CI 1.315–5.779, p = 0.007), and grade 4 myelosuppression (OR 4.409, 95% CI 1.133–14.466, p = 0.031).

Short-acting G-CSF utilization and clinical visits

In the study group, 15/79 cycles (19.0%) utilized short-acting G-CSF as a remedial treatment for myelosuppression, and the mean dose of short-acting G-CSF was 1.8 ± 1.2 per cycle. In the control group, 86/207 cycles (41.5%) utilized short-acting G-CSF for myelosuppression, and the mean dose of short-acting G-CSF was 3.5 ± 3.1 per cycle. The study group utilized significantly fewer doses of short-acting G-CSF (p = 0.035, Table 2).

The patients in the study group had fewer visits to the ER (p = 0.060), significantly fewer visits to outpatient clinics (p = 0.009), significantly fewer all visits (p = 0.008), and significantly fewer CBC tests (p = 0.046, Table 2).

Adverse events of G-CSF

Long-acting G-CSF lead to less pain associated with G-CSF (Table 2). However, the VAS score was not significantly different, with a mean VAS score of less than 2 in both groups. The incidence of skin rash, which was present only in grade 1 myelosuppression, was similar in both groups. No other adverse events associated with G-CSF were reported. No mortality occurred for any reason.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study on the prophylactic effects of long-acting G-CSF in chemotherapy patients with a history of pelvic radiotherapy. Radiotherapy can cause significant leukopenia in cervical cancer patients [20], which persists even after long-term follow-up [21]. However, the severe myelosuppression rate in the present study was lower than the previously reported 40% in neoadjuvant chemotherapy patients with locally advanced cervical cancer [15]. The main reason is likely the strict inclusion criteria, which guarantee the good performance status of the participants. The degree of grade 3/4 myelosuppression the present study was similar to that reported in an NRG Oncology/Gynecologic Oncology Group study for the treatment of persistent or recurrent carcinoma of the uterine cervix [22]. The incidence of FN was similar to that reported in a study on ovarian and breast cancer by Balducci et al. [17] In the present study population, the timing of radiotherapy had no significant effect on the incidence of FN or myelosuppression, suggesting that the same dose of long-acting G-CSF is suitable in such situations. Although long-acting G-CSF was not a protective factor against FN in the regression model, the use of G-CSF at the physician’s discretion led to inferior outcomes of myelosuppression in the univariate and multivariate analyses. However, use of prophylactic short-acting G-CSF according to a stringent protocol may not result in inferior outcomes, which has been validated in a systematic review and meta-analysis [7]. On the basis of the present findings, an active comparison of prophylactic long- and short-acting G-CSF for the prevention of FN and/or severe myelosuppression is needed to clarify the role of various categories in gynecological cancer patients after radiotherapy.

The study and control groups included 14 and 47 patients, respectively. The imbalance in patient number was due mainly to the study design. In the present study, if patients dropped out of either group, they were not included in the analysis to guarantee the consistency of the G-CSF regimens. However, owing to the greater expenses associated with long-acting G-CSF than with short-acting regimens, fewer patients selected the long-acting G-CSF regimen, even when they were educated about the potential cost-effectiveness of long-acting G-CSF. As each patient underwent several courses of chemotherapy, such arrangement may decrease selection bias in the final analysis.

Few studies have evaluated the prophylactic effects of G-CSF on myelosuppression associated with targeted therapy. In the present study, the addition of bevacizumab to platinum-based chemotherapy had no significant effect on the efficiency of G-CSF, which agreed with the findings of a previous study in patients with advanced colorectal cancer [23] and our previous experience in ovarian cancer treatment [16]. The administration of pegfilgrastim does not negatively affect the tumor response or survival of patients with colorectal cancer [23]. However, long-term follow-up is needed to verify the impact of long-acting G-CSF on the prognosis of patients in the present study. The higher number of chemotherapy cycles in the study group than in the control group suggested a nonnegative impact of long-acting G-CSF on treatment consistency.

The timing and dose of pegfilgrastim have been extensively explored. In the present study, 6 mg of long-acting G-CSF was prescribed for 48 h after chemotherapy, which was consistent throughout all the chemotherapy cycles. Such administration has been demonstrated to be reasonable [24] and is generally commended [18]. A prospective, multicenter study has demonstrated that pegfilgrastim has similar effects if it is administered on Day 4 or Day 2 with respect to grade 4 leucopenia, FN, or infections [25]. A previous multicenter study has revealed that the continued use of primary pegfilgrastim prophylaxis during all chemotherapy cycles has clinical relevance in patients with early breast cancer at high risk for FN [26], but it has a higher cost than prophylaxis limited to the first two cycles [27]. These findings may provide opportunities for new trials for the most cost-effective medication approaches.

The present study revealed no severe adverse events associated with long- or short-acting G-CSF. Similar to patients with breast cancer [28], bone pain was the most common symptom, and the VAS score was equal between the two groups in the present study. Although rare, long-acting G-CSF may be associated with interstitial pneumonia [29] or neutrophilic panniculitis and Sweet’s syndrome lesions [30].

Few reports have investigated the cost-effectiveness of long-acting G-CSF. In lymphoma and myeloma patients after high-dose chemotherapy and peripheral blood stem cell transplantation, the use of pegfilgrastim is not associated with increased cost [31]. A post hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial has suggested that although the total medical cost during chemotherapy increases, pegfilgrastim reduces the costs associated with both drugs and hospitalization for adverse events, including FN [32]. In the present study, although the utilization of PEG-rhG-CSF reduced the use of short-acting G-CSF, reduced short-acting G-CSF doses, decreased outpatient clinic visits, and decreased the number of CBC tests, the relevant economic significance needs further exploration.

The present study had several limitations. First, the design of a case‒control study and the limited sample size of the study group may cause bias for the generalization of the present study, even though the sample size achieved the estimated statistical power. In the present study, no matching factors were taken into consideration, the study group also used short-acting G-CSF as a remedial treatment; these factors may cause statistical bias. Second, the control group did not consistently utilize active prophylactic treatment, which may suppress the treatment effects in this population. Finally, the impact of G-CSF on survival outcomes was unclear, thereby requiring long-term follow-up.

Conclusions

PEG-rhG-CSF, a long-acting G-CSF, significantly reduces the incidences of FN, all grades of myelosuppression, and grade 3/4 myelosuppression in chemotherapy patients with gynecologic malignancies after pelvic radiotherapy. The adverse events, including bone pain and skin rash, were mild.
