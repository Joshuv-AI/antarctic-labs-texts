Food-associated sexual arousal in psychiatric disorders: A case series
Authors: Rajiv Mehta, Payal Tulsan, Anurag Sengar, Suyash Shendye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Food-associated sexual arousal, frequently termed sitophilia, is a rarely reported phenomenon with no established diagnostic validity or prevalence data.Cases:We describe it, in exploratory terms, as a secondary phenomenon across three psychiatric presentations. Three patients from a tertiary care psychiatry outpatient service were assessed through clinical interview and ICD-11 criteria. A 14-year-old girl with anorexia nervosa experienced arousal to food imagery that reinforced restriction; a 46-year-old man with depression developed arousal while cooking, as a compensatory pleasure; and a 24-year-old woman with obsessive–compulsive disorder felt arousal on achieving culinary perfection. In each, the arousal was concealed, was not the presenting complaint, and was linked to the underlying psychopathology.Conclusion:Food-associated sexual arousal may represent a context-dependent secondary phenomenon shaped by underlying psychiatric conditions rather than a distinct paraphilic disorder. Systematic research using structured assessments is needed.

INTRODUCTION

Food occupies a central place in survival, pleasure, and socio-cultural life. Within psychiatry, food-related preoccupations are well recognized across affective and eating disorders, yet the explicit sexualization of food-related stimuli has attracted little attention and remains poorly conceptualized.

Such atypical interests sit within the wider spectrum of paraphilias, which DSM-5 and ICD-11 define as recurrent, intense sexual interests directed at objects, situations, or individuals outside the normative range. Both classifications separate paraphilic interests from paraphilic disorders, reserving the latter for cases involving distress, impairment, or risk of harm.[123] Community surveys suggest such interests are more common than clinical impressions imply.[45] Sexual arousal tied to food, often labeled sitophilia, has been described only anecdotally and carries no established diagnostic validity, no recognized syndromic status, and no reliable prevalence estimate.

There are grounds for thinking the two domains can become linked. Food reward and sexual reward draw on overlapping mesolimbic dopaminergic circuitry, which makes conditioning between them at least plausible.[6] Cultural and Internet-mediated exposures may further facilitate such conditioning in predisposed individuals, though this remains speculative. Against this background, we describe three patients in whom food-associated sexual arousal arose as a secondary feature of differing psychiatric illnesses, aiming to draw clinical attention and invite systematic study rather than to advance a new diagnostic category.

CASE 1: FOOD-ASSOCIATED SEXUAL AROUSAL IN ANOREXIA NERVOSA

A 14-year-old girl presented with a 6-month history of restrictive eating, an intense fear of weight gain, body-image distortion, and secondary amenorrhea. Throughout this period, she exercised and walked for 3–4 hours daily, allowing herself only a single evening meal that she framed as a reward for the day’s effort. Her weight fell from a premorbid 85 kg to 50 kg, consistent with anorexia nervosa, restricting type.

Despite this severe restriction, she spent long prolonged hours watching online videos of elaborate cooking, food reviews, and people eating high-calorie meals. While watching, she reported excitement, bodily warmth, genital sensations, and sexual arousal, most strongly with slow-motion eating videos and dessert preparation. She described stimulating her genitals during these videos to the point of orgasm, after which she felt guilty and confused. Notably, the arousal carried no wish to eat; rather, it intensified her restrictive behavior.

She began with about an hour of viewing each night when alone. The arousal, at first occasional, grew more frequent, and over the 2 months before presentation, she masturbated two to three times a day, with viewing extending to 2 or 3 hours, sometimes in daytime when the house was empty. The experiences were privately gratifying and kept hidden from the family. The family history was notable: Her mother had obsessive-compulsive disorder and was taking fluoxetine, while her father showed obsessive-compulsive personality traits and high expressed emotions.

Phenomenologically, the arousal seemed woven into the restrictive psychopathology of anorexia nervosa, offering a vicarious gratification without calorie intake. It was distinguished from fetishistic disorder, attaching to visual food content rather than an inanimate object, and from compulsive sexual behavior disorder, being neither uncontrollable nor ego-dystonic.

CASE 2: FOOD-ASSOCIATED SEXUAL AROUSAL EMERGING IN DEPRESSIVE DISORDER

A 46-year-old married man, an IT professional working from home, had been married for 20 years and shared a house with his wife and two daughters. He described longstanding marital discord. The couple slept in separate rooms, sexual activity was minimal, and there had been no intimacy for 3–4 years, with his wife taking little part in running the household. He had been depressed for the past 2 years. To cope and to provide nutritious meals to his daughters, he took up cooking, watching recipe videos and browsing for dishes online.

Within a few weeks, he found himself taking intense pleasure in the smell of food as it cooked, the feel of ingredients in his hands, and the look of a dish coming together. He started searching for recipes compulsively late at night and noticed a progressive association between cooking-related sensory stimuli and sexual arousal. He subsequently linked his cooking sessions to masturbation, particularly after prolonged exposure to food aromas, and subscribed to multiple cooking channels to sustain the hobby.

He denied any similar interests prior to the depressive episode. The behavior functioned as a self-soothing, compensatory source of pleasure that temporarily alleviated his low mood. His masturbation increased from once daily to 3–4 times daily, and he began spending most of his free time cooking and masturbating. When this routine began to cause socio-occupational impairment, he presented to the outpatient clinic with complaints of depressive symptoms and increased masturbation. A diagnosis of moderate depressive disorder (ICD-11) was made.

The arousal appeared to emerge within and be sustained by the depressive episode rather than as an independent disorder. Its olfactory and activity-based specificity appeared to operate as a transient pleasure substitute for emotional deprivation and anhedonia.

CASE 3: FOOD-ASSOCIATED SEXUAL AROUSAL EMERGING IN OBSESSIVE-COMPULSIVE DISORDER

A 24-year-old unmarried woman working in hotel management presented with a 3-year history of obsessive-compulsive disorder of mixed type, with contamination and doubt-related obsessions followed by washing and checking compulsions.

She had been drawn to cooking for years, an interest that shaped her decision to train and work in hotel management. At first, it was simply neutral and professionally rewarding, with no sexual connotation. In time, though, her obsessive-compulsive traits began to shape her cooking behavior. She felt an intense internal urge to produce dishes that matched her own subjective standards of precision, which meant repeated adjustments and prolonged preparation.

When a dish finally met those standards, she experienced distinct sexual arousal, bodily warmth, and genital sensations, which gave way to masturbation and a sense of release. The arousal hinged specifically on the sense of having achieved perfection, not on the aroma of food, its consumption, or any deliberate fantasy, and she noticed nothing similar with other perfectionistic tasks. Because it was pleasurable, the experience reinforced her perfectionism, and she devoted even longer periods to each dish, with clear costs at work, late orders, falling efficiency, and friction with colleagues. She came to the outpatient clinic on account of these difficulties, and the co-occurring phenomenology was identified. A diagnosis of obsessive-compulsive disorder with co-occurring food-associated sexual arousal was made.

Unlike a classic compulsion, the arousal did not neutralize anxiety; it formed a reward-based reinforcement loop running alongside her compulsive symptoms. It was distinct from fetishistic disorder, being bound to the attainment of a cognitive standard rather than to food as an object.

Management and follow-up

All three patients received treatment directed primarily at the underlying psychiatric disorder, comprising appropriate diagnosis-specific pharmacotherapy together with nonpharmacological measures. Psychoeducation was provided to the patients and their family members, and the patients were counseled regarding the nature of their symptoms, including the food-associated sexual arousal, with attention to the associated guilt and distress. The patient with anorexia nervosa was lost to follow-up. The remaining two patients continued to attend the outpatient clinic, and in both, improvement in the underlying disorder was accompanied by a parallel reduction in the food-associated sexual arousal, which diminished as the primary illness remitted.

DISCUSSION

This exploratory case series describes food-associated sexual arousal as a secondary psychopathological phenomenon across three distinct psychiatric presentations, illustrating context-dependent sexualized responses shaped by the specific psychopathology of each underlying disorder.

In Case 1, vicarious arousal through food imagery in anorexia nervosa appeared to permit symbolic gratification without caloric intake, consistent with altered reward and control circuitry described in this disorder.[7] In Case 2, arousal emerged amid depressive anhedonia and emotional deprivation, functioning as a maladaptive hedonic substitute that escalated into impairment. In Case 3, arousal was integrated into a reward-based reinforcement loop within OCD, linked specifically to cognitive perfectionism.[8]

Food and sexual reward circuits may share overlapping mesolimbic dopaminergic substrates,[6] but direct neurobiological claims cannot be substantiated from case observations alone. Internet-mediated conditioning through food-content platforms may have contributed to Cases 1 and 2, though this remains speculative. Notably, none of the three patients disclosed these experiences spontaneously, underscoring the likelihood of underreporting unless sensitive sexual history-taking is routinely practiced. The attenuation of food-associated sexual arousal alongside improvement in the primary disorder, observed at follow-up, further supports its interpretation as a secondary, context-dependent phenomenon rather than an independent paraphilic disorder.

Limitations

With only three patients from a single tertiary center, the observations cannot be generalized, and their anecdotal character allows neither causal inference nor any estimate of prevalence. The sexual phenomena were characterized through clinical interview alone, without structured assessment tools. Restraint against overpathologizing is also warranted since atypical sexual interests are not in themselves disordered unless they cause distress or impairment.

Clinical Implications

Food-associated sexual arousal can surface as a secondary feature across a range of psychiatric disorders, shaped by underlying psychopathology. Clinicians should incorporate sensitive sexual history-taking into routine assessment to avoid misclassification. These observations require replication with structured tools and larger samples before treatment implications can be drawn.
