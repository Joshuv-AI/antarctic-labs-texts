Determinants of birth preparedness and complication readiness among postpartum women in Bugembe, Jinja City, Eastern Uganda: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Andrew Marvin Kanyike, Seungwon Lee, Samuel Kasibante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBirth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) is vital for reducing maternal deaths by promoting proactive planning for safe childbirth and medical emergency management. Despite its importance in improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes, the field is still under-explored in Uganda. This study investigates factors associated with BPCR among postpartum women in Eastern Uganda, where maternal mortality remains high.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Bugembe town council, Jinja City, Eastern Uganda, from August to September 2022. The study included 228 postpartum women who had given birth within the last 12 months. A multistage random sampling technique was used to select participants from five villages. Data were collected using a validated structured questionnaire. The primary outcome was BPCR, assessed through five key components: (1) identifying a health facility for delivery, (2) identifying a skilled birth attendant, (3) arranging transport, (4) setting aside funds, and (5) identifying a blood donor. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were performed to determine factors associated with good BPCR (well-prepared).ResultsThe median age of participants was 27 years (Interquartile Range [IQR]: 23–31). Most were married (82.4%) and completed up to secondary education (49.6%). Only 33.8% of women were deemed ‘well-prepared’ for birth and relevant potential complications. The most prioritized component of BPCR was saving funds for delivery (73.7%). A small proportion of women had good knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy (29.8%, 95% CI: 24.0% – 36.2%) and childbirth (10.1%, 95% CI: 6.5% – 14.8%). Good BPCR was associated with having secondary or tertiary education (aOR: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.01–4.65) and poor maternal health knowledge (aOR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.78).ConclusionThe study revealed low levels of BPCR and poor knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy and childbirth among post-partum women in Eastern Uganda. BPCR practices should be strengthened through targeted maternal health education and antenatal care interventions.

Background

Globally, preventable pregnancy-related causes contribute to 830 maternal deaths per day [1, 2]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is disproportionately affected, bearing the highest burden of maternal mortality, which is mostly from preventable causes [3]. Despite Uganda’s notable progress in reducing its maternal mortality ratio (MMR) from 336 per 100,000 live births in 2016 to 189 per 100,000 live births in 2022, it is still one of the highest MMRs globally and falls below the World Health Organization’s (WHO) target of less than 70 per 100,000 live births [4]. Such high rates of maternal mortality and morbidity are often linked to delays in seeking, reaching, and receiving adequate care, which could be addressed by recognizing danger signs and being prepared for obstetric complications [5, 6]. Therefore, birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) for pregnant women and their families is a major strategy to decrease maternal mortality rates [7].

BPCR is part of the internationally recognized safe motherhood initiatives that encourage pregnant women to plan for childbirth while foreseeing actions for emergencies [5]. It involves choosing a safe place for childbirth with skilled birth attendants for delivery care, organizing blood donors in case of a hemorrhage as many hospitals in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) don’t have readily equipped blood banks, setting aside funds for any childbirth-related costs, and arranging transport options to the health facility to reduce delays in obtaining appropriate obstetric care [5, 8]. A systematic review shows that BPCR interventions effectively reduced 28% of maternal and 18% of neonatal mortality in low-resource settings [9]. BPCR improves preventive behaviors, baseline knowledge of danger signs of mothers, and care-seeking during obstetric emergencies [3].

A key facilitator of BPCR in LMICs is antenatal care (ANC), where most BPCR services are routinely provided [10]. Other factors associated with BPCR include attaining secondary and tertiary education levels, young maternal age, giving birth at a health facility, and receiving help from community health workers [11–13]. However, the proportion of women who are ‘well-prepared’ for childbirth throughout their pregnancy is very low in LMICs, partly due to low ANC attendance rates [8]. For instance, less than a third of women were well-prepared for birth in facility-based studies from Rwanda (22.3%), Kenya (28%), and Uganda (28.3%) [11, 14, 15]. Furthermore, community-based studies in Southwestern and Eastern Uganda also showed low levels of knowledge of key danger signs of pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum periods among women who had recently delivered [16, 17].

Despite Uganda’s progress in reducing maternal mortality, the burden remains high. Low awareness of BPCR among women may contribute to high maternal mortality rates, but evidence regarding BPCR is limited in Uganda, particularly in the Eastern region. Few community-based studies have examined BPCR among postpartum women in this region, yet it has a high MMR of over 90 per 100,000 deliveries as of 2022 [4]. Understanding BPCR levels and predictors among women is important for identifying gaps and developing interventions to improve MMR in Uganda. Therefore, this study determined the levels of birth preparedness and complication readiness and associated factors among women who had delivered in the past year in Eastern Uganda.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted in Bugembe Town Council (BTC), Jinja City, in Eastern Uganda from August to September 2022. BTC is one of the three sub-counties that form the Northern division of Jinja city, and is situated along the Jinja-Iganga highway, approximately 5 km from Jinja town and 77 km northeast of Uganda’s capital city, Kampala. BTC comprises 15 villages with an estimated population of 45,600. BTC was chosen for its high population and convenient location, where Communities for Childbirth International (CCI), an international organization focused on MCH, has established a bottom-up, community-based model to improve MCH and train Village Health Teams (VHTs). The primary economic activity in BTC is small-scale trading, particularly in grocery stores.

Study population

We included postpartum women who had given birth within the last 12 months preceding the study and were willing to consent. We excluded those seriously ill/too sick to participate and those unwilling to participate voluntarily.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using the Kish-Leslie formula: Z^2 p(1-p)/ d^2 [18]. The prevalence of knowledge of MCH was estimated at 0.19 from a study that reported the prevalence of knowledge of 3 or more key obstetric danger signs during three periods (pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum) among 19% of women in rural Uganda [16]. Taking the standard deviation at 95% Confidence Interval (1.960) and the precision of the estimate of 5%, the calculated sample size was 237. We then used the modified Kish and Leslie equation to calculate the available sample size: N/1+(N-1)/K, where K is the estimated overall study population. In 2020, Uganda’s crude birth rate was 36.67 births per thousand population [19]. Bugembe had a population of 45,600 in 2020 [20]. The number of pregnant women would be approximately 3. 36.67 / 1000 × 45,600 = 1672.15. Therefore, K is 1672. The calculated sample size was 207. To cater for non-respondents, 10% of the sample size was added. The final sample size was 207 + 21 = 228 participants.

Sampling strategy

Multistage sampling was employed. At the parish level, Bugembe town council has 15 villages. The random selection of villages was conducted using the “fishbowl technique,” where the names of all 15 villages in Bugembe parish were written on separate pieces of paper and placed into a bowl. Then, five villages were randomly drawn to participate in the study. Women were identified from households within the selected villages and enrolled by trained VHTs who are community health workers in Uganda. Equal proportions of participants from the total sample were enrolled from each village. VHTs easily identified potential participants who met the eligibility criteria due to their deep connections with community members and years of experience working as the first point of contact for Uganda’s healthcare system [21].

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected using a validated questionnaire with questions about BPCR adopted from the monitoring BPCR tools for maternal and newborn health [5]. The English questionnaire was translated into Lusoga, the most commonly used local language. Trained VHTs collected the data using paper-based questionnaires, which were checked for completeness and entered into the KoboToolbox (KoBoToolbox, Harvard Humanitarian Initiative, Cambridge, MA, USA) using in-built data validation and quality checks, and a study coordinator reviewed them at the end of every data collection day.

Study variables

The main outcome variable was BPCR, which was assessed by asking the woman if she prepared or considered the following during their most recent pregnancy: (1) identifying a place of delivery (i.e. deciding where to deliver from), (2) identifying who would perform the delivery (i.e. knowing which healthcare staff will attend her birth), (3) setting aside funds for the delivery, (4) arranging transport fees to reach the health facility, and (5) identifying a blood donor in case of a hemorrhage. A woman was considered ‘well-prepared’ for birth and its potential complications if she satisfied at least three out of five key components of BPCR, ‘less prepared’ if she mentioned between one to three, and ‘not prepared’ if she mentioned none.

The secondary outcome variable was knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy, labor, and postpartum. Women were asked open-ended questions about danger signs they knew as the data collector tallied them against a list of all danger signs in the questionnaire. A woman was considered to have ‘good knowledge’ about danger signs if she spontaneously mentioned at least three danger signs during pregnancy, labor, and postpartum, ‘poor knowledge’ if she mentioned less than three, and ‘no knowledge’ if she mentioned none. This approach has been used in previously published literature [2, 8, 10].

Independent variables included maternal age, level of education, occupation, marital status, place of delivery, number of ANC visits, monthly household income, number of previous pregnancies, and religion.

Data analysis

The data from the KoboToolbox form were exported to Microsoft Excel 365 for data cleaning and coding. The cleaned data were then transferred to Stata Version 15 (StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC; 2017) for analysis. Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages, while continuous data was described using mean and standard deviations. A univariable logistic regression model was used to investigate the relationships between each independent variable and the well-prepared category of BPCR. Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the univariate model were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to assess their association with the dependent variable (well-prepared category of BPCR) while accounting for the confounding effects of the independent variables. Results were presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Sociodemographic characteristics

The study included 228 women with a median age of 27 years (IQR: 23–31). The majority were 20–29 years old (n = 132, 57.9%). Most participants had secondary education (n = 113, 49.6%), had informal employment (n = 85, 37.3%), were married (n = 188, 82.4%), and belonged to the Anglican religious denomination (n = 116, 50.9%). Obstetric characteristics

Most women delivered from a health facility (n = 218, 95.61%), with only a few delivering at home (n = 10, 4.39%). The mean number of previous pregnancies was 2.46 (SD = 1.39), with a majority having carried 2–4 pregnancies (n = 137, 60.1%). Most participants reported that their most recent pregnancy was planned (n = 171, 75.0%). ANC attendance for the most recent pregnancy was mostly between 4 and 7 visits (n = 142, 62.3%), with only 18.4% (n = 42) attending the recommended minimum of eight visits. The gestational age at ANC initiation was predominantly after 12 weeks (n = 135, 59.21%) – Table 1.

Knowledge of danger signs for pregnancy and newborns

Participants’ knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy, labor, childbirth, post-partum, and for newborns was generally poor. For danger signs during pregnancy, 19.3% (95% CI: 14.4% – 25.0%) had no knowledge, 50.9% (95% CI: 44.2% – 57.5%) had poor knowledge, and 29.8% (95% CI: 24.0% – 36.2%) had good knowledge. Participants performed worse when asked about danger signs during labor and childbirth, with the majority having poor knowledge (60.5%; 95% CI: 53.9% – 66.9%) and only 10.1% (95% CI: 6.5% – 14.8%) having good knowledge. Knowledge of danger signs after birth was also predominantly poor (53.1%; 95% CI: 46.4% – 59.7%). However, a difference was observed with knowledge of danger signs for newborns, with the majority of participants (48.8%; 95% CI: 41.7% – 55.8%) having good knowledge, as shown in Fig. 1.

Factors associated with BPCR among participants

Several factors were significantly associated with being well-prepared for BPCR. Women with secondary or tertiary education were more than twice as likely to be well-prepared as those with no formal or only primary education (aOR: 2.17, 95% CI: 1.01–4.65, p = 0.047). Knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy also had a significant impact on BPCR. Participants with poor knowledge were 73% less likely to be well-prepared than those with good knowledge (aOR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.78, p = 0.016).

Although other factors, such as marital status and gestational age at ANC initiation, showed significant crude odds ratios, their associations were not statistically significant after adjustment. For example, while married women initially appeared more likely to be well-prepared (cOR: 2.79, 95% CI: 1.17–6.65, p = 0.020), this association was not significant after adjustment (aOR: 2.09, 95% CI: 0.68–6.42, p = 0.195). Similarly, early ANC initiation (< 12 weeks) showed a significant crude association (cOR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.05–3.21, p = 0.031), but this was not significant in the adjusted model (aOR: 1.31, 95% CI: 0.58–2.93, p = 0.506) – see Table 3.

Discussion

We provide findings on BPCR levels and determinants among women of reproductive age in a community-based setting who had given birth in the year before the study.

About a third of the women (33.8%) were well-prepared for birth and its potential complications in their most recent pregnancy. Relatively similar average rates of well-preparedness (32%) have been reported in community-based surveys from Ethiopia [22–24] and higher rates (58.3%) in Tanzania [12]. In contrast, lower rates have been reported in facility-based studies in Rwanda (22.3%), Kenya (28%), and Uganda (28.3%) [11, 14, 15]. The higher level of birth preparedness in community-based studies could be attributed to the more diverse population captured, providing a more comprehensive picture of BPCR practices than facility-based studies. Additionally, differences among countries may be due to variations in cultural and socio-economic factors that influence BPCR rates. Despite the differences, the rates of BPCR are generally low in LMICs. This could be due to poor antenatal care attendance among women in LMICs, the primary avenue to access BPCR services [10]. For instance, in this study, most participants (74%) did not comply with the minimum eight ANC visits recommended by the World Health Organization for every pregnant woman. Similarly low rates of ANC attendance have been reported in other studies in Uganda [25, 26]. Besides ANC attendance, low levels of education and inadequate counseling on BPCR during ANC visits may also account for low BPCR [8]. Therefore, the emphasis on attending ANC contacts should be on the number of hospital visits and the quality of care, ensuring that expecting women receive high-quality pregnancy care [27].

In this study, the most prioritized BPCR practice was saving money for childbirth, similar to other BPCR studies [8, 11, 22, 28, 29]. We observed that about three-quarters of the participants (73.7%) saved money for delivery, similar to findings in Tanzania (73%) [8] but lower than in Rwanda (87.5%) [11] and Nigeria (92.4%) [29]. This indicates that income is a significant determinant of health. Differences are likely due to variations in health system financing and out-of-pocket healthcare costs. The lower rate in Uganda may be because healthcare is free in public facilities, reducing the perceived need to save money compared to other countries where healthcare costs are not fully covered. On the other hand, no participant identified a blood donor in the event of severe hemorrhage during childbirth. This finding aligns with several other studies, which reported few participants identified blood donors before childbirth; about 7.8% and 0.6% [22, 23] in Ethiopia and 17.5% in Tanzania [12]. This could be because individual women identifying blood donors is discouraged in high HIV/AIDS prevalence countries, where voluntary donation to centralized blood banks is preferred [16].

This study revealed that the level of education affects BPCR practices. Women with higher education levels (secondary or tertiary) were more than twice as likely to be prepared than those with primary or no education. Similar findings were reported among women in Kenya [8] and Rwanda [30]. Higher education may be associated with a better understanding of health-related information and a more careful approach to health. Another factor that determined well-preparedness was knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy. Women with poor knowledge of danger signs were 73% less likely to be well-prepared than those with good knowledge. Similarly, in studies from Kenya, Tanzania, Ethiopia, and Rwanda, women with any knowledge of danger signs during the postpartum period were more likely to plan for birth and any potential complications [8, 11, 28]. This aligns with the general understanding that when women are more knowledgeable about danger signs, they can promptly seek maternal healthcare services without delay, further highlighting the importance of BPCR in enhancing maternal and neonatal health outcomes [31]. Furthermore, this study aligns with other studies conducted in rural Southwestern, Northern, and Eastern regions of Uganda, revealing low levels of knowledge among women on the dangerous signs of pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum periods [16, 17, 32]. Efforts to educate expecting families about peripartum danger signs should be strengthened to empower them to take preventative action. Routine ANC visits should also incorporate structured assessment and reinforcement of women’s knowledge of key pregnancy danger signs.

Strengths and limitations

The study provides essential information on the critical topic of birth preparedness, which is under-researched in Uganda but highly important for addressing the high maternal mortality rate. The community-based approach of the study’s data collection methods provides a more representative understanding of BPCR activities within the general population. However, there were some limitations. A specific single community and a relatively small sample size of recruited participants may limit generalization. Reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias, particularly regarding participants’ practices during their previous pregnancy. This also introduces social desirability bias, which could have led some participants to overreport desirable behaviors such as saving money, identifying a health facility, or arranging transport for delivery. However, to minimize these biases, VHTs were rigorously trained to ask the questions in a nonjudgmental way as part of their data-collection training. The participants were also informed that these surveys would be anonymous and would not be traced back to any individual. Future large-scale multi-site studies among immediate post-partum or pregnant women could further explore the subject in Uganda. These studies could also incorporate a qualitative approach to understand the contextual factors influencing BPCR practices. This study also did not assess maternal, fetal, or neonatal health outcomes. Future research should examine the relationship between BPCR and maternal and neonatal health outcomes to better understand the extent to which improved preparedness translates into better health outcomes at the community level. Despite these limitations, the study provides important community-level evidence on BPCR among postpartum women in Eastern Uganda, and the results remain valuable for guiding local maternal health programs and informing future larger, multi-site studies in similar settings.

Conclusion

The study shows low rates of preparedness for birth complications and inadequate knowledge of danger signs during pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum among women in Eastern Uganda. It reveals the importance of education and knowledge of pregnancy and childbirth danger signs in improving women’s birth preparedness and complication readiness. Furthermore, while many women saved money for delivery, other essential components of BPCR, such as identifying blood donors, were overlooked. This study highlights the need for targeted interventions to enhance education and counselling on BPCR aspects among women. This could be integrated and emphasized during ANC visits or delivered through community health workers.
