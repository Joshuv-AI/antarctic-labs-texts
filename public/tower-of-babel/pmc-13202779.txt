Unraveling the colonization process of microeukaryotic communities on artificial micro-ecological islands
Authors: Siyu Gu, Chuanqi Jiang, Peng Zhang, Shuai Luo, Yingchun Gong, Weisong Feng, Jie Xiong, Jing Zhang, Kai Chen, Kang Ning, Wei Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicro-ecological islands provide unique habitats for microbes and play a crucial role in the functioning of aquatic ecosystems. Microbes settle on these micro-ecological islands, forming distinct microbial communities. Previous studies have provided some understanding of the colonization processes and regulatory mechanisms of protozoa in microbial communities. However, these islands are also subject to colonization by a variety of microbes beyond protozoa, and comprehensive cross-kingdom studies and their potential mechanisms remain largely unexplored.ResultsUsing polyurethane foam units (PFU) to simulate micro-ecological islands, we studied the colonization dynamics of microbes in two distinct aquatic ecosystems, the Yangtze River and East Lake. Over 10-day colonization survey was conducted, we applied eDNA-PFU technology combined with metagenomic sequencing to comprehensively identify species present in the microbial communities, including bacteria, fungi, flagellates, protozoa, and metazoa. We found that microeukaryotes, rather than prokaryotes, were the primary colonizers in these two aquatic ecosystems. Our study reveals a colonization process of microeukaryotes in PFUs, profoundly influenced by their motility modes. Additionally, we propose a hypothetical food web framework within micro-ecological islands that maintains community stability, representing the most fundamental biological interactions.ConclusionsOverall, this study enriches our understanding of micro-ecological islands and provides deeper insights into the colonization processes and regulatory mechanisms of microbial communities. It highlights the practical significance of micro-ecological islands in biological resource management, environmental protection, and biodiversity conservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00897-5.Keywords: Micro-ecological islands, Artificial substrate, eDNA-PFU technology, Colonization process, Food web framework

Background

The colonization process, mechanisms of community formation, distribution, succession, and functions of microbial communities are fundamental aspects of aquatic ecology and are essential for the study of microbial ecosystems [1, 2]. In aquatic ecosystems, micro-ecological islands such as woodblocks, sediment surfaces, submerged plants, pebbles, and artificial substrates are commonly present, providing habitats for microbes including bacteria, fungi, flagellates, protozoa, and metazoans [3–10]. However, observing the complete colonization process of microbial communities on natural micro-ecological islands is challenging.

Polyurethane foam units (PFUs) serve as effective artificial substrates that support microbial colonization. Previous studies have utilized PFUs to investigate the colonization process and community dynamics of microbial communities [9–20]. Research has shown that the colonization process of protozoa follows the MacArthur-Wilson island biogeography model [7, 21], in which species richness on isolated habitats results from a balance between immigration and extinction. The PFU system acts as a micro-ecological island, and its colonization patterns reflect this equilibrium process, offering insights into community regulation [9, 11, 22–24]. The colonization ability of protozoa is influenced by their nutritional modes (feeding preferences) and body size [15, 25]. These studies enhance our understanding of the formation of protozoan communities in aquatic ecosystems. It is crucial to underscore those micro-ecological islands can also be colonized by bacteria, fungi, flagellates, and metazoa. To date, comprehensive studies on microbial colonization processes at cross-kingdom levels are limited, and the underlying mechanisms driving these processes remain insufficiently understood.

In this study, we conducted a continuous ten-day monitoring of microbial communities colonizing PFU systems in two natural aquatic ecosystems: East Lake and the Yangtze River. We employed a novel combined approach, termed eDNA-PFU, which integrates the traditional PFU method for species enrichment with environmental DNA (eDNA) technology for species detection [26]. Environmental DNA technology has been proven to be an efficient and reliable method for species detection, covering both prokaryotes and eukaryotes [26, 27], compared with traditional morphological identification. To comprehensively capture microbial diversity, we employed shotgun metagenomic sequencing rather than amplicon-based methods, enabling simultaneous identification of diverse microbial taxa across kingdoms without the biases of specific primers. While shotgun sequencing can also provide functional genomic information, our analysis focused on taxonomic composition to maximize cross-kingdom coverage of the community. Additionally, we continuously monitored the microbial community in the surrounding water of the PFU. The objectives of this study were: (1) to investigate the microbial colonization process at the community level; (2) to elucidate the physical mechanisms determining microbial colonization; and (3) to explore the mechanisms that maintain microbial community balance.

Methods

Sample collection and preparation

We conducted the experiments in natural aquatic ecosystems in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China. Sampling points were established in an urban lake, East Lake (30.54°N, 114.38°E, elevation ~ 40 m), and in the section of the Yangtze River flowing through the city (30.66°N, 114.36°E, elevation ~ 40 m) (Fig. 1A). Using a method based on the national standard “Water quality—Microbial community biomonitoring—PFU method” (GB/T 12990-91) [28], microbe-colonized PFU samples were collected from both water bodies. The PFU blocks measured 5 × 6.5 × 7.5 cm, with pore sizes ranging from 200–400 μm. Prior to deployment, PFU blocks were soaked in sterile water for 12 h to ensure no other microbes were introduced, and the water was then squeezed out [28]. At each sampling site, several PFU blocks were tied together with nylon string and submerged 30–50 cm below the water surface, secured to rocks (Fig. 1B). Sampling was conducted on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 post-immersion, resulting in samples from five continuous time points. At each sampling time point, three PFU blocks were randomly selected from each site. Additionally, three surrounding water environment samples were collected (Fig. 1B). All samples were stored in an insulated ice box (~ 4 °C) and transported to the laboratory within three hours.

Sampling strategy for PFU and water samples, the major analysis process, and basic statistical analyses. A Map showing the sampling locations in East Lake and the Yangtze River, where samples were recovered on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9; B Diagram illustrating the PFU placement method and sampling strategy for PFU and water samples; C Flowchart of sample collection and analysis; D Rarefaction curves and Venn diagram showing similarities and differences among the genera identified at East Lake and Yangtze River sampling sites

After returning to the laboratory, each PFU block was thoroughly rinsed and squeezed with distilled water to collect the entire microbial community, including both free-living and surface-attached microbes. The resulting liquid from each PFU block was filtered through a 0.22 μm polycarbonate (PC) membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to prepare PFU eDNA samples. Similarly, for water eDNA samples, 1 L of water from each collection was filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate (PC) membranes.

eDNA extraction, metagenomic sequencing and sequence assembly

We extracted eDNA from the filter membranes using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [29, 30]. DNA purity was determined using a NanoPhotometer (Implen, Westlake Village, CA, USA), and DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit on a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). DNA samples with yields greater than 1 µg and meeting quality criteria were then used to construct libraries. The Illumina sequencing connector was ligated to both ends of the library DNA using T4 ligase, and all samples were sequenced on an MGISEQ-2000 platform.

The raw metagenomic data were processed to obtain high-quality sequencing reads using fastp (v0.23.1) for quality filtering (-q 30, -T 0.5). Reads with an error rate less than 0.001 and a high-quality length greater than 75 bp were retained for further analysis [31]. The rDNA sequences were extracted by RiboDetector (v0.2.3) [32], then assembled by MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with a minimum contig length threshold of 500 bp. The SSU regions were recognized by nhmmer (v3.1b2) according to the rDNA HMM model (https://rfam.org/) [33]. Finally, non-redundant OTUs were constructed for all assembled contigs using CD-HIT (v4.7) [34], with the clustering parameter set to 97% sequence identity.

OTU annotation and community composition abundance assessment

Prokaryotic and microeukaryotic OTUs were annotated by the SILVA database (https://www.arb-silva.de/) [35, 36] and the PR2 database (https://github.com/vaulot/pr2_database), respectively [37]. BWA (v0.7.17-r1188) was used to map the recovered SSU reads to the OTUs. Each OTU read count was normalized using the RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) algorithm based on the number of mapped reads and the length of the corresponding OTUs. Based on the read coverage, the relative abundance of each OTU was calculated by SAMtools (v1.18) [38] and custom scripts. Then, the “microeco” R package [39] was used to compute the relative abundance statistics of taxa at each taxonomic level (from kingdom to species) in each sample.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed in R version 4.4.1, using the latest available versions of all relevant packages for this release. To obtain basic information on the composition of the microbial communities in the two aquatic environments, we calculated the Shannon–Wiener diversity index and performed principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and PERMANOVA analysis based on genus-level abundance data using the “microeco” package in R software [39].

We used a null modeling approach [40] to explore how microeukaryotic communities assemble in PFUs and aquatic environments, considering both deterministic ecological processes (variable selection, homogeneous selection) and stochastic ecological processes (dispersal limitation, homogeneous dispersal, and drift). To identify microbial taxa colonizing the PFU, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) [41] was performed at the phylum level to detect taxa with significantly differences between water and PFU, using an LDA score > 2 and a relative abundance > 1% as selection criteria.

To group taxa based on similarities in their temporal abundance patterns, we employed the fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm implemented in the “Mfuzz” R package [42, 43]. Specifically, we clustered genus-level abundance trajectories across five time points, using normalized log2-transformed relative abundance values. Prior to clustering, data were standardized to a mean of zero and unit variance to ensure comparability across taxa. We selected four clusters based on the minimum·centroid distance (MCD), inspection of membership values, and biological interpretability of the resulting groups.

To classify the colonization stages of microeukaryotes, we assigned numerical values (1–4) to clusters 1–4, For each taxon, we calculated a weighted average by summing the products of the number of genera in each cluster and its corresponding value, then dividing by the total number of genera. Values between 1 and 2, 2–3, and 3–4 were defined as early, middle, and late stages, respectively.

To analyze the correlations between taxa in the microbial community, we utilized the rcorr function from the “Hmisc” R package [44] in R. First, we calculated the correlations between genera, and only Spearman correlation coefficients (r) greater than 0.5 were considered statistically significant. Then, we established correlations between taxa based on the correlations between their constituent genera. The average correlation between the genera of one taxon and the genera of another taxon was used to represent the correlation between the two taxa. Taxa that contained only one genus were excluded to reduce bias and facilitate the identification of core colonizers.

The motility modes of identified microeukaryotic genera were annotated according to published references and recent studies involving morphology-based observations. Specifically, we referred to literature sources including Shen et al. [7], Lee et al. [45], Lynn [46], and other relevant taxonomic monographs and research articles. For genera lacking clear reference descriptions, annotations were based on accumulated morphological observations and motion behavior patterns from our long-term microscopic monitoring of environmental samples. The annotation results and references are provided in Table S4.

Results

Significant differences in microbial community composition between lake and river water environments

We collected samples on 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 days after PFU placement, including 30 environmental water samples obtained by direct sampling and 30 PFU samples collected using the eDNA-PFU method (Fig. 1A, B, Table S1). DNA was extracted from all 60 samples, followed by metagenomic sequencing. A total of 1.76 TB of data were generated, with each sample yielding an average of 29 GB of sequencing data (Fig. 1C, Table S2).

In total, we recovered 2,382,762 SSU reads from the East Lake and 1,815,036 reads from the Yangtze River. Assembly of these reads yielded 26,632 contigs (East Lake) and 31,063 contigs (Yangtze River) ≥ 500 bp. After clustering sequences with identity greater than 97%, we obtained 7978 OTUs (East Lake) and 7597 OTUs (Yangtze River). These OTUs were assigned to 1568 and 1545 genera, respectively (Fig. 1C). Rarefaction curves showed that both East Lake and the Yangtze River ecosystems reached saturation at the genus level. Moreover, approximately 60% of the genera were shared between the two ecosystems, whereas about 40% were unique to each (Fig. 1D).

We analyzed the microbial communities comprising bacteria, archaea, and microbial eukaryotes present in both environmental water and PFU samples to assess species composition and relative abundance. In this section, we first focused on the water samples to compare microbial communities between the two aquatics environments. The Shannon–Wiener index showed that species diversity in the Yangtze River samples was higher compared to the East Lake samples (Fig. S1A). PCoA indicated significant differences in microbial community composition between the two aquatic environments (PERMANOVA: R2 = 0.71, p < 0.001, Fig. S1B). The similarity in community composition of samples collected at different times reflects the stability of the community, suggesting that the microbial community in East Lake was more stable than that in the Yangtze River (Fig. S1C). Overall, there were significant differences in the composition of microbial communities between the two aquatic environments.

Relative enrichment of eukaryotes and depletion of prokaryotes in PFUs across both environments

In the same aquatic habitat, the microbial communities within the PFU differed in species diversity and composition from those in the surrounding water (Fig. 2A, B, and C). Notably, the proportion of microeukaryotes in the PFU system was significantly higher than that in the surrounding water (Fig. 2D). Since the total relative abundance was normalized to 100%, this also implies a lower proportion of prokaryotes in the PFU system. In East Lake, microeukaryotes represented 19.7% ± 4.3% of the microbial community in environmental water samples and 32.5% ± 3.6% of the microbial community in the PFU system. In samples from the Yangtze River, the respective proportions were 15.0% ± 5.2% and 20.8% ± 4.8%. The results of the null model further indicated that environmental filtering (a deterministic process) played a stronger role in the assembly of microeukaryotic communities within the PFU in both East Lake and the Yangtze River compared to the water (Fig. S2). The variable selection rates within the PFU were 64% and 29%, respectively, while those in the water were 56% and 22%. These findings suggest that microeukaryotes exhibited a higher relative abundance in the PFU community compared to prokaryotes, relative to their proportions in the water.

The trends in relative abundance of microeukaryotes differed between the two aquatic ecosystems (Fig. 2E, F). In Yangtze River samples, the species abundance in the PFUs peaked on day 1 and then declined, whereas in East Lake samples, species relative abundance in the PFUs reached its maximum on day 5. These differences may reflect distinct colonization dynamics influenced by environmental factors.

Distinct microeukaryotic and prokaryotic phyla between PFU and water

We applied LDA to detect microbial phyla with differential relative abundances between PFU samples and surrounding water (Fig. 3, Table S3). We identified 17 phyla: 12 microeukaryotes (Cercozoa, Chlorophyta, Ciliophora, Cryptophyta, Dinoflagellata, Euglenozoa, Fornicata, Fungi, Metazoa, Ochrophyta, Perkinsea, and Streptophyta) and 5 prokaryotes (Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Cyanobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria). These phyla capture the temporal and spatial fluctuations in the overall microbial community composition, as they account for approximately 85% of the total relative abundance identified in this study.

Similar microeukaryotic colonization pattern but different prokaryotic pattern

To investigate the temporal dynamics of microbial colonization in PFUs, we analyzed genus-level distribution patterns for the 17 phyla identified as significantly differentially abundant between PFU and water samples. We categorized these patterns into four clusters after normalizing relative abundance (Fig. 4A, B). Clusters 1, 2, and 3 represent genera whose relative abundance peaked on days 1, 3, and 5, respectively, while cluster 4 includes genera showing a continuous increase in relative abundance. To ensure robust clustering, we included only genera with a membership score > 0.5 in the taxonomic composition analysis (Fig. 4C, D). The pattern of change in specific microeukaryotic taxa (e.g., Metazoa, Ciliophora, Ochrophyta, Chlorophyta, Cryptophyta, and Euglenozoa) in PFUs from East Lake and the Yangtze River was predominantly concentrated in one or two clusters (Fig. 4C, D), indicating that these taxa follow observable regular patterns in PFU colonization. In contrast, other microeukaryotic groups (e.g., Fungi, Streptophyta, Cercozoa) and most prokaryotic taxa did not show consistent cluster associations, suggesting a more stochastic or non-regular colonization behavior (Fig. 4C, D, Fig. S3).

The x-axis represents the colonization time (days), and the y-axis shows the normalized relative abundance (log2-transformed). According to the fluctuation patterns in the relative abundance of microeukaryotic taxa that successfully colonized the PFUs, we proposed a model describing the colonization process (Fig. 5A, B). The earliest colonizers were flagellates, including Chlamydomonadales in Chlorophyta (e.g., Chlamydomonas, Chloromonas, Lobochlamys, Pandorina), Cryptophyceae in Cryptophyta (e.g., Cryptomonas, Komma, Hemiselmis, Goniomonas), Chrysophyceae in Ochrophyta (e.g., Poterioochromonas, Paraphysomonas, Acrispumella), and Kinetoplastea in Euglenozoa (e.g., Bodo, Neobodo, Rhynchomonas). These taxa exhibited patterns corresponding to cluster 1 in East Lake and clusters 1 or 2 in the Yangtze River. The next colonizers were Ciliophora group I species (small, free-swimming ciliates with oval cells ≤ 50 μm), such as Oligohymenophorea (e.g., Cyrtolophosis) and Prostomatea (e.g., Coleps). Followed by Euglenida in Euglenozoa (e.g., Euglena, Phacus, Trachelomonas) and Ciliophora group II species (defined as ciliates not assigned to groups I or III), including Bistichella in Spirotrichea, Chilodonella in Phyllopharyngea, and Hemiophrys in Litostomatea. These groups generally showed patterns corresponding to clusters 2 and 3 in East Lake and clusters 3 and 4 in the Yangtze River. The final colonizers were Ciliophora group III (sessile ciliates) and Rotifera, with Vorticella and Adineta representing the most typical examples, as their temporal patterns matched clusters 3 and 4 at both locations.

Using the weighted cluster assignment values, we divided the colonization process of microeukaryotes into three stages: early (E), middle (M), and late (L) (Fig. 5C, D). However, the duration of each stage varied across different aquatic environments. The early colonization stage in the Yangtze River PFUs was longer than in East Lake, resulting in a slight delay in subsequent stages. For example, the relative abundance of Ciliophora group II species consistently increased in the Yangtze River, while it peaked at days 3–5 in the East Lake (Fig. 5A, B). Nonetheless, it is important to emphasize that the colonization sequence of these microeukaryotic groups was generally conserved across environments.

Subsequently, we examined the relationship between the three colonization stages and the motility modes of genera. A chi-square test based on contingency tables revealed a significant association between motility patterns and colonization stages (East Lake: χ2 = 141.85, df = 10, p < 1.75 × 10− 25; Yangtze River: χ2 = 109.19, df = 10, p < 7.79 × 10− 19). This indicates that microbial taxa with different motility modes are nonrandomly distributed across successional stages, highlighting the key role of motility in determining colonization timing.

Microbial food web framework in PFU

As the colonization process progresses, species interactions tend to stabilize, although some community shifts may still occur. Constructing cross-kingdom taxa interaction networks lays a foundation for understanding how microbial balance is dynamically maintained. Correlation analyses revealed that the microbial communities in PFUs from the Yangtze River and East Lake exhibit similar patterns of positive and negative interactions (Fig. 6). Within the same colonization stage (early or late), the relative abundances of microeukaryotic taxa in the PFU were positively correlated, suggesting beneficial interspecies relationships such as synergy. In contrast, negative correlations between microeukaryotic taxa abundances across early and late colonization stages indicated antagonistic interactions such as predation or competition (Fig. 6A, B).

Heatmaps depicting correlations between species abundance in PFUs. A, B Correlations among microeukaryotic taxa in samples from East Lake (A) and the Yangtze River (B). C, D Correlations between microeukaryotic and prokaryotic taxa in samples from East Lake (C) and the Yangtze River (D)

When comparing the relative abundances of prokaryotic and microeukaryotic taxa across early, mid, and late colonization stages, most correlations among taxa did not exhibit clear patterns (Fig. 6C, D). However, Alphaproteobacteria and Cyanobacteria were notable exceptions. The relative abundances of these two groups exhibited consistent correlation patterns with microeukaryotes in both Yangtze River and East Lake PFUs. Specifically, Cyanobacteria showed a negative correlation with late-stage microeukaryotic groups, while Alphaproteobacteria displayed a negative correlation with early-stage microeukaryotic groups (Fig. 6C, D). As these correlation patterns were absent in environmental water samples (Fig. S4), they may reflect unique microbial community characteristics within the PFUs.

Based on these correlations, we constructed a hypothesis-generating food web framework for the microbial community within the PFUs (Fig. 7). To elucidate community structure, taxa were grouped according to similarities in positive correlations, reflecting their co-occurrence or shared ecological niches. These groups include the CCC group (Chlamydomonadales, Chrysophyceae, Cryptophyceae), the KS group (Kinetoplastea, Scuticociliatia), and the LSC group (Litostomatea, Suctoria, and Cyrtophoria). Based on negative correlations, the CCC and KS groups formed distinct food chains with rotifers and Alphaproteobacteria, respectively. Both groups also exhibited negative associations with the LSC cluster and Alphaproteobacteria, forming additional chains. The LSC group and rotifers showed a negatively correlated with Cyanobacteria, suggesting another potential food chain. Moreover, a separate chain comprised rotifers, Hypotrichia, Chlamydomonadales, and Alphaproteobacteria. These interaction patterns were consistent across lake and river habitats. However, ecological interactions in East Lake appeared more complex than those in the Yangtze River, particularly in the relationships involving the KS group and rotifers, and between Hypotrichia and rotifers (Fig. 7).

Discussion

A cross-kingdom perspective reveals consistent patterns of microeukaryotes colonization

Micro-ecological island ecosystems play a pivotal role within aquatic environments, serving as unique habitats that support a diverse range of microorganisms. In this study, we employed PFUs as artificial substrates to simulate micro-ecological islands in natural water bodies. Through metagenomic sequencing, we comprehensively analyzed colonization processes across the full breadth of life kingdoms, from prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) to microeukaryotes. This cross-kingdom approach overcomes the constraints of prior studies restricted to protozoa or other single groups, enabling a holistic view of microbial community assembly on island-like substrates.

East Lake and the Yangtze River were selected as representatives of lentic (lake) and lotic (river) water systems, respectively. Our findings revealed significant differences in microbial community composition between the two systems (Fig. 2); however, the overall colonization process remained broadly comparable, albeit with potential temporal variations (Fig. 5). Initially, flagellates such as chlorophytes and cryptophytes appeared, consistent with their known capacity for rapid dispersal and colonization [47, 48]. These were followed by the arrival of some ciliates, and finally by the settlement of the fixed ciliates and metazoa in the PFU. Once established in the PFUs, these later colonizers persisted on the substrate either in free-living or attached forms, in agreement with earlier morphological observations [49–51].

Deterministic navigation may contribute to the temporal colonization patterns of microeukaryotes

Our study revealed a significant association between the motility modes of microeukaryotes and the patterns of community colonization. For instance, flagellates are often regarded as pioneer species during the initial stages, a finding consistent with previous studies [7, 47]. Similarly, free-swimming ciliates tended to arrive earlier than crawling types. In later stages, sessile protozoa and rotifers generally increased in relative abundance.

Furthermore, we found that organisms exhibiting consistent colonization patterns shared a common trait—the use of a regulated or patterned navigation system. Organisms have evolved complex strategies such as directed movement, active feeding, mating, and predatory responses to adapt to constantly changing environments. Wan and Jekely divided these movements into three categories: stochastic navigation, spatial sensing, and helical klinotaxis [52]. Klinotaxis, often considered a form of regulated or patterned navigation, is regarded as the most complex type. Several microeukaryotes [53–55], including Cryptomonas [56], Chrysophyceae [54], Kinetoplastea [57], Dinoflagellata [58], and various ciliates, are known to exhibit klinotactic behavior for active steering and directed migration [59]. Microeukaryotes known or suspected to possess such navigation abilities tended to display more consistent aggregation patterns, whereas organisms likely relying on stochastic navigation or spatial sensing—such as fungi and Cercozoa among microeukaryotes, as well as most prokaryotes—exhibited more irregular distribution patterns.

Although this study did not obtain direct behavioral or functional data, these observations suggest a potential link between tactic-based navigation system and structured colonization dynamics. We therefore hypothesize that navigation modes may influence the colonization dynamics of microeukaryotes within micro-ecological islands.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sampling period covered only 10 days within a single season, limiting our ability to capture longer-term temporal dynamics and seasonal shifts in microbial community structure. Future studies that include extended time series across multiple seasons would provide a more comprehensive understanding of colonization patterns and community succession. Second, although the PFU serves as a practical and standardized artificial substrate, it may introduce ecological bias by preferentially capturing taxa that are surface-attached or capable of actively navigating porous substrates. As a result, planktonic or loosely associated organisms may be underrepresented, which could skew the observed community composition. Third, all abundance data derived from metagenomic SSU rRNA reads are relative rather than absolute and thus subject to the compositional inherent in sequencing data. This poses challenges for cross-kingdom comparisons, particularly due to substantial variation in rRNA gene copy numbers across taxa, especially between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Quantitative corrections or complementary approaches (e.g., qPCR or cell counts) could help improve interpretability. Finally, we did not conduct direct observational or experimental validations—such as microscopy, in situ imaging, or culture-based growth assays—to confirm the ecological interactions or succession mechanisms inferred from the sequencing data. Therefore, our interpretations remain hypothesis-driven and should be regarded as preliminary frameworks to be tested in future studies.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights that not only support fundamental predictions of classical colonization theory but also reveal novel kingdom-level differences in microbial colonization dynamics. Based on our findings, the colonization process of microeukaryotes may have practical applications in pollution monitoring and pathogen detection. Several commonly used pollution indicator species [7, 49, 60–62]—such as Bodo, Cyclidium, Euglena, Euplotes, Spathidium, and Vorticella—were consistently detected in PFUs across different aquatic environments. Furthermore, we identified consistent enrichment of potential pathogens (Table S5), notably Giardia, highlighting micro-ecological islands as promising platforms for biological surveillance using eDNA-based approaches. These findings emphasize the dual scientific and applied significance of micro-ecological island systems in aquatic ecosystems.
