1.
Introduction

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers. Denote by
E_{\lambda}
the elliptic curve with Legendre equation

Y^{2}Z=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z)

and consider this as a family of elliptic curves
E_{\lambda}\rightarrow Y(2)=\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\left\{0,1\right\}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will denote by
E_{\lambda}^{m}
the
m
-fold fibered power
E_{\lambda}\times_{Y(2)}\ldots\times_{Y(2)}E_{\lambda}
, which defines another family
E_{\lambda}^{m}\rightarrow Y(2)
. In this article we will work with the product

E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}\xrightarrow{\kern 4.09723pt\pi\kern 4.09723pt}Y(2)\times Y(2).

Here,
E_{\mu}\rightarrow Y(2)
is the Legendre family with parameter
\mu
.

Take an irreducible curve
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
, defined over a number field
k
, not contained in a fixed fiber. Then, for each point
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
, let
\pi(\mathbf{c})=(\lambda(\mathbf{c}),\mu(\mathbf{c}))\in Y(2)\times Y(2)
, where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the coordinate functions on
Y(2)^{2}
. Also,
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
defines
m
points
P_{1}(\mathbf{c}),\ldots,P_{m}(\mathbf{c})
on the elliptic curve
E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})}
and
n
points
Q_{1}(\mathbf{c}),\ldots,Q_{n}(\mathbf{c})
on the elliptic curve
E_{\mu(\mathbf{c})}
. Let
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
denote the generic endomorphism rings of
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
when restricted to
\mathcal{C}
, respectively. In general, these are equal to
\mathbb{Z}
, except in the case where one of the elliptic curves is constant on
\mathcal{C}
and has complex multiplication.
For example, if
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is constant on
\mathcal{C}
and
E_{\lambda_{0}}
has complex multiplication, then
R_{1}
is strictly larger than
\mathbb{Z}
.

We will assume that
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are not generically isogenous on
\mathcal{C}
and that the
P_{i}
are linearly independent over
R_{1}
and similarly for the
Q_{i}
. This is of course equivalent to saying that there are no generic non-trivial linear relations between the
P_{i}
and the
Q_{i}
. Another way of rephrasing this is to say that
\mathcal{C}
is not contained in a proper subgroup scheme of
E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}\rightarrow Y(2)\times Y(2)
, again assuming that
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are not generically isogenous on
\mathcal{C}
.

We define the map

\displaystyle J:Y(2)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow Y(1)=\mathbb{A}^{1}

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\longmapsto 2^{8}\dfrac{(\lambda^{2}-\lambda+1)^{3}}{\lambda^{2}(\lambda-1)^{2}}

which sends
\lambda
to the
j
-invariant of
E_{\lambda}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will also denote by
J
the map
Y(2)^{2}\rightarrow Y(1)^{2}
obtained by applying
J
component-wise.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}
be an irreducible curve and let
X,Y
be the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{A}^{2}
. We say that
C
is
asymmetric
(see
[
Hab10
]
) if
\deg(X|_{C})\neq\deg(Y|_{C})
. Here, by convention, we set the degree of a constant map to be 0.

If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}\xrightarrow{\kern 4.09723pt\pi\kern 4.09723pt}Y(2)\times Y(2)
is an irreducible curve, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
asymmetric
if the curve
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}=(J\circ\pi)(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{2}
is asymmetric.

We are now ready to state the main result of this article.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
be an irreducible asymmetric curve defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
not contained in a fixed fiber, and define
P_{i},Q_{j}
as above.
Suppose moreover that
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are not generically isogenous on
\mathcal{C}
and that there are no generic non-trivial relations among
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
on
E_{\lambda}
and among
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
on
E_{\mu}
with coefficients in
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, respectively. Then, there are at most finitely many
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C})
such that there exist an isogeny
\phi:E_{\mu(\mathbf{c})}\rightarrow E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})}
and
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+n})\in\en(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})})^{m+n}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
with

a_{1}P_{1}(\mathbf{c})+\ldots+a_{m}P_{m}(\mathbf{c})+a_{m+1}\phi\left(Q_{1}(\mathbf{c})\right)+\ldots+a_{m+n}\phi\left(Q_{n}(\mathbf{c})\right)=O.

Notice that this theorem is a special case of the Zilber–Pink Conjecture.
In combination with the results of
[
BC16
]
,
[
BC17
]
,
[
Bar19
]
, and
[
HP16
]
, and including the case in which one factor has complex multiplication and a linear relation holds among the points on the other factor (which will be addressed in future work), it yields a proof of the conjecture for asymmetric curves in
E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
For an account on the Zilber–Pink conjecture and other problems of Unlikely Intersections, see
[
Zan12
]
and
[
Pil22
]
.

Remark 1.3
.

Notice that if
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are generically isogenous, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}=Y_{0}(N)
(for some
N\geq 1
) which is not asymmetric, since the modular polynomials are symmetric (see Section
2.1
), and therefore have equal degree in both variables. Thus, in principle, the assumption that
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are not generically isogenous could be removed from the theorem.
However, in view of the Zilber–Pink conjecture, we expect that the theorem should remain valid even without the asymmetry assumption. For these reasons, in anticipation of a possible generalization of this result beyond the asymmetric setting, we prefer to leave the statement as it is.

Depending on
\pi(\mathcal{C})\subseteq Y(2)^{2}
, we can distinguish three cases:

(i)

the coordinate functions
\lambda,\mu
on
\mathcal{C}
are both non-constant;

(ii)

(exactly) one between
\lambda
and
\mu
is constant and the associated elliptic curve is not CM;

(iii)

(exactly) one between
\lambda
and
\mu
is constant and the associated elliptic curve is CM.

For each
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C})
, let
\rho(\mathbf{c})\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\en(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})})\cong\mathbb{Z}\left[\rho(\mathbf{c})\right]
.

In case
(i)
, by a theorem by André
[
And98
]
, there are only finitely many
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})}
and
E_{\mu(\mathbf{c})}
both have complex multiplication. So, recalling that isogenous elliptic curves have the same endomorphism algebra, we can discard those finitely many points and assume that
\rho=0
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
.

Similarly, in case
(ii)
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is constant with
E_{\lambda_{0}}
not CM. Therefore, there are no points
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})}
and
E_{\mu(\mathbf{c})}
both have complex multiplication, so we can take
\rho=0
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m+n}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
in this case as well.

In case
(iii)
, we can assume again that
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is constant. However, in this case there are infinitely many points
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
such that
E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c})}=E_{\lambda_{0}}
and
E_{\mu(\mathbf{c})}
are both CM, so we cannot simplify our hypothesis as before. On the other hand, since
\lambda
is constant, we can choose
\rho
to be a generator of
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})\cong\mathbb{Z}[\rho]
.

]
and later used, among others, by Masser and Zannier

Since the elliptic curves
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are analytically isomorphic to the complex tori
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau_{1}}

\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau_{2}}

By work of Peterzil and Starchenko, after restricting to a suitable fundamental domain, this map is definable in the o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{\text{an, exp}}
, so the preimage of
\mathcal{C}
is a definable surface

.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the subset of
\mathcal{C}
we want to prove to be finite. Then, the points

correspond to points on

, provided that the

and the

.

We then use a result by Habegger

Remark 1.4
.

Let
C\subseteq E_{\lambda}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
be an irreducible curve not contained in a fixed fiber, not necessarily asymmetric, and define
P_{i},Q_{j}
and
\tau_{1},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
as above (see also Section
2.2
for more details).
Assume also that
E_{\lambda}
and
E_{\mu}
are not generically isogenous on
C
and that there are no generic non-trivial relations among
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
on
E_{\lambda}
and among
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
on
E_{\mu}
with coefficients in
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, respectively.

Then, in case
(i)
let
\ell=0
, while in cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
let
\ell\geq 0
be the greatest integer such that there are
\widetilde{a}_{i,j}\in\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
and
\widetilde{P}_{j}\in E_{\lambda_{0}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
,
i=1,\ldots,m
and
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, such that the vectors
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j}:=(\widetilde{a}_{1,j},\ldots,\widetilde{a}_{m,j})
, for
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, are
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
-linearly independent and

\widetilde{a}_{1,j}P_{1}(\mathbf{c})+\ldots+\widetilde{a}_{m,j}P_{m}(\mathbf{c})=\widetilde{P}_{j}

for every
\mathbf{c}\in C
.
In particular, the assumption that there is no generic non-trivial relation among the
P_{i}
and the assumption on the vectors
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell}
implies that
\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{P}_{\ell}
are
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
-linearly independent.
Up to reordering the
P_{i}
, we can then assume that the matrix
(\widetilde{a}_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,\ell}
has maximal rank.
Hence, we can consider the isogeny

\displaystyle\Phi:E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}

\displaystyle(P_{1},\ldots,P_{m},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{a}_{i,1}P_{i},\ldots,\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{a}_{i,\ell}P_{i},P_{\ell+1},\ldots,P_{m},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}\right)

which sends
(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})\in C
to
(\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{P}_{\ell},P_{\ell+1},\ldots,P_{m},\mathbf{Q})\in\Phi(C)
. Note that the maximality of
\ell
also implies that
P_{\ell+1},\ldots,P_{m}
are generically
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
-linearly independent modulo constants, i.e. there is no relation of the form

a_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{c})+\ldots+a_{m}P_{m}(\mathbf{c})=\widetilde{P}

with
a_{\ell+1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
not all zero and
\widetilde{P}\in E_{\lambda_{0}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, that holds for every
\mathbf{c}\in C
.

Notice that if
C
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
, then
\Phi(C)
satisfies them as well, and vice versa. Moreover, as the restriction of
\Phi
to any fiber is again an isogeny, images and preimages under
\Phi
of algebraic subgroups of a fiber are again algebraic subgroups. This implies that Theorem
1.2
holds for
C
if and only if it holds for
\Phi(C)
.

Therefore, up to applying the isogeny
\Phi
, we will always assume that on the (asymmetric) curve
\mathcal{C}
that we are considering in Theorem
1.2
,
P_{1},\ldots,P_{\ell}
are constant and
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
-linearly independent, as above.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Isogenies and modular curves

Let
E_{1}\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{1}
and
E_{2}\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{2}
be two elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{C}
. Up to homothety, any lattice in
\mathbb{C}
is of the form
\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau
for some
\tau
in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
, and any two such lattices define isomorphic complex tori if and only if the corresponding parameters
\tau
lie in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{H}
by fractional linear transformations. Therefore, we may choose
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\Lambda_{1}=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{2}
, with
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
lying in the standard fundamental domain
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
for the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. This domain is given by

(2.1)

\mathfrak{F}=\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{H}:\left|\tau\right|\geq 1,-\frac{1}{2}\leq\mathrm{Re}(\tau)<\frac{1}{2}\right\}\setminus\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{H}:\left|\tau\right|=1,0<\mathrm{Re}(\tau)<\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

In particular, the choice of
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathfrak{F}
ensures that the associated elliptic curves are uniquely determined up to isomorphism.

Recall that for each isogeny
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
there exists a unique non-zero complex number
\alpha
such that
\alpha\Lambda_{1}\subseteq\Lambda_{2}
and
\phi
corresponds to the multiplication-by-
\alpha
map
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{2}
.

Therefore, if
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are isogenous, then there exist
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and integers
A,B,C,D
not all zero (not necessarily coprime) such that

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=A\tau_{2}+B

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot 1

\displaystyle=C\tau_{2}+D

thus

\tau_{1}=\dfrac{A\tau_{2}+B}{C\tau_{2}+D}.

Moreover, the converse is also true. If
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\tau_{1}=\frac{A\tau_{2}+B}{C\tau_{2}+D}
for integers
A,B,C,D
, then there exists an isogeny
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
, corresponding to
\alpha=C\tau_{2}+D
.

More generally, we have an action of the group
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
(here
+
means that the matrices have positive determinant) on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
which is given by

M\tau=\dfrac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}

for
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Q})
. If
M\in\textrm{Mat}(\mathbb{Z},2)
, we say that
M
is
primitive
if
\gcd(a,b,c,d)=1
.

We say that an isogeny
\phi
is
cyclic
if
\ker\phi
is a (finite) cyclic group. Then it is known (see
[
DS05
, Section 1.3]
) that any isogeny can be written as the composition of a cyclic isogeny and a multiplication-by-
n
isogeny, for some integer
n
. In particular, cyclic isogenies
E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
correspond to relations
\tau_{1}=M\tau_{2}
with
M
primitive. In this case, the degree of the isogeny is equal to
\det M
.

Recall also that the modular polynomials
\Phi_{N}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}\left[X,Y\right]
are the irreducible symmetric polynomials parametrizing pairs of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves with a cyclic isogeny of degree
N
between them
[
Lan87
, Chapter 5]
. In other words,
\Phi_{N}(j_{1},j_{2})=0
if and only if there exists a cyclic isogeny of degree
N
between the elliptic curves with
j
-invariants
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
.
We then define the classical modular curve
Y_{0}(N)\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
as the plane curve defined by the equation
\Phi_{N}(X,Y)=0
.

Finally, the following result provides an effective bound for the size of the integers
A,B,C,D
when the degree of the isogeny is fixed. It is a consequence of Theorem 1.1 of
[
Orr18
]
(see Section 1.A therein for details), which constitutes an improvement of both Lemma 2.1 of
[
MZ20
]
and Lemma 5.2 of
[
HP12
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
with the following property: if

E_{1}=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{1}),\qquad E_{2}=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{2})

are elliptic curves with
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathfrak{F}
, and there exists a cyclic isogeny
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
of degree
N
, then there are integers
A,B,C,D
such that

AD-BC=N\qquad\tau_{1}=\dfrac{A\tau_{2}+B}{C\tau_{2}+D}\qquad\left|A\right|,\left|B\right|,\left|C\right|,\left|D\right|\leq cN.

2.2.
Uniformization

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the quasi-projective variety in
Y(2)\times(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{m}\times Y(2)\times(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
with coordinates

(\lambda,\left[X_{1}:Y_{1}:Z_{1}\right],\ldots,\left[X_{m}:Y_{m}:Z_{m}\right],\mu,\left[U_{1}:V_{1}:W_{1}\right],\ldots,\left[U_{n}:V_{n}:W_{n}\right])

and defined by the
n+m

\displaystyle Y_{i}^{2}Z_{i}

\displaystyle=X_{i}(X_{i}-Z_{i})(X_{i}-\lambda Z_{i})

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,m

\displaystyle V_{j}^{2}W_{j}

\displaystyle=U_{j}(U_{j}-W_{j})(U_{j}-\mu W_{j})

\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,n.

We set
P_{i}=\left[X_{i}:Y_{i}:Z_{i}\right]
and
Q_{j}=\left[U_{j}:V_{j}:W_{j}\right]
and we have an irreducible curve
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
defined over a number field
k
such that the projection of
\mathcal{A}
to
Y(2)\times Y(2)
restricts to rational functions
\lambda
and
\mu
on
\mathcal{C}
not both constant.

The aim of this section is to define a uniformization map for
\mathcal{A}
, following closely the exposition in
[
Pil09b
, pp. 2489–2491]
.

As said before, any elliptic curve over
\mathbb{C}
is analytically isomorphic to a complex torus
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}
, where
\tau
has positive imaginary part and
\Lambda_{\tau}
is the lattice generated by
1
and
\tau
, with fundamental domain

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=x+\tau y,x,y\in\left[0,1\right)\right\}.

The classical Weierstrass
\wp
-function
\wp(z,\Lambda_{\tau})=\wp(z,\tau)
associated to the lattice
\Lambda_{\tau}
, is
\Lambda_{\tau}
-periodic and satisfies the following differential equation

(\wp(z,\tau)^{\prime})^{2}=4\wp(z,\tau)^{3}-g_{2}(\tau)\wp(z,\tau)-g_{3}(\tau)

where
\wp(z,\tau)^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dz}\wp(z,\tau)
and
g_{2}(\tau),g_{3}(\tau)
are defined in
[
Sil09
, Remark 3.5.1]
. Then, the zeros of the polynomial
4X^{3}-g_{2}(\tau)X-g_{3}(\tau)
are exactly the values of
\wp
at the half-periods:

e_{1}(\tau)=\wp\left(\dfrac{1}{2},\tau\right)\quad e_{2}(\tau)=\wp\left(\dfrac{1+\tau}{2},\tau\right)\quad e_{3}(\tau)=\wp\left(\dfrac{\tau}{2},\tau\right).

Note that the
e_{i}(\tau)
are pairwise distinct (see
[
Sil09
, Proposition VI.3.6]
and
[
For51
, Section 63]
) and that the function
e_{3}-e_{1}
has a regular square root on all of
\mathbb{H}
. Therefore, we can define

\xi(z,\tau)=\dfrac{\wp(z,\tau)-e_{1}(\tau)}{e_{3}(\tau)-e_{1}(\tau)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta(z,\tau)=\dfrac{\wp(z,\tau)^{\prime}}{2(e_{3}(\tau)-e_{1}(\tau))^{\frac{3}{2}}}

so that we have the following relation

\eta(z,\tau)^{2}=\xi(z,\tau)(\xi(z,\tau)-1)(\xi(z,\tau)-L(\tau))

where

(2.2)

L(\tau)=\dfrac{e_{2}(\tau)-e_{1}(\tau)}{e_{3}(\tau)-e_{1}(\tau)}.

This gives a parametrization of the Legendre family via the map
(z,\tau)\mapsto\left(L(\tau),P(z,\tau)\right)
, where

P(z,\tau)=\begin{cases}\left[\xi(z,\tau):\eta(z,\tau):1\right]&\text{ if }z\not\in\Lambda_{\tau}\\
\left[0:1:0\right]&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

Finally, define the map

\varphi:\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C})

sending
\left(\tau_{1},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)
to

\left(L(\tau_{1}),P(z_{1},\tau_{1}),\ldots,P(z_{m},\tau_{1}),L(\tau_{2}),P(w_{1},\tau_{2}),\ldots,P(w_{n},\tau_{2})\right).

Since this map is not injective, we would like to find a subset of the domain over which it is possible to define a univalued inverse function of
\varphi
.

By
[
For51
, Section 70]
, there exists a finite index subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
L(\gamma\tau)=L(\tau)
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Moreover, as a fundamental domain for the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{H}
one can take the union of six suitably chosen fundamental domains for the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(see
[
For51
, Figures 48 and 49]
). We will call this set
\mathcal{B}
and define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\left(\tau_{1},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right):\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathcal{B},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{2}}\right\}.

Then,
\varphi
has a univalued inverse
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}
and we set

(2.3)

\mathcal{Z}=\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}))\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}.

Following Remark
1.4
, we assume that
P_{1}=\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,P_{\ell}=\widetilde{P}_{\ell}
are constant on
\mathcal{C}
, so that
\mathcal{Z}
consists of points of the form
\left(\tau_{1},\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)
, where
\widetilde{z}_{i}
is the (constant) elliptic logarithm of the constant point
\widetilde{P}_{i}
.

Having described the uniformization of
\mathcal{A}
, we now turn to a key result of functional transcendence that will play an essential role in our arguments.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
be the subset of the smooth points of
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C})
that are not ramified points of
\pi_{|_{\mathcal{C}}}
. In this way, the set
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
consists only of finitely many algebraic points of
\mathcal{C}
.

Fix a point
\mathbf{c}_{*}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
and let
D_{\mathbf{c}_{*}}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
be a small open disc containing
\mathbf{c}_{*}
. Let
\mathbf{t}_{*}=\varphi_{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}}^{-1}(\mathbf{c}_{*})
. Then, there exists an open connected neighbourhood
U_{*}\subseteq\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
of
\mathbf{t}_{*}
such that
\varphi(U_{*})=D_{\mathbf{c}_{*}}
.
Thus, it follows that
\tau_{1},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
are well-defined holomorphic functions (possibly constant) on
U_{*}
and, with a slight abuse of notation, we consider them as holomorphic functions on
D_{\mathbf{c}_{*}}
.

With these definitions, we have the following transcendence result.

Lemma 2.2
.

The functions
z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
on
D_{\mathbf{c}_{*}}
.

Proof.

In case
(i)
we can apply Corollary 2.5 from
[
BC17
]
(which is based on a result by Bertrand
[
Ber09
]
).

In cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
, notice that our assumptions on
\mathcal{C}
imply that

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\left\{\left(\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{P}_{\ell}\right)\right\}\times E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m-\ell}\times E_{\mu}^{n}.

Let also
F=\mathbb{C}\left(\tau_{2}\right)
(note that
F=\mathbb{C}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
, since
\tau_{1}
is constant in these cases) and assume by contradiction that

\mathrm{tr.deg}_{F}F\left(z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)<m+n-\ell.

Then, if
\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}}
are elliptic logarithms of the constant points
\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{P}_{\ell}
, on
\mathcal{C}
we must have

\mathrm{tr.deg}_{F}F(\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=\mathrm{tr.deg}_{F}F\left(z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)<m+n-\ell.

Applying Theorem 7.1 from
[
Dil21
]
to
\mathcal{C}
, we obtain a subvariety
\mathcal{W}\subseteq E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
containing
\mathcal{C}
. This subvariety is a translate of an abelian subscheme of
E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m}\times E_{\mu}^{n}
by the product of a constant section of
E_{\lambda_{0}}^{m}
(defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
) with a torsion multisection of
E_{\mu}^{n}
. Moreover, one has
\dim(\mathcal{W})\leq m+n-\ell
.

Since
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
are linearly independent by hypothesis, this implies that there are
\widetilde{a}_{i,\ell+1}\in\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
and
\widetilde{P}_{\ell+1}\in E_{\lambda_{0}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
,
i=\ell+1,\ldots,m
, such that
\widetilde{a}_{\ell+1,\ell+1},\ldots,\widetilde{a}_{m,\ell+1}
are not all zero and

\widetilde{a}_{\ell+1,\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}+\ldots+\widetilde{a}_{m,\ell+1}P_{m}=\widetilde{P}_{\ell+1}

contradicting the maximality of
\ell
and proving that

\mathrm{tr.deg}_{F}F\left(z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)=m+n-\ell.

∎

2.3.
Heights

Let
h
denote the logarithmic absolute Weil height on
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, as defined in
[
BG06
]
and, if
\alpha
is an algebraic number, define
h(\alpha)=h\left(\left[1:\alpha\right]\right)
. Define also the multiplicative Weil height as
H(P)=\exp(h(P))
.

For an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and a point
P\in E(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, we also have the Néron-Tate height
\widehat{h}
, defined as follows (see also
[
Sil09
, VII.9]
):

\widehat{h}(P)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{4^{n}}h(2^{n}P).

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}
two elliptic curves defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and let
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
be an isogeny also defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Denote by
\widehat{h}_{1}
and
\widehat{h}_{2}
the Néron-Tate heights on
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, respectively. Then for any
P\in E_{1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, we have

\widehat{h}_{2}(\phi(P))=\deg\phi\cdot\widehat{h}_{1}(P).

Proof.

Recall that the height
h
, given by the embedding of an elliptic curve
E
into
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, is exactly the height associated to the divisor
3(O)
as described in
[
HS13
, Section B.3]
, where
O
is the identity element on
E
. Moreover, if
O_{i}
is the identity on
E_{i}
and
\phi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
is an isogeny, we have

\phi^{*}2(O_{2})=2\sum\limits_{T\in\ker\phi}(T)\sim 2(T^{\prime})+(2\deg\phi-2)(O_{1})

where
T^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{T\in\ker\phi}T
is either
O_{1}
or a non-trivial 2-torsion point. In either case,
2(T^{\prime})\sim 2(O_{1})
, so that the pull-back
\phi^{*}2(O_{2})
is linearly equivalent to
(2\deg\phi)(O_{1})
.
Then, using
[
HS13
, Theorem B.5.6]
, we get

\displaystyle 2\widehat{h}_{2}(\phi(P))

\displaystyle=2\widehat{h}_{E_{2},3(O_{2})}(\phi(P))=\widehat{h}_{E_{2},6(O_{2})}(\phi(P))

\displaystyle=3\widehat{h}_{E_{2},2(O_{2})}(\phi(P))=3\widehat{h}_{E_{1},\phi^{*}2(O_{2})}(P)=

\displaystyle=3\widehat{h}_{E_{1},(2\deg\phi)(O_{1})}(P)=2\deg\phi\cdot\widehat{h}_{E_{1},3(O_{1})}(P)=2\deg\phi\cdot\widehat{h}_{1}(P)

which is equivalent to
\widehat{h}_{2}(\phi(P))=\deg\phi\cdot\widehat{h}_{1}(P)
.
∎

Using the same notation as in the previous section, we have that if
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}\left(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, then standard properties of heights (see
[
HS13
, Theorem B.2.5]
) imply that, if
\lambda
and
\mu
are both non-constant, we have

(2.4)

h(P_{i}(\mathbf{c}))\ll h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}))+1\quad\textrm{and}\quad h(Q_{j}(\mathbf{c}))\ll h(\mu(\mathbf{c}))+1

for every
i=\ell+1,\ldots,m
and
j=1,\ldots,n
. In cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
, if
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is constant, then
h(P_{i}(\mathbf{c}))\ll h(\mu(\mathbf{c}))+1
, as we can use
\mu
as uniformizing parameter on the base
\pi(\mathcal{C})=\left\{\lambda_{0}\right\}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
Moreover, note that if
\mathcal{C}
is defined over a number field
k
, we also have

\left[k(\mathbf{c}):k\right]\ll\left[k(\lambda(\mathbf{c}),\mu(\mathbf{c})):k\right].

Finally, we will also need another definition of height (from
[
Pil09a
]
).

Definition 2.4
.

If
\alpha
is a complex number, we define

H_{1}(\alpha):=\begin{cases}H(\alpha)=\max\left\{\left|p\right|,\left|q\right|\right\}\quad\text{ if }\alpha=\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q},\gcd(p,q)=1\\
+\infty\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

For
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we also define
H_{1}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})=\max\left\{H_{1}(\alpha_{i})\right\}
.

2.4.
Complex Multiplication

Given a
\lambda_{0}\in Y(2)
such that
E_{\lambda_{0}}
has complex multiplication, we know that the associated
\tau_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
is an algebraic number of degree 2, with minimal polynomial
aX^{2}+bX+c\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
and discriminant
\Delta_{0}=b^{2}-4ac<0
. In this case, we know by
[
Lan87
, Theorem 1, p. 90]
, that

\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})\cong\mathbb{Z}\left[\rho_{0}\right]=:\mathcal{O}_{\lambda_{0}}

where
\rho_{0}=\frac{\Delta_{0}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}}}{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and the isomorphism is given by
[
Sil94
, Proposition II.1.1]
. Using this proposition and with a slight abuse of notation, we will identify endomorphisms with the corresponding complex number.

By Theorem II.4.3. of
[
Sil94
]
,

\left[\mathbb{Q}(j_{0}):\mathbb{Q}\right]=\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda_{0}})

where
j_{0}
is the
j
-invariant of
E_{\lambda_{0}}
(which is algebraic by
[
Sil94
, Proposition II.2.1]
) and
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda_{0}})
is the class number of
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda_{0}}
.

Moreover, a theorem of Siegel in the form of Theorem 1.2 of
[
Bre01
]
gives us the estimate

\left|\Delta_{0}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\ll_{\epsilon}\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{O}_{\lambda_{0}})\ll_{\epsilon}\left|\Delta_{0}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}

so that, in particular, we have
\left|\Delta_{0}\right|\ll\left[\mathbb{Q}(j_{0}):\mathbb{Q}\right]^{3}
. Finally, using Proposition
2.3
and the fact that the endomorphism
\rho_{0}
has degree
\left(\Delta_{0}^{2}-\Delta_{0}\right)/4
, we get that

(2.5)

\widehat{h}(\rho_{0}P)\ll\left|\Delta_{0}\right|^{2}\widehat{h}(P)\ll\left[\mathbb{Q}(j_{0}):\mathbb{Q}\right]^{6}\widehat{h}(P)\ll\left[\mathbb{Q}(\lambda_{0}):\mathbb{Q}\right]^{6}\widehat{h}(P)

for every
P\in E_{\lambda_{0}}\!\!\left(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
.

3.
O-minimality and definable sets

In this section we recall the basic properties and some results about o-minimal structures. For more details see
[
vdD98
]
and
[
vdDM96
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
structure
is a sequence
\mathcal{S}=\left(\mathcal{S}_{N}\right)
,
N\geq 1
, where each
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is a collection of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that, for each
N,M\geq 1
:

•

\mathcal{S}_{N}
is a boolean algebra (under the usual set-theoretic operations);

•

\mathcal{S}_{N}
contains every semi-algebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

•

if
A\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
and
B\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
, then
A\times B\in\mathcal{S}_{N+M}
;

•

if
A\in\mathcal{S}_{M+N}
, then
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{M+N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the projection onto the first
M
coordinates.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a structure and, in addition,

•

\mathcal{S}_{1}
consists of all finite unions of open intervals and points

then
\mathcal{S}
is called an
o-minimal structure
.

Given a structure
\mathcal{S}
, we say that
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a
definable set
if
S\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

Given
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a function
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we say that
f
is a
definable function
if its graph
\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}:x\in S,y=f(x)\right\}
is a definable set. One can easily prove that images and preimages of definable sets via definable functions are still definable.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M+N}
. For
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we set
U_{t_{0}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(t_{0},x)\in U\right\}
and call
U
a
family
of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, while
U_{t_{0}}
is called the
fiber
of
U
above
t_{0}
. If
U
is a definable set, then we call it a
definable family
and it is easy to prove that the fibers
U_{t_{0}}
are also definable.

While there are many examples of o-minimal structures (see
[
vdDM96
]
), in this article we will work with the structure
\mathbb{R}_{\text{an,exp}}
(see
[
Pil22
, Chapter 8]
for details about this structure), which was proved to be o-minimal by van den Dries and Miller
[
vdDM94
]
.

For a family
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{M+N}
and a positive real number
T
define

Z^{\sim}(\mathbb{Q},T):=\left\{(y,z)\in Z:y\in\mathbb{Q}^{M},H_{1}(y)\leq T\right\}

where
H_{1}(y)
is the 1-polynomial height defined in the previous section and let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
be the projections of
Z
to the first
M
and last
N
coordinates, respectively.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
HP16
]
, Corollary 7.2)
.

Let
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M+N}
be a definable set. For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a positive constant
c=c(Z,\varepsilon)
with the following property. If
T\geq 1
and
\lvert\pi_{2}(Z^{\sim}(\mathbb{Q},T))\rvert>cT^{\varepsilon}
, then there exists a continuous definable function
\delta:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow Z
such that:

(1)

the restriction
\delta|_{(0,1)}
is real analytic (since
\mathbb{R}_{\text{\emph{an, exp}}}
admits analytic cell decomposition);

(2)

the composition
\pi_{1}\circ\delta:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
is semi-algebraic and its restriction to
(0,1)
is real analytic;

(3)

the composition
\pi_{2}\circ\delta:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is non-constant.

Lastly, we want to prove that the set
\mathcal{Z}
defined in (
2.3
) is definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{an,exp}}
. In the following, definability will always be considered in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{an,exp}}
, and we say that
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{N}
is definable if the set

\left\{\left(\mathrm{Re}(z_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(z_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Re}(z_{N}),\mathrm{Im}(z_{N})\right):(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})\in X\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2N}

is definable. Similarly, a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is definable if and only if
\mathrm{Re}(f)
and
\mathrm{Im}(f)
are both definable.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be the fundamental domain for the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{H}
defined in Section
2.1
; then the restriction of
\wp(z,\tau)
to
\left\{(z,\tau):\tau\in\mathfrak{F},z\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\right\}
is definable by work of Peterzil and Starchenko
[
PS05
]
. Therefore,
\wp(z,\tau)
is definable even if restricted to
\left\{(z,\tau):\tau\in\gamma\mathfrak{F},z\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\right\}
, for any fundamental domain
\gamma\mathfrak{F}
for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is the union of six such fundamental domains, we have that
\wp(z,\tau)
is also definable when restricted to
\left\{(z,\tau):\tau\in\mathcal{B},z\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\right\}
.
Thus, the uniformization map
\varphi
, defined in the previous section and restricted to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}
, is definable. Since
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C})
is semi-algebraic and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}
is definable, we get that
\mathcal{Z}=\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}))\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}
is definable.

4.
The main estimate

We continue with the notation established in the previous sections and, for every
T\geq 1
, define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(T)=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\left(\tau_{1},\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right)\in\mathcal{Z}:\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert,\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert\leq T,\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1})\geq\dfrac{1}{T},

\displaystyle\text{there exist }A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{Z}\cap\left[-T,T\right]\text{ with }AD-BC\neq 0,\tau_{2}=\dfrac{A\tau_{1}+B}{C\tau_{1}+D},

\displaystyle\text{there exists }\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m+n}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2m+2n}\text{ with }

\displaystyle\left(a_{\ell+1}+b_{\ell+1}\rho,\ldots,a_{m+n}+b_{m+n}\rho\right)\neq\mathbf{0},\max\left|a_{i}\right|,\left|b_{i}\right|\leq T\text{ and }

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}\!(a_{i}+b_{i}\rho)\widetilde{z}_{i}+\sum\limits_{i=\ell+1}^{m}\!(a_{i}+b_{i}\rho)z_{i}+(C\tau_{1}+D)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}(a_{m+j}+b_{m+j}\rho)w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{1}\Bigg\}

where
\mathcal{Z}
is the set defined in (
2.3
),
\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell}
are the elliptic logarithms of the constant points
\widetilde{P}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{P}_{\ell}
and
\rho
is either
0
in cases
(i)
and
(ii)
, or a fixed quadratic integer in case
(iii)
.

The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\#\mathcal{Z}(T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{\varepsilon}
, for all
T\geq 1
.

To prove this, we will apply Proposition
3.2
to the definable set
W
consisting of tuples of the form

\displaystyle(

\displaystyle\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m+n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m+n},A,B,C,D,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},

\displaystyle\zeta_{1},\theta_{1},\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{x}_{\ell},\widetilde{y}_{\ell},x_{\ell+1},y_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{m},\zeta_{2},\theta_{2},u_{1},v_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{n})

in
\mathbb{R}^{2m+2n+6}\times\mathbb{R}^{2m+2n+4}
, satisfying the following relations:

For every

Proof.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

If
(\tau_{1},\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1},\ldots,z_{m},\tau_{2},w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}(T)
, then there are integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m+n}
,
A,B,C,D
with absolute value bounded by
T
and integers
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
such that

\left(\alpha_{\ell+1}+\beta_{\ell+1}\rho,\ldots,\alpha_{m+n}+\beta_{m+n}\rho\right)\neq\mathbf{0}\qquad AD-BC\neq 0\qquad C\tau_{1}+D\neq 0

\left(C\tau_{1}+D\right)\tau_{2}=A\tau_{1}+B

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}(a_{i}+b_{i}\rho)\widetilde{z}_{i}+\sum\limits_{i=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{i}+b_{i}\rho)z_{i}+\left(C\tau_{1}+D\right)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}(a_{m+j}+b_{m+j}\rho)w_{j}=\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1}

And since
\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert,\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert,\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert,\lvert C\rvert,\lvert D\rvert,\lvert a_{1}\rvert,\ldots,\lvert a_{m+n}\rvert,\left|b_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|b_{m+n}\right|\leq T
and
\widetilde{z}_{p},z_{q}\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}}
,
w_{r}\in\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{2}}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}(a_{p}+b_{p}\rho)\widetilde{z}_{p}+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{q}+b_{q}\rho)z_{q}+(C\tau_{1}+D)\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}(a_{m+r}+b_{m+r}\rho)w_{r}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}\left(\lvert a_{p}\rvert+\left|b_{p}\right|\left|\rho\right|\right)\lvert\widetilde{z}_{p}\rvert+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}\left(\lvert a_{q}\rvert+\left|b_{q}\right|\left|\rho\right|\right)\lvert z_{q}\rvert+\lvert C\tau_{1}+D\rvert\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}\left(\lvert a_{m+r}\rvert+\left|b_{m+r}\right|\left|\rho\right|\right)\lvert w_{r}\rvert

\displaystyle\ll T\cdot\max\left\{1,\lvert\tau_{1}\rvert\right\}+(T\cdot\max\left\{1,\left|\tau_{1}\right|\right\})\cdot T\cdot\max\left\{1,\lvert\tau_{2}\rvert\right\}\ll T^{4}

Therefore, we have

\left|\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1}\right|=\left|\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}(a_{p}+b_{p}\rho)\widetilde{z}_{p}+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{q}+b_{q}\rho)z_{q}+(C\tau_{1}+D)\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}(a_{m+r}+b_{m+r}\rho)w_{r}\right|\ll T^{4}

from which we deduce that
\left|\xi_{2}\right|\ll T^{5}
, since
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1})\geq\frac{1}{T}
and

T^{4}\gg\left|\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1}\right|\geq\left|\mathrm{Im}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1})\right|=\left|\xi_{2}\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1})\right|\geq\frac{\left|\xi_{2}\right|}{T}.

It follows that

\left|\xi_{1}\right|=\left|\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1}-\xi_{2}\tau_{1}\right|\leq\left|\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\tau_{1}\right|+\left|\xi_{2}\right|\cdot\left|\tau_{1}\right|\ll T^{4}+T^{5}\cdot T\ll T^{6}.

\displaystyle\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m+n},A,B,C,D,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}),\mathrm{Re}(\widetilde{z}_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(\widetilde{z}_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Re}(\widetilde{z}_{\ell}),\mathrm{Im}(\widetilde{z}_{\ell}),\mathrm{Re}(z_{\ell+1}),\mathrm{Im}(z_{\ell+1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Re}(z_{m}),\mathrm{Im}(z_{m}),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{2}),\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{2}),\mathrm{Re}(w_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(w_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Re}(w_{n}),\mathrm{Im}(w_{n})\right)\in W^{\sim}(\mathbb{Q},\nu T^{6})

for some positive constant
\nu
.
Finally, consider the map

\begin{array}[]{ r c l }\mathcal{Z}(T)&{}\longrightarrow&\pi_{2}\left(W^{\sim}(\mathbb{Q},\nu T^{6})\right)\\
(\tau_{1},\ldots,w_{n})&{}\longmapsto&\left(\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}),\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Re}(w_{n}),\mathrm{Im}(w_{n})\right).\end{array}

Since this map is injective, the conclusion follows from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and let
N_{0}
be the minimal degree of an isogeny
\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}}:E_{\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\rightarrow E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist positive constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
(depending on
\varepsilon
) such that

h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})),\;h(\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\leq\gamma_{1}D_{0}^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ and }\quad N_{0}\leq\gamma_{2}D_{0}^{2+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Fix
\mathbf{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, and let
N_{0}
be as above. Note that an isogeny of minimal degree between two elliptic curves is necessarily cyclic, since otherwise it would factor as the composition of a cyclic isogeny and a multiplication-by-
n
map.

Therefore,
(J(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})),J(\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0})))\in(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\cap Y_{0}(N_{0}))(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
.
Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
is asymmetric, we may apply
[
Hab10
, Theorem 1.1]
and standard properties of heights to deduce

h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})),\;h(\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\ll\log(1+N_{0}).

Next, by Théorème 1.4 of
[
GR14
]
, we have

N_{0}\ll D_{0}^{2}\cdot\max\left\{h_{F}(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}),\log(D_{0}),1\right\}^{2},

where
h_{F}(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})})
is the (stable) Faltings height of
E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}
. By Proposition 2.1 of
[
Sil86
]
, one has

h_{F}(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})})\ll h\left(j(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})})\right)+1.

Since
j(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})})
is a rational function in
\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})
, Proposition 3.2 of
[
Zan14
]
gives

h\left(j(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})})\right)+1\ll h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))+1\ll\log(1+N_{0})\ll_{\epsilon_{1}}N_{0}^{\epsilon_{1}}

for every
\epsilon_{1}>0
.
Moreover, for every
\epsilon_{2}>0
we have
\log D_{0}\ll_{\epsilon_{2}}D_{0}^{\epsilon_{2}}
. Hence

N_{0}\ll D_{0}^{2}\cdot\max\left\{h_{F}(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}),\log(D_{0}),1\right\}^{2}\ll_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}D_{0}^{2+2\epsilon_{2}}\cdot N_{0}^{2\epsilon_{1}}.

Now fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choosing
\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{8+4\varepsilon}
and
\epsilon_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
yields

N_{0}\ll_{\varepsilon}D_{0}^{2+\varepsilon}.

Finally, recalling that
h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})),h(\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\ll\log(1+N_{0})\ll_{\epsilon}N_{0}^{\epsilon}
, we conclude

h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})),\;h(\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\ll_{\varepsilon}D_{0}^{\varepsilon}

for every
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then,

\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))=\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\dfrac{a\tau_{1}^{\prime}+b}{c\tau_{1}^{\prime}+d}\right)=\dfrac{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})}{\left|c\tau_{1}^{\prime}+d\right|^{2}}\leq\dfrac{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})}{c^{2}-cd+d^{2}}\ll_{\,\!{}_{M}}\max\left\{1,\log\left|j(\tau_{1}^{\prime})\right|\right\}.

As the
j
-function is invariant under the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have that

j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))=j(M\cdot\tau_{1}^{\prime})=j(\tau_{1}^{\prime}),

so that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\ll_{\,\!{}_{M}}\max\left\{1,\log\left|j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right|\right\}
.

Furthermore, we have that
j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))=J(L(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0})))=J(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))
, where
J(\lambda)=2^{8}\frac{(\lambda^{2}-\lambda+1)^{3}}{\lambda^{2}(\lambda-1)^{2}}
and
L
was defined in (
2.2
). Since
\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\left\{0,1\right\}
, this implies that
j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Then, using the inequality
\log\left|\alpha\right|\leq\left[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}\right]h(\alpha)
for every non-zero
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, we get

\displaystyle\log\left|j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathbb{Q}(j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))):\mathbb{Q}\right]h(j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0})))=\left[\mathbb{Q}(J(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))):\mathbb{Q}\right]h(J(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})))

\displaystyle\ll\left[\mathbb{Q}(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})):\mathbb{Q}\right](h(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))+1)\ll D_{0}^{2}

by Lemma
5.1
and
[
Zan14
, Proposition 3.2]
.
Combining this with the previous bound gives
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\leq\gamma_{14}(M)D_{0}^{2}
, for some positive constant
\gamma_{14}(M)
depending on
M
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is the union of finitely many translates of
\mathfrak{F}
by elements of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, there are only finitely many such
M
to consider. Thus, we have that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\leq\gamma_{15}D_{0}^{2}
, where
\gamma_{15}
is an absolute constant, and that
\left|\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right|\ll 1
, as
\mathcal{B}
is a set of bounded real part. Therefore, we get that
\left|\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right|\ll D_{0}^{2}
.

For the lower bound on the imaginary part, first note that if
\tau\in\mathfrak{F}
, then
\mathrm{Im}(\tau)\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
. Again, assume that
\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0})=M\cdot\tau_{1}^{\prime}
, for some
\tau_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}
and
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then,

\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))=\mathrm{Im}\left(\dfrac{a\tau_{1}^{\prime}+b}{c\tau_{1}^{\prime}+d}\right)=\dfrac{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})}{\left|c\tau_{1}^{\prime}+d\right|^{2}}\geq\dfrac{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})}{(\left|c\right|+\left|d\right|)^{2}\cdot\max\left\{1,\left|\tau_{1}^{\prime}\right|\right\}^{2}}\gg_{\,\!{}_{M}}\dfrac{1}{\max\left\{1,\left|\tau_{1}^{\prime}\right|\right\}^{2}}.

From before we get that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})\ll\max\left\{1,\log\left|j(\tau_{1}^{\prime})\right|\right\}=\max\left\{1,\log\left|j(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right|\right\}\ll D_{0}^{2}
, which implies
\left|\tau_{1}^{\prime}\right|\ll D_{0}^{2}
, since
\tau_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}
implies
\left|\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Hence,

\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\gg_{\,\!{}_{M}}\dfrac{1}{\max\left\{1,\left|\tau_{1}^{\prime}\right|\right\}^{2}}\gg\dfrac{1}{D_{0}^{4}}.

As before, we need to consider only finitely many choices of
M\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, so we have that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\gg 1/{D_{0}^{4}}
, where the implied constant is absolute.

Similar arguments give the respective bounds for
\tau_{2}(\mathbf{c}_{0})
.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We want to show that the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
(defined at the start of the previous section) is finite. Since the map

\pi_{|_{\mathcal{C}}}:\mathbf{c}\mapsto(\lambda(\mathbf{c}),\mu(\mathbf{c}))

is finite-to-one, Northcott’s theorem together with Lemma
5.1
reduces the problem to bounding the degree
D_{0}
of
\lambda(\mathbf{c})
and
\mu(\mathbf{c})
over
k
.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
\sigma\in\Gal(\overline{k}/k)
. Notice that
\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Indeed, we have

j\left(E_{\lambda(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}\right)=j\left(E_{\sigma(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}\right)=J\left(\sigma(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right)=\sigma\left(J\left(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right)\right)=\sigma\left(j\left(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\right)\right)

and an analogous identity holds for
\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0})
in place of
\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})
. If
N_{0}
is defined as in Lemma
5.1
, it follows that

\Phi_{N_{0}}\left(j\left(E_{\lambda(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}\right),j\left(E_{\mu(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}\right)\right)=\sigma\left(\Phi_{N_{0}}\left(j\left(E_{\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\right),j\left(E_{\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\right)\right)\right)=0,

so there exists a cyclic isogeny
\phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})}:E_{\mu(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}\to E_{\lambda(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}
of degree
N_{0}
. Since
\deg\sigma(\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}})=\deg\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}}=N_{0}
, we can take
\phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})}=\sigma\left(\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}}\right)
.

Thus, as
\mathcal{C}
is defined over
k
, we also have

\sigma(\widetilde{P}_{p})=\widetilde{P}_{p}\,,\qquad P_{q}(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))=\sigma\left(P_{q}(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right),

\phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\left(Q_{r}(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\right)=\phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})}\left(\sigma\left(Q_{r}(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right)\right)=\sigma\left(\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}}\left(Q_{r}(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right)\right)

for every
p=1,\ldots,\ell
,
q=\ell+1,\ldots,m
,
r=1,\ldots,n
.
Moreover, in case
(iii)
, we can assume without loss of generality that the generator
\rho
of
\en(E_{\lambda_{0}})
is defined over
k
, so that
\sigma(\rho)=\rho
. Recall that we are using
[
Sil94
, Proposition II.1.1]
to identify endomorphisms with complex numbers. Furthermore,
[
Sil94
, Proposition II.2.2]
guarantees that under this identification the action of
\Gal(\overline{k}/k)
on these two objects is the same.

So, in all cases, we have:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}(a_{p}+b_{p}\rho)\widetilde{P}_{p}+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{q}+b_{q}\rho)P_{q}(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}(a_{m+r}+b_{m+r}\rho)\phi_{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})}(Q_{q}(\rho(\mathbf{c}_{0})))

\displaystyle=\sigma\Big(\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}(a_{p}+b_{p}\rho)\widetilde{P}_{p}+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{q}+b_{q}\rho)P_{q}(\mathbf{c}_{0})+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}(a_{m+r}+b_{m+r}\rho)\phi_{\mathbf{c}_{0}}(Q_{r}(\mathbf{c}_{0}))\Big)=O

on
E_{\lambda(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))}
, since the
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are integers.

Now, consider the point
\varphi^{-1}_{|_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}}}\left(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right)\in\mathcal{Z}
with coordinates

\left(\tau_{1}^{\sigma},\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1}^{\sigma},\ldots,z_{m}^{\sigma},\tau_{2}^{\sigma},w_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,w_{n}^{\sigma}\right)

(here the superscript
\sigma
does not denote a Galois conjugate). By the previous equation and lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
we have relations

\sum\limits_{p=1}^{\ell}(a_{p}+b_{p}\rho)\widetilde{z}_{p}+\sum\limits_{q=\ell+1}^{m}(a_{q}+b_{q}\rho)z_{q}^{\sigma}+(C^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}+D^{\sigma})\left(\sum\limits_{r=1}^{n}(a_{m+r}+b_{m+r}\rho)w_{r}^{\sigma}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau_{1}^{\sigma},

\tau_{2}^{\sigma}=\dfrac{A^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}+B^{\sigma}}{C^{\sigma}\tau_{1}^{\sigma}+D^{\sigma}}

with
(a_{\ell+1}+b_{\ell+1}\rho,\ldots,a_{m+n}+b_{m+n}\rho)\neq\mathbf{0}
and

\max\left\{\left|a_{i}\right|,\left|b_{i}\right|\right\}\leq\gamma_{10}D_{0}^{\eta_{1}},\qquad\left|\tau_{1}^{\sigma}\right|,\left|\tau_{2}^{\sigma}\right|\leq\gamma_{11}D_{0}^{2},\qquad\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1}^{\sigma})\geq\gamma_{12}\dfrac{1}{D_{0}^{4}}

and
\left|A^{\sigma}\right|,\left|B^{\sigma}\right|,\left|C^{\sigma}\right|,\left|D^{\sigma}\right|\leq\gamma_{16}N_{0}\leq\gamma_{17}D_{0}^{3}
by Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
5.1
.

So,

\varphi^{-1}_{|_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}}}\left(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})\right)\in\mathcal{Z}\left(\gamma D_{0}^{\eta}\right)

where
\gamma=\max\left\{\gamma_{10},\gamma_{11},\frac{1}{\gamma_{12}},\gamma_{17}\right\}
and
\eta=\max\left\{\eta_{1},4\right\}
.

Finally, there are at least
\left[k(\mathbf{c}_{0}):k\right]\geq\left[k(\lambda(\mathbf{c}_{0}),\mu(\mathbf{c}_{0})):k\right]=D_{0}
different

\left(\tau_{1}^{\sigma},\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{\ell},z_{\ell+1}^{\sigma},\ldots,z_{m}^{\sigma},\tau_{2}^{\sigma},w_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,w_{n}^{\sigma}\right)

in
\mathcal{Z}\left(\gamma D_{0}^{\eta}\right)
. However, applying Proposition
4.1
with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2\eta}
gives a contradiction if
D_{0}
is large enough. This proves that
D_{0}
is bounded and, consequently, Theorem
1.2
.
