Emergency management of ureteral stones with SWL: is there any advantage over elective approach?
Authors: Rasim Guzel, Ahmet Fatih Kanberoglu, Salih Yildirim, Gamze Simsek, Kemal Sarica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe optimal timing of extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) for ureteral stones remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of emergency versus elective ESWL in patients presenting with ureteral calculi.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 66 patients who underwent ESWL at our institution between June 2023 and December 2024 were included. Patients were categorized into emergency (n = 32) and elective (n = 34) ESWL groups. All procedures were performed using a Modulith SLK Inline lithotripter (Storz Medical, Switzerland) with up to 3,000 shocks per session at 60–90 shocks per minute under NSAID analgesia. Demographic characteristics, stone-related parameters (size, volume, density), procedural variables (session number, duration), stone-free (SF) rates, post-procedural emergency visits, and infectious complications were analyzed. Stone-free status was assessed 7 days after the final ESWL session.ResultsThere were no significant differences between the emergency and elective groups regarding age, sex, body mass index (BMI), or comorbidities. Stones in the emergency ESWL group were significantly larger (8.2 ± 2.65 mm vs. 7.7 ± 1.74 mm; p = 0.023) while stone volume was similar. The number of ESWL sessions and total procedure duration did not differ significantly. Stone-free rates were comparable between groups (65.6% vs. 67.6%; p = 0.862). Post-treatment emergency department visits (31.3% vs. 20.6%; p = 0.322) and infectious complications (15.6% vs. 5.9%; p = 0.199) did not differ significantly.ConclusionEmergency ESWL was associated with stone-free rates comparable to elective ESWL in carefully selected patients, despite a larger mean stone size in the emergency ESWL group. However, the numerically higher infectious complication rate in the emergency group warrants cautious interpretation. These findings suggest that emergency ESWL may represent a feasible early treatment option for selected patients with symptomatic ureteral stones.

Introduction

Urolithiasis represents a major global health burden, affecting millions of individuals worldwide [1]. The prevalence of kidney stone disease shows substantial geographic variation, with reported rates of approximately 10–15% in developed countries [2]. Moreover, the incidence continues to rise, particularly in industrialized regions, largely attributed to dietary patterns and lifestyle changes associated with modernization [3].

The treatment options for ureteral stones are observation with medical expulsive therapy (MET), SWL, URS (retrograde or antegrade), and laparoscopic or open ureterolithotomy [4]. Among these options, the preferred treatment alternatives are MET, SWL, and URS. Current EAU guidelines recommend URS as the first-line treatment for ureteral stones > 10 mm, whereas SWL may be considered for selected patients, particularly those with stones ≤ 10 mm, depending on stone location, patient-related factors, and patient preference [1]. Although many factors are expected to influence the treatment decision for patients with upper urinary tract calculi, pain relief remains the first concern in an acute episode [3]. Nevertheless, the role of emergency procedures for ureteral stone treatment remains to be defined, and definitive recommendations and guidelines regarding their implementation are lacking [1].

Although many factors are expected to influence the treatment decision for patients with upper urinary tract calculi, pain relief remains the first concern in an acute episode [3].

Nevertheless, the role of emergency procedures for ureteral stones treatment remains to be defined, and definitive recommendations and guidelines regarding their implementation are lacking [1].

Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) is a well-established, non-invasive treatment modality for renal and ureteral calculi [4]. By delivering focused shock waves, ESWL fragments calculi into smaller pieces that can be spontaneously expelled through the urinary tract [5]. Current clinical guidelines recommend ESWL as a first-line treatment option for ureteral stones measuring less than 1 cm in diameter. Despite a relative decline in its use in recent years, ESWL remains a safe and effective therapeutic option due to its avoidance of anesthesia and hospitalization, lower costs compared to invasive interventions, and favorable complication profile [6].

The success of ESWL is influenced by multiple factors, including stone-related characteristics such as size, location, and composition, as well as patient-related factors such as skin-to-stone distance, skeletal anatomy, and vertebral alignment [7].

Emergency and elective ESWL procedures represent two distinct management strategies in the treatment of urinary stone disease [8]. Emergency ESWL is typically performed in patients presenting with acute symptoms or complications requiring prompt intervention, whereas elective ESWL allows for planned treatment in clinically stable patients under controlled conditions [9].

In the present study, we aimed to compare the outcomes of emergency ESWL sessions performed within 48 h in patients presenting with acute renal colic and elevated serum creatinine levels with those of patients undergoing electively scheduled ESWL treatment.

Patients and methods

Patients

A total of 66 patients were included into the study program and based on the timing of ESWL application, they were sub-divided into two groups: Group 1 (n = 32) Patients undergoing emergency ESWL and Group 2 (n = 34) elective application of ESWL in patients without any evident obstruction and colic pain requiring immediate treatment measures. Patients in both groups were compared with respect to demographic characteristics (age, sex, and body mass index), comorbidities, stone-related parameters (location, size, and density), procedural variables (duration of ESWL and number of sessions), infectious complications, stone-free (SF) rates, and post-procedural emergency department visits. Emergency ESWL was defined as the treatment of ureteral stones within the 48 h after diagnosis mostly made in the emergency department units.

Patients with single radiopaque ureteral stones confirmed by non-contrast CT and treated with ESWL were included. Patients with congenital or acquired urinary tract anomalies, active urinary tract infection or sepsis, non-opaque stones, or incomplete clinical or imaging data were excluded from the study.

Baseline clinical severity parameters, including pre-treatment serum creatinine, hydronephrosis grade, and pre-treatment visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score, were recorded before treatment. Hydronephrosis grade was used as a radiological surrogate marker of obstruction severity. For comparative analysis, minimal or absent hydronephrosis was categorized as grade 1. Patients with fever or positive leukocyte/nitrite findings suggestive of active urinary tract infection were not included in the emergency ESWL group; such patients were managed with antibiotic therapy and urinary diversion when clinically indicated. In elective cases, urine culture negativity was confirmed before ESWL.

Study design

Ureteral calculi were initially diagnosed using non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) and subsequently confirmed by kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) radiography. Patients with congenital or acquired urinary tract anomalies were excluded from the study.

All ESWL procedures were performed by using a Modulith SLK Inline lithotripter (Storz Medical GmbH), Switzerland). Patients received analgesia with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and were placed in the supine position during treatment. None of the patients required sedative and/or general anesthesia. A total of 3000 shock waves were delivered per treatment session. Stone localization was achieved using either fluoroscopy or ultrasonography.

Stone volume was estimated from preoperative non-contrast CT images using the ellipsoid formula: length × width × height × π/6, based on maximal stone dimensions measured in three planes.

Before ESWL, all patients were screened for active urinary tract infection. Emergency ESWL patients had no fever and negative leukocyte/nitrite findings on urinalysis; urine culture was not routinely available due to the urgent setting. Elective ESWL was performed only after confirmation of negative urine culture.

Emergency ESWL was not performed solely at the discretion of the treating physician. In routine clinical practice, patients presenting with symptomatic ureteral stones were counseled regarding all available management options, including observation with medical expulsive therapy, elective ESWL, ureteroscopy, and emergency ESWL. Emergency ESWL was offered to selected patients with persistent renal colic despite analgesic treatment, radiological evidence of obstruction, early renal functional deterioration, or a strong preference for immediate active treatment after counseling. The procedure was performed only in patients who accepted emergency ESWL after being informed about the potential benefits and alternatives, including the possibility of spontaneous stone passage.

Stone localization and targeting during ESWL were achieved using the inline fluoroscopic imaging capabilities of the Modulith SLK Inline system, allowing real-time visualization and accurate alignment of the stone within the shock wave focal zone. This imaging-guided approach enabled effective stone fragmentation while minimizing collateral tissue injury.

Treatment sessions were repeated at 5-day intervals, with a maximum of three sessions per patient, depending on the degree of stone fragmentation and the patient’s clinical response [8]. Stone-free status was assessed by performing KUB radiography obtained 7 days after the final ESWL session. Treatment failure was defined as the presence of residual stone fragments larger than 4 mm after three ESWL sessions. Ureteroscopic stone management was performed in symptomatic patients with residual stones after 3 ESWL sessions. Early treatment response was assessed using KUB radiography 7 days after the final ESWL session. Final stone-free status was evaluated at the 3-month follow-up visit. Routine follow-up included KUB radiography and/or ultrasonography, and non-contrast computed tomography was performed at 3 months for definitive assessment when required. Residual fragments smaller than 4 mm were considered clinically insignificant.

Although patients with infection received elective ESWL following appropriate antibiotic management, none of the patients in emergency group had any urinary tract infection prior to the management. In patients without renal functional impairment, a single intramuscular dose of 75 mg diclofenac was administered prior to the procedure. All patients were discharged on the same day (within 1–3 h) following ESWL, and none of them required hospital admission.

The timing of ESWL (emergency vs. elective) was determined by the treating urologist based on symptom severity, persistence of renal colic despite analgesics, radiological obstruction, and early renal functional changes.

Ancillary ureteroscopic stone management was performed in patients with treatment failure, defined as symptomatic residual stones or residual fragments > 4 mm after a maximum of three ESWL sessions.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was stone-free rate following ESWL. Secondary outcomes included the number of ESWL sessions, post-treatment emergency department visits, and infectious complications.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were analyzed using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

To address potential confounding related to stone size and density, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed with stone-free status as the dependent variable. Treatment timing (emergency vs. elective ESWL), stone size, and HU value were included as independent variables. HU value was entered into the model per 100-HU increase.

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study was conducted after obtaining approval from Sancaktepe Sehit Prof Dr Ilhan Varank Training and Research Hospital ethical committee (date: 14.01.2026 file number:09). Departmental medical records of patients undergoing emergency or elective extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) for ureteral stones at our department between June 2023 and December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed.

Results

A total of 66 patients undergoing emergency or elective ESWL procedures were included into the study program. Regarding the patient related parameters, there was no statistically significant difference between the emergency and elective ESWL groups with respect to the mean age (42.87 ± 11.83 vs. 42.50 ± 10.35 years; p = 0.356) of the treated cases. Gender distribution was comparable between the groups, with 45 male and 21 female patients (p = 0.655). Similarly, no significant differences were observed with respect to body mass index (BMI) and the rate of associated comorbidities (p = 0.79 and p = 0.66, respectively).

Evaluation of the stone-related characteristics revealed that mean stone size was significantly larger in the emergency ESWL group compared with the elective ESWL group (8.2 ± 2.65 mm vs. 7.7 ± 1.74 mm; p = 0.023). However, stone volume did not differ significantly between the two groups (42.6 ± 33.78 mm³ vs. 42.6 ± 32.74 mm³; p = 0.657). Stone density, measured in Hounsfield units (HU), was numerically higher in the emergency ESWL group, but the difference was not statistically significant (948.4 ± 247.8 HU vs. 924.7 ± 347.6 HU; p = 0.752). Baseline clinical severity parameters were also evaluated. Pre-treatment serum creatinine levels and hydronephrosis grade distribution were comparable between the emergency and elective ESWL groups. However, pre-treatment VAS pain scores were significantly higher in the emergency ESWL group than in the elective ESWL group (7.09 ± 0.89 vs. 4.44 ± 0.86, p < 0.001) (Tables 1 and 2).

Evaluation of the procedural outcomes demonstrated that final stone-free rates at the 3-month follow-up were comparable between the emergency and elective ESWL groups (65.6% vs. 67.6%; p = 0.862). Post-treatment emergency department visit rates did not differ significantly between the two groups (31.3% vs. 20.6%; p = 0.322), and the incidence of infectious complications were numerically more frequent in the emergency ESWL group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (15.6% vs. 5.9%; p = 0.199). Notably, although stone size and stone density were significantly higher in the emergency ESWL group, these differences did not translate into statistically significant differences in stone-free rates or complication-related outcomes.

An exploratory comparison was also performed between the emergency ESWL group and a conservatively managed no-intervention group to address the possibility of spontaneous stone passage. Baseline characteristics, renal functional parameters, successful stone clearance rates, infectious complications, and emergency department referral rates were comparable between the groups (Table 4).

None of the patients in the emergency ESWL group had fever or positive leukocyte/nitrite findings on preoperative urinalysis, whereas all patients in the elective ESWL group had negative preoperative urine cultures.

Although stone size and Hounsfield unit values differed statistically between groups, the absolute differences were small and their clinical relevance may be limited.

Ancillary ureteroscopic stone management was required in 11 patients in the emergency ESWL group and 11 patients in the elective ESWL group after failed ESWL treatment.

Discussion

Current treatment options for the medium sized (< 15 mm) ureteral stones include extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureterorenoscopy (URS), miniaturized percutaneous nephrolithotomy (Mini-PCNL) to some extent and, rarely, open surgical approaches [10]. Although URS has gained a particular increasing popularity in recent years, ESWL continues to be widely used due to its truly non-invasive nature, favorable safety profile, and cost-effectiveness [11]. Reported success rates of ESWL for ureteral stones range from approximately 48% to 89%, depending on stone characteristics, patient-related factors, and treatment protocols [12]. Related complications with this modality are generally mild and self-limiting, with flank pain, transient discomfort, and microscopic hematuria being the most commonly reported adverse events [13]. Regarding the application approaches, the role of emergency extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) in the management of such calculi remains insufficiently defined, particularly in comparison with electively scheduled routine standard applications.

In the present study, we compared the clinical and radiological outcomes of emergency and elective ESWL applications in patients with ureteral stones. Our findings demonstrate well that, when appropriate patient selection criteria are applied, emergency ESWL was associated with comparable stone-free rates to the electively scheduled ESWL approach. Our results are consistent with those reported by Ghalayini et al., who evaluated the efficacy of emergency ESWL in patients with obstructing ureteral stones and found similar favorable outcomes obtained after ESWL performed in the acute setting [14]. Collectively, these data suggest the potential feasibility of emergency ESWL in selected patients as an effective treatment modality for symptomatic ureteral stones.

In the current literature, the effectiveness and safety of emergent interventions for symptomatic ureteral stones have been evaluated in a limited number of comparative studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Arcaniolo et al. reported that emergent SWL achieved significantly higher stone-free rates and lower auxiliary procedure requirements compared to delayed SWL, while complication rates were comparable. This evidence supports the feasibility of emergency SWL as a definitive modality in appropriately selected patients presenting with acute renal colic [15].

From a pathophysiological standpoint, the potential benefit of early SWL may be explained by the fact that ureteral wall edema progressively develops after 24–48 h, ultimately impairing stone clearance. Edema may inhibit expansion chamber formation and reduce the fluid interface between the stone and surrounding tissue, thereby decreasing shockwave fragmentation efficiency. Thus, performing SWL before the development of significant periureteral edema, impaction, or fibrotic changes may contribute to improved outcomes.

Recent prospective data have also attempted to define which patients benefit most from emergency SWL. Kurkar et al. showed that lower HU density, mild hydronephrosis at presentation, and presentation during the first colic episode predicted pain relief after emergency SWL, while lower HU was the strongest predictor of successful stone clearance after a single session. These findings emphasize the importance of patient selection, and suggest that emergency SWL may be particularly suitable for symptomatic appropriate patients [16, 17].

Nevertheless, emergent intervention may carry an overtreatment risk, especially in smaller stones with a high likelihood of spontaneous passage [18]. A subsequent observation emphasized that ureteral stones ≤ 10 mm may pass spontaneously and that emergent treatment may lead to overtreatment particularly for stones ≤ 6 mm. Therefore, emergency SWL should be applied selectively, balancing symptom burden, obstruction/renal impairment, and spontaneous passage probability [19].

To further address this concern, we performed an exploratory comparison between the emergency ESWL group and a conservatively managed no-intervention group from the same study period. This additional analysis showed comparable successful stone clearance rates between emergency ESWL and conservative management. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that emergency ESWL is superior to observation or medical expulsive therapy. Rather, emergency ESWL may be considered an early active treatment option for selected patients with persistent pain, radiological obstruction, early renal functional deterioration, or a strong preference for immediate intervention after appropriate counseling.

The absence of significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics between the two group of patients treated in our trial strengthens the validity of our findings. Comparable distributions of age, sex, body mass index, and comorbidity status of these cases suggest that the observed outcomes are largely attributable to stone-related factors and the timing of the treatment rather than patient-related confounders.

In the present study, mean stone size was significantly larger in the emergency ESWL group, whereas HU values were only numerically higher and did not differ significantly between the groups. Although larger stone size and higher Hounsfield unit values have been reported as negative predictors of ESWL success, only stone size differed significantly between the groups in our cohort. Despite this difference, stone-free rates were comparable between the emergency and elective ESWL groups. This finding is particularly noteworthy and suggests that emergency ESWL, when performed under appropriate indications and with careful patient selection, physicians may achieve successful outcomes comparable to elective treatment even in the presence of more challenging stone profiles. Because stone size and HU value differed between the groups and are known predictors of ESWL success, we performed an additional multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjustment for these parameters, emergency treatment timing was not independently associated with stone-free status. This finding supports the interpretation that the comparable stone-free rates between groups were not explained solely by baseline differences in stone size or density. In addition, although hydronephrosis grade distribution was comparable between groups, pre-treatment VAS pain scores were significantly higher in the emergency ESWL group, supporting the presence of greater symptomatic burden in patients undergoing emergency treatment.

Regarding the possible advantages of emergency ESWL applications in patients, it is a well known fact that the increased duration of stone presence in the involved ureter may be well associated with stone impaction and periureteral fluid formation over time [20]. In the light of this fact which may compromise the application and outcomes of ESWL treatment, the lack of stone impaction and periureteral edema/ fibrotic changes around the stone may be associated with improved ESWL efficacy in patients undergoing emergency ESWL applications. Additionally, and more importantly, early active management may reduce symptom-related distress in selected patients and psychological changes in these cases waiting for the elective management which could be associated varying degrees of pain requiring emergency department referral in some occasions.

Furthermore, the similarity in the mean number of treatment sessions and overall procedural duration between the two groups in this present trial indicates that emergency ESWL was not associated with a higher procedural burden in this cohort or procedural burden compared with elective ESWL. From the clinical and logistical standpoint, this observation is relevant, as it suggests that emergency ESWL does not substantially increase workload, resource utilization, or treatment time in routine practice.

Although infectious complications were numerically more frequent in the emergency ESWL group, this difference did not reach statistical significance. From a safety perspective, however, this finding is clinically relevant and should be interpreted cautiously. Infectious complications occurred almost three times more frequently in the emergency ESWL group than in the elective group. Given the relatively small sample size and low number of infectious events, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no clinical difference. This trend may be partially explained by the acute clinical presentation of these patients, higher pre-treatment pain scores, and larger mean stone size in the emergency cohort. In addition, anxiety and symptom-related distress may contribute to emergency department referral in some patients, even in the presence of limited pain. Therefore, emergency ESWL should be performed only in carefully selected patients without fever or urinalysis findings suggestive of active infection, and close post-procedural monitoring is warranted, particularly with respect to symptom control and infectious complications.

On the other hand our results demonstrated comparable outcomes between “emergency” and “elective” management of ureteral calculi with SWL in experienced hands. Our current findings do not directly imply that “ emergency SWL” may be applied in a routine manner during daily practice. But it is evident that with its comparable efficient and safe outcomes this approach could be a reasonable alternative in cases requiring an early stone as well as pain relief for certain reasons ( cases with intractable pain, patients social/working status, cases with a noninfected moderately obstructed kidneys, patients choice etc.). As discussed above this approve will render the selected cases “stone and pain free” in a quick manner which will limit the risk of stone impaction which may end up with risky procedures under general anesthesia.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of our findings and because of the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of predefined decision criteria for treatment timing may have introduced selection bias. The relatively small sample size limits statistical power and precludes conclusions regarding equivalence between emergency and elective ESWL. Although KUB radiography was used for early post-ESWL assessment 7 days after the final session, final stone-free status was determined at the 3-month follow-up. Routine follow-up was performed using KUB radiography and/or ultrasonography, and CT was used when clinically indicated. We avoided routine early CT because all patients had pre-treatment NCCT and unnecessary radiation exposure was not justified in routine practice. Stone size and Hounsfield unit density differed between groups and are known predictors of ESWL success [21, 22]. The absence of multivariable adjustment may have resulted in residual confounding, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Key clinical parameters reflecting the severity of acute presentation, including degree of hydronephrosis, level of obstruction, renal functional impairment, and pain severity [23–25], were not systematically available, which limits clinical interpretation and comparison between groups. Prospective, randomized studies with larger patient populations and comprehensive outcome measures are warranted to further clarify the role of emergency ESWL in the management of ureteral stone disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, emergency ESWL was associated with stone-free rates comparable to those observed with elective ESWL in carefully selected patients, despite a larger mean stone size in the emergency ESWL group. However, infectious complications were numerically more frequent in the emergency ESWL group, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and low number of events. Therefore, emergency ESWL should not be regarded as a routine alternative for all patients with acute renal colic, but may represent a feasible early treatment option for appropriately selected patients without evidence of active infection. Given the retrospective design and potential residual confounding, these findings should be considered associative and hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.
