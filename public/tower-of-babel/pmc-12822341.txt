Ethnomycological knowledge of Slavic immigrant descendants in southern Brazil
Authors: Amanda Prado-Elias, Rafaela Helena Ludwinsky, Marcin Kotowski, Denis Augusto Zabin, Nelson Menolli Jr., Larissa Trierveiler-Pereira, Natalia Hanazaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthnomycological studies explore the relationships between humans and fungi, often reflecting cultural practices and ecological knowledge. While such studies have expanded across Brazil, the Southern region remains understudied. This research aims to document the ethnomycological knowledge of Polish and Ukrainian immigrant descendant communities in a subtropical area in Southern Brazil, and discuss the persistence, transformation, and erosion of mycological knowledge.MethodsFieldwork was conducted in rural areas of Itaiópolis and Papanduva (Santa Catarina, Brazil), with 35 participants interviewed using semi-structured methods. A photographic catalog was used to support species identification. Fungal specimens were collected, morphologically identified, and in some cases, DNA barcoded. The data were analyzed qualitatively.ResultsA total of 19 fungal species were reported, with Agaricus, Armillaria, Macrolepiota, and Auricularia being the most cited genera. Mushrooms were primarily used as food, though medicinal, ornamental, recreational, and symbolic uses were also documented. Traditional recipes and vernacular names in Polish and Ukrainian were preserved, illustrating cultural continuity. Some reports suggest stock knowledge persists, even when active use has declined.ConclusionsThe study reveals that mushrooms remain culturally significant among immigrant descendant communities in Southern Brazil. While some traditional knowledge is preserved and even adapted, other aspects show signs of erosion or restricted transmission. The findings highlight how immigration, environmental factors, and cultural resilience interact in shaping mycological knowledge. This research contributes to the broader understanding of how human-fungus relationships evolve in migratory contexts and calls attention to the importance of preserving biocultural heritage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00840-x.

Background

Ethnomycological studies aim to document the uses, perceptions, and knowledge associated with different fungal species [1–5], as well as to investigate the cultural affinity of human communities with these organisms. In this context, some societies are classified as mycophilic or mycophobic [6], terms that refer to individuals or groups who exhibit, respectively, attraction or aversion to mushrooms and other fungi. Currently, this classification is understood as a gradient rather than fixed categories [7, 8]. Moreover, various cultural and ecological factors influence how different groups construct their relationships with fungi [9, 10].

In some parts of the world, there is a strong tradition of wild mushroom foraging, particularly among mycophilic communities that possess detailed and complex knowledge about gathering locations, seasonality, and identification of edible and toxic species [11]. In Europe, several studies highlight the predominance of mycophilic attitudes, especially in Eastern European countries with Slavic languages, such as Poland and Ukraine [12–16].

In Brazil, systematic studies of ethnomycology began in the 1960s and were primarily developed in the Northern region [17–22]. Currently, during what is referred to as the “diversity period,” research is being conducted in different parts of the country, expanding the geographic scope of this field and the diversity of groups studied, which were initially focused on indigenous communities [23]. However, no ethnomycological studies have been carried out in Southern Brazil [24].

The history of Brazil is deeply marked by several waves of immigration. After the Portuguese settlement in the 16th century, and the violent movement of translocation of people from Africa with the slave trade, since the end of the 19th century the country experienced an intense influx of European immigrants, also motivated by political instability and food scarcity in Europe [25–27]. From the European perspective, migration was especially driven by Brazil’s immigration policy, which actively promoted settlement through promises of free land and a better life, as part of a strategy to populate the countryside and replace the workforce after the abolition of slavery [28]. In the Southern region of Brazil, the groups of immigrants who then arrived were mostly of European origin, mainly from Germany, Italy, Poland, and Ukraine [29, 30].

Between 1890 and 1900 about 60,000–80,000 smallholders and landless peasants from Congress Poland immigrated to the American continent. They were followed by around 25,000 people from the kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria [31, 32]. It was during this period that the Polish Kolonia Lucena (now the northern part of Itaiopolis) was established, near the German settlement of Lucena, and the primary influx of Polish migrants to Papanduva took place [33, 34], making the Northern Plateau of the state of Santa Catarina stands out as a hub for Polish and Ukrainian immigration [29, 35]. The immigrant settlements in this region formed small colonies whose insular cultural identity contributed to the preservation of their heritage. In this sense, the formation of these colonies has played a fundamental role in maintaining the traditions and knowledge brought by the immigrants, which have remained alive across generations [30]. As part of this cultural continuity, Ludwinsky [36] recorded preliminary information about the consumption of mushrooms in this region. However, Ludwinsky [36] did not identify the species consumed or other important aspects of the associated knowledge, such as preparation methods, vernacular names, and complementary details of their use.

The Southern region of Brazil presents remarkable ecological diversity, making it favorable for traditional practices of gathering and resource management. Araucaria forests, common in the study area, are already recognized as cultural landscapes [37], reflecting the historical interaction between human populations and the environment. Today, however, this ecosystem is considered critically endangered, with less than 3% of its original coverage remaining [38, 39, 40], which makes it urgent to document the knowledge associated with this environment. In addition, it is one of the regions with the greatest diversity of immigrants in the country, overlapping with the presence of Indigenous peoples who had long inhabited the area [26, 29, 30, 41, 42]. This cultural mosaic makes Southern Brazil a privileged context for investigating how traditional knowledge is transformed in situations of migration and intercultural contact.

Although there were informal reports about mushroom consumption [36, 43–45], this study provides the first systematic documentation of ethnomycological knowledge in the region, focusing on communities of Polish and Ukrainian immigrant descendants established there [29, 35, 46, 47]. We aimed to document the existing ethnomycological knowledge in the northern Plateau of the state of Santa Catarina, in Southern Brazil, and to understand whether this knowledge is related to the historical process of immigration and settlement in this region. This study sought to illustrate how ethnomycological knowledge is dynamic and how it plays a role in shaping identity and preserving the biocultural heritage [48–50] of immigrant communities. We documented uses of mushrooms as food, recipes, vernacular names, as well as medicinal and recreational uses.

Study area

This project was carried out in the northern plateau region of the state of Santa Catarina (Southern Brazil) with groups of Polish and Ukrainian immigrant descendants living in the municipalities of Itaiópolis (26º20’11"S, 49º54’23"W) and Papanduva (26º22’13"S, 50º08’40"W) (Fig. 1), mainly in rural areas. According to the most recent census conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics [51, 52], Itaiópolis has 22,051 inhabitants and Papanduva has 19,150 inhabitants. However, according to the Polish and Ukrainian cultural associations, there are currently no official estimates of the number of inhabitants who self-identify as descendants of these immigrant groups in the municipalities (personal communication). Families in the region still preserve strong traditions and a distinct Slavic cultural identity. The material culture of these communities is expressed in many ways, such as folk dances, traditional clothing for special events, handicrafts, language, architecture, and typical dishes, which remain common in the region [26, 35].

The study area is located within the Atlantic rainforest domain, specifically in Mixed Ombrophilous Forest fragments [53]. This vegetation type predominates at higher altitudes in Southern Brazil and is characterized by the prominent presence of Araucaria angustifolia (Bertol.) Kuntze, known as “Araucária” or “Paraná pine” [53]. The region’s climate is classified as Cfb according to the Köppen system, characterized as temperate, with high relative humidity [54, 55], and receives approximately 1,700 mm of rainfall per year. Precipitation is distributed throughout the year, with no defined dry season, although both intensity and frequency vary. In spring (September to November) rainfall is often more intense. Seasons are relatively well marked. Mean annual temperatures range from 18 to 20 °C, with frosts commonly occurring in winter (June to August). Summers are typically mild, averaging around 22 °C, though temperatures above 30 °C are also recorded [56, 57].

Much of the original forested landscape has been modified [29, 46]. In the region, exotic tree species such as Eucalyptus spp. and Pinus spp. are cultivated, mainly for commercial purposes, although small patches of native forest remain.

The mushroom species consumed in the region occur at different times of the year. For instance, Macrolepiota bonaerensis (Singer & Digilio) Heinem. & Rammeloo and Boletus edulis Bull are typically found in autumn and winter (March to August), whereas Agaricus spp. and Auricularia fuscosuccinea (Mont.) Henn. are more commonly collected in spring and summer (September to February).

Methods

Data collection was based on interviews and the collection of mushroom material for species identification. After obtaining authorization from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Santa Catarina Federal University (UFSC), three field expeditions were carried out. Data collection began in the municipality of Itaiópolis, where previous records of mushroom consumption already existed [36], and was later extended to the municipality of Papanduva. The expedition months were strategically chosen to observe and collect different mushroom species occurring during both warmer (September to March) and colder (April to August) seasons. Three field expeditions were conducted: two during the warmer season (December 2023 and February 2024) and one during the colder season (July 2024).

Interview procedures

This research had an exploratory qualitative character, with data obtained through semi-structured interviews with local experts that were selected using the snowball sampling method [58, 59]. To identify local experts, we initially relied on people interviewed in Ludwinsky [30, 41] who had mentioned mushroom foraging and consumption. Subsequently, we asked each local expert to suggest five additional indications of people with knowledge of mushroom use to compose the next interviews. This process was repeated until the recommendations began to overlap or were exhausted. At that point, we intentionally selected interviewees targeting individuals of similar ethnic background in restaurants, food markets, or among local leaders, to diversify our sample. We adopted as inclusion criteria: knowledge of mushrooms, being of legal age (more than 18 years old), and willingness to participate.

To facilitate the recognition of fungal species occurring in the region, a photographic catalog of pre-selected mushroom color images, printed in A4 size, was used during the interviews. The mushroom species included in the catalog were: Agaricus campestris L., Agaricus subrufescens Peck, Amanita muscaria (L.) Lam., Amanita rubescens Pers., Armillaria puiggarii Speg., Boletus edulis Bull., Lactarius quieticolor Romagn., Macrolepiota bonaerensis (Speg.) Singer, Oudemansiella platensis (Speg.) Speg., Phlebopus beniensis (Singer & Digilio) Heinem. & Rammeloo, Pycnoporus sanguineus (L.) Murrill, Ramaria flavo-brunnescens (G.F. Atk.) Corner, Pleurotus albidus (Berk.) Pegler and Suillus luteus (L.) Roussel (Fig. 2). The authorship names of the fungal species follow the nomenclature listed in the MycoBank database [60]. The species selected for the photographic catalog reflected a balance between fungi traditionally consumed in Europe and those already recorded in Brazil, with the addition of species and genera that could correspond to, or resemble, taxa known in Slavic traditions. This strategy was intended to facilitate recognition, stimulate memory, and capture both continuity and potential adaptations in local ethnomycological knowledge. When mushrooms were encountered in the field, they were also used as a tool to stimulate memory recall among participants. Whenever possible, walk-on-the-woods technique [61] was used with the interviewees to collect mushrooms. Additionally, field notes were taken through passive observation, during which the researcher did not interfere with the participants’ responses or questions [62]. All participants were born in Brazil and speak Brazilian Portuguese; however, 15 reported Ukrainian or Polish as their first language, and five of them stated that they learned Portuguese only upon entering school. Only three have ever visited their ancestors’ country of origin. The interviews were conducted in rural areas, where many families remain involved in agricultural activities, particularly the cultivation of soybeans, corn, tobacco, and livestock farming. However, some families live in rural areas but work in urban settings. All interviews were carried out in Portuguese by the first author, accompanied by another person from the team. When authorized, interviews were audio-recorded and deposited in the interview archive of the Laboratory of Human Ecology and Ethnobotany at UFSC. A total of 35 people were interviewed, including 15 men and 20 women; participants’ ages ranged from 29 to 80 years, with a mean age of 57.

Data analysis

The interviews addressed topics such as vernacular names of species, preparation methods, collection sites, and the origin of mushroom-related knowledge (see Supplementary Material 1). The interviews for which audio recording was authorized were transcribed, while those without such authorization were documented through written notes taken during the interview. All data were organized in spreadsheets and grouped according to the analytical categories derived from the interview guide (e.g., edibility, knowledge transmission, preparation methods, vernacular names). The qualitative material was then analyzed through content analysis [59, 63]. Coding was carried out manually by the first author and revised by the second, sixth and last authors, and the results were synthesized to identify patterns, variations, and illustrative narratives.

To assess whether the sampling effort was sufficient to capture and reflect the diversity of knowledge in the study area, a species accumulation curve was constructed based on the number of species cited by each interviewee as more interviews were conducted [64]. The curve was generated in R software [65] using the vegan package [66], and its interpretation was performed visually.

Specimen collection and identification

Mushrooms occurring in the region were collected and identified, with particular attention given to those used by the communities. The collection of macrofungi followed traditional techniques for taxonomic studies in mycology [67]. After macro- and microscopic analyses and subsequent identification, specimens were deposited at FLOR Fungarium (#82360–82382) at UFSC [68]. Sequencing of the nuclear ribosomal Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region was carried out for some specimens to confirm their identifications. Genomic DNA extraction and Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) were performed using the same methods as indicated by Zabin et al. [69]. After visualization of positive PCR products on a 1.5% agarose gel with Safedye Nucleic Acid Stain (Cellco Biotech, Cat. # PCK-301 S), they were cleaned up prior to sequencing using a commercial mixture containing exonuclease I and shrimp alkaline phosphatase (Exo + Sap, Cellco Biotech, Cat. # DPK-100 S, São Paulo, Brazil). For each sample, 2 µL of Exo + Sap mixture were added to 10 µL of PCR product, the solutions were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, then at 80 °C for 15 min in a thermal cycler. Sanger sequencing was carried out at Macrogen Korea (Seoul, South Korea). Raw data were processed using Geneious Prime v2023.2.1 [70] and the obtained sequences were analyzed using BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) [71] from GenBank [72]. After manual inspection and certification, the sequences were deposited at GenBank and the accession codes are indicated in Supplementary Material 2.

Community outreach

We developed a communication strategy to disseminate research results in response to community demands. Participants requested materials to help them identify both the mushrooms they already consume and other species they were curious to learn more about, and potentially include in their diets [73, 74]. In response, we produced, printed, and delivered these materials to project collaborators, local Polish and Ukrainian cultural associations in the region, and schools located near the neighborhoods where the interviews were conducted. A free online version was also made available.

Results

People and mushrooms

Among the categories investigated, the most frequently cited was edibility (83%), followed by recreational use (37%), medicinal use (20%), other uses (14%), and psychoactive use (3%). Other uses refer to mushroom uses that were not initially anticipated in the interview protocol or within the primary scope of the study. These uses were mentioned spontaneously by participants and did not fit into the predefined categories (such as food, medicinal or recreational uses), and included uses such as cosmetic and ornament. Several vernacular names were documented (Table 1), most of them in Polish or Ukrainian.

A total of 19 different fungal species were cited during the interviews. The most frequently mentioned was Agaricus spp. (morphologically similar to A. campestris and A. subrufescens), with 28 citations (Fig. 3).

Edible mushrooms and recipes

The most frequently cited edible species were: morphologically similar to Agaricus campestris, Agaricus subrufescens, Armillaria puiggari (possibly), Macrolepiota bonaerensis, Auricularia fuscosuccinea, and Boletus edulis, respectively. Other species that were cited as being consumed, but are not commonly known as edible, included Amanita muscaria and Auriscalpium villipes.

The most commonly reported preparation methods included: (a) placing the mushroom cap directly on a hot griddle with salt; (b) sautéing the mushrooms with scrambled eggs for breakfast; (c) cooking the mushrooms in Zapraska sauce (Fig. 5).

Zapraska is a sauce made by frying wheat flour in some type of fat, such as pork lard, and then adding water, allowing it to simmer until it reaches a thick consistency [75]. It was reported that this sauce is typically eaten with broa (a type of bread made from rye and potatoes). As illustrated by one participant: “Sometimes he [my father] would heat the mushroom in lard and add two tablespoons of wheat flour. Or he would wash it, cut it into four pieces, place it directly on the griddle with the white side down, and season it with salt and pepper…” (man, 57 years old).

Knowledge transmission and other mushroom uses

Of the 35 participants, 83% reported that they learned about collecting mushrooms within the family, a case of vertical knowledge transmission as illustrated by the statement: “There were so many, we filled baskets… The grandparents taught the parents, who taught the children. My father was the one who taught me [how to collect and prepare them], and he did the cooking, because my mother had passed away…” (man, 57 years old). About 14% of participants mentioned learning from their father or their father’s family, while 31% cited learning from their mother or their mother’s family. Only 5.7% accounts described learning about mushroom consumption from neighbors or other community members. Additionally, approximately 17% of participants reported learning about edible mushrooms from their fathers, grandfathers, or other male figures, while 46% reported learning from their mothers or other women in the community. Also, they emphasized that it was the women who collected, cooked, and transmitted knowledge on the subject. Another six participants stated that everyone in the family knew how to identify and collect mushrooms, without any gender-specific distinction. This gendered dimension of knowledge transmission and practice is further explored in the Discussion.

Other purposes for mushroom use were documented, such as the medicinal use of puffballs (e.g., Calvatia spp.) to help stop bleeding and promote wound healing, with one citation (man, 58 years old) specifically mentioning Geastrum sp. In this case, the participant reported having learned this use from an Indigenous chief (cacique), referring to the fungus as “pó pá tapa taio” (powder for obstructing wounds), but the indigenous ethnicity was not mentioned. This account illustrates interaction and knowledge exchange between different groups occupying the region, highlighting a case of horizontal knowledge transmission. Recreational uses of puffballs were also reported as children’s play objects.

Additionally, Pycnoporus sanguineus was reported to be used as a tea to treat heat rash (brotoeja) in children (woman, 70 years old and man, 39 years old). One participant (man, 63 years old) noted that the fungus appears on “lageana” Baccharis uncinella DC (rotting wood or logs) and added that specimens should be collected before the sheep arrive, as they eat the wood ear. The same species was also mentioned for cosmetic and recreational use. One woman (64 years old) related that, as a child, she would play by using it to color her cheeks red.

One participant (man, 80 years old) reported managing logs with wood ear by bringing them closer to his house as a way to decorate the yard. He also mentioned spreading pieces of agaricoid mushrooms (Macrolepiota spp. and Agaricus spp.), no longer suitable for consumption, across the pasture to promote spore dispersal. Another participant (man, 31 years old) reported offering Hygrocybe sp. to pigs as fodder, motivated by curiosity about the mushroom’s edibility. Two other participants mentioned that when they found Amanita muscaria, they would take it home to decorate their garden (Fig. 6) (woman, 62 years old and woman, 75 years old).

Discussion

Although ethnomycological studies have expanded to different regions of Brazil in recent years, the Southern region has not yet been covered by systematic investigations on the subject [23]. When investigating ethnomycological knowledge in communities of descendants of Slavic immigrants, this research contributes to filling this gap and brings new insights into a cultural context that has been little explored, such as the persistence and transformation of knowledge related to food in migratory contexts linked to edible fungi and the less frequent mentions of medicinal and symbolic uses.

The arrival of European immigrants in Southern Brazil since the 19th century also marked the opening of the forest and significant changes in the landscape. In turn, the Araucaria forests are recognized as cultural landscapes.

While immigrants faced a difficult adaptation process in Brazil, they were not the only ones negatively impacted by the region’s occupation. With the immigration policy, Indigenous communities, particularly in the South of the country, came to be seen as an obstacle to “national progress”. Thus, this process also marked a period of disputes and conflicts with the region’s indigenous peoples, such as the Lã-Klãno Xokleng, who suffered from territorial loss and violence. It is important to note that the interactions between immigrants, Indigenous, and other local groups in this historical context remain underexplored [26, 29, 30, 42]. Recent research, including a few from Indigenous authors [76, 77], are redesigning the history of landscapes in Southern Brazil. This means that the landscape resulted from a coevolution between the practices of human groups who lived in the region and the available natural resources [37, 46, 78, 79]. Although mushrooms may not stand out in cultural landscapes, they are nonetheless present and hold cultural significance. At a local level, some practices of fungi management to bring them closer to the houses and promote spore dispersal, as reported in the interviews, demonstrate both the knowledge about fungal ecology, as well as management techniques and attempts at domesticating fungi within this cultural landscape.

The formation of colonies in the study region played a key role in preserving the knowledge brought by immigrant groups. In contemporary mass migrations, cultural elements, especially those related to food, are strongly tied to personal and collective identity. In these new contexts, environmental changes and other sociocultural factors may contribute to the loss, transformation, or adaptation of traditional knowledge [27, 80, 81].

Knowledge related to food elements often persists in migratory contexts due to its strong association with cultural identity. While migration typically entails adapting to new environments, such knowledge frequently maintains both symbolic and practical significance [82, 83]. Hanazaki et al. [27] observed that adaptations in the use of food species tend to be more common for wild-gathered plants than for cultivated ones, a pattern that may also apply to the wild mushrooms analyzed in this study, which are collected rather than cultivated. The literature further documents cases of food substitution following migration, frequently involving the incorporation of locally available species in the new environment. In the communities studied, participants reported consuming Macrolepiota spp., such as M. bonaerensis, M. capelariae, and another taxon that, according to specialists, may represent an undescribed species (C. Nascimento, personal communication), as well as field-growing Agaricus spp. The continuity of these practices appears to be linked to the persistence of taxa related to those ancestrally consumed, such as Agaricus campestris and Macrolepiota procera (Scop.) Singer, despite broader processes of knowledge incorporation or loss [80]. In this perspective, within the conceptual model proposed by Hanazaki et al. [27] mushroom-related practices can function as cultural anchors that preserve identity. The transmission or erosion of mycological knowledge thus reflects broader socioecological negotiations, where identity, memory, and resource availability intersect. Thus, the persistence of certain species in Brazil reflects biocultural heritage [48–50] and identity more than ecological availability alone.

In addition, landscape transformations driven by immigrants in Southern Brazil included the introduction and management of exotic tree species such as Pinus spp. and Eucalyptus spp., which form ectomycorrhizal associations with edible mushrooms. Some of these mushrooms are highly valued in European gastronomy, such as Boletus edulis, Lactarius spp., Suillus spp., and Amanita spp. These ecological relationships may have contributed to the continuity of mycological knowledge even after migration, despite the fact that Lactarius spp. and Suillus spp. were not frequently cited as edible.

Uses other than culinary, such as medicinal and symbolic, were mentioned less frequently. This pattern may be related to the idea, as reported in the interviews, that mushrooms were primarily viewed as food and, for that reason, were treated accordingly. Such a pattern contrasts with other communities in Brazil that do not traditionally consume mushrooms, where alternative uses, such as for play purposes, were more frequently cited [84].

Local mushroom names

A significant portion of the popular names are in the ancestral languages of the interviewees, reflecting an interesting cultural continuity that persists in the region. In some families, Ukrainian or Polish is still spoken as a first language [35, 47]. The vernacular names are mainly based on the morphological and ecological characteristics of the macrofungi. For agaricoid mushrooms (the typical morphology with a cap and stipe), the most common names are Pieczarka and Peczerecza, while for wood ears (or bracket mushrooms, with both gelatinous or rigid texture), the most common names are Podpinki, Pidpenke, and Pipenke. Language is a central element of culture, carrying meanings, memories, and identities [46]. In this context, the fact that many vernacular names for mushrooms were not translated into Portuguese reflects not only linguistic preservation but also the symbolism and biocultural heritage embedded in this traditional knowledge.

Some vernacular names are identical or similar to those still used in Europe, such as Pieczarka for Agaricus spp. and Podpiniek for Armillaria spp., as documented in Poland by Kotowski [85]. Other comparable names were recorded by Łuczaj and Köhler [86], such as Peczczycia and Czepczyci, used in Ukraine, which resemble Peczerecza and Czeperecza, terms cited by Ukrainian descendants in the study area, both referring to Agaricus spp.

Łuczaj and Köhler [86] also mentioned names such as Grzyb and Babie uszy, although with some divergences. For example, Babie uszy was used to refer to Morchella spp., whereas in the study region, a similar term, Babiusz, was used for Auricularia spp. The term Grzyb is commonly used to refer to Boletus edulis both in Ukraine and Poland, but in the study area it was applied to various genera. Additionally, multiple variations were recorded for the term Podpinki. Although here we adopt a standardized nomenclature for clarity, it is important to acknowledge the wide variety of vernacular terms used by participants to refer to edible mushrooms.

Medicinal uses

Among the uses documented by the interviewees in the Polish and Ukrainian communities in this study, the medicinal application of puffballs stands out. Similar records have been reported in other regions of Brazil [87, 88], and in Europe [86]. Pycnoporus sanguineus, a species widely distributed across Brazil, has also been reported for medicinal use in various contexts, including as a hemostatic, anti-inflammatory, gastric antispasmodic, and menstrual cycle regulator among Indigenous communities in Northern Brazil [17] and rural communities in the Northeast region [89, 90]. In Mexico, P. sanguineus has also been recorded for cosmetic use by Indigenous groups, who used it to redden their cheeks [91]. The antimicrobial activity of P. sanguineus has been known since 1946 [92], and recent studies have also documented antiparasitic and antiviral activities [93].

Documented species, local identification, and health implications

The consumption of Auriscalpium villipes was reported by one participant (man, 54 years old). The species was collected and morphologically identified, although its preparation and consumption were not directly observed. No records of its consumption were found in the literature; however, Meijer [43] includes the species in a list of fungi considered potentially interesting for testing edibility. Nevertheless, the mention of a hydnoid mushroom as edible may reflect the persistence of stock knowledge associated with other hydnoid genera that are widely consumed in Europe, such as Sarcodon spp. and Hydnum spp. These genera are traditionally appreciated in mycophilic cultures [2, 4, 16, 94], and the memory of their use may have shaped local perceptions or contributed to the recognition of similar fungal forms.

Additionally, one participant reported consuming Amanita muscaria, without mentioning any adverse effects. The species was identified using the photographic catalog presented during the interviews. Field guides generally classify A. muscaria as a toxic species, and documented cases of its consumption without adverse effects are rare [95]. However, its toxicity can be reduced through specific preparation methods, such as parboiling [96]. Accidental ingestion of A. muscaria is relatively common and often results from confusion with other edible species in the same genus, such as Amanita caesarea [97]. Therefore, misidentification cannot be ruled out, particularly given the morphological similarities with Amanita rubescens, a species that also occurs in the region and is considered edible by the local community. However, it is important to note that although A. rubescens is widely regarded as edible, cases of mild intoxication have been reported when specimens are consumed raw or insufficiently cooked [98]. It is also worth noting that the English common name “fly agaric” refers to its traditional use as an insecticide [95, 99].

The same participant reported consuming Auricularia fuscosuccinea and experiencing malaise and hallucinations. The mushroom had been prepared as farofa, a traditional Brazilian side dish made primarily from toasted manioc flour. This case may be related to food poisoning (false mycetism), as the participant mentioned that the dish had been reheated. In Brazil, few cases of intoxication related to mushrooms have been reported [44, 100]. The most common cases involve the use of mushrooms for its psychoactive effects [101, 102]. However, intoxication caused by edible mushroom species has also been documented and is often associated with factors such as the consumption of spoiled mushrooms, excessive intake, mushroom consumption in combination with substances like alcohol, collection from chemically contaminated areas (since mushrooms are known bioaccumulators), improper preparation and storage, as well as individual susceptibility [100].

Historical narratives describe the extreme poverty and food insecurity experienced by Polish and Ukrainian immigrants during the early stages of settlement in Prudentópolis, a municipality nearby to the study area. Accounts mention the lack of adequate housing, spoiled or insufficient food rations, and difficult sanitary conditions. In this context, people relied on local resources such as pinhões (Araucaria seeds) and wild mushrooms to survive [45, 103]. However, due to the lack of knowledge about native fungal species, some individuals consumed poisonous mushrooms, resulting in severe poisoning and, in some cases, death [103]. These stories were remembered and reported by the communities in this work and may have contributed to a more cautious attitude toward mushroom consumption in the region. Such cases may reflect a process of ethnomycological knowledge erosion and the persistence of stock knowledge [104]. This same phenomenon has also been documented in relation to plant use among communities in the same region as the present study [30].

Cross-cultural comparisons of ethnomycological practices

Ethnomycological practices are not restricted or determined by political or administrative borders, but are shaped by cultural continuities, memory, and shared ecological experiences. As highlighted by Sulaiman et al. [105] in their study of Kurdish groups in Western Iran, cultural borders often provide a more meaningful framework than state boundaries when analyzing the transformations of traditional knowledge. From this perspective, the practices observed among Polish and Ukrainian descendants in Southern Brazil can be better understood as part of broader transcultural processes, where migration and adaptation interact with memory to shape mushroom-related knowledge. For this reason, many of the practices and species documented across Eastern European countries show strong parallels with one another, and with some practices reported among immigrant descendant communities in Southern Brazil, reflecting the biocultural heritage across different ecological and cultural contexts.

Similar dynamics have also been described where migration has often been accompanied by the persistence and transformation of food-related practices that serve as cultural markers. For instance, the consumption of yerba maté among Syrian residents and diaspora communities functions as a socio-cultural marker of identity and belonging [106]. In this light, mushrooms documented in the region we studied can likewise be interpreted as a cultural marker.

In the present study, women appeared to have greater knowledge regarding the collection of edible mushrooms. These findings are consistent with observations in various communities worldwide, where women are often the primary holders of ethnomycological knowledge [107]. This pattern has also been documented in other regions of Brazil [89]. However, Łuczaj and Nieroda [108] reported that in Poland, mushroom gathering and identification are not limited to one gender, reflecting the cultural importance of fungi in that context.

It is noteworthy that fungi most commonly consumed by the communities studied in Brazil correspond to the same or morphologically similar species traditionally consumed in Eastern Europe. Among them are Agaricus campestris, Boletus edulis, Macrolepiota spp. and Armillaria spp. In the current context of Poland, Ukraine, and other countries in Europe, studies reported a consistent pattern in the most commonly used mushroom taxa, including members of the Boletaceae family, as well as Russula spp., Lactifluus spp., Lactarius spp., and Cantharellus spp [5, 11, 14, 15, 109]. The absence of reports of certain taxa such as Russula spp., Lactifluus spp., Lactarius spp., and Cantharellus spp., which are frequently documented in European studies, may be related to the difficulty of finding these fungi in the study region. These taxa require more specific environmental conditions and are more commonly found in the southern part of the state of Santa Catarina or in Rio Grande do Sul [110]. Thus, the difficulty in finding representatives of such genera, combined with its absence from local use, may reflect an ongoing process of traditional knowledge erosion. An interesting parallel can be drawn with the consumption of Pleurotus spp. Kotowski et al. [16] also reported its use in Poland, a taxon not traditionally consumed, but which has gained popularity in recent decades due to its widespread commercial cultivation and frequent inclusion in contemporary culinary recipes.

Łuczaj and Nieroda [108] analyzed the preferences for mushroom species consumed by communities in southeastern Poland. They observed that although people in the region collect many fungal species, they do not consume all of the edible ones, even when those species are common. Among the edible species that are typically avoided are Amanita rubescens and Auricularia auricula-judae (Bull.) Quél. This pattern was not observed in the present study, where the consumption of Amanita rubescens and Auricularia spp. was documented. No particular preference or avoidance regarding these species was reported. One possible explanation for this pattern could be the limited availability of other known edible species. One participant compared A. fuscosuccinea to a pig’s ear, a comparison also reported in a rural community in the state of São Paulo [84]. The preparation methods were also similar: in the studied community, the species was documented as being cooked in broths, while in southeastern Brazilian rural communities, it was prepared with beans.

Although species similar to those in this study are still consumed in Eastern Europe, traditional preparation methods have changed over time [108]. This may help explain the difficulty in finding references to Zapraska in more recent studies despite its once being a traditional form of consumption. It is worth noting that other studies conducted in Slavic communities in Brazil [45, 111], such as in the municipalities of Irati and Prudentópolis, state of Paraná, indicate that local gastronomy seeks to preserve traditional recipes. The studies by Grechinski [111] and Ramos & Olinto [45] did not detail specific mushroom-based recipes but highlighted the relevance of these elements in the Slavic cuisine of the region. Grechinski [111] did not mention which mushroom species were used, nor whether they were foraged or purchased at markets, but the documented reports suggest that mushrooms remain alive in the community’s memory.

Whilst most reports of mushroom consumption in this study referred to immediate preparation after collecting, an interesting aspect observed was the mention of mushroom storage in three interviews, described in two distinct forms: (1) boiling followed by preservation in vinegar, similar to pickling, with no specific mention of the species used; and (2) boiling followed by freezing, specifically cited for Boletus edulis and Armillaria spp. Both practices show parallels with records from Ukrainian and Polish communities. While the custom of preserving mushrooms for winter use has deep traditional roots, Stryamets et al. [14] and Łuczaj and Nieroda [108] noted that storage techniques have evolved throughout the 20th century, with drying, pickling, and fermentation giving way to the predominance of freezing pre-boiled mushrooms. A similar pattern has also been observed in other European regions [5]. These patterns support the idea that traditional practices related to mushrooms are dynamic and influenced by technological and contextual factors.

A narrative also found in ethnomycological studies from European countries and documented in this research is the association of mushrooms with survival food [14, 15, 112]. In three interviews, participants mentioned that others would say mushrooms were consumed “because they were going hungry” or referred to them as “poor man’s meat”. In the present study, such comments may be linked to reports about the adaptation process of immigrants to the new environment, including reported cases of mushroom poisoning and even deaths, as described by Ramos & Olinto [45]. However, this stigma did not appear to cause shame among the interviewees regarding the practice itself. Still, it was suggested that such associations may contribute to the erosion of ethnomycological knowledge in the region or to the reluctance to speak openly about mushroom use within the community. A similar perception was reported by G.T. Prance, who noted that among various Indigenous groups in the Amazon, only the Yanomami regularly consumed mushrooms as food, while others regarded them as “survival food” [23].

The Brazil-Poland connection through mushrooms

At the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries, over 100,000 Poles immigrated to Brazil in a wave of emigration known in Poland as the ‘Brazilian Fever’ [31]. The period of Polish settler immigration to Brazil coincided with a 150-year period during which Poland did not exist as a sovereign country on the map. This period followed the loss of independence caused by the partitions and came before the restoration of Polish borders after World War I. During this time, traditional knowledge became especially important for Poles, as it played a crucial role in sustaining their national identity. For a population constantly subjected to Russification and Germanisation, preserving both cultural and linguistic traditions at home became particularly important [113]. As a highly mycophillic community, the use of wild mushrooms was also an important part of this carefully preserved culture [16, 85].

The case of Polish settlers who immigrated to South America is particularly interesting in the context of ethnobiology. These settlers were raised in a distinctly different natural and cultural environment, and were completely isolated from it upon relocation. The majority of the settlers came from farming families and had previously gained their knowledge of nature only in the area adjacent to their native villages. After travelling over 11,000 km to Southern Brazil, they were forced to apply this knowledge [41, 46]. Information obtained directly from the Polish settlers at the time indicated that their initial decisions regarding the use of mushrooms in their new surroundings were driven more by necessity than sentiment [45, 107]. Elements of nature in fungi that were familiar to them, to which they could most easily escape in times of scarcity, include species belonging to genera traditionally used in Poland, such as Agaricus spp., Auricularia spp., Armillaria spp., Lactarius spp., Macrolepiota spp., and Pleurotus spp [16]. This supports the theory that, as species with a more cosmopolitan distribution than plants [114, 115], fungi may serve as one of the first transmitters of traditional ecological knowledge among mycophilic communities.

The presence of known mushroom taxa and innate mycophilia further emboldened the settlers and prompted them to continue testing the local funga. The reported cases of poisoning are therefore somewhat painful evidence of the adaptation of a foreign culture to a new environment through the trial and error method well known in ethnobiology [116]. The need to rely on Polish traditional knowledge about mushroom use in Brazil also stemmed from the mycophobia prevalent among the Portuguese-descended population [117]. It is less likely that there was any exchange of indigenous ethnomycological knowledge, given the violent contact of white settlers and indigenous peoples in this region [76].

The relative isolation of Polish settlers has resulted in the preservation of cultural elements that are now disappearing from their native area. An example of this is Zapraska. This term comes from the word zaprażka used in the gradually disappearing Lviv dialect [75, 118]. In internationally accepted culinary terminology, it can be categorised as a ‘roux’, which is a mixture of flour and fat used to thicken sauces [119]. In the case of the community studied in this publication, ‘Zapraska’ refers to a mushroom sauce based on roux.

Conclusions

Various elements documented here indicate that ethnomycological knowledge in the region is closely linked to the immigration process and has been preserved through the sociocultural organization that prevailed from the formation of immigrant colonies in the area, even after more than three generations since the first immigrants arrived.

This knowledge is still present within the communities, and although it remains alive, there is also evidence of a process of traditional knowledge dynamism. While the findings reinforce a strong cultural tradition associated with Slavic peoples, this cultural component —the collection and consumption of mushrooms—is also tied to the local biodiversity. These findings suggest an adaptive process in which knowledge initially related to exotic species has gradually incorporated native species. Such a dynamic highlights the flexibility and cultural resilience, which involves mechanisms such as assimilation and integration.
