Heritable gut microbiota decreases fat accumulation in pregnant sows and optimizes gestational outcomes
Authors: Mingyu Wang, Lin Zhang, Songbai Li, Erqi Cha, Ao Guo, Boyuan Sun, Xinjian Li, Shengru Wu, Taiyong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the interplay between host genetics and gut microbiota in regulating fat deposition and reproductive outcomes in sows. Integrating multiomics data from 348 Yorkshire sows—including whole-genome sequencing, 16S rRNA sequencing, and metabolomic profiling—we identified 37 microbial taxa significantly associated with thick backfat (TBP) through microbiome-wide association studies (MWAS). Microbial genome-wide association analysis (mGWAS) revealed heritable genera, including Ezakiella and Corynebacterium, and implicated host genes such as MED17 and VSTM5 in microbial modulation. Concurrently, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) identified MC4R and MEDAG as candidate genes directly influencing TBP. Metabolomic analysis highlighted acetate, a short-chain fatty acid, as a key mediator in host adipogenesis. Functional validation in experimental models demonstrated that exogenous acetate supplementation alters lipid metabolism and enhances reproductive performance. These findings elucidate a mechanistic axis wherein host genetics shape gut microbial composition, which in turn modulates fat metabolism via acetate production, ultimately affecting pregnancy outcomes. This integrated approach provides insights into host–microbe co-regulation of metabolic traits and highlights potential targets for improving reproductive efficiency in livestock.Subject terms: Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Excessive fat deposition in adipose tissue leads to obesity and various serious health issues, such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and various cancers. Notably, maternal obesity not only increases the risk of pregnancy-related complications such as hypertensive disorders and gestational diabetes mellitus1,2 but also significantly adversely affects pregnancy outcomes3. These include a substantially increased likelihood of macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, and postpartum hemorrhage, as well as preterm birth, stillbirth, and congenital anomalies4,5. Pigs are well known for their high backfat deposition capacity. Notably, backfat thickness in pigs serves as a critical phenotypic trait that not only reflects body fat content, energy reserve status, feed efficiency, and meat quality but also functions as a key parameter for evaluating reproductive performance in pregnant sows. Consequently, modulating backfat deposition has become a key target for pig breeders when considering the high meat quality, feed efficiency in pigs, and high pregnancy outcomes in pregnant sows. Previous studies have suggested that this backfat thickness trait has high heritability (h2 = 0.4–0.6), making it a valuable selection criterion in swine genetic breeding6,7. However, current methods of genetic breeding for backfat thickness have been only partially successful because the phenotype represents a complex trait influenced by multiple factors. With the growth of the world’s population, coupled with increased consumption of high-quality meat driven by rising individual incomes and urbanization, there is an urgent need to identify the key factors affecting backfat thickness and to dissect in detail its regulatory roles in pregnancy outcomes8–11.

In recent years, several studies have supported the role of the commensal microbiota in the gastrointestinal tract in the regulation of metabolism and fat deposition in pigs6; therefore, the ability of the commensal microbiota to regulate pigs backfat deposition through manipulation of the gut microbiota has increased. In this context, it is increasingly recognized that the gut microbiome plays vital roles in the production of SCFAs, which provide metabolizable energy for the host, promote fat synthesis and deposition and thereby affect the backfat thickness phenotype in sows12. Moreover, the composition of the gut microbiota can also affect maternal nutrient absorption and energy metabolism while also regulating the host’s physiological state through its metabolic byproducts, which in turn impacts fetal growth and development, as well as pregnancy outcomes. Notably, growing evidence from other animal species has suggested that the host can affect the gut microbiota composition and metabolism processes. However, it is still unclear to a large extent whether the host’s genetics can influence the thickness of the backfat of sows in late pregnancy by regulating the stability of the gut microbiota or whether there is an interaction between the two that can affect the backfat thickness of sows in late pregnancy. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the interplay between host genetics and gut microbiota in regulating host fat deposition and pregnancy outcomes using an integrated multiomics approach.

Moreover, although human studies have shown a substantial correlation between maternal metabolic health during pregnancy and the gut microbial composition13, there is still limited information linking TBP status with changes in the gut microbiome and microbially produced metabolites, specifically with respect to fetal development and health outcomes in the offspring. To clarify the contributions of host genetics and the gut microbiota to fat accumulation in pregnant sows during late gestation, this study analyzed TBP phenotypes from 348 late-pregnancy Yorkshire sows, integrating genomic, metagenomic, and metabolomic sequencing data. The findings identified key microbial populations that regulate fat accumulation in these sows, along with heritable microbial species and variant loci influenced by host genes. This study seeks to offer insights for designing sustainable methods to regulate fat accumulation in pregnant sows in future research. The overall process of analysis is depicted in Fig. 1.

Methods

All the animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University (approval number: NWAFU-314021168). All operations were carried out in accordance with the university guidelines for animal research. All pigs were cared for and sacrificed following the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University (Yangling, China).

Animals and sample collection

A total of 348 purebred Yorkshire sows from Shaanxi Shunxin Pig Breeding Co., Ltd. were enrolled in this study. To minimize potential confounding effects and ensure cohort homogeneity, all animals were selected with comparable parity, gestation stage at sampling, and uniform housing conditions. Throughout the experimental period, sows were maintained under identical controlled environmental conditions and had ad libitum access to water and a standardized corn–soybean diet delivered via automated electronic feeders (Osborne Industries, USA). Detailed information on complete dietary composition is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Fecal samples were collected daily from experimental sows between 10:00 and 12:00 via induced defecation. All samples were obtained within two consecutive working days. The standardized protocol was as follows: rectal palpation was performed with sterile, single-use nitrile gloves to collect rectal content samples. Immediately after collection, the samples were transferred into sterile cryovials, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C prior to laboratory analysis. Seven days before parturition, the backfat thickness of the sows was measured, and tissue samples were collected. After sample collection, the samples were placed in 2 ml cryotubes and stored in liquid nitrogen. The host deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and gut microbial DNA were isolated via the Tiangen DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) and the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), respectively. The phenotypic indicators of reproductive performance included total number born (TNB), number born alive (NBA), litter birth weight (LBW), gestation length (GL), number of piglets born weak (NBW), number of still births (NSB), number of mummies (MUM), number of male piglets (NMP), number of female piglets (NFP) and TBP in late pregnancy. All the measured data were corrected and analyzed via SPSS 22.0. To detect specific microbes significantly affecting backfat thickness at the end of pregnancy, analysis of variance was employed to test the microbial differences between the sows with the highest backfat thickness (HBT ≥ 24 mm, N = 70) and those with the lowest backfat thickness (LBT ≤ 16.5 mm, N = 70). Finally, 20 sows were selected from the HBT group, and 20 sows were selected from the LBT group for targeted metabolomic analysis of their gut contents. All sows used in the experiment were housed in different pens within the same pig house, which was equipped with temperature- and humidity-controlled facilities such as water curtains. The temperature in the pig house was maintained at 20–23 °C, and the humidity was maintained between 65% and 80%.

Intestinal microbiota DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

A QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hamburg, Germany) was used to extract total genomic DNA following the manufacturer’s protocols14. The concentration and quantity of DNA were measured via a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA), and the DNA was subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 1 ng/μL with sterile water. 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequencing was performed by Shanghai Sangon Biotech on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (the equipment was purchased from Illumina, Inc., San Francisco, USA). The specific V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified via the designated primers (forward: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA; reverse: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) containing barcodes for sample identification. Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed in triplicate in a total volume of 30 μL. Each reaction consisted of 4 μL of primers, 30 ng of DNA template, 25 μL of PCR Master Mix, and molecular biology-grade water, as needed. The following PCR thermocycling procedure was used: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 98 °C for 45 s, 55 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. The PCR products were purified via Agencourt Ampure XP beads from Beckman Coulter, Inc. (Shanghai, China) and subsequently used for library construction. Finally, 250 bp paired-end sequencing of 348 samples was performed via the Illumina HiSeq/MiSeq platform at Shanghai Sangon Biotech (the equipment was purchased from Illumina, Inc., San Francisco, USA).

Host DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

Host DNA was isolated from each tissue sample via a Tiangen DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure successful DNA isolation, the concentration and purity of the DNA extract were measured via a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the completeness of the DNA sample was then assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

The host DNAs were sheared by sonication, and the fragmented DNA was then end-repaired, dA-tailed, ligated to Illumina paired-end adaptors and amplified via PCR with 500 bp inserts for library construction. After PCR amplification and purification, resequencing was performed via the Illumina HiSeq 2500 Sequencer (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. To minimize mapping errors, low-quality reads were removed via FastQC software (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), with the parameters supplied by Yan et al.15. The clean reads from each pig were aligned to the pig reference genome (the corresponding results of the self-assembled Yorkshire pig reference genome have not yet been published) via the Burrows–Wheeler alignment tool (BWA v. 0.7.15)16, with the default parameters. We subsequently used the Picard toolkit (v.1.119, http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) to sort the alignment results and remove potential PCR duplicate reads. The resulting alignments were indexed via SAMtools (v. 1.6)17 and processed according to the best practices for the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v.4.1.1)18. The GenomicsDBImport module was subsequently used to merge the population’s gVCF files, and finally, population genetic analysis was performed via genotype gVCFs to obtain the population VCF file. The population’s Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) and insertion‒deletion (InDel) variant dataset was filtered through hard filtering.

For the SNP dataset, quality control was performed via PLINK (v.1.9.0) software6 by excluding SNP loci with a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 0.05, a genotype call rate less than 0.05, an individual call rate <0.05, and a Hardy‒Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P value <0.000001. The –indep-pairwise 50 5 0.5 parameter was subsequently applied for linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning. A total of 434,862 SNP loci on 18 autosomes were ultimately retained for subsequent GWAS analysis.

For the InDel dataset, quality control was performed via PLINK (v.1.9.0) software with the following parameters: –geno 0.1, –mind 0.1, –maf 0.01. LD pruning was applied with a window size of 50 bp, a step size of 5 bp, and an LD r2 threshold of 0.5. Finally, a total of 616,390InDel loci on 18 autosomes were retained for subsequent GWAS analysis.

Identification of the specific microbiota associated with backfat thickness

Since fewer taxa at lower detection rates are formative for association analysis, we retained only taxa that were present in a specific sample type in >70% of the samples. The associations between the qualified taxa and Backfat thickness were analyzed via a two-part model with a customized R script as described by Fu et al.7. The second part of the quantitative analysis was only for the samples in which the specific microorganism was present. The details of the two-part model are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 119. p values were obtained from the two-part model association analysis and adjusted by the BH method. If the adjusted P value from the binary model was less than 0.05, the presence or absence of microorganisms could influence the backfat thickness. If the adjusted P value from the quantitative model was less than 0.05, reproductive performance was associated with the relative abundances of the microorganisms.

To detect specific microorganisms that significantly influence backfat thickness, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify key microbial species associated with pig backfat thickness phenotypes. Additionally, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to determine the relative abundance of each taxon between HBT and LBT pigs. A microorganism was considered significant if the adjusted P values from the two-part model association analysis, LEfSe and Wilcoxon rank-sum test were all <0.05.

The BLINK model in the GAPIT3 software package24 was utilized for genome-wide association analysis. Targeted metabolomics of feces

To investigate the differences in SCFA profiles between the HBT and LBT groups and elucidate their potential role in modulating the TBP phenotype in sows, we quantified SCFA levels in fecal samples via the following protocol:

Rectal content samples were subjected to aqueous extraction (2:1 water-to-sample ratio, v/w) followed by protein precipitation with 10% (v/v) phosphotungstic acid (Sigma‒Aldrich, Shanghai, China), as previously described27. 2-Ethylbutyrate (Sigma‒Aldrich) served as the internal standard for calibration. All the samples were analyzed in duplicate. Chromatographic data were acquired and processed via Open Lab Chem Station C.01.06 software (Agilent, Les Ulis, France), with SCFA concentrations expressed as micromoles per gram of wet feces (μmol/g).

To investigate the effects of key SCFAs on reproductive performance in mice, we conducted an intervention study with a grouped experimental design. The control group (n = 15) received 2 mL/kg distilled water daily via oral gavage, whereas the intervention group (n = 15) was administered 5% sodium acetate solution at the same dosage (2 mL/kg) (Sigma‒Aldrich, Shanghai, China). In Experiment 1 (estrous cycle intervention), after 15 consecutive days of gavage treatment, 7 mice were randomly selected from each group and were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation. Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest and absence of pedal reflexes. Tissues were then collected: we isolated and measured the following adipose tissues: inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), and brown adipose tissue (BAT). The remaining mice were subjected to Experiment 2 (mating experiment), where we evaluated reproductive performance indicators, including total number born (TNB) and litter birth weight (LBW). All the data were subsequently subjected to statistical analysis.

Results

Analysis of gut microbiota diversity, interactions, and potential functions

Through sequencing the 16S rRNA gene of the gut microbiota, we obtained a total of 121,173,319 raw reads. After quality control and merging, 87,885,079 clean reads were generated. Each sample contained at least 64,410 clean reads, with an average of 235,087 clean reads. Amplicon sequence variant (ASV) clustering was subsequently performed using a 97% similarity threshold. This study selected 140 sows in the later stages of pregnancy with HBT and LBT for microbiota diversity analysis. The HBT group contained 1468 ASVs, whereas the LBT group contained 1,542 ASVs.

We analyzed the α and β diversity of the gut microbiota, and the results revealed that the LBT group presented significantly greater Chao1 and Shannon indices than did the HBT group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2A, B). PCA further demonstrated a clear separation between the LBT and HBT groups (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that Yorkshire pigs in the LBT group presented significantly greater diversity and richness of the gut microbiota than those in the HBT group did during late pregnancy, indicating a more complex microbial environment. Additionally, the gut microbiota composition in the LBT group significantly differed from that in the HBT group.

The analysis of gut microbiota diversity at the phylum level revealed that the intestinal microbiota of Yorkshire sows in both the HBT and LBT groups during late pregnancy was predominantly composed of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, which collectively accounted for over 87% of the total intestinal microbiota. Additionally, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (F/B ratio) in the LBT group (1.9) was lower than that in the HBT group (2.0), further indicating that the maternal fat accumulation of sows in the HBT group during late pregnancy was greater (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Table 3). The analysis at the genus level also yielded similar results, with the gut microbiota of Yorkshire sows in both the HBT and LBT groups consisting primarily of Christensenellaceae_R_7_group, Lachnospiraceae, Muribaculaceae, Ruminococcus, Oscillospiraceae_UCG_002, Prevotella, and Treponema, among others. However, the relative abundance of different genera varied in the intestines of Yorkshire sows with different backfat thickness phenotypes. Notably, the “others” category accounted for 37.39% of the LBT group, which was significantly greater than the 8.35% in the HBT group, indicating that a larger number of low-abundance microbial species in the LBT group play crucial roles (Fig. 2E). To clearly understand the gut microbiome ecology of sows with different backfat thickness phenotypes, we constructed interspecies interaction networks for the HBT and LBT groups. As shown in Fig. 3F, G, the LBT group presented a more complex microbiome interaction network. We identified 9 key species with high core degrees (degree ≥8) in the LBT group, including Eubacterium_coprostanoligenes_group, Oscillospiraceae_NK4A214_group, Oscillospiraceae, Lachnospiraceae, Oscillospiraceae_UCG_002, Ruminococcus, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Oscillospiraceae_UCG_005, and Porphyromonas. In contrast, the HBT group identified Oscillospiraceae_UCG_002 as a key species with a high degree of correlation.

This study performed functional and pathway enrichment analyses of the gut microbiota and conducted differential analysis, the results of which are displayed in Fig. 3A, B. The gut microbiota of LBT Yorkshire sows was significantly enriched in several pathways, including the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, various types of N-glycan biosynthesis, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, apoptosis, the PI3K‒Akt signaling pathway, and carbon fixation pathways in prokaryotes, all of which were significantly enriched compared with those in the HBT group (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that the gut microbiota of the LBT group is more active in the immune response, signal transduction, carbon fixation, and apoptosis pathways than the HBT group is (p < 0.05). The functional enrichment analysis revealed that the gut microbiota of the LBT group was significantly enriched in multiple SCFA utilization and metabolism pathways, including malonate decarboxylase alpha subunit, decarboxylase gamma, adhesin transport system, and malonate decarboxylase beta subunit, which were significantly enriched compared with that of the HBT group (p < 0.01). These findings indicate that different gut microbiome environments modulate host phenotypes through metabolic pathways such as those involving SCFAs.

Key microbiota identified as influencing the TBP

The results of the MWAS analysis and meta-analysis based on various models are shown in Fig. 3C, D. The LDA model identified 30 key species, the Wilcoxon model identified 96 key species, the quantitative model identified 288 key species, and the binary model identified 785 key species. After meta-analysis, we defined the microbiota species identified by three or more models in the MWAS analysis as candidate key species. We ultimately identified 37 candidate key species, including Acinetobacter, Turicibacter, Christensenellaceae_R_7_group, Butyricicoccus, Oscillospiraceae, Ezakiella, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcaceae, Bacteroidales_p_2534_18B5_gut_group, Corynebacterium, and Ruminococcus (Supplementary Table 4).

Differential analysis of TBP phenotypes in individuals exhibiting extreme differences in the abundance of key microbial communities was performed. The results revealed that individuals with high abundances of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Acinetobacter, Ruminococcus, and Corynebacterium had lower TBP phenotypes, whereas those with high abundances of Peptostreptococcaceae, Ezakiella, Fusobacterium, and Bacteroidales presented higher TBP phenotypes. These reverse validation results confirm the reliability of our MWAS and meta-analysis findings and further demonstrate the crucial regulatory role of key microbiota in fat accumulation in the mother during the later stages of pregnancy (Fig. 3E–L; Supplementary Fig. 2).

Identification of key genes directly associated with the TBP

This analysis involved 348 individuals and focused on significant SNPs/InDels affecting the TBP. We identified 82 significant SNP loci across chromosomes 1, 2, 5, 11, and 18. Through colocalization analysis, we identified MC4R, FGF23, MEDAG, PTN, and DGKI as candidate genes that strongly influence TBP. Of particular interest is the region on chromosome 1, spanning from 160.704 Mb to 161.995 Mb, where strong LD was detected, with the MC4R gene situated within this LD block (Fig. 5A–C).

Contribution of host genetics, the gut microbiota, and metabolites to TBP regulation

SCFAs are the primary functional metabolites derived from gut microbial fermentation and exert more pronounced physiological effects than other microbial byproducts such as bile acids. To quantitatively assess the SCFA landscape in relation to host reproductive status, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling of intestinal contents. This analysis revealed significant differences in the abundance of nine SCFAs between groups: acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, isovaleric acid, 2-methylbutyric acid, hexanoic acid, and 4-methylvaleric acid. The analysis indicated that the concentrations of acetic acid, butyric acid, propionic acid, isobutyric acid and 2-methylbutyric acid were significantly greater in the LBT group than in the HBT group (p < 0.05). Specifically, the acetic acid concentration was substantially greater in the LBT group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5D–G). However, no significant differences in the levels of isovaleric acid, valeric acid, 4-methylvaleric acid or hexanoic acid were detected between the LBT and HBT groups (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To further investigate the associations between the differential SCFAs and key microbial species, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on five SCFAs and nine key microbial communities. The findings are illustrated in Fig. 5H. Research has revealed that the fecal abundance of acetic acid in late-pregnant sows is modulated by several microbial communities, including Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Bacteroidales_p_2534_18B5_gut_group, Peptostreptococcaceae, Turicibacter, and Ezakiella, with the most robust correlation observed between acetic acid and Peptostreptococcaceae (correlation coefficient of 0.72). Propionic acid levels are predominantly influenced by Turicibacter and Corynebacterium, with correlation coefficients of 0.63 and 0.65, respectively. Butyric acid is primarily controlled by Fusobacterium and Corynebacterium, with correlation coefficients of 0.90 and 0.82, respectively. The concentration of 2-methylbutyric acid is mainly associated with Ruminococcus and Acinetobacter, with correlation coefficients of 0.66 and 0.63, respectively. These findings suggest that metabolic byproducts serve as crucial conduits through which key host genes genetically associated with microbial communities associated with the host TBP phenotype exert regulatory effects on the host phenotype.

The relationship between host gestational outcomes and the TPB

The phenotypic data of host reproductive performance are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The analysis of reproductive performance revealed that Yorkshire sows with greater backfat thickness in the late gestation period had a significantly greater GL (p = 0.05). However, sows with lower backfat thicknesses presented greater values for TNB, NBA, LBW and NSB (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A–E). To further validate these findings, we analyzed the correlations among the reproductive performance of all 348 Yorkshire sows, as shown in Fig. 2F. There was a strong negative correlation between TBP and TNB, NBA and LBW, with correlation coefficients of −0.19, −0.17, and −0.19, respectively. A moderate positive correlation was observed between TBP and GL, with a correlation coefficient of 0.11. Notably, GL, LBW and TNB, which are strongly correlated with TBP, are among the most critical indicators in livestock production. These factors affect the production costs, economic benefits, and growth development potential of pigs. Therefore, we analyzed the correlation trends between these variables and the TBP. These results were consistent with our previous findings (Fig. 2G–I). The analysis results indicate that the TBP not only provides a reliable indicator of fat accumulation in the mother during late pregnancy but also serves as a key marker of the host’s reproductive performance.

In the final validation experiment, we investigated the relationships between key host genetics associated with gut microbes and TBP phenotypes. As shown in Fig. 6J, significant positive correlations (p < 0.05) were detected between TBP and Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcaceae, Ezakiella, and Bacteroidales, whereas significant negative correlations (p < 0.05) were detected with Ruminococcus, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Corynebacterium, and Acinetobacter. These findings were consistent with our previous analyses, further confirming the impact of the gut microbiota on TBP phenotypes.

Finally, to confirm that the key gut microbiota influences host adipose deposition through microbial metabolites, we integrated metabolomics data and identified acetate as the primary metabolite derived from these microbial communities. Consequently, we administered sodium acetate via oral gavage to mice and evaluated its effects on adipose accumulation and reproductive outcomes. The results of the sodium acetate supplementation experiment (Fig. 6K–M) demonstrated that acetate administration reduced adipose tissue deposition during the estrous cycle while moderately improving gestational outcomes in mice. On the basis of these findings, our study ultimately confirms the existence of a functional axis: host genetics—the gut microbiota—short-chain fatty acid (acetate)-reduced fat deposition—improves pregnancy outcomes.

Discussion

Backfat thickness is a key economic trait in pigs that significantly influences feed conversion efficiency, meat quality, and reproductive performance28–30. As such, it has garnered widespread attention from both industry and the academic community. Substantial evidence indicates that the gut microbiota modulates nutrient digestion, energy harvesting, and lipid metabolism—and consequently fat deposition—through the production of various bioactive metabolites, such as SCFAs, which act as signaling molecules or metabolic substrates. However, it remains unclear whether the highly heritable components of the microbial community directly regulate backfat thickness31–33. Although host genetics significantly shape the gut microbiota, it remains unclear whether this effect further influences backfat thickness via microbial metabolic phenotypes. The maternal environment primarily affects offspring gut microbiota during parturition through initial vaginal microbial colonization, similar to cesarean delivery exposure. In contrast, host genetics regulate microbial composition by modulating the intestinal microenvironment to form selective niches for specific taxa. This gene–microbe co-evolution is largely independent of external environments and distinct from maternal vertical transmission34,35. Furthermore, while several studies have suggested a link between sow backfat thickness and reproductive outcomes—particularly offspring growth traits—this relationship requires comprehensive validation under controlled experimental conditions36–38. To address these questions, this study selected 348 sows with similar physiological statuses that were raised under identical dietary conditions. We integrated multiomics data, including the gut microbiome, metabolome (covering key metabolites such as SCFAs), and genomic information. Through this integrated analytical approach, we aimed to systematically evaluate the heritable characteristics of the gut microbiota and their metabolic functions and to further investigate their potential mechanisms in regulating backfat thickness and reproductive traits.

In this study, we compared the gut microbiota composition between the HBT and LBT groups and identified 37 microbial taxa significantly associated with backfat deposition,including Fusobacterium, Bacteroidales_p_2534_18B5_gut_group, Peptostreptococcaceae, Turicibacter, and Ezakiella. Notably, prior studies have indicated a significant correlation between Fusobacterium and lipid accumulation and adipogenesis. Wu et al. suggested that this genus may modulate hepatic lipogenesis through the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway39. Furthermore, multiple studies have reported that Fusobacterium plays a critical role in various adverse pregnancy outcomes, including chorioamnionitis, preterm birth, stillbirth, neonatal sepsis, and preeclampsia40. Liang et al. further demonstrated altered reproductive tract microbiota in patients with chronic endometritis and endometrial polyps, with notable differences in microbial composition and abundance across the vaginal, cervical, and uterine environments41. While Lactobacillus predominates the female reproductive tract, its abundance varies in endometrial pathologies; conversely, an endometrial microbiota enriched with Staphylococcus, Gardnerella, Atopobium, Streptococcus, Peptostreptococcus, Chlamydia, Fusobacterium, and Acinetobacter has been closely linked to these conditions. On the other hand, Bacteroidales_p_2534_18B5_gut_group has been associated with feed conversion efficiency in pigs and may influence host performance by modulating nutrient absorption42. Similarly, certain strains of Turicibacter have been shown to affect host bile acid metabolism, thereby regulating lipid metabolic processes43. In summary, the key microbiota identified in this study as associated with the backfat thickness phenotype are likely to contribute to the regulation of host fat deposition through diverse mechanisms, exerting considerable influence on backfat-related traits.

We further identified genomic variations associated with indicator microorganisms and elucidated the relationships between microbial metabolism and TBP-related metrics, underscoring the potential utility of microbial intervention in modulating adipose deposition. To elucidate the mechanisms by which host genetics indirectly influences host phenotypes via the microbiota, we conducted a comprehensive GWAS on 37 microbes known to affect the host TBP phenotype. To bolster the accuracy of our findings, we supplemented our GWAS results with a colocalization analysis focused on SNPs/InDels. This rigorous approach led to the identification of several candidate genes that are pivotal in regulating microbial abundance, such as CEPT295, DEUP1, TAF1D, C9H11orf54, MED17, IZUMO1R, FUT4 and VSTM5. The FUT4 gene, for example, is notably expressed during the early stages of human embryogenesis. MED17 is associated with microcephaly and developmental delays in fetuses, whereas IZUMO1R is a key player in the fertilization process across various animal species. The roles of these genes substantiate our hypothesis that host genetics can exert indirect control over host phenotypes through intricate interactions within the microbiota44–46. Through metabolomic profiling of the high- and low-backfat thickness (HBT vs. LBT) groups, this study revealed significant differences in the levels of multiple SCFAs between the two phenotypes. Notably, acetic acid not only accounted for the highest proportion of total SCFAs but also exhibited the most pronounced intergroup difference. Previous studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota-derived acetic acid can modulate host lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, thereby alleviating metabolic-associated fatty liver disease47. Similarly, Hong et al. reported that several gut microbes significantly ameliorate high-fat diet-induced hepatic steatosis in mice through acetate metabolism48. In this study, correlation analysis further revealed a significant association between the abundance of key microbial taxa and acetic acid levels, providing empirical evidence that heritable microbes may influence host adipogenesis through acetate. These findings support a mechanistic hypothesis in which specific gut microbiota contribute to the regulation of host lipid synthesis and deposition—and potentially affect sow reproductive performance and gestational outcomes—by modulating metabolites such as acetic acid.

Finally, we established a link between the TBP trait and sow reproductive performance. The findings of the present study indicate that sows exhibiting an HBT phenotype are associated with reduced reproductive outcomes, such as lower TNB and NBA and an increased incidence of LBW, while concurrently displaying a longer GL phenotype. This counterintuitive relationship suggests that sows with better fat accumulation may experience poorer reproductive performance, suggesting potential overnutrition conditions. Two primary factors may contribute to these findings. First, overnutrition in the dam could lead to an inadequate oxygen supply within the uterus during gestation, causing fetal mortality and the birth of weak piglets3. Second, the mechanized approach to livestock production often initiates breeding in young sows, which are still in their growth phase. This competition for nutrients between the dam’s own growth and that of the fetus can result in insufficient nutrient transfer to the fetus, slowing their development and leading to lower birth litter weights and higher mortality rates49. Consequently, fetuses with suboptimal nutrition require extended periods to reach maturity, which is reflected in the longer gestation periods observed in sows with higher HBT. To avoid potential biases stemming from extreme variations in the TBP phenotype within individuals, we analyzed the correlation between TBP and reproductive phenotypes across the entire cohort of 348 individuals. A heatmap was generated to visualize these correlations. We subsequently focused on three pivotal phenotypes in pig production—GL, LBW and TNB—and conducted a trend analysis to assess their correlation with TBP. The findings were intriguing: a significant negative correlation was observed between TBP and both TNB and LBW, whereas a significant positive correlation was noted with GL. The analyses provided further validation of the conjecture we previously discussed.

On the basis of these findings, we propose that the host genetic background influences the abundance of specific microbes—including Ezakiella, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Turicibacter—which may modulate backfat thickness and further affect pregnancy outcomes through acetate production. To test this hypothesis, we administered acetate via oral gavage in a mouse model and evaluated its effects on adipogenesis and reproductive performance. The results demonstrated that acetate supplementation significantly inhibited fat deposition and concurrently improved total litter size and litter birth weight. These observations are consistent with the literature. Zhang et al. reported that Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group regulates glucose and lipid metabolism in mice through acetate production, thereby ameliorating metabolic disorders. These findings suggest that the gut microbiota–acetate axis may represent a promising therapeutic target for improving metabolic health50. Similarly, Liu et al. reported that microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids, particularly acetate, contribute to the suppression of obesity, further supporting the crucial role of microbial acetate in lipid metabolism50. Moreover, multiple studies have shown that acetate supplementation during gestation enhances reproductive performance in sows, improves piglet survival, and optimizes farrowing outcomes51,52, which aligns with the conclusions drawn in this study. Collectively, these lines of evidence support the mechanism proposed herein: host genetics shape specific microbial communities that modulate fat deposition and pregnancy outcomes via acetate metabolism, providing a novel theoretical basis for nutritional strategies aimed at improving reproductive traits.

In summary, our study demonstrated that both host genetics and gut microbiota variations regulate maternal fat accumulation in late pregnancy. While most microbial changes indirectly affect nutrition through environmental factors, a subset interacts directly with the host genotype to modulate phenotypic outcomes. We identified 37 microbial species significantly associated with the late-pregnancy TBP phenotype, including nine key host genetics-related microbes, along with numerous significant SNP loci and candidate genes. These results highlight the collaborative role of host‒microbiota interactions in determining late-pregnancy fat accumulation. Notably, we identified direct genetic regulators of the TBP phenotype (MC4R, FGF23, MEDAG, PTN, and DGKI). Through integrated metabolomic analysis, we systematically characterized the collective influence of host genome‒microbiome‒metabolome interactions on late-pregnancy nutrition. These findings provide mechanistic insights into maternal nutritional regulation and may inform sustainable strategies for managing pregnancy nutrition in livestock.
