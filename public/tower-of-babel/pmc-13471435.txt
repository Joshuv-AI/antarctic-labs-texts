Effects of mindfulness-based intervention combined with aerobic training on Kinesiophobia and heart rate variability after percutaneous coronary intervention: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qifang Mi, Liangmei Jiang, Min Leng, Haifeng Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of mindfulness-based intervention (MBI) combined with aerobic training on treatment adherence, kinesiophobia (an excessive fear of movement), heart rate variability (HRV), exercise tolerance, and psychological stress response in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) post-percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsPost-PCI patients (n = 150) were randomly assigned to an intervention group (receiving combined MBI and aerobic training) and a control group (receiving aerobic training only). Treatment adherence, the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia-Heart (TSK-SV Heart), HRV parameters, exercise tolerance, and psychological stress responses were assessed before and after the intervention. Psychological assessments included the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Repeated-measures mixed analysis of variance was used to evaluate changes in outcomes between groups and across time points.ResultsA total of 150 post-PCI patients were enrolled, including 97 males and 53 females, with a mean age of 50.79 ± 6.83 years. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups (P > 0.05). After the intervention, the intervention group showed significantly greater improvements than the control group in treatment adherence, HRV indices (standard deviation of all normal-to-normal RR intervals, standard deviation of the average normal-to-normal RR intervals in all 5-minute segments, and percentage of successive normal-to-normal RR intervals that differ by more than 50 milliseconds), exercise tolerance (6-minute walk test, peak oxygen uptake, and anaerobic threshold oxygen uptake), as well as reductions in kinesiophobia scores, anxiety and depression scores (SAS, SDS) (p < 0.001).ConclusionAn adapted MBI combined with aerobic training can significantly improve treatment adherence and exercise tolerance, while reducing kinesiophobia and psychological stress in post-PCI patients. This combined rehabilitation strategy may provide additional benefits beyond conventional aerobic rehabilitation alone.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) ranks among the primary causes of death across the globe. With advances in interventional techniques, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become the standard treatment for CHD, significantly improving both patient survival and quality of life [1, 2]. However, despite the efficacy of PCI in resolving acute cardiac events, the postoperative rehabilitation process remains fraught with challenges. In particular, patients often require a prolonged recovery period in which it is essential not only to improve cardiac function but also to rebuild exercise tolerance, alleviate kinesiophobia, regulate psychological stress, and gradually resume normal daily activities [3–6].

During the rehabilitation phase after PCI, several factors may negatively affect recovery outcomes. Key factors influencing rehabilitation include insufficient exercise tolerance, presence of kinesiophobia, reduced heart rate variability (HRV), and mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression [7]. Inadequate exercise tolerance often results in fatigue during daily activities, negatively impacting quality of life. Kinesiophobia, defined as an excessive and irrational fear of physical movement or activity due to concerns about discomfort, cardiac strain, or adverse cardiovascular events, can reduce participation in rehabilitative exercise programs [8]. Meanwhile, HRV, an important indicator of autonomic nervous system function, is closely associated with cardiovascular risk, with lower HRV indicating increased cardiovascular risk [9]. Furthermore, psychological factors such as anxiety and depression may reduce patients’ motivation and willingness to actively participate in rehabilitation training, thereby negatively affecting adherence to conventional cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs [10, 11]. Poor adherence to CR may further compromise long-term rehabilitation outcomes in patients following PCI.

Although traditional cardiac rehabilitation methods such as aerobic training have shown benefits in improving cardiopulmonary function and exercise capacity, they often fail to adequately address the physiological and psychological challenges experienced by patients [12, 13]. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), originally derived from mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) approaches developed by Kabat-Zinn [14], emphasize nonjudgmental awareness of present-moment experiences and have been increasingly applied in chronic disease rehabilitation and psychological management. Previous studies have shown that mindfulness-related interventions may help alleviate anxiety and depression, improve autonomic nervous system regulation, and enhance rehabilitation adherence in cardiovascular patients [15, 16].

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of an adapted mindfulness-based intervention combined with aerobic training on treatment adherence, kinesiophobia, HRV, exercise tolerance, and psychological stress response in patients following PCI. To reduce potential bias caused by individual and clinical differences among patients, a randomized controlled design was used to more reliably evaluate the effectiveness of the combined intervention. By integrating psychological and physical rehabilitation strategies, this study seeks to provide a more comprehensive approach for cardiac rehabilitation practice.

Materials and methods

Baseline characteristics

This study was designed as a randomized controlled trial. From October 2023 to January 2025, a total of 150 patients diagnosed with CHD who had undergone successful PCI were consecutively recruited from the Department of Cardiology of our hospital during their hospitalization. Eligible participants were randomly assigned using a random number table to either intervention or control group. All participants underwent baseline assessment prior to randomization. The control group received aerobic training-based cardiac rehabilitation, while the intervention group received a combined intervention of MBI and aerobic training. The research received approval from the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: confirmed diagnosis of CHD, successful completion of PCI, absence of contraindications to rehabilitation exercise, stabilization of vital signs, andwillingness to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria included severe organic disease, malignancy, or cognitive impairment.

Based on previous research [17], the estimated effect size (Cohen’s d) for the self-rating depression scale (SDS), the primary outcome, was 0.54. Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1, with α of 0.05 (two-tailed) and 1-β of 0.80, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 108 participants. To account for potential attrition and unforeseen variables, a total of 150 patients were ultimately enrolled to ensure adequate statistical power and result reliability.

Intervention

All participants received standard pharmacological treatment after PCI according to current clinical guidelines, with medication regimens adjusted based on individual clinical condition. Rehabilitation was initiated during hospitalization after patients achieved clinical stability following PCI. Before participation in the rehabilitation program, all patients underwent routine baseline evaluations conducted by the multidisciplinary rehabilitation team, including assessments of cardiac status, exercise tolerance, vital signs, and psychological condition. The assessment results were used to determine rehabilitation eligibility and formulate individualized rehabilitation prescriptions, including exercise intensity and progression schedules tailored to each patient’s clinical condition and functional capacity. The rehabilitation program was initiated during hospitalization and continued as a structured home-based rehabilitation program after discharge. Both groups participated in rehabilitation training three times weekly for eight consecutive weeks.

Conventional aerobic cardiac rehabilitation

Patients in the control group received aerobic exercise–based cardiac rehabilitation. Each training session began with a 5-minute warm-up consisting of joint mobility exercises and light full-body movements, including shoulder, knee, and ankle rotations, as well as low-intensity gait activities to improve muscle and joint flexibility and reduce exercise-related injury risk.

This warm-up phase was followed by 40 min of aerobic exercise, including brisk walking or cycling. During the initial two weeks, exercise intensity was maintained at 50%–60% of the patient’s peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak), and was gradually increased to 60%–70% according to the patient’s recovery condition and exercise tolerance. Each session was divided into two exercise sets separated by a 5-minute rest interval. Finally, patients completed a 5-minute flexibility and relaxation session to facilitate muscle recovery and circulation.

Heart rate was continuously monitored throughout training to ensure exercise safety and maintain exercise intensity within individual tolerance ranges. Exercise intensity and progression were adjusted according to heart rate response, exercise tolerance, patient-reported fatigue symptoms, and perceived exertion during rehabilitation training.

Mindfulness-based intervention (intervention group)

In addition to the aerobic rehabilitation protocol described above, patients in the intervention group received additional MBI delivered by a multidisciplinary rehabilitation team consisting of cardiologists, psychological counselors, nutritionists, physical therapists, and certified mindfulness instructors. All personnel received standardized training before study initiation to ensure consistency of intervention delivery. The mindfulness program was designed to improve emotional regulation, psychological adaptation, and autonomic balance during cardiac rehabilitation. The mindfulness intervention included the following components:

Mindful breathing: Patients were instructed to remain seated in a quiet and comfortable environment while focusing attention on the sensation of breathing. Each session lasted 10–15 min and was performed 2–3 times daily. Sessions lasted 15–20 min once daily to enhance body awareness and promote mind–body relaxation.Loving-kindness meditation: Patients were guided to cultivate positive emotional awareness toward themselves and others while maintaining attention on breathing. The above frequencies and durations represented the recommended mindfulness practice schedule during the rehabilitation period, and patients completed the intervention according to individual tolerance and clinical condition. Mindfulness practice participation and home-based rehabilitation completion were monitored through rehabilitation practice logs and follow-up assessments during the intervention period. During hospitalization, mindfulness training was conducted in a designated rehabilitation setting under professional supervision. After discharge, patients continued structured home-based mindfulness practice for 8 weeks.

Post-discharge follow-up

Following discharge, all participants continued home-based rehabilitation according to the prescribed rehabilitation protocol. Nursing staff conducted telephone follow-ups every two weeks to assess clinical status, exercise adherence, psychological well-being, and compliance with mindfulness practice. Patients were instructed to maintain rehabilitation practice logs during the home-based rehabilitation period. Individualized rehabilitation guidance was provided according to follow-up findings when necessary.

Outcome measures

Rehabilitation-related compliance behavior was assessed using a self-developed questionnaire administered to both groups. The questionnaire was developed by the research team based on current cardiac rehabilitation guidelines and previously published rehabilitation compliance assessment frameworks. Content validity was evaluated by a multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of cardiologists, rehabilitation specialists, and nursing experts before study initiation. The questionnaire included four dimensions: medication adherence (6 items), exercise participation (4 items), rehabilitation engagement (5 items), and routine follow-up compliance (5 items) during the study period. Each item was scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (poor compliance) to 5 (excellent compliance), resulting in a maximum possible total score of 100. Higher scores indicated better rehabilitation-related compliance behavior. The internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire in the present study was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.785). Objective rehabilitation adherence was additionally evaluated based on completion of prescribed cardiac rehabilitation sessions during the intervention period. The prescribed cardiac rehabilitation program consisted of 24 rehabilitation sessions during the 8-week intervention period. Patients completing ≥ 80% of prescribed rehabilitation sessions were considered adherent.

Kinesiophobia was evaluated using the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia-Heart (TSK-SV Heart) [18], a 15-item scale covering four domains: fear of injury, perceived decline in physical function, avoidance of physical activity, and perceived cardiac risk. Items were scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with reverse scoring for items 4, 8, and 12. A total score > 37 indicated clinically significant kinesiophobia. A higher score indicates a greater level of kinesiophobia.

HRV was assessed using an electrocardiographic analyzer (Model: ECG1200G; Contec Medical Systems, Qinhuangdao, Hebei, China) after five minutes of seated rest in a relaxed state, prior to PCI. Parameters included the standard deviation of all normal-to-normal RR intervals (SDNN), the standard deviation of the average normal-to-normal RR intervals in all 5-minute segments (SDANN), and the percentage of successive normal-to-normal RR intervals that differ by more than 50 milliseconds (PNN50).

Exercise tolerance was measured using a cardiopulmonary exercise testing system (Model: CARDIOVIT CS-200 Ergospiro; Hanfei Medical Technology, Shanghai, China) to obtain anaerobic threshold oxygen uptake (VO2AT) and VO₂peak. The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) was also conducted to assess functional exercise capacity [19]. Patients completed a 6-minute walk test, during which the total walking distance was recorded to assess exercise tolerance and cardiopulmonary endurance.

Psychological stress response was assessed using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the SDS [20]. Each scale contains 20 items rated on a 4-point scale, yielding total scores between 20 and 80. Scores below 50 on the SAS and below 53 on the SDS were regarded as normal, while higher scores reflected more severe symptoms of anxiety or depression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality of continuous data. Comparison of psychological stress response

Repeated-measures mixed ANOVA revealed significant time effects and significant group × time interaction effects for both SAS and SDS scores (all P < 0.001). Psychological stress responses were significantly alleviated after intervention in both groups; however, the intervention group showed greater reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms compared with the control group (Table 7).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the effects of a combined intervention involving MBI and aerobic exercise on treatment adherence, kinesiophobia, HRV, exercise capacity, and psychological stress responses (anxiety and depression) in patients with CHD after PCI. The results demonstrated that the combined intervention significantly outperformed conventional aerobic training alone across multiple dimensions of rehabilitation. Notably, the combined approach was superior in enhancing treatment adherence, alleviating kinesiophobia, improving exercise tolerance, increasing HRV, and relieving anxiety and depression symptoms. These findings provide important practical implications for cardiac rehabilitation, particularly highlighting the value of integrative psychological interventions in promoting the overall physical and mental recovery of post-PCI patients.

Compared to previous study [13], this research proposes a novel theoretical perspective by demonstrating the significant advantages of combining MBI with aerobic training in improving various rehabilitation outcomes among post-PCI patients. Existing research has consistently confirmed the benefits of aerobic training in improving cardiovascular health and physical fitness in patients with CHD, especially post-PCI, where conventional aerobic training has been widely recognized for enhancing exercise capacity, cardiopulmonary function, and cardiovascular health [21, 22]. However, while aerobic exercise remains the cornerstone of cardiac rehabilitation, it has shown relatively limited effects in addressing psychological concerns such as kinesiophobia and in promoting exercise engagement, which may ultimately affect long-term recovery outcomes and quality of life [23, 24]. A meta-analysis by Taylor et al.concluded that aerobic training effectively improves exercise capacity, quality of life, and reduces recurrent cardiac events in CHD patients [25]. These conclusions align with the current study’s findings, further validating the key role of aerobic training in post-PCI cardiac rehabilitation. Nonetheless, existing studies have also pointed out that aerobic training alone does not fully address the psychological barriers encountered during rehabilitation, such as kinesiophobia, anxiety, and depression, which often limit patients’ exercise participation and consequently hinder optimal rehabilitation outcomes [24, 26, 27].

Therefore, a key innovation of this study is the first-time integration of MBI with aerobic training in the context of cardiac rehabilitation among post-PCI patients [28]. MBI, a psychological intervention strategy, has been shown to be effective in mitigating anxiety and depression, and in improving overall psychological well-being [29]. Our study further confirms the positive effects of MBI in enhancing emotional regulation, boosting psychological resilience, and promoting exercise engagement in cardiac rehabilitation patients. In particular, MBI helped reduce kinesiophobia and increased patients’ willingness to participate in exercise, which in turn improved treatment adherence and exercise tolerance. This intervention effectively compensates for the limitations of conventional aerobic training, especially in terms of emotional and psychological regulation, showcasing its unique advantages.

Kinesiophobia is common among cardiac patients, particularly after PCI, due to concerns about physical stress and cardiac function. As a result, patients may avoid participating in rehabilitation exercises, negatively impacting recovery [30]. This study found that MBI plays a critical role in reducing kinesiophobia through emotional regulation and relaxation techniques. Mindfulness meditation, breathing exercises, and body scanning interventions help patients accept physical discomfort and reduce anxiety and worry during physical activity [29]. These findings are consistent with Abdul et al.‘s study, which reported that mindfulness interventions effectively reduced kinesiophobia and improved psychological health in cardiac patients, promoting better exercise engagement and recovery [28].

HRV is widely used as a physiological marker of autonomic nervous system balance in cardiac rehabilitation research [31, 32]. A decrease in HRV is often associated with cardiac events, heart failure, and poor clinical prognosis [33, 34]. Improving HRV enhances autonomic function, strengthens cardiac self-regulation, and reduces the risk of recurrent cardiac events [9]. This study found significant improvements in HRV indices (such as SDNN, SDANN, and PNN50) following intervention, suggesting that MBI can enhance cardiac adaptability by modulating the autonomic nervous system, particularly by enhancing parasympathetic activity. Meanwhile, conventional aerobic training contributed to increased HRV by improving cardiopulmonary function and promoting circulation. These findings indicate that the combination of MBI and aerobic training not only supports emotional regulation and exercise participation but also facilitates overall recovery by improving autonomic function.

Despite demonstrating the positive effects of the combined intervention in post-PCI cardiac rehabilitation, this study presents a few limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and restriction to a single research center may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should be conducted in multiple centers and across different geographic regions with larger samples to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of this intervention in broader populations. Secondly, the duration of follow-up was comparatively brief, limiting the ability to thoroughly assess the long-term outcomes of the intervention on cardiac function and overall quality of life. Future research should incorporate extended follow-up periods to better evaluate the sustained effects of the intervention and its influence on the recurrence of cardiovascular events. In addition, this study did not conduct subgroup analyses based on clinical characteristics such as age, sex, or disease duration. Since different patient groups may respond differently to the intervention, future research could explore personalized intervention strategies to enhance precision and efficacy. With the advancement of precision medicine, designing individualized interventions based on factors such as age, gender, disease course, and psychological state may further improve the effectiveness of cardiac rehabilitation and patient quality of life. Moreover, combining pharmacological treatment and intensified exercise regimens with other therapeutic approaches to explore the synergistic effects of multimodal interventions could offer new directions for future cardiac rehabilitation research. Such integrative strategies may optimize current rehabilitation protocols and provide patients with more personalized and comprehensive treatment options, thereby enhancing overall recovery outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the combination of mindfulness-based stress reduction and aerobic training provides significant therapeutic benefits in the cardiac rehabilitation of post-PCI patients. The intervention effectively improves treatment adherence, reduces kinesiophobia, enhances heart rate variability, increases exercise capacity, and promotes psychological well-being. This integrated approach holds promising clinical value and may offer new insights and strategies for future cardiac rehabilitation practices.
