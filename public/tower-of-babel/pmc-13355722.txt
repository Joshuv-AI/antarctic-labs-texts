Shared Genetic Architecture and Multidimensional Modifiable Correlates between Myopia and Retinal–Optic Nerve Diseases
Authors: Yuelan Gao, Jianming Xu, Jun Yu, Yuzhou Zhang, Haoyu Chen, Mary Ho, Ka Wai Kam, Alvin L. Young, Chi Pui Pang, Clement C. Tham, Jason C. Yam, Li Jia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to characterize the shared genetic architecture and modifiable correlates between myopia and retinal–optic nerve diseases (RONDs).DesignGenetic pleiotropy analysis and population-based cohort study.ParticipantsA total of 81 491 UK Biobank participants and summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies were included.MethodsWe examined associations between myopia and 5 common RONDs using population-based analyses of prevalent and incident outcomes, followed by cross-trait genetic analyses, locus-level annotation, colocalization, and enrichment analyses. Among myopic individuals, modifiable correlates and gene–environment interactions were evaluated, and Mendelian randomization was used as complementary evidence for selected associations.Main Outcome MeasuresMyopia and RONDs.ResultsThe primary findings were that, in baseline prevalent analyses, myopia was associated with higher risks of retinal detachment (RD) and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), but a lower risk of primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG), with evident risk gradients across myopia severity, especially for RD. Diabetic retinopathy (DR) risk estimates were <1.0 across myopia severity categories but were not statistically significant. A U-shaped association was observed between refractive status and age-related macular degeneration, with elevated risk in both high myopia and hyperopia. Incident analyses during follow-up showed broadly consistent patterns. As supportive genetic evidence, cross-trait analyses revealed significant genetic correlations and overlaps between myopia and RONDs, identifying 66 pleiotropic loci and 115 candidate genes, with enrichment in immune–inflammatory, receptor-mediated signaling, and retinal developmental pathways. Among myopic individuals, exploratory analyses further showed that prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidity was associated with various modifiable correlates spanning health status, lifestyle, diet, mental health, sleep patterns, and medication use, and that 7 environmental factors interacted with 5 pleiotropic variants. Mendelian randomization analyses provided complementary and hypothesis-supporting evidence consistent with a positive association of myopia with RD and an inverse association with PACG, whereas findings for DR and POAG required more cautious interpretation because of pleiotropy or heterogeneity.ConclusionsThis study reveals a shared genetic architecture and identifies modifiable correlates linking myopia to five common RONDs. These findings provide new insights into shared susceptibility patterns and offer a framework for future mechanistic, validation, and prevention-oriented research in myopic populations.Financial Disclosure(s)The author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.

Myopia is one of the most common ocular disorders worldwide, typically developing during childhood and adolescence.1 In individuals with high myopia, which is usually caused by excessive axial elongation, the risk of ocular complications increases substantially. In many countries, the prevalence of myopia among adults ranges from 10% to 30%, whereas in young populations of East and Southeast Asia, it can reach 80%–90%.2 The increasing prevalence of myopia underscores the need for early diagnosis and effective management strategies to control its progression and prevent associated ocular complications.3

Retinal–optic nerve diseases (RONDs) are major causes of irreversible vision loss and represent a significant public health concern.4 Common RONDs include age-related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), retinal detachment (RD), primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), and primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG).5 Accumulating evidence suggests that myopia is accompanied by structural remodeling of the retina and optic nerve, which may overlap with pathological processes involved in RONDs.6, 7, 8 While previous studies have reported epidemiological associations between myopia and several RONDs,9,10 uncertainties remain regarding the magnitude, shape, and underlying pathways and correlates of these associations across different diseases and populations. Furthermore, systematic multitrait analyses are lacking, and the specific mechanisms and risk factors underlying their comorbidity remain unclear.

Both myopia and various RONDs have complex etiologies involving genetic modifiers, environmental factors, and gene–environment (G×E) interactions.11, 12, 13 Understanding the shared genetic architecture and modifiable correlates may help inform future prevention-oriented research, lifestyle modification strategies, and mechanistic investigations related to myopia and RONDs.13 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered numerous genetic variants associated with ocular diseases.14 Building on these findings, cross-trait analyses can leverage GWAS signals to identify pleiotropic loci across multiple conditions, thereby highlighting potential targets for interventions that could simultaneously affect several diseases.15 Nevertheless, a thorough understanding of the shared genetic and environmental correlates connecting myopia and RONDs remains limited.

In this study, we conducted an integrative analysis linking myopia with RONDs across epidemiological, genetic, and environmental dimensions. We treated the epidemiological associations and genome-wide genetic correlation analyses as the primary components of the study, the Pleiotropic Analysis under Composite Null Hypothesis (PLACO)/Genetic analysis incorporating Pleiotropy and Annotation (GPA) and colocalization analyses as supportive genetic evidence for shared susceptibility, and the analyses of modifiable correlates, G×E interplay, and Mendelian randomization (MR) as complementary or exploratory components. Our aims were to characterize the shared susceptibility architecture linking myopia to RONDs and to identify candidate correlates and interactions that could inform future mechanistic and prevention-focused research.

Methods

Individual-Level Data from the UK Biobank Cohort

The UK Biobank is a population-based prospective cohort study of >500 000 adults aged 40–69 years recruited across 22 assessment centers.16 Only individuals with complete refractive measurements, diagnostic information, and genotype data passing quality control were included in this study. To ensure genetic homogeneity, only participants of European ancestry were included.17 Ultimately, a total of 81 491 individuals were included, of whom 27 355 were myopic, and this subset was further used to assess modifiable exposures and G×E interactions. Ethical approval was obtained from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (REC reference: 21/NW/0157), and the study was conducted under UK Biobank application number 91320, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnosis of Myopia and RONDs

In the UK Biobank, refractive status was measured using the RC-5000 device (Tomey). We excluded refractive results labeled unreliable and calculated the mean sphere and cylinder for the left and right eyes, respectively. The mean spherical equivalent (MSE) refractive error, calculated as sphere + (0.5 × cylinder), was used to define myopia, with myopia classified as MSE ≤ –0.50 D.18 For severity-stratified analyses, myopia was categorized using refractive error as mild (> –3.0 to –0.50 D), moderate (> –6.0 to –3.0 D), and high myopia (≤ –6.0 D), with nonmyopia as the reference group. We defined cases of RONDs, including AMD, DR, POAG, PACG, and RD, based on (1) International Classification of Diseases 10 and International Classification of Diseases 9 diagnosis codes, (2) responses to the touchscreen question “Eye problems/disorders,” and (3) self-reported noncancer illness (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).19 For each ROND, participants were classified as cases if they met the corresponding definition in any available source. International Classification of Diseases 9 and International Classification of Diseases 10 codes were obtained from linked hospital inpatient records available up to October 1, 2023. Information on previous cataract surgery or refractive surgery was obtained from self-reported medical history in the UK Biobank. Prevalent ROND cases were defined as participants diagnosed with the corresponding ROND at or before baseline enrollment. Incident ROND cases were defined as newly diagnosed cases occurring after baseline among participants free of the corresponding ROND at baseline. Follow-up began at baseline and continued until the diagnosis of RONDs, death, lost to follow-up, or October 1, 2023, whichever occurred first.

GWAS Data Sets

Genome-wide association studies summary statistics were obtained from publicly available datasets of European ancestry, and only studies with a sample size >50 000 were included to ensure sufficient statistical power (Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Genome-wide association studies data for myopia were derived from the VA Million Veteran Program,20 while GWAS data for the 5 RONDs and diabetes were obtained from the UK Biobank and FinnGen.21, 22, 23 All GWAS studies had received ethical approval and obtained informed consent from participants. Prior to analysis, all data sets were harmonized and alleles aligned.

Epidemiological Associations between Myopia and RONDs

To evaluate the relationship between myopia and RONDs, logistic regression was used to examine associations with prevalent RONDs at baseline, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations with incident RONDs during follow-up after excluding participants with the corresponding ROND at baseline. Thus, the epidemiological analyses included both prevalent and incident ROND outcomes. All epidemiological models were adjusted for age, sex, Townsend Deprivation Index, education, smoking, body mass index, drinking, diabetes, hypertension, assessment center, and the first 10 principal components. Restricted cubic spline models were employed to evaluate potential nonlinear relationships between MSE and risk of RONDs. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the findings, including exclusion of participants with ≤2 years of follow-up to reduce potential reverse causation, additional adjustment for cancer, and exclusion of participants with previous cataract surgery or refractive surgery.

Genetic Correlation and Overlap

Genome-wide genetic correlations between myopia and RONDs were estimated using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) and high-definition likelihood. The LDSC intercept was also used to assess potential sample overlap between the GWAS data sets. Because genetic correlation reflects the average shared genetic signal across the genome, we further examined overall genetic overlap between traits using GPA.15 Genetic analysis incorporating Pleiotropy and Annotation complements LDSC and high-definition likelihood by evaluating whether 2 traits show excess overlap in associated variants and by estimating model parameters relevant to pleiotropy and annotation enrichment. Accordingly, discordance between LDSC/high-definition likelihood and GPA is possible, particularly when shared effects are confined to a subset of loci or vary in direction across the genome.24,25 Trait pairs showing GPA overlap but nonsignificant LDSC correlation should therefore be interpreted cautiously. A Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.01 (0.05/5) was used.15

Identification and Colocalization of Pleiotropic Loci

For trait pairs exhibiting significant genetic correlation or overlap, pleiotropic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were identified using PLACO, a method based on the composite null framework.26 We scored all variants based on their squared z-scores and removed single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with exceptionally high Z2 values (>80). In addition, due to the potential correlation between the 2 traits, the correlation matrix of z-scores was calculated and taken into account. The final pleiotropic P values were determined by testing the null hypothesis of no pleiotropy using the α-level cross-unit test. Variants with P < 5 × 10–8 were considered significantly pleiotropic. Because PLACO detects shared statistical association with 2 traits, the identified loci were interpreted as indicating statistical pleiotropy rather than definitive evidence of independent horizontal biological pleiotropy. Subsequently, the Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) platform was employed to annotate and localize potential pleiotropic loci. Based on these loci, Bayesian colocalization analysis was conducted to identify shared causal variants. Loci with a posterior probability of hypothesis 4 > 0.5 were deemed colocalized.27

Enrichment and Functional Analysis of Pleiotropic Genes

Using the pleiotropic loci identified by PLACO, we conducted gene-level multimarker analysis with Multimarker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation implemented in the FUMA platform to identify candidate pleiotropic genes. Candidate genes included those physically mapped within ±10 kb of significant SNPs or functionally annotated to them, and genes were considered significant if they reached a Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05. Using genotype–tissue expression data (GTEx v.8) integrated in FUMA, tissues exhibiting significant enrichment of trait heritability were identified. Cell-type specific expression analysis was then conducted via the online WebCSEA platform (https://bioinfo.uth.edu/webcsea) to determine the specific cell types in which pleiotropic genes are highly expressed across 1355 cell types, with a P value reaching the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 3.69 × 10–5 (0.05/1355) being considered statistically significant. Significant genes were further annotated to pathways and biological processes using the Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases. Finally, to validate the role of pleiotropic loci in retinal development, the expression trajectories of candidate genes were examined using transcriptomic data from developing human retinas (GSE98370).19

Gene–Environment Interaction Analysis of Pleiotropic Variants in UK Biobank

To further investigate the additional effects of candidate pleiotropic genetic variants on risk of RONDs in individuals with myopia, G×E interaction analyses were conducted using genetic and phenotypic data from the UK Biobank. The analysis included 77 modifiable environmental exposures covering 3 health status indicators, 2 socioeconomic factors, 11 dietary intake variables, 4 air pollutant measures, 2 medication-related variables, 2 alcohol consumption indicators, 4 physical activity–related measures, 4 tobacco exposure variables, 32 mental health–related indicators, 3 traffic-related air exposure measures, 7 sleep pattern–related indicators, and 3 sun exposure variables (Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). First, logistic regression models (1: prevalent comorbidity cases; 0: healthy controls) were applied to assess the associations between each environmental exposure and risk of RONDs among myopic individuals. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Environmental exposures showing significant associations (PFDR, FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05) were subsequently selected for multi-interaction analyses with the candidate pleiotropic genetic variants:logitP(Y|G,E)=β0+βGG+βEE+βInteractG×Ewhere Y represents myopia-RONDs comorbidity, E denotes exposure, G indicates the additively coded genotype of a pleiotropic SNP (values 0, 1, or 2), and βInteract stands for the interaction coefficient quantifying G×E effects.17 Gene–environment interaction effects at each locus were corrected for multiple testing using the FDR method to identify significant interactions. All models were adjusted for age, sex, assessment center, and the top 10 principal components to control for potential confounders.

MR

To further evaluate potential causal relationships between myopia and RONDs, 2-sample MR analyses were performed. The sources of GWAS summary statistics were as described earlier. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms strongly associated with the exposure (P < 5 × 10–8) were selected as instrumental variables. To ensure independence among instruments, linkage disequilibrium clumping was conducted using PLINK software (r2 < 0.001, clumping window = 10 000 kb), and SNPs with F-statistics <10 were excluded to minimize weak instrument bias. The primary MR analysis was conducted using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method under a multiplicative random-effects model. To account for potential pleiotropy and provide robust estimates, complementary MR methods were applied, including MR Egger regression, weighted median, and mode-based approaches (simple and weighted mode). We further evaluated heterogeneity across SNP-specific causal estimates using Cochran Q test, and directional horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR–Egger intercept. Lastly, we conducted sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out analysis and visualization of scatter, funnel, and leave-one-out plots, to assess the influence of individual SNPs.

All analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-sided raw or adjusted P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Results

Epidemiological Associations between Myopia and RONDs

This study included 81 491 participants (mean age 57.5 ± 5.7 years; 54.6% female), of whom 27 355 (33.6%) had myopia (Table 1). To comprehensively characterize the epidemiological relationships between myopia and RONDs, we evaluated associations with both prevalent RONDs at baseline and incident RONDs during follow-up. Myopia was analyzed both as a binary variable and as a continuous measure of MSE to capture its severity, with lower MSE values indicating higher myopia severity (Fig 2A; Table S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In adjusted models, myopia was positively associated with RD (odds ratio [OR] = 6.01, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 3.22–11.92, P < 0.001), POAG (OR = 2.10, 95% CI = 1.60–2.74, P < 0.001), and AMD (OR = 1.57, 95% CI = 1.05–2.33, P = 0.026), but negatively with PACG (OR = 0.18, 95% CI = 0.03–0.60, P = 0.019). The mean spherical equivalent showed opposite associations, being negatively associated with RD (OR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.72–0.81, P < 0.001), POAG (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.83–0.89, P < 0.001), and AMD (OR = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.86–0.96, P = 0.005), but positively with PACG (OR = 1.39, 95% CI = 1.20–1.61, P < 0.001). Interestingly, although binary myopia status was not significantly associated with DR (OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.49–1.12, P = 0.167), lower MSE values, corresponding to greater myopia severity, were nominally associated with lower DR risk (OR = 1.08, 95% CI = 1.01–1.17, P = 0.039). Incident analyses during follow-up yielded results broadly consistent with the baseline prevalent analyses (Table S5, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Associations between Myopia Severity and RONDs

Compared with nonmyopic participants, RD risk increased progressively with myopia severity (mild: hazard ratio [HR] = 1.71, 95% CI = 1.39–2.11; moderate: HR = 3.16, 95% CI = 2.54–3.93; high: HR = 4.88, 95% CI = 3.77–6.32; all P < 0.001). Primary open-angle glaucoma risk was higher in moderate and high myopia (moderate: HR = 1.48, 95% CI = 1.05–2.09, P = 0.024; high: HR = 2.01, 95% CI = 1.27–3.16, P = 0.003), whereas the mild myopia association was not statistically significant (HR = 1.21, 95% CI = 0.92–1.60, P = 0.172). Age-related macular degeneration risk was mainly concentrated in high myopia (HR = 1.70, 95% CI = 1.37–2.11, P < 0.001). Primary angle-closure glaucoma showed a strong inverse association in mild and moderate myopia (mild: HR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.15–0.50, P < 0.001; moderate: HR = 0.10, 95% CI = 0.02–0.39, P = 0.001), while the high-myopia estimate was unstable due to sparse events. Accordingly, the high-myopia PACG estimate should be interpreted cautiously. To further explore potential nonlinear relationships between myopia severity and ROND risk, we performed restricted cubic spline analysis (Fig 2B–F). The results revealed a significant U-shaped nonlinear association between MSE and AMD risk (P for nonlinearity <0.001), with the lowest risk near emmetropia and elevated risk at both high myopia and hyperopia sides. The mean spherical equivalent was linearly and inversely associated with POAG risk (P for nonlinearity = 0.490), indicating that greater myopia severity was associated with a higher risk of POAG. A nonlinear decreasing relationship was observed for RD (P for nonlinearity = 0.034), with risk rising sharply beyond moderate-to-high myopia. The mean spherical equivalent was linearly and positively associated with PACG risk (P for nonlinearity = 0.801), indicating that higher hypermetropia is associated with higher PACG risk. No significant nonlinear association was found for DR (P for nonlinearity = 0.637).

Stratified Analyses

Participants were stratified using a 50-year cutoff, representing the transition from middle to older age when many disease risks increase. Age-stratified analyses revealed that the positive association between myopia and RD risk was significantly stronger in the younger cohort than in the older cohort (P < 0.001) (Fig S1; Table S7, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Conversely, the inverse association between MSE and AMD risk was more pronounced in the younger cohort than in the older cohort. Furthermore, sex-stratified analyses demonstrated that the protective effect of MSE against AMD was significant in males but not in females (Fig S1; Table S7).

Shared Genetic Factors between Myopia and RONDs

We further evaluated the genetic correlations between myopia and 5 RONDs for their shared genetic components and potential mechanistic links. Linkage disequilibrium score regression analysis revealed significant positive correlations of myopia for RD and POAG and a negative correlation for PACG, with LDSC intercepts indicating no significant confounding (P > 0.05; Table S8, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org; Fig 3A), supporting the reliability of these estimates. High-definition likelihood analyses showed consistent patterns, further validating the genetic correlations. Notably, although neither AMD nor DR exhibited genetic correlations with myopia in LDSC, GPA detected significant genetic overlap between myopia and both AMD and DR (Fig 3B). Collectively, these results highlight a complex pattern of shared genetic architecture between myopia and RONDs. Pleiotropic Loci between Myopia and RONDs

We then examined the 5 myopia–ROND trait pairs to identify pleiotropic loci. Pleiotropic Analysis under Composite Null Hypothesis identified 5546 potential pleiotropic SNPs (Fig 3C; Table S9, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), from which FUMA defined 66 independent genomic risk loci (Fig 3D): 12 for myopia–PACG, 15 for myopia–RD, 7 for myopia–POAG, 20 for myopia–AMD, and 12 for myopia–DR (Fig S2; Table S10, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). These loci were mapped to 64 chromosomal regions (45 unique), including 1 locus shared across 4 trait pairs (12q13.2 for myopia–AMD, DR, and RD), 4 loci shared across 3 trait pairs (6p22.3 for myopia–POAG, RD, and DR; 13q32.3 for myopia–PACG, POAG, and DR; 15q14 for myopia–PACG, RD, and DR; and 16p13.3 for myopia–RD, DR, and AMD), and 8 loci shared between 2 trait pairs (Table S11, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). These loci indicate broad patterns of shared statistical association across myopia–ROND pairs, with 12q13.2 emerging as a recurrent region of interest.

Among the 66 pleiotropic loci, 36 lead SNVs (60%) showed concordant allelic directions between myopia and RONDs, whereas 30 (40%) were discordant, suggesting heterogeneous patterns of shared genetic effects across trait pairs (Table S12, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Notably, all 12 SNVs for Myopia–PACG were discordant, and all 15 for Myopia–RD were concordant, while SNVs for Myopia–AMD, Myopia–POAG, and Myopia–DR displayed mixed patterns. These results indicate heterogeneous genetic relationships between myopia and different RONDs, with the most consistent effects for RD and pronounced inverse effects for PACG.

Pleiotropic Genes and Functional Enrichment of Myopia–ROND Pairs

We then assessed the pleiotropic genes shared across the 5 myopia–ROND trait pairs (Fig 4A). Using Multimarker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation, we identified 115 candidate pleiotropic genes located within or overlapping the 66 pleiotropic loci shared between myopia and RONDs (Table S14; Figs S5 and S6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), including 41 for myopia–AMD, 35 for myopia–DR, 14 for myopia–PACG, 4 for myopia–POAG, and 21 for myopia–RD. Seven genes were identified in 2 trait pairs, while 5 genes were shared across 3 pairs. Among these, ORMDL2, RDH5, RP11-644F5.10, and RP11-762I7.5 were pleiotropic across myopia–AMD, myopia–PACG, and myopia–RD, whereas PCCA was shared among myopia–PACG, myopia–DR, and myopia–POAG. Retinal Developmental Expression of Shared Pleiotropic Genes

We further examined the potential roles of the pleiotropic genes in retinal development (Figs S9 and S10, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Among the 115 pleiotropic genes, 57 showed significant differential expression across embryonic, fetal, and adult retinal tissues, including 1 gene shared across 3 trait pairs and 5 genes shared across 2 trait pairs (Table S16, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). RDH5, shared by myopia–AMD, myopia–PACG, and myopia–RD, was significantly upregulated in fetal versus embryonic retina and remained upregulated in adult retina relative to both embryonic and fetal retina, suggesting a potential role in retinal maturation. BMP3, shared by myopia–RD and myopia–PACG, displayed a bell-shaped pattern, increasing from the embryonic to fetal stage and decreasing in adulthood. CD55 and KCNQ5, shared by myopia–RD and myopia–PACG, were downregulated in adults, whereas CD63 was upregulated relative to the fetal stage. PRSS56, shared by myopia–DR and myopia–PACG, was expressed at low levels during embryogenesis but upregulated from the fetal to adult stages. These stage-specific expression patterns are likely to reflect developmental regulation across retinal maturation. They support the potential relevance of shared pleiotropic genes to retinal development, although the drivers of these expression differences cannot be determined from the present data alone.

Multidimensional Modifiable Correlates for RONDs in Myopic Patients

Among individuals with myopia, we evaluated the associations between modifiable factors and prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidity (Fig 5A; Table S17, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Health status indicators were significantly associated with increased AMD (diabetes and hypertension), DR (diabetes, hypertension, and obesity), and POAG (diabetes) risk. Lifestyle factors associated with prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidity included drinking (increasing AMD, RD, and DR risk), physical activity (lowering DR risk), smoking and passive smoking (increasing DR and AMD risk), and longer outdoor time (raising AMD and POAG risk). Several dietary factors were associated with prevalent AMD, DR, and POAG, including fresh fruits, cooked vegetables, processed meats, oily fish, and dried fruit. Mental health influenced only DR, with loneliness, anxiety, and depression increasing DR risk, while satisfaction with economic status and overall health was protective. Sleep-related traits were associated with different prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidities, including daytime nap (increased AMD, DR, and POAG risk), daytime dozing (increased AMD and DR risk), sleeplessness (increased AMD), excessive sleep (increased POAG risk), and early rising (protective for AMD). In addition, aspirin use was positively associated with AMD, DR, and POAG. MR Analyses of Myopia and RONDs

Mendelian randomization analyses provided complementary evidence for the direction of association between myopia and selected RONDs (Fig 5C; Table S19, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Specifically, genetically predicted myopia was inversely associated with PACG risk (IVW: β = –0.64, P = 0.001) and positively associated with RD risk (IVW: β = 0.68, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant associations were observed for AMD, DR, or POAG across the IVW, MR–Egger, weighted median, and mode-based methods (all P > 0.05). However, the MR–Egger intercept suggested directional horizontal pleiotropy for DR (intercept = 0.06, P = 0.030), indicating that the null result for myopia–DR should be interpreted cautiously, because pleiotropic bias cannot be excluded (Table S20, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Significant heterogeneity was observed for AMD, DR, and POAG, whereas no significant heterogeneity was observed for PACG or RD (Table S20; Fig S11, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). For AMD and POAG, despite the observed heterogeneity, the complementary MR methods remained nonsignificant and were broadly consistent with the IVW results.

Discussion

Leveraging large-scale UK Biobank data and GWAS summary statistics, we systematically characterized the links between myopia and 5 RONDs across epidemiological, genetic, and environmental dimensions. The primary findings were the epidemiological associations and genome-wide genetic correlation patterns, which together showed heterogeneous but structured relationships between myopia and different RONDs, with the clearest evidence for RD, POAG, and PACG. The PLACO/GPA and colocalization analyses provided supportive genetic evidence for shared susceptibility, identifying 66 pleiotropic loci and 115 shared genes, including BMP3, LAMA2, RDH5, and LINC00862, implicating immune–inflammatory processes, receptor-mediated signaling, and retinal development. Among individuals with myopia, the analyses of modifiable correlates and G×E interactions should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, although they help identify candidate behavioral and environmental domains for future investigation. Collectively, our findings support heterogeneous shared susceptibility patterns underlying myopia–ROND co-occurrence and provide a framework for future mechanistic, validation, and prevention-oriented research.

Myopia has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple RONDs. Epidemiological studies have consistently reported that myopia increases the risk of POAG and RD, while appearing protective against DR and PACG.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 Our findings largely corroborate these observations. Similarly, the MR results for PACG and RD were directionally consistent with previous MR studies,35, 36, 37 and therefore serve mainly as complementary evidence within the present integrative framework.

In contrast, the observational and MR findings for myopia and POAG were not fully concordant in this study. While myopia was associated with higher POAG risk in epidemiological analyses, MR did not support a statistically significant direct causal effect. This discrepancy may reflect residual confounding, limited MR power in the presence of heterogeneity, or pathways mediated through intermediate ocular traits such as intraocular pressure or optic disc morphology. Clinically, myopic individuals should still be considered at elevated risk for POAG and monitored accordingly, even though the direct causal contribution of refractive error remains uncertain.38

The association between myopia and AMD is complex. Some studies have suggested a protective effect of myopia against AMD, whereas others have reported increased risk in older individuals with high myopia.9,39 In our study, MSE showed a U-shaped association with AMD risk, with elevated risk at both the high-myopia and hyperopia ends of the spectrum. The increased risk in hyperopic eyes may reflect mechanisms distinct from those in highly myopic eyes, potentially related to shorter axial length. Hyperopic eyes are generally smaller and may have a thicker and stiffer sclera, which could increase resistance to choroidal venous outflow, reduce choroidal perfusion, and impair metabolic exchange across the retinal pigment epithelium and Bruch’s membrane complex, thereby promoting drusen-related degenerative change.40,41 However, axial length was not available in the present analysis, so we could not determine whether this association was independent of ocular biometry. Further studies using precise axial length measurements, standardized control definitions, and diverse populations are needed.

The MR findings for DR require cautious interpretation because the MR–Egger intercept suggested directional horizontal pleiotropy, indicating that pleiotropic bias cannot be excluded. Significant heterogeneity was also observed for AMD and POAG, although the complementary MR methods remained nonsignificant and were broadly consistent with the IVW results. Overall, these findings do not support clear direct causal effects of myopia on DR, AMD, or POAG, but they also do not definitively exclude such effects.

Given the established epidemiological associations between myopia and 5 RONDs, we investigated their relationships and underlying mechanisms from a genetic perspective. We identified significant genetic correlations and overlaps, uncovering 66 candidate pleiotropic variants, including 5 loci not previously reported: 4q21.3 (shared susceptibility locus for myopia and AMD), 10q22.3 (risk-enhancing locus for myopia and RD), 12q24.12 and 13q32.3 (shared susceptibility loci for myopia and DR), and 19q13.33 (protective for myopia but conferring risk for PACG). Although these loci are novel in the GWAS of myopia or RONDs, the nearby genes have been implicated in diverse biological pathways.42 AFF1 at 4q21.3 is involved in lipid metabolism and redox balance,43 KCNMA1 at 10q22.3 regulates neuronal signaling,44 SH2B3 at 12q24.12 participates in insulin–glucose signaling and immune regulation,45 PCCA at 13q32.3 is essential for amino acid degradation and lipid metabolism,46 and CABP5 at 19q13.33 modulates calcium signaling in retinal photoreceptors.47 Our pathway analysis revealed that genes shared between myopia and RONDs are predominantly enriched in immune–inflammatory processes, receptor-mediated signaling, and retinal development. In particular, genes such as APOE, CFH, and HTRA1 are involved in immune regulation and complement activation that may contribute to retinal degeneration in myopia–AMD,48 whereas BMP3, LAMA2, and RDH5 are implicated in neuronal signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and photoreceptor maintenance relevant to myopia–RD.49,50 While immune dysregulation has been implicated separately in AMD39 and glaucoma,40 our findings suggest that immune, metabolic, and neuronal regulatory pathways may converge across myopia and multiple RONDs, highlighting candidate biological pathways for future investigation.

Importantly, the pleiotropic loci identified by PLACO should be interpreted as loci showing shared statistical association between myopia and RONDs. Pleiotropic Analysis under Composite Null Hypothesis does not distinguish independent horizontal biological pleiotropy from effects mediated through myopia, linkage disequilibrium between distinct causal variants, or other sources of apparent overlap.51 This consideration is particularly relevant for loci such as LAMA2, RDH5, BMP3, and LINC00862, which are strongly associated with myopia and may influence ROND risk partly through myopia-related ocular remodeling.52 Therefore, these loci should be viewed as candidate regions of shared susceptibility rather than definitive evidence of direct shared disease mechanisms.

Myopia may lead to complications, including certain RONDs, which can result in irreversible vision loss over time.53 However, according to our observations, most existing studies have focused on the associations and prediction of myopia-related complications, while systematic investigations into modifiable correlates of these complications remain limited.9,10,54 In our study, we systematically investigated modifiable correlates of RONDs in individuals with myopia, providing a reference for the prevention and management of myopia-related complications. Our findings suggest that metabolic disorders, lifestyle, diet, psychological health, and sleep patterns were associated with prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidity in myopic individuals, although the directions of association varied across exposures and outcomes. These findings may help prioritize multidomain factors for future prevention research in myopic individuals at risk of RONDs, although causal interpretation remains premature. These associations should be interpreted cautiously because the modifiable factor analyses were based on prevalent myopia–ROND comorbidity. For some variables, particularly diet, aspirin use, mental health, and sleep-related traits, reverse causation is possible, as these factors may partly reflect postdiagnosis behavioral changes, treatment patterns, or broader health consequences rather than causal antecedents.

Gene–environment interactions can help delineate the boundaries between genetic and environmental influences and identify individuals who are more susceptible to risk exposures or more likely to benefit from preventive and therapeutic interventions.55 Gene–environment interactions have been demonstrated in myopia and various RONDs, suggesting that these conditions arise from complex interplay between innate genetic factors and environmental influences.56, 57, 58 To our knowledge, studies systematically investigating G×E interactions in individuals with myopia who develop RONDs are lacking. Our findings suggest that in individuals with myopia, certain genetic susceptibilities may influence the risk of specific RONDs through interactions with lifestyle, psychological, and health-related factors, providing exploratory evidence for differential susceptibility that warrants further investigation. Although several significant G×E interactions were identified, their clinical significance remains uncertain. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require replication in independent datasets before any clinical implications or prevention-oriented applications can be considered.

This study has several limitations. First, the analyses were restricted primarily to individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations, particularly East and Southeast Asian populations that bear a high burden of myopia. Because the epidemiology and phenotype distribution of several RONDs differ across ancestries, especially for PACG and POAG, and because East Asian populations also bear a higher burden of high myopia and related macular complications, the genetic correlations, pleiotropic loci, and modifiable associations identified here require independent validation in East Asian and other non-European cohorts. Second, although we applied multiple approaches to correct for sample overlap and potential biases, the observed genetic associations remain at the statistical level and require further validation through functional experiments. Third, the generally limited evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy based on the MR–Egger intercept does not resolve the interpretational scope of PLACO findings, because MR–Egger and PLACO address different inferential questions. Fourth, although participants with previous cataract surgery or refractive surgery were not excluded from the primary analyses, sensitivity analyses excluding these individuals yielded similar results. Nevertheless, some degree of refractive misclassification cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, environmental exposures and related G×E analyses were based on measures obtained at or around UK Biobank assessment in adulthood. These measures may not fully capture etiologically relevant exposures during childhood or adolescence, when myopia typically develops, and their effects may not be equivalent across life stages. Sixth, PACG was a low-prevalence outcome in this cohort, and the small number of myopic PACG cases limited statistical power, particularly in severity-stratified analyses. As a result, the corresponding estimates, especially in the high-myopia group, may be imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously. Seventh, although continuous MSE suggested an inverse association with DR, this pattern was not statistically significant across myopia severity subgroups and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In addition, diabetes duration and glycemic control are important potential confounders in the myopia–DR relationship and were not included in the present analyses. Eighth, the MR analysis for DR showed evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy, and the MR analyses for AMD, DR, and POAG showed significant heterogeneity. Therefore, the null MR results for these outcomes should be interpreted cautiously and do not definitively exclude possible causal effects. Ninth, the G×E analyses were exploratory and involved a large number of statistical tests. Although FDR correction was applied, false-positive findings remain possible, and the identified interactions require independent replication. Finally, despite including a wide range of environmental exposures, the G×E interaction analyses may have overlooked some unmeasured or unknown key environmental factors.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides integrative evidence linking myopia to 5 common RONDs through heterogeneous epidemiological associations and shared genetic architecture. The identified pleiotropic loci and candidate genes, including BMP3, LAMA2, RDH5, and LINC00862, provide supportive evidence for shared susceptibility and highlight priorities for future mechanistic investigation in myopia-related RONDs. The MR findings should likewise be interpreted as complementary and hypothesis-supporting rather than definitive, particularly for DR and POAG, where pleiotropy or heterogeneity limits causal interpretation. The findings for modifiable correlates and G×E interactions should be interpreted as exploratory, but may help prioritize candidate domains for future prevention research. Extrapolation to East Asian and other non-European populations should be made with caution, particularly for PACG and high myopia-related disease. Further multiethnic validation, replication, and functional studies are needed to clarify the generalizability and clinical relevance of these findings.
