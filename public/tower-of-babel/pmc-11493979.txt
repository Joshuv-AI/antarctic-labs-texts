A causal role of the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in random exploration
Authors: Armin Toghi, Mojtaba Chizari, Reza Khosrowabadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Decision to explore new options with uncertain outcomes or exploit familiar options with known outcomes is a fundamental challenge that the brain faces in almost all real-life decisions. Previous studies have shown that humans use two main explorative strategies to negotiate this explore-exploit tradeoff. Exploring for the sake of information is called directed exploration, and exploration driven by behavioral variability is known as random exploration. While previous neuroimaging studies have shown different neural correlates for these explorative strategies, including right frontopolar cortex (FPC), right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), there is still a lack of causal evidence for most of these brain regions. Here, we focused on the right DLPFC, which was previously supported to be involved in exploration. Using the continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) and Horizon task on twenty-five healthy right-handed adult participants, we showed that inhibiting rDLPFC did not change directed exploration but selectively reduced random exploration, by increasing reward sensitivity over the average reward of each option. This suggests a causal role for rDLPFC in random exploration, and further supports dissociable neural implementations for these two explorative strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76025-5.

Introduction

Deciding whether to explore new options with uncertain outcomes or use familiar options with known outcomes is a fundamental challenge that the brain faces in almost all real-life decisions. A familiar option such as choosing our favorite food has a high immediate reward compared to choosing a new food we have never tested before. However, adaptive behavior requires exploring new options to increase the probable future reward1. Thus, in a dynamic environment, there has to be a balance between choosing a familiar option with a known expected reward (Exploitation) and choosing new options to test their probable future reward (Exploration). This balance, commonly referred to as the exploration-exploitation tradeoff1,2.

Previous studies have identified two primary approaches that humans utilize to address the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. These approaches can be classified into two groups: direct and random exploration2. Direct exploration involves choosing an option that is more informative about underlying values3. This process needs a comparison between the exploration and exploitation of expected values4. Exploitation expected values (referred to as ‘expected reward’), is a predicted short-term payoff, while exploration expected values (referred to as ‘future expected reward’) estimate the long-term value of choosing the option with higher information bonus4–6. Meanwhile, random exploration occurs when there is stochasticity in decisions7, or when there is complete uncertainty regarding the available options5,8, potentially leading an agent to select a suboptimal choice. This explorative behavior is commonly implemented as random decision noise, which interferes with the optimal decision process of choosing an option with the highest expected reward4.

In recent years, there has been converging evidence showing dissociable neural implementation underlying these two explorative strategies in the human brain. Previous neuroimaging studies showed that the right frontopolar cortex (rFPC) and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) drive information-seeking and directed exploration5,9–12, while the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rDLPFC) drives random exploration5,9. This has been attributed to model-based decision-making process in rFPC10–12 and dACC13,14 when gaining information is beneficial15 or when there is relative uncertainty regarding available options5, and value-based random exploration in rDLPFC when there is total uncertainty across action options5. Moreover, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) over the rFPC decreases directed exploration while intact random exploration12. However, there is no direct causal evidence for the involvement of the rDLPFC exclusively for random exploration.

The rDLPFC has been central to various cognitive control functions16. This includes facilitating exploratory behaviors17 by accumulation of uncertainty18, or encode the immediate and future value of choice options, before making a decision19,20, and it also associated with risk-taking and impulsivity21–24. However, brain stimulation techniques over right DLPFC yielded mixed results in decision-making tasks25. While some studies showed that inhibiting rDLPFC decreases impulsivity21, others support increasing risk-taking behavior23,26. Given the established role of the rDLPFC in impulsivity, risk-taking, and exploratory behavior, studies have not yet converged on operational definitions of the relation between these cognitive control functions and the role of the rDLPFC.

To investigate the role of rDLPFC in different explorative strategies, we used continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) to selectively inhibit the rDLPFC in 25 (11 female, mean age: 23.84, Sd: 2.76) healthy participants performing 192 games (three 64-game blocks) of the Horizon task (see Figs. 1 and 2).Figure. 1 The horizon task. participants engage in decision-making between two armed bandits that offer probabilistic rewards. In the Horizon task, participants make choices between two slot machines that pay probabilistic rewards drawn from different Gaussian distribution2. Participants are instructed to maximize their rewards, but can only identify the best option by first exploring both machines12,15.

This task is designed to effectively distinguish between directed and random exploration by manipulating two key factors that influence exploratory choices2,12: (1) the amount of information (uncertainty) available about each option before making a decision, which is controlled through forced-choice trials, and (2) the decision horizon, which determines the number of choices participants can make, implemented through short and long horizon conditions in the free-choice trials.

In the forced-choice trials, participants were instructed which option to play. In that sense, the experimenter could control over the information (uncertainty) each participant has before making a free choice. This creates an “unequal information” condition [1 3] (i.e., one option plays three times and the second option plays once), and an “equal information” ([2 2]) condition (i.e., two options play in each bandit)4. This manipulation ensures that participants receive information about each option independent of its mean payout, allowing us to minimize the reward-information confound on the first free-choice trial12 (see Supplementary Fig. 1). In other words, on the first free-choice trial, there is minimal correlation between reward and information12, and we could attribute the decision to information seeking or behavioral variability without reward-information confound. However, after the first free-choice, participants tend to favor more rewarding options, leading to a rapid confounding of reward and information12 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, we focus our analysis on the first free-choice trial to avoid this confounding factor.

Following the forced-choice trials, participants engage in either one (short horizon, h1) or six (long horizon, h6) free-choice trials. In long horizon condition, participants make more exploration than short horizon condition27, probably because gaining information is more valuable if it can be used in the future2, or from another perspective, in a longer horizon participants could compensate less-rewarding early explorative choices, in later trials28.

Based on a previous neuroimaging study that highlights the role of rDLPFC in random exploration in total uncertainty conditions5, we hypothesized that rDLPFC suppression leads to a decrease in random exploration. Meanwhile, we don’t expect to see changes in directed exploration, as previous literature that tries to disentangle neural correlates of information-seeking from behavioral variability failed to find rDLPFC activation5,29,30.

Results

In the Horizon task, directed and random exploration can be identified through either a simple behavioral analysis (i.e., model-free approach) or computational modeling of decision parameters (i.e., model-based approach). In the model-free approach, directed exploration is calculated as the probability of choosing the high-informative option, denoted as p(high info), in the [1 3] condition, while random exploration is reflected by the probability of selecting the low-mean reward option, denoted as p(low mean), in the [2 2] condition (Fig. 1). However, this approach may not fully account for other influential factors, such as spatial bias. To address this concern, we applied an established logistic model to examine exploration-related parameters more thoroughly, including information bonus, decision noise and spatial bias (See Table 1)12. Model-free analyses

First free-choice trial analyses

For model-free analysis, we conducted two repeated measure ANOVA (for direct and random) with the horizon, TMS condition as within-subject factors, and order, and gender as between-subject factors. In both directed and random exploration, we observed a significant main effect of the horizon condition (for directed exploration, F(1,21) = 7.43, p = 0.01, η2p = 0.19; for random exploration, F(1,21) = 16.83, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.33). This was attributed to the higher accuracy observed in horizon 1 (M = 0.73, SD = 0.04), compared to horizon 6 condition (M = 0.66, SD = 0.02).

In random exploration, we also observed a significant effect of the stimulation condition (F(1,21) = 9.73, p = 0.005, η2p = 0.06). However, such an effect was not evident in directed exploration (F(1,21) = 0.31, p = 0.58, η2p = 0.002). Post hoc analyses revealed that the changes in random exploration were driven by changes in p(low mean) in both horizon 1 (paired t-test, p = 0.003, d = 0.69) and horizon 6 (paired t-test, p = 0.046, d = 0.44) conditions (Fig. 3). First free-choice trial analyses in first two blocks

In our task, participants completed a total of 192 trials (three blocks of 64 trials), which typically took 50 min to complete. This duration could potentially lead to disengagement from the task, and previous studies have suggested that random exploration might be influenced by disengagement or boredom15. Additionally, this duration might exceed the window of the cTBS effect. To address these concerns, we conducted additional analyses on the p(low mean) and the p(high info) within the first (1–64), second (64–128), and first two blocks (1-128).

The results revealed a significant reduction in random exploration within the second (horizon 1, p = 0.004 and horizon 6, p = 0.04), and the first two blocks (horizon 1, p = 0.01, and horizon 6, p = 0.03) (see Fig. 4). Model-based analyses

According to the reinforcement learning framework31, participants learn the expected reward of each bandit via observing forced-choice trials4,32. These learned expected values shape the decisions they make in the first free-choice trial.

The value of each option (Q) depends on three parameters, including the average reward of each bandit (R), their information value (I), and their side of presentation (S)15. Meanwhile, three decision components including “information bonus,” “decision noise,” and “spatial bias” are considered free parameters estimated by fitting an established logistic choice model to the first free-choice trial (see Table 1). In this model, directed and random exploration is captured by information bonus and decision noise parameters, respectively (see Table 1).

These decision components were further categorized based on different horizons (h1 or h6) and information (uncertainty) conditions ([1 3] or [2 2])12. Overall, our model consisted of 10 free parameters (four parameters for decision noise and spatial bias, and two parameters for information bonus). For more detail on model parameters and codes used for this model see Methods and supplementary material.

Results indicated a significant reduction only in decision noise for horizon 1, [2 2] condition (p = 0.042) and horizon 6, in total uncertainty [2 2] condition (p = 0.025) (Fig. 6). Discussion

Previous neuroimaging studies suggest a functional role for the rDLPFC in exploration5,19,17. In this study, we used non-invasive brain stimulation (cTBS) to directly investigate the role of the rDLPFC in different types of exploration, while participants completed 192 trials of the computerized Horizon task (see Figs. 1 and 2). This task selectively distinguishes between directed and random exploration, allowing us to specifically examine the involvement of the rDLPFC in both types of exploratory strategies.

Model-free results, showed a significant reduction in random exploration in both horizon conditions, while directed exploration remained intact (Fig. 3). As random exploration is heavily affected by boredom and disengagement from the task, and engaging in 192 trials might exceed the time window of cTBS effect, we further analyze the impact of TMS in the first (1–64 trial), second (64–128) and first two (1-128) blocks. Results were the same in the second and first two blocks indicating a reduction in random exploration (Fig. 4).

Then, we turned to computational modeling to have a closer look at exploration parameters. Among 10 free parameters extracted from fitting an established logistic choice model to behavioral data (see Table 1), only two decision noise parameters, in unequal information condition (total uncertainty condition), were significantly reduced following rDLPFC suppression (Fig. 6). Together, these results support the role of rDLPFC in decreasing the sensitivity to average rewards in total uncertainty conditions and driving random exploration.

The role of the rDLPFC in exploration is well-established in human and primate neuroimaging studies5,19,9,17,33, but our findings specifically highlight its involvement in random exploration. This raises two key questions: Firstly, why there were no observed changes in directed exploration? Secondly, what accounts for the decrease in random exploration?

Direct exploration is a bias toward more informative options. Previous studies that tried to disentangle directed and random exploration, did not find rDLPFC activation in directed exploration, instead, they supported the role of dorsal ACC13 and rFPC5,10 specifically for directed exploration (for review see14). These studies are aligned with our observation that showed rDLPFC stimulation didn’t change information-seeking behavior.

Random exploration is defined as behavioral or neural variability, but the selective neural substrate for random exploration is less investigated15. In an fMRI study conducted by Tomov et al.5 human participants performed a two-armed bandit task with safe (i.e., a fixed amount of reward) and risky (i.e., a variable amount of reward) options. They revealed that in total uncertainty conditions in which both bandits were risky or safe, bold signal in rDLPFC predicts trial variabilities in random exploration, but not directed exploration. In another study conducted by Jahn et al.9, monkeys performed a modified version of the Horizon task in an MRI scanner. They saw when monkeys selected the low-value option, ACC/MCC and DLPFC had the greatest activity, which highlights the role of this network in decreasing the reliance on expected values and driving exploration to the low-value option9. To interpret our results in light of these studies, it is possible that the rDLPFC inhibition decreases random exploration by disrupting the DLPFC-ACC/MCC network and drives more reward-sensitive choices.

As random exploration is deeply connected to risk-taking and impulsivity, we could also discuss our results in light of previous TMS studies that target rDLPFC and its effect on risky decision-making tasks. First, previous studies showed that stimulating rDLPFC with cTBS protocol reduces impulsivity levels in delayed discounting task21. In a recent study, Dubois and Hauser34 showed a strong correlation between random exploration and general impulsivity in a large healthy sample size (N = 580) across multiple questionnaires. These studies could support the idea that cTBS over rDLPFC reduces random exploration by reducing impulsivity and enhancing self-control. At some point, this possibility could be ruled out as a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of noninvasive brain stimulation over DLPFC, reveals a functional lateralization between right and left DLPFC, which stimulation of left DLPFC and not rDLPFC, associated with reducing impulsivity, and enhancing self-control35. Second, exploration (either directed or random), could also be linked to risk-taking behavior, as choosing an option with high uncertainty or lower expected value could be interpreted as taking risk36. Previous non-invasive brain stimulation studies showed that suppressing rDLPFC increases risk-taking behavior in gambling task23,24,26. However, in our study rDLPFC suppression facilitates reward-sensitive choices. Although our results might appear inconsistent with these studies, one should consider differences in the task paradigm and the possible difference in network activation. In the Horizon task, participants were partially informed about the expected values of each bandit in forced-choice trials, while in other gambling tasks (e.g., Maastricht Gambling Task) used in previous TMS studies, each trial provide different payoffs and risk levels which might put an extra complication on executive control23. Complementary, Zhen et al.17 conducted a meta-analysis of fMRI studies that used exploration-exploitation tasks. They compared the brain activations related to exploration to the coordinates of risk-related activation from another meta-analysis that focused on risky decision-making tasks37. Their results showed that rDLPFC activation in explore-exploit tasks is specific for exploration and not risk. In that sense, although risk-taking and exploration are two interdependent processes in the explore-exploit tradeoff, their neural correlates might be dissociable in some brain areas such as the rDLPFC17.

Spreng and Turner38 proposed a network-based model for transitioning from exploration to exploitation mode during late-life development. Based on the premise that exploration needs more cognitive control resources than exploitation38, they suggested that the frontoparietal network (FPN) promotes explorative behavior by engaging externally-directed cognitive processes, and default mode network (DMN) supports exploitative behavior with internally-directed cognitive functions. A recent meta-analysis of explore-exploit fMRI studies in young adults supported this model30, which showed that exploration-related regions are reliably aligned with FPN, dorsal attention network (DAN), and salience network (SN), and exploitation-related activation was mostly related to DMN, more specifically the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)30. Results from the present study could support this model, as we showed inhibiting rDLPFC, a core cortical node in FPN39, reduced explorative behavior, and could further extend this model and suggest different cortical nodes in FPN facilitate different explorative strategies.

However, TMS could have direct and indirect effects on nearby regions, and our study could not specify exactly which network is stimulated. More importantly, vmPFC has strong connectivity to DLPFC and previous noninvasive brain stimulation studies support the idea that DLPFC suppression could also affect vmPFC activity23. The vmPFC is a core region in DMN network, responsible for evaluating the value of choice options40, reward anticipation41, and exploitative decisions29,30,40. Future work combining neuroimaging, TMS, and network neuroscience methods is necessary to shed light on the interaction between these nodes supporting explore-exploit balance.

Moreover, an analysis of random exploration across the first two blocks showed that differences between the stimulation conditions started to appear from the second block. Although interpreting random exploration across separate blocks may lack statistical power, the significant reduction observed in the second block—but not the first—is worth discussing. This could be attributed to several reasons. First, non-human studies which investigate the influence of cTBS on single-unit responses, showed that cTBS doesn’t have a unitary effect on neural populations42, some groups of neurons showed early inhibitory responses and other groups showed late inhibitory responses42. That’s why, one could expect a dynamic change in cTBS effect in terms of behavioral variability. Second, we showed that rDLPFC stimulation caused considerable accuracy improvement in both horizon conditions (Fig. 5). Previous studies have similarly suggested that random exploration tends to decrease as learning throughout an experiment32. Therefore, it is possible that cTBS facilitated learning during the experiment, contributing to the reduction in random exploration. Future studies incorporating computational models with learning parameters12,32, could better explain these findings.

It is worth noting that, while we defined random and directed exploration as behavioral variability and information seeking in both horizon conditions (following2,12), another approach to quantifying exploration considers only when information gain is useful36. This approach considers exploration as the difference between information bonus and decision noise in long-horizon (h6) and short-horizon (h1) conditions32. Based on this interpretation, however, our results only suggest an increased general reward sensitivity over the expected values of each option after rDLPFC inhibition, as we observed a quite similar reduction in decision noise in both horizon conditions.

These results could also have potential clinical implications, given the known associations between exploration-exploitation imbalances and various psychopathologies such as schizophrenia43–45, depression46,47, anxiety47–49, and addiction50. Specifically, the overuse of random exploration observed in schizophrenia patients correlates with poorer decision-making performance43,44 suggests a targeted therapeutic opportunity. Future research could explore interventions that can specifically modulate cortical excitability of the rDLPFC to possibly modify random exploration imbalance.

There are important limitations to consider when interpreting the results. First, although our final sample size meets the requirements of the estimated power analysis, it remains relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the forced-choice trials were not properly randomized in our task between the two stimulation conditions. Although a similar previous study found no significant behavioral differences after randomizing the forced-choice trials12, this could be seen as a limitation due to reduced control over forced-choice payoffs. Finally, while we used vertex as a control condition, a study by Jung et al.,51 has shown suprathreshold TMS stimulation on the vertex could induce deactivation in a few brain regions, which might interfere with the current results.

With the above limitations in mind, our study provides the first direct evidence of the involvement of the right DLPFC in random exploration, but not in directed exploration. This finding aligns with previous neuroimaging studies and further supports the notion of distinct neural mechanisms for directed and random exploration.

Methods

Participants

For estimating the sample size, a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.752. We used 0.62 for power, based on a meta-analysis study that calculates the statistical power of NIBS studies53, and reports the provided power as a mean power for TMS studies. As TBS-induced effect sizes were found to be larger than in rTMS studies54,55, we used a medium estimated effect size (η2p = 0.06). With the above effect size and power, the minimum sample size needed to find group differences within two repeated measurements was 23. Thus, we recruited 30 healthy right-handed, adult volunteers (14 female, 18–32) for this study. Participants were recruited from paper flyers distributed at the university campus.

None of the subjects had a history of brain injury, head trauma, psychiatric or neurological disorders, and had no previous experience with Horizon task, or TMS, with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Subjects were screened for depression and anxiety with the use of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales – DASS-21 with an exclusion criterion of the score of 10 for each subscale. Furthermore, we don’t include economic and technical science students with prior knowledge of decision theories, which might induce bias in the study.

Three participants were excluded due to discomfort during stimulation (2 female, 1 male). Two participants were excluded due to the high resting motor threshold (2 female, 0 male). The final data contains 25 participants (11 female, mean age: 23.84, sd: 2.76) with completed data (see Table 2). In data analysis, we further excluded two participants due to chance level performance in experimental sessions.

All participants were informed about the potential risks of TMS and signed informed consent before the first session. The study was conducted under the Helsinki regulations and the ethics of the experiment was approved by the university and the Humanities Ethics Committee with reference number IR.SBU.REC.1402.188.

TMS protocol

We used continuous theta burst stimulation protocol (cTBS) with Magstim Pro 2 devices, which contain a continuous 40-sec train of 600 pulses, with short bursts (3 stimuli) of 50 Hz pulses repeated at theta range (5 Hz)55. This protocol is expected to decrease cortical excitability for up to 50 min55.

Our protocol contains two sessions (Fig. 2A). In the first session, participants got familiar with the task, and then we obtained their resting motor threshold (RMT). For finding RMT we looked at the lowest stimulation intensity that could elicit a visible contraction of the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) in five out of ten pulses after stimulating the motor cortex. Then stimulation was applied based on individual resting motor threshold. While previous studies mostly applied an 80% individual resting motor threshold, recent studies suggested higher doses for proper stimulation conditions33. That’s why, we applied a 100%individual resting motor threshold.

To find stimulation sites, we used the 10–20 EEG international system. One session was the vertex, where we put the TMS coil on the Cz position, and for stimulating the rDLPFC, the coil was located in the F4 position (Fig. 2B). Although using neuronavigational methods with MRI coordinates is better for localizing the specific brain area, DLPFC is a large structure and we considered its localization with a general 10–20 EEG system to be sufficient to suppress target area24.

Procedure

Each participant undergoes two TMS experimental sessions with an interval of at least 5 days (see Fig. 2). In the first session, they went through 10 training games to get familiar with the task. Next, we obtained a resting motor threshold and then the stimulation took place. The order of stimulation crossover across participants. To minimize the potential adverse effects of TMS (e.g., dizziness) on behavioral performance and to allow the downregulating effects of cTBS to take effect55,56, participants began the Horizon task five minutes after the stimulation.

Horizon task

The Horizon task was designed based on Wilson and colleagues’ 2014 paper with psychtoolbox in MATLAB2. This task includes 192 games (in three 64-game blocks) of a bandit task with four forced-choice trials and one (horizon 1) to six (horizon 6) free-choice trials. Forced-choice trials established two information (uncertainty) conditions: “unequal information” ([1 3]), requiring one option to be played once and the other three times, and “equal information” ([2 2]), with each option played twice. Meanwhile, free-choice trials established two horizon game length conditions including horizon 1 with one free-choice trial and horizon 6 with six free-choice trials. Game dimensions including Information conditions, game length and mean difference were counterbalanced over the entire experiment. However, the forced-choice trials were not randomized between two stimulation conditions. Although this might be considered as a limitation due to the poor control over forced-choice payoffs, a previous similar study found no evidence of a behavioral difference after randomization of forced-choice trials12.

Throughout each game, the choice and outcome history were displayed onscreen within each slot machine. Following the selection of a particular option, the reward for that trial was added to the corresponding slot machine, while the unchosen option’s space was filled with “X” (see Fig. 1).

The provided payouts were sampled from a Gaussian distribution with an 8-point standard deviation. The generative mean of one bandit is set to 40 or 60, while the others vary relative to the first, and the difference between two bandit means randomly sampled from 4, 8, 12, and 20 points. Participants received verbal task instructions derived from the original task instructions outlined in the paper by Wilson et al.2. Overall, participants were directed to maximize their earned points and informed that one option consistently offered better outcomes on average.

The overall performance of participants throughout the experiment was evaluated to assess task performance. Any participant whose performance did not exceed the chance level at a threshold of p = 0.55 correct responses was excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

In the model-free analysis, directed exploration was assessed by calculating the probability of choosing the high-informative option, denoted as p(high info), in the [1 3] condition. Random exploration was estimated as the probability of choosing the low-mean reward option, p(low mean), in the [2 2] condition. As p(high info) and p(low mean) data were normally distributed, a repeated measures ANOVA was performed with the horizon, TMS condition, order, and gender as factors, followed by post hoc analyses (paired t-tests) to examine the effect of TMS in different horizon conditions. Order and gender were included as factors to account for potential effects of performance improvements over time, as well as any gender-related differences in response to TMS57. Furthermore, we assessed the proportion of correct responses across all trials, defined as selecting the option with the higher generative mean, and conducted paired t-tests to examine differences between the two TMS conditions. Partial η2 for ANOVA is calculated using custom MATLAB code as the sum of squares of the variable effect divided by the sum of squares of the variable effect plus the sum of squares of the errors. For post-hoc analysis, Cohen’s d is calculated as the difference in means divided by the difference in standard deviations.
