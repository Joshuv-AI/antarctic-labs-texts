Validation of screening criteria for interstitial lung disease in rheumatoid arthritis in routine clinical practice: the VAR-EPIDSER study
Authors: Natalia Mena-Vázquez, Marta Domínguez-Álvaro, María Gema Bonilla Hernán, Belén Atienza-Mateo, María Fernández Velilla Peña, Inmaculada Pinilla Fernández, Ana M Ortiz, Hye Sang Park, Nair Pérez Gomez, Chamaida Plasencia-Rodríguez, Maria Carmen Aguilar Hurtado, Susana Holgado Pérez, Ana Serrano-Combarro, Nuria Vegas-Revenga, José Antonio Bernal Vidal, Joaquín Lacasa-Molina, Stefanie Burger, Cristina Campos Fernández, Jordi Fiter Aresté, Juan Antonio Meraz Ostiz, María del Mar Muñoz Gómez, Diego Alvaredo, Santos Castañeda, on behalf of the VAR-EPIDSER Project Collaborating Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the validity of the rheumatoid arthritis–interstitial lung disease screening project of the Spanish Society of Rheumatology (AR-EPIDSER) screening criteria for detecting rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated interstitial lung disease (ILD) and to estimate the prevalence of undiagnosed and subclinical ILD in patients with RA in Spain.Patients and methodsMulticentre cross-sectional observational study conducted in 15 Spanish hospitals. Adult patients with RA (American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 1987 or ACR/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology 2010 criteria) without a previous ILD diagnosis were included. During a rheumatology visit, the AR-EPIDSER screening criteria were applied, and all participants underwent high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest as the reference test, with centralised reading. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were estimated with 95% CI as well as the prevalence of ILD and subclinical ILD.ResultsA total of 477 patients were recruited; 424 had evaluable HRCT (73.6% women; median age 59 years). The prevalence of ILD on HRCT examinations was 13.0% (55/424; 95% CI 9.9% to 16.6%). Subclinical ILD (no cough, dyspnoea or ‘Velcro-like’ crackles) was 8.3% (35/424; 95% CI 5.7% to 11.1%), accounting for 63.6% of ILD cases. Screening was positive in 68.2% (289/424). The AR-EPIDSER criteria showed sensitivity=0.93 (95% CI 0.82 to 0.98), specificity=0.36 (0.31 to 0.41), PPV=0.18 (0.13 to 0.23) and NPV=0.97 (0.93 to 0.99).ConclusionIn Spanish patients with RA, undiagnosed ILD is frequent and mostly subclinical. The AR-EPIDSER criteria are useful as an initial screening tool due to high sensitivity and excellent NPV; however, low specificity suggests that positive cases should be confirmed by HRCT.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects up to 1% of the global population, predominantly women, with a female-to-male ratio of 3:1 to 4:1.1 Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) is one of the most common extra-articular manifestations of RA. This complication is associated with a significant increase in morbidity and mortality, to the extent that it is considered the second leading cause of death after cardiovascular disease.2 3 Increased morbidity and mortality have been related to ILD progression, RA severity, use of certain treatments and coexistence of comorbidities.4 5 Estimates of RA-ILD incidence and prevalence vary widely depending on detection methods, population characteristics and study design.6 7 Population-based studies have reported prevalences of 3.2–6.0 cases per 100 000 inhabitants and incidences of 2.7–3.8 per 100 000.2 When systematic screening using high-resolution CT (HRCT) is performed in patients with RA, ILD frequency ranges from 3.6% to 58%, although clinically significant forms are around 10–15%.810 Although RA diagnosis precedes ILD diagnosis in most cases, ILD prevalence at the time of RA diagnosis has been estimated at approximately 5%, increasing over the subsequent 5 years.11 In addition, a relevant proportion of patients present ILD in subclinical or paucisymptomatic stages (15–57%), detectable only by imaging or functional tests.12 Reported risk factors for ILD development in RA include male sex, older age, smoking, presence and high titers of rheumatoid factor (RF) and/or anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA), late disease onset and persistent inflammatory activity over time.6 13 14 Given its high frequency, the existence of subclinical or paucisymptomatic forms, and its implications for treatment and outcomes, it is essential to define strategies for early detection of ILD in patients with RA. In the absence of homogeneous screening criteria adapted to the Spanish healthcare setting, the Spanish Society of Rheumatology (SER), in collaboration with the Spanish Society of Pulmonology and Thoracic Surgery (SEPAR), developed the Spanish acronym for the rheumatoid arthritis–interstitial lung disease screening project of the Spanish Society of Rheumatology (AR-EPIDSER) project, proposing screening criteria for early detection of ILD in RA (online supplemental appendix 1).15 However, these criteria have not been yet validated in clinical practice. Therefore, the objectives of this study were: (1) to estimate the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of the ILD screening criteria defined in the AR-EPIDSER study; (2) to estimate the prevalence of undiagnosed ILD in patients with RA in Spain; and (3) to estimate the prevalence of subclinical ILD in this population.

Patients and methods

Study design and participating centres

A multicentre cross-sectional observational study was conducted in patients with RA followed in rheumatology outpatient clinics. Participating centres were selected via an open call through SER communication channels. Hospitals were selected on the basis of having sufficient patient activity to meet the study objectives and timelines, as well as available and coordinated rheumatology, pulmonology (for pulmonary function tests) and radiology (for chest HRCT) services. Ultimately, 15 university hospitals from different Spanish autonomous communities participated (online supplemental table 1).

Study population and selection criteria

Adult patients (≥18 years) with RA diagnosed according to ACR 1987 or ACR/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology 2010 classification criteria,16 able to communicate effectively with research staff, were included. Exclusion criteria were: another autoimmune rheumatic disease other than RA (except secondary Sjögren syndrome), prior ILD diagnosis, active respiratory infection at inclusion, follow-up in pulmonology for another respiratory condition, pregnancy, participation in clinical trials or other studies interfering with its participation in current study, or any other health issue that—according to the investigator—compromised inclusion.

Sampling and sample size

Sample size was calculated assuming an ILD prevalence of ~30% in patients with RA undergoing systematic screening,17 18 and expected screening characteristics of 90% sensitivity and 70% specificity. Under these assumptions, NPV was estimated at ~0.94 and PPV at 0.56. Assuming 15% losses, 470 patients (310 screening positive and 160 screening negative) were required to achieve the desired precision. With 15 centres involved, recruitment of 32 patients per hospital was planned.

Study protocol

Selected patients were evaluated by their rheumatologist during a routine follow-up visit. The ILD screening criteria defined in the AR-EPIDSER study15 were applied to all patients with RA meeting eligibility criteria, who were then invited to participate and enrolled consecutively until reaching the target numbers of screening-positive and screening-negative cases per centre. The sample was intended to be representative of the Spanish RA population,19 ensuring at least 25% men and adequate representation across age groups (≥10% under 40 years and ≥20% over 65 years), as described in Variability in the Management of Rheumatoid Arthritis and Spondyloarthritis in Spain study.20 After obtaining patient’s informed consent, the rheumatologist completed the case report form including clinical and sociodemographic variables. All included patients underwent HRCT to detect or rule out ILD and to record radiologic pattern and extent. Parenchymal evaluation followed the protocol described in the American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS)/Japanese Respiratory Society/Latin American Thoracic Association guideline for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis diagnosis.21 HRCT consisted of non-contrast volumetric acquisition at maximal inspiration, using parameters ensuring adequate image quality with minimal radiation dose. Inspiratory and expiratory images were obtained to assess air trapping and, when needed, prone acquisition. Images were reconstructed using soft-tissue and high-resolution kernels for interstitial assessment; the former were used for automated processing of lung attenuation. All HRCT scans were read centrally by two radiologists specialised in cardiothoracic imaging (MFVP; IPF), each with >15 years of experience in interstitial lung pathology, who issued a radiologic diagnosis and characterised interstitial involvement. Additionally, per the expert criteria defined in AR-EPIDSER,15 in patients with cough and/or dyspnoea lasting >3 months or with moderate ILD risk, X-ray of the chest, spirometry and diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide were performed for validation purposes. Moderate risk was defined using a risk-factor score assigning points based on age (≥60 years), male sex, smoking history (higher score for ≥20 pack-years), RA duration (>5 years), persistent moderate–high inflammatory activity, serology (high RF and ACPA) and family history of ILD (online supplemental appendix 2).

Main outcome

The primary outcome was the presence of ILD on HRCT (yes/no), considered the reference standard for estimating sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV of the AR-EPIDSER screening criteria.15 ILD was defined as interstitial abnormalities on HRCT compatible with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organising pneumonia (OP), diffuse alveolar damage or other RA-attributable interstitial patterns, according to ATS/ERS classification21 as judged by expert radiologists. Subclinical ILD prevalence was also calculated (detection of ILD on HRCT in the absence of clinically relevant respiratory symptoms).

Other variables

Demographic and anthropometric variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, weight, height and date of the study visit. Smoking status was recorded as current smoker, former smoker or never smoker and exposure quantified in pack-years. RA-related variables included symptom onset date, diagnosis date and persistent moderate–high disease activity, defined as an average Disease Activity Score on 28-joint count using Erythrocyte Sedimentation Score (DAS28-ESR)>3.2 from diagnosis in early RA (≤12 months since diagnosis) or DAS28-ESR>3.2 maintained for at least 6 months since diagnosis in established RA. RF and ACPA values/titers were recorded (IU/mL), and seropositivity was interpreted according to each participating centre’s laboratory reference ranges/cut-offs. Functional status was assessed with the Health Assessment Questionnaire.22 Current RA treatment was recorded, specifying drugs and exposure time in months. Respiratory variables included presence of diagnosed respiratory disease at inclusion (and type), cough and/or dyspnoea lasting >3 months and dyspnoea severity according to the modified Medical Research Council Scale.23 Presence of ‘Velcro-like’ crackles on pulmonary auscultation and family history of ILD were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the main sample characteristics was performed. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean and SD or median and IQR [p25–p75], depending on normality assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were described as absolute frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons used Student’s t test (if normality and homoscedasticity were assumed) or Mann-Whitney U test otherwise. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ² test and Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequency was <5. For the main outcome (ILD on HRCT), PPV and NPV of the screening criteria applied by the rheumatologist were estimated using HRCT as the reference test. PPV was calculated as the number of screening-positive patients with ILD confirmed on HRCT divided by the total number of screening-positive patients. NPV was calculated as the number of screening-negative patients without ILD on HRCT divided by the total number of screening-negative patients. Exact binomial 95% CIs were calculated for both values. Sensitivity and specificity were then derived from these values and the proportion of screening-positive and screening-negative patients. Prevalence of ILD and subclinical ILD were estimated with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Analyses were performed using statistics programme R V.4.4.2.

Results

Patient flow and baseline cohort characteristics

During the inclusion period, a total of 477 patients with RA were recruited across 15 participating centres. After monitoring, 3 patients were excluded for not meeting eligibility criteria (prior pulmonology follow-up for another respiratory condition, diagnosis other than RA and withdrawal of informed consent, respectively), leaving 474 patients for analysis. Of these, 46 did not undergo HRCT of the chest and 4 had incomplete or non-evaluable HRCT, resulting in a final cohort of 424 patients with complete and evaluable HRCT for the primary analysis (figure 1).

HRCT was performed within a time window close to the baseline visit. In patients scanned after the visit, the median interval was 1.5 months (0.9–3.3), with no differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic patients (1.6 vs 1.4 months; p=0.190). In patients with HRCT prior to the baseline visit, the median interval was 2.5 months (1.0–5.9), also with no significant differences by presence of respiratory symptoms (2.8 vs 2.0 months; p=0.407).

Baseline characteristics are shown in table 1. Most patients were women (73.6%) and white/Caucasian race (82.8%), with a median age of 59 years (48–67.5). Overall, the cohort was representative of the Spanish RA population, with 26.4% men and 31.1% older than 65 years, meeting the predefined sex and age distribution targets.

A total of 2.4% patients reported a family history of ILD. Approximately half were never smokers, 19% were current smokers and 35% were former smokers, with a median cumulative exposure of 22 pack-years.

Regarding RA characteristics, median disease duration was 81.5 months (28.3–179.1), with relatively short diagnostic delay (median 5 months) and 23.1% had persistent moderate–high disease activity. Most patients were RF positive (60%) and ACPA positive (80.7%).

All patients were receiving a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) at inclusion, most commonly methotrexate, followed by leflunomide and hydroxychloroquine. A quarter received tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitor (anti-TNF) drugs and 10.8% Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, while non-anti-TNF biologics (anti-interleukin 6, abatacept, anti-CD20) were less frequent. Nearly 20% received glucocorticoids at baseline, with a prednisone median dose of 5 mg/day.

Respiratory symptoms, screening results and ILD presence according to HRCT are summarised in table 2. Overall, ILD prevalence in the cohort was 13.0% (55/424; 95% CI 9.9% to 16.6%) based on HRCT findings of the chest. Subclinical ILD prevalence in patients with RA (no cough, dyspnoea or ‘Velcro-like’ crackles on auscultation) was 8.3% (35/424; 95% CI 5.7% to 11.1%).

At baseline, 11.1% reported cough and 9.9% dyspnoea, lasting>3 months in 78.7% and 95.2% of cases, respectively. ‘Velcro-like’ crackles were detected in 25 patients (5.9%), predominantly bilateral.

Among the 55 patients with ILD, 20 (36.4%) had respiratory symptoms and/or crackles consistent with clinical ILD (4.7% of the cohort), while the remaining 35 (63.6%) had no respiratory symptoms or auscultatory findings suggestive of ILD and were classified as subclinical ILD.

On radiologic analysis, 73.3% had some abnormal HRCT finding, mostly not compatible with ILD (primarily non-interstitial findings). Among patients with ILD, the most frequent radiologic finding was subpleural reticulation (25.5%). Regarding patterns, NSIP was the most frequent (21.8%), followed by OP (12.7%) and UIP (7.3%). ILD extent was limited in most cases, with 78.2% showing 1–25% parenchymal involvement.

Clinical characteristics and factors associated with ILD

Patients with ILD were significantly older than those without pulmonary involvement (p<0.001), with no significant differences by sex or race. Among comorbidities, only gastro-oesophageal reflux was more frequent in the ILD group, although absolute numbers were small. No significant differences were observed for other comorbidities (asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, rhinitis or heart failure). Regarding smoking, the proportion of current and former smokers was higher among patients with ILD (p=0.010), as was pack-year exposure, although the latter did not reach statistical significance (p=0.093). No differences were found in RA duration, diagnostic delay or inflammatory activity between groups (table 3).

Regarding treatment, all patients in both groups received a DMARD, mainly methotrexate, with no significant baseline differences between patients with ILD and non-ILD (58.1% vs 50.7%). Use of other csDMARDs, bDMARDs and JAK inhibitors was also similar. In contrast, baseline glucocorticoid use was significantly more frequent in patients with ILD (23.6% vs 18.7%; p=0.041), although the median daily dose was 5 mg in both groups (table 3).

The general AR-EPIDSER criteria—combining respiratory symptoms and the risk-factor score—showed the best performance as an initial screening strategy, with sensitivity of 93% (95% CI 0.82 to 0.98) and NPV of 97% (95% CI 0.93 to 0.99). Specificity was low (36%; 95% CI 0.31 to 0.41), resulting in a low PPV (18%; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.23). Overall accuracy was moderate to low (table 4).

Screening criteria based solely on respiratory symptoms yielded low sensitivity, particularly cough and dyspnoea lasting >3 months (16% each), indicating that most patients with ILD do not present marked respiratory symptoms in early stages. However, these criteria showed high specificity (92%), meaning that prolonged symptoms increase ILD probability. ‘Velcro’ crackles showed even higher specificity (96%) and the highest PPV (40%) among all criteria, suggesting they are clinically useful to prioritise HRCT despite low sensitivity (18%). When clinical information was combined into an expanded ‘respiratory symptoms’ criterion (cough and/or dyspnoea >3 months and/or crackles), sensitivity increased to 36% at the cost of reduced specificity (85%), while NPV remained high (90%), indicating that a negative result reasonably lowers ILD probability.

Conversely, a risk-factor score ≥5 had very high sensitivity (86%) and excellent NPV (95%), useful for ruling out ILD in low-risk patients. However, specificity was low (39%), with many false positives, implying many patients with non-ILD meet this criterion.

Discussion

This is a multicentre study designed to validate the AR-EPIDSER screening criteria in patients with RA followed in Rheumatology clinics under routine clinical practice in Spain. With this study, we confirmed an ILD prevalence of 13% (95% CI 9.9% to 16.6%) in a cohort representative of the Spanish RA population,19 with a substantial proportion of subclinical forms (8.3%; 95% CI 5.7% to 11.1%). We also observed that the general AR-EPIDSER criteria display a screening tool profile, with high sensitivity and high NPV at the cost of modest specificity and PPV.

Published estimates of ILD prevalence in RA are highly heterogeneous (3.6–58%), depending on detection methods, population characteristics and study design.67 9 2427 However, the prevalence observed in our cohort is close to the adjusted prevalence reported in the recent meta-analysis by Wang et al,8 which estimated RA-ILD prevalence around 14.3% in European studies with systematic radiologic screening. The slight difference may reflect the higher proportion of women in our sample, designed to represent the general RA population, given that ILD is more frequent in men.6 13 14 25 The high proportion of subclinical ILD is consistent with other studies and systematic reviews indicating that most ILD cases detected through HRCT and/or functional screening correspond to asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic disease.12 13 28

In our cohort, the general AR-EPIDSER criteria showed very high sensitivity (93%) and excellent NPV (97%), with modest specificity (36%) and low PPV (18%). This is the expected profile of a screening tool and supports its usefulness for ruling out ILD rather than confirming it: a negative result makes disease unlikely, whereas a positive result should always be confirmed by HRCT. Component analysis helps interpret these findings: criteria based only on respiratory symptoms (cough or dyspnoea>3 months, and ‘Velcro-like’ crackles) had low sensitivity, confirming that many patients with ILD remain asymptomatic or have very mild symptoms early on, but showed high specificity; crackles substantially increased ILD probability and remain useful to prioritise HRCT. By contrast, the risk-factor score≥5 provided very high sensitivity and high NPV, at the cost of low specificity and many false positives.

Overall, these findings align with a recent validation study of SER/SEPAR criteria in patients with early RA, which also reported sensitivity>90% and NPV around 96% with moderate specificity and PPV, as well as with other clinical/risk-based scoring systems prioritising sensitivity over specificity.29 Similarly, recent reviews emphasise that screening strategies should focus on detecting nearly all cases (high sensitivity and NPV), even at the expense of lower specificity and more false positives.25 30 This reinforces the concept that AR-EPIDSER criteria15 should be used as the first step in a stepwise screening strategy, reserving HRCT for patients with positive screening results, especially when persistent respiratory symptoms or ‘Velcro-like’ crackles coexist.

Recent data suggest that lung ultrasound (LUS) may have a complementary role in RA-ILD screening. Vermant et al31 reported that the number of B lines outperformed cough-based and dyspnoea-based questionnaires for detecting both clinical and subclinical ILD. In addition, Vicente-Rabaneda et al reported good-to-excellent intra- and inter-observer reliability for several feasible B-line and pleural LUS scores, reinforcing the feasibility and reproducibility of LUS as a potential tool for RA-ILD assessment in clinical practice.32 However, because of operator dependency and the lack of standardised protocols and cut-offs, it should currently be considered complementary rather than a replacement for HRCT-based confirmation.

Another clinically relevant issue is the timing and repetition of screening. Although our cross-sectional study was not designed to define the optimal interval for repeated screening, the high proportion of subclinical ILD in our cohort suggests that screening should not be delayed until respiratory symptoms become evident. This is in line with the recent prospective study by Froidure et al33 performed in patients with RA of relatively recent duration, which reported a relevant burden of lung involvement and supported prioritising screening in patients at higher risk. Taken together, these findings support a risk-based approach in which ILD screening is considered from early stages of RA, particularly in older patients, men, smokers, seropositive individuals and those with persistent inflammatory activity. During follow-up, repeat assessment may be especially appropriate in high-risk patients and whenever new respiratory symptoms or ‘Velcro-like’ crackles appear.33 Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish the optimal frequency of repeated screening and the most appropriate timing for HRCT reassessment.

From a radiologic perspective, most patients with ILD had limited disease extent (1–25% of lung parenchyma), with predominance of subpleural reticulation and other non-specific interstitial changes, while NSIP, OP and UIP patterns were less frequent than typically reported. This profile—subtle HRCT abnormalities, subpleural reticulation and limited fibrosing patterns—may reflect an early fibrosis stage31 and be compatible with early or subclinical ILD.25 34

Regarding factors associated with ILD, our data confirm the role of risk factors described in the literature, such as age and smoking.830 3537 Patients with ILD were older and more often current/former smokers; additionally, a higher proportion received glucocorticoids at baseline. In contrast, we did not observe clear differences in other factors such as RA duration, diagnostic delay or inflammatory activity, possibly due to relative cohort homogeneity in disease control.

Study limitations include the cross-sectional design, which prevents analysis of ILD progression and evaluation of the criteria’s ability to predict incident ILD over time. This should be addressed in future longitudinal studies. However, the design specifically addressed the validity evaluation of AR-EPIDSER criteria,15 a need previously identified by SER-SEPAR. Second, the predominance of tertiary hospitals could have increased the proportion of more complex patients or those with more comorbidities, potentially limiting generalisability. Nevertheless, this was a large multicentre cohort recruited in 15 university hospitals across Spain, with demographic and clinical characteristics representative of the Spanish RA population.19 Systematic screening application at baseline, HRCT performed within a close time window, and centralised expert radiology reading reduce variability and strengthen internal validity. Another limitation is that the observed ILD prevalence was lower than anticipated in the sample size calculation (13.0% vs approximately 30%). Although the calculation included a 15% increase to account for expected losses, the lower number of ILD cases available for analysis may have reduced the precision of estimates based on diseased subjects, particularly sensitivity and PPV. In contrast, estimates mainly based on non-ILD subjects, such as specificity and NPV, are likely to be more stable. Finally, approximately 10% of initially recruited patients lacked evaluable HRCT, which may introduce selection bias; however, the final sample size remained within the margin anticipated in the initial calculation.

In conclusion, our results support the usefulness of AR-EPIDSER criteria as an initial screening tool for ILD in patients with RA, particularly due to high sensitivity and high NPV. We found an ILD prevalence of 13% with a substantial proportion of subclinical disease. These findings reinforce the need to integrate AR-EPIDSER criteria into stepwise screening strategies combining risk factors, respiratory symptoms and pulmonary auscultation, using them as the first screening step. HRCT of the chest should be reserved for patients with positive screening results—especially when persistent respiratory symptoms or ‘Velcro-like’ crackles are present.
