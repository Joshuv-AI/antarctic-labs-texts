Ciliated foregut cyst of the pancreas: a rare mimic of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm
Authors: Raquel Gonçalves, Pedro Moutinho-Ribeiro, Ana Marques, Joanne Lopes, Guilherme Macedo
Published: 2026

Pancreatic cystic lesions are increasingly identified due to widespread use of cross-sectional imaging, often as incidental findings. Accurate characterization of these lesions is essential because they encompass a broad spectrum ranging from benign to premalignant or malignant entities. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) combined with fine-needle aspiration (FNA) plays a key role in their evaluation, but distinguishing between neoplastic and non-neoplastic cysts remains challenging.1

We describe a case of a 68-year-old man with hepatitis C virus-induced cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, and labial basal cell carcinoma. He had been followed since 2021 for a suspected intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) in the pancreatic uncinate process, measuring 23 × 22 mm. In 2022, he was referred to gastroenterology due to increased lesion size associated with anorexia and weight loss. EUS revealed a 35-× 25-mm cystic lesion in the pancreas uncinate process, without septa or solid components, with a normal main pancreatic duct (Fig. 1). No other abnormalities of the pancreatic parenchyma were noted. The fluid content obtained by FNA revealed low-cellularity cytology with degenerative alterations, high carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) (36,939 ng/mL), and slightly increased amylase (162 U/L). These findings were consistent with a mucinous cyst, most likely an IPMN. Serum tumor markers were normal (CEA <5 and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 <37). Due to the large, increasing size of the cyst, the patient underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy. CFCs are uncommon, mostly described in individual case reports,2 and are particularly rare in the pancreas. They are lined by pseudostratified ciliated epithelium with possible goblet cells.2 Although benign, malignant transformation has been reported.3,4 They are often misdiagnosed as IPMNs due to overlapping imaging and cytological challenges. Surgical resection is recommended when symptomatic.3,4

In our case, CEA was markedly elevated, strongly suggestive of a mucinous cyst. Although CEA >192 ng/mL is widely used to identify mucinous lesions, this case shows that even extremely high values are not specific and may occur in CFCs. This highlights the limitations of CEA as a standalone marker and the risk of misclassification when interpreted without histology. Cytology was inconclusive, as often occurs with EUS-guided FNA. Recognition of ciliated epithelium or goblet cells could aid diagnosis, but these features are rarely captured.

Advanced tools, including molecular testing (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homologue and guanine nucleotide-binding protein, alpha stimulating) or imaging (contrast EUS and confocal endomicroscopy), may help distinguish CFCs from IPMNs, although their role remains unclear.5,6

This case expands the limited literature on pancreatic CFCs and stresses considering this rare entity in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic cystic lesions.
