Behavioral and cardiac responses to a model startle test to assess retired Thoroughbred racehorses for equestrians
Authors: Seung-Ho Ryu, Kyung-Eun Lee, Eliot Forbes, Sung-Jun An, Joon Gyu Kim, Hang Lee, Byung Sun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceEquine behavioral traits are important criteria to assess the suitability of retired Thoroughbred racehorses for equestrian sports.ObjectiveThis study aims to develop a practical procedure to assess retired Thoroughbreds for their suitability for equestrian sports or leisure purposes and to assess if horses identified as having reactive temperaments in response to a standardized startle test, will have improved test results following a period of transition training.MethodsBehavioral reactivity data and cardiac autonomic responses to a startle test (comprising of a plastic bag applied to the neck area) were collected from twelve retired Thoroughbred racehorses (6 males and 6 females; average age: 4.33 ± 0.89 years old; average body weight 465.17 ± 25.33 kg), before and after the three-month transition training for equestrian sports. A priori power calculation indicated a requirement of 12 individuals for a power of 80%.ResultsFollowing the training, there was a significant reduction in the behavioral score (BS), peak heart rate (HR), and response duration (RD) of all horses compared to their pre-training scores. However, 25% of horses (3/12) retained a BS of 2 or above post-training, exhibiting significantly higher peak HR and longer RD compared to those with a BS below 2. Post-training, the females demonstrated a significant increase in the root mean square of the successive differences between normal heartbeats compared to their pre-training levels.Conclusions and RelevanceTransition training of retired Thoroughbreds can assist in improving horses' suitability for equestrian sports, based on their successive responses to a novel startle test.Keywords: Heart rate, heart rate variability, response duration, behavioral score, startle test, horse

INTRODUCTION

The racing industry faces scrutiny regarding the use or treatment of Thoroughbreds that have finished their racing careers. According to a previous study, 60% of Thoroughbred racehorses retired between 2015 and 2017 were utilized for equestrian riding, however, 20% of those horses subsequently retired from equestrian riding, with the suspected reason being their temperament. There is a view in the racing and equestrian communities in Korea, that Thoroughbreds are excitable and intractable to training for equestrian purposes and/or are dangerous when they are fractious [1]. It is likely that some retired Thoroughbreds are not used to their full potential because of the breed’s reputation. Establishing robust and objective assessment methodologies to demonstrate improvements following retraining, should assist to improve the perception of the breed and enhance post-racing career options.

Temperament is defined as a set of behavioral tendencies, present early in life and that are relatively stable across various kinds of situations over the course of time [2]. Equine temperament is an important consideration when determining the use of a horse, ranging from hippotherapy to show jumping competitions. It has been reported that the majority of equestrian riders expect emotional satisfaction and safety in equitation [3,4].

Although several kinds of stimuli (acoustic, visual or tactile) can induce the startle response in horses, the response to a plastic bag (a complex stimulus combining auditory, visual, and tactile elements) has been identified as a useful approach to evaluate horse temperament [5,6]. Horses exhibit different individual levels of behavioral responses in response to startle stimuli.

Physical exercise represents a physiological stressor for animals, demanding adaptations in the cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic and musculoskeletal systems to restore homeostasis [7]. Cardiovascular parameters are the most studied as they represent good indices of the fitness level and of workload effort [8]. Also, the heart rate (HR) may indicate the animal’s internal physiological state, indeed, the rise in this physiological parameter is not only caused by an increase in physical activity but also reflects heightened emotional reactivity [9]. In light of this, startlement could induce autonomic alterations in addition to behavioral responses. The literature identifies that the transitory increase in HR induced by a startle was consistently observed in different experimental animals and humans and is mediated by the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system [10,11,12]. The degree of cardiac autonomic responses to a startle was different between individuals. These differences could reflect differences in temperament [13].

The purpose of the study was to develop a procedure for a preliminary assessment of retired Thoroughbreds for their suitability for equestrian sports and to suggest selection parameters which may help identify retired Thoroughbred horses best suited for equestrian sports. It was hypothesized that the horse’s individual temperament would determine its behavioral and cardiac autonomic responses following a startling test. More accurate selection and integration into meaningful second careers after racing should increase the utility and welfare of retired Thoroughbreds. Selecting horses for equestrian riding, who have an appropriate temperament, should also improve rider safety.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Seoul National University (acceptance No.: SNU-211217-2) on December 17, 2021. All procedures were performed according to the IACUC guidelines.

Animals and management

Twelve Thoroughbred horses (6 males and 6 females; average age: 4.33 ± 0.89 years; average body weight 465.17 ± 25.33 kg) were used to: 1) examine behavioral and cardiac autonomic responses, as a measure of reactivity to being startled by plastic bag stimuli; and 2) re-examine the horses’ response to the same test, following a three-month transition training program for equestrian sports.

After their last race, the subject Thoroughbred racehorses stayed at the racetrack premises for approximately one month, with no race training. The horses were then relocated to an equestrian training center. Following a two-week familiarization period, the first startle test was conducted and the transition training commenced one week after the test.

During the transition period, the Thoroughbreds were trained for equestrian sports, including jumping and dressage exercises. They were ridden by professional equestrian riders, who were skilled to ride at competitive level dressage. The horses were taught to stand to be mounted and dismounted by riders without the assistance of other people; to appropriately respond to leg, rein, and seat aids; to respond to soft hand-rein communication; to make smooth transitions from one gait to another (e.g., to go into canter on the correct leading leg, from walk or trot); to change the leading leg while cantering; and to lengthen the stride in basic gaits. One-h training sessions were performed daily, five days per week. There was no desensitization training undertaken using plastic bags or any other stimuli similar to the startle test.

The horses were housed in 3.6 × 3.6 m individual masonry box stalls, with a water dispenser, feeder, and wood shaving bedding. The stall doors allowed visual contact between horses. The horses were fed commercial cereals for equestrian riding horses that were sourced locally, at a rate of 3.5 to 4 kg per horse per day (11% crude protein, 5.0% crude fat and 11.0% crude fiber), in addition to hay that was provided three times a day (06:00 - alfalfa, 12:00 and 18:00 - ryegrass) at an amount of 7.5 to 8 kg per horse per day. They also had free access to water. The owner of each horse provided consent for their participation in the study prior to the experiment. The owner had the option to withdraw their horse from the study at any time. Consent was received for 19 horses. Before starting the study, horses’ medical records were reviewed, and they were subjected to clinical examination with routine hematology and biochemical analysis at rest conditions to ensure that they were in good condition and auscultated to ensure there were no cardiac abnormalities. Seven out of 19 horses who demonstrated lameness were excluded from the study. Before the study, all horses were familiarized with the cardiac measuring devices being attached to their bodies.

Equipment

The HR monitor (Polar Equine V800 Heart Rate Sensor; Polar Electro Oy, Finland) consisted of an electrode belt with a built-in transmitter and a wristwatch receiver. Water and conductive gel were used to improve electrode-skin contact. Data was uploaded to Polar Flow software and analyzed using Kubios HRV software (Kubios standard 3.2; Biomedical Signal Analysis Group, Department of Applied Physics, University of Kuopio, Finland). Measurements of HR and heart rate variability (HRV) were made after visual inspection, to correct any artifacts by setting the custom filter to 0.3.

Experimental design

Test environment

The experiments were conducted in a 30 × 70 m riding arena, located in a building adjacent to the stables at the equestrian training center and familiar to the horses.

Test objects

A white plastic bag was used as the stimuli for the startle test. The plastic bag was tied to the end of a slender, flexible 1 m long stick. The plastic bag is a complex stimulus combining visual, auditory, and tactile elements [6]. To ensure consistency between stimuli, each animal was tested with a newly air-inflated, 2 L sealed plastic bag. Horses’ prior exposure to a similar novel stimulus, either in terms of location or duration, is extremely unlikely under normal, racetrack, husbandry conditions.

Test procedures

The pre-training tests were conducted at the equestrian training center one week before entering the three-month training and the post-training tests were conducted one week after the end of the training period. The test involved observing the behavioral response and measuring the cardiac autonomic parameters of each horse. Subjects were equipped with a HR monitor 10 min before the test, in the horse’s home environment (half-open stable), to establish a baseline 2-min recording.

A halter with a 5 m lead rope was used to lead the horses. A handler, known to the horses, was responsible for the subject in the test area. The handler did not change during the experiment. The horse (equipped with HR equipment) was led to the arena and positioned at a pre-determined location, held loosely by its lead rope. During this period, the experimenter stood quietly next to the horse without touching it. A second experimenter familiar with the horse measured HR. The horse was left undisturbed until it displayed signs of quietness consistent with its baseline HR. A third experimenter who was hiding the plastic bag back passed it to the first experimenter when the horse was ready.

The experimenter then gently touched the area between the horse’s left shoulder and neck with the plastic bag, at a rate of approximately once per second, moving the bag within a 30 cm range. If the horse exhibited an escape response, the tester loosened the lead rope but kept startling the horse. To maintain consistency, the same experimenter performed the tests on all horses.

Cardiac parameter analysis

Cardiac autonomic responses, as a measure of reactivity to being startled by a plastic bag, were examined before entering the training program. During the test, a HR monitor was strapped to the chest of the horses and HR was continuously monitored. A time series of cardiac measures was generated. Six parameters were recorded: baseline HR, peak HR in response to startling, the time taken for the peak HR to return to baseline HR (response duration; RD), the root mean square of successive differences (rMSSD) between normal heartbeats, standard deviation of normal-to-normal interval (SDNN), and the ratio of low frequency to high frequency power (LF/HF ratio). The same testing regime was applied after the three-month transition training period.

Behavioral analysis of reactivity

Following completion of all tests, a behavioral score (BS) was assigned to each horse. The BS was adapted from a scoring system used by Christensen et al. [14]. The BS of the horses to the stimuli was evaluated by three experimenters using a five-score scale to describe each horse’s behavior (Table 1). Half units were permitted if the behavior was assessed to be genuinely in-between the whole number scores. The mean BS for each horse was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using open-source Python version 3.7.12. The obtained data did not pass the Shapiro-Wilk normality test (p < 0.05). The outcome variables were analyzed using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess if there were differences before or after the training within each group. Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to assess if there were differences between groups before and after training. Spearman correlation coefficients between BS and cardiac parameters were calculated for all horses. Given that horse trainers and riders may have preconceived ideas regarding horse temperament based on sex and age [15], the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Mann-Whitney U test were conducted to assess the differences between two sex groups and two age groups before and after transition training. Due to the small and skew towards the 4- and 5-year-old age group, data was allocated to two groups: ≤ 4 years old (n = 7) and ≥ 5 years old (n = 5).

In determining the sample size needed for this study, an initial a priori sample calculation was conducted. This involved utilizing an effect size of 0.85, an α of 0.05, and a β of 0.2 (80% power) for comparing behavioral and autonomic responses between different test events. The outcome indicated a minimum sample size requirement of at least 12 individuals. Additionally, a post hoc sample calculation was performed. This involved determining the effect size through calculations involving the mean, standard deviation, and correlation of behavior and HR, with an α of 0.05. Both behavior and HR exhibited a power of 0.99. G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4) facilitated these calculations, adhering to the recommendations of Beck [16] and Faul et al. [17].

Additional analysis was conducted on the horses’ scores, taking into account the results of the BS from the post-training test. The New South Wales (NSW) Government’s Code of Practice, “Managing risks when new or inexperienced riders or handlers interact with horses in the workplace,” states that new or inexperienced riders or handlers are more at risk of harm. The NSW Code identifies that horses’ acting unpredictably, not being properly trained, or not being assessed as suitable to interact with new or inexperienced riders or handlers, are potential hazards for ensuring compliance with health and safety duties in the Work Health and Safety Act [18]. The horses assigned a score of 2 or above in the current study, were considered to display behaviors consistent with the hazards identified in the NSW Code and hence unsuitable for new or inexperienced riders or handlers and could be perceived as less suitable for equestrian sports.

Intra-observer reliability was calculated using Cohen’s weighted κ (Cκ) comparing the agreement of BS evaluated twice by each observer. Linear weighted kappa assigns equal weights to all disagreements, while quadratic weighted kappa assigns greater weights to more severe disagreements, making it more sensitive to larger discrepancies. To evaluate the inter-observer reliability, a Fleiss’ κ was calculated. Kappa values were interpreted using the following thresholds: 0 no agreement, 0.01–0.20 poor agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement, ≥ 0.81 near perfect agreement [19].

RESULTS

The horses showed a range of responses, from little reaction to a heightened degree of alertness, in response to the plastic bag being touched over the neck area. BS (p < 0.01), peak HR (p < 0.001), and RD (p < 0.001) after the training were significantly reduced in all horses, when compared to before the training (Fig. 1). Near-perfect intra-observer agreement was observed in both intra- and inter-observer reliability assessments for BS evaluation (Table 2). BS after the training were significantly reduced in male (p < 0.05), female (p < 0.05), 3 and 4 years old group (p < 0.05), when compared to before the training, however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the sexes and between ages both before and after the training. Peak HR and RD after the training were significantly reduced in male (p < 0.05), female (p < 0.05), 3 and 4 years old group (p < 0.05) and 5 and 6 years old group (p < 0.05), when compared to before the training, however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the sexes and between ages both before and after the training (Table 3).

Upon further analysis, a significant difference in RD was observed between the two categories both before (p < 0.05) and after (p < 0.05) the training (Fig. 2). Additionally, the RD of horses with a post-training BS below 2 significantly decreased compared to the pre-training RD (p < 0.01), while the RD of horses with a post-training BS score 2 and above did not show a significant change (p = 0.25) (Fig. 3).

rMSSD (p = 0.450), SDNN (p = 0.875), and LF/HR ratio (p = 0.239) after the training were not significantly different in all horses, when compared to before the training (Table 3). Although no statistically significant differences were observed in rMSSD and SDNN before or after the training for all horses and categories, it is worth noting that the mean values of rMSSD and SDNN of all males were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than the mean value of all females, when measured before the training. No statistically significant differences in rMSSD and SDNN were observed between the sexes and between ages after the training. The mean rMSSD in females was significantly increased after training (p < 0.05), while the mean SDNN after training was increased in females, although this was not statistically significant (Table 3). No statistically significant differences in the LF/HF ratio were observed between the sexes and between ages both before and after the training.

Correlations were calculated between peak HR, RD, and BS. The correlation between BS and peak HR (r = 0.94, p < 0.001) was higher than the correlation between BS and RD (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), and the correlation between peak HR and RD (r = 0.81, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the response of retired Thoroughbreds to a three-month period of transition training and assessed if an anecdotal threshold for behavior rating could be useful for screening horses better suited to equestrian sports. Horses typically react to unknown circumstances or possible threats by fleeing or avoidance, however, over time they become accustomed to their surroundings and non-threatening stimuli [20]. All horses demonstrated significant reduction in reactivity after the three-month transition training. The BS and peak HR were significantly decreased and the RD was significantly shortened, after the training in all horses. These results are likely due to the calmer nature of basic equestrian training, which involved tasks such as standing still for mounting and dismounting without additional assistance of other people, etc. [21,22].

Nevertheless, 25% of the horses received a BS of 2 or higher after the training, posing a potential hazard for inexperienced riders and handlers according to the NSW Code of Practice. Furthermore, a significant difference in BS was observed between horses with a post-training BS of 2 and above and those with a post-training BS below 2, both before and after the training. Previous studies showed similar results with horses demonstrating more sensitive behavioral responses, with higher HRs before training and lower HRs after training, when subjected to the startle test [5,23]. In the present study, horses with post-training BS of 2 and above did not exhibit a significantly diminished fear response to challenging objects or situations following the training, with a marked difference when compared to horses with BS of below 2, future research is warranted to explore whether extended or more intensive transition training could enhance horses’ post-training responses of BS 2 and above to match those of horses with BS of below 2. Guidelines could be established for assessing horses before transition training, and horses scoring 2 or higher could undergo tailored training protocols designed to enhance post-training outcomes.

Measurement of HRV is a non-invasive technique that can be used to investigate the function of the autonomic nervous system. HRV measures have been used in animal research, to analyze changes in sympathovagal balance related to psychological and environmental stressors or individual characteristics such as temperament [24]. In horses, HRV is used to assess the limbic system and sympathetic nervous system’s response to harmful events like pain, fear, stress, or anxiety. These events trigger physiological changes such as tachycardia, tachypnea, hypertension, and hyperthermia, which are linked to increased HRV. These modifications are often accompanied by altered emotional states, poor rest, and even aggression, situations observed in the present study, which in the long term would favor that animals may show deficiencies in their ability to cope with changes in the environment due to alterations in the functioning of their immune, nervous and endocrinological systems [25,26,27,28,29,30]. Though a high correlation, reliability, and agreement between HRV measured by Polar and electrocardiogram has been reported [31], the usefulness of HRV data in horses has nevertheless come under scrutiny. HRV data may be less reliable when horses are in motion or moving [24,32] or the measurement duration is short. It is generally recommended to use HRV data collected for at least 5 min [33,34,35]. In this study, the mean duration to reach peak HR was 30.58 sec, and any additional stimulus was discontinued once peak HR was attained.

Females showed considerably increased mean rMSSD and SDNN after training compared to before training. Even though females are considered to be more sensitive than males [36], this study confirms that both sexes can be desensitized following transition training. The rMSSD and SDNN values showed significant variability among the horses, and there were inconsistent correlations observed with other test outcomes. A previous equine study found that rMSSD values measured when horses were more static (compared to this experiment) were more reliable [37]. The movement of the horses when responding to the startle test in this study, likely impacted the usefulness of rMSSD as an indicator. Similar to rMSSD, SDNN values also exhibited significant differences between the sexes before the training. This finding aligns with previous research that has identified a positive correlation between rMSSD and SDNN [23]. However, this study did not find a significant difference in the LF/HF ratio, whereas a significant increase in the LF/HF ratio was detected after the startle test with horses in another study [12]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the short duration of measurement in this study.

Significant correlations between BS and cardiac variables during novel object tests in horses have been reported [38,39,40]. The present study aligns with previous research and indicates that the correlation between BS and peak HR will be a useful observation, to assess the suitability of retired Thoroughbreds for equestrian sports. This study was conducted on a relatively small number of animals and future studies could be strengthened by enlarging the number of test animals. A practical challenge to study design was the limited availability of sufficient numbers of professional equestrian riders for the three-month transition training.

Based on the observations in the present study, it is suggested that retired Thoroughbreds from racing can be evaluated for behavioral traits suitable for equestrian sports prior, to commencing any transition training. The importance of transition training cannot be underestimated in preparing horses for careers after racing. The startle model employed in the current study can be easily reproduced and provides a practical evaluation of horses’ responses that can assist in planning the transition training. It is important to note that this study was conducted using experienced riders and handlers. The NSW Government’s Code of Practice identifies horse handling risks to new or inexperienced riders. While it may be useful to utilize inexperienced staff in experimental design this was not feasible due to requirements of the Occupational Safety and Health Act in Korea (Article 140), which state that in the case of dangerous and skill-intensive work, a person who lacks the necessary experience or skills should not be allowed to do the work. Additional behavioral measures, such as spontaneous and forced approach tests to analyze horse-human interactions, as well as tracking the time each horse takes to reach specific training milestones, would provide deeper insights into horses’ reactivity and their relationships with humans. This approach would likely lead to a more thorough evaluation of safety and suitability. Future studies will be necessary to develop additional transition training techniques with additional behavioral measures that may enhance the responses of reactive horses after the three-month training employed in this study.
