Patient and Attendant Satisfaction With Services at Medical College Hospital in Bangladesh: A Cross-Sectional Survey
Authors: Amit Bhowmick, Sumaiya Tohfa Afindi, Suchandana Rani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient satisfaction is a key indicator of healthcare quality, particularly in resource-constrained public health systems. Despite growing evidence on healthcare service quality in Bangladesh, comprehensive assessments incorporating both patients and attendants remain limited. This study evaluated satisfaction with healthcare services at Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital using the SERVQUAL framework.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 400 respondents, including 167 patients and 233 attendants, selected through multi-stage cluster sampling. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire measuring five SERVQUAL dimensions: tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. Descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, chi-square tests, and binary logistic regression were employed for data analysis.ResultsReliability and assurance received the highest ratings, particularly accurate diagnosis (mean = 3.96), reliable test results (mean = 3.99), and confidence in specialist doctors (mean = 4.07). In contrast, tangibility recorded the lowest ratings, with 74.6% dissatisfied with bed availability and 70.3% dissatisfied with seating arrangements. Empathy scores revealed concerns regarding equitable care, especially for poor patients (mean = 2.22). Independent-samples t-tests showed no significant differences between patients’ and attendants’ perceptions across SERVQUAL dimensions or overall satisfaction. Logistic regression identified assurance (OR = 6.86), empathy (OR = 4.64), tangibility (OR = 2.47), and responsiveness (OR = 2.08) as significant predictors of overall satisfaction, whereas reliability was not significant. Age, education, profession, and frequency of hospital visits were associated with satisfaction, while gender and income were not.ConclusionsInterpersonal aspects of care, particularly assurance and empathy, are the strongest determinants of satisfaction in a public medical college hospital in Bangladesh. Although respondents expressed confidence in healthcare providers, substantial deficiencies in infrastructure and perceived inequities in care for vulnerable populations remain. Addressing these gaps is essential for improving patient-centered service delivery and healthcare quality in public hospitals.Keywords: patient satisfaction, service quality, SERVQUAL, healthcare, government hospital, Bangladesh

Introduction

It is well known that patient satisfaction is a universal metric of healthcare quality in any health system worldwide as it is a result of interaction of organizational structure, clinical practices, and interpersonal relationships. It is proven that in a supportive work environment and low patient-nurse ratio, hospitals perform markedly better in patient satisfaction and higher care outcomes, such as lower mortality, decreased readmissions and shorter hospitalization periods.1,2 In a study of 540 hospitals across four US states, better nurse staffing (fewer patients per nurse), a higher proportion of bachelor’s-educated nurses, and a more favorable nurse work environment were each independently associated with higher Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) patient experience ratings. 3 The role of communication is identified as the most reliable predictor of satisfaction in various healthcare environments, 4 and empathy, technical skills, professionalism, on-time service, integration of technology, pain management, and patient-centered care contribute greatly to the positive patient experiences.5-8 Other cross-national studies also point out that the degree of satisfaction depends on the competence of doctors, their responsiveness to staffing, patient attributes, and their cultural diversity.9-13 Though the fact that measurement consists of heterogeneity across survey instruments does not preclude direct international comparisons, it does not reduce these broad patterns. 4

In the more developed countries, patient satisfaction studies have developed to investigate traditional dimensions of care as well as the new technological factors. The Japanese studies show that satisfaction is strongly predicted by physician communication and safety issues have negative impacts on patient experiences. 14 The dimensions of service quality such as admission procedures, medical services, discharge planning, and social responsibility justify a considerable difference in satisfaction. 15 There is continuity of care and family-centeredness, which contribute to the patient loyalty, 16 and telemedicine, which enhances patient satisfaction by minimizing costs, waiting time, and travel burden. 17 Also, emotional artificial intelligence can be accepted based on familiarity with technology and perceived control, and with social engagement positively associated with positive attitudes towards care innovations. 18 The Diagnosis Procedure Combination, used in Japan, represents a payment system that helps to evaluate the outcomes comprehensively. 19

By contrast, the healthcare system in the developing and post-conflict countries has underlying issues that influence the levels of satisfaction in a different way. The waiting time is always found to be the best negative predictor of satisfaction, with 66% variance and the importance of making improvements to the operations rather than upgrading the facilities. 20 The systematic reviews have proven that competence, communication, responsiveness, drug availability, and queuing systems have a strong impact on satisfying and patient loyalty. 21 Facility adequacy, quality care, and doctors and nurses positive perceptions increase in patient satisfaction, 22 whereas SERVQUAL dimensions also increase the perceived value and intention to revisit. 23

SERVQUAL has been applied across diverse healthcare settings to assess service quality and patient satisfaction. In a study of 400 patients across 13 hospitals in Qatar, patients reported high overall service quality (mean 4.31/5). Among the five SERVQUAL dimensions, empathy had the greatest impact on patient satisfaction (β = 0.23), while assurance had the lowest (β = 0.05). 24 Indonesian research confirmed that all SERVQUAL dimensions simultaneously and significantly influence patient satisfaction, with individual contributions ranging from 10% to 33.2%. 25 In Turkey, despite patients’ expectations not being fully met, their satisfaction levels with hospital services remained high, supporting SERVQUAL’s utility in predicting revisit intentions. 26 Vietnamese hospitals in Ho Chi Minh City showed considerable room for quality enhancement, with public hospitals scoring higher satisfaction gaps than private ones. 27 Indian public hospitals successfully improved patient satisfaction by implementing basic enhancements in responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. 28 Paraguayan insured patients expressed 73.7% overall satisfaction with external consultation services, though the quick response dimension scored lowest, highlighting areas for further improvement. 29

The same trends are visible throughout East African contexts. Ethiopian researches indicate that total satisfaction is 63.7, and the highest is labor and delivery services (79.6) and the lowest inpatient services (51.1). 30 The satisfaction with referral services is 73.5% with a significant impact on the communication and coordination with providers. 31 South Wollo has an outpatient satisfaction of 64.4, which is considerably influenced by the availability of drugs, the politeness of the providers (physicians), and privacy. 32 It has higher satisfaction in the emergency department (81.9), which is limited by waiting times and physical environment. 33 According to ward-specific studies, there is less satisfaction with laboratory services, health education, and privacy, and sociodemographic factors influence the perception.34,35

Outpatient research in South Sudan indicates that there is great discontent with cleanliness, waiting time, comfort in facilities, and politeness of staff. 36 Nevertheless, the level of satisfaction with the health worker attitude and communication in terms of the antenatal and maternal services at Juba Teaching Hospital is high (78 to 93). 37 Conflict regions Field hospitals are highly satisfied with medical care and lowly with the quality of food. 38 In Sudan and Somalia, researchers find positive correlations between satisfaction and older age, female sex, and low-income status and long waiting times, poor physical environments, and the lack of privacy negatively impact experiences.39-41

Burundi is a post-conflict environment with compounded challenges. The emergency care preparedness at district hospitals is in severe flaws as only one-fourth of them offers 24/7 physician services and have reduced access to diagnostics. 42 Nurses have the sufficient knowledge but face structural obstacles such as a lack of training and staffing shortage. 43 There is a lack of workforce and competence of the providers in emergency obstetric facilities despite being very expensive to train. 44 The data is poorly managed and leads to poor performance of hospital information systems, 45 as irrational drug use is common. 46 The patient satisfaction underlines the relevance of reception, hygiene, attitude towards providers, management of waiting time, and governance in determining the utilization of services.47,48

The lower-middle-income nation of Bangladesh with a resource-constrained state healthcare system has numerous issues that can be seen in other developing environments. Hospital Improvement Initiative in Sylhet division enhanced outpatient waiting and consultation time and disclosed unresolved weaknesses: there was a decrease in medicine availability, dirty toilets, and insufficient privacy. 49 These problems are verified by modern research. In the Shaheed M. Monsur Ali Medical College Hospital, patients were generally positive in their interaction with the physician, but serious gaps were still observed in terms of poor sanitation, availability of medications, time to consult women, and the efficiency of the administration. 50 The level of satisfaction was highly affected by demographics. The study conducted within the Dhaka population hospitals indicated that the level of satisfaction was low due to inadequate communication, fast consultations, privacy issues, lack of equipment, and long wait times. 51 In previous research works at Dhaka medical college hospital, patient overload, partial medication supply and poor behavior of the staff were found among the recurring themes. 52

Systemic reviews demonstrate that there are more structural barriers: low doctor-to-patient ratios, extremely short consultations, high expenditures out of pocket, shortage of facilities in rural areas, unregulated drug dispensing, 53 and increasing medical tourism due to dissatisfaction. 49 Facility-specific investigations highlight the lack of diagnostic services, equipment, human resources, privacy, and hygiene. 54 The consistent comparative reports show that private hospitals are more satisfied than public ones due to the service orientation of the doctors and responsiveness of the staff involved but the cost burden is an issue.55-57

Knowledge Gap and Contribution of the Study

Although patient satisfaction has been widely studied worldwide, important gaps remain in the Bangladeshi context. Existing studies have primarily focused on specific aspects of healthcare services or particular patient groups, with limited use of comprehensive frameworks such as SERVQUAL in public medical college hospitals. Moreover, the perspectives of attendants, who play a significant role in patient care, have received little attention in previous research. Additionally, evidence regarding the relative influence of different service quality dimensions on overall satisfaction remains limited.

This study addresses these gaps by applying the SERVQUAL framework to assess service quality among both patients and attendants in a government medical college hospital. The study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it provides a comprehensive assessment of healthcare service quality across the five SERVQUAL dimensions - tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. Second, it incorporates the perspectives of both patients and attendants, thereby offering a more holistic understanding of healthcare experiences. Third, it identifies the specific service quality dimensions that significantly predict overall satisfaction through multivariate analysis. Finally, the findings provide evidence-based insights for policymakers and hospital administrators to design targeted interventions aimed at improving service quality, enhancing patient-centered care, and strengthening the performance of public healthcare institutions in Bangladesh.

Hypotheses Development

The SERVQUAL model proposes that service quality is a multidimensional construct comprising tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. Previous studies have demonstrated that these dimensions significantly influence patient satisfaction in healthcare settings. Based on the SERVQUAL framework and existing empirical evidence, the following hypotheses are proposed:

HA1: The SERVQUAL dimensions (tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy) are positively associated with overall satisfaction among patients and attendants receiving healthcare services at a public hospital.

HA2: The SERVQUAL dimensions significantly predict overall satisfaction among patients and attendants after controlling for socio-demographic characteristics.

HA3: There is a significant difference in service quality perceptions between patients and attendants across the five SERVQUAL dimensions at a government medical college hospital in Bangladesh.

Methods

The reporting of this cross-sectional study conforms to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement for cross-sectional studies. 62 The completed STROBE checklist is provided as a Supplemental file.

In this study, a cross-sectional survey method was used. The research area was selected as Sylhet City Corporation because it serves as a major urban and healthcare hub for the entire Sylhet Division in northeastern Bangladesh. The city hosts one of the largest tertiary-level public hospitals, Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital, which provides healthcare services to a large population across multiple districts of the region. This hospital alone serves nearly nine million people in Sylhet Division, indicating its critical role in delivering public healthcare services. 63 In addition, Sylhet has recently been included in initiatives aimed at digital transformation of public healthcare services, positioning its major medical college hospital as a leading institution in adopting digital health management systems. This ongoing transformation further increases the relevance of studying patient satisfaction in this setting. 64

The population of this study consisted of all patients and their attendants who received healthcare services from Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital located in Sylhet City Corporation. Eligible respondents comprised patients or attendants aged 18 years or older who were residents of the Sylhet City Corporation area. Individuals were excluded if they were below 18 years of age or were unable to respond to the survey due to physical or mental conditions that hindered meaningful participation. Attendants were included only if they were directly involved in the patient’s care and were familiar with the healthcare services received. To minimize potential information bias, the questionnaire focused on observable aspects of healthcare service quality and care experiences that could be reasonably assessed by both patients and attendants. Furthermore, the same standardized questionnaire and data collection procedures were applied to all respondents to ensure consistency.

The sample size for this study was determined by following the minimalistic approach to sample size calculation. 65 Based on the minimalistic approach,

A multi-stage cluster sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, 20 wards were randomly selected from the 42 wards of Sylhet City Corporation using the lottery method to ensure geographical representation of hospital service users. In the second stage, one mahalla (neighbourhood) was randomly selected from each selected ward. Subsequently, from each mahalla, 20 respondents (patients and their attendants) who were receiving services at Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital were selected through simple random sampling during the data collection period. A sampling frame was prepared in each selected mahalla by listing all eligible patients and attendants who had received healthcare services from Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital and met the inclusion criteria. Each eligible individual was assigned a unique identification number, and 20 respondents were selected using a lottery-based simple random sampling procedure.

After obtaining ethical approval on October 7, 2025, the study was conducted over a period of six months, from mid-October 2025 to March 2026. Data were collected using a structured patient satisfaction questionnaire developed through a review of relevant literature and based on the SERVQUAL framework originally proposed by 58 The measurement items for the five SERVQUAL dimensions - tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy were adapted from previous healthcare service quality and patient satisfaction studies, particularly27,55,59 The items were linguistically and contextually modified to reflect the healthcare service environment of Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital and the broader public healthcare context of Bangladesh. The questionnaire consisted of 38 items divided into seven sections. Five sections measured service quality, including tangibility (six items), reliability (five items), responsiveness (five items), assurance (five items), and empathy (seven items). An additional section collected socio-demographic information (seven items), while the final section assessed overall satisfaction using three items adapted from previous healthcare satisfaction studies.60,61

All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Highly Dissatisfied) to 5 (Highly Satisfied), where higher scores indicated higher levels of perceived service quality and satisfaction. A composite overall satisfaction score was calculated by summing the three satisfaction items (range: 3–15). For binary logistic regression analysis, the score was dichotomized using a theoretically defined threshold: respondents scoring ≥12 were categorized as satisfied (1), while those scoring <12 were categorized as not satisfied (0). This threshold corresponds to an average item score of at least 4 (satisfied) on the 5-point Likert scale, which is commonly used in patient satisfaction studies to indicate a positive evaluation of services.

Service quality was evaluated across the five SERVQUAL dimensions. Composite scores were calculated for each dimension and dichotomized using a cutoff equivalent to an average item score of 4 (“satisfied”). The thresholds applied were tangibility ≥24, reliability ≥20, responsiveness ≥20, assurance ≥20, and empathy ≥28. These binary variables were subsequently used in chi-square tests and binary logistic regression to examine associations and identify predictors of overall patient satisfaction. The reliability of the SERVQUAL dimensions was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which ranged from .753 to .908, indicating good internal consistency. Construct validity was confirmed through the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure (0.946) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (p < 0.001), demonstrating the adequacy of the data for factor analysis. 66

At the end of the data collection phase, data were entered into SPSS and categorized. Then, these data were analyzed and presented by using both descriptive (percentage, mean, standard deviation) and inferential (t-test, chi-square, and binary logistic regression) statistics. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards to ensure integrity, confidentiality, and the protection of participants. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, and they were fully informed about the purpose of the study, their voluntary participation, and the use of the data. To maintain confidentiality, all respondent data were anonymized, and no personal identifiers were recorded. The research was conducted objectively, with honesty in data collection, analysis, and reporting, ensuring that findings accurately reflect participants’ responses. All procedures prioritized the emotional and physical comfort of respondents, and no coercion was applied at any stage.

Socio-Demographic Differentials in SERVQUAL Dimensions (N = 400)

Age differences were notable, with respondents aged 31–59 years demonstrating the highest satisfaction across all dimensions (tangibility: M = 17.53, SD = 3.76; reliability: M = 20.34, SD = 4.56; responsiveness: M = 17.05, SD = 3.99; assurance: M = 20.43, SD = 4.22; empathy: M = 24.12, SD = 4.55). Younger respondents (18–30 years) reported comparatively lower scores, while those above 60 years had moderate perceptions.

Gender differences showed that male respondents reported higher satisfaction than females across all dimensions, particularly in empathy (M = 23.58, SD = 4.92 vs. M = 21.73, SD = 5.68) and assurance (M = 19.11, SD = 4.60 vs. M = 18.15, SD = 5.34).

Education level was inversely related to perceived service quality; respondents with primary education reported the highest mean scores across dimensions (e.g., reliability: M = 19.95, SD = 4.54), whereas those with higher education reported lower scores (e.g., tangibility: M = 12.43, SD = 3.88; empathy: M = 18.73, SD = 5.48).

Profession also influenced satisfaction, with drivers exhibiting the highest scores across all dimensions, particularly reliability (M = 22.59, SD = 2.43) and empathy (M = 25.33, SD = 2.71). Students reported the lowest satisfaction across most dimensions.

Income differences indicated that respondents earning less than BDT 10,000 per month reported relatively higher satisfaction, especially for empathy (M = 23.54, SD = 5.33), while those earning above BDT 70,000 reported lower scores, notably in tangibility (M = 13.42, SD = 4.12) and reliability (M = 14.83, SD = 4.42).

Frequency of hospital visits also affected perceptions. Repeated visitors (74.8%) reported higher satisfaction across all dimensions compared to first-time visitors, particularly in reliability (M = 19.53, SD = 4.88 vs. M = 15.55, SD = 4.04) and assurance (M = 19.57, SD = 4.89 vs. M = 15.80, SD = 4.31).

In summary, the findings indicate that age, gender, education, profession, income, and visit frequency are associated with variations in patient and attendant perceptions of hospital service quality, with older adults, males, lower-educated respondents, and frequent visitors generally reporting higher satisfaction across the SERVQUAL dimensions.

Model 1 (Demographics only) showed that gender and education were significant predictors of satisfaction. Female respondents were less likely than males to report satisfaction (OR = 0.545, 95% CI [0.306, 0.969], p < .05). Educational attainment was inversely associated with satisfaction, with respondents having higher secondary education (OR = 0.298, 95% CI [0.127, 0.701], p < .01) and higher education (OR = 0.146, 95% CI [0.050, 0.423], p < .001) being less likely to report satisfaction compared to respondents with primary education. Age above 60 (OR = 0.416, 95% CI [0.163, 1.058]) and frequency of visit (repeated vs. first-time) (OR = 2.114, 95% CI [1.215, 3.678], p < .01) also influenced satisfaction, with repeated visitors being more likely to report satisfaction. Other demographic factors, including profession and income, were not significant predictors in this model.

Model 2 (Demographics + Service Quality) revealed that several service quality dimensions significantly predicted overall satisfaction, above and beyond demographic factors. Respondents satisfied with tangibility (OR = 2.474, 95% CI [1.409, 4.344], p < .01), responsiveness (OR = 2.075, 95% CI [1.125, 3.827], p < .05), assurance (OR = 6.859, 95% CI [3.031, 15.522], p < .001), and empathy (OR = 4.637, 95% CI [2.559, 8.400], p < .001) were significantly more likely to report overall satisfaction. Among demographic predictors, respondents with secondary (OR = 0.479, 95% CI [0.244, 0.942], p < .05) or higher (OR = 0.215, 95% CI [0.057, 0.812], p < .05) education were still less likely to report satisfaction. Other demographic variables, including gender, age, profession, income, and frequency of visit, were not significant when service quality factors were included.

Model fit statistics indicated good explanatory power for Model 2, with Cox & Snell R2 = .389 and Nagelkerke R2 = .522. The Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients was significant (χ2 = 197.33, p < .001), and the Hosmer and Lemeshow test indicated adequate fit (p = .341).

These findings suggest that while some demographic factors influence satisfaction, perceptions of service quality particularly assurance, empathy, tangibility, and responsiveness are the strongest predictors of overall patient and attendant satisfaction in government medical hospitals.

Discussion

This study evaluated patient and attendant satisfaction with healthcare services at a government medical college hospital in Bangladesh using the SERVQUAL framework 58 which conceptualises service quality as the perceived gap between expectations and experiences across five dimensions: reliability, assurance, tangibility, empathy, and responsiveness. The findings are discussed below in relation to extant SERVQUAL-based literature from comparable healthcare settings, with attention to both convergent and divergent patterns, and with critical interpretation of the theoretical and contextual factors that underlie them.

The findings indicate that age, education, profession, and frequency of hospital visits were significant socio-demographic predictors of overall satisfaction, whereas gender and income were not retained in the final model. Respondents aged 31–59 years reported higher satisfaction than younger and older age groups. Rather than simply reflecting generational differences, this pattern likely indicates that middle-aged individuals hold more calibrated expectations of public healthcare expectations shaped by accumulated experience with institutional constraints - thereby narrowing the gap between perceived and expected service that lies at the core of the SERVQUAL framework. 58 This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence from comparable settings. 39 Education was inversely associated with satisfaction, which can be theoretically attributed to the expectation-perception gap: more educated respondents tend to hold higher service quality standards, making the same level of service delivery less likely to meet or exceed their expectations.55,57 Frequent visitors, conversely, reported higher satisfaction, suggesting that repeated exposure to the hospital environment reduces uncertainty and moderates negative perceptions of service gaps. 32 The absence of a significant gender effect diverges from SERVQUAL-based findings in other developing country settings where gender consistently predicted satisfaction differences,39,41 suggesting that gender-based evaluative differences in service quality may be context-specific rather than universal - a distinction with implications for how demographic subgroup analyses are prioritised in SERVQUAL research.

Among the five SERVQUAL dimensions, reliability and assurance received the highest ratings, particularly regarding accurate diagnosis, reliable test results, and confidence in physicians’ expertise. From a SERVQUAL perspective, these high ratings indicate a narrow expectation-perception gap in the technical domain of care. This pattern converges with SERVQUAL-based evidence from Indonesia, where 25 identified reliability and assurance as the strongest contributors to patient satisfaction in public hospitals, and from Turkey, where 26 similarly found that patients prioritise clinical trustworthiness and technical competence in their service quality evaluations. Comparable results were reported in Qatar, where assurance - specifically confidence in medical staff - emerged as the highest-rated SERVQUAL dimension. 24 The convergence of findings across these diverse national contexts suggests that clinical competence and physician trust function as consistent cross-contextual anchors of patient satisfaction in public hospital settings, irrespective of broader infrastructural conditions.14,22 However, as the regression analysis reveals, high descriptive ratings on reliability did not translate into predictive significance at the multivariate level - a divergence that is theoretically important and is addressed in a subsequent section.

In contrast, tangibility and responsiveness represented the most significant areas of weakness. Respondents expressed substantial dissatisfaction with hospital cleanliness, seating arrangements, and bed availability - deficiencies that reflect a wide expectation-perception gap on the physical dimension of care. These findings are consistent with a body of SERVQUAL-based research conducted in Bangladesh that has documented persistent infrastructural inadequacies across public hospital settings,49,50,54 indicating that tangibility gaps are not facility-specific but systemic in nature. Internationally, comparable SERVQUAL findings have been reported in Vietnam, where deficiencies in physical facilities and cleanliness were the primary contributors to service quality gaps in public hospitals, 27 and in Qatar, where inadequate physical infrastructure similarly widened the tangibility gap. 24 Responsiveness also scored poorly, particularly regarding staff helpfulness and complaint management. This mirrors SERVQUAL evidence from other resource-constrained healthcare systems, where operational inefficiencies in staff responsiveness are consistently among the lowest-rated dimensions. 20 Critically, the parallel between Bangladesh and these international SERVQUAL findings implies that tangibility and responsiveness deficits are not idiosyncratic features of a single institution but reflect structural underfunding and human resource management challenges that are endemic to public hospitals in low- and middle-income country contexts - a systemic problem that cannot be resolved through clinical improvement alone but requires sustained policy-level investment.

The empathy dimension revealed a more nuanced picture. Respondents reported moderate satisfaction with respect, dignity, and confidentiality, suggesting that the basic norms of patient-centred interaction are broadly observed. However, markedly low ratings for the care of elderly and economically disadvantaged patients indicate a critical and inequitably distributed empathy gap. Within the SERVQUAL framework, empathy is defined as the provision of caring, individualised attention to patients25,58 identified this dimension as among the most influential predictors of satisfaction in Indonesian public hospitals, underscoring its theoretical centrality. The equity dimension of the empathy gap identified in the present study extends this literature by demonstrating that empathy deficits are not uniformly experienced but disproportionately affect vulnerable populations - a pattern documented in low-resource and post-conflict settings where structural constraints reduce the quality of individualised care for marginalised groups.47,48 Importantly, this finding suggests that the empathy gap cannot be attributed solely to resource scarcity: unlike tangibility deficits that demand capital investment, inequitable empathetic care may also reflect the absence of equity-oriented staff training and inclusive care protocols - organisational interventions that are feasible within existing resource constraints and represent an underexplored policy lever in the Bangladeshi public hospital context.

The regression analysis identified SERVQUAL service quality dimensions as substantially stronger predictors of overall satisfaction than socio-demographic characteristics. Among the dimensions, assurance and empathy emerged as the strongest independent predictors, followed by tangibility and responsiveness. This finding is consistent with the broader literature emphasising that interpersonal dimensions of care - trust, compassion, and communication - carry greater evaluative weight than technical service aspects in determining overall patient satisfaction.4,5 The dominant predictive role of assurance specifically aligns with SERVQUAL evidence from Qatar, where 24 found assurance to be the single strongest predictor of satisfaction in a resource-constrained public hospital setting - a finding that parallels the present results and suggests that physician trust may carry particular salience in contexts where patients have limited provider choice. The present findings, however, partially diverge from who reported that all five SERVQUAL dimensions significantly predicted satisfaction in Indonesian public hospitals. 25 This divergence is theoretically significant: it suggests that the relative predictive weight of individual SERVQUAL dimensions is not fixed but context-dependent, varying with the institutional setting, the baseline distribution of service quality across dimensions, and the expectations that patients bring to the clinical encounter.

A particularly noteworthy finding was that reliability despite receiving the highest descriptive ratings - did not retain significance as an independent predictor in the binary logistic regression analysis (Model 2). This result contrasts with several SERVQUAL-based studies in developing country settings where reliability consistently emerged as a key satisfaction determinant.21,23 This divergence can be theoretically explained through 67 distinction between technical quality - the accuracy and competence of what is delivered and functional quality - the interpersonal and environmental manner in which it is delivered. When technical quality is perceived as consistently adequate across the patient population, as the high reliability scores in this study indicate, it is unlikely to function as a differentiating predictor of satisfaction; instead, patients shift their evaluative focus toward functional dimensions. In other words, reliability appears to operate as a threshold condition: its absence generates dissatisfaction, but once it is perceived as satisfactory, further variation in reliability scores no longer drives meaningful differences in overall satisfaction. This interpretation implies that in settings where clinical reliability is broadly adequate, quality improvement efforts are likely to yield greater returns by targeting assurance and empathy - the dimensions that actively discriminate between satisfied and dissatisfied respondents rather than investing further in technical competence alone.

A further contribution of this study is the finding that patients and attendants held statistically similar perceptions across all five SERVQUAL dimensions and overall satisfaction. This extends the existing Bangladeshi literature, which has predominantly focused on patients alone, by demonstrating that attendants - who play an active role in the caregiving process - evaluate healthcare services through a comparable evaluative lens. The SERVQUAL framework posits that service quality perceptions are shaped by the gap between prior expectations and experienced service 58 the convergence between patient and attendant perceptions observed here suggests that both groups form similar reference points through shared exposure to the same service environment. This finding has a practical implication: while including attendants in service quality assessments adds breadth and representativeness to the data, the similarity in perceptions indicates that attendant perspectives provide a valid and complementary - rather than contradictory - account of healthcare experiences. Future assessments of service quality in Bangladeshi public hospitals should routinely incorporate attendants to ensure a more complete picture of the care environment.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that interpersonal aspects of care, particularly assurance and empathy, are the primary determinants of patient and attendant satisfaction in a public medical college hospital in Bangladesh. Contrary to expectations, despite the confidence in physicians and caring treatment becoming the major sources of satisfaction, the structural deficiencies such as inadequate bed availability, poor cleanliness, and insufficient seating arrangements continue to negatively affect service experiences. It is important to note that the level of satisfaction with care of elderly and poor patients has been critically low, which indicates the systemic inequities. A lack of substantial differences between the patients and the attendants indicates that there is no notable difference in the way the patients and attendants are treated, which supports the idea of involving attendants in the satisfaction outcomes. To improve the quality of the services, the policymakers and hospital managers must focus on investments with reference to physical infrastructure, improving staff training in terms of communication and empathy, and implementing specific interventions to provide vulnerable groups with balanced care. These gains will be needed in developing an effective, patient-centered, public healthcare system in Bangladesh.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single public medical college hospital, limiting generalizability to other healthcare settings in Bangladesh. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretations. In addition, the questionnaire was not pilot-tested before data collection, which may have affected the measurement properties of the instrument. Additionally, the study did not include qualitative insights, which could have provided deeper understanding of patient and attendant experiences.

Further Study

Future research should expand to multiple hospitals, including private and rural facilities, to enhance generalizability. Longitudinal studies are recommended to examine changes in satisfaction over time. Incorporating qualitative methods would provide deeper insights into patient experiences. Further studies should also explore healthcare provider perspectives and evaluate interventions aimed at improving empathy, infrastructure, and service responsiveness. Additionally, future research may employ structural equation modeling (e.g., PLS-SEM) to examine causal pathways between SERVQUAL dimensions and overall satisfaction, as well as to test mediational effects.
