Foveal crowding increases with age under limited viewing conditions
Authors: Noah Rappeport, Maria Lev, Uri Polat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is associated with changes in visual function, even in the absence of ocular pathology. While visual acuity remains largely intact, older individuals frequently report difficulties with real-world visual tasks. One potential contributing factor is visual crowding, the inability to recognize a target in the presence of surrounding clutter. Although peripheral crowding has been widely studied, foveal crowding was once considered controversial and has only been observed under specific conditions. Its relationship with aging, however, remains poorly understood. This study investigates the impact of aging on foveal crowding under both unlimited and limited viewing conditions. Thirty-three participants (ages 20–89) completed a letter recognition task with varying target-flanker spacing. Under unlimited viewing conditions, crowding effects were insignificant across age groups. However, when a brief presentation time was introduced, older participants exhibited a significantly greater crowding effect, with a pronounced decline in visual acuity. Reaction time also increased with age, particularly in the unlimited viewing condition, where older adults took longer to respond despite maintaining high accuracy. These findings suggest that aging primarily affects foveal crowding under time-limited conditions, likely due to declines in processing speed. The results highlight the importance of considering temporal constraints in visual assessments of older adults, with potential implications for daily activities such as reading and driving.

Introduction

Crowding is best described as the inability to identify a target that one fixates on when it is surrounded by visual clutter1–6. Targets that are easy to recognize when presented as an isolated object become increasingly more difficult to recognize in cluttered visual stimuli. They do not disappear from one’s sight and, even though they are detectable, they become much harder and sometimes impossible to identify. Crowding is therefore considered a fundamental bottleneck in object recognition, reflecting constraints in spatial integration within the visual cortex, whereby target and flankers are pooled together, preventing isolated identification even though the target remains visible.

Most research into crowding has been performed in peripheral vision, with foveal [center of visual field] crowding being considered rare7–11It has been shown that crowding is correlated with the size of receptive field1,6,12, hence it’s pronounced in the periphery, and in the fovea of amblyopes which is considered to have larger receptive fields1,6,13. Due to its high resolution and small perceptive field (the psychophysical equivalent of the visual cortex’s receptive field), foveal crowding is not simple to create14. Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated that crowding can indeed be observed in the fovea under specific conditions. For example, contour interactions at the resolution limit of the visual system have been shown to produce robust foveal crowding11. Other research has found that oculomotor factors contribute to shape foveal crowding, suggesting that eye movement dynamics also influence performance10,15. Recent findings also indicate that similarity and attentional demands can strongly modulate the magnitude of foveal crowding when using Landolt C stimuli9. In addition, it has been shown that foveal crowding is influenced by spacing and presentation time16. The larger the spacing between the target and the flankers and the longer the presentation time, the less crowding is found. For a single letter spacing between flanker and target, foveal crowding was revealed at stimulus presentations of less than 120ms16.

To further understand the mechanisms of foveal crowding, our lab has carried out extensive studies using the tumbling E paradigm across a range of presentation times and participant groups1,12,13,16–24. These studies measured the effect of crowding using tumbling E letters and employed presentation times ranging from 240 ms down to 30–40 ms13. These studies demonstrated that maximal crowding emerges at brief presentation times and diminishes with increasing the presentation time, with little or no crowding effect between 120 and 240 ms. However, the magnitude of the crowding effect varies across groups. In young participants, there was no crowding effect at120- 240 ms (0 logMar), while it reached to approximately 0.25 logMAR at 30 ms. However, in older participants with presbyopia (aging eye, average 48.8 ± 2.9, mean ± SD), the effect was larger. Interestingly, crowding in pilots with presbyopia was significantly less than age-matched presbyopes, but significantly higher than the young group17. Results from our lab also found an increased effect of foveal crowding in young participants with heterophoria (a latent misalignment of the eyes normally compensated by fusion)22 and in young participants with ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder)25. Moreover, training has been shown to reduce the magnitude of crowding17,21. Thus, the accumulated data from our lab has shown that the effect of crowding depends on several parameters such as the presentation time, age group, group type and training.

While these studies have deepened our understanding of crowding, they have almost exclusively focused on younger adults, or early stage presbyopes, leaving open the question of how crowding may be altered across the full aging process, from young adulthood through to old age.

As of 2020 there were over 1 billion people above the age of 6026. It has been well documented that even in the absence of pathological conditions, aging can cause various cognitive and perceptual changes such as visual acuity, contrast sensitivity27, and slower processing speed28,29.

Vision dominates sensory processing in the human brain and as such has been found to be our most prominent sense30,3132. Many older individuals complain of a decline in their visual capabilities, despite getting a clean bill of eye health. Adding to their frustration is being told that not only were no clinical pathologies found in their routine eye exam, but that they have 20/20 vision or close to 20/20 vision on the Snellen chart, which historically has been considered to be a benchmark for normal vision32. This discrepancy lies in the way visual acuity is measured. Visual acuity is largely measured at clinically ideal conditions. A black letter on a white background with maximum contrast. This condition does not fully represent or recreate real-life situations and conditions. The Smith-Kettlewell longitudinal study of vision of 900 people aged 58 and up showed that despite good visual acuity many people displayed vision impairment33. This study and additional later studies have shown deficits in various visual functions due to age, such as contrast sensitivity, low light and dark adaptation and visual processing speed, despite visual acuity being for the most part intact28,29,34.

Studies have suggested that processing speed has a main effect on visual functions35–37. In aging, it has been shown that visual processing speed declines and has been shown to stem from difficulty with divided attention tasks, leading to longer response times38,39. Our studies have shown that crowding has a major effect on processing speed as demonstrated by longer reaction time1,16,25,40.

Previous studies have shown conflicting results whether there is a difference between younger and older adults in mid peripheral, peripheral and parafoveal crowding3,41–43. Foveal crowding and the ageing process, however, have not been systematically researched until now. In this study we seek to provide insights into the effects the ageing process has on foveal crowding. Since crowding influences processing speed, which has been found to deteriorate with age, and because foveal crowding is most evident at brief presentation times, we hypothesized that aging would amplify the impact of crowding as presentation time decreases. We tested this hypothesis using two primary conditions, unlimited viewing duration and limited viewing duration, on a large age spectrum, including subjects in their late 80s, using a crowding-with-letter paradigm.

Methods

Participants

33 participants with a best corrected visual acuity of at least 20/40 (0.3 logMAR) participated in the limited viewing experiment, while 31 of the same participants took part also in the unlimited viewing experiment. The reason for the drop out of 2 of the participants was due to health reasons, causing an inability to continue participating in the research project. A visual acuity of 20/40 was chosen as the cutoff, as it represents functional vision and is widely recognized as the minimum requirement for driving an automobile44,45. Each participant was required to have undergone an eye exam with an ophthalmologist in the last year to ensure eye health was intact and that there were no underlying eye pathologies. The age of the participants was between 20 and 89 years (57.8 ± 24, mean ± STD).

Each participant underwent a full optometric exam by a certified optometrist to verify that functional visual acuity was at least 0.3 logMAR or better and were corrected when required in-order to obtain that acuity. A standard ETDRS (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study) “Tumbling E” chart was used to test visual acuity. The ETDRS chart is a standardized logMAR chart in clinical and scientific research that uses a fixed progression of letter sizes and an equal number of optotypes per line, providing improved reliability and reproducibility compared to the traditional Snellen chart46.

The study protocol was approved by the Internal Review Board (IRB) of Bar-Ilan University.

(ISU202007001). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All experimental protocols were performed following the guidelines provided by the committee approving the experiments.

Apparatus

Participants were seated at a fixed distance of 150 cm from the screen for both the unlimited and limited viewing experiments. The stimuli were presented on a Microsoft PC tablet with a 23 cm screen with the screen resolution set at 1920 × 1280 pixels and a refresh rate of 60 Hz. The pixel size was 0.012 cm (1920 pixel/23.0 cm = 83.5 pixels. For 1 cm/83.5, pixel = 0.012 cm). A custom developed psychophysics software (PSY, Bonneh, 2004) was used to run the experiment. The experiments were conducted in a darkened room and under binocular conditions. Targets and flankers were black and were displayed on a white screen. Gamma correction was applied.

Both the unlimited and limited viewing experiments had two parts, an uncrowded condition and afterwards a crowded condition. Participants were given a detailed explanation of the two stages of the experiment before starting. The limited viewing experiment was run first and was run 3 times with the first set being considered a trial run. The trial run was implemented to familiarize the participants with the experiment’s paradigm. The unlimited viewing experiment was run twice as the paradigm was familiar to the participants. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation throughout the experiments.

Stimuli and task – limited duration experiment

Uncrowded condition

The experiment was designed as a 3:1 adaptive staircase with a two-alternative forced choice (2AFC) paradigm and commenced with a large fixation circle. The participants were instructed to look at the circle and when ready to press any key. Once pressed, a large letter ‘E’ equivalent to the size of a 0.8 logMAR letter was displayed on the screen at one of two orientations, facing right, or facing left [see Fig. 1A]. The ‘E’ stimulus was chosen for consistency with the visual acuity testing procedure, as it has been shown to correlate strongly with ETDRS scores13, has been widely used in crowding research in our lab1,12,13,16–24, and directly targets the process of identification. The participants were instructed to press either the left or right button on the mouse accordingly. As the threshold was measured using an adaptive staircase 3:1 (3-down 1-up), when answered correctly 3 times in a row, the ‘E’ progressively became smaller by one step size of 0.1 log units, until no longer visible and a mistake was made, or when the smallest target was reached [see Fig. 2]. The smallest target was programmed at a display size of 5 pixels (0.12 cm), to avoid screen display distortion. When a mistake was made, an auditory cue was used as feedback and the target’s size was increased by one step size (0.1 log units) until it became visible again. Between each ‘E’ presentation a large fixation circle reappeared, and participants were instructed to use these intervals between presentations to blink and when ready to press any key to move on to the next ‘E’ presentation. The threshold was calculated once 79% accuracy was attained.

Crowded condition

Once the threshold was reached in the uncrowded condition, the program automatically moved on to the crowded condition. This part of the experiment was based on the principles of the first uncrowded condition, but differed from it as the target was presented in a crowded matrix format [see Fig. 1B, C]. There were 2 consecutive blocks in this part of the experiment. The first block had a one-letter spacing between the target and the flankers and the second block had a half-letter spacing between the target and flankers. The threshold detection method was identical to the first uncrowded condition. The target-flanker separation spacing remained constant in each block regardless of the display size.

The crowded and uncrowded conditions had a limited viewing duration element. The target and flankers were displayed for 120ms. The choice of a 120-ms presentation was based on Lev et al. (2014), and Siman-tov et al. (2025), who found no foveal crowding at one-letter spacing in younger participants at this presentation duration and minimal crowding for 0.5-letter-spacing, in contrast to the foveal crowding observed with shorter presentations16,40.

Stimuli and task- unlimited viewing duration experiment

This experiment was almost identical to the limited viewing experiment. It too was divided into an uncrowded and crowded condition and differed only by not having a limited viewing element. The target and flankers remained displayed constantly until a participant responded.

The crowding effect was calculated individually for each participant by subtracting their average crowded threshold (which was found in the first block of the second part of the experiment for a 1-letter spacing, and in the second block of the second part of the experiment for a 0.5 letter spacing) by their uncrowded threshold which was found in the first part of the experiment. The order of conditions was fixed such that unlimited viewing experiment always preceded the limited viewing experiment, and in both experiments isolated targets preceded flanked targets. This sequence was chosen to facilitate task comprehension and minimize confusion, particularly for older participants. To mitigate potential practice or fatigue effects, the unlimited viewing and unlimited viewing experiments were conducted in separate sessions rather than consecutively. In the early phase of the study, we also verified that the fixed-order results were consistent with findings from our previous lab studies.

Data and statistical analysis

For statistical analysis we first examined age as a continuous variable across the full cohort (20–89 years) to assess whether results varied systematically with age across experiments and conditions. These analyses provided an overview of age-related effects across the adult lifespan. To complement this approach, we also conducted a dichotomous analysis by dividing participants into two groups. An older group included participants in their 50th year and above (unlimited experiment: N = 19, 72.52 ± 12 years, mean ± STD; limited experiment: N = 21, 74.04 ± 12.3 years mean ± STD), and a younger group included participants in their 49th years and below (N = 12, 29.58 ± 7.59 years, mean ± STD). The reason 50 was chosen as the cut off age between the two groups was presbyopia, which is the physiological aging of the crystalline lens which affects the eye’s ability to focus at near and far smoothly. By age 50 this ageing process has been shown to affect 100% of the population47.

Group sizes were determined with reference to Siman-Tov et al. (2025)40, who reported a large effect size between crowded and uncrowded conditions at a 120 ms presentation time (Cohen’s d = − 2.14) with N = 10 participants. Given such a large effect size, smaller group numbers were sufficient to detect robust differences, particularly in the younger cohort where effects are known to be minimal16. We therefore prioritized recruiting a larger sample of older participants, where greater variability and stronger age-related effects were expected, resulting in uneven but appropriate group sizes (19 vs. 12).

Independent samples t-tests and paired t-tests were conducted to assess whether there was a significant difference in visual acuity and crowding between the two study groups and Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to evaluate the relationship between age and the crowding effect, as well as between reaction time and age. Additionally, to examine the relationship between age and reaction time across conditions, a linear regression analysis was conducted to determine whether there was a significant difference in the slopes between the limitedl and unlimited conditions. All p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) procedure. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to research the way ageing may affect functional vision, focusing specifically on foveal crowding and including a wide range of ages with participants up to 89 years. By including participants up to 89 years of age, we sought to extend the relevance of our results across the full adult lifespan, demonstrating that the observed crowding effects persist across the full lifespan of adulthood. Our results showed that under unlimited viewing conditions, age does not have a significant effect on crowding regarding the letter recognition but has a remarkable effect on RT (reaction time). This finding was consistent for both one-letter and half-letter spacing conditions.

We hypothesized a correlation between short presentation times and an increased crowding effect in older adults, due to slower processing speed with advancing age. Our results indicated that a presentation time of 120ms which has previously been shown not to cause significant foveal crowding in younger participants16,40, produced a monotonic increase in the crowding effect and significantly increased foveal crowding in older participants. In fact, crowding was 3 times greater at one-letter spacing than that of the younger group. A notable finding emerged when analyzing RTs across both experiments. Regardless of letter spacing, the older participants exhibited slower RTs in the unlimited viewing condition compared to the limited condition. This suggests that when the stimulus remained available, older individuals took longer to decide, whereas under limited viewing durations they responded more quickly, though still slower than younger participants. Younger participants, by contrast, showed little difference in RT between conditions at one-letter spacing. These results indicate that the slower responses of older adults in the unlimited viewing condition should not be attributed solely to motor response, since their RTs improved under limited viewing conditions, but rather to differences in how decisions are made when visual information remains continuously available, versus when it is transient.

We performed a linear regression analysis examining the relationship between age and reaction time between limited and unlimited conditions. There was a significant difference in the slopes in both the one-letter spacing and the half-letter spacing affirming the significant difference between the relationship between age and reaction time between the limited and unlimited conditions (Mixed linear regression one-letter spacing, interaction between age and group: β = 3.6142, t(32.2969) = 5.065, p = 0.0000161, Mixed linear regression half-letter spacing, interaction between age and group: β = 2.4072, t(32.1305) = 2.821, p = 0.0082) (Fig. 9).

It is important to note that faster RTs in older individuals do not necessarily indicate better performance, as evidenced by the crowding effect observed in this study. When given unlimited time, older participants took longer to respond, with high accuracy and no substantial crowding effects. In contrast, when given limited times, older individuals responded more quickly but their accuracy significantly reduced, as reflected in the increased crowding effect. We hypothesize that this effect may be attributed to the slower processing speeds in older individuals. When ample time was available to process the stimuli in the unlimited viewing experiment, older participants were not limited by processing time, thus increasing their accuracy. However, when the stimuli were presented briefly, their relatively decreased reaction time in comparison with the younger participants did not translate to improved accuracy, likely due to insufficient time for proper target processing. This effect may help explain why older adults adopt slower, more cautious behavior in real-life visual tasks allowing for longer processing.

Comparison with other crowding studies

In the present study we employed matrices of tumbling “E”s as flankers, which are commonly used in clinical and psychophysical settings to assess foveal crowding and letter recognition. This approach has the advantage of being widely recognized, and directly comparable to clinical letter charts. At the same time, we acknowledge that the crowding effects observed in our younger control participants were minimal, even at 0.5-letter spacing, raising the possibility of a floor effect. Previous studies have demonstrated that the strength of crowding is highly sensitive to stimulus configuration and grouping principles rather than spacing alone48,49. For example, Manassi et al.50, and Livne and Sagi51 have shown that flankers can sometimes reduce crowding (“uncrowding”) when arranged in ways that promote Gestalt grouping, and that global configuration plays a critical role in determining crowding magnitude50. In this context, our choice of tumbling E stimuli may not have captured the full range of crowding effects that other paradigms can reveal. However, the tumbling-E paradigm with a 120 ms presentation time was chosen, as previous work has shown that young participants do not exhibit crowding at this duration, making it an appropriate boundary condition for assessing age-related changes16,40. By doing so we were able to reveal a clear difference between younger and older adults which showed that while crowding was minimal in the younger group, older adults showed a measurable effect under the same conditions.

A few possible interpretations of our findings should be considered. One is that aging introduces a qualitative change such that foveal crowding, absent in younger adults at 120 ms, emerges only in older observers. Another, which we find more likely, is that aging slows temporal integration processes, consistent with well-documented age-related increases in reaction time. Under this view, younger participants resolve crowding within 120 ms, whereas older adults require longer exposures to achieve comparable performance. A third possibility is that these accounts are not mutually exclusive, and aging may involve both changes in foveal sensitivity and a slowing of temporal integration, together producing the measurable crowding effect observed in our older participants.

It is also important to emphasize that the aim of the present study was not to explore the full temporal profile of foveal crowding across aging, but rather to examine how crowding effects manifest under two controlled temporal conditions: a brief presentation (120 ms) and an unlimited viewing condition. By focusing on these two conditions, we isolated the impact of age under time-limited versus unrestricted viewing, without introducing the added complexity of multiple stimulus durations. This design allowed us to demonstrate clear age-related differences while keeping the paradigm clinically grounded and experimentally tractable.

Although we did not manipulate stimulus duration systematically in this study, previous findings suggest that shorter presentation times (30–60 ms) would likely amplify age-related crowding effects, while longer durations would attenuate them16,40. Situating our design at 120 ms therefore provided a sensitive test of age-related vulnerability to brief presentations without requiring a full mapping of the temporal profile. Future work should expand on this by varying presentation times systematically to determine at what point older adults are able to overcome these temporal limitations.

Temporal processing and reaction time

We found that the RT becomes slower with increasing age, regardless of the task. Temporal processing deficits with aging are not restricted to vision alone. Older adults have been shown to exhibit reduced sensitivity in auditory temporal tasks, such as gap detection and two-tone fusion52,53, and are more susceptible to multisensory illusions like the sound-induced flash illusion54,55. Similar declines have also been observed in the tactile domain with older adults having higher thresholds for distinguishing two successive tactile stimuli56,57. These findings, together with the results of our present study, indicate that temporal processing is a limiting factor across multiple sensory systems in older adults.

However, such limitations are not confined to aging. Developmental populations also show similar vulnerabilities when processing brief stimuli. In dyslexia, reduced sensitivity to rapid sequences of visual stimuli, which is critical for fluent reading, has been found58,59. Temporal deficits have also been reported in ADHD, where children show poorer performance on temporal order and timing tasks60, including recent research on crowding tasks25. Additionally, in ASD (autism spectrum disorder), individuals have been shown to be less sensitive to audiovisual asynchrony and require larger temporal offsets to detect when signals are out of sync61. Together, these findings highlight that reduced temporal precision is a shared bottleneck across both younger and older populations, reinforcing the view that temporal sensitivity is a fundamental constraint on perception.

Neural mechanisms and theoretical considerations of crowding

Several theories attempt to explain visual crowding, ranging from low level spatial integration of targets and flankers within the same integration field, to higher-level attentional mechanisms [for review see2. Since crowding is considered a bottleneck of visual processing, many studies consider crowding as a bottom-up (feedforward) process. However, there are other models that suggest that crowding is affected by top-down processing. One prominent model focuses on the role of spatial attention in object recognition. It proposes that attention is required to integrate basic visual features into unified perceived objects62,63, arguing that crowding results from limited spatial resolution of attention7 and introduced the concept of “attentional resolution”, distinct from visual resolution. This limited attentional resolution in peripheral vision may restrict conscious access to crowded targets64,65, making the attention model important for understanding crowding effects. However, in the fovea, visual resolution is high, receptive fields are small, and attention is typically already focused on the target. Even so, our study and previous studies from our lab show that crowding can still occur under brief stimulus presentations16,18,24,66. Crowding is critically dependent on target–flanker separation and has been considered in some studies as a form of masking2,40. Both masking (suppressive effects) and crowding occur within a certain range of target–flanker separations, known as the critical distance, which increases with eccentricity. In the fovea, masking is typically observed at short target–flanker separations67–71; whereas crowding is found at very small separations of less than 5 arcmin3,6,11,72. Facilitation in collinear configurations is observed beyond the critical distance for masking67–69,71,73, but it is relatively rare in the case of crowding74. Lateral interactions involved in crowding have been found to occurr during early visual processing1,67, supporting the idea that crowding is processed in the early visual cortex and reflects feedforward processing. Our model, which is based on lateral interactions, posits that crowding is related to the size of the human PF (perceptive field)1. Results have shown large variability in PF size and crowding effects across observers. Nevertheless, under a specific range of spatiotemporal parameters, crowding was found to correlate with the estimated size of the PF in both the fovea and the periphery1,12. Within the PF, inhibition arises from the combination of flanker input and local target processing. Lateral interactions include both excitatory and inhibitory components, and the balance between them affects the PF size (for more details see1. Therefore, lateral excitation and local inhibition interact within the temporal window of target processing, leading to a complex outcome that depends on several spatiotemporal parameters, including presentation time, target–flanker separation, and contrast.

The size of the PF plays a critical role in visual processing. A smaller PF reduces noise and thereby improves sensitivity. Studies in non-human primates have shown that receptive fields in older monkeys are larger than those in younger monkeys, possibly due to decreased lateral inhibition75. This expansion of receptive fields could explain the increased noise observed with age.

Temporal aspects of crowding and visual processing speed

Lateral masking and crowding are affected by spatial and temporal parameters (for more details see1 and depend on the time constraints of inhibition and excitation1,69,76. Crowding is also affected by presentation times2,16,77–80. In the temporal domain, foveal crowding is evident at short presentation times and is substantially reduced at longer presentation times. Thus, the critical time required to overcome crowding depends on the dynamics of excitation and inhibition (E/I). Since E/I balance varies with stimulus parameters and task demands, it may be reached under different spatiotemporal parameters (see Lev, Polat1. Therefore, crowding may be affected by the dynamics of neural interactions.

The magnocellular and parvocellular systems have different spatial and temporal parameters that define their processing of visual inputs. The magnocellular system processes information rapidly (transient), is sensitive to low contrast, low spatial frequency, and high temporal frequency, and is insensitive to color. In contrast, the parvocellular system processes information slowly (sustained), with higher sensitivity to contrast, high spatial frequency, low temporal frequencies and color81,82 .In our study, the only manipulated feature was presentation time. Longer presentation times likely allowed sufficient time for the parvocellular system to process the target’s information. However, brief presentation times (transient) are suboptimal for the parvocellular system, meaning that processing may shift toward the magnocellular system. However, this explanation is insufficient to account for our findings, since both the target and flankers were presented at high contrast, and the task involved target identification, neither of which favors magnocellular processing.

While most crowding studies have focused mainly on spatial aspects, our results demonstrate consistent differences in reaction times which became more pronounced with increasing age, highlighting the importance of considering temporal processing in crowding and aging. Notably, individuals with increasing age exhibited significantly slower reaction times in both the unlimited and limited tasks, despite comparable accuracy in the unlimited condition. This dissociation suggests that temporal processing inefficiencies contribute to performance differences beyond perceptual limitations. Previous research has indicated that aging is associated with deficits in temporal processing, including delays in sensory evidence accumulation34, and increased variability in response times. These delays may be further compounded by elevated internal or external noise, which has been proposed as a mechanism underlying slower reaction times in general and in aging83,84. At present, however, it remains unclear which mechanism predominates.

To summarize, our findings indicated that age did not significantly influence crowding under unlimited viewing conditions. However, it did have a pronounced effect under limited viewing conditions, with older adults exhibiting significantly greater crowding effects under time-limited conditions. Aging was also found to significantly affect reaction times, with older adults being significantly slower than younger adults in all conditions. In the unlimited viewing condition reaction times were slower but high accuracy was maintained across all ages. In contrast, in the limited viewing condition, reaction times were faster, but accuracy dropped in older adults and a crowding effect was present, suggesting that aging impairs the ability to process visual information correctly and efficiently under brief presentation times. These findings highlight the critical role of processing speed in visual function and imply that age-related changes in visual perception are not solely due to optical factors but may also stem from neural processing limitations.

The implications of these findings extend beyond the laboratory setting, particularly in tasks requiring rapid visual processing, such as driving and daily navigation. Understanding the relationship between aging, visual crowding, and processing speed could help inform strategies for improving visual accessibility and developing interventions to mitigate the functional limitations associated with aging.
