Obesity-derived metabolites modulate anti-tumor immunity in the tumor microenvironment: from mechanisms to clinical applications
Authors: Yihang Cai, Junyi Jin, Yihui Wang, Dongchen Han, Xinmiao Yu, Feng Jin, Mu Li, Yang Li, Xiaoying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor microenvironment (TME) is increasingly understood as a metabolically dynamic ecosystem in which local metabolite availability, composition, and trafficking shape immune cell fitness and therapeutic responsiveness. Against the backdrop of the global obesity epidemic, obesity-associated systemic metabolic dysregulation has been implicated in tumor initiation and progression and may also influence the immune contexture and treatment responsiveness of tumors. This Review examines how obesity-derived systemic metabolites may modulate anti-tumor immunity and influence cancer immunotherapy, with particular emphasis on pathways involved in metabolic reprogramming and TME remodeling. We further discuss intervention strategies spanning (i) upstream metabolite generation, (ii) systemic-to-local trafficking into the TME, and (iii) direct functional antagonism within the TME. However, the available evidence remains heterogeneous across metabolite classes, tumor types, and experimental contexts, and this heterogeneity is likely to contribute to divergent immunotherapeutic phenotypes, including obesity-associated heterogeneity in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) responsiveness. By integrating these layers into a source-to-sink framework, we highlight translationally relevant directions for understanding how systemic metabolites may reshape the metabolic niche of the TME and for informing more precise, context-aware, and safety-conscious metabolite-directed strategies in cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: obesity, metabolites, tumor microenvironment, metabolic remodeling, immunotherapy

Background

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is increasingly recognized as a specialized metabolic niche in which nutrient availability, metabolite composition, and metabolic competition can critically influence immune cell fitness and treatment response. Within this niche, metabolites are increasingly understood not only as components of intermediary metabolism but also as context-dependent regulators of tissue states, tumor-immune crosstalk, and anti-tumor immunity [1–3]. Although cancer immunotherapy has transformed the therapeutic landscape across multiple malignancies, its clinical efficacy remains frequently constrained by an immunosuppressive TME [4]. Conceptually, this niche is shaped by an integrated network that includes tumor-intrinsic metabolism, reciprocal metabolite exchange between malignant and non-malignant cells, intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and whole-body metabolic homeostasis [5–7]. Compared with tumor-derived metabolites, however, considerably less attention has been devoted to how systemically derived metabolites modulate local metabolic conditions within the TME and thereby influence anti-tumor immunity. A deeper understanding of how whole-body metabolic state, together with specific circulating metabolites, modulates metabolite availability and signaling within the TME may help inform the development of more precise and context-aware cancer immunotherapy.

Obesity, a chronic and complex endocrine-metabolic disorder, has been widely implicated in tumor initiation and progression through its capacity to establish a pro-inflammatory and pro-metastatic tissue milieu [8–10]. A representative hallmark of obesity-associated adipose inflammation is the formation of crown-like structures (CLS), in which hypertrophic or dying adipocytes are encircled by infiltrating macrophages [11, 12]. Through sustained paracrine signaling, these inflammatory niches reinforce immunosuppressive, pro-inflammatory and pro-metastatic programs that favor tumor progression. Consistent with this, CLS are enriched in obesity-associated malignancies, including breast and ovarian cancers, and correlate with adverse clinical outcomes [9, 13, 14]. Previous work on obesity-cancer interactions has largely emphasized chronic adipose inflammation, adipokine/cytokine signaling, and endocrine dysfunction, whereas the contribution of systemically altered metabolites to local TME remodeling and anti-tumor immune regulation has been comparatively less well delineated [15, 16]. At the same time, with growing interest in combining metabolic intervention with immunotherapy, obesity-associated circulating metabolites are being investigated as candidate mediators linking whole-body metabolic dysregulation to local tumor immunity [17]. Recent evidence further suggests that diet-induced obesity, as an important contributor to systemic metabolic imbalance, may reshape the TME through metabolite-mediated mechanisms and thereby modulate responsiveness to immunotherapy [18].

Accordingly, elucidating how obesity-derived systemic metabolites are generated at the source, trafficked into the TME, and functionally engaged within local metabolic niches is important for understanding how whole-body metabolic dysregulation remodels anti-tumor immunity and therapeutic response. In this Review, we summarize the molecular mechanisms through which obesity-derived systemic metabolites contribute to TME metabolic remodeling and anti-tumor immune reprogramming. We further discuss targeted intervention strategies that may help inform efforts to improve immunotherapeutic responsiveness, spanning source-level metabolic modulation, interception of systemic-to-local metabolite trafficking, and local antagonism of metabolite-driven immunosuppressive programs. By integrating these layers into a source-to-sink conceptual framework, we seek to refine the translational understanding of how systemic obesity-associated metabolites shape local tumor immunity and to highlight potential translational opportunities for more precise metabolic-immunotherapeutic strategies.

Mechanisms linking obesity-derived metabolites to TME remodeling and modulation of anti-tumor immunity

In the context of this Review, obesity-derived metabolites are considered systemically altered, bioactive metabolic intermediates or end products that can traffic to the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulate local metabolite availability and signaling, and thereby influence the phenotype and function of tumor, stromal, and immune cells [19]. Obesity constitutes a global public health crisis and is frequently accompanied by metabolic dysfunction, which can be associated with elevated circulating cholesterol, fatty acids, lactate, and branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) [20]. These obesity-derived metabolites may enter the TME through the circulation or transporter-mediated processes. Once present within the TME, they can contribute to local metabolic disequilibrium and metabolic reprogramming, while also shaping the abundance, spatial organization, and functional states of resident cell populations, with downstream implications for anti-tumor immunity [21–23]. Accordingly, this Review conceptualizes these metabolites as bioactive mediators through which systemic metabolic dysregulation is translated into local metabolic remodeling, immune reprogramming, and ultimately variable therapeutic responsiveness within the TME (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Obesity-derived metabolites within the TME

Cholesterol

Obesity, especially abdominal obesity, commonly co-occurs with dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triglyceride (TG) levels, together with reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels [24, 25]. This lipid disturbance may arise, at least in part, from increased free fatty acid (FFA) flux released by hypertrophic and hyperplastic visceral adipocytes, which is delivered to the liver and can contribute to hepatic TG deposition and increased synthesis and secretion of very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C) [24, 26]. In parallel, hypertrophic adipocytes can produce pro-inflammatory adipokines and cytokines, including leptin, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), thereby sustaining chronic adipose tissue inflammation, which may further impair hepatic cholesterol handling [27, 28].

Recent preclinical studies suggest that tumor cells may actively exploit this excess cholesterol to establish an immunosuppressive niche. In colorectal cancer (CRC), tumor-intrinsic ATP6V0A1 has been shown to promote the uptake and intracellular trafficking of exogenous cholesterol by accelerating endosomal acidification and maturation, thereby favoring cholesterol accumulation in the endoplasmic reticulum. This, in turn, has been linked to increased generation of 24-hydroxycholesterol (24-OHC), with subsequent activation of LXR signaling, induction of TGF-β1 release, and SMAD-dependent suppression of memory CD8+ T cell function, changes consistent with a more persistent metabolically encoded immunosuppressive state [29, 30].

More broadly, cholesterol homeostasis within the TME appears to be important for effective CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [31]. Under physiologic conditions, cholesterol has been reported to interact with the transmembrane domain of the TCR-β chain, facilitating TCR clustering at the T cell surface and supporting antigen-triggered TCR signaling and T cell activation [32]. However, both cholesterol deficiency and cholesterol excess within the TME may impair T cell function through distinct metabolic mechanisms. On the one hand, insufficient cholesterol availability may limit T cell proliferation, at least in part by reducing lysosomal cholesterol content and thereby impairing activation of the cholesterol-sensing SLC38A9-NPC1-complex-dependent mTORC1 pathway. Reduced mTORC1 activity has been associated with suppressed ribosome biogenesis and Cyclin D1 translation, G1-S cell-cycle arrest, and increased autophagic and apoptotic signaling, collectively contributing to T cell loss and diminished anti-tumor immunity [33]. On the other hand, excessive cholesterol within the TME may promote lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic vulnerability in CD8+ T cells. Under hypercholesterolaemic conditions, tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells can upregulate CD36, a transporter of FFAs and oxidized lipids, as an adaptive response to lipid excess. Increased CD36 expression in turn may facilitate the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (Ox-LDL), resulting in intracellular accumulation of oxidized lipids, enhanced lipid peroxidation, and ferroptotic cell death, thereby compromising T cell cytotoxicity [34].

The immunoregulatory effects of cholesterol also extend to myeloid compartments. Cholesterol homeostasis appears to contribute to dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation [35, 36], whereas cholesterol-driven metabolic programs in melanoma and CRC have been associated with increased exosomal cholesterol secretion and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), with corresponding enhancement of their T cell-suppressive function [37–39]. Notably, in high-cholesterol-diet CRC models, systemic cholesterol elevation has been reported to attenuate the immune activation induced by XBP1 knockdown, a finding consistent with the possibility that obesity-associated systemic cholesterol may enter the TME and support myeloid-mediated immune suppression [39]. Collectively, these findings suggest that obesity-driven cholesterol dysregulation may influence both lymphoid and myeloid compartments within the TME, favoring an immunosuppressive metabolic ecosystem (Fig. 2).

Fatty Acids

In patients with obesity and cancer, the TME frequently exhibits heightened fatty-acid metabolism, representing an important axis of metabolic reprogramming in both tumor and non-tumor compartments [40, 41]. Obesity-derived fatty acids function as energetic substrates and may also act as immunoregulatory signals that modulate metabolic conditions within the TME, with potential consequences for anti-tumor immunity. Importantly, saturated fatty acids (SFAs), exemplified by palmitic acid (PA) and stearic acid (SA), and unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), exemplified by oleic acid (OA) and linoleic acid (LA), can exert divergent biological effects, underscoring the need to distinguish their context-dependent roles in obesity-associated immune-metabolic regulation [42].

The TME is widely recognized as a lipid-enriched metabolic niche. In obese melanoma-bearing mice, PA is markedly elevated in plasma and becomes the most abundant free fatty acid within the TME, whereas OA, a monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA), shows only a modest increase; notably, these two fatty acids have been reported to exert divergent effects on T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Preclinical evidence indicates that PA may impair T cell mitochondrial function and is associated with reduced anti-tumor immunity, whereas OA may counterbalance these effects. Mechanistically, excess PA has been reported to enter T cells via CD36-dependent transport, with subsequent suppression of Akt/mTOR phosphorylation, disruption of Ca²⁺ signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and features of T cell exhaustion, collectively limiting anti-tumor immune responses. By contrast, OA has been associated with attenuation of PA-induced abnormalities along the CD36-Akt/mTOR-Ca²⁺ axis, with corresponding improvement in mitochondrial metabolic function and reduction of T cell exhaustion, potentially supporting anti-tumor immunity [43].

Preclinical evidence suggests that linoleic acid (LA), the most abundant dietary ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), may enhance CD8+ T cell anti-tumor function in selected settings through coordinated metabolic reprogramming, subcellular structural remodeling, and signaling rewiring [44]. Following uptake via the membrane transporter SLC27A1 (FATP1), LA has been reported to be incorporated into phospholipid bilayers of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), mitochondria, and mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), where it may reduce membrane saturation and increase membrane fluidity, changes that have been associated with a lower threshold for TCR activation and enhanced antigen sensitivity. Concomitantly, LA has been linked to metabolic reprogramming away from glycolysis and toward the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), thereby supporting NADPH generation, lipid biosynthesis, and antioxidant defense, while also upregulating enzymes involved in fatty acid and triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis. These adaptations may promote lipid droplet formation, which could support fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in memory T cells and limit lipotoxicity associated with free fatty acid accumulation. LA has also been associated with increased ER-mitochondria contact sites (MERCS/MAMs) [45], potentially facilitating Ca²⁺ transfer from the ER to mitochondria and supporting mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Collectively, these metabolic and structural adaptations have been associated with increased expression of memory-related transcription factors, including TCF1, Eomes, and CD127, together with reduced expression of exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and TOX, features consistent with a shift from an exhausted toward a more memory-like CD8+ T cell state within the TME [44].

However, the immunological effects of LA are not uniformly beneficial and appear to be strongly context dependent. In a murine mammary tumor model, dietary exposure to oils enriched in LA has been reported to impair anti-tumor T cell responses, with proposed mechanisms involving epidermal fatty acid binding protein (E-FABP)-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, increased reactive oxygen species generation, lipid peroxidation, naïve T cell apoptosis, and reduced TNFα production, findings collectively consistent with compromised anti-tumor immunity [46]. In a distinct metabolic and tissue context, LA has also been shown to induce CD4+ T cell apoptosis, at least in part through upregulation of carnitine palmitoyltransferase-dependent fatty acid oxidation and oxidative stress, thereby favoring hepatocellular carcinoma development in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; previously termed NAFLD)-associated settings [47]. These observations suggest that LA may support anti-tumor immunity in some settings while impairing T cell fitness in others (Fig. 3).

Taken together, current evidence supports a context-dependent and potentially bidirectional role of LA in anti-tumor immunity rather than a uniformly immunostimulatory effect. The apparently divergent findings may reflect differences in immune cell subset, tumor type, tissue milieu, metabolic background, and exposure paradigm including the mode and duration of LA exposure. More specifically, the consequences of LA likely differ between activated intra-tumoral CD8+ T cells and other T cell states such as naïve or CD4+ T cells, and may also vary between controlled cellular exposure and chronic LA-rich dietary overload. This difference may partly reflect state-dependent metabolic programming, because resting/naïve and memory-like T cells are relatively more reliant on oxidative metabolism and FAO-related programs, whereas activated effector T cells preferentially increase aerobic glycolysis to sustain rapid biosynthetic and functional demands [48–50]. Accordingly, current data do not support interpreting LA as a uniformly immunostimulatory metabolite across cancer types or therapeutic settings, underscoring the need for caution when extrapolating its immune effects beyond specific biological contexts.

Lactate

Lactate is one of the most abundant metabolites in systemic circulation and is primarily generated from pyruvate via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as the end product of glycolysis [51]. Under physiological conditions, circulating lactate levels are generally maintained at approximately 1–2 mM. Within this range, lactate has been reported to support CD8+ T cell stem-like properties and anti-tumor function in specific contexts [52–54]. However, lactate levels beyond the physiological range may adversely affect cellular function, and obesity may contribute to systemic lactate elevation [55, 56].

Obesity frequently establishes a high-lactate metabolic niche within the TME. This is partly attributable to hypertrophic adipose tissue, a hallmark of diet-induced obesity, which is intrinsically hypoxic [57]. Under hypoxic conditions, increased glycolytic flux promotes lactate production, which may subsequently diffuse or be transported into the TME and contribute to local acidification [58–60]. By exacerbating lactate accumulation and TME acidification, obesity may further restrict immune-cell infiltration and migration, thereby favoring the development of an immunologically “cold” tumor state.

Lactate accumulation within the TME reshapes the immune microenvironment by altering the abundance, distribution and functional state of immune and stromal cells, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive niche [61, 62]. In mouse models of colorectal cancer and melanoma, lactate has been shown to serve as an alternative carbon source and to be imported into Tregs through monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), a key transmembrane transporter involved in lactate, pyruvate, and ketone-body trafficking. In this context, lactate uptake has been reported to promote the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFAT1), with subsequent PD-1 upregulation and enhanced Treg-associated immunosuppressive activity [63]. In melanoma and colorectal cancer mouse models, lactate accumulation has also been associated with immunosuppressive remodeling through metabolic rewiring in CD8+ T cells [64]. Under physiological metabolic conditions, cytotoxic CD8+ T cells preferentially convert pyruvate into oxaloacetate through pyruvate carboxylase (PC), thereby supporting tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity and succinate generation. Succinate may then engage succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1) on the T cell surface and support IFNγ production, thereby reinforcing cytotoxic function. However, elevated lactate has been reported to suppress PC activity in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, redirect pyruvate utilization toward pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH)-dependent entry into the TCA cycle, and reduce TCA intermediates and succinate secretion, collectively attenuating SUCNR1 signaling and effector T cell cytotoxicity [65, 66].

Furthermore, lactate accumulation within the TME may promote macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. In breast cancer models, lactate-associated activation of Gpr132 [67, 68], HIF-1α [69, 70] and Nrf2 [71] signaling has been linked to macrophage M2 polarization, with potential consequences for anti-tumor immune responses. In parallel, lactate may suppress anti-tumor immunity not only by favoring M2 polarization but also by limiting M1-like polarization [72]. As an oxidative substrate that can contribute to tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolism, lactate-derived acetyl-CoA has been reported to facilitate H3K27 acetylation, a process associated with reduced expression of pro-inflammatory genes in macrophages, impaired M1-like polarization, and diminished anti-tumor immune activity [73, 74] (Fig. 4).

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)

BCAAs, comprising valine, leucine and isoleucine, are three essential amino acids that play central roles in human metabolism. As key nitrogen donors for macromolecular biosynthesis, BCAAs can support tumor-cell survival and proliferation [75]. Branched-chain α-ketoacids (BCKAs) are obligate intermediates of BCAA catabolism, and both BCAAs and their downstream BCKAs are commonly elevated in obesity-associated metabolic states [76].

Owing to their high proliferative demands, tumor cells often exhibit increased dependence on exogenous BCAA acquisition and utilization, and can transaminate intracellular BCAAs into BCKAs through branched-chain aminotransferases (BCAT1/2), with subsequent release of these metabolites into the TME [77]. In breast, colorectal, and lung cancer cell models, BCAT1/2-mediated transamination has been shown to generate three major BCKA species, including α-ketoisocaproate (KIC), α-keto-β-methylvalerate (KMV), and α-ketoisovalerate (KIV). Their extracellular accumulation has been linked to monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1)-mediated trafficking, which may contribute to BCKA enrichment within the TME, whereas macrophages have also been reported to internalize these BCKAs through MCT1. Notably, these BCKA subtypes may exert distinct and context-dependent effects on macrophage polarization. KIC and KMV have been reported to favor an M2-like, pro-tumoral program characterized by increased expression of canonical M2 markers and pro-tumoral mediators, together with suppression of inflammatory cytokine output. By contrast, KIV has been associated with a more pro-inflammatory, M1-like state, including induction of inflammatory markers and cytokine release, which may be consistent with enhanced anti-tumor immune activation [78].

BCAA accumulation may also contribute to metabolic reprogramming in CD8+ T cells and support cytotoxic function. In Lewis lung carcinoma and melanoma mouse models, elevated BCAA levels within the TME have been reported to activate the PI3K-Akt-FoxO1-mTOR axis in CD8+ T cells, with associated upregulation of the glucose transporter Glut1, enhanced glucose uptake, and glycolytic reprogramming [79]. In this context, such changes may help sustain effector function and may improve the metabolic competitiveness of CD8+ T cells relative to tumor cells for glucose, with potential benefits for anti-tumor immunity [75]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the immunological consequences of BCAA/BCKA dysregulation are shaped not only by metabolite abundance, but also by metabolite identity, cellular context, and niche-specific metabolic wiring, underscoring the need for deeper mechanistic dissection (Fig. 5).

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)

Under physiological conditions, most dietary free choline, an essential nutrient, is absorbed in the small intestine [80]. When choline intake is excessive, however, a proportion can be metabolized by gut microbiota in the colon, including members of the Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria phyla, into trimethylamine (TMA), which is subsequently oxidized to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) by hepatic flavin-containing monooxygenases (FMO1 and FMO3) [81]. Clinical studies have reported elevated circulating TMAO levels in patients with colorectal cancer and obesity [82]. This association may partly relate to enrichment of choline utilization gene clusters (Cut) within Firmicutes [83], taxa that are frequently expanded in individuals with obesity. In parallel, high-fat dietary patterns, which are often rich in choline, may further enhance microbial choline catabolism and thereby increase systemic TMAO production [81]. Collectively, these observations are consistent with a positive, although context-dependent, association between obesity and circulating TMAO levels [84].

In the TME of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models, TMAO has been reported to activate the endoplasmic reticulum stress kinase (PERK) and to induce pyroptosis in tumor cells, together with increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, collectively suggesting a potential role in promoting a more inflamed tumor state and supporting CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [85]. In TMAO-treated mouse models of PDAC, immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs, MDSCs, and DCs have been associated with phenotypic shifts that may be more permissive for immunotherapy, accompanied by increased T cell infiltration and enhanced responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy. Current research also suggests that TMAO may enhance T cell activity through the IFN signaling pathway, although the precise mechanism remains unclear [86] (Fig. 6). Taken together, these findings suggest that TMAO may exert immunologically relevant effects in selected preclinical settings. However, its overall role in anti-tumor immunity remains context dependent and should not be interpreted as uniformly beneficial across tumor types, host metabolic states, or therapeutic contexts.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are fatty acids containing fewer than six carbon atoms and mainly include acetate (C2), propionate (C3), and butyrate (C4) [87]. In obesity, particularly under high-fat-diet conditions, SCFA-producing taxa within the phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes are reduced, and accordingly, circulating levels of propionate and butyrate have been reported to be decreased [88, 89]. As major gut microbiota-derived systemic metabolites, SCFAs may enter the tumor microenvironment (TME) and influence the phenotype and function of multiple immune-cell populations, thereby modulating local immune ecology and anti-tumor immunity. Among SCFAs, mechanistic evidence is currently most developed for butyrate, whereas the roles of acetate and propionate in obesity-associated tumor immunity remain comparatively less well defined [88].

Among SCFAs, butyrate has emerged as a particularly important immune-metabolic regulator owing to its potent histone deacetylase (HDAC)-inhibitory activity [90, 91]. On the one hand, butyrate has been reported to enhance CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity through epigenetic reprogramming. Specifically, butyrate has been shown to promote histone acetylation at the ID2 locus and thereby increase ID2 transcription. ID2, a helix-loop-helix transcriptional regulator lacking direct DNA-binding activity, antagonizes E proteins such as E2A, which otherwise repress Il12rb2 transcription through direct promoter binding [92]. By relieving this repression, butyrate may facilitate IL-12 signaling and support the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells. On the other hand, butyrate may also promote macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype through HDAC inhibition and increased H3K9 acetylation, thereby supporting STAT6-dependent transcription of immunoregulatory genes such as IL-10 [93–95]. These apparently divergent effects suggest that the immunological consequences of butyrate within the TME are context dependent and likely shaped by cell type, tissue milieu, and disease state.

Beyond transcriptional regulation, SCFAs can also reprogram immune-cell metabolism to alter their functional state. In particular, butyrate has been associated with enhanced differentiation and persistence of memory-like CD8+ T cells through G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), also known as free fatty acid receptors (FFARs), which are broadly expressed across immune-cell populations [96]. Acetate, propionate, and butyrate can activate GPR41 (FFAR3) and GPR43 (FFAR2), whereas GPR109A preferentially responds to butyrate and niacin [97]. In CD8+ T cells, butyrate has been reported to promote memory potential through GPR41/43-dependent signaling by uncoupling glycolysis from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and redirecting metabolism toward glutaminolysis and fatty acid oxidation to sustain oxidative phosphorylation [98]. This metabolic switch may support long-term T cell persistence and durable effector fitness, thereby favoring sustained anti-tumor immunity.

Macrophages also represent a major target of SCFA-mediated immunoregulation. In tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), butyrate has been reported to suppress the expression of PD-L1 and IL-10 through inhibition of NF-κB and STAT3 signaling, thereby potentially restoring aspects of anti-tumor immune responses. Taken together, these findings suggest that obesity-associated SCFA deficiency may remodel the TME by weakening metabolite-dependent support of CD8+ T cell effector and memory programs, while altering macrophage polarization and immunoregulatory signaling [88] (Fig. 7). More broadly, the pleiotropic and sometimes bidirectional effects of SCFAs suggest that their role in anti-tumor immunity cannot be inferred solely from metabolite abundance, but instead depends on a context-specific interplay among metabolite species, receptor usage, target-cell identity, and local tissue state.

The obesity paradox in immune checkpoint blockade

Although obesity is generally associated with inferior outcomes in many settings of cancer prevention and treatment, several clinical studies have paradoxically reported improved survival among some patients with obesity receiving immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), a phenomenon often termed the “obesity paradox” [99, 100]. In melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma, for example, higher body mass index (BMI) has been associated with improved responses to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [101, 102]. However, this apparent survival advantage should be interpreted cautiously, because it likely reflects the combined influence of context-dependent biology together with substantial epidemiological and methodological limitations, rather than a uniform protective effect of obesity across cancers and treatment settings.

From a mechanistic perspective, several obesity-associated pathways have been proposed that could, under specific conditions, contribute to enhanced responsiveness to ICB. Because the TME is the principal site of anti-tumor immunity and T cell functional state is a major determinant of ICB response [103], obesity-derived metabolites may in some contexts influence immunotherapeutic efficacy by modulating T cell fitness, tumor infiltration, and immune-checkpoint expression. One proposed mechanism involves ferroptosis. Obesity has been linked to increased lipid peroxidation and iron-dependent cell death, whereas ICB-activated T cells may further amplify ferroptosis-mediated tumor-cell clearance [104]. A second explanation centers on leptin. In obesity, chronic leptin overproduction induces leptin resistance despite persistent hyperleptinemia [105–107]. In T cells, leptin-STAT3 signaling has been reported to upregulate PD-1 expression through enhancer elements within the PD-1 locus, potentially rendering T cells more susceptible to reinvigoration by PD-1 blockade. In parallel, leptin-resistant T cells may retain effector function more effectively under chronic stimulation, whereas leptin-sensitive T cells may be more prone to exhaustion [108, 109]. In addition, obesity-associated elevation of circulating branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which have been reported to support CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, has also been proposed as another candidate mechanism that may contribute to paradox-like benefit under ICB [110].

Obesity-derived metabolites may also, in selected settings, enhance anti-tumor immunity under ICB by supporting antigen presentation. Preclinical studies in murine melanoma models indicate that obesity-associated cholesterol can be metabolically converted into pregnenolone, subsequently processed into testosterone, and ultimately aromatized to estrogen by CYP19A1 in adipose tissue [111]. Adipocyte-derived estrogen then activates estrogen receptor alpha (ER alpha) signaling, promotes dendritic-cell differentiation and activation, enhances antigen-presenting capacity, and thereby potentiates the cytotoxic activity of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, ultimately reinforcing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [112]. Notably, this effect appears to be sex specific, as the estrogen-mediated enhancement of ICB efficacy was observed in male melanoma mice with obesity but not in females. Collectively, these findings suggest that obesity may, in selected biological contexts, generate metabolite-dependent conditions that are more permissive for ICB responsiveness. However, such effects are unlikely to be universal and should not be generalized across tumor types, sexes, or metabolic states (Fig. 8).

However, a substantial component of the obesity paradox may also arise from how obesity is defined and interpreted. Conventional classification relies primarily on BMI, with values between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2 classified as overweight and those exceeding 30 kg/m2 as obesity [113]. Most clinical studies rely heavily on BMI, which does not distinguish fat mass from lean mass and fails to capture metabolic health, fat distribution, age, or genetic background. As a result, BMI-based analyses may aggregate biologically distinct populations under a single label, thereby generating apparently protective associations that do not necessarily reflect a true causal survival advantage. In addition, paradox-like observations may be inflated by reverse causality and clinical confounding, including cachexia, pre-diagnostic weight loss, and frailty, which can shift patients with more aggressive disease into lower-BMI reference groups and artifactually favor patients with higher BMI in survival analyses.

This limitation is further compounded by marked heterogeneity within obesity itself. Metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO) differ substantially in insulin sensitivity, inflammatory burden, and adipose-tissue function, and may therefore confer distinct cancer risks and treatment outcomes [114–117]. Likewise, visceral adipose tissue (VAT), rather than subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), is more closely linked to insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and tumor-promoting endocrine signaling, yet surrogate anthropometric measures such as waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio still incompletely resolve depot-specific adiposity [118–120]. Age and genetic background introduce additional complexity. Large-scale analyses indicate that the association between BMI and mortality appears to be age dependent [121, 122], whereas obesity-susceptibility variants such as those in FTO and MC4R tend to track with metabolically unfavorable adiposity, in contrast to adiposity-favorable variants such as IRS1, which are associated with more subcutaneous fat deposition and a metabolically healthier phenotype [120, 123–127]. These factors, together with tumor-type variability, further argue against interpreting the obesity paradox as a universal BMI-defined phenomenon.

Taken together, the obesity paradox in cancer is best understood through two complementary lenses: context-dependent biology and methodological limitation. On the one hand, obesity may, in selected settings, be associated with enhanced ICB responsiveness through metabolite-linked mechanisms involving leptin, ferroptosis, BCAAs, or steroid-dependent antigen-presentation programs. On the other hand, reverse causality, cachexia-related confounding, BMI-based misclassification, metabolic heterogeneity, fat-distribution differences, age effects, and tumor-type variability may all distort epidemiological inference and exaggerate paradox-like survival associations. A more balanced interpretation therefore requires moving beyond BMI-only definitions toward multidimensional stratification frameworks that integrate metabolic health, adipose distribution, age, sex, tumor type, and treatment context. Such an approach may be important for clarifying conflicting evidence surrounding the obesity paradox and for identifying the patient subsets in whom obesity-associated metabolic features are most likely to influence immunotherapy response.

Targeting obesity-derived metabolites within the TME for cancer immunotherapy

Metabolic homeostasis within the TME is an important determinant of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) outcome across multiple solid malignancies [128]. Obesity-derived metabolite-rich niches, including cholesterol-, lactate-, and fatty acid-enriched TMEs, may impose metabolic stress on T cells through processes such as ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation, and enhanced fatty-acid oxidation, thereby contributing to exhaustion and reduced infiltration of functional CD8+ T cells, with potential implications for resistance to ICB therapy [129–131].

Current therapeutic interventions targeting obesity-derived metabolite availability within the TME can be conceptually organized into three complementary approaches: source-level control of metabolite production, interception of systemic trafficking into the TME, and local functional antagonism within the TME. Together, these strategies are being explored as potential means of countering metabolite-driven immunosuppression and, in selected contexts, may help support conditions that are more favorable for response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) (Table 2; Fig. 9).

Lifestyle interventions to regulate obesity for cancer immunotherapy

Systemic metabolites, many of which are influenced by dietary obesity, contribute to the metabolic landscape of the TME, which may in turn affect immunotherapeutic sensitivity. Although metabolism-targeted therapies are under active development in cancer immunotherapy, upstream lifestyle interventions, particularly dietary modification, may provide a safer and more accessible means of supporting anti-tumor immune responses in clinical practice.

Dietary intervention

Dietary intervention represents a direct and effective way to control whole-body weight and mitigate obesity [132]. By shifting toward diets lower in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats and higher in dietary fiber and unsaturated lipids, adiposity may be reduced while beneficial gut commensals are expanded [133–135]. This coordinated effect may lower the systemic burden of immunosuppressive metabolites such as free fatty acids, secondary bile acids, and lipopolysaccharide, thereby helping recalibrate host metabolism and potentially modulating the tumor microenvironment in ways that may favor anti-tumor immunity [1, 136]. In the clinical context, personalized dietary interventions tailored to a patient’s metabolic profile may complement immunotherapies, while population-level adoption of healthful dietary patterns may also contribute to cancer prevention [137].

Low-carbohydrate, low-fat, calorie-restricted diets

Reducing the intake of saturated fatty acids, trans-fatty acids, and cholesterol may help lower LDL-C levels and reduce fatty-acid oversupply, thereby potentially limiting conditions that favor a fatty acid-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment [138, 139]. Conversely, increasing dietary intake of unsaturated fatty acids such as olive oil and fish oil may support lipid regulation and metabolic homeostasis, with possible benefits for the immune composition of the TME [140]. Maintaining a body mass index (BMI) within the normal range (18.5–24.9 kg/m²) is generally associated with more favorable lipid profiles, although BMI alone does not fully capture metabolic health or adipose distribution [130, 141]. Collectively, low-glycemic, low-fat, calorie-restricted diets may represent potentially useful upstream approaches for modulating systemic obesity-associated metabolites, although their immunotherapeutic relevance will require context-specific clinical validation.

Ketogenic diet (KD)

The ketogenic diet shifts systemic metabolism from glycolysis toward mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation and may reduce lactate accumulation, thereby partially alleviating the acidic, immunosuppressive niche of the TME [142]. Clinical studies indicate that KD can reduce visceral adiposity and elicit 5–10% body-weight loss within six months, with particularly pronounced decreases in abdominal fat [143, 144]. Preclinical models further suggest that KD may restrain tumor growth, and emerging evidence indicates that it may enhance responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade in selected cancer settings [145, 146]. Proposed mechanisms include metabolic rewiring of tumor cells, modulation of gene expression, and remodeling of the TME in ways that may favor anti-tumor immunity [147, 148]. Moreover, KD may offer practical advantages, including relatively low cost, feasibility of implementation, and acceptable tolerability in selected settings [143].

Fasting-mimicking diet (FMD)

Fasting-mimicking diet (FMD) is a cyclical low-calorie, low-protein, low-carbohydrate regimen that transiently recapitulates the metabolic milieu of fasting while permitting limited intake [149]. By exploiting differential metabolic vulnerabilities of normal versus malignant cells, FMD imposes selective metabolic stress on tumors, a strategy often described as “tumor-starvation” [150–153]. Mechanistically, FMD may lower the levels of glucose, insulin and IGF-1, thereby imposing metabolic pressure on tumors that rely on exogenous substrates and suppressing PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling to hinder proliferation [151, 154, 155]. In parallel, these systemic alterations may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by increasing CD8+ T cell infiltration and effector function [156]. Concurrently, FMD also may mobilize fatty-acid oxidation and intermittent hepatic ketogenesis, elevating ketone bodies such as β-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB) during the restriction window, which has been reported to limit IFN-γ-driven PD-L1 upregulation and, in selected models, to potentiate PD-1 blockade [157, 158]. Moreover, FMD-induced ketosis is transient and reversible on refeeding, distinguishing it from the chronic ketosis of sustained KD, which may engender persistent metabolic adaptations that, in some contexts, support tumor growth [159–161]. In metabolically dependent tumors, such as triple-negative breast cancer, periodic FMD combined with chemotherapy or immune checkpoint blockade has been associated with improved pathologic complete response rates in preliminary translational studies (NCT05763992 [162], NCT03709147 [163]). Thus, periodic FMD may represent a potential adjunct to chemo-immunotherapy, but its efficacy, safety, and optimal implementation still require more rigorous clinical validation.

Physical activity and regular routine

Lifestyle modification through regular exercise and structured sleep-wake cycles may offer a cost-effective and potentially sustainable approach to reducing systemic metabolite burdens that may infiltrate the TME. Sedentary behavior is a key driver of obesity [164]. Conversely, consistent aerobic exercise in individuals with visceral adiposity may lower serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein levels, thereby supporting more favorable lipid homeostasis and reducing the reservoir of obesity-derived metabolites available to the TME [165, 166].

Disruption of circadian homeostasis, manifested by chronic sleep deprivation, irregular sleep patterns, and shift work, perturbs endocrine signaling and lipid metabolism and has been associated with fatigue-associated weight gain [167]. Core clock genes such as BMAL1 directly regulate hepatic and adipose metabolic gene expression. BMAL1 deficiency may suppress PPARα activity, impair fatty-acid oxidation, and promote lipid accumulation, potentially leading to elevated levels of unsaturated fatty acids and cholesterol within the TME [168]. Normally, orexigenic ghrelin peaks in the morning and wanes at night, while anorexigenic leptin follows the opposite pattern. Circadian misalignment may disrupt this balance, resulting in nocturnal ghrelin surges, reduced leptin sensitivity, and greater nighttime appetite for energy-dense foods [169, 170]. Moreover, clock disruption may alter diurnal oscillations of the gut microbiota, including an increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, enhanced microbial energy harvest from the diet, increased obesity risk, greater likelihood of intestinal microbiota-derived metabolites entering the TME, and suppression of anti-tumor immune responses [171–173]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential value of integrating regular physical activity and circadian-aligned sleep habits into broader strategies for TME metabolic reprogramming and cancer prevention.

Targeting cholesterol within the TME

Strategies targeting cholesterol within the TME span all three levels of intervention, including inhibition of cholesterol biosynthesis, interception of systemic cholesterol delivery to the TME, and enhancement of cholesterol clearance.

Cholesterol synthesis inhibitors

Clinically established lipid-lowering agents that suppress cholesterol biosynthesis mainly include statins and the squalene epoxidase (SQLE) inhibitor terbinafine. Statins such as atorvastatin and rosuvastatin are first-line agents that suppress cholesterol synthesis through selective inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in the mevalonate pathway, thereby reducing systemic cholesterol synthesis at its source [174–177]. Squalene epoxidase (SQLE) represents another rate-limiting checkpoint in de novo cholesterol biosynthesis. Pharmacological inhibition of SQLE with terbinafine, a canonical SQLE inhibitor, has shown activity in preclinical hepatocellular carcinoma models and has been associated with improved responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade in selected settings [178–180].

Interception of systemic cholesterol delivery to the TME

Preventing obesity-derived systemic cholesterol from accessing the local TME can be approached pharmacologically by targeting cholesterol absorption and trafficking, with ezetimibe representing a prototypical example. Ezetimibe, a clinically approved therapeutic for hypercholesterolemia, reduces plasma LDL-C by selectively inhibiting Niemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1), a cholesterol transporter located at the brush-border membrane of intestinal epithelial cells, thereby limiting intestinal cholesterol absorption [181–183]. In patients with obesity, particularly those intolerant to statins, ezetimibe may serve as a useful adjunct to further reduce LDL-C when used alone or in combination with statins [184–188]. Recent studies in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) further suggest that tumor cells may overexpress NPC1L1, a feature associated with cholesterol sequestration and suppression of T cell receptor (TCR) signaling in CD8+ T cells, thereby attenuating their proliferation and cytotoxic activity. In this setting, ezetimibe has been reported to support restoration of CD8+ T cell function and may synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical pancreatic cancer models [189].

Cholesterol clearance agents

One significant cholesterol clearance mechanism involves LDL receptors (LDL-R), which are chiefly responsible for the systemic removal of LDL-C. Proprotein convertase subtilisin-kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a secreted serine protease, may regulate LDL-R abundance by binding hepatic LDL-R and promoting its lysosomal degradation, consequently increasing circulating LDL-C levels [190]. Clinically approved PCSK9 inhibitors, such as Evolocumab and Alirocumab, neutralize PCSK9 and prevent its interaction with LDL-R. This may result in increased LDL-R expression on hepatocyte surfaces, enhanced LDL-C clearance from the circulation, and reduced plasma LDL-C levels. Given their established safety profile in lipid management, PCSK9 inhibitors represent candidates of interest for combination with ICB, although dedicated evaluation of efficacy and safety in oncologic settings remains necessary [191–193].

Collectively, strategies targeting cholesterol synthesis, systemic delivery, and clearance may help rebalance TME cholesterol availability and preserve effector T cell function, thereby providing a rationale for addressing cholesterol-associated metabolic barriers to ICB.

Targeting fatty acids within the TME

Therapeutic strategies targeting fatty acids within the TME primarily aim to reduce the availability and utilization of obesity-associated lipids by inhibiting fatty acid synthesis, limiting fatty acid transport and uptake, or suppressing fatty acid oxidation.

Fatty acid synthase inhibitors

Fatty acid synthase (FASN) is the key enzyme responsible for fatty acid biosynthesis and may also negatively regulate antigen presentation via MHC class I. Elevated expression of FASN in tumor cells may impair the presentation of tumor antigens on the cell surface, thereby limiting recognition by CD8+ T cells and contributing to immune evasion [194]. Moreover, elevated FASN expression has been associated with reduced cytotoxic CD8+ T cell infiltration within tumors. Thus, FASN inhibitors may not only limit fatty acid availability in the TME at its source but also locally counter immune-evasive programs, thereby potentially restoring the capacity of CD8+ T cells to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. In preclinical therapeutic studies employing orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma models, orlistat, initially approved by the FDA as a lipase inhibitor for obesity management [195], together with another FASN inhibitor, TVB-2640, were associated with enhanced anti-tumor activity of anti-PD-L1 antibodies and reduced tumor growth in vivo [194]. These findings support further evaluation of fatty acid synthesis pathways as modulators of ICB efficacy.

Fatty acid transport inhibitors

In the TME, fatty acid metabolic reprogramming not only depends on the abundant lipid supply associated with obesity but also is critically regulated by the activity of key enzymes involved in mitochondrial β-oxidation. For instance, carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1) is the rate-limiting enzyme facilitating the transport of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation. Pharmacological inhibition of CPT1 effectively prevents fatty acid transport and subsequent oxidation, reducing intracellular fatty acid metabolism [196]. Etomoxir, a potent, irreversible CPT1 inhibitor extensively employed in preclinical studies, was originally developed as an oral antidiabetic agent [197]. However, clinical application was halted following the identification of significant hepatotoxicity during phase II trials, preventing its approval for human use by the FDA [198]. Despite these setbacks, CPT1 inhibition remains an attractive therapeutic approach, warranting continued development of safer and more selective inhibitors to modulate fatty acid oxidation in the context of tumor immunotherapy.

Targeting lactate within the TME

Therapeutic strategies targeting lactate within the TME are primarily aimed at limiting lactate production, blocking lactate transport, or antagonizing lactate-driven immunosuppressive programs.

Inhibitors of lactate synthesis

Targeting lactate production may increase the pH of the TME, thereby potentially facilitating the infiltration of immune cells such as T cells and NK cells into tumor tissues [199]. When combined with ICB, this strategy may support activated immune cells in more effectively accessing the tumor site and exerting anti-tumor activity.

Metformin, a widely used antidiabetic drug that may also contribute to weight control, may modulate lactate metabolism through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), suppressing tumor glycolysis and enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [200, 201]. Innovative delivery methods, such as mannose-modified macrophage-derived microparticles (Man-MPs) loaded with metformin (Met@Man-MP), have demonstrated targeted reprogramming of M2-like TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration and reducing immunosuppressive cell populations, changes associated with enhanced anti-PD-1 activity. Additionally, Met@Man-MP possesses collagen-degrading properties, which may enhance the penetration and tumor accumulation of therapeutic antibodies [202, 203].

Inhibitors of lactate transport

Monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) facilitate lactate shuttling across cellular membranes and may play important roles in sustaining tumor cell metabolism and contributing to the immunosuppressive TME [204]. AZD3965, a selective inhibitor of MCT1, blocks lactate efflux from tumor cells and thereby disrupts metabolic symbiosis within the TME [129]. Preclinical studies have shown that AZD3965 can reduce tumor cell proliferation and exhibits anti-tumor activity in xenograft models, notably in small-cell lung carcinoma [205]. Currently, AZD3965 is under evaluation in phase I/II clinical trials for various malignancies [206]. Recent advances have led to the development of tumor-specific delivery systems, such as pH-responsive nanoparticles encapsulating AZD3965 (AZD-UPS NPs). This targeted strategy has been reported to inhibit MCT1-mediated lactate transport within tumors, with associated increases in CD8+ T cell infiltration, reductions in T cell exhaustion, and enhanced anti-tumor activity of anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical models [207].

Although encouraging preclinical results and early-phase clinical trials suggest the potential of these strategies to alleviate immunosuppression and enhance tumor responsiveness to ICB, further large-scale clinical trials are necessary to validate their efficacy and safety and to clarify their roles in routine clinical practice.

Targeting BCAAs within the TME

Therapeutic modulation of BCAAs primarily involves preserving or augmenting metabolite availability in ways that support anti-tumor immune function, although the context-dependent effects of BCAA/BCKA metabolism remain to be carefully defined.

Obesity may contribute to elevated systemically derived BCAA availability within the TME [208, 209]. BCAAs are key circulating nutrients that may shape the metabolic landscape of the TME and modulate anti-tumor immunity. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), a clinical study reported that higher serum BCAA levels were associated with treatment response and prolonged overall survival [75], providing clinical support for the possibility that BCAA-linked metabolic fitness may enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy. This possibility is further being explored in clinical studies, including NCT02613507 and NCT03195491. Preclinical evidence likewise supports a functional link between intra-tumoral BCAA accumulation and enhanced T cell activity. In mice, deficiency of the mitochondrial phosphatase PP2Cm impairs BCAA catabolism, resulting in intra-tumoral BCAA accumulation that reprograms CD8+ T cell metabolism and enhances effector function. Consistent with this, dietary BCAA supplementation has been reported to synergize with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and suppress tumor growth [75]. Small-molecule inhibitors of branched-chain aminotransferases (BCATs), such as BCAO-1, may further preserve elevated BCAA levels within the TME by blocking BCAA transamination, although such agents remain at the preclinical stage [210]. Given the limited availability of clinically mature BCAT inhibitors, precision modulation of BCAA availability, including dietary strategies, may represent a translationally accessible approach to augment ICB efficacy. However, because the immunological consequences of BCAA dysregulation are highly context dependent, and because BCAA/BCKA metabolism can exert divergent effects across tumor and immune compartments, such strategies will require careful mechanistic and clinical validation before broader therapeutic implementation.

Targeting TMAO within the TME

TMAO-directed strategies remain exploratory and are currently centered on upstream metabolic modulation, although their translational development will require careful attention to tumor specificity and systemic safety.

Notably, dietary choline supplementation, a nutritional intervention with potential translational relevance, has been reported to enhance anti-tumor immune responses by increasing systemic TMAO production, in a manner broadly analogous to direct TMAO administration [85, 86]. Compared with high-fat dietary regimens, choline supplementation may represent a more tractable strategy for increasing TMAO availability while potentially avoiding concomitant accumulation of other obesity-associated immunosuppressive metabolites. Even so, the translational relevance of this approach remains provisional, and whether such modulation can reliably convert immunologically “cold” tumors into more inflamed states or potentiate immune checkpoint blockade remains to be established.

However, in patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), elevated TMAO levels have been associated with inferior immunotherapy responses and poorer clinical outcomes, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [211]. These observations suggest that the immunological effects of TMAO are unlikely to be uniformly beneficial, but instead are likely to be shaped by tumor type, host metabolic state, and disease context. Moreover, elevated TMAO levels have also been associated with increased risk of systemic disorders, including chronic kidney disease, diabetes, and atherosclerosis [212]. Accordingly, translational efforts to modulate TMAO will require careful evaluation of both tumor-specific efficacy and systemic safety.

Rather than indiscriminate systemic elevation of TMAO, future studies should prioritize strategies capable of achieving more precise control of intra-tumoral TMAO exposure, with the goal of preserving potential immunostimulatory effects while minimizing off-tumor metabolic liabilities. In this context, TMAO modulation may represent an intriguing therapeutic avenue, but whether it can be broadly incorporated into clinical cancer immunotherapy will require substantially more rigorous mechanistic, pharmacologic, and translational validation.

Targeting SCFAs within the TME

Current SCFA-targeted strategies mainly involve direct butyrate supplementation and gut microbiota modulation to restore microbiota-derived immune-metabolic support within the TME.

Direct butyrate supplementation

Among SCFAs, mechanistic and translational evidence is currently most developed for butyrate. Butyrate may enhance mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in CD8+ T cells, support effector function, and limit exhaustion-related programs. Accordingly, exogenous administration of butyrate or its more stable derivatives may provide a direct means of increasing bioavailable SCFA levels. Clinical observations further suggest that higher circulating butyrate levels may be associated with improved immunotherapy efficacy [213, 214]. In the adjuvant treatment of colorectal cancer, butyrate enema has been reported to increase intra-tumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration and, when combined with PD-1 blockade, to be associated with improved tumor response [215]. Collectively, these findings support the view that butyrate supplementation may serve as a feasible adjunctive strategy to reinforce SCFA-dependent anti-tumor immunity.

Gut microbiota modulation

An alternative strategy is to enhance endogenous SCFA production through modulation of the gut microbiota. This approach is particularly relevant because intestinal microbiota constitute the major source of systemic SCFAs and thereby influence metabolite availability within the TME. Bifidobacterium, a core probiotic genus in the human gut ecosystem [216, 217], produces acetate and lactate, which can in turn serve as metabolic substrates for other beneficial taxa, including butyrate-producing bacteria, thereby promoting cross-feeding and microbial diversity. In this context, probiotic-based interventions may augment SCFA pools and indirectly improve anti-tumor immune responses. Consistent with this rationale, the clinical-stage Bifidobacterium animalis strain EDP1503 has shown evidence of immunomodulatory activity in early-phase trials [218]. In advanced melanoma, oral EDP1503 combined with pembrolizumab was associated with encouraging clinical activity, and in a phase I/II study involving solid tumors, including colorectal cancer and triple-negative breast cancer, the combination was accompanied by activation of CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, with complete tumor regression reported in a subset of patients [218].

Taken together, these findings suggest that SCFA-targeted interventions may enhance anti-tumor immunity either by directly replenishing bioactive butyrate or by restoring microbiota-dependent SCFA production. However, because the immunological effects of SCFAs remain highly context dependent and are influenced by metabolite species, dose, host metabolic state and microbial composition, current evidence is best viewed as establishing a strong translational rationale rather than a broadly validated therapeutic standard. Future studies will therefore need to define the optimal formulation, delivery route and patient-selection framework for integrating SCFA-targeted strategies with immune checkpoint blockade.

Overall, the effective clinical exploitation of these strategies will require a deeper mechanistic understanding of the biosynthetic origins, systemic trafficking routes, spatial and temporal distribution, and context-dependent immunological functions of obesity-derived metabolites within the TME. Beyond mechanistic resolution, successful translation will also depend on more precise patient stratification, improved control of off-tumor metabolic liabilities, and rational integration of lifestyle interventions with therapies targeting specific metabolic pathways. Such a multidimensional framework may help refine strategies to overcome immunotherapeutic resistance, support more precise implementation of cancer immunotherapy, and inform a more clinically viable approach to metabolite-directed cancer treatment.

Safety considerations for targeting obesity-derived metabolites

Despite the therapeutic promise of targeting obesity-derived metabolites to help reprogram the tumor microenvironment (TME) and support immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), the safety boundaries of such strategies require careful consideration. Because metabolic pathways are broadly distributed across normal tissues and integrated into whole-body homeostasis, interventions aimed at modulating obesity-associated metabolites may induce systemic metabolic perturbation, organ-specific toxicity, and amplified adverse effects in combination settings. These risks may be particularly relevant in patients with obesity, who frequently harbor baseline metabolic dysfunction and may therefore be more vulnerable to treatment-induced metabolic decompensation.

First, inhibitors of central metabolic pathways may exert off-tumor toxicities because their targets are rarely confined to malignant tissues [219, 220]. For example, cholesterol synthesis inhibitors such as statins and terbinafine may reduce cholesterol availability within the TME, but may also interfere with steroidogenic organs, including the adrenal glands and gonads, thereby disturbing endocrine homeostasis. Statins can additionally cause myalgia and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. Similar concerns extend to other metabolic inhibitors [221–223]. The CPT1 inhibitor etomoxir may disrupt hepatic energy metabolism and promote liver injury, whereas systemic metabolic regulators such as metformin may impose additional hepatic and renal burden [224, 225]. Importantly, because these agents are often combined with ICB or chemotherapy, their toxicities may overlap or become synergistically amplified, thereby exacerbating multi-organ injury.

Second, exogenous metabolite supplementation also carries non-negligible risks. Metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) often display narrow therapeutic windows and context-dependent biological effects, such that exposures capable of enhancing anti-tumor immunity in one setting may prove detrimental in another. Although TMAO has been reported to enhance ICB efficacy by promoting inflamed tumor states, elevated systemic TMAO has also been associated with chronic kidney disease, diabetes, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and poorer immunotherapy outcomes in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma [211]. Similar concerns apply to SCFAs and probiotics, as excessive supplementation may disrupt intestinal microbial homeostasis and aggravate gastrointestinal or nutritional complications [226]. The case of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) appears even more complex, as their immunological effects are highly heterogeneous and cannot be predicted solely by concentration. In this context, indiscriminate supplementation of BCAAs or their derivatives may, in some settings, inadvertently favor tumor progression [227, 228].

Third, dietary interventions designed to reshape systemic metabolite pools, including ketogenic diet (KD) and fasting-mimicking diet (FMD), also present safety and feasibility limitations. Although these strategies may improve immune-metabolic crosstalk, prolonged use can increase the risk of dietary monotony, micronutrient deficiency, renal burden, and hypoglycaemia when combined with glucose-lowering agents [228, 229]. Moreover, preclinical studies suggest that KD may exert context-dependent adverse effects, including paradoxical shortening of survival under specific inflammatory conditions and acceleration of metastatic progression in certain models [230, 231]. These findings indicate that dietary metabolic interventions should be implemented only after comprehensive clinical evaluation and under specialist supervision.

Finally, the biological heterogeneity of obesity itself further complicates the safety profile of metabolite-targeted therapy [232]. Patients with metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) may retain greater tolerance to such interventions, whereas those with metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO) may be particularly susceptible to cumulative toxicities owing to pre-existing metabolic derangements.

Collectively, to enable safe clinical translation, metabolite-targeted therapies should be embedded within an individualized, multidimensional safety framework that (i) increases intervention specificity, (ii) optimizes therapeutic windows, (iii) aligns treatment choices with obesity subtypes and comorbidities, and (iv) incorporates longitudinal monitoring of metabolic parameters and organ function. Such a framework may be important for preserving anti-tumor benefit while minimizing off-tumor risk, and for supporting safer and more precise clinical translation of metabolite-guided immunotherapy.

Challenges and prospects

Although the rapid development of immunotherapy has significantly improved clinical outcomes, therapeutic resistance remains a major challenge. To address immunotherapy resistance more effectively, increasing attention has focused on strategies aimed at modulating metabolite availability and composition within the TME from a whole-body metabolic perspective, with the broader goal of supporting anti-tumor immunity and informing clinical translation. However, several core challenges must still be addressed before such approaches can be more effectively and safely implemented. These challenges mainly fall into three categories: individualized clinical application, insufficient delivery precision and TME specificity, and the spatiotemporal and context-dependent complexity of metabolite-mediated immune regulation.

First, the clinical translation of metabolite-targeted strategies will require more individualized implementation. Personalized dietary interventions, taking into account age, sex, comorbidities, dietary habits, and metabolic status, may be necessary when integrating metabolic targets into clinical therapy. In addition, circadian and behavioral factors, including sleep patterns, may influence anti-tumor immune responses and therapeutic responsiveness, potentially through interactions with the circadian-immune axis. Accordingly, further investigation of chronotherapy, together with individualized biological clock assessment such as salivary melatonin profiling and peripheral blood clock-gene expression, may help refine future immune-metabolic intervention frameworks.

Second, current metabolite-directed interventions remain limited by insufficient delivery efficiency from the systemic compartment to the local TME and by inadequate targeting specificity once within the TME. These constraints support the rationale for exploring TME-selective delivery platforms, including nanocarrier-based localization of MCT1 inhibitors such as AZD3965 or cell-selective delivery strategies that may improve the local precision of LDHA inhibition. Integration of metabolite antagonists with advanced delivery technologies may therefore help improve the precision of TME metabolic reprogramming and support anti-tumor immune responses while limiting off-tumor effects.

Third, the immunological effects of metabolites within the TME are not static, but vary according to tumor type, stage, and local immune context. In some settings, the same metabolite may exert opposing effects at different stages of tumor evolution. For example, BCAAs may support Treg expansion in earlier-stage disease, yet under other conditions may enhance effector T cell function through mTOR-dependent metabolic reprogramming. This spatiotemporal and context-dependent heterogeneity underscores the importance of defining appropriate therapeutic windows for metabolite-targeted intervention across different cancers. Future studies should therefore focus on clarifying how metabolite-mediated TME remodeling interacts with tumor heterogeneity, tumor-type specificity, and stage-dependent immune states to shape therapeutic response and resistance. Such efforts may be important for developing more precise strategies to manipulate metabolite networks within the TME and to inform approaches aimed at improving responsiveness to immunotherapy.

Conclusions

Obesity-associated systemic metabolic dysregulation provides a whole-body metabolic context in which circulating metabolites can alter the composition, availability, and biological activity of substrates and signals reaching the TME. These systemically altered metabolites may thereby contribute to local metabolic remodeling and immune reprogramming, extending the focus beyond tumor-intrinsic metabolism alone. A deeper understanding of how obesity-derived systemic metabolites alter metabolite availability, composition, and signaling within the TME, and thereby rewire immune and stromal cell function, may be important for the rational development of metabolite-directed immunotherapeutic strategies. On this basis, targeted intervention against these systemic obesity-associated metabolites may be conceptually organized across three interrelated levels: modulating upstream metabolite production at the source, intercepting systemic trafficking into the TME, and functionally antagonizing metabolite-driven immunosuppressive programs within the TME itself. When integrated with immunotherapy, such multifaceted approaches may help improve the specificity and effectiveness of anti-tumor immune responses in appropriately selected settings.

Looking forward, translating metabolite targets into precision therapeutic opportunities will require more than mechanistic insight alone. It will also depend on improved patient stratification according to metabolic state, adipose distribution, tumor type, sex, age, and treatment context, as well as more precise control of delivery specificity and off-tumor metabolic liabilities. To achieve this, a more sophisticated understanding of the biosynthetic origins, systemic trafficking routes, spatial and temporal distribution, and context-dependent immunological effects of distinct metabolites within the TME will be required. Accordingly, the future of this field lies not only in combining lifestyle-based metabolic interventions with precision-targeted modulation of specific pathways in conjunction with ICB, but also in building a translationally informative source-to-sink framework that integrates mechanistic resolution, patient heterogeneity, and safety-aware translation. Such an approach may help refine strategies for addressing immunotherapy resistance, support more precise implementation of cancer immunotherapy, and inform future development of metabolite-directed therapeutic concepts. More broadly, this framework may also help contextualize the heterogeneous biology underlying the obesity paradox in the setting of ICB and support the development of metabolite-directed strategies that more explicitly account for tumor specificity, patient heterogeneity, and systemic safety.
