Basal ganglia cryptococcomas presenting as parkinsonism in an immunocompetent patient
Authors: Lorena Lucía Plazas Ruiz, Paula Fernanda Arévalo-Carvajal, Lorena García Agudelo, Daniela Arias Mariño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cryptococcal disease typically presents as meningitis in immunocompromised hosts, whereas basal ganglia cryptococcomas causing parkinsonism in immunocompetent patients are uncommon and easily overlooked. We describe a 61-year-old apparently immunocompetent man with subacute meningoencephalitis, abnormal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and a pulmonary nodule initially managed as tuberculous meningitis, who subsequently developed severe asymmetric parkinsonism. Neuroimaging later demonstrated diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement and multiple basal ganglia and brainstem cryptococcomas and repeat CSF and lung biopsy confirmed cryptococcosis. Opening CSF pressure was elevated (24 cm H2O). Induction therapy consisted of liposomal amphotericin B (4 mg/kg/day) for two weeks, followed by high-dose fluconazole consolidation; flucytosine was not available in our institution. Despite guideline-oriented antifungal therapy and a levodopa trial, parkinsonism persisted, suggesting possible structural nigrostriatal injury; however, long-term follow-up and functional imaging were not available to confirm irreversibility. Serial CSF findings summarized (Table 1) highlight the importance of repeated lumbar punctures and targeted mycological testing in subacute meningitis with nondiagnostic initial studies. To contextualize this presentation, we reviewed previously reported cases of cryptococcal parkinsonism (Table 2), which delineate a spectrum from fully reversible to L-dopa–responsive or permanently disabling syndromes, largely determined by the extent and localization of basal ganglia involvement and timeliness of treatment. This integrated case and literature review emphasizes that cryptococcosis should be systematically considered in atypical or rapidly progressive parkinsonism with inflammatory CSF and basal ganglia lesions, even in apparently immunocompetent hosts, and underscores the need for early recognition, comprehensive evaluation of pulmonary and CNS disease, and prolonged antifungal therapy to prevent irreversible neurological sequelae.

Introduction

Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii are encapsulated yeasts acquired primarily by inhalation, with initial pulmonary infection and potential hematogenous dissemination to the central nervous system (CNS). Their polysaccharide capsule, melanin production, and other virulence factors promote survival, immune evasion, and neurotropism [1], [5].

Although the global burden of cryptococcal meningitis is concentrated in people living with HIV, infections also occur in apparently immunocompetent individuals. Species-level identification was not performed in the present case; therefore, references to C. gattii are included for contextual discussion rather than definitive attribution [2], [3], [4].

Movement disorders related to CNS infections are increasingly recognized, and cryptococcal disease has been associated with parkinsonism, often in the context of basal ganglia involvement, in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. We describe an apparently immunocompetent patient with disseminated cryptococcosis, basal ganglia cryptococcomas, and severe parkinsonism, and compare this presentation with published cases to highlight diagnostic and therapeutic considerations.

Case report

A 61-year-old right-handed male cocoa and poultry farmer, with long-term exposure to soil and birds and no history of HIV infection, malignancy, transplantation, diabetes, or immunosuppressive therapy, presented with 15 days of fever, holocranial headache, lumbar pain, nausea, vomiting, blurred vision, and fluctuating disorientation. On admission, he was mildly confused, afebrile, and hemodynamically stable, without focal neurological deficits or overt meningeal signs. Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (15.16 × 10 ³/μL) and elevated C-reactive protein (93.4 mg/L). Initial brain MRI revealed subtle T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the left postcentral gyrus and subcortical white matter. Chest CT demonstrated a solitary, well-defined nodular lesion in the right upper lobe.

Three early lumbar punctures showed pleocytosis, hypoglycorrhachia, and elevated protein. India ink staining and cultures were negative, and cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) testing was not available. Opening CSF pressure measured during lumbar puncture was 24 cm H2O. In the context of a tuberculosis-endemic region, subacute meningitis with hypoglycorrhachia and a pulmonary nodule was interpreted as probable tuberculous meningitis, and empirical antituberculous therapy was initiated. Multiple tuberculosis-directed studies, including CSF acid-fast bacilli staining, sputum evaluation, bronchoalveolar lavage, and interferon-gamma release assay (QuantiFERON), were negative.

Over the following weeks, the patient developed rapidly progressive, markedly asymmetric parkinsonism: resting tremor, cogwheel rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, freezing of gait, hypomimia, and micrographia, predominantly in the left hemibody. HIV serology and immunologic assessment were negative for immunodeficiency. However, extended immunologic evaluation—including CD4/CD8 lymphocyte subsets, quantitative immunoglobulins, anti–GM-CSF autoantibodies, and advanced hematologic screening—was not available in our institution. Therefore, the patient is more appropriately described as “apparently immunocompetent.”

The patient was treated with liposomal amphotericin B (4 mg/kg/day) for two weeks followed by high-dose oral fluconazole as consolidation. Flucytosine was not administered because it was not available. Systemic and meningeal symptoms partially improved; however, parkinsonism persisted with disabling rigidity and bradykinesia and minimal response to levodopa.

A levodopa trial was attempted; however, dosage, duration, and objective motor response measures were not systematically documented, representing a limitation. Posteriorly, he was transferred to another institution; the patient subsequently died during hospitalization. The precise cause of death could not be definitively established from available records, and long-term neurological follow-up was therefore not possible.

Discussion

This case illustrates disseminated cryptococcosis with pulmonary involvement and multiple basal ganglia cryptococcomas presenting predominantly as severe parkinsonism in an apparently immunocompetent host. The clinic-radiologic pattern aligns with current understanding of cryptococcal virulence, neuroinvasion, and infection-related movement disorders.

The patient’s occupational exposure to soil and birds represents a recognized environmental risk factor. This is described in the literature as a critical determinant for infection, where the interaction of environmental exposure and particular host characteristics is crucial [11]. In Colombia, research has been undertaken to ascertain the geographical distribution and ecological niche of various Cryptococcus species, revealing that the C. gatti species complex is predominantly isolated from Eucalyptus spp. trees, whereas C. neoformans is primarily derived from bird feces, particularly Columba livia (pigeons), both of which are extensively prevalent in the country [12]. Despite this knowledge, the environmental characteristics of the patient remain unclear, constituting a limitation of our investigation.

Cryptococcus expresses key virulence factors, including the polysaccharide capsule and melanin, which significantly contribute to virulence, protect against host defenses, and favor survival within the CNS. Additional determinants such as urease and phospholipases, together with intracellular survival in macrophages, facilitate endothelial damage, immune evasion, and dissemination [1]. CNS invasion occurs through transcellular traversal, paracellular migration, and a phagocyte-mediated “Trojan horse” mechanism, all supported by experimental and translational data [5], [13]. Microglial activation and infection-driven neuroinflammation can further exacerbate neuronal dysfunction and contribute to long-term sequelae [14], [15].

Pulmonary involvement in this patient, confirmed as cryptococcal infection, is consistent with inhalational acquisition and subsequent hematogenous spread. Pulmonary cryptococcosis frequently presents with solitary or multiple nodules that may mimic neoplasm or bacterial infection and can be asymptomatic or incidental [2], [3], [16]. The coexistence of a cryptococcal lung nodule and CNS lesions supports a disseminated process arising from the respiratory tract, although species-level identification (C. neoformans vs C. gattii) was not performed in this case and therefore should not be inferred [17].

The lesion distribution in both putamina, internal capsule, thalami, and mesencephalon directly involves the nigrostriatal and thalamocortical motor circuits. Prior reports of cryptococcal parkinsonism have associated parkinsonian features with basal ganglia or related structural involvement. Some patients exhibit reversible parkinsonism after antifungal treatment when injury is mainly functional; others show persistent deficits when there is extensive destructive basal ganglia disease [6], [7], [8], [9], [10].

Experimental evidence that cryptococcal infection can induce dopaminergic neuron degeneration and α-synuclein accumulation provides biological plausibility for infection-related parkinsonism [18]. However, in our patient this mechanism remains speculative, as no patient-specific biomarker or histopathologic confirmation of α-synucleinopathy was available.

Our patient’s persistent severe parkinsonism with poor levodopa response, in the setting of multiple basal ganglia cryptococcomas, is suggestive of possible structural nigrostriatal injury; however, definitive confirmation of irreversibility is limited by the absence of functional imaging and long-term neurological follow-up. This phenotype is comparable to reported cases in immunocompetent hosts with basal ganglia cryptococcal abscesses or extensive lesions [7], [8].

Current guidelines recommend induction therapy with amphotericin B plus flucytosine for at least two weeks (and longer in cases of cryptococcomas, C. gattii infection, or persistent culture positivity), followed by consolidation with high-dose fluconazole for at least eight weeks and maintenance therapy for 6–12 months, tailored to host status and disease severity. In settings without flucytosine, amphotericin B plus high-dose fluconazole is an accepted alternative [19], [20]. In our setting, flucytosine was not available, and the patient received liposomal amphotericin B followed by high-dose fluconazole, consistent with accepted alternative regimens when flucytosine cannot be used.

Infection-related movement disorders should be managed primarily by treating the underlying infection; dopaminergic therapy may be useful when residual pathway integrity allows symptomatic benefit [21]. Reversible or L-dopa–responsive parkinsonism has been reported in selected cryptococcal cases without extensive structural damage, whereas destructive basal ganglia involvement is typically associated with limited response, as in this patient [6], [7], [8], [9], [10].

This case underscores the need to consider cryptococcosis in atypical or rapidly progressive parkinsonism with inflammatory CSF and basal ganglia lesions, even in the absence of evident immunosuppression. Repeated CSF evaluation including CrAg, targeted neuroimaging, and assessment of pulmonary lesions are essential to achieve timely diagnosis and to initiate appropriate, prolonged antifungal therapy aimed at preventing irreversible neurological sequelae.

Limitations

Limitations reflect real-world practice in a resource-variable setting. Species-level identification (C. neoformans vs C. gattii) and extended immunologic studies were not performed, and detailed documentation of intracranial pressure–directed interventions and levodopa trial parameters was limited. These constraints do not alter the central clinico-radiologic correlation but should be considered when interpreting mechanistic and prognostic inferences.

Conclusion

Cryptococcal infection should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical or rapidly progressive parkinsonism, particularly when accompanied by inflammatory CSF findings and basal ganglia lesions, even in apparently immunocompetent hosts. This case illustrates how disseminated cryptococcosis with pulmonary involvement and basal ganglia cryptococcomas may result in significant disruption of nigrostriatal motor circuits when diagnosis is delayed or incomplete. Repeated CSF evaluation including cryptococcal antigen, targeted neuroimaging, and systematic assessment of pulmonary nodules are essential to avoid misdiagnosis as tuberculous or neoplastic disease. Early implementation of guideline-based, prolonged antifungal therapy may improve neurological outcomes and prevent permanent motor disability.
