Life course socioeconomic trajectories and chronic disease risk in later life among 30,055 older adults across 27 European countries (the SHARE study)
Authors: Taymara C Abreu, Joline W J Beulens, Ilonca Vaartjes, Joreintje D Mackenbach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough socioeconomic position (SEP) is consistently associated with health outcomes, little is known about life course SEP in relation to chronic disease risk over long-term follow-up and in multiple countries. This study aimed to identify life course socioeconomic trajectories and examine their relation with chronic disease risk in later life in a cohort study of an older European population of 27 countries.MethodsWe used data from the European Longitudinal Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). SEP was measured in childhood (household overcrowding in childhood), adulthood (educational attainment) and older adulthood (income). Occurrence of eight chronic diseases was self-reported over a 16-year follow-up period. We identified life course socioeconomic trajectories with latent class growth analysis (LCGA) and assessed their association with chronic disease risk using covariate-adjusted Poisson regression.ResultsAmong 30,055 participants, we identified a five-trajectory solution. The risk of chronic disease in older adulthood was lower for those in the stable high trajectory (0.87; 95% CI: 0.82–0.93) and steep upward stable (0.90; 95% CI: 0.86–0.94) trajectories compared to those in the most disadvantaged trajectory (stable low). In gender-stratified analysis, models showed similar findings as those for the main analysis.ConclusionsOur study revealed five distinct life course SEP trajectories in a sample of European individuals aged 50 years or older. Two trajectories were associated with chronic disease risk later in life, with more advantageous trajectories associated with reduced chronic disease risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04544-7.Keywords: Social determinants of health, Social mobility, Ageing, Multimorbidity, Health inequalities, Poverty, High-income countries, Cardiovascular disease, Diabetes, Lifespan

Background

Low socioeconomic position (SEP) is one of the social environment factors most consistently associated with poor physical and mental health [1–6]. For instance, a meta-analysis with cohort studies from high-income countries found that individuals with low education are 50% more likely to experience cardiovascular events than those with high education [7]. Indeed, chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading causes of death in Europe. Moreover, they are unequally distributed across socioeconomic groups [8–12]. However, the pathways through which SEP can affect health, particularly chronic disease risk, are not yet fully understood. Hypothesised pathways include unhealthy lifestyle behaviours [13], poor health literacy, limited access to healthcare[14, 15], and psychological stress triggered by exposure to poverty-related issues (e.g. low-SEP individuals may experience lower social cohesion than their peers) [14].

There is substantial evidence base for the impact of SEP on chronic disease risk at different life stages [15–19]. However, studies have often not fully accounted for the fact that SEP develops and may change across one’s life span, and that SEP may have a life-long influence on health [20, 21]. As generally agreed, the association between life course SEP and health should be examined through the lens of a life course theoretical framework, which accounts for the evolving and bidirectional association between SEP exposure and health [18, 19]. Within this perspective, one approach to assess the effects of life course dynamics of social hierarchy on health is life course trajectories. Underlying this approach is the hypothesis that distinct socioeconomic trajectories (i.e. latent sub-groups of individuals who share a common pattern of life course SEP) exert differential effects on health outcomes due to differences in magnitude, timing, duration, and sequence/order of changes (or continuity) in life course SEP [22–24]. Examples of distinct SEP courses include upward and downward mobility, persistent disadvantage, intermittent improvements, and instable trajectories. Compared to other life course analyses, this method can help uncover nuanced, non-linear, and unexpected patterns of life course SEP exposure. Consequently, trajectories can characterise at-risk sub-groups that may not be revealed by other designs, such as simple transition models with pre-defined SEP categories (e.g. social mobility models) [25, 26].

A number of studies applied a socioeconomic life course perspective. In general, studies in high-income countries have found that disadvantaged life course SEP is associated with an increased risk of chronic disease [27–32]. For instance, a study by Jungo et al. explored the association between life course SEP categories with multimorbidity and polypharmacy. They concluded that disadvantageous SEP, either in childhood or throughout life, can predict multimorbidity later in life [29]. However, notwithstanding previous research, life course SEP trajectories have not been sufficiently explored in relation to chronic disease risk. Most studies have relied on a priori theoretical categories of life course SEP, which may not fully capture the relevant SEP trajectories present in some contexts [33]. As an alternative, person-centered data-driven methods (e.g. latent class growth analysis) offer a flexible approach that can uncover additional patterns and sub-groups in the data while integrating multiple longitudinal time points of complex data [33–36]. This approach has been used to derive distinct life course SEP trajectories in relation to other health outcomes [37–39].

Other important gaps remain. For instance, even when using a life course approach, studies often measure a limited time span [40] and include a young population while most chronic diseases occur later in life [28]. Furthermore, studies are restricted to single or few countries [41] and often include small and non-generalisable samples. Thus, the trajectories derived are potentially less informative in a cross-country context, and their impact in later life stages remains undetermined.

In order to fill these research gaps, this study aims to identify life course SEP trajectories and examine their relation with physical chronic disease in later life using data from a large cross-national longitudinal cohort study of the older European population. By examining a sample of older European individuals from 27 countries, accounting for heterogeneity in socioeconomic dynamics across these countries, we expect to uncover broader and more generalisable findings.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective study complemented by retrospective data, drawing on data from the European Longitudinal Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) (www.share-eric.eu) [42, 43]. SHARE is an ongoing, large representative multi-national longitudinal cohort of non-institutionalised individuals aged 50 years and older, encompassing 28 European countries (i.e. Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Ireland, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland) and Israel [42, 43]. Data have been collected every 2 years since 2004 in face-to-face interviews by trained interviewers using standardised computer-assisted personal interviewing. Retrospective data, including early-life health and socioeconomic circumstances, was collected in waves 3 and 7 (SHARELIFE). In the present study, we included data from waves 1 to 8, collected between 2004 and 2020 [44–52]. In wave 3, only retrospective data (SHARELIFE) was collected.

Sampling design and recruitment strategies were designed to obtain nationally representative samples of participants, as described previously [43]. Refreshment samples were included to reduce sample attrition. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Mannheim and the Ethics Council of the Max Planck Society. Participants provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

We included individuals who participated in a retrospective wave (i.e. SHARELIFE) and at least one prospective wave (i.e. waves 1–2 and 4–8). We excluded participants who i) did not provide information on chronic diseases; ii) reported chronic disease at their baseline assessment (i.e. upon entering the study); iii) were aged below 50 or above 90 years upon entering the study; and v) reported poor health status either during childhood or adulthood (before age 50). The latter was done because we modelled the relationship between SEP (independent variable) and chronic disease risk (outcome variable) as a social causation phenomenon. We also excluded participants from Israel as it is not a European country, and Ireland as the country participated in only one prospective wave. The initial study sample included 91,704‬ respondents. After the exclusion criteria, the final sample comprised 30,055 individuals from 27 countries (Fig. 1).

Independent variable: life course socioeconomic trajectory

The independent variable “life course socioeconomic trajectory” was generated from measures of SEP that reflect three life periods (i.e. childhood, adulthood, and older adulthood). The measures that refer to the first two life periods were collected retrospectively at wave 3 or wave 7 (SHARELIFE). The repeated measures that refer to older adulthood were obtained over the prospective waves 1–2 and 4–8.

Because no single SEP measure is consistently assessed across the lifespan in SHARE, or it is not relevant for all life stages (e.g. in childhood, participants have likely not reached their highest education attainment; therefore, the measure is less relevant), we measured SEP at different life phases based on different indicators, consistently measured across countries. We included (i) household overcrowding in childhood, (ii) education attainment and (iii) income in older adulthood. For the robustness check, a different set of SEP measures was considered (i.e. occupation of the household’s main breadwinner in childhood, participants’ occupation during adulthood, and wealth in older adulthood).

Childhood SEP

Household overcrowding (i.e. rooms/capita) was categorised into three groups: underoccupied (more rooms than inhabitants in the household), balanced, and overcrowded (fewer rooms than inhabitants) [53]. The occupational skill level of the household’s main breadwinner was measured based on the 10 main occupational groups of the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO) developed by the International Labour Office (1990) according to skill levels [54]. Following categorisation previously employed in SHARE [55], the 10 levels were grouped into four categories: 1 — elementary occupations and armed forces, 2 — skilled (service, shop or market sales worker, skilled agricultural or fishery worker, craft or related trades worker, and plant/machine operator or assembler), 3 — associate (technician or associate professional, clerk), and 4 — managerial (legislator, senior official or manager, professional).

Adulthood SEP

Participants’ highest education attainment was based on the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED-97) and followed the categorisation adopted by SHARE [56, 57]. Due to missing data in the education variable, we used existing imputed data provided by the SHARE team [56]. We categorised the highest education attained as primary education or less, secondary education, or tertiary education. Participants’ occupation skill level was operationalised using the same approach for the “Occupation skill level of the household’s main breadwinner” in childhood. Unlike the main breadwinner, we obtained the ISCO of participants’ longest occupation between the ages of 30 and 49.

Older adulthood SEP

Household net income was calculated as the sum of income from early retirement and survivor pensions, private and occupational pensions, disability pensions-benefits, unemployment benefits, insurance and social assistance [46]. Household net wealth was calculated as the sum of net wealth in property, cars, company shares, and liquid funds minus debts [46]. For income and wealth, values were converted into Euros, adjusted for purchasing power parities to German Euros in 2015, and subsequently adjusted for the individuals’ household size. For each time point and within each country, income and wealth were categorised into tertiles and quartiles, respectively. Due to the high percentage of missing data in these variables, we used existing imputed data provided by the SHARE team [56, 58].

Outcome

Previous studies are hindered by a single-disease approach, while unfavourable socioeconomic conditions are likely to affect a range of health outcomes across the lifespan. As such, our outcome variable (chronic disease risk) was defined as developing at least one of eight self-reported physical chronic conditions (heart attack, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, stroke or cerebral vascular disease, diabetes or high blood sugar, chronic lung disease, Parkinson’s disease, and cancer) during follow-up. These conditions were assessed in every wave (except wave 3) through the following question: “Has a doctor ever told you that you had / Do you currently have any of the conditions on this card?” Although SHARE collects data on a range of health conditions, in the present study, we only considered those consistently assessed across all waves, excluding diseases that are not commonly of acute nature (e.g. fractures and infections). Since physical chronic conditions are generally less prone to underdiagnosis/underreporting than mental health conditions, particularly in older populations [59–61], we restricted our analysis only to physical health conditions to minimise the potential impact of underdiagnosis and underreporting of health conditions.

Covariates

Associations between the independent variable (life course socioeconomic trajectories) and the outcome (chronic diseases risk) were adjusted for age at baseline (continuous), gender (male vs. female), birth cohort (between 1918 and 1938 [reference], between 1939 and 1948, and 1949 and later), living arrangements at baseline (living with a partner vs. living alone), attrition (died [reference], dropped out, intermittent missing data, and interviewed throughout the study), and welfare regime of the country of residence at older adulthood (Social Democratic (Denmark, Finland and Sweden), Conservative (Austria, Belgium, France, Germany, Luxembourg, Netherlands and Switzerland), Southern (Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Malta, Portugal and Spain), and Central and Eastern European and Baltic (Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovakia and Slovenia)), which followed categorisation previously used in SHARE [62, 63]. SHARE data does not distinguish between gender and biological sex; therefore, in the context of this study, we refer to gender to account for the effects of both social and biological mechanisms of socioeconomic factors on health.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data (i.e. median, interquartile range (IQR), and percentage (%)) was performed to describe characteristics of the whole sample. Descriptive data by life course socioeconomic trajectories are also presented.

First, we conducted latent class growth analysis (LCGA) to derive the independent variable (life course socioeconomic trajectories) from SEP indicators (here used as dependent variables of the LCGA model), using a maximum of 9 time points per participant (i.e. two retrospective measures from childhood and adulthood and a maximum of seven prospective measures from older adulthood). In this step, participants’ development of SEP over time was used to identify latent sub-groups of individuals sharing similar trajectories [35]. We used the lcmm package in R, setting ‘thresholds’ as the link function [64] and accounting for “welfare regime of country of residence”. We determined the optimal number of socioeconomic trajectory groups with a linear function. For model selection, we considered Bayesian Information Criterion and Akaike Information Criterion (BIC and AIC, respectively, for which a lower value indicates a better fit), the number of individuals in each trajectory (i.e. trajectories should include at least 5% of the total sample) and the plausibility of the shape of the optimal solutions as depicted in trajectory plots. We performed a post hoc assessment of the optimal solutions using Average Posterior Probability of Assignment (APPA; threshold of 0.70), Odds of Correct Classification (OCC; threshold of 5.0) and relative entropy (threshold of 0.5) [36, 65]. Finally, based on the optimal solution, participants were assigned to the socioeconomic trajectory for which they presented the highest probability of membership. Conditional probabilities of SEP categories by life course socioeconomic trajectory and time point were produced for the optimal solution.

Second, we conducted modified Poisson regression to investigate the association between the life course socioeconomic trajectories — generated by LCGA — and the outcome variable (chronic disease risk). Given the high frequency of the outcome, we employed modified Poisson to provide more accurate estimates [66]. We hypothesised that a disadvantaged life course socioeconomic trajectory increases the risk of developing chronic disease in older adulthood compared with a consistently advantaged trajectory. The reference category was the trajectory considered to be the “worst-off”. We evaluated crude and fully adjusted models (see the “ Covariates” section). We hypothesised that life course SEP would have differential effects on chronic disease risk by gender; therefore, we performed gender-stratified analysis. We additionally tested for interaction between life course socioeconomic trajectories and the welfare regime of the country of residence (i.e. Social Democratic, Conservative, and Central and Eastern European and Baltic), providing estimates for the full sample and gender-specific models.

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses. First, because we modelled the relationship between SEP and chronic disease risk as social causation and therefore excluded participants who reported poor health status during childhood or adulthood, we performed a sensitivity analysis including these individuals, resulting in a sample size of 36,933 participants. Second, we reran the analysis using a second set of SEP indicators (i.e. robustness check). Third, given a high percentage of missing data for the occupation variables in the second set of SEP indicators (i.e. robustness check), we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants from countries with more than 20% missing data on the occupation variables (i.e. occupation of the household’s main breadwinner at childhood or participants’ occupation during adulthood). Removing these countries (i.e. Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta and Romania) resulted in a sample of 25,299 participants. Fourth, we performed a sensitivity analysis including participants with prevalent cases of chronic disease at baseline, which resulted in a sample of 62,712 participants. We used the survey package in R. See Additional file 1: Fig. 1 for an overview of analyses, SEP indicators, and sample sizes included.

LCGA models with the lcmm package use all available data, and Modified Poisson regression with the glm package employs complete case analysis. Despite missing data in the exposure variables “household overcrowd” (n = 1292, 4%), “household main breadwinner occupation” (n = 5285, 18%) and “participant’s occupation” (n = 3214, 11%), the LCGA models utilised all available data to determine an individual’s class-membership probabilities (i.e. probability to be assigned to a given trajectory) for all participants. As a result, unadjusted modified Poisson regression models included all participants from the analytical sample. For the adjusted models, missing data on covariates were only present in the variable living arrangements (n = 316, 1%) and were then excluded, resulting in a sample size of 29,739‬. For the occupation variables, due to a higher proportion of missing data (18% and 11%), sensitivity analyses were performed (see the “ Sensitivity analysis” section).

The significance level for this study was set at p-value < 0.05. We used the packages lcmm, glm and survey in the software R (version 4.2.2).

Results

Descriptive statistics

Our main analysis included 30,055 participants (16,223 women, 111,460 observations). At baseline, the median (IQR) age was 57 [51–64] years, and the majority was living with a partner (76%). Participants were followed for an average (SD) of 6.5 years (4.5), and 44% developed a chronic disease throughout the study (Table 1).

Main analysis and gender-stratified analysis

Following the model selection methodology, a five-trajectory model was considered the optimal solution in the main analysis and in the gender-specific analysis (Fig. 2 and Additional file 2: Table 1). In post hoc analysis, the values of relative entropy for optimal solutions were all above the recommended threshold of 0.5, ranging between 0.55 and 0.56. In general, all models presented suboptimal APPA and OCC for at least one class within the optimal solutions. The values of APPA remained close to the recommended threshold of 0.70 (classes ranging between 0.60 and 0.74), while only one of the classes within the optimal solution presented OCC values below 5.0 (classes ranging between 2.7 and 36) (Additional file 3: Table 2).

Life course socioeconomic trajectories for overall and gender-specific samples, Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). Life course socioeconomic trajectories for the overall and gender-specific samples. Each plot depicts five distinct patterns of socioeconomic status over the lifespan. Trajectories were identified using latent class growth analysis (LCGA) employing different socioeconomic indicators at multiple life stages

We did not find any differences in the shape of the five derived socioeconomic trajectories when the main analysis and gender-stratified models were compared (Fig. 2). The trajectories found were labelled as (1) stable low (set as the reference trajectory), (2) (stable) high, (3) steep upward, (4) steep upward stable, and (5) upward. The patterns and magnitude of the plotted trajectories are consistent with the conditional probabilities of SEP categories by life course socioeconomic trajectory and time point (Additional file 4: Table 3).

Participants’ characteristics varied across the socioeconomic trajectories to which they were assigned (Table 2 and Additional file 5: Table 4). For the main sample (Table 2), in general, the trajectories contrasting the most were stable low (most disadvantaged trajectory) and stable high (most advantaged trajectory), while the other trajectories presented mid-range patterns. For instance, in the stable low trajectory, 3% of the individuals completed tertiary education and 49% developed chronic disease throughout the study vs. 76% and 37% in the stable high trajectory, respectively.

Sensitivity analysis

Additional file 2–6: Tables 1–5 and Additional file 7: Fig. 2 present the results of sensitivity analyses. Similarly to the main analysis, in sensitivity analyses including participants that reported poor health status either during childhood or adulthood and in the one including participants with prevalent cases of chronic disease at baseline, a five-trajectory model was the optimal solution. In the former, a similar association between trajectories and chronic disease risk was observed. In the latter, although individuals in better-off trajectories showed a reduced risk of chronic disease compared to the reference category, the trajectories statistically associated with chronic disease risk differed from those in the main analysis. For the sensitivity analysis using a second set of SEP indicators (robustness check) and the sensitivity analysis excluding participants from countries with a high percentage of missing data on the occupation variables, the optimal number of trajectories differed from the main analysis. In the former, a six-trajectory model was considered the optimal solution. The shape of the trajectories was somewhat comparable to the main analysis, except for the absence of a stable high trajectory and the presence of the trajectories steep downward and steep downward stable (Additional file 7: Fig. 2). In the latter sensitivity analysis, the number of participants included was reduced, and a two-trajectory model was the optimal solution (Additional file 2: Table 1). Despite differences in the optimal solutions revealed, we observed similar findings for the association between life course socioeconomic trajectories and chronic disease risk in all sensitivity analyses. That is, more advantageous socioeconomic trajectories were associated with decreased risk of chronic disease in older adulthood when compared to the most disadvantageous trajectory (fully adjusted model, Additional file 6: Table 5).

Discussion

In this study among older European adults from SHARE, we identified five life course socioeconomic trajectories in the total sample and in gender-stratified samples. Compared to the most disadvantaged trajectory (stable low), stable high and steep upward trajectories were associated with lower chronic disease risk in later life, with individuals in these two trajectories having up to a 13% reduced risk of chronic disease compared to those in the reference trajectory.

To our knowledge, this is the first study employing LCGA, a data-driven approach, to derive life course socioeconomic trajectories and examine them in relation to chronic disease risk. Previous research on life course SEP and chronic disease risk have used varying methods to operationalise SEP across the life course. An a priori, theory-driven equivalent of LCGA employs pre-determined SEP life course categories, which makes it infeasible to directly compare the shapes of the life course trajectories with LCGA studies such as ours. Both approaches have advantages and disadvantages [33]. A data-driven approach allows for flexibility in the modelling of trajectories. Moreover, its probabilistic assignment can provide insights and uncover meaningful real-world trajectories that a study solely relying on a priori hypothesis would fail to consider otherwise. Among its known limitations are the limited reproducibility and instability. This was observed in our sensitivity analyses, where the optimal solution differed in the number of trajectories and shape, albeit comparable with our main analysis. Moreover, although data-driven methods can match relevant theoretical hypotheses, this is not always the case [33, 35], and interpreting them assuming they follow a theoretical foundation is often not recommended [36]. On the other hand, theory-based methods follow a conceptual framework and allow for comparison across different settings; however, these methods can be rigid and may not represent the reality in some contexts [33]. In a study comparing social relations trajectories derived from a data-driven approach and a theoretical approach as predictors of functional limitations, the authors found that the methods did not map out the same trajectories, with the data-driven revealing two additional groups. The data-driven approach was preferred, although the authors recommend combining both approaches in future research to corroborate the findings [67]. It is noteworthy that in our study, theory and plausibility were also considered in the choice of the number of optimal classes by examining trajectory plots.

Our findings on the association of socioeconomic trajectories with chronic disease risk are in line with the social trajectory hypothesis of life course models [23, 40]. Studies from high-income countries have generally found evidence for an association between disadvantaged life course SEP and an increased risk of chronic diseases [27–32], as well as chronic disease risk factors [68–71], and other health outcomes, such as hospitalisation [72] and mortality [73]. While two trajectories were significantly associated with chronic disease risk, no significant difference was observed between upward and steep upward trajectories and the worst-off trajectory in relation to disease risk. This could be explained by the different timing of the increase in SEP, whereby an increase in SEP later in life may not neutralise the potential negative long-term effects of a more disadvantaged SEP earlier in life (e.g. the critical period hypothesis). Moreover, individuals who transition to high SEP may experience trade-off effects from upward mobility, a phenomenon referred to as “skin-deep resilience” [74].

We observed that the findings in gender-stratified samples remained similar to those in the total sample. While women may experience lower levels of SEP throughout their lifespan [75] and be more vulnerable to exposure to socioeconomic disadvantages (e.g. experiencing worse physiological responses) [76, 77], the literature presents mixed findings in relation to the differential effect of life course SEP on chronic disease risk according to gender [31, 32, 78, 79]. Moreover, a study with SHARE data showed that a gendered effect of SEP on health seems to depend on the country’s welfare state. In Western and Southern European countries, there was a stronger effect of SEP on women’s health than on men’s health, whereas in Social Democratic countries, no gender differences were observed [80]. Such regional variabilities could help explain a lack of SEP-gendered effect on chronic disease risk in our study.

The strengths of our study include a large sample size from a representative cohort encompassing 27 European countries. The standardised data collection approach, the long time lag between exposure and outcome, and the use of advanced methodological techniques to model and reveal life course socioeconomic trajectories offered the opportunity to examine life course SEP and chronic disease risk in a unique way. Moreover, we were able to consider differences across countries based on the welfare of the country of residence.

Limitations must also be acknowledged. We cannot rule out residual confounding. Also, we used retrospective data to measure SEP in childhood and adulthood. However, a validation study within SHARE showed that the effect of memory on answers to childhood retrospective questions was minimal [81]. Self-reported data on the presence of chronic diseases could potentially lead to recall bias; however, it has been shown that self-reports on cardiometabolic diseases have a reasonable agreement with medical records [82]. Additionally, relying on self-reported data for the outcome variable could underestimate the prevalence of chronic conditions. This may be particularly pronounced among individuals with low SEP, as access to diagnostic opportunities and/or underreporting may be socially patterned [83–85], which could potentially lead to an underestimation of our findings. Moreover, selection bias from sample selection or sample attrition throughout the study could have impacted our findings. Although SHARE provides weights to account for complex sampling design and panel attrition [46], longitudinal weights from waves 1 through 8 are only available to a small subsample of the participants (4% of our total sample). Nonetheless, we have adjusted our regression models for attrition. Another limitation of our study concerns the absence of a potentially relevant downward SEP trajectory. Absent in the main analysis, such a trajectory was revealed by our robustness check, where wealth (instead of income) was employed as the SEP indicator for older adulthood. Beyond the data-driven nature of our approach, the absence of a downward income trajectory may partly be attributed to relatively stable pension incomes provided by European social welfare systems and also reflect that a large proportion of older individuals in SHARE lived through the “golden age” of upward social mobility in Europe after World War II [86]. This may have resulted in too few individuals experiencing downward income mobility to form a distinct trajectory. Additionally, underrepresentation of individuals experiencing downward mobility — who may be more likely to drop out or not participate in surveys than those with consistently low SEP — may have contributed to the under-detection of downward trajectories. In contrast, moderate wealth decumulation among retirees, as seen in SHARE [87], may have contributed to the identification of distinct and sizable downward wealth trajectories in our robustness check analysis.

We employed a single-indicator SEP approach. Although other indicators could yield different findings, we obtained comparable results with our robustness check with a different set of SEP indicators. In fact, despite a debate in the literature over the use of single or multiple SEP variables [88–91], some authors argue that single measures can be appropriate. For instance, a study in a European population compared the predictive performance of a SEP index with a single SEP indicator in relation to cardiovascular risk and mortality. Although the SEP index seemed slightly superior concerning mortality outcomes among women, the results were very similar for incidence, demonstrating that using a single SEP indicator may be justified [27].

Our study contributes to broader literature by highlighting the role of disadvantaged life course SEP in increasing the risk of chronic disease later in life. Future research in the field should not only continue to refine the application of statistical methods, such as LCGA, but also focus on obtaining high-quality, longitudinal data, which can help elucidate, for instance, the potential role of timing of exposure to chronic disease risk. While our results do not provide specific leverage points for intervention, our findings suggest that the prevention of chronic disease later in life may be achievable by intervening on socioeconomic inequalities throughout life, which widens the opportunities for interventions. More specifically, informed by a life course perspective, examples of interventions may include increased access to high-quality education for children and young adults and financial support, such as tax credits or cash transfers, for adults and older adults.

Conclusions

Our study revealed and characterised five distinct life course socioeconomic trajectories in a sample of European individuals aged 50 years or older. Among the five identified trajectories, two were associated with chronic disease risk in later life, with individuals belonging to these two trajectories having a lower risk of chronic disease compared to those in the most disadvantaged trajectory. Our findings offer valuable insights into the complex relationship between life course SEP and health. Future research is warranted and should focus on obtaining high-quality, longitudinal data and advancing methodological techniques for statistical analysis.
