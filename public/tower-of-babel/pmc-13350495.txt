Glycemic control and blood product quality of Canadian blood donors with diabetes
Authors: Elyn M Rowe, Marcus Shew, Noor Ali‐Mohamad, Celina Phan, Mindy Goldman, Jason P Acker, Dana V Devine, James D Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany blood centers now accept donors with diabetes, provided their condition is “well‐controlled”. However, glycemic control is not routinely assessed at donation, and the impact of donor diabetes on blood product quality remains unclear.Study Design and MethodsTo screen glycemic control, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured in whole blood from 256 unique donors with either type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), or without known diabetes. In parallel, routine quality control (QC) data were linked with donor metadata to assess the impact of donor diabetes on red cell concentrate (RCC) and apheresis platelet concentrate (PC) quality at the time of their expiry.ResultsNearly half of donors with diabetes had suboptimal glycemic control (HbA1c >7 %) at donation, including 57.1 % of donors with T1D and 43.4 % with T2D. RCC units from donors with T2D (n = 1,329) had higher hemolysis (p <.001) and a greater proportion exceeding the clinical threshold of 0.8 % hemolysis (p <.001). These differences were not observed with T1D (n = 46). Apheresis PC units from donors with T2D (n = 164) had lower expiry pH (p = .014), with a trend toward more units below the clinical cutoff of pH 6.4 (p = .059). Differences in both products were most pronounced in donors with T2D using both metformin and insulin.ConclusionMany donors with diabetes have suboptimal glycemic control, and RCC and PC from donors with T2D have reduced quality. The observed effects are modest, likely not driven by glycemic control alone, and do not support additional deferral criteria for donors with diabetes.Keywords: diabetes, HbA1c, hemolysis, pH, platelet concentrate, quality control, red cell concentrate, T1D, T2D

INTRODUCTION

The global prevalence of both type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) is rising. 1 , 2 Given the increasing demand for blood products on a backdrop of aging donor populations with rising diabetes prevalence, 3 donor eligibility criteria continue to be re‐evaluated to reduce unnecessary deferrals. Canadian Blood Services (CBS) now accepts most individuals living with diabetes as whole blood and apheresis donors. People living with diabetes currently represent ~2% of the CBS donor pool 4 and this number is expected to rise.

Donor eligibility criteria vary internationally. Many centers outside North America have blanket deferrals for insulin use or diabetes in general, 5 which were classically rooted in both donor safety concerns (hypoglycemia or syncopal reactions) and recipient risk (bovine‐derived insulin use and bacteremia). 4 , 6 Updated 2025 guidance from the European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & Healthcare now aligns with North American practice; revising recommendations to remove insulin‐based deferrals and to allow individuals with diabetes to donate provided their condition is “well‐controlled”. 7 Yet, CBS and most other blood centers do not measure glycemic control at the time of donation, but rather rely on self‐reported control, including the specific CBS criteria of no severe hypoglycemia requiring assistance or hyperglycemia requiring hospitalization within the past 3 months.

While glycemic control may not impact adverse reactions in donors, 8 there is ample evidence that hyperglycemia impacts both red blood cells and platelets, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 which may translate to altered quality of blood products from donors with diabetes. In fact, elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) in the diabetes diagnostic range associates with worse storage performance of red cell concentrate (RCC), 13 while data are limited but mixed on the impact of T2D on platelet concentrate (PC). 14 , 15 Importantly, products from donors with T1D have not been characterized.

Extra tubes of whole blood—called ‘retention samples’—are routinely collected at CBS donations. CBS also routinely collects quality control (QC) data in a fraction of blood products at expiry. This QC data has previously been linked with donor metadata collected in screening questionnaires to identify differences in product quality based on donor age/sex, 16 but linkage with medication data to infer the impact of donor health conditions on product quality has not been explored.

As one of the first countries to remove all insulin‐based deferral criteria, Canada is well‐positioned to lead assessment of blood product quality in donors with diabetes and evaluate the accuracy of self‐reported “well‐controlled” diabetes at the time of donation. Here, we leveraged existing CBS resources to provide some of the first empirical data on blood donors with diabetes. Our two primary aims were: (1) Screen glycemic control in CBS donors with diabetes, and (2) Assess potential differences in the quality of blood products from donors with diabetes using existing CBS expiry QC data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimen and data sources

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board (H22‐00413) and the CBS Research Ethics Board (2022.016). At the time of donation, CBS donors are presented with an information pamphlet, outlining the possibility of their donated products or metadata being used in research. Therefore, additional informed consent was not required to obtain de‐identified specimens or metadata.

Donor record flagging for HbA1c screening

De‐identified donor questionnaire data (age, gender, self‐reported diabetes [response to “Do you have diabetes?” and additional note on type], and medications taken within the last 3 days) were obtained from CBS for all unique donors between January 2021 and April 2022. As less than 1% of CBS donors self‐identify as transgender, 17 gender was used as a proxy for donor sex. Medication data were cleaned using a custom R script to standardize entries to generic names. Donors were classified as T1D, T2D, or No (known) Diabetes based on self‐reported diabetes and/or medication data. As both oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs) and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) are common first‐line treatments for T2D, 18 they were grouped to support T2D classification, despite occasional off‐label uses during this period.

Donor classification for the HbA1c screen is summarized in Figure 1A. Donors were classified as T1D if they either: (a) self‐reported diabetes AND specified T1D, (b) self‐reported diabetes AND reported taking insulin but no OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs, or (c) did not have data for self‐reported diabetes but reported taking insulin without OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs. As there were more donors with likely T2D, classification criteria were more stringent. Donors were classified as T2D if they either: (a) self‐reported diabetes AND specified T2D AND did not report taking insulin, or (b) self‐reported diabetes AND reported taking OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs but no insulin. These criteria exclude donors with T2D on insulin—a group with more advanced T2D, representing an estimated 4.1% of Canadian donors with diabetes 4 —but was chosen to better delineate the types of diabetes with the available donor metadata. Donors without diabetes were defined as those who did not self‐report diabetes AND did not report taking either OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs or insulin.

Overview of donor classification strategies for distinct datasets. (A) Flowchart of donor classification criteria for donors with T1D, T2D, and with no (known) diabetes based on donor questionnaire data for HbA1c screen. (B) Flowchart of modified donor classification criteria for expiry QC data analyses, removing GLP‐1 RA criteria and including T2D donors on insulin.

We ‘flagged’ donor records of random samples of 300 donors from each diabetes group, and 300 donors without diabetes who were age‐ and sex‐matched to the donors with T2D—the more common type of diabetes and the age range more likely to capture undiagnosed pre‐diabetes or diabetes—using the MatchIt package in R. These 'flags' were used by national testing centers to ship unused retention samples from donors, following routine donations across Canada.

HbA1c screen

Retention samples of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)‐anticoagulated whole blood were aliquoted and stored frozen at either −20°C or −80°C, up to 6 days post‐collection. Specimens were shipped on dry ice to DynaLIFE Medical Labs, a division of Alberta Precision Laboratories (Edmonton, Alberta, Canada), for HbA1c measurement with the A1cX3 immunoassay on the Cobas c513 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics). For most specimens, aliquots stored at −20°C were used, but 13 specimens were analyzed using aliquots stored at −80°C. We confirmed that HbA1c results were consistent between storage temperatures (Supplementary Figure 1).

Expiry QC data

Expiry CBS QC data from January 2018 to January 2025 were obtained for RCC stored in saline‐adenine‐glucose‐mannitol (SAGM‐RCC) and apheresis PC stored in either 100% autologous plasma (before June 2023) or 60% platelet additive solution (PAS‐E) and 40% autologous plasma. The proportion of PC units collected after June 2023 was not significantly different between any compared groups. QC data was linked with donor metadata to identify units from donors with diabetes, using a similar framework as described above, with minor modifications, summarized in Figure 1B. Given the rising popularity of GLP‐1 RAs prescribed beyond diabetes management, 19 only OHAs were used in the medication‐based classification of T2D. Here, insulin use was not filtered out in the T2D group to capture potentially more advanced T2D donors taking insulin. Definitions of T1D and No (known) Diabetes remained the same but allowed for GLP‐1 agonists in both groups.

As storage hemolysis associates with donor age and sex 20 and can differ by manufacturing method and bag type, 21 RCC units from the same production site, manufacturing method, and bag type, from age/sex‐matched donors without diabetes, were randomly selected using the MatchIt R package to more accurately assess the impact of donor diabetes alone on RCC quality. The same matching approach was used for PC, given associations between platelet metabolism over storage and donor age/sex. 22

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed with R (version 4.5.0). Proportions of donors with suboptimal glycemic control were compared with results from a published Canadian community‐based screen 23 using the chi‐squared test. Continuous quality parameters from diabetes groups were compared with either matched units or all units using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, given skewed and non‐normal distributions. For sterility assessments of each product type or to compare the proportions of units passing clinically relevant cutoffs indicating suboptimal quality, proportions of units from donors with diabetes were compared with the proportion of all units from donors without diabetes using the chi‐squared test, or Fisher's exact test when there were <5 observations in any group.

RESULTS

Glycemic control in CBS donors

Average glycemic control at the time of donation was evaluated in a total of 263 specimens from 256 unique donors between January 2023 and December 2024 (Figure 2A). In total, specimens were received from n = 66 from donors without diabetes, n = 106 from donors with T2D, and n = 91 from donors with T1D. Importantly, most donors taking both OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs and insulin (~4.1% of Canadian donors with diabetes 4 ) were excluded from this screen for simplicity of donor grouping, which may slightly lower the average HbA1c in the T2D group given that insulin is typically prescribed as a treatment escalation strategy in T2D. 24 Repeat donors in the sample were n = 3 T1D, n = 3 T2D, and n = 1 without diabetes, and their HbA1c values correlated strongly between donations that ranged from 14 days (male plasma donor with T2D) to 183 days (whole blood donor without diabetes) apart (Figure 2B). This is expected within shorter time frames between donations, as HbA1c is a marker of average glycemic control over 2–3 months.

In donors without self‐reported diabetes or diabetes‐related medications, only one male donor had an HbA1c in the pre‐diabetes range as defined by Diabetes Canada (>6%) (Table 1), and the mean HbA1c of that group was 5.48% ± 0.31%. The mean HbA1c of all screened donors with T1D was 7.24% ± 1.00%, and T2D was 7.14% ± 1.20%, with several male donors having very high HbA1c, up to 12.5% (Figure 2A). Using the Diabetes Canada target of 7% HbA1c as a threshold for optimal glycemic control, 25 57.1% of donors with T1D and 43.4% of donors with T2D had suboptimal glycemic control with a HbA1c >7% (Table 1), indicating that many donors are not well‐controlled. However, when compared with a Canadian community pharmacy‐based HbA1c screening study that reported 67.4% of individuals with T1D and 58.1% of individuals with T2D had a HbA1c >7%, 23 glycemic control in the donor population with diabetes was slightly better (Table 1). The proportion of donors with suboptimal HbA1c was significantly lower in donors with T2D than the community pharmacy‐based study, but this difference did not reach statistical significance in donors with T1D. When split by donor sex (Table 2), higher proportions of males in both T1D (62.9%) and T2D (51.4%) groups had suboptimal glycemic control, with HbA1c >7%.

CBS SAGM‐RCC expiry QC data

Of the 49,892 units of CBS SAGM‐RCC that had available hemolysis and sterility data, we identified 1,329 units from donors with T2D (Figure 3A–G), and 46 from donors with T1D (Figure 3H–K). To control for confounding by sample size and donor age/sex, units from donors with diabetes were first compared with equal numbers of units from age/sex‐matched donors without diabetes. Here, there was a statistically significant increase in expiry hemolysis in units from donors with T2D (median 0.31% [IQR 0.12–0.50]; Figure 3A) but not T1D (median 0.24% [IQR 0.13–0.35]; Figure 3H). This T2D effect persisted when compared with all units from all donors without diabetes (median 0.24% [IQR 0.11–0.36]), across both manufacturing methods (Figure 3E), and the proportion of units above the clinical cut‐off of 0.8% hemolysis was significantly higher relative to units from all donors without diabetes (Figure 3F). No units from donors with T1D had hemolysis >0.8% (Figure 3E). Hemoglobin and hematocrit were also significantly higher in units from donors with T2D (Figure 3B,C), but no differences were detected in units from donors with T1D relative to matched units (Figure 3I,J). The frequency of bacterial growth in initial screening was not significantly different in units from donors with either type of diabetes, relative to all units from donors without diabetes (Figure 3D,K), and two of the three T2D units with growth were false positives, while one was an unconfirmed positive with follow up BACT/3D alert testing.

Next, we parsed apart our units from donors with T2D based on insulin and/or metformin use to gain insight into the impact of these medications on hemolysis levels in RCC. In the small group of donors with T2D using insulin but no metformin, there was no significant difference in hemolysis levels. However, in all other groups there was significantly higher hemolysis in T2D relative to matched units (Figure 3G), with the greatest effect size observed in donors using both metformin and insulin (Supplementary Table 1).

CBS apheresis PC QC data

Of the 5,385 units of CBS apheresis PC with available pH and sterility data, we identified 5,221 units from donors without diabetes, 164 from donors with T2D, and 1 from a donor with T1D. Due to limited T1D sample size, we only proceeded with analysis for apheresis PC data from donors with T2D (Figure 4A–G). When compared with matched units from donors without diabetes (mean 7.22 ± 0.17), expiry pH was significantly lower in units from donors with T2D (mean 7.14 ± 0.22; Figure 4A), and this persisted when compared with units from all donors without diabetes (mean 7.19 ± 0.22; Figure 4E). However, the proportion of units with a pH below the clinical cut‐off of 6.4 only approached statistical significance (p = .059) relative to units from all donors without diabetes (Figure 4F). There were no significant differences in platelet concentration (Figure 4B) or the number of platelets per unit (Figure 4C) between the matched groups. The frequency of bacterial growth was not significantly different in units from donors with T2D relative to all units from donors without diabetes (Figure 4D), and three of the four T2D units with growth were false positives, while one was an unconfirmed positive with follow‐up BACT/3D alert testing.

When parsed apart by insulin and metformin use, the small group of units from T2D donors on both metformin and insulin had significantly lower pH, but the smaller group on neither medication had no significant difference in pH relative to control units at expiry (Figure 4G). The much larger group units from donors with T2D on metformin but not insulin also had significantly lower pH than matched controls at expiry, and there were no units from donors with T2D on insulin but not metformin. Again, effect sizes varied across medication groups, with the largest in donors using both metformin and insulin (Supplementary Table 1).

With the rollout of INTERCEPT‐treated pathogen inactivated PC in June 2023, we also had QC data for 812 units of psoralen‐treated platelets: 796 from donors without diabetes, and 16 from donors with T2D. No significant T2D‐related differences were detected in expiry pH (Figure 4H), platelet concentration (Figure 4I), or total platelets per unit (Figure 4J). Unsurprisingly, there were no units with bacterial growth (Figure 4K).

DISCUSSION

Our goal was to provide the field with empirical data on blood donors with diabetes. We found that nearly half of CBS donors with diabetes had suboptimal glycemic control at the time of donation. We also found that RCC and PC from donors with T2D had small but statistically significant reductions in quality by expiry. Importantly, our HbA1c screen was distinct from our expiry QC data analysis, and we cannot directly link glycemic control to blood product quality in these datasets.

Several studies have screened HbA1c in blood donor populations, 8 , 13 , 26 , 27 , 28 but to our knowledge, this is the first study to assess long‐term glycemic control in blood donors with diabetes to determine how well‐controlled their condition is at the time of donation. Interestingly, an untargeted donor HbA1c screening study from Brazil detected HbA1c values up to 18.2%, 26 while a similar study in Norway found a maximum value of 7.2%. 29 Here, with targeted screening of donors with diabetes, the maximum detected HbA1c was 12.5%. This indicates considerable variability in the control of diabetes in blood donors, despite eligibility criteria across these sites being similar in the recommendation of well‐controlled diabetes.

While we found a handful of HbA1c values above 10% in Canadian donors, HbA1c levels were overall lower in donors with diabetes when compared with the broader Canadian population with diabetes. 23 This could suggest that additional layers of eligibility screening for blood donors may be selecting for individuals with more well‐controlled diabetes to some degree, as is the case for the general healthy donor effect observed in older blood donors. 30 It could also be a result of reductions in HbA1c attributable to frequent blood donation, as screened individuals were all repeat‐donors. 31

In SAGM‐RCC expiry QC data, hemolysis levels were significantly higher in units from donors with T2D, but not T1D, relative to both age/sex‐matched controls and all donors without diabetes, and the proportion of units with hemolysis over 0.8% was significantly higher in donors with T2D. However, absolute differences between median hemolysis were small (0.05%–0.07%), and most T2D units (96.9%) remained below the clinical threshold. While small, this observed effect corroborates a recent study that demonstrated increased hemolysis in units from donors with glycemic control in the diabetes range; 13 notwithstanding, the lack of difference in units from donors with T1D suggests that factors beyond glycemic control alone underlie the difference in RCC storage performance. While not directly assessed in this study, it is likely that factors related to elevated body mass index (BMI) contributes to the observed difference in T2D hemolysis. In Canada, 83.1% of individuals with T2D in the general population are overweight or obese (BMI >25 kg/m2), 32 and a recent report leveraging QC data in Australia found that elevated BMI (>27 kg/m2) was associated with increased hemolysis. 33

With apheresis PC QC data, we found significantly lower expiry pH in units from donors with T2D relative to both matched and all donors without diabetes, but with small absolute differences between groups of 0.05–0.08 pH units. The proportion of units from donors with T2D below pH 6.4 also trended higher, but it was still very few overall (4 units, 2.5%). A smaller study that characterized single‐donor buffy coat PC over storage did not detect any difference in expiry pH in units from donors with T2D, 15 but they were not powered to detect the relatively small effect size observed here. Importantly, apheresis PC QC data from donors with T1D was insufficient for analysis here, and should continue to be monitored.

A recent study reported that metformin is a causative agent for platelet storage failure, marked by an increase in lactate production and mirrored decrease in pH. 34 When we grouped our donors with T2D and matched controls by metformin and insulin use, effect sizes of the reduction in quality at expiry for both RCC and PC were largest in the T2D groups using metformin (Supplementary Table 1). This may support the idea of metformin altering storage performance of both PC and RCC, however the large‐scale Recipient Epidemiology and Donor Evaluation Study (REDS‐III) did not detect a strong correlation between RCC storage hemolysis and metformin levels detected by mass spectrometry. 35 Notably here, the T2D group taking both metformin and insulin had the largest quality differences relative to controls in both products. Use of both medications may indicate a treatment escalation strategy when glycemic control targets could not be managed by a single treatment or other interventions, 24 which could suggest a more advanced condition involving other systemic changes in the donor. Unfortunately, most donors taking both OHAs/GLP‐1 RAs (including metformin) and insulin were excluded from our initial HbA1c screen to simplify the T1D and T2D groupings. Since we did not assess HbA1c in donors on both metformin and insulin, and our HbA1c screen and QC data analysis were distinct, it is unclear whether observed differences in the QC data are linked to glycemic control.

Despite reports of increased bacteremia and infection risk in diabetes, 36 , 37 we did not detect any differences in sterility testing results of RCC or PC from donors with diabetes. These findings suggest that current donor eligibility criteria are effective in mitigating transfusion‐relevant infectious risk within the donor population, and added safeguards of pathogen inactivation will reduce this risk further. We had limited data on pathogen‐inactivated platelets, but found no indication of reduced pH in units from donors with T2D. Some studies have found that pathogen inactivation technologies like INTERCEPT used by CBS can increase lactate production in platelet concentrates, 38 which can lead to decreases in pH. It is possible that psoralen treatment of platelet products may ‘level the playing field’ in terms of platelet pH from donors with diabetes, but this is a purely speculative hypothesis. More research is warranted as pathogen‐inactivated platelets become standard across blood centers.

Strengths of this study include the novelty of the first targeted HbA1c screen in blood donors with diabetes, and the use of expiry QC data to investigate the impact of donor diabetes on blood product quality. There are also several limitations to this study. First, our classification of diabetes type based on medications is simplified and imperfect. While relatively uncommon, individuals with T1D can be on both insulin and metformin, and individuals with T2D may be on insulin without an additional hypoglycemic agent. 39 C‐peptide—a marker of endogenously produced insulin—could have been measured to verify classifications, but this would not change the broad conclusions of this study and was deemed unnecessary. Second, our T1D group in the SAGM‐RCC expiry QC data analysis was relatively small (n = 46), limiting statistical power to detect small effects on RCC quality. As such, findings related to T1D should be interpreted with caution. Continued monitoring of QC data from donors with T1D is required to generate more robust conclusions for this emerging donor group. Given our donor record flagging strategy for the HbA1c screen, we also only assessed glycemic control in repeat donors, who may have better glycemic control than first‐time donors and may have HbA1c values impacted by blood donation. 31 Further, apheresis PC data was limited to primarily (96.8%) male donors with T2D, which is common given the body weight and hemoglobin requirements for apheresis donations. However, females contribute to pooled buffy coat platelets, and the impact of diabetes on platelet quality from female donors remains unclear. Importantly, donor BMI data was not incorporated into this study as donor height and weight are not routinely measured by CBS, but it should be considered alongside diabetes in future analyses to more accurately identify donor factors associating with suboptimal product quality.

Overall, our data indicate that diabetes is often not optimally controlled at the time of donation. RCC and PC units from donors with T2D have suboptimal storage performance, but effect sizes are generally small and may not be clinically significant. The observed differences in T2D may reflect underlying differences in medication use (e.g., metformin), BMI, or diabetes progression. These potential confounders were not explicitly accounted for in the primary analyses—aside from exploratory stratification by insulin and metformin use—and further studies incorporating detailed clinical data will be required to disentangle these effects. While these results do not support additional deferral criteria for donors with diabetes, a deeper characterization of blood products from donors with both T2D and T1D is warranted to better understand how donor diabetes impacts blood product characteristics over storage.
