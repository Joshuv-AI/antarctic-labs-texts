Majorana string simulation of nonequilibrium dynamics in two-dimensional lattice fermion systems
Matteo D’Anna, Jannes Nys, Juan Carrasquilla

Abstract
The study of real-time dynamics of fermions remains one of the last frontiers beyond the reach of classical simulations and is key to our understanding of quantum behavior in chemistry and materials, with implications for quantum technology. Here we introduce a Heisenberg-picture algorithm that propagates observables expressed in a Majorana-string basis using a truncation scheme that preserves Trotter accuracy and aims at maintaining computational efficiency. The framework is exact for quadratic Hamiltonians–remaining restricted to a fixed low-weight sector determined by the physical observable–admits variational initial states, and can be extended to interacting regimes via systematically controlled truncations. We benchmark our approach on one- and two-dimensional Fermi-Hubbard quenches, comparing against tensor network methods (MPS and fPEPS) and recent experimental data. The method achieves high accuracy on timescales comparable to state-of-the-art variational techniques and experiments, demonstrating that controlled Majorana-string truncation is a practical tool for simulating two-dimensional fermionic dynamics.



I
Introduction

Real-time dynamics of fermionic lattice models underpin transport, relaxation, and nonequilibrium phase formation in correlated quantum materials.
In two spatial dimensions, direct classical simulation is especially challenging: exact methods scale exponentially and are limited in system size, tensor-network approaches face entanglement growth
Schollwöck 2011
, dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
Georges et al. 1996
is approximate away from large coordination, quantum Monte Carlo methods are limited by the sign problem
Troyer and Wiese 2005
, and neural-network approaches provide a promising yet still developing alternative
Nys et al. 2024
.
These limitations have motivated both the development of quantum simulators
Fauseweh 2024
and the development of new classical algorithms that push the entanglement frontier
Preskill 2012
.

Ultracold fermions in optical lattices now realize the two-dimensional (2D) Fermi-Hubbard model and provide new insights into phenomena of interacting fermions. Milestones include probing pairing phenomena
Hirthe et al. 2023
;
Hartke et al. 2023
, long-range antiferromagnetic order
Mazurenko et al. 2017
, time-resolved dynamics of magnetic polarons
Ji et al. 2021
, and the observation of Nagaoka polaron
Lebrat et al. 2024
. Most recently, improvements in cooling and homogeneity have pushed 2D simulators deep into the cryogenic regime at half filling
Xu et al. 2025
. These advances deliver high-fidelity, local observables and quench protocols in regimes that are challenging for numerical simulation, offering a strong incentive for classical methods to provide scalable and quantitatively reliable benchmarks for interpreting analog experiments.

Gate-based devices encode fermions using transformations such as the Jordan-Wigner or Bravyi-Kitaev mappings
Jordan and Wigner 1928
;
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002a
;
Seeley et al. 2012
, enabling fermionic dynamics to be simulated via product formulas.
Early demonstrations implemented small-scale Hubbard models on superconducting qubits
Barends et al. 2015
;
Wecker et al. 2015
.
Various studies have clarified the role of geometric locality on the simulability
Campbell 2019
;
Cade et al. 2020
;
Carobene et al. 2025
, and recent experiments have scaled to 2D Hubbard-like models
Karamlou et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Srinivasan et al. 2024
.
Current processors remain depth-limited, highlighting the continuing value of classical approaches.

Parallel to hardware progress, classical fermionic simulation continues to advance. Although quadratic Hamiltonians can be simulated efficiently
Terhal and DiVincenzo 2002
;
Bravyi 2005
, generic interactions break Gaussianity, leading to challenging numerical simulations due to extensive Heisenberg operator spreading and entanglement growth. Here we present a non-variational, Heisenberg-picture algorithm that operates directly in a Majorana-string basis and pushes the boundaries of simulating geometrically local and parity-preserving lattice Hamiltonians
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002b
.
The Majorana propagation (MP) framework we develop here generalizes the recently introduced Pauli propagation (PP, or sparse Pauli dynamics), framework
Rall et al. 2019
;
Begušić et al. 2024
;
Rudolph et al. 2025
to fermionic degrees of freedom. While PP was originally introduced for classical simulations of noisy quantum circuits
Shao et al. 2024
;
Fontana et al. 2025
, it has since proven broadly applicable. The most relevant applications in the current context range from reproducing utility experiments
Rudolph et al. 2023
to simulating two- and three-dimensional dynamics
Begušić and Chan 2025
. The connection between PP and MP was also presented in Ref.
Miller et al. 2025
, which introduced MP to find circuits that approximate ground states of molecular systems.

We develop MP for structured fermionic lattice Hamiltonians and benchmark it on one- and two-dimensional Fermi-Hubbard quenches against matrix product state (MPS) and fermionic projected entanged pair states (fPEPS) calculations, as well as against recent analog Fermi-Hubbard experiments
Ji et al. 2021
. Together, these results show that controlled truncations in the Majorana basis enable accurate estimates of real-time observables in 2D fermionic systems, reaching (and in some regimes surpassing) the accessible time scales of state-of-the-art variational techniques.

II
Methods

II.1
Majorana basis

We consider
N
fermionic modes with fermionic operators
f_{i}^{(\dagger)}
, that are used to define
2N
Majorana operators

\gamma_{j}=f_{j}^{\dagger}+f_{j},\quad\gamma_{j}^{\prime}=i\left(f_{j}^{\dagger}-f_{j}\right).

(1)

Majorana operators respect the anticommutation relations

\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}=\{\gamma_{i}^{\prime},\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\}=2\delta_{ij},\qquad\{\gamma_{i}^{\prime},\gamma_{j}\}=0.

(2)

These self-adjoint Majorana operators are used to construct hermitian Majorana strings, represented by the binary vector
v\in\{0,1\}^{2N}
as
Bettaque and Swingle 2025

\mu(v)\equiv(i)^{v^{T}\omega_{\mathrm{L}}v}\cdot\gamma_{1}^{v_{1}}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}^{v_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{N}^{v_{2N-1}}{\gamma_{N}^{\prime}}^{v_{2N}}.

(3)

The prefactor
(i)^{v^{T}\omega_{\mathrm{L}}v}\in\{1,i\}
, with the
2N\times 2N
lower triangular matrix
(\omega_{L})_{ij}\equiv\delta_{i<j}
, recovers the hermiticity of
\mu(v)=\mu(v)^{\dagger}
. Note that the matrix multiplication
v^{T}\omega_{\mathrm{L}}v
is to be understood as
\operatorname{mod}2
. Since
v
uniquely determines
\mu(v)
, we will refer to both
v
and
\mu(v)
as (Majorana) strings. We introduce the shorthand notation
\mu(v_{ij})\equiv i\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j},\mu(v_{ij^{\prime}})\equiv i\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}^{\prime}
.

A Majorana mode
i
is said to be
unpaired
if a string contains Majorana operator
\gamma_{i}
without
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
, or vice versa, i.e.
v_{2i-1}\neq v_{2i}
. The number of unpaired Majorana operators in the string
\mu(v)
is denoted by
w_{s}(v)\in\{0,\cdots,N\}

w_{s}(v)=N-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{v_{2i-1},v_{2i}}\right).

(4)

Some useful relations between Majorana strings and fermionic operators are derived in Appendix
A
, of which we summarize the results here. The number operator
n_{i}
on site
i
reads

n_{i}=f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+i\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\mu\left(v_{ii^{\prime}}\right)\right).

(5)

Hopping operators between sites
i,j
(assuming that
i<j
in the site labeling order chosen in Eq. (
3
)) yield

f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}+f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(i\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}^{\prime}-i\gamma_{i}^{\prime}\gamma_{j}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu(v_{ij^{\prime}})-\mu(v_{i^{\prime}j})\right).

(6)

Majorana strings are closed under multiplication (and hence form a group)

\mu(v)\mu\left(v^{\prime}\right)=\zeta\cdot\mu\left(v+v^{\prime}\right),

(7)

where
v+v^{\prime}
is to be understood as the bitwise addition
\operatorname{mod}2
and
\zeta=\pm 1,\pm i
. A closed expression for
\zeta=\zeta\left(v,v^{\prime}\right)
is given in Appendix
A
.

More importantly, the strings satisfy the (anti)commutation relation

\mu(v)\mu\left(v^{\prime}\right)=(-1)^{v^{T}\omega v^{\prime}}\mu\left(v^{\prime}\right)\mu(v),

(8)

where
\omega\equiv\omega_{L}+\omega_{L}^{T}
. Commutation or anticommutation between two strings
v,v^{\prime}
is therefore fully determined by the value of
v^{T}\omega v^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
.

For any Majorana string
\mu(v)
we define the weight
w(v)\in\{0,\cdots,2N\}
as the number of unique Majorana operators in
\mu(v)
or, equivalently, as the number of non-zero entries in
v
, i.e.

w(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{2N}v_{i}.

(9)

The fermion parity
p(v)
is defined as
p(v)=(-1)^{w(v)}
.
Hermitian Majorana strings describing physical observables must commute with the parity operator in order to respect fermionic superselection rules, and this is only satisfied by even parity strings, i.e. strings with an even number of Majorana operators.

In Appendix
A
we show the simple but informative relation for the phase factor in Eq. (
8
)

v^{T}\omega v^{\prime}=\left(w(v)w(v^{\prime})-w(v\odot v^{\prime})\right)\operatorname{mod}2,

(10)

where
\odot
is elementwise multiplication. With Eq. (
10
) we find that such even parity strings commute if they have an even number of Majorana operators in common, and they anticommute if they have an odd number in common. This relates to the fact that
w(v)w(v^{\prime})
is always even for even parity strings, and hence
w(v\odot v^{\prime})
determines the parity in Eq. (
10
).

II.2
Majorana propagation

We focus on simulating the dynamics of fermionic lattice Hamiltonians.
Given an initial state
\rho
and an observable
O
, we aim to evaluate the expectation value

\Tr\left(U(\tau)\rho U^{\dagger}(\tau)O\right)
, where
U(\tau)
denotes the time-evolution operator over a duration
\tau
.
As within PP, we switch to Heisenberg picture, and write
O
as a linear combination of Majorana strings
O=\sum_{v}\alpha_{v}\mu(v)
, such that

\Tr\left(U(\tau)\rho U(\tau)^{\dagger}O\right)=\sum_{v}\lambda_{v}\Tr\left(\rho\mu(v)\right).

(11)

with
\lambda_{v}\in\mathbb{R}
a real-valued coefficients to each string that we aim to determine. Writing the unitary dynamics as a sequence of gates (i.e. using Trotterization)
U(\tau)=U_{m}\cdots U_{1}
, we obtain the coefficients
\lambda_{v}
using

\begin{split}U(\tau)^{\dagger}&OU(\tau)=U_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots U_{m}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{v}\alpha_{v}\mu(v)\right)U_{m}\cdots U_{1}\\
&=U_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots U_{m-1}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{v}\lambda^{(m)}_{v}\mu(v)\right)U_{m-1}\cdots U_{1}\\
&~\vdots\\
&=\sum_{v}\lambda_{v}\mu(v)\end{split}

(12)

To compute the coefficients
\lambda^{(m)}_{v}
after applying the
m
’th unitary, written in the generic form
\exp(-i\theta\mu(v^\prime)/2)=\cos(\theta/2)\mathbb{I}-i\sin(\theta/2)\mu(v^{\prime})
, and find the following
MP splitting rules
(analogous to splitting rules in PP)

\begin{split}&e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}\mu\left(v^{\prime}\right)}\mu(v)e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}\mu\left(v^{\prime}\right)}=\\
&~=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\mu(v)\quad\quad\quad\text{ if }[\mu(v^{\prime}),\mu(v)]=0\\
\begin{array}[]{r}\cos(\theta)\mu(v)+\sin(\theta)(-1)^{g\left(v,v^{\prime}\right)}\mu\left(v+v^{\prime}\right)\\
\text{ if }\{\mu(v^{\prime}),\mu(v)\}=0\end{array}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(13)

where
v+v^{\prime}
again corresponds to a new string obtained through bitwise
\operatorname{mod}2
addition.
The explicit form of
g(v,v^{\prime})\in\{0,1\}
is given in (
37
) in Appendix
A
.
As already remarked, the (anti)commutation of
v,v^{\prime}
is determined by Eq. (
8
). We loosely refer to the case of anti-commutation as the “splitting branch”, since it causes an increase in the total number of Majorana strings in the observable,
v\to v,v+v^{\prime}
.

Evidently, the splitting branch is the reason why Majorana propagation for a general Hamiltonian will inevitably face a computational barrier due to the increasing number of strings with non-negligible
\lambda_{v}
. However, below we discuss two special cases where the simulation avoids this computational barrier.

First, unitaries consisting solely of fermionic Clifford gates, corresponding to the rotation
\exp(-i\theta\mu(v^\prime)/2)
with rotation angles
\theta\in\{k\pi/2,k\in\mathbb{N}\}

Bettaque and Swingle 2025
, can be simulated efficiently with Majorana propagation. This can be easily understood from the splitting rule in Eq. (
13
), where the “splitting branch” does not generate additional strings.

A second, more important exception is the simulation of fermionic Gaussian dynamics, or the dynamics governed by a quadratic Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}h_{ij}f_{j}.

(14)

This is less obvious, and is proven in Appendix
D
, where we show that the dynamics is constrained to a low-weight subspace of Majorana strings determined by the observable
O
. Hence, as long as the subspace of a given weight is small enough—as for the spatially local observables considered here—the dynamics is classically efficient (for more details see Appendix
D
).
The “weight conservation” can be understood as follows. New strings are solely generated through the sine branch in (
13
). For a unitary generated by a weight
2
Majorana string
\mu(v_{ij^{\prime}})
(e.g. a hopping unitary) applied to a string
\mu(v)
, they are of the form
v\to v^{\prime}=v+v_{ij^{\prime}}
. However, at the same time, to activate the sine branch,
\mu(v)
must anticommute
\mu(v_{ij^{\prime}})
, which by Eq. (
10
) means that they share exactly one non-zero index:
w(v\odot v_{ij^{\prime}})=1
. Since we have
w(v_{ij^{\prime}})=2
and in general
w(v+v^{\prime})=w(v)+w(v^{\prime})-2w(v\odot v^{\prime})
, we obtain
w(v)=w(v+v_{ij^{\prime}})
, showing that the weight is indeed unaltered.
Hence, from this observation, we can regard non-Gaussianity of the Hamiltonian (rather than the physically less relevant Clifford structure) as a degree of complexity for Majorana propagation.

In the general case, to mitigate the exponential growth in the number of strings of the observables while retaining accuracy, we employ truncation schemes to reduce the number of Majorana strings.

II.3
Truncations

We perform two truncations to mitigate the growth of strings:

1.

A first truncation is coefficient truncation: whenever a string
\mu(v)
in the linear combination in Eq. (
12
) has
|\lambda_{v}^{(m)}|<\varepsilon
, for a fixed
\varepsilon
, we remove
\mu(v)
from the linear combination.

2.

The second truncation is based on the overlap between Majorana strings and initial Fock states (
16
): we introduce a cutoff
S\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
on the number of unpaired Majoranas
w_{s}(v)
in Eq. (
4
).

To motivate the second rule, we observe that only Majorana strings
\mu(v)
with fully paired Majoranas have a non-vanishing overlap with Fock states

\left|n_{1}\cdots n_{N}\right\rangle:=\big(f_{N}^{\dagger}\big)^{n_{N}}\cdots\big(f_{1}^{\dagger}\big)^{n_{1}}|0\rangle,

(15)

for
|0\rangle
the fermionic vacuum. This condition reads

v_{2k-1}=v_{2k}\quad\forall k=1,\cdots,N.

(16)

and the overlap for such strings
v
is given by

\left\langle n_{1}\cdots n_{N}\right|\mu(v)\left|n_{1}\cdots n_{N}\right\rangle=(i)^{v^{T}\omega_{L}v}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(i(-1)^{n_{j}}\right)^{v_{2j-1}}.

(17)

A detailed discussion is given in Appendix
B.1
.
Notice that the amplitude of the overlap is the same for all strings
v
with
w_{s}(v)=0
:
\absolutevalue{\left\langle n_1 \cdots n_N\right| \mu(v)\left|n_1 \cdots n_N\right\rangle}=1
.
As we propagate further towards the initial state, it becomes increasingly unlikely that strings
v
with a large
w_{s}(v)
will contribute significantly to the final expectation value.
A simple truncation rule based on estimations of the potential overlap with the initial state is not always available for a general initial state
\rho
(possibly beyond a pure Fock state). For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to initial states close to pure Fock states.

To maintain high accuracy, it is important that the errors introduced by the truncation of Majorana strings are consistent with the errors introduced by the Trotterization scheme. Although this has not been pointed out in the context of Pauli propagation, we introduce the concept of Trotter-consistent cutting, which is also directly applicable to the former framework.
Consider a single
p
-th order Trotter layer
U(\tau)
such that

\displaystyle U(\delta\tau)=e^{-iH\delta\tau}+\order{(\delta\tau)^{p+1}}.

(18)

To analyze the Heisenberg action on an observable
O
, we apply Campbell’s identity
Hall 2015

U^{\dagger}(\delta\tau)OU(\delta\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\frac{(i\delta\tau)^{k}}{k!}[H,O]_{k}+\order{(\delta\tau)^{p+1}},

(19)

where we defined the nested commutators

[X,Y]_{k}=\begin{cases}Y&\text{if }k=0\\
\underbrace{[X,\cdots[X,[X}_{k\text{ times }},Y]]\cdots]&\text{if }k>0,\end{cases}

(20)

We now split the Hamiltonian into parts that preserve and alter the number of unpaired Majorana operators
\gamma
,

H=H_{P}+H_{NP},

(21)

Here,
H_{P}
preserves the number of unpaired Majoranas (e.g., density or interaction terms) and
H_{NP}
is the non-preserving part (e.g., hopping terms). At order
k
in (
19
), the largest change in the number of unpaired Majorana operators arises from the contribution with
k
commutators by
H_{NP}
, i.e.
[H_{NP},O]_{k}
.
If
H_{NP}
is composed of hopping terms, each commutator can change the number of unpaired Majoranas by at most
2
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\Delta w_{s}(v)\in\{+2,0,-2\}.

(22)

Applying this with
k=p
shows that the largest possible increase in the number of unpaired Majoranas arising at order
(\delta\tau)^{p}
is
2p
.
Our truncation strategy is therefore:

•

Fix a global cap
S
on the allowed number of unpaired Majoranas.

•

After each Trotter layer, truncate all strings with
w_{s}(v)>S
.

•

Within a Trotter layer, allow
temporary
growth up to
S^{\prime}=S+(2p)/2
, since such contributions can recombine at order
(\delta\tau)^{p}
into strings with
w_{s}\leq S
after the Trotter layer (via
p
nested commutators with hopping terms).

Coefficient truncation is nevertheless enforced after each gate application. A typical truncation in PP is
weight truncation
(also used for MP in Ref.
Miller et al. 2025
). Instead of counting the number of unpaired operators
w_{s}(v)
, weight truncation is imposed on the total weight
w(v)
in Eq. (
9
), i.e. one truncates strings
v
with
w(v)>W
. However,
w_{s}(v)
is a better indicator for estimating the overlap of a Majorana string with an initial Fock state (see Eq. (
16
)), and hence, we continue with the unpaired truncation.

\displaystyle H

III.3
Interacting Fermi-Hubbard dynamics: 2D

We investigate the applicability of the MP method to the simulation of modern quantum analog experiments at strong interactions. IV
Summary and outlook

We presented a Heisenberg-picture simulator for interacting local lattice fermion Hamiltonians based on a closed calculus of Majorana strings and a Trotter-consistent truncation that caps the growth of unpaired Majoranas. Numerically, we demonstrated the method on free and interacting models: (i) Gaussian dynamics, where weight conservation keeps the evolution in a tractable subspace; (ii) 1D Fermi-Hubbard quenches, where results are reliable to time scales beyond those of fermionic tensor networks; and (iii) a challenging 2D setup relevant to cuprates, where our hole-dynamics agree qualitatively with state-of-the-art ultracold-atom experiments and expose finite-size effects through Lieb-Robinson diagnostics. Furthermore, we demonstrate how the method can be combined with variational representations of the initial state.
Together, these results show that controlled truncations in the Majorana basis provide useful, systematically improvable classical estimates of real-time observables in two-dimensional fermionic systems at short to intermediate time scales.

The operator

All the terms in (

e^{-i\theta n_{i}n_{j}}=e^{-i\theta/4}e^{-i\theta/4\mu(v_{ii^{\prime}})}e^{-i\theta/4\mu(v_{jj^{\prime}})}e^{i\theta/4\mu(v_{ii^{\prime}jj^{\prime}})}.

Hopping terms are given by

A.2
Commutation relations with Majorana binary vectors

Majorana strings are uniquely described in terms of their binary vector

3

Gaussian fermion dynamics

Majorana propagation can efficiently simulate Gaussian fermion dynamics of observables with a low (or very high) Majorana weight. We demonstrated this in the main text, starting from the viewpoint of the branching structure for Majorana propagation in Eq. (

First, we introduce some notation. Consider, for simplicity of notation, a quadratic Hamiltonian that is also charge conserving. The results can be easily generalized. We have

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=h_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}

\displaystyle d_{i}(t)

(61)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[-i(U_{jk}(t)-U_{jk}^{*}(t))\gamma_{k}+(U_{jk}(t)+U_{jk}^{*}(t))\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\right].

(62)

In short

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=R_{jk}\gamma_{k}-I_{jk}\gamma_{k}^{\prime},

(63)

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=I_{jk}\gamma_{k}+R_{jk}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}.

(64)

where we introduced

\displaystyle R_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}[U(t)]_{ij},\qquad I_{ij}=\mathrm{Im}[U(t)]_{ij}.

(65)

such that the unitarity of
U
yields the constraints

\displaystyle[U(t)]_{ik}[U^{\dagger}(t)]_{kj}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\begin{cases}R_{ik}R_{jk}+I_{ik}I_{jk}=\delta_{ij},\\[6.0pt]
R_{ik}I_{jk}-I_{ik}R_{jk}=0.\end{cases}

(66)

For a time-dependent Majorana product, we then get for
i\neq j

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(t)\gamma_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[R_{ik}R_{jk}+I_{ik}I_{jk}\right]-\left[R_{ik}I_{jk}-I_{ik}R_{jk}\right]\gamma_{k}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\neq\ell}\Big[R_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}-R_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}-I_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}+I_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}\Big]

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\neq\ell}\Big[R_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}-R_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}-I_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}+I_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}\Big].

(68)

where we used unitarity to remove the diagonal contribution given by the first line. The latter would form a problematic term that might otherwise generate Majorana strings of different weights.

We similarly get

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(t)\gamma_{j}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\big(R_{ik}R_{jk}+I_{ik}I_{jk}\big)\gamma_{k}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}

(69)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\neq\ell}\Big[R_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}+R_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}-I_{ik}I_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}-I_{ik}R_{j\ell}\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}\Big].

(70)

Hence, we find that
\gamma_{i}(t)\gamma_{j}(t)
and
\gamma_{i}(t)\gamma_{j}^{\prime}(t)
remain in the subspace generated by the set

\displaystyle\left\{\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell},\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime}\right\}_{k\neq\ell}\cup\{\gamma_{k}\gamma_{\ell}^{\prime},\gamma_{k}^{\prime}\gamma_{\ell}\}_{k,l}

(71)

Hence, we showed explicitly that any weight
2
Majorana string (see Eq. (
9
)) stays in a subspace spanned by weight
2
Majorana strings when subjected to the Gaussian dynamics of quadratic Hamiltonians. This result can trivially be generalized to any even-parity (or even weight) Majorana string, by combining the above results on all sets of two Majoranas. Therefore, we can conclude that
any weight
w
Majorana string remains in a subspace spanned by weight
w
Majorana strings when subjected to the Gaussian dynamics of quadratic Hamiltonians.

This implies that, as long as the subspace of a given weight is small enough, MP can simulate the dynamics of a given observable efficiently. For the spatially local observables considered here, this generally holds. In the more general case, the dimension of the subspace of weight
w
is
D=\binom{2N}{w}
where
N
is the number of fermionic modes.

Appendix E
Gaussian initial states

We consider the expectation value of an operator
O
under a time evolution
U(\tau)
, starting from an initial state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=W\ket{n_1 \cdots n_N},

(72)

where
\ket{n_1 \cdots n_N}
again denotes a simple Fock state, see Eq. (
15
). If the columns of
W
contain the one-body eigenstates ordered by increasing single-particle energies, this state represents the Hartree–Fock (HF) ground state.

When
\ket{\psi}
is the ground state of a quadratic Hamiltonian, it can be advantageous to absorb the transformation
W
into the time evolution of the operator—rather than explicitly representing
\ket{\psi}
as a potentially high bond-dimension tensor network. In this way, the expectation value can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\matrixelement{n_1 \cdots n_N}{W^\dagger U^\dagger(\tau) O U(\tau) W}{n_1 \cdots n_N}.

(73)

The unitary
W
associated with the quadratic Hamiltonian is obtained through a one-body diagonalization (see Section
D
, Eq. (
49
)), with the columns of the diagonalizing matrix ordered according to energy. Specifically, if

\displaystyle d_{i}=[V^{\dagger}]_{ik}f_{k},

(74)

defines the single-particle transformation from the original fermionic operators
f_{k}
to the diagonal modes
d_{i}
, then there exists an anti-Hermitian matrix
K
such that
e^{K}=V
. The corresponding many-body rotation is the second-quantized unitary

\displaystyle W=\exp\left(\sum_{ij}K_{ij}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}\right),

(75)

which implements the transformation
Wf_{i}W^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}V_{ij}f_{j}
and thus generates the desired Gaussian state.

Appendix F
Excitation error

Here we report a brief summary of the excitation error introduced in
Dai et al. 2025
. For each site
i
we define
n_{i}
as the occupation number
relative to the ground state
. We introduce
n_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{i}n_{i}
, and we select excitation sites according to their contribution to the total occupation number
n_{\mathrm{tot}}

\mathcal{I}_{e}=\left\{i\mid\sum_{n_{j}<n_{i}}n_{j}<(1-\eta)n_{\mathrm{tot}}\right\}.

(76)

The excitation error is then

\text{ Excitation error }=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{e}}\Delta n_{i}/n_{\text{tot }},

(77)

where
\Delta n_{i}=\absolutevalue{n_{i,\text{sim}}-n_{i,\text{exact}}}
.
