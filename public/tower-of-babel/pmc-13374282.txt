The impact of social appearance anxiety and peer bullying on eating behaviors in Syrian migrant adolescents
Authors: Fatma Tayhan Kartal, Ayşe Çiftci, Esma Demirkıran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigration may increase psychosocial vulnerability in adolescents and affect body image and eating behaviors through appearance-based social interactions. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the effects of social appearance anxiety, teasing perception and sociodemographic characteristics on the eating behaviors of Syrian migrant adolescents.MethodsA total of 600 adolescents aged 12–18 residing in the Reyhanlı district of Hatay, Türkiye participated (mean age: 15.94 ± 1.48 years; 47.2% female and 52.8% male). Data were collected using the Social Appearance Anxiety Scale, the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire for Adolescents, and the Perception of Teasing Scale, along with anthropometric measurements.ResultsFemale adolescents had significantly higher levels of social appearance anxiety than males. Social appearance anxiety was negatively associated with both emotional and uncontrolled eating. Adolescents who were dissatisfied with their physical appearance had higher emotional eating scores (β= -0.109, p < 0.05). A lower number of main meals was significantly associated with increased emotional, uncontrolled, and cognitive restraint eating behaviors (p < 0.01). Likewise, not consuming snacks was linked to higher emotional and uncontrolled eating tendencies (p < 0.01). Additionally, lower maternal education level was related to unhealthy eating behaviors.ConclusionThese findings indicate that eating behaviors in migrant adolescents are shaped not only by physiological needs but also by psychosocial and environmental factors. Interventions should focus on promoting positive body image, regular eating patterns, and parental education, while school-based programs targeting peer bullying may help reduce psychosocial risks associated with disordered eating.Keywords: Migrant adolescents, Teasing, Body image, Peer bullying, Eating behaviors

Introduction

The civil war that began in Syria in 2011 caused millions of people to be forcibly displaced, and Türkiye became one of the countries that accepted the largest number of Syrian refugees during this process. The first group of migrants entered Türkiye from the Reyhanlı district of Hatay on April 29, 2011 [1]. Forced migration is not merely a spatial relocation process; it can lead to serious adaptation problems for individuals in social, cultural, and psychological contexts [2, 3]. Migrant individuals face multilayered sources of stress such as language differences, economic uncertainty, cultural exclusion, discrimination, and social isolation after migration. Türkiye has attempted to provide basic services such as health care, education, and housing to migrants; however, structural inequalities in access to these services, especially for adolescents, remain noteworthy [4, 5]. It is known that these multifaceted challenges are often experienced during adolescence, a developmentally vulnerable period. Adolescence is a critical developmental stage in which, in addition to biological changes, identity formation, the need for social acceptance, and self-perception are shaped. Immigrant adolescents must cope not only with developmental conflicts related to their age but also with additional stressors associated with their immigrant identity [6, 7]. Recent research has also highlighted how stress associated with acculturation and challenges with cultural adaptation may raise the likelihood of psychological distress and behavioral issues among adolescent migrants [8–10]. Furthermore, studies conducted in Türkiye show that Syrian refugee teenagers face psychosocial issues such as language barriers, social exclusion, and difficulty in peer interactions during their transition process [11–13].

Immigrant adolescents are often exposed to social judgment not only because of visible physical differences but also due to cultural markers such as clothing style, accent, and lifestyle. Previous research has shown that migrant adolescents tend to experience higher levels of social anxiety than their native peers, mainly due to peer victimization, acculturation stress, and discrimination [3, 14]. Comparative studies have also found that migrant students are more likely to experience peer victimization and social anxiety symptoms than native adolescents [14, 15]. However, there is limited evidence regarding a more specific subtype of social anxiety—social appearance anxiety, which refers to the fear of being negatively evaluated based on one’s physical appearance in social contexts [16]. This construct may be particularly relevant during adolescence, a period characterized by heightened sensitivity to body image, peer comparison, and social evaluation [17]. Elevated levels of social appearance anxiety have been associated with reduced self-esteem, social withdrawal, body dissatisfaction, and maladaptive eating behaviors [3, 18]. According to recent studies, adolescents who experience higher levels of social appearance anxiety also express more anxiety about appearance-based evaluation and are more sensitive to peer criticism [14, 19, 20].

Gender differences are also significant when studying appearance-related anxiety and eating behaviors in adolescence [21–23]. Previous research indicates that adolescent girls experience higher levels of body dissatisfaction, social appearance anxiety, and emotional eating than boys [24, 25]. These disparities are often explained by larger societal pressures on girls’ physical beauty and increased sensitivity to peer appraisal during adolescence. Boys, on the other hand, may be more likely to face bullying in the form of physical or verbal assault rather than appearance-related taunting [24]. Therefore, considering these gender-related trends may offer a more thorough knowledge of how psychosocial factors affect the wellbeing of adolescents.

Immigrant adolescents may be even more vulnerable, as observable differences such as accent, appearance, or communication style can make them more visible targets for peer bullying [26]. According to research conducted on Syrian refugee pupils in Turkish schools, peer bullying and social exclusion are rather prevalent experiences that may contribute to emotional discomfort and difficulty in school integration [27]. Appearance-based teasing has been shown to increase social appearance anxiety and significantly reduce self-esteem [28]. Peer bullying generally refers to the intentional, repetitive harm inflicted by same-age peers through physical, verbal, social, or cyber means [29, 30]. Psychosocial stressors such as bullying and appearance-related anxiety have been demonstrated to shape adolescents’ eating behaviors and influence emotional regulation, body image, and academic functioning. Adolescents who encounter such stressors may exhibit increased emotional eating, using food as a coping mechanism [31–33]. Moreover, dissatisfaction with physical appearance and frequent mockery may contribute to uncontrolled or irregular eating behaviors [34]. In immigrant populations, these tendencies may be intensified by contextual factors such as low parental education levels, impaired family communication, and limited nutritional awareness [35]. Additionally, recent studies have indicated that stress and psychological distress associated with acculturation may be a factor in migrant adolescents disturbed or emotional eating practices [13, 24].

The cultural context may also influence these psychosocial experiences. Adolescent migrants frequently negotiate the cultural norms of their home country and the host community, which may have an impact on attitudes toward food and eating, social acceptance, and body image judgments [3, 24]. Adolescents from Syria residing in Türkiye may experience increased anxiety about peer acceptance, belonging, and appearance-related evaluation as they adjust to a new sociocultural setting while preserving elements of their cultural identity. Research on Syrian adolescents in Türkiye has also revealed that perceived prejudice and difficulties adjusting to a new culture may have a detrimental impact on social integration and psychological health [36].

Studies examining the combined impact of peer bullying and social appearance anxiety on eating behaviors are still scarce, despite earlier research examining psychosocial adjustment, bullying experiences, or eating patterns among adolescents independently. This disparity is notably noticeable in groups of migrants, particularly Syrian adolescents residing in Türkiye, whose psychosocial experiences may be influenced by both cultural adaption processes and migration-related stressors [12]. Therefore, a more thorough knowledge of the psychosocial factors impacting eating behaviors in migrating adolescents may be obtained by examining these variables within a unified framework.

Given these psychosocial vulnerabilities, the present study aims to examine the impact of social appearance anxiety and peer bullying on the eating behaviors of Syrian migrant adolescents living in Türkiye. The study specifically looks into whether peer bullying experiences are linked to higher levels of social appearance anxiety and whether these psychosocial factors are connected to the eating habits of adolescents. Additionally, possible gender variations in these factors are investigated because prior research indicates that females may have higher levels of emotional eating and anxiety related to looks than boys. It is hypothesized that: (1) social appearance anxiety will be positively correlated with higher levels of peer bullying; (2) social appearance anxiety and peer bullying will be positively correlated with more problematic eating behaviors; and (3) female adolescents will report higher levels of emotional eating and social appearance anxiety than male adolescents. This study intends to add to the literature by investigating these associations within a migrant adolescent’s community and offering insight into psychosocial factors impacting eating behaviors among Syrian adolescents residing in Türkiye.

Method

Research design

This study employed a cross-sectional correlational design to examine the relationships between social appearance anxiety, perception of teasing, eating behaviors, and body mass index among Syrian migrant adolescents. Quantitative data collection methods were used.

Participants

The final sample comprised 600 adolescents aged between 12 and 18 years (M_age = 15.94, SD = 1.48), including 317 males (M_age = 16.06, SD = 1.56) and 283 females (M_age = 15.80, SD = 1.48). A total of 696 adolescents were initially assessed for eligibility. Participants without parental consent (n = 15), those with missing anthropometric measurements (n = 32), and those who were not Syrian migrant adolescents (n = 49) were excluded. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 600 participants. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Instruments

Although the scales were validated in Turkish populations, the participants attended Turkish schools and were considered proficient enough in the Turkish language to understand the questionnaires.

Child three-factor eating questionnaire

The Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire for Adolescents (CTFEQ), developed by Bryant et al. [37] and adapted into Turkish with validity and reliability studies conducted by Demir et al. [38], is a scale that evaluates adolescents’ eating behaviors in three sub-dimensions: cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating. Examples of items include: “I eat small portions to control my weight” (cognitive restraint), “When I start eating, I sometimes feel that I cannot stop” (uncontrolled eating), and “When I feel sad or stressed, I tend to eat more” (emotional eating). It consists of a total of 17 items and is applied using a 4-point Likert scale. The scores obtained from the scale are calculated as average scores according to the sub-dimensions, and high scores indicate that the relevant eating behavior is more intensely experienced. Cognitive restraint (items 1, 5, 11): Indicates the level of conscious restriction of eating behavior by the individual for the purpose of weight control. Uncontrolled eating (items 3, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 15, 17): Reflects eating behavior that arises from the inability to control the urge to eat, other than physical hunger. Emotional eating (items 2, 4, 7, 10, 14, 16): Measures eating behavior without the feeling of hunger in response to emotional states. In the Turkish validity and reliability study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was 0.81, while the alpha coefficients for the subscales were 0.82 for uncontrolled eating, 0.85 for emotional eating, and 0.70 for cognitive restraint [38]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.861 for cognitive restraint, 0.912 for uncontrolled eating, and 0.926 for emotional eating, indicating good internal consistency.

The social appearance anxiety scale for adolescents

Social appearance anxiety was assessed using the Social Appearance Anxiety Scale (SAAS). The Social Appearance Anxiety Scale for Adolescents (SAAS-A) was developed by Hart et al. [16] in 2008 to measure individuals’ levels of anxiety regarding their physical appearance in social environments, and it was adapted into Turkish by Doğan [18]. The scale consists of 16 items evaluating anxiety related to negative evaluation of one’s overall physical appearance by others. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all appropriate) to 5 (completely appropriate). Example items include “I feel tense when my photograph is taken” and “I worry that people will judge my appearance negatively.” The Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient of SAAS was found to be 0.91, and the test-retest reliability coefficient was found to be 0.80. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.951, indicating excellent internal consistency. As the scores obtained from the scale increase, the individual’s anxiety about their appearance also increases [18].

The perception of teasing scale

The Perception of Teasing Scale (POTS), developed by Thompson et al. [39], was used to assess teasing experiences related to physical appearance (particularly weight) and personal competencies, as well as the perceived impact of these experiences. The scale consists of 11 items, including six weight-related teasing items and five competency-related teasing items. For each item, participants first indicate how often teasing occurs using a five-point scale (1 = never, 5 = very often) and then rate how upset they feel about it (1 = not upset, 5 = very upset). Example items include “People tease you because you are overweight” (weight-related teasing) and “People laugh at you when you do not understand something” (competency-related teasing). The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Topaloğlu and Arıkan [40]. In the Turkish validity and reliability study conducted with adolescents, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were reported as 0.86 for the weight-related teasing subscale and 0.81 for the competency-related teasing subscale. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.970 for the weight-related teasing subscale and 0.961 for the competency-related teasing subscale. The scale evaluates teasing across two domains (weight-related teasing and competency-related teasing) and two dimensions (frequency and emotional impact). Higher scores indicate more frequent teasing experiences and greater emotional impact of teasing [40].

Anthropometric measurements

The body weight (kg) and height (cm) measurements of Syrian adolescents were taken. Anthropometric measurements were conducted at the school by a member of the research team who was also working as a teacher in the region during the data collection period. Body weight was measured after at least four hours of fasting, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes, using a calibrated digital scale. Height was measured without shoes using a non-stretchable measuring tape while participants stood upright. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). The obtained BMI values were evaluated according to age and gender and divided into percentile slices. The BMI percentile evaluation was conducted based on the growth references developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) for adolescents and teenagers as follows: <5th percentile ‘Very Underweight’, 5th–<15th percentile ‘Underweight’, 15th–<85th percentile ‘Normal’, 85th–<95th percentile ‘Overweight’, and ≥ 95th percentile ‘Obese’ [41].

Procedures

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Cankiri Karatekin University Ethics Committee of Health Sciences Research (Application no: 20d463dbf8f34e02), and the study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In Türkiye, individuals under the age of 18 are legally considered minors; therefore, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of the adolescents, and assent was obtained from the participants prior to data collection. Participation in the study was voluntary and confidentiality was assured. The research data were collected in the Reyhanlı district of Hatay province, Türkiye an area where individuals benefiting from temporary accommodation centers for Syrian migrants predominantly reside. Data were collected from 600 adolescents through face-to-face interviews, and the responses were entered into an online questionnaire form developed by the researchers. The population of the study consists of Syrian migrant adolescents and teenagers aged 12–18 studying in state schools affiliated with the Ministry of National Education in the Reyhanlı district of Hatay province. The sample size of the study was determined using the Power Analysis and Sample Size (PASS) software. The minimum sample size calculated according to an effect size of 0.3 and a statistical power level of 95% was found to be 580. In this regard, it is planned to include at least 580 participants in the study. Volunteering to participate in the study, being in the age range of 12–18, not having any metabolic or psychological disorders, and not following a special diet have been determined as inclusion criteria.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 25.0 software was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics (mean±standard deviation) were applied to continuous variables, while frequency and percentage values were used for categorical variables.

In the comparison of categorical variables between groups, the Pearson Chi-Square Test was employed. For comparisons of continuous variables between two independent groups, the Independent Samples t-test was applied.

The Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to examine the relationships between the scale scores. Separate linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between psychosocial factors, socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle variables, and eating behaviors. Three independent models were constructed including (A) psychosocial variables, (B) socio-demographic variables, and (C) lifestyle and dietary variables. Categorical variables were dummy coded prior to analysis (e.g., gender: 0 = female, 1 = male) and included in the multiple linear regression models together with continuous predictors. Assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were assessed through visual inspection of histograms and P–P plots. Independence of errors was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic, with values ranging between 1.56 and 1.64, indicating no autocorrelation. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance values; all VIF values were close to 1, indicating no multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and a 95% confidence interval was adopted. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant [42].

Results

A total of 600 Syrian migrant adolescents participated in the study. The sample consisted of 47.2% females and 52.8% males, with a mean age of 15.94 ± 1.48 years. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and linear regression models were conducted to examine the relationships between perception of teasing, social appearance anxiety, eating behaviors, and body mass index.

While there is no significant difference between the father’s education levels of male and female adolescents (p = 0.865), a significant difference has been found in the mother’s education levels (p = 0.048); the mothers of female adolescents have a higher education level. Three separate regression models were conducted to examine factors associated with cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating. In Model A, social appearance anxiety was negatively associated with cognitive restraint (β=-0.175, p < 0.001), uncontrolled eating (β=-0.198, p < 0.001), and emotional eating (β=-0.181, p < 0.001). The effect of weight-focused teasing is positively associated with all three eating behaviors (p < 0.05). Model A explained 3.5% of the variance in cognitive restraint, 4.9% in uncontrolled eating, and 4.6% in emotional eating.

In Model B, adolescents whose mothers had lower educational levels showed higher levels of cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating and emotional eating (p < 0.05). Adolescents who were dissatisfied with their physical appearance had higher emotional eating scores (β= −0.109, p < 0.05). Model B explained 3.2% of the variance in cognitive restraint (R²=0.032), 3.8% in uncontrolled eating (R²=0.038), and 4.3% in emotional eating (R²=0.043).

In Model C, an increased number of main meals was associated with lower cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating scores (p < 0.01). In addition, not consuming snacks between meals was significantly associated with uncontrolled eating and emotional eating (p < 0.01). Model C explained 5.4% of the variance in cognitive restraint (R²=0.054), 6.4% in uncontrolled eating (R²=0.064), and 5.2% in emotional eating (R²=0.052) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated the eating behaviors of Syrian migrant adolescents from a holistic perspective by examining their associations with social appearance anxiety, teasing perception, and various sociodemographic factors. Overall, the findings show that social appearance concern and teasing events are strongly associated with emotional and uncontrolled eating, but cognitive restraint is associated with meal habits. Weight-related teasing was positively correlated with both BMI and emotional eating, while female adolescents showed higher levels of social appearance anxiety. These findings imply that eating habits in this group are significantly influenced by psychosocial stressors associated with appearance and peer relationships.

Psychosocial risks of migration

Migration inherently involves a range of psychosocial risks for children and adolescents [14, 35]. Since the individuals in this study are refugees rather than voluntary migrants, their psychological and environmental exposures are influenced by trauma connected to conflict, forced relocation, and assimilation into a host society [12, 43]. Particularly during adolescence, heightened sensitivity to social acceptance, physical appearance, and body image may intensify the psychosocial burden of the migration process [44].

In the present study, the mean social appearance anxiety score of migrant adolescents was found to be 39.38 ± 14.77, which is higher than those reported in non-migrant samples. For instance, Papapanou et al. [19] reported a mean SAAS score of 34.64 ± 13.60, while Özcan et al. [45] found a considerably lower score of 12.6 ± 11.6. According to recent research, peer assessment pressures and acculturation stress may cause refugee adolescents to have higher levels of social appearance anxiety [36, 46]. Furthermore, as demonstrated in this study, the literature consistently indicates that social appearance anxiety is higher among female adolescents compared to males [17, 19, 24].

Perceived discrimination and body satisfaction

Perceived discrimination has been shown to play a significant role in body satisfaction among migrant and ethnic minority groups. A study by Kim et al. [44] conducted in Korea revealed that perceived discrimination significantly reduced physical appearance satisfaction among migrant/ethnic minority adolescents. This finding supports the view that social appearance anxiety is not solely an individual concern but rather a socially shaped construct influenced by interactions, discriminatory experiences, and cultural norms. Accordingly, social appearance anxiety among migrant adolescents may not only stem from self-evaluation of physical appearance but also from perceived social judgment, fear of exclusion, and the need for belonging. In this regard, migration may increase social appearance anxiety and indirectly influence eating attitudes and body-related psychosocial processes.

Eating behaviors

In Taiwan, Lin et al. [47] reported mean scores for the subscales of the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire as 1.78 ± 0.70 for emotional eating, 2.12 ± 0.56 for uncontrolled eating, and 2.11 ± 0.53 for cognitive restraint. In contrast, the present study found comparatively higher values (2.62 ± 0.87, 2.73 ± 0.78, and 2.72 ± 0.94, respectively). The inclusion of adolescents’ refugees, who are more likely to engage in disordered eating behaviors as a result of psychosocial stressors, may be associated with this discrepancy [14, 24]. The literature indicates that having migrant parents may be associated with an increased the risk of disordered eating behaviors [48]. Appearance-based teasing, social evaluation, and cultural adaptation pressures may be associated with emotional eating in minority groups. Therefore, the relatively high emotional eating scores observed in this study may reflect patterns associated with exposure to appearance-based negative social interactions.

Social appearance anxiety and eating behaviors

The study further revealed a significant negative association between social appearance anxiety and both emotional and uncontrolled eating. This result contradicts the idea that social appearance concern and peer bullying would be positively connected to more problematic eating behaviors, implying an inverse association between these factors. This may suggest that migrant adolescents experiencing intense social appearance anxiety tend to regulate and restrict their eating behaviors consciously. As noted by Hart et al. [16], individuals seeking social acceptance may suppress their eating behaviors. Although this suppression process provides a tenable explanation, the observed pattern may not be entirely explained by it. However, such suppression may impair hunger–satiety cues and increase the risk of binge or uncontrolled eating episodes in the long term.

Other explanations must to be taken into account as well. For example, rather than the emotional or uncontrolled eating patterns typically observed in Western groups, cultural norms and family-based eating practices among Syrian adolescents may encourage more controlled or regulated eating behaviors in response to appearance-related concerns. Environmental constraints and regulated meal contexts in migrant households may also reduce opportunities for uncontrolled eating.

Moreover, participants who reported dissatisfaction with their physical appearance obtained significantly higher scores in emotional eating (β= −0.109; p < 0.05). This finding indicates that social appearance anxiety and physical appearance satisfaction do not necessarily reflect the same psychological construct. Social appearance anxiety may not solely emerge from a personal evaluation of one’s body but may be shaped by perceived social judgment, fear of rejection, and social comparison mechanisms.

Overall, this unexpected finding emphasizes the significance of taking cultural and contextual variables into account when evaluating the association between psychosocial factors and eating behaviors, as well as the possibility that frequently assumed patterns do not apply to all migrant communities.

Meal patterns and eating regulation

Eating behaviors are not merely individual choices but are associated with social environment, cultural belonging, psychosocial stress, and dietary habits [48–50]. In this study, a lower number of main meals was found to be significantly associated with cognitive restraint, uncontrolled eating, and emotional eating (p < 0.01). Likewise, skipping snacks was associated with higher uncontrolled and emotional eating tendencies (p < 0.01). These findings suggest that regular meal consumption not only is associated with physiological regulation of hunger and satiety but may also be linked to the psychological dimensions of eating behaviors.

Weight-based teasing, BMI, and social appearance anxiety

Furthermore, a positive association was identified between weight-based teasing and emotional eating. This indicates that adolescents who experience appearance-based negative social interactions may be more likely to use food consumption as a coping strategy for emotional distress. This result is consistent with the findings of Sweetingham & Waller [33] and Puhl & Suh [32], who reported associations between peer victimization and emotional eating or disordered eating.

Weight-based teasing also had a positive relationship with BMI and social appearance anxiety, implying that appearance-related social criticism may be associated with influence both body image and eating habits [51, 52]. This indicates that appearance-related social criticism may be linked to increase not only eating-related behaviors but also concerns regarding body image and weight perception. Consistent with the literature, previous studies have shown that appearance-based bullying and social exclusion are associated with body dissatisfaction and increased social appearance anxiety, particularly among adolescents with higher BMI values [53, 54]. These findings suggest that weight-based teasing may be associated with the relationship between body dissatisfaction, BMI, and social appearance anxiety.

Conclusion

The present study is among the limited research investigating the eating behaviors of Syrian migrant adolescents in relation to social appearance anxiety, teasing perception, and sociodemographic factors. The findings indicated that the eating behaviors of migrant adolescents living in Türkiye are not solely determined by physiological needs but are strongly influenced by multidimensional psychosocial factors such as social appearance anxiety, teasing perception, parental education level, and dietary habits. In particular, the positive associations observed between weight-related teasing, emotional eating, body mass index, and social appearance anxiety suggest that social pressure based on physical appearance may create psychological as well as behavioral effects. Taken together, these findings indicate that migration should not only be considered as a cultural adaptation process but also as a potential psychosocial stressor affecting body image, the need for social acceptance, and eating behaviors in adolescents. Therefore, it is essential to develop comprehensive intervention programs for migrant adolescents that strengthen body image, prevent peer victimization, promote regular meal patterns, and encourage family involvement. Future studies may consider testing a mediation model to examine whether social physique anxiety mediates the association between perceived discrimination and disordered eating behaviors among migrant adolescents.

Limitations

This study’s shortcomings include its cross-sectional design and its use of self-report scales. Furthermore, the study was restricted to adolescent Syrian refugees residing in the Reyhanlı region, which could limit the findings’ applicability to other refugee groups in Türkiye or elsewhere. The observed relationships between eating habits and social appearance anxiety cannot be directly linked to refugee status because this study lacked a non-refugee comparison group. One question was used to gauge subjective body satisfaction; using a validated multi-item scale would have improved the measure’s reliability. To determine the precise psychosocial effects of forced migration, future studies should compare adolescents who are refugees and those who are not. Furthermore, causal interpretations should be done cautiously because the cross-sectional design makes it difficult to explain the surprising inverse link between social appearance concern and problematic eating practices.

Additionally, while the regression models were statistically significant, the proportion of variance explained was relatively low. This suggests that eating behaviours are influenced by a complex interplay of psychosocial, cultural and environmental factors that were not fully captured by the present study. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should incorporate additional variables and longitudinal designs to provide a more comprehensive explanation of these relationships.
