Experiences of breastfeeding among mothers of preterm infants during their infants’ hospital stay: a qualitative systematic review protocol
Authors: Tippawan Srichalerm, Donruedee Kamkhoad, Ratchanok Phonyiam
Published: 2024

Introduction

Breastfeeding provides significant advantages for preterm infants born before 37 weeks’ gestation, supporting their growth, development, and overall well-being.1 The World Health Organization and the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund actively promote and advocate for breastfeeding as a crucial component of infant and child health.2 These health organizations also highlight the unique and optimal nutritional composition of breast milk, particularly its adaptability to meet the specific needs of preterm infants.2 Preterm infants who receive breast milk, whether through direct breastfeeding or expressed breast milk, experience substantial health benefits in terms of optimal nutrition,2 enhanced immune protection,2–4 lower risk of complications,2–4 and cognitive advancements.5 The overwhelming evidence supporting the clinical practice of feeding preterm infants with human milk underscores the importance of promoting and facilitating breastfeeding in neonatal care settings.6

Nevertheless, various barriers to breastfeeding arise due to factors that differentiate the experiences of mothers of preterm infants from those with healthy, full-term newborns. Mothers of preterm infants may encounter unique challenges when it comes to breastfeeding their infants, such as difficulties in establishing milk supply, heightened maternal stress and anxiety, exposure to a stressful environment, and their baby’s immature sucking reflexes. These challenges can contribute to earlier discontinuation of breastfeeding than recommended by infant breastfeeding guidelines.7

Certainly, maternal health, encompassing both physical and emotional well-being, is integral to the success and sustainability of breastfeeding.8 Disruptions in the normal development of the mammary organ (breast) can have significant implications for breastfeeding. Toward the end of pregnancy, the mammary organ and mammary cells undergo complex changes in preparation for milk production (lactation).9 When mammary gland development is interrupted by preterm birth, a mother’s ability to initiate and sustain breastfeeding can be impacted.10 This is because the premature transition into an active lactation state can pose challenges in achieving typical milk volume.8 In other words, due to preterm birth, mammary cells may not fully develop, potentially impacting milk production.10,11 Mothers of preterm infants may, thus, experience delays in the onset of lactation. Moreover, mothers of preterm infants experience a range of emotions, such as stress, anxiety, guilt, and self-doubt, that can significantly impact breastfeeding initiation and continuation.

Mothers may face challenges in establishing a consistent breastfeeding routine, especially if their infants require care in neonatal units,12 namely the neonatal intensive care unit or neonatal special care units. Frustration and disappointment may arise from difficulties with latching, inadequate milk supply, or the need for supplemental feedings.12,13 Mothers may experience feelings of guilt when they are unable to breastfeed their infants. The perception of not fulfilling their parental role can lead to feelings of detachment.14 Mental health conditions, including postpartum depression, can further complicate the emotional landscape, affecting a mother’s motivation and energy levels for breastfeeding.15

In addition to the mother’s health conditions, preterm infants’ health conditions can significantly influence the success of breastfeeding. These infants often encounter difficulties with breastfeeding due to their immaturity. Factors such as an underdeveloped sucking reflex; weak coordination of sucking, swallowing, and breathing; low muscle tone; and an immature digestive system can hinder breastfeeding initiation and continuation.16 Furthermore, preterm infants often experience extended hospital stays compared with full-term infants. Such a hospital environment can significantly impact breastfeeding for mothers of preterm infants. Challenges include limited access to preterm infants and the need for pumping. The separation of preterm infants from their mothers after birth may delay breastfeeding establishment and bonding.17 Access to lactation support and flexibility in breastfeeding approaches are crucial. Mothers may also require emotional support, and collaborative efforts with health care providers are essential to address individual needs and facilitate successful breastfeeding.

Breastfeeding decisions are frequently made early in pregnancy, and a mother’s attitude toward breastfeeding is a significant predictor of when and how long to breastfeed.18 Preterm birth is a sudden and unexpected event that interrupts a mother’s expectations. Mothers of preterm infants can face a variety of challenges related to breastfeeding, as well as emotional responses associated with this experience. Successfully overcoming breastfeeding challenges can lead to positive emotions, such as empowerment and joy, increasing a mother’s likelihood of being satisfied with her breastfeeding experiences. The positive experiences improve the mother’s attitude toward breastfeeding in the future, enhancing the mother’s motivation, self-efficacy, confidence, and intention to breastfeed.19 On the other hand, some mothers may face more breastfeeding difficulties, leading to a negative attitude toward breastfeeding. Such factors can influence a mother’s decision to discontinue breastfeeding prematurely.19,20

The challenges associated with mothers’ breastfeeding experiences and establishing breastfeeding in neonatal units may have a significant impact on the outcomes in terms of breastfeeding initiation and continuation for this population. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the mother’s experiences in providing breastfeeding or mother’s own milk feeding, ensuring her hospitalized infant receives only her own milk, excluding formula or donor breast milk.21 Understanding the breastfeeding experiences of mothers of preterm infants is vital for addressing the breastfeeding challenges they face, promoting optimal infant health, and fostering positive maternal-infant bonding. This knowledge will inform the development of targeted interventions and support programs to enhance the overall breastfeeding journey for this vulnerable population.

The reviewers conducted a preliminary search of PROSPERO, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and JBI Evidence Synthesis. No current or in-progress qualitative systematic reviews on the topic were identified. The objective of this review is to determine the available evidence on how mothers of preterm infants experience the initiation and continuation of breastfeeding, including mother’s own milk management, during their infants’ hospital stay.

Review question

What are mothers’ experiences of breastfeeding or mother’s own milk management, during their preterm infants’ hospital stay?

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review will encompass studies involving mothers or female parents of preterm infants, with unrestricted criteria regarding the mother’s age, gravidity, parity, singleton/multiple pregnancies (twin, triplet, or more), gender identity, marital or social status, or preterm infant’s gestational age. This review will examine mothers across various contexts in terms of their support networks, access to resources, cultural norms and attitudes, immigration status, or financial circumstances.

The mother’s physical and mental health status will be taken into consideration. Mothers who have undergone previous breast surgeries, whether for aesthetic or medical reasons, may be included if they are capable of breastfeeding or providing their own milk to their infants. Mothers facing mental health challenges, such as those dealing with the recent loss of one of their twins, may also be considered for inclusion if there are no contraindications to breastfeeding. Additionally, mothers with special needs, such as deafness, muteness, blindness, or motor or mobility challenges, may be eligible for inclusion, provided they have no medical reasons preventing them from breastfeeding or providing their own milk to their preterm infants.

Mothers with underlying health conditions requiring medication or those carrying diseases transmittable to newborns through breastfeeding (eg, hepatitis, HIV, mononucleosis, cytomegalovirus) will be excluded due to contraindications against providing their own milk to the infants. Mothers with health conditions affecting their cognitive functions, such as dementia and/or Alzheimer’s disease, may be excluded because they might not accurately recall and express their breastfeeding experiences. Furthermore, mothers’ indirect experiences of breastfeeding (eg, as retold by a sister or close friend) will be excluded from this review.

Phenomena of interest

This review will consider studies that explore the experiences of breastfeeding, also called nursing, which involves feeding a mother’s breast milk to her infant (whether directly or indirectly from the breast). It will also cover the management of mother’s own milk, including breast milk expression, storage, and delivery to an infant by mothers of preterm infants during their infants’ hospital stay.

Context

The context of interest is in a hospital unit (which could also be called a neonatal intensive care unit, special care nursery, sick newborn unit, nursery, postnatal or postpartum unit). Other units providing care for preterm infants in a hospital area will also be included.

This review will encompass all health system and settings related to mothers of preterm infants. It will include health care system characteristics, such as private, public, universally accessible (covering immigrants, refugees, and uninsured individuals), citizen-only, or mixed models (combining private and public sectors). Regulations in maternity units, such as policies on parental presence, breastfeeding restrictions, and visiting availability, will also be included. Study findings related to breastfeeding of preterm infants at discharge or home will be excluded from this review.

Types of studies

This review will consider studies that focus on qualitative data including, but not limited to, designs such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, action research, and feminist research. Qualitative data from mixed methods studies will also be included if qualitative findings can be extracted from the study. This review will not include alternative forms of evidence, such as textual evidence.

Methods

The proposed systematic review will be conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of qualitative evidence.22 The review title has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024501454).

Search strategy

The search strategy will aim to locate both published and unpublished studies. A 3-step search strategy will be utilized in this review. First, an initial limited search of PubMed, CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Embase (EBSCOhost) was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles were used to develop a full search strategy for PubMed (see Appendix I). A research librarian specializing in search strategies reviewed and applied the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guideline23 to evaluate the search strategy. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for each included database and/or information source. The reference lists of all included sources of evidence will be screened for additional studies. The initial keywords to be used are breastfeeding or nursing, mother experience or maternal view, preterm or low birth weight infant, and hospital or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay.

The databases to be searched include PubMed, Embase (EBSCOhost), and CINAHL (EBSCOhost). Additionally, sources of unpublished studies and gray literature to be searched include Mahidol Library Catalogs and Open Access Theses and Dissertations.

Due to language limitations and to reduce the risk of errors or misinterpretations, this review will only include English-language articles. We recognize this restriction may introduce language bias.24

Study selection

Following the search, all identified citations will be collated and uploaded into EndNote v20 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) and duplicates will be removed. Following a pilot test, titles and abstracts will be screened by 2 independent reviewers for assessment against the inclusion criteria for the review. Potentially relevant studies will be retrieved in full and their citation details imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI; JBI, Adelaide, Australia).25 The full text of selected citations will be assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by 2 or more independent reviewers. Reasons for exclusion of papers at full text that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be recorded and reported in the systematic review. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process will be resolved through discussion or with an additional reviewer. The results of the search and the study inclusion process will be reported in full in the final systematic review and presented in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram.26

Assessment of methodological quality

Eligible studies will be critically appraised by 2 independent reviewers for methodological quality using the standard JBI critical appraisal checklist for qualitative research.22 The assessment criteria are built into JBI SUMARI25 and use a series of criteria that can be scored as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” Reviewers will establish predetermined criteria for study eligibility. Authors of papers will be contacted a maximum of 2 times, with approximately 2 weeks between attempts, to request missing or additional data for clarification as needed. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer. The results of critical appraisal will be reported in narrative format and in a table.

Following critical appraisal, studies that do not meet a certain quality threshold will be excluded. This decision will be based on receiving a “no” response from 2 reviewers on criteria 2, 3, and 4, indicating “low” methodological quality, and on criterion 8 for inadequately capturing the participants’ voices and meanings.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted from studies included in the review by 2 independent reviewers using the standardized JBI data extraction tool from JBI SUMARI.25 The data extracted will include specific details about the populations, context, culture, geographical location, study methods, and the phenomena of interest relevant to the review objective. Findings, and their illustrations, will be extracted verbatim and assigned a level of credibility. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer. Authors of papers will be contacted a maximum of 2 times, with approximately 2 weeks between contacts, to request missing or additional data for clarification as needed.

Data synthesis

Qualitative research findings will, where possible, be pooled using JBI SUMARI and the meta-aggregation approach.27 This will involve the aggregation or synthesis of findings to generate a set of statements that represent that aggregation, through assembling the findings and categorizing these findings on the basis of similarity in meaning. These categories will then be subjected to a synthesis in order to produce a single comprehensive set of synthesized findings that can be used as a basis for evidence-based practice. Where textual pooling is not possible, the findings will be presented in narrative format. Only unequivocal and credible findings will be included in the synthesis.

Assessing confidence in the findings

The final synthesized findings will be graded according to the ConQual approach for establishing confidence in the output of qualitative research synthesis and presented in a Summary of Findings.28 The Summary of Findings includes the major elements of the review and details how the ConQual score is developed. Included in the Summary of Findings will be the title, population, phenomena of interest, and context for the specific review. Each synthesized finding from the review will then be presented, along with the type of research informing it, scores for dependability and credibility, and the overall ConQual score.

Declaration

We acknowledge that not every birthing parent identifies as a woman or as a mother. We have used the terms mother and woman in this manuscript to represent any person who gives birth to a child and breastfeeds.
