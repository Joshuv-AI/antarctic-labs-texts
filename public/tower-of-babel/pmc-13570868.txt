Elevated Plasma GDF-15 Levels are Associated with the Immune Non-Responder Phenotype in People with HIV
Authors: Aida López López, Jacobo Alonso Domínguez, Inés Martínez Barros, Alexandre Pérez González, Antonio Ocampo, Luis Morano, Noemí Martínez López de Castro, Otilia Bisbal Pardo, Cristina Díez, María del Mar Arcos Rueda, Ignacio Álvarez-Rodríguez, Irene Portilla-Tamarit, Enrique Bernal-Morell, Luis López Cortés, Ezequiel Ruiz-Mateos, Eva Poveda, CoRIS cohort

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMost people with HIV (PWH) achieve immune recovery with antiretroviral therapy (ART); however, a clinically relevant subset, known as immune non-responders (INRs), fails to restore CD4+ T-cell counts despite sustained virological suppression and shows persistent immune dysfunction. Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15), a stress-responsive cytokine associated with inflammation, aging, chronic disease, and multimorbidity, has not been characterized across distinct HIV viro-immunological phenotypes.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory cross-sectional study including 80 PWH classified as ART-naïve individuals, virally suppressed INRs, elite controllers (ECs), and ART-treated immune responders (IRs), as well as 20 HIV-negative controls. Plasma GDF-15 levels were measured by immunoassay. Associations with log-transformed GDF-15 concentrations were assessed using multivariable linear regression including age, CD4+ T-cell nadir, and INR status.ResultsINRs showed the highest plasma GDF-15 levels (median, 1143.9 pg/ml), significantly exceeding those observed in ART-naïve individuals, ECs, IRs, and HIV-negative controls (all p ≤ 0.002). GDF-15 levels correlated positively with age, time since HIV diagnosis, and ART duration, and inversely with CD4+ T-cell nadir and the CD4/CD8 ratio (all p ≤ 0.001). In adjusted analysis, INR status remained independently associated with higher log-transformed GDF-15 levels (β = 0.271, 95% CI 0.062–0.480; p = 0.012), corresponding to 31.1% higher GDF-15 concentrations relative to non-INR participants.ConclusionPlasma GDF-15 levels were higher in virally suppressed INRs than in other viro-immunological groups and remained independently associated with INR status after adjustment for selected covariates. These findings support further investigation of GDF-15 as a potential biomarker of incomplete immune recovery and residual biological stress in virally suppressed PWH. Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV care, by effectively suppressing viral replication and markedly improving life expectancy for people with HIV (PWH) [1]. Nevertheless, despite highly effective contemporary ART regimens and previous efforts to improve immune recovery through ART intensification and adjunctive immunomodulatory strategies, a clinically relevant subset of treated individuals fails to achieve adequate CD4+ T-cell restoration under sustained virological suppression [2]. This phenotype, commonly referred to as immune non-response or incomplete immune reconstitution, remains biologically incompletely understood. Persistent systemic inflammation, immune activation, and immunosenescence have been implicated as potential contributors [3, 4]. Although immune non-responders can be identified using established immunovirological criteria, the biological mechanisms underlying incomplete immune recovery remain incompletely understood. Identifying biomarkers and biological pathways associated with this phenotype may help elucidate the drivers of persistent immune dysfunction, support the development of preventive or adjunctive interventions aimed at improving immune reconstitution, and provide further insight into the pathogenesis of inflammation-driven comorbidity in HIV.

Biomarkers reflecting inflammation, immune activation, and cellular stress may help capture biological processes not fully represented by conventional immunological indices. In this context, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15) emerges as a plausible marker of inflammaging and residual biological stress. GDF-15 is a stress-responsive cytokine of the transforming growth factor-β superfamily that is minimally expressed under physiological conditions but markedly upregulated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction, tissue injury, inflammation, and activation of cellular stress-response pathways [5, 6]. Circulating GDF-15 levels increase with aging and have been associated with chronic disease burden and mortality [7], supporting its potential value as a biomarker of adverse biological aging and systemic stress. In PWH, previous work from our group has linked elevated GDF-15 levels to aging, multimorbidity, and tobacco exposure [8]. In addition, higher GDF-15 concentrations have also been associated with HIV reservoir markers [9], further supporting its relevance as a marker of biological vulnerability and persistent HIV-related stress in this population. However, GDF-15 has not been well characterized across distinct viro-immunological phenotypes of HIV infection.

Based on differences in immune recovery, viral control, and ART exposure, distinct viro-immunological phenotypes can be defined among PWH. INRs represent a clinically relevant phenotype characterized by persistently low CD4+ T-cell counts despite sustained virological suppression [10], whereas elite controllers (ECs) maintain spontaneous viral control without ART and generally preserve CD4+ T-cell counts. In contrast, ART-treated immune responders (IRs) achieve both virological suppression and immune recovery. These phenotypes provide a useful framework to explore whether GDF-15 is more closely related to persistent immune dysfunction than to HIV infection, viral control, or ART exposure alone.

Building on this rationale, we conducted an exploratory cross-sectional study to compare plasma GDF-15 levels across key viro-immunological phenotypes of HIV infection, including ART-naïve individuals, virally suppressed INRs, ECs, and ART-treated IRs, as well as HIV-negative controls. We also assessed clinical and immunological correlates of GDF-15 after adjustment for relevant covariates. We hypothesized that GDF-15 levels would differ across phenotypes and would be highest in INRs, supporting its potential relevance as a marker of biological processes associated with incomplete immune recovery in PWH.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted an exploratory cross-sectional study including 100 participants: 20 HIV-negative controls and 80 PWH classified into four predefined viro-immunological groups (n = 20 per group): (1) ART-naïve individuals, defined as people without previous exposure to ART and with detectable plasma HIV viral load at study inclusion; (2) INRs, defined as ART-treated individuals with sustained virological suppression (< 50 copies/ml) for at least 2 years and persistently low CD4+ T-cell counts (< 250 cells/µl); (3) ECs, defined as individuals maintaining undetectable plasma HIV viral load for at least 1 year in the absence of ART, none had received ART prior to sample collection; and (4) ART-treated IRs, defined as individuals receiving ART for at least 2 years, with sustained viral suppression and adequate CD4+ T-cell restoration (> 400 cells/µl). ART-treated IRs and negative controls were recruited from the Galicia Sur Health Research Institute (IISGS) Infectious Diseases Cohort, which includes PWH receiving care at Álvaro Cunqueiro Hospital (Vigo, Spain). The IISGS Biobank is a biomedical research platform authorized by the Regional Ministry of Health and registered in the National Biobank Registry (B.0000802), ensuring secure handling of biological samples and associated clinical data in accordance with national biomedical research regulations.

ART-naïve individuals were selected from CoRIS, a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort of adults with confirmed HIV infection who are naïve to ART at enrollment. CoRIS operates across 47 clinical centers in 14 Autonomous Regions in Spain and collects standardized socio-demographic, immunological, and clinical data following the HIV Cohorts Data Exchange Protocol (HICDEP). Detailed cohort methodology has been published previously [11].

The INR group was recruited from the Clinical Unit of Infectious Diseases, Clinical Microbiology, and Parasitology Unit (UCEIMP) at Virgen del Rocío University Hospital (Sevilla, Spain). Samples were stored at the Collection of Samples of People with HIV (code C330024) belonging to the Andalusian Public Health System Biobank.

ECs were recruited from the ECRIS cohort. The samples were received, processed and stored in the Spanish HIV HGM BioBank belonging to the AIDS Research Network (RIS) [12], and data were registered in the RIS cohort of HIV Controllers Study Group (ECRIS) and CoRIS.

Participants were selected from available biobank samples and cohort repositories according to the predefined viro-immunological criteria for each study group. Selection was based on eligibility criteria and sample availability. No additional exclusion criteria were applied beyond the availability of sufficient plasma volume and complete essential clinical data for analysis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable regulatory requirements. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Galicia (CEIm-G) (Ref: 2022/400). The CoRIS and ECRIS cohorts were approved by the Research Ethic Committee of the Gregorio Marañón Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Biobank samples and associated data were processed following standardized governance procedures and in full compliance with national legislation on biomedical research and personal data protection. All datasets were pseudonymized before analysis, ensuring the protection of participant confidentiality and data integrity.

Data Collection and Definitions

Demographic, clinical, and pharmacological data were retrieved from electronic medical records and managed via REDCap (hosted at IISGS Biobank). For participants included from the CoRIS and ECRIS cohorts, data were obtained from the corresponding cohort databases. Collected variables included sex, age, country of origin, reported HIV acquisition route, time since HIV diagnosis and ART, CD4+ T-cell counts at study inclusion, multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more chronic comorbidities, CD4/CD8 ratio and CD4+ nadir.

Variable selection for multivariable analysis was based on clinical relevance and univariable associations with GDF-15. Time since HIV diagnosis and time on ART were not included in the final model due to conceptual overlap with age and viro-immunological phenotype, as well as collinearity considerations.

All data were harmonized across cohorts prior to analysis to ensure consistency in variable definitions and reduce inter-cohort variability.

Biomarker Quantification

Plasma concentrations of GDF-15 were quantified using the Simplex ProcartaPlex Human GDF-15 Panel (EPX010-12258–901; Thermo Fisher Scientific). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Plasma samples were stored at − 80 °C until the analysis and were thawed only once prior to biomarker quantification. Measurements were conducted in duplicate, and the mean value was used for analysis. Data points with a coefficient of variation exceeding 20% were excluded to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results.

Data Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple pairwise comparisons when appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with plasma GDF-15 concentrations. Because plasma GDF-15 concentrations showed a skewed distribution, values were natural log-transformed prior to regression analysis to improve residual normality and homoscedasticity. Multicollinearity among variables included in the regression model was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, and a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and Demographic Characteristics of the Study Participants

A total of 100 participants were included: 80 PWH (20 naïve, 20 ART-treated, 20 INRs, and 20 ECs) and 20 HIV-negative controls. Median age differed significantly across groups. INRs showed higher median age values (median 55.9 years, IQR 52.7–60.2) than ART-naïve individuals (median 34.3 years, IQR 30.1–40.4; p < 0.001) and HIV-negative controls (median 39.8 years, IQR 34.0–42.8; p < 0.001). ART-treated IRs (median 46.7 years, IQR 40.5–52.5; p = 0.014), and ECs (median 44.6 years, IQR 40.9–52.9; p = 0.018) were also older than naïve individuals. No significant age differences were observed between ECs and INRs. Overall, women accounted for 34.0% of the study population. Plasma GDF-15 Concentrations Across Study Groups

Median plasma GDF-15 concentrations differed substantially across viro-immunological phenotypes (Table 2 and Fig. 1). INRs exhibited significantly higher GDF-15 levels (1143.9 pg/ml, IQR 890.1–1544.9), exceeding those observed in naïve (297.3 pg/ml, IQR 235.8–455.9; p < 0.001), HIV-negative controls (380.8 pg/ml, IQR 355.4–516.9; p = 0.001), ART-treated IRs (504.8 pg/ml, IQR 298.6–611.7; p = 0.002), and ECs (416.3 pg/ml, IQR 281.9–660.1; p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed among naïve, ART-treated IRs, ECs, and HIV-negative controls after Bonferroni correction.

Correlations of GDF-15 with Clinical and Immunological Variables

Plasma GDF-15 levels showed moderate positive correlations with age (ρ = 0.401, p < 0.001), time since HIV diagnosis (ρ = 0.369, p = 0.001), and baseline viral load (ρ = 0.330, p = 0.019), as well as a strong positive correlation with time on ART (ρ = 0.519, p < 0.001). In contrast, GDF-15 was moderately negatively correlated with CD4+ counts at diagnosis (ρ = – 0.341, p = 0.005), CD4+ counts at sample collection (ρ = – 0.411, p < 0.001) and CD4/CD8 ratio (ρ = – 0.421, p < 0.001), and strongly negatively correlated with CD4+ nadir (ρ = – 0.547, p < 0.001).

Multivariable Analysis of Predictors of GDF-15 Levels

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to assess whether the association between the INR phenotype and plasma GDF-15 levels was independent of age and CD4+ nadir. INR status remained independently associated with higher log-transformed GDF-15 levels (β = 0.271, 95% CI 0.062–0.480; p = 0.012). This corresponded to 31.1% higher GDF-15 concentrations in INRs compared with non-INRs after adjustment for covariates. Neither age nor CD4+ nadir showed significant associations with GDF-15 levels (Table 3). No relevant multicollinearity was identified (all Variance Inflation Factors values < 2.1).

Discussion

In this exploratory cross-sectional analysis including well-defined viro-immunological phenotypes and HIV-negative controls, virally suppressed INRs showed the highest plasma GDF-15 levels. Importantly, INR status remained independently associated with higher GDF-15 concentrations, after adjustment for age and CD4+ T-cell nadir. These findings suggest that elevated GDF-15 is not merely a marker of chronological aging or low historically CD4+ T-cell counts but may capture residual biological stress linked to incomplete immune recovery in treated HIV infection. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare plasma GDF-15 levels across distinct HIV viro-immunological phenotypes, including INRs, ART-treated IRs, ECs, ART-naïve individuals, and HIV-negative controls.

GDF-15 correlated positively with age, duration of HIV infection, and time on ART, and inversely with indices of immune restoration, including CD4+ nadir and CD4/CD8 ratio. This profile is consistent with the role of GDF-15 as a stress-responsive cytokine and aligns with current concepts of INR pathophysiology, in which persistent immune activation, immunosenescence, impaired immune restoration, and mitochondrial or bioenergetic dysfunction may coexist despite virological suppression. Although these upstream biological processes were not directly measured, the marked elevation of GDF-15 in INRs supports the hypothesis that this biomarker may reflect a cumulative burden of cellular stress and immune dysfunction [5, 6].

The inclusion of contrasting viro-immunological groups strengthens the interpretation of these findings. ECs and ART-treated IRs showed GDF-15 concentrations comparable to those observed in HIV-negative controls, whereas INRs displayed a markedly different profile. This suggests that elevated levels of GDF-15 are more closely related to persistent immune dysfunction than to HIV infection, viral control, or ART exposure alone. The comparison with ECs is particularly informative, as these individuals maintained spontaneous viral control without ART and generally preserve CD4+ T-cell counts, whereas IRs represent successful immune restoration under ART.

These results extend our previous evidence supporting the relevance of GDF-15 in PWH. We recently reported that elevated plasma levels of GDF-15 were associated with aging, multimorbidity, and tobacco exposure in PWH, suggesting that this biomarker may capture biological vulnerability beyond conventional clinical markers [8]. Although the INR group was older than the other study groups, age was included in the multivariable regression model and the association between GDF-15 levels and the INR phenotype remained statistically significant. Nevertheless, given the well-established relationship between GDF-15 and biological aging, the contribution of age to the observed differences cannot be completely excluded and should be further explored in larger, age-matched studies. Recent evidence has also linked plasma GDF-15 levels to HIV reservoir markers in ART-treated PWH, independently of classical inflammatory markers, supporting a potential connection between viral persistence, mitochondrial stress, and comorbidity risk in treated HIV infection [9]. Together with our findings, these data suggest that GDF-15 may reflect a broader biological stress signature in PWH, encompassing immune dysfunction, HIV-related persistence, and comorbidity-associated pathways.

Importantly, a recent prospective study has shown that the INR phenotype is associated with a markedly increased long-term risk of both AIDS-defining and non-AIDS-defining diseases, highlighting that conventional CD4+ T-cell monitoring alone may not fully capture the clinical vulnerability of this subgroup [13]. Within this context, our findings support the hypothesis that GDF-15 may reflect the cumulative burden of biological processes underlying the INR phenotype and reinforce its potential as a candidate biomarker for improving the biological characterization of INR (Fig. 2). Although the present cross-sectional study was not designed to evaluate the prognostic value of GDF-15, our results provide a rationale for future longitudinal studies assessing whether GDF-15 may contribute to risk assessment beyond conventional immunological markers.

This study has several strengths, including clinically relevant and well-defined HIV viro-immunological phenotypes, HIV-negative controls, standardized sample handling, duplicate GDF-15 measurements, and multivariable adjustment for key covariates. The stringent INR definition strengthened phenotypic specificity by identifying participants with marked incomplete immune recovery, although this may limit generalizability to broader populations with less severe immune non-response. Limitations of this study include the cross-sectional design, limiting the assessment of temporal changes in GDF-15 levels and causal relationships with immune recovery, modest sample size, and limited ability to adjust for multiple covariates. Although predefined viro-immunological criteria were applied consistently across groups, the possibility of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. The sample size limited adequately powered analyses of individual comorbid conditions, and therefore multimorbidity rather than disease-specific variables was used in the multivariable models. Ethnicity data were not available and therefore could not be considered in the analyses. In addition, information on cytomegalovirus serostatus or viremia was also unavailable, and its potential contribution to immune activation and GDF-15 variability cannot be excluded. In addition, participants were recruited from different cohorts and clinical centers, and residual confounding or pre-analytical differences cannot be fully excluded. Direct measurements of inflammatory, metabolic, mitochondrial, aging-related, and HIV reservoir markers were not available. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, prospectively followed cohorts and investigate whether GDF-15 may serve as a dynamic marker of immune recovery over time. Further research should also explore the biological mechanisms underlying the association between GDF-15 and the INR phenotype.

Conclusion

Plasma GDF-15 levels were markedly elevated in virally suppressed INRs compared with other HIV viro-immunological phenotypes and HIV-negative controls, and INR status remained independently associated with higher GDF-15 concentrations after adjustment for selected covariates. These findings support further investigation of GDF-15 as a potential biomarker of incomplete immune recovery and residual biological stress in treated HIV infection. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate its prognostic value and to clarify the biological pathways linking GDF-15, immune dysfunction, HIV persistence, and comorbidity risk in PWH.
