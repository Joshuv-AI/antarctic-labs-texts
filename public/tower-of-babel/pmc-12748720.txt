Ferroptosis mediated by ferritinophagy is involved in liver injury caused by sepsis
Authors: Haidan Zhang, Hongyao Li, Shixian Liu, Jiahui Zheng, Peiwu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Ferritinophagy is a type of selective autophagy mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), and its overactivation can promote ferroptosis. However, the role of ferritinophagy in sepsis-induced liver injury needs to be further explored. The aim of this study was to investigate the role of ferritinophagy in liver injury caused by sepsis through the regulation of ferroptosis and its underlying mechanisms. Methods The sepsis rat model was established by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). In the CLP + Fer-1 and CLP + 3-MA groups, Fer-1 (10 mg/kg) and 3-MA (20 mg/kg) were intraperitoneally injected at 1 h and 2 h before surgery, respectively. The levels of the serum inflammatory factors interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were detected. The levels of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured. The pathological alterations in liver tissue and mitochondrial structure were examined. The levels of ferroptosis-related markers, including ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH), prostaglandin endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2), and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), were quantified. The expression levels of nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), autophagy-related protein p62/SQSTM1 (p62), and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B (LC3B) in liver tissue were detected. Result (1) Ferroptosis was activated in the hepatocytes of septic rats induced by CLP. (2) In sepsis, the expression of NCOA4 in hepatocytes increased, leading to the degradation of intracellular ferritin through ferritinophagy, an increase in intracellular Fe²⁺ concentration, and the induction of ferroptosis in hepatocytes. Conclusion Ferritinophagy is involved in sepsis-induced liver injury, and inhibiting ferritinophagy can alleviate hepatocyte ferroptosis and mitigate sepsis-induced liver injury. Introduction

In Sepsis 3.0, sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection1,2, which is one of the major health challenges worldwide3–5. The liver is the most important metabolic and immune organ in the human body and is one of the most commonly affected organs in sepsis. It is essential for pathogen ingestion and clearance in sepsis6 due to its roles in immune defense and metabolic regulation7,8. Liver injury caused by sepsis is considered an independent risk factor for early mortality in patients; once sepsis patients develop liver injury, their mortality rate is significantly higher than that of patients with other types of organ damage8,9. Therefore, the effective prevention and treatment of septic liver injury are of great importance. The pathogenesis of septic liver injury is complex and may be closely related to pathogen infection, elevated endotoxin levels, excessive release of inflammatory factors, oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation damage, dysfunction of liver tissue microcirculation, and metabolic disorders10. However, there are currently no targeted interventions, and progress in sepsis treatment is still limited to antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and organ support. Therefore, further clarification of the pathogenesis of sepsis and identification of potential therapeutic targets are urgently needed.

Iron is a crucial element for cellular proliferation and growth. Additionally, it serves as a vital cofactor for metabolic enzymes and is intricately involved in numerous biological processes, such as erythrocyte maturation, oxygen transport, neurotransmitter transmission, cell division, and energy production11. Iron is primarily stored in the liver as ferritin, along with its presence in hemoglobin. When iron levels are sufficient, ferritin synthesis increases. In situations where cells are deficient in iron or have increased iron requirements, ferritin undergoes degradation through ferritinophagy, releasing iron ions from ferritin stores and reducing them to ferrous ions12,13. Ferritinophagy was first defined in 2014 as a selective autophagy mechanism that regulates cellular iron content by specifically mediating the transport of ferritin to the lysosome and releasing free iron through degradation14–16. The ferritinophagy marker protein NCOA4 was shown to be expressed at relatively high levels in H9c2 cells from mice with sepsis induced by lipopolysaccharide, and ferroptosis was activated, accompanied by obvious lipid peroxidation, revealing that ferritinophagy is one of the important mechanisms causing heart damage following sepsis. Therefore, targeted intervention against ferritinophagy to protect cardiomyocytes from injury may represent a novel avenue for future sepsis-induced myocardial protection. However, the literature on targeting ferritinophagy to prevent organ damage in sepsis is limited, necessitating further comprehensive exploration17.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. At the molecular level, the accumulation of iron leads to the production of free radicals and lipid peroxides, as well as a reduction in GPX4 activity, resulting in an imbalance in oxidation–reduction homeostasis and severe damage to the cell membrane, ultimately culminating in ferroptosis18. Professor Tang proposed that gaining insight into the immunological characteristics and mechanisms of ferroptosis not only elucidates the crosstalk between cell death and immunity but also has the potential to pave the way for new treatments for immune-pathological diseases19. Research suggests that ferritinophagy, an upstream regulatory pathway of ferroptosis, can influence iron release and subsequent ferroptosis by modulating the levels of the autophagy-related proteins NCOA4 and ferritin20.

Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that sepsis might trigger the up-regulation of hepatocyte ferritinophagy and that inhibiting the excessive activation of hepatocyte ferritinophagy to reduce ferroptosis could alleviate hepatocyte injury. The present study aimed to (1) confirm the involvement of ferroptosis in sepsis-induced liver injury; (2) investigate whether autophagy inhibitors can reduce iron overload in hepatocytes during sepsis by inhibiting ferritinophagy, down-regulate hepatocyte death, and alleviate hepatocyte injury, thereby further clarifying the mechanism of ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis in sepsis-induced liver injury. This study aims to provide a theoretical reference and experimental support for the clinical prevention and treatment of sepsis-induced liver injury in the future.

Materials and methods

Animals and models

Male SD rats (250 g, 50 days old), SPF grade, were provided by the Experimental Animal Center of Lanzhou University (license number: SCXK (Gan)2018-0002). The experimental animals were housed in the SPF-level laboratory at the Lanzhou University Experimental Animal Center at a temperature of 22 °C and a relative humidity of 45–55% for one week for acclimatization. During this period, the rats had free access to food and water. The animal experimenters received professional training. All animal experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Experimental Animal Management Regulations and were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (No. D2024-061). We followed the guidelines of the ARRIVE report for our in vivo studies (https://arriveguidelines.org/arrive-guidelines/experimental-animals), and the method of euthanasia was chosen according to the “Euthanasia Guidelines” from the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) (https://research.umn.edu/units/rar/guidelines/euthanasia). The experimental procedures ensured that the animals were not subjected to unnecessary suffering. The method of euthanasia complied with the requirements of the “2020 Edition of the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.”

A rat model of sepsis was established using the CLP method (Fig. 1), which is the gold standard model for studying sepsis21–24. The control group of rats underwent a sham operation, in which the abdomen was opened, exposed for 5 min, and then closed layer by layer without intestinal puncture or ligation25. The CLP, CLP + Fer-1, and CLP + 3-MA groups were created using the CLP method to establish a septic rat model24. The main steps were as follows: the rats were fasted for 12 h prior to surgery and allowed free access to water. The rats were anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital sodium via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.2 mL/10 g body weight25–27. After successful anesthesia, the rats were placed supine on the operating table, the limbs were fixed, the skin was prepared and sterilized, and a sterile drape was applied. A 2 cm longitudinal incision was made along the midline of the abdomen. The skin and muscle layers were dissected layer by layer to open the abdominal cavity and expose the cecum. The mesentery adjacent to the cecum was carefully removed, and a 3 − 0 sterile suture was used to ligate the ileum from the ileocecal valve to the midpoint of the blind end. An 18G sterile needle was used to puncture the intestine, and a small amount of content was gently squeezed out to ensure a patent perforation. The blind end of the ileum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the abdominal wall was sutured in layers. The incision was disinfected with iodine. All rats were given a subcutaneous injection of 0.9% NaCl (50 mL/kg) for supportive treatment after surgery. In the CLP + Fer-1 group, rats were injected intraperitoneally with Fer-1 (10 mg/kg) 1 h before surgery; in the CLP + 3-MA group, rats were injected intraperitoneally with 3-MA (20 mg/kg) 2 h before surgery (Fig. 1). After surgery, the rats were allowed to awaken from anesthesia and had free access to water. They were observed until 24 h post-operation and then euthanized by overdose anesthesia. Euthanasia was performed via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (100 mg/kg) to induce deep anesthesia, which minimized animal pain by rendering them unconscious. Blood and liver tissues were collected.

Experimental protocol of the animal model. A rat model of sepsis was established using the CLP method. Fer-1 and 3-MA were intraperitoneally injected into the rats 1 h and 2 h before the operation, followed by cecum ligation and puncture. Samples were collected 24 h later.

After CLP, the rats gradually exhibited biological behavioral changes, such as refusal to eat, reduced activity, piloerection, increased ocular secretions, and diarrhea. Upon opening the abdominal cavity for sample collection, we observed intestinal distension and discoloration, hemorrhagic and necrotic cecal walls, swollen and blood-stained livers, and a large amount of bloody, foul-smelling ascites.

Inflammatory factors and markers of liver injury in serum

After the blood samples were collected, they were left for 30 min and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, after which the upper layer of serum was collected for measurement. Serum levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were detected using an ELISA kit (Ruixinbio, Mlbiom, China). Serum ALT and AST levels were determined with a detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). The absorbance of the samples was read at 510 nm.

Histological analysis (HE staining)

Liver tissue was isolated and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning, the tissue was stained with hematoxylin and eosin and sealed with resin. Pathological changes in hepatocytes in each group, including cellular morphology and inflammatory infiltration, were observed under an optical microscope.

MDA and GSH contents in liver tissue

The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) in liver tissue lysates were quantified using a commercially available kit (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transmission electron microscopy

A Petri dish equipped with electron microscope fixating solution was prepared before sampling, and a small volume of liver tissue block was removed in vitro and immediately placed into the Petri dish. A scalpel was used to cut cuboid pieces with a volume of approximately 1 mm3 in the fixative solution of the Petri dish, and the sampling process was controlled within 1–3 min. The liver tissues were subjected to fixation, permeation, dehydration, and embedding overnight. The samples were examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan), and the extent of mitochondrial ultrastructural damage was assessed using the Flameng method28.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Paraffin sections of liver tissue were dewaxed in water and incubated with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 5–10 min to eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed after the samples were immersed in PBS. The samples were blocked with 5–10% goat serum, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, the serum was discarded, and the samples were incubated at 4 °C with primary antibodies overnight. After rinsing with PBS, the corresponding fluorescent or biotin-labeled secondary antibodies were incubated with the liver tissue, followed by chromogenic development, hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and mounting. The expression levels of the target proteins were observed and analyzed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Liver tissue sections were processed through dewaxing and dehydration, then immunostained with antibodies against GPX4, FTH1, ACSL4, and LAMP2, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM880, Germany) and an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX53, Japan). Analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ software.

Total RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR analysis

The Trizol (Beyotime, China) method was used to extract RNA from liver tissue. The purity of the extracted RNA was assessed using a BioTek analyzer (Bio-Rad, USA). Then, the reverse transcription reaction system was prepared, gently mixed, and centrifuged, followed by quantitative real-time PCR performed on a PCR instrument (Eastwin, China). The samples were subsequently subjected to PCR amplification, which was completed on a fluorescence quantitative PCR machine (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative quantification was performed using β-actin as an internal reference. The primers were synthesized by Wuhan Service Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in China. Western blot

First, liver tissue was homogenized, and then a specific proportion of lysis buffer and proteinase inhibitors was added. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). Each protein sample (40 µg) was subjected to 10% SDS–PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA) and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane (0.45 μm, Merck Millipore Ltd., USA). The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were analysed with SPSS 26.0 software and visualized with GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate survival rates. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the differences between multiple groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Welch correction was applied when necessary. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Ferroptosis is involved in CLP-induced liver injury in septic rats

To evaluate the induction of ferroptosis, we established a model of sepsis-induced liver injury using the CLP technique and applied interventions with ferroptosis inhibitors. We found that the level of Fe²⁺ in the liver tissue of rats with sepsis-induced liver injury was significantly increased (Fig. 2A). In addition, the mRNA and protein expression levels of the ferroptosis marker PTGS2 were significantly elevated in the liver tissue of septic rats (Fig. 2B, C). ACSL4 is an important isoenzyme involved in the metabolism of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). The level of ACSL4 was significantly increased in the hepatocytes of rats with sepsis-induced liver injury (Fig. 2D), as were the MDA levels (Fig. 2E). In contrast, the specific activity and mRNA and protein expression of the antioxidant enzymes GPX4 and GSH were significantly reduced (Fig. 2F–I). Similarly, mitochondrial volume reduction, decreased mitochondrial membrane density, loss of mitochondrial cristae, and mitochondrial outer membrane rupture were observed in CLP-treated rat hepatocytes (Fig. 2J). We found that, in addition to the Fe²⁺ level within the liver tissue, all of the above results could be modulated by ferrostatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor. We hypothesize that the reason for this may be that Fer-1 primarily exerts its anti-ferroptotic function by counteracting lipid peroxidation rather than through iron ion chelation.

Ferroptosis mediated by ferritinophagy is involved in sepsis-induced liver injury in rats

Previous studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis can be mediated by ferritinophagy, a process that promotes the degradation of intracellular ferritin and the subsequent release of free iron, ultimately triggering cellular ferroptosis under conditions of iron overload. To further investigate the role of ferritinophagy in liver injury caused by sepsis, we treated rats with 3-MA, an autophagy inhibitor, for 2 h before modeling and measured the level of ferritin expression in liver tissue. The results (Fig. 3A) revealed that the expression level of FTH1, the heavy chain of ferritin in liver tissue, was lower in rats with septic liver injury induced by CLP but higher in rats treated with 3-MA. The level of ferrous ions in the liver tissues of the CLP group increased, whereas that in the liver tissues of the 3-MA pretreatment group decreased (Fig. 3B).

In addition, 3-MA pretreatment inhibited the expression of PTGS2 (Fig. 3 C, D), decreased the level of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3E, F), increased the level of GSH in the liver tissues of septic rats, and increased the relative activity and expression level of GPX4 (Fig. 3G–I).

Inhibition of ferroptosis attenuates liver injury in septic rats

We explored the role of ferroptosis inhibition in alleviating liver function damage caused by sepsis in rats. The results suggest that inhibiting ferroptosis can improve the 7-day survival rate of CLP-induced septic rats (Fig. 4A), alleviate inflammatory responses (Fig. 4D, E), and exert a protective effect on the liver. These findings were confirmed by histopathological analysis of liver tissue, which revealed decreased levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 4D, E) and reduced levels of ALT and AST in the serum (Fig. 4B, C). As shown in Fig. 4F, CLP-induced septic rats exhibited significant hepatocyte damage, including inflammatory infiltration, disordered cell arrangement, hepatocyte swelling, and cell necrosis, all of which were alleviated by pretreatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 and the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA.

Discussion

We investigated whether ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis was involved in sepsis-induced liver injury and showed that inhibition of ferritinophagy attenuated sepsis-induced liver injury by reducing iron overload and down-regulating ferroptosis in hepatocytes (Fig. 6). The key findings of this study were as follows: (1) Ferroptosis was activated in hepatocytes of septic rats induced by CLP, and inhibiting ferroptosis could reduce the level of lipid peroxidation in hepatocytes, alleviate mitochondrial damage, and simultaneously improve liver function in septic rats. (2) In sepsis, the expression of NCOA4 in hepatocytes increased, intracellular ferritin was degraded via ferritinophagy, the intracellular Fe²⁺ concentration rose, and ferroptosis in hepatocytes was induced. Based on the results of this study, we hypothesize that specific inhibition of ferritinophagy may become a potential therapeutic target for sepsis-induced liver injury.

Regulated cell death (RCD) has recently become a hot topic because of its precise response mechanisms, regulatory targets and close relationship with the pathophysiological mechanisms of sepsis29. RCD is involved in various processes of sepsis pathophysiology, including the activation of signalling pathways, the release of inflammatory factors, and the body’s self-regulation and repair mechanisms19. During sepsis, excessive inflammation, immunosuppression, and cell death create a vicious cycle that causes irreversible damage to the body30. Another notable feature of RCD is the massive release of molecules such as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which subsequently trigger strong inflammatory and immune responses31,32. Additionally, some scholars describe sepsis as a maladaptive starvation response, emphasizing that the body produces high-energy metabolites such as lactic acid and free fatty acids in response to a profound state of stress or starvation33.

During sepsis, the liver, which has dual metabolic and immune functions, becomes a key target organ. Liver injury markers, such as elevated ALT and AST, are significantly associated with mortality in patients with sepsis34. Hepatocyte death is the core mechanism underlying the progression of liver injury in sepsis, as both dead and dying cells release DAMPs and cellular debris, which can directly or indirectly trigger liver inflammation and further damage hepatocytes35,36. Apoptosis is a carefully regulated form of programmed cell death. Studies have shown that in the CLP-induced sepsis model, the levels of AST and ALT are reduced by modulating the level of apoptosis, thereby alleviating pathological damage to hepatocytes37. Necroptosis is a form of programmed cell death discovered in recent years, featuring characteristics of both necrosis and apoptosis. Some studies have shown that necroptosis may be involved in sepsis-related liver injury38. In addition, in the CLP-induced sepsis model, elevated levels of pyroptosis markers were detected. Pyroptosis is a programmed, proinflammatory mode of cell death characterized mainly by cell membrane rupture, the release of cellular contents, and the triggering of a strong inflammatory response. Studies have shown that hepatocyte injury can be alleviated by inhibiting pyroptosis39. Autophagy can contribute to tissue damage during sepsis by coordinating macrophage polarization, inflammasome activation, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines. During sepsis, dysregulation of autophagy results in hepatocyte injury40. In conclusion, considering the limitations of traditional treatment methods, an increasing number of studies have recently focused on exploring the mechanisms of ferroptosis and the regulatory roles of apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and other forms of cell death36–38.

Ferroptosis is a form of RCD characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, with morphological features including mitochondrial condensation, reduction or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae, and rupture of the mitochondrial outer membrane41. Previous studies have mainly focused on exploring the possible mechanisms of ferroptosis involvement in infection. Research has indicated that in viral infections, the intestinal uptake of iron and the increase in free iron in cells can induce ferroptosis42. Tissue damage caused by bacterial infections is also associated with the activation of ferroptosis; for example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Mycobacterium tuberculosis can cause lung tissue damage by inducing lipid peroxidation and triggering ferroptosis43,44. Because bacteria need iron to reproduce, iron overload may be a “feast” for bacteria, which in turn can provide substrates for lipid peroxidation, such as fatty acids and reactive oxygen species (ROS), thus creating a cycle that exacerbates infection and may ultimately lead to sepsis and multiorgan failure45. In summary, ferroptosis contributes to the progression of infection by providing raw materials for bacterial proliferation, impairing immune function, and potentially inducing autoimmune disorders, thereby leading to immune dysfunction—a key factor in the development of sepsis.

The role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of liver diseases such as liver injury, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma has been extensively studied, and the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis in liver diseases has also been confirmed46. Although the role of ferroptosis in liver injury caused by sepsis has been confirmed47, its exact mechanism needs to be further clarified. As the most important rate-limiting enzyme in arachidonic acid metabolism, PTGS2 is closely associated with inflammation and ferroptosis. In studies on myocardial injury in sepsis, Li et al. reported that PTGS2 levels were elevated, suggesting ferroptosis activation in cardiomyocytes. Fer-1 pretreatment reduced PTGS2 levels and lipid ROS in cardiomyocytes in vivo, demonstrating that inhibition of ferroptosis can alleviate inflammation and dysfunction in cardiomyocytes during sepsis17. Our research revealed that ferroptosis occurred in the hepatocytes of rats with sepsis induced by CLP and that inhibiting ferroptosis could reduce lipid peroxidation levels in hepatocytes, alleviate mitochondrial damage, and simultaneously exert anti-inflammatory and liver-protective effects in these rats. Our experimental results are consistent with the conclusions of previous researchers, such as He47 and Wang et al.25, further indicating that ferroptosis is involved in sepsis-induced liver injury.

In addition, our research further explored the changes in the levels of markers related to iron metabolism disorders, glutathione metabolism disorders, and lipid peroxidation in the hepatocytes of septic rats undergoing ferroptosis. The accumulation of intracellular ferrous ions is a hallmark of ferroptosis. Consistent with the findings of Wang et al.25, we detected elevated levels of ferrous ions in the hepatocytes of septic rats. GSH is an important intracellular antioxidant that maintains redox balance. Cells synthesize GSH by importing cystine via the Xc- system, which has antioxidant functions, and then use GSH to assist GPX4 in reducing peroxides. GPX4 is an important member of the selenoprotein family. Its primary function is to reduce peroxides (ROOH) to their corresponding alcohols (R-OH), thereby exerting antioxidant effects. Studies on renal failure have shown that GPX4 knockout mice develop renal failure due to the loss of GPX4’s inhibitory effect on ferroptosis48. Our research revealed that the levels of GSH and GPX4 expression and activity in septic rat cells were significantly reduced, and that these effects could be reversed by treatment with Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, suggesting a decrease in the antioxidant capacity of hepatocytes. ACSL4 can determine the sensitivity of cells to ferroptosis by promoting the incorporation of PUFAs into membrane phospholipids and driving lipid peroxidation. Studies have shown that when the expression of ACSL4 is decreased in gastric cancer, the level of the lipid peroxide MDA decreases, and ferroptosis resistance increases49. In related studies on acute liver injury, rociletinib was shown to inhibit the enzyme activity of ACSL through covalent modification and to downregulate ferroptosis, thereby alleviating acute liver injury50. Consistent with previous studies, we detected increased ACSL4 expression and MDA content in hepatocytes from septic rats, demonstrating the regulatory role of ACSL4 in ferroptosis-induced liver injury in sepsis. This suggests that future research on sepsis-induced liver injury could further focus on targeting ACSL4 to regulate ferroptosis. Targeted inhibition of ferroptosis may be a promising approach for treating liver injury caused by sepsis. In conclusion, delving into the mechanisms by which ferroptosis is regulated and identifying targeted methods to inhibit ferroptosis may offer promising therapeutic strategies for sepsis-induced liver injury.

The concept of ferritinophagy, first proposed by Mancias et al. in 2014, involves selective autophagy mediated by NCOA451. Ferritinophagy is mediated by NCOA4 as a cargo receptor, transporting ferritin to autophagosomes and promoting ferritin-targeted degradation by lysosomes to release iron ions52. Ferritinophagy occurs in two stages: the formation of the NCOA4-ferritin complex and the degradation of ferritin. Moderate ferritinophagy is beneficial for cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation and can help maintain the body’s metabolism. When ferritinophagy is excessively activated, it releases large amounts of free iron, leading to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, and may even contribute to disease development53. In our study, the expression level of NCOA4 in the hepatocytes of septic rats was significantly increased, ferritinophagy was activated, and a significant increase in the expression of ferroptosis markers was detected, suggesting that ferritinophagy may be an upstream regulatory mechanism of ferroptosis in septic liver injury. Through 3-MA intervention, ferroptosis could be downregulated by inhibiting ferritinophagy. Notably, 3-MA had no significant effect on the expression level of NCOA4. Therefore, we speculate that 3-MA inhibits ferritinophagy by blocking the initial stage of autophagosome formation, reducing the conversion of LC3B-I to LC3B-II and promoting the accumulation of p62, thereby impeding autophagic flux. Thus, ferritinophagy is involved in the maintenance of iron homeostasis both within the cell and throughout the body53,54.

Approximately 300 to 1,000 milligrams of iron in the human body are estimated to be stored in the form of ferritin in locations such as the liver, spleen, and bone marrow, with the highest concentration in the liver55; therefore, ferritin is considered an indicator that directly reflects the body’s iron stores. The ferritin complex is a hollow iron-storage protein composed of the ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and the ferritin light chain (FTL), capable of storing up to 4,500–5,000 iron atoms, and forms a nanocage-like multiprotein structure. FTH, with a molecular weight of 21 kDa, has 178 amino acids and contains an iron-binding site within its α-helix that exhibits ferroxidase activity, converting Fe²⁺ into Fe³⁺. Since the isoelectric point of iron-containing proteins with FTH1 subunits is slightly acidic, the FTH1 subunit is more likely to bind and release iron than the FTL subunit56. The FTH1 subunit can specifically bind to the C-terminal domain of NCOA4 to form the NCOA4–ferritin complex, which represents the initial step in the ferritinophagy process. Zhang et al. reported that overexpression of Yes-associated protein 1 disrupts the NCOA4–FTH1 interaction and downregulates ferritinophagy, thereby alleviating lung injury in sepsis57. Our research revealed that in the hepatocytes of septic rats, the expression level of FTH1 heavy chains decreased, indicating an increase in degradation. The colocalization of the NCOA4-FTH1 complex with lysosomes was increased, suggesting that ferritinophagy was enhanced and that 3-MA intervention reversed these phenomena. Consistent with previous studies, the NCOA4-ferritin complex can bind to the autophagy-related protein microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3), leading to the sequestration of the complex within the autophagosome. The autophagosome then fuses with the lysosome to promote the degradation of ferritin and the release of iron, thereby completing the ferritinophagy process56,58.

Ferritinophagy is an upstream regulatory mechanism for ferroptosis59, and excessive activation of ferritinophagy can promote ferroptosis. The iron released from ferritin during autophagy not only reacts with membrane phospholipids to generate lipid peroxides, but also generates large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the Fenton reaction, causing severe cell damage and inducing ferroptosis60,61. In other disease-related studies, silencing the key ferritinophagy protein-encoding gene NCOA4 through different means has been shown to inhibit ferroptosis and increase cellular sensitivity62, which is in accordance with our results. Consistent with our current study, in sepsis, the expression of NCOA4 in hepatocytes increases, intracellular ferritin is degraded via ferritinophagy, the intracellular Fe²⁺ concentration rises, and ferroptosis in hepatocytes is induced.

However, this study had several limitations: (1) Future research will compare the different effects of several ferroptosis inhibitors on liver protection in patients with sepsis and explore the specific mechanisms. (2) We only confirmed that ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis is involved in liver injury in sepsis, and further research is needed to elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms. The inhibition of ferritinophagy by 3-MA in this study had certain limitations. In the future, we will further use gene editing technology to silence or overexpress NCOA4 and autophagy-related genes both in vitro and in vivo, and explore the specific mechanism by which the inhibition of ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis contributes to liver protection in sepsis at the molecular level.

Conclusion

Ferroptosis was activated in the hepatocytes of CLP-induced septic rats, and by inhibiting ferroptosis, hepatocyte lipid peroxidation levels were decreased, mitochondrial damage was alleviated, and liver function in septic rats was improved. Additionally, NCOA4 expression was increased in the hepatocytes of septic rats, and ferritin was degraded via ferritinophagy, leading to an increase in intracellular Fe²⁺ concentration and inducing hepatocyte ferroptosis. By inhibiting ferritinophagy, hepatocyte ferroptosis was alleviated, and septic liver injury was ameliorated. Based on the results of this study, we hypothesize that specific inhibition of ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis may become a potential therapeutic target for sepsis-induced liver injury.
