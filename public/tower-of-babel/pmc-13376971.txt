Sustainable decontamination of crude oil-contaminated soil using Sapindus mukorossi extract: A green intervention to synthetic surfactant treatment
Authors: Sreelakshmi Diddi, Shivakumar Ramachandra, Samita Maitra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remediating crude oil-contaminated sites is vital for environmental recovery. Surfactant based remediation strategies have gained importance in the management of such sites. Combining biogenic surfactant flushing with conventional methods is noted to be effective than using a single approach. Plant-based surfactants, such as saponins from Sapindus mukorossi, are easier to produce than microbial biosurfactants which require stringent control of conditions. However, research on their application in crude oil contaminated site remediation is limited. This study is an exploration of Sapindus mukorossi extract (saponin extract) for flushing crude oil from contaminated sand. It was observed that the dissolution of crude oil was controlled by its diffusion from sand. A pseudo first order model was used as an empirical description of the removal process. The saponin extract demonstrated a higher solubility enhancement factor (1.32) and better removal of asphaltenes compared to water and favored synthetic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS). The optimal duration of flushing was 22 h, 2 h and 106 h for distilled water (DW), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and saponin extract respectively. The dominant mechanism of flushing was inferred, based on the experimental data, to be mobilization. This affirms that a low concentration of saponin extract would render satisfactory flushing. The emulsion forming ability of saponin extract was more pronounced with crude oil- water mixtures having higher proportion of water. A lab-scale setup simulated continuous soil flushing, examining the effects of aqueous phase composition, flow rate, and flushing duration. Saponin extract flushed 60% of the crude oil from contaminated sand. Given its recognized renewability, biodegradability, low toxicity and cost, and the laboratory scale effectiveness demonstrated in the current study, saponin extract may serve as a preliminary intervention to reduce initial contaminant loads and render the soil matrix amenable for subsequent synthetic surfactant action and/or natural microbial degradation. Such an approach might contribute to the reduction in consumption of synthetic surfactants in remediation. These results indicate the potential of the approach under controlled conditions and motivate further evaluation for real remediation scenarios.

Introduction

Crude oil is one of the most important sources of energy across the world and is referred to as the ‘lifeblood’ of industrial nations1,2. It is a highly complex mixture of several hundred hydrocarbons extracted from the underground reservoirs in sedimentary rocks3. The exploration, production and transportation of crude oil have an adverse effect on the environment. Spills during transportation, leaks from storage tanks, disposal of industrial/domestic oil wastes have become major human and environmental hazards. Due to oil’s low solubility in water and consequent non-availability for biodegradation, it remains in the soil matrix for a long time. Different technologies are available for the remediation of contaminated sites4–6. Ex-situ technologies include solidification/stabilization, thermal desorption, bioremediation, physical separation, washing etc. while soil vapor extraction, multi-phase extraction, chemical treatment and flushing are some used in-situ technologies. Bioremediation is the most used innovative technology. It is a complex and time-consuming technology as it requires providing a combination of oxygen, nutrients, moisture, and stringent control of temperature and pH, for microorganisms to grow and remediate7,8. Soil flushing and soil washing are water-based technologies and are classified as innovative technologies. In flushing, an appropriate aqueous solution is injected into the contaminated soil and is recovered along with the flushed contaminants as depicted in Fig. 1. In washing, contaminated soil and the aqueous solution are mixed ex-situ and separated using gravity settling9–11. In-situ flushing with surfactants (Surfactant enhanced soil remediation/SER) gained importance as a reliable means of remediating contaminated soil and aquifer12. Full-scale remediation was conducted at a fuel oil contaminated site at Clark Oil Company, Fredericton, Canada, using Ivey-sol, a proprietary compound of Ivey Environmental Services, that removes PAHs and petroleum hydrocarbons by micellar solubilization. A rock fill at an earthmover refueling bay in Western Australia contaminated with diesel fuel was flushed with biosurfactant and nitrogen-phosphorous-potassium nutrient (N-P-K). It was reported that the biosurfactant was successful in dispersing the diesel in the rock fill and bring down its high concentration in the sediment, thereby making the sediment amenable for the natural biodegradation processes to happen. Remediation of a site contaminated with gasoline and diesel was completed at a Bulk Petroleum Plant, Quebec Province, Canada. Injecsol TM surfactant solution was used for diesel while simultaneous pumping of ground water and air was done for the extraction of gasoline13.

Schematic of soil flushing.

USEPA identified surfactant flushing as a niche technology for sites contaminated with NAPLs. The main advantage of this technology is its ability to quickly remove a large fraction of the NAPL contaminant as compared to other technologies14,15. Additionally, use of this technology in conjunction with other technologies such as bioremediation, chemical oxidation helped bring contaminated sites to closure16–18. The major limitation of this technology is the detrimental effect on the environment due to the retention of the synthetic surfactants in the soil matrix after SER19,20. Further, synthetic surfactants are derived from fossil fuels which are non-renewable resources. Plant surfactants possess excellent surface activity, high selectivity, greater environmental compatibility, low toxicity, and are biodegradable21–27. The biodegradability of the aqueous extract of Sapindus mukorossi was evaluated in our earlier work, where it exhibited a BOD₅/COD ratio of 0.8, indicating high biodegradability25. They are produced by renewable resources and are cost-effective. They are also known to enhance biological activity in the soil. Hence, they could provide a good alternative to synthetic surfactants in SER27–32. Sapindus mukorossi saponin showed trends similar to typical synthetic surfactants in solubilizing hexachlorobenzene (HCB) and naphthalene. Saponin solution was able to recover 100 times more HCB than water flooding after 12 pore volumes33. Investigations on the emulsification of crude oil using commercial biosurfactants such as rhamnolipid, tannin, quillaja saponin, lecithin and aescin proposed that surfactants which formed greater emulsion phase only and/or water phase with less crude oil phase would be effective for soil flushing34,35. Traditionally, the aqueous extract from the fruit pericarp of Sapindus mukorossi has been valued for its detergent properties and medicinal uses. Studies reported that Sapindus mukorossi extract has great potential to be used as a surfactant for enhanced oil recovery schemes as it significantly reduced the interfacial tension of crude oil-water system36,37. Aqueous extract of sapindus mukorossi was able to desorb zinc from contaminated soil without chemically interacting with zinc and negligible damage of the soil38. Sapindus mukorossi displayed high removal efficiencies for heavy metals like arsenic, chromium and lead23,24.

While surfactant flushing and washing are established approaches for remediating contaminated soil, most studies focused on synthetic or microbial surfactants. Published literature highlights the potential of plant surfactants for enhancing removal efficiency and biodegradation of contaminants but their applicability to soil flushing is underexplored. Further, most work focused on compounds such as PAHs, HCB, ammonia and heavy metals whereas crude oil is a highly complex mixture of hydrocarbons. Studies probing the use of aqueous extracts of Sapindus mukorossi for remediating crude oil-contaminated sites are rare. This study addresses the research gap by systematically evaluating the aqueous extract of Sapindus mukorossi fruit pericarp, for crude oil removal from contaminated sand under controlled flushing conditions, characterizing the kinetics, mechanisms, and optimal operational parameters. Saponin extract’s performance was compared with distilled water (DW) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), a commonly used synthetic surfactant. The oil removal process was modeled to estimate kinetic parameters. The emulsification capacity of the saponin extract was evaluated. Factors such as the type and concentration of the aqueous phase, as well as shaking speed, were systematically studied. Sand column experiments were conducted to simulate field conditions, assessing the influence of aqueous phase type, flow rate, and flushing duration on oil removal efficiency.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was procured from S D Fine-Chem Limited and used as received. Sapindus mukorossi fruits were procured from the local market. Brass River crude oil was obtained from Chennai Petroleum Corporation Limited. Tables 1 and 2 display properties of the surfactants and crude oil used in this work respectively. Sand was obtained from the neighbourhood ground. Millipore Double distilled water (Millipore (India) Private Limited) was used for all the experiments. Absorbance measurements were done on a Jasco UV-VIS 530 spectrophotometer (Jasco Incorporation, Japan). A Mieco RH-P100L-200 peristaltic pump (Mieco Pumps & Generators Private Limited, India) was used to pump the aqueous phase into the sand column. All the experiments were carried out at room temperature (21 ± 4 °C).

The Brass River crude oil was chemically characterized by High Temperature Simulated Distillation (SIMDIS-HT), and ‘Saturates, Aromatics, Resins, and Asphaltenes (SARA)’ analysis.

Preparation of aqueous saponin extract

The fruit was crushed to separate the pericarp and seed. The pericarps were dried in an oven at 50 °C for three days, ground, sieved and stored in an air-tight container. The dry powder was stirred in distilled water for three hours and filtered using a 20 μm mesh. 10 g of the powder was stirred in 100 ml of DW to obtain a 10 wt% solution36.

Sand cleaning, contamination and weathering

Soil was cleaned with water, sodium dithionite, hydrogen peroxide, hydrochloric acid and distilled water until the pH of the wash water was 6.8-7.0, to remove dirt, metal oxides, organic matter and carbonates, and obtain chemically inert sand39,40. It was sieved to get particles size 0.65 mm and dried in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h. A crude oil loading of 75% of the saturation value was used and mixed in a horizontal drum mixer at 100 RPM for 30 min. The loaded sand was weathered in a hot air oven at 40 °C and its weight was monitored at regular intervals of time. The time after which there was no change in the weight of the sand sample was considered as the weathering time.

Quantification of crude oil in sand

Two grams of the sand sample was taken in a screw capped conical flask and 8 mL of hexane was added to it. The mixture was left to stand for 10 min and then put in a wrist shaker for 5 min. The hexane extract was decanted. The above procedure was repeated thrice, and the cumulative extract was centrifuged at 10,000 RPM for 20 min and analyzed in the UV-Spectrophotometer41. Kinetic experiment

The kinetic experiment was carried out to determine the optimal time for flushing. Two grams of the weathered contaminated sand with 10mL of the flushing solution was taken in each of the screw capped conical flasks (sacrificial samples designated with time). The flasks were kept in a temperature-controlled shaker. The concentration of crude oil in the bulk aqueous phase was estimated. Experiments were conducted in triplicate and the mean values reported within an error range of ± 5%.

Emulsification

The formation of crude oil- aqueous phase emulsion during the flushing process positively impacts the aqueous phase’s removal efficiency, by increasing the interfacial area between crude oil and the aqueous phase. The ability of the surfactants to form such emulsions was examined by conducting emulsification experiments with different crude oil-to-water ratios34,42. Surfactant solutions of varying concentrations were prepared. Crude oil and surfactant solutions were taken in the volume ratios 2.5:7.5, 5:5 and 7.5:2.5, stirred at 1200 RPM for 5 min and left undisturbed. The phase volumes were measured at 24 h and 72 h. A control study involving crude oil and water without the use of surfactants, was conducted, to observe their inherent tendencies when mixed. Experiments were conducted in triplicate and the mean values reported within an error range of ± 2%.

Flushing experiments were conducted in batch mode to identify the mechanism of crude oil removal and, the effect of aqueous phase composition, concentration and agitation speed on crude oil removal. The optimum flushing time obtained from the kinetic experiment was used. Table 3 displays experimental parameters in detail. Continuous flushing

A laboratory scale process to mimic field-scale soil washing was designed based on the inferences gained from kinetic experiments, batch flushing emulsification and preliminary column experiments. A cylindrical acrylic column of outer diameter − 44 mm and inner diameter − 38.5 mm fitted with threaded stainless-steel caps and PTFE O-rings at both ends was used. Layers of stainless-steel mesh were fixed inside the column at both ends to provide good liquid distribution and to prevent sand getting carried away by the liquid. For each run, 200 g of weathered contaminated sand was loaded into the column with tapping, to form a uniform bed of height 200 mm.

Characterization of the sand column

The sand column was characterized by measuring the bulk density, porosity, particle density and permeability.

Bulk density

A known mass of sand was taken and loaded into the column with frequent tapping to form a uniform compact bed. The height and cross-sectional area of the sand bed were noted down.

Porosity

A known mass of sand was taken and loaded into the column with frequent tapping to form a uniform compact bed. The height and cross-sectional area of the sand bed were noted down. A measured volume of water was taken in a beaker and slowly added to the sand till a thin film of water was visible above the sand surface. The volume of water left in the beaker was noted down.

Permeability

The permeability of the sand column was determined using the constant head permeability procedure as illustrated in Fig. 2. Sand was filled into the column with frequent tapping to get a uniform compact bed of a predetermined height. The column was saturated with water to remove any air trapped within. Water was let from the constant head overhead reservoir into the sand column at the top of the column and allowed to flow out from the outlet at the bottom of the column. After the flow attained steady state, the time required for collecting water was noted using a measuring cylinder. The procedure was repeated for different hydraulic heads and column heights.

Results and discussion

Sand contamination and weathering

The saturation limit of crude oil was found to be 4 mL per 50 g sand. An oil loading of 75% of the saturation value i.e. 3 mL per 50 g sand was used for the experiments. Figure 4 shows the variation of the weight of contaminated sand during the weathering process. There was no change in the weight of the contaminated sand sample after 130 h. Therefore, 130 h was taken as the weathering time for the preparation of samples. The weathered contaminated sand was stored in air-tight glass containers for further use.

Fig. 4Variation of the weight of contaminated sand with time.

Variation of the weight of contaminated sand with time.

After an oil spill, typically, crude oil components with boiling points up to 200 ° C are lost within 24 h. Due to the preferential loss of low molecular weight compounds, the weathered oil will have higher proportion of high molecular weight compounds and asphaltenes compared to the un-weathered oil. To identify the compound class of crude oil lost due to weathering, the Saturates-Aromatics-Resins- Asphaltenes analysis (SARA) was done on sand samples before and after weathering. SARA analysis breaks down crude oil into four compound classes: Saturates, Aromatics, Resins and Asphaltenes. Table 5 depicts the results of SARA analysis of crude oil before and after weathering respectively. The results indicate prominent variation in the aromatic and asphaltene compound classes of crude oil due to the weathering process.

K. et al. regarding the solubilization of naphthalene using glycolipids produced by Rhodococcus, Huiqin Guo et al. on the feasibility of washing p-nitro chlorobenzene with SDBS and Tween80 and Sun X et al. on the thermal desorption of organic contaminated soil. Emulsifying ability

Distilled water showed no emulsion formation with crude oil for all the volume ratios tested. The phases separated after the agitation stopped indicating that an emulsion cannot be formed in the absence of a surfactant. With the addition of surfactants, three phases were observed, viz. crude oil phase, water-in-oil emulsion phase and oil-in-water emulsion phase as shown in Figs. 6 and 7. These observations concur with those of Alabdulmohsen Z who tested the emulsification of crude oil using IGEPAL Co-530, SDS and CTAB. The formation of both oil continuous and water continuous emulsion phases was reported42. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and saponin extract differed in their emulsifying ability. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) showed increasing emulsifying ability with increase in its concentration and with samples having higher proportion of crude oil. Saponin extract’s emulsifying ability improved with its concentration, but it worked better for samples having lower a proportion of crude oil. This finding is consistent with the observation of Urum K P et al. on emulsification of crude oil using quillaja saponin. The emulsification ability of saponin decreased as the crude oil volume ratio increased34.

Batch flushing

The batch flushing results are presented in Figs. 8, 9 and 10. With DW (Fig. 8), it was observed that the removal of crude oil increased with speed of shaking (RPM) in a near linear manner. With both sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Fig. 9) and saponin extract (Fig. 10), it was evident that crude oil removal increased with RPM and did not vary significantly with the concentration. Maximum removal of 90% and 93% were obtained at 142 RPM for sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and saponin extract respectively. As the shaking speed increases, more surface area of the contaminated sand is exposed to the surfactant solution facilitating the removal of oil. Further, the maximum crude oil removal in the present work was observed at concentrations below the CMC, and no substantial increase in removal occurred at higher surfactant concentrations. These observations resonate with the reporting of Urum et al. on the action of microbial biosurfactants for soil washing, Gina J et al. on mobilization and micellar solubilization of NAPLs from aquifer rocks, Ming et al. on the mobilization of tight oil by spontaneous surface imbibition of surfactants and Sun C et al. on the mobilization mechanisms of microscopic residual oil in sandstone reservoirs30,34,49–51. Urum concluded that biosurfactants are capable of solubilizing in the vicinity of their CMC and thereafter the increase in solubilization was insignificant. The two probable mechanisms for crude oil removal from porous media are mobilization and solubilization, for surfactant concentrations below and above CMC respectively. Mobilization is due to the interfacial tension reduction and contact angle reduction abilities of the surfactant. As the interfacial tension between oil and water, and water-sand contact angle are reduced, the capillary force holding crude oil to sand is reduced, leading to its removal. The dominant mechanism of crude oil removal in the present study is inferred to be mobilization, based on observed experimental trends rather than direct physicochemical measurements49,52,53. Hence, it was decided that surfactants’ concentration near the CMC would be used for continuous flushing experiments.

Flushing

The percentage removal of crude oil from continuous flushing is presented in Fig. 11. The maximum removal obtained with DW was 14.5%, while the removal was 60% with both sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and saponin extract. The flushing duration and aqueous phase flow rate are influential parameters for crude oil removal. With an increase in flushing duration, the volume of surfactant flowing through the column increases. As the column gets saturated with the surfactant solution, there will be more penetration of surfactant into the interface between oil and soil, which enhanced removal27,54.

Crude oil removal in continuous flushing.

During the flushing with DW, it was observed that the removal of crude oil increased with an increase in flow rate and run duration. Higher flow rates increase the drag force exerted by the aqueous phase on contaminated sand, leading to the abrasion of oil from the sand surface.

Flow rate of the aqueous phase through the column impacts the aqueous phase-oil contact time and hydraulic removal. With an increase in flow rate, the contact time between the surfactant and the oil decreases. On the other hand, with an increase in flow rate, the aqueous phase exerts a higher drag force on the oil layer leading to the hydraulic removal of crude oil. With sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), it was observed that the removal increased with flow rate. This could be attributed to the mechanism of hydraulic removal. With saponin extract, it was observed that contact time is the influential parameter. This result agrees with the observations drawn from kinetic study where the rate coefficient of saponin extract was lower than that of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). Interfacial properties of surfactants are influenced by the surfactant chemical structure and molecular weight and affect its performance in crude oil removal. Saponin extract has a bulky structure and would need longer time of interaction with the oil in the column. These outcomes support previous reporting of Razika K et al. that surfactant flow rate and wash duration are important parameters in the washing of diesel contaminated soil with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)47. Zhao et al. reported that the rate of removal of petroleum hydrocarbons using composite surfactants improved rapidly with time initially and thereafter stabilized27. The selection of suitable flushing parameters is highly site-specific and further detailed exploration of flow conditions, soil heterogeneity and contaminant loading are required for potential systems55.

Conclusions

In the present study, the ability of aqueous extract of Sapindus mukorossi fruit to remove crude oil from contaminated site is evaluated for potential application in surfactant enhanced remediation processes. Use of aqueous extract eliminates the complexity and high cost of production of pure saponin. It appeared that the dissolution of crude oil was governed by diffusion from the sand matrix, and the process was empirically characterized by pseudo-first-order kinetics. Flushing duration had a significant effect, and the optimum time of flushing was 22 h, 2 h and 106 h for DW, sodium dodecyl sulfate and saponin extract respectively. Compared to sodium dodecyl sulfate, the saponin extract’s solubility enhancement factor was higher and its rate coefficient lower. Hence, it is expected that decontamination with saponin extract requires longer duration. Based on the batch flushing experimental observations, it was inferred that mobilization could be the primary mechanism driving removal, and a satisfactory flushing performance is achievable even at low concentrations, with saponin extract compared to sodium dodecyl sulfate. Saponin extract was more effective than sodium dodecyl sulfate in the removal of asphaltene fraction of the crude oil. The emulsifying ability of saponin extract was higher with crude oil- water mixtures having a higher proportion of water. The laboratory-scale process designed to mimic field-scale soil flushing indicated that the aqueous phase composition, flow rate and flush duration are influential parameters affecting removal. Saponin extract removed 60% of the crude oil after 7 h of flushing. In summary, these laboratory results demonstrate that the aqueous extract of Sapindus mukorossi fruit can effectively disperse contaminants, potentially enhancing soil amenability to subsequent synthetic surfactant action and microbial degradation. These results provide valuable insights under controlled conditions and highlight saponin extract as a promising candidate for reducing synthetic surfactant consumption in remediation. Given its low critical micelle concentration (CMC), and literature supported attributes of biodegradability, and renewability, the saponin extract could serve as a potential component of sustainable remediation frameworks. Further evaluation encompassing varied flow regimes, soil types and contaminant loadings are required for extrapolation to field-scale applications.
