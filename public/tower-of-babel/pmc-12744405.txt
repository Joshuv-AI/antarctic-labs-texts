Impact of different polishing and aging procedures on the surface characteristics of bulk-fill resin composites
Authors: Hande Filiz, Ayse Tugba Erturk-Avunduk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate the effects of different polishing systems and artificial aging procedures on the surface roughness, gloss, and microhardness of conventional and bulk-fill resin composites.MethodsOne nanohybrid resin composite [Clearfil Majesty Esthetic (CME)] and three bulk-fill resin composites [SonicFill (SF), Opus APS (O-APS), and FiltekTM Bulk-Fill (FBF)] were evaluated. Ninety specimens were prepared for each material and subjected to 3 polishing protocols [Mylar strip (control), OneGloss, and OptiDisc]. Following polishing, the specimens underwent 3 different aging procedures: distilled water storage, thermocycling, and ultraviolet (UV) aging. Measurements for surface roughness (Ra), gloss (GU), and Vickers microhardness (VHN) were recorded at baseline (T0), after polishing (T1), and post-aging (T2). Atomic force microscopy images of 2 randomly selected specimens from each test subgroup. Data were analyzed using three-way ANOVA and Spearman’s correlation analysis.ResultStatistically significant differences were observed among the materials, polishing systems, and aging procedures for all measured parameters (p < 0.001). FBF consistently exhibited the highest gloss and hardness values and the lowest surface roughness, while SF showed the poorest surface characteristics. The interaction of control × distilled water resulted in superior surface properties. Significant two-way and three-way interactions were found among material, polishing, and aging variables (p < 0.05). Strong negative correlations were identified between surface roughness and gloss values at all time points (r = -0.717 to -0.850), whereas a very weak positive correlation was noted between surface roughness and hardness after aging (r = 0.173, p = 0.001).ConclusionMaterial composition, polishing approach, and aging conditions significantly affected the surface behavior of the resin composites. Among the tested materials, FBF exhibited the most stable microhardness, gloss, and surface smoothness, while SF showed the greatest surface deterioration. Multi-step polishing (OptiDisc) maintained smoother and glossier surfaces than the single-step system (OneGloss). Thermocycling and UV aging produced the most adverse effects on surface quality. The proper combination of a stable bulk-fill composite and a multi-step polishing system is therefore recommended to achieve optimal long-term clinical performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07469-y.

Background

Resin composites have been widely used in restorative dentistry due to their esthetic properties, ease of handling, and improved physical characteristics [1]. Among the various clinical parameters that determine the long-term success of resin composite restorations, microhardness and surface roughness are critical as they directly affect mechanical durability, wear resistance, color stability, and susceptibility to plaque accumulation [2]. Smooth and durable surfaces help minimize staining, bacterial buildup, and gingival irritation, thereby improving patient comfort [3]. In recent years, bulk-fill resin composites have gained prominence as a result of advancements in filler technology and resin matrix formulations [4]. These materials are designed to be placed in thicker increments (up to 4–5 mm) without compromising depth of cure or mechanical properties [5]. Bulk-fill resin composites have less polymerization shrinkage and these properties result in lower shrinkage stress on the cavity walls [6].

Finishing/polishing can be considered as the basic steps in restorative procedures that increase the esthetics and clinical life of restored teeth [7]. The resin matrix and the filler particles vary in hardness; as a result, they do not exhibit uniform wear [8]. Therefore, due to the removal of some particles during the finishing/polishing process; surface defects such as microcracks and irregularities in the materials may occur, which reduce the wear resistance of the restorations [9]. Obtaining optimum surface properties depends not only on the material itself but also on the finishing and polishing procedures applied [10]. Selecting an appropriate polishing technique is essential to improve the functional and esthetic performance of resin composites [11]. Polishing systems differ in terms of abrasiveness, composition and adaptation to anatomical contours, such as diamond burs, stones, rubber cups, points, discs, strips, and pastes [12]. In polishing systems, efforts have been made to simplify multi-step applications into single-step approaches. Considering that simplified application systems are less time-consuming, it is important for clinicians to know which finishing/polishing systems offer sufficient surface quality to extend the life of resin composite restorations [13].

In restorative dentistry, materials have been subjected not only to polishing procedures but also to a range of intraoral environmental challenges including thermal variations and water absorption [14, 15]. These factors collectively contribute to aging-induced changes that may compromise the surface integrity, reduce microhardness, increase surface roughness, and ultimately impair the clinical longevity of restorations [14]. To mimic the oral environment in laboratory settings, various artificial aging protocols have been employed. Among these, thermocycling has been the most widely adopted method in the literature [16]. Nonetheless, alternative techniques such as water storage, boiling, acid immersion, autoclaving, exposure to water–ethanol mixtures, and ultraviolet (UV) aging have been also utilized to simulate clinical degradation [17]. A previous study [18] has emphasized the significance of simulating fluctuations in light and humidity, particularly when evaluating the durability of resin-based materials. In UV aging protocols, specimens are exposed to UV-emitting light under controlled cycles of temperature, heat, and humidity. thereby offering a multifactorial simulation of oral aging conditions. These processes are expected to induce physicochemical alterations within the material [19]. However, it is essential to consider that modern restorative systems including bulk-fill resin composites may demonstrate differing levels of resilience against such degradation. primarily owing to variations in filler content and matrix–filler interfacial properties. To date, the biomechanical properties of bulk-fill resin composites under aging conditions remain insufficiently characterized, with inconsistent findings likely attributable to methodological disparities across studies.

Therefore, the present in-vitro study aimed to evaluate the effects of different polishing systems and artificial aging protocols on the microhardness, surface roughness, and gloss of conventional resin composite and bulk-fill resin materials. The null hypotheses of the study were (a) different resin composite materials have no effect on the microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness values; (b) different polishing systems have no effect on the microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness of tested resin composites; and (c) different aging techniques have no effect on the microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness of tested resin composites.

Methods

Restorative materials used in the study

This study evaluated one nanohybrid conventional resin composite [Clearfil Majesty Esthetic (CME; Kuraray, Tokyo, Japan)] and three different bulk-fill resin composites [SonicFill-2 (SF; Kerr Corp., Orange, CA, USA), Opus APS (O-APS;FGM, Santa Catarina, Brazil), and Filtek™ Bulk-Fill (FBF; 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA)] were used. Three polishing procedures, Mylar strip (Hawe StopStrip, Kerr Corp., Bioggio, Switzerland), OneGloss (Shofu Inc., Kyoto, Japan) and OptiDisc (Kerr Corp., Orange, CA, USA) were used in the current study. Specimens preparation

The sample size of the study was calculated using the G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4. Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The significance level (α) was set at 0.05, and the statistical power was determined as 90%. Considering the parameters of surface roughness, gloss, and microhardness, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 348. Taking into account a potential sample loss rate of 20%, the total number of specimens was finalized as 360. Each final subgroup consisted of 10 specimens (n = 10) for each material. Schematic illustration of study design

For this study, a total of 90 disk-shaped specimens (6 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness) were fabricated from each resin composite material using a Teflon mold. To achieve a uniform surface and remove excess material, a transparent polyester strip and a glass slide were applied during specimen preparation. Polymerization of all specimens was performed using a light emitting diode (LED) curing unit (SmartLite Pro, Dentsply Sirona, York, USA) at an irradiance of 1200 mW/cm2 for 20 s, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions for each material. A 1 mm transparent strip was placed between the light source and the specimen to standardize the curing distance. After removal from the Teflon molds, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h to allow for post-polymerization [20]. To distinguish upper and lower surfaces, each specimen was marked on the lateral side before all measurements.

Microhardness measurement

Initial (T0) microhardness measurements of the specimens were performed using a Vickers microhardness tester (Microbul–1000d, Bulut Makine, Istanbul, Türkiye). Once the surface of the resin composite specimen was clearly visualized under the lens, an indentation was made using a Vickers diamond indenter with a 200 g load applied for 10 s. The resulting quadrilateral indentation on the specimen surface was examined under a × 40 magnification lens. The distances between the diagonals of the indentation were measured manually using the device’s lever arm—first in the vertical direction and then horizontally. Based on these measurements, the microhardness tester automatically calculated the Vickers Hardness Number (VHN). For each specimen, three separate measurements were taken, and the average VHN was recorded [21].

Gloss measurement

Baseline gloss units (GU) measurements were done using a gloss meter (3nh yg60, Amastone, Italy). A black opaque plastic mould was placed over the specimens to eliminate the influence of ambient light and to maintain the exact position of the specimens during the measurements. For each specimen, 3 measurements were made at 60° light incident and reflection angles and the surface gloss values were recorded by averaging the values obtained. The American Dental Association (ADA) has established the range of 40–60 GU as generally desirable gloss; therefore, these values have been adopted as the threshold standards [20].

Surface roughness measurement

Baseline surface roughness (Ra) measurements (T0) were performed with a mechanical contact profilometer (Mitutoyo, Surftest SJ-410, Japan) with a measuring distance of 4 mm and a cut-off value of 0.8 mm. Before each measurement, the profilometer was calibrated with a reference block with a Ra value of 3.05 μm. The average values of 3 measurements taken at the centre of the surface of each specimen were recorded in micrometres (μm). The Ra threshold was set at 0.2 μm [21].

Polishing procedures

Following the initial microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness measurements, specimens of each restorative material were randomly divided into 3 subgroups (N = 30/group) in terms of polishing procedures. These procedures are described in Table 1. All polishing procedures were performed by a single experienced operator (H. F.) to ensure consistency. The microhardness, gloss and surface roughness measurements were repeated for all materials and groups after the application of polishing procedures (T1).

Accelerated artificial aging (AAA) procedures

The finishing/polishing groups were randomly divided into 3 subgroups based on the aging procedures: distilled water, thermocycling, and UV fluorescence aging (n = 10/per subgroup).

Specimens allocated to the distilled water aging protocol were stored at 37 ± 1 °C, with the water renewed on a daily basis. To maintain comparability with the other aging protocols, final evaluations for this group (n = 10) were performed after a 1-year storage period.

For the thermocycling procedure, each subgroup of resin composite specimens was individually marked using color-coded threads and arranged in a specific sequence to prevent mixing during the cycling process. The specimens were subjected to aging using a thermocycling machine (MOD Dental, Ankara, Türkiye). The thermocycling protocol involved alternating immersion of the specimens in water baths maintained at 5 °C and 55 °C (± 2 °C), with dwell times of 30 s in each bath and a transfer time of 10 s between them. A total of 10.000 cycles were applied to simulate thermal aging, which is considered to correspond to approximately 1-year of clinical service [22].

For the fluorescence UV test procedure, 5 metal plates consisting of 24 discs were prepared for the specimens. The plates were made of stainless steel to prevent damage to the specimens. In the UV aging device (Atlas UV test, Ametek, UK), 300 h aging process was applied at 90% humidity, 120 min periods with 18 min distilled water spraying, 8 h irradiation at 60 °C ± 3 °C and 4 h condensation cycle at 50 °C ± 3 °C. The specimens were subjected to 150 kJ/m2 radiant energy for 300 h which corresponds to approximately 1-year of clinical effect [23].

Following the aging procedures, final microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness measurements (T2) of the specimens were conducted.

Topographic surface imaging with Atomic Force Microscope (AFM)

At the end of the experimental procedures, AFM images of 2 randomly selected specimens from each test subgroup were obtained. These specimens were examined using an AFM instrument (Multimode AFM, Veeco Instruments Inc., CA, USA). Tapping mode was used and a spring constant of 40 N/m was used. The device scanned 10 × 10 μm2 areas at a resonant frequency of 300 kHz and produced 3D images with a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), Minitab 14, and the R statistical software. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using both the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. For surface roughness values that did not follow a normal distribution, as well as to examine the relationship between gloss and hardness values, Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient was applied. For variables that met the assumption of normal distribution, comparisons based on material type, polishing system, and aging procedure were performed using three-way ANOVA. For non-normally distributed variable, a three-way robust ANOVA was employed. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni test. Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation or trimmed mean ± standard error, depending on distribution characteristics. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the initial time period (T0), GU values differed significantly among materials (p < 0.001). FBF specimens demonstrated the highest GU, while SF exhibited the lowest. No significant difference was detected among the polishing systems (p = 0.18). However, aging procedures significantly influenced gloss (p = 0.001). Specimens aged in distilled water or UV light exhibited higher gloss than thermocycled ones. Interaction analysis revealed that material × aging (p = 0.001) and polishing × aging (p = 0.001) interactions were statistically significant. Conversely, material × polishing (p = 0.27) and the three-way interaction (p = 0.138) were not significant.

In time period T1, significant differences were observed among materials (p < 0.001) and polishing systems (p = 0.003), while aging procedures did not show a statistically significant effect (p = 0.14). FBF specimens continued to exhibit the highest gloss, reflecting their superior surface polishability and optical stability, whereas SF maintained the lowest GU. CME and O-APS presented comparable gloss levels that were statistically lower than those of FBF but higher than those of SF. Among polishing systems, the control (Mylar strip) group produced the highest gloss, followed by OptiDisc, whereas the OneGloss system resulted in the lowest gloss values. Although aging alone was not significant, material × polishing, material × aging, polishing × aging, and the three-way interactions were all significant (p = 0.001). Notably, the O-APS/OneGloss/distilled water combination yielded the highest gloss, while SF/OptiDisc/UV produced the lowest.

In time period T2, statistically significant differences were maintained among the materials (p < 0.001). FBF specimens exhibited the highest GU values, followed by CME and O-APS, whereas SF continued to show the lowest gloss performance. Significant differences were also observed among the polishing systems (p < 0.001). The control group exhibited the highest gloss values, while OneGloss yielded the lowest. Aging protocols demonstrated a modest but statistically significant effect (p = 0.045). The thermocycling group showed the highest mean GU, suggesting that mechanical cycling alone may not severely impair surface reflection, while UV light led to the lowest gloss values. However, pairwise comparisons among aging types did not reveal statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). The material × polishing (p = 0.001) and material × aging (p = 0.001) interactions remained significant, demonstrating that gloss loss patterns were material-dependent. The three-way interaction (p = 0.018) was also significant, with FBF specimens polished with the control strip and aged under thermocycling or UV conditions showing the highest gloss, while CME/OneGloss/UV combinations resulted in the lowest readings.

Across all time points, GU values of all tested materials remained within the clinically acceptable range of 40–60 GU as defined by ADA standards, indicating that although statistically significant differences were found, all materials maintained acceptable esthetic performance throughout the experimental period.

In the initial time period (T0), significant differences in Ra values were observed among the materials (p < 0.001). FBF specimens exhibited the smoothest surfaces, while SF demonstrated the highest roughness. CME and O-APS presented intermediate, statistically distinct values from both FBF and SF, indicating that filler size, distribution and resin matrix composition strongly affected initial topography. Polishing systems did not produce a significant effect at this stage (p = 0.73), suggesting that baseline differences were primarily material-dependent rather than treatment-related. However, aging protocols significantly influenced Ra (p = 0.001). Specimens stored in distilled water exhibited the lowest surface roughness, whereas thermocycled specimens showed the most pronounced increase, likely due to hydrothermal stress and resin–filler interface fatigue. Interaction analysis revealed significant material × aging (p = 0.001) and polishing × aging (p = 0.002) effects, meaning that the response to aging differed among materials and polishing methods. The three-way interaction (p = 0.482) was not significant, indicating that no combined synergistic effect of all three factors was detected at baseline.

In time period T1, statistically significant differences were again found among materials (p < 0.001), polishing systems (p < 0.001), and aging procedures (p = 0.001). The control (Mylar strip) group exhibited the lowest Ra values, confirming that the absence of active finishing produced the smoothest surfaces. In contrast, the OneGloss system yielded the highest roughness, while OptiDisc provided intermediate results. Aging procedures continued to influence Ra, with specimens aged in distilled water maintaining smoother surfaces and those exposed to thermocycling exhibiting the greatest roughness increase. These outcomes indicate that both the mechanical action of polishing and environmental stresses contributed to surface degradation. Significant two-way interactions were observed: material × polishing (p = 0.002), material × aging (p = 0.001), and polishing × aging (p = 0.015). This suggests that the extent of surface roughening varied depending on the material–protocol combination. Such findings highlight the differential susceptibility of composite resins to finishing and aging processes.

In time period T2, surface roughness remained significantly influenced by all main factors (p < 0.001). CME specimens displayed the lowest Ra values, indicating superior long-term surface stability, whereas SF continued to exhibit the highest roughness. FBF and O-APS demonstrated comparable roughness, reflecting moderate resistance to surface degradation. Among the polishing systems, OneGloss consistently produced the roughest surfaces, whereas the control and OptiDisc groups achieved smoother profiles (p < 0.001). Aging procedures had a significant effect (p = 0.001), with thermocycling producing the greatest surface deterioration, followed by UV exposure, while storage in distilled water preserved smoother textures. Significant material × polishing (p = 0.009) and material × aging (p = 0.001) interactions indicated that each composite responded differently to polishing and aging conditions. In particular, SF and O-APS specimens showed more pronounced surface changes under thermal and UV aging compared with CME and FBF.

Across all evaluated time periods, Ra values for all groups exceeded the clinically acceptable threshold of 0.2 μm, suggesting that while all tested resin composites maintained acceptable optical gloss, their surfaces were sufficiently rough to potentially promote plaque accumulation or staining over time.

Discussion

This in-vitro study aimed to evaluate the influence of different polishing systems and artificial aging procedures on the surface characteristics—specifically microhardness, gloss, and surface roughness—of bulk-fill and conventional resin composites. The findings led to the rejection of all null hypotheses, demonstrating that material type, polishing technique, and aging protocol each had a significant effect on the surface properties of the resin composites. Among the tested materials, FBF consistently exhibited superior performance across all evaluated parameters, demonstrating the highest gloss and microhardness values and the lowest surface roughness. In contrast, SF yielded the poorest surface properties, suggesting a potential vulnerability to surface degradation under intraoral-simulated conditions. Additionally, significant two-way and three-way interactions among the material type, polishing method, and aging protocol were observed, emphasizing the multifactorial nature of surface integrity in resin-based restorations.

The comparative analysis of resin composite materials revealed marked differences in their resistance to surface degradation. FBF demonstrated superior surface quality, maintaining higher GU and VHN values while exhibiting lower roughness across all time points. This behavior can be explained by its optimized filler composition (zirconia/silica and ytterbium trifluoride, 76.5 wt%), which not only contributes to increased hardness but also enhances light scattering, thereby preserving gloss. Furthermore, the strong filler–matrix bonding with Bis-GMA and UDMA may limit hydrolytic degradation at the interface, improving long-term stability. These results are in line with previous studies that attribute such performance to the optimized filler particle size, distribution, and resin matrix composition in FBF formulations [1, 4, 6]. A previous study [24] attributed the high microhardness values of FBF to the content of its filler particles (zirconia, silica, and ytterbium trifluoride) In contrast, SF, despite its high filler content (83.5 wt%), consistently showed the poorest surface outcomes, potentially due to its increased filler content and less favorable filler-matrix interface, which may render it more susceptible to microstructural changes during aging [2, 5]. The presence of EBP-DMA and reduced interfacial compatibility between the matrix and fillers may predispose the material to filler debonding and microcrack formation during thermal and hydrolytic aging. Notably, the nanohybrid composite (CME), containing silanated barium glass fillers with a broad particle size range (0.37–1.5 μm), exhibited intermediate performance, likely due to the heterogeneous filler distribution, which provides moderate hardness but a higher tendency for gloss reduction after aging. O-APS, formulated with an advanced polymerization system (APS) and UDMA matrix, showed intermediate performance, suggesting that the APS technology may not provide additional benefit in preserving surface characteristics under long-term artificial aging [2]. These findings collectively indicate that material composition, especially filler morphology and polymer network chemistry, plays a pivotal role in determining long-term surface behavior.

The polishing protocol applied significantly influenced the surface characteristics of the tested composites, corroborating previous reports that highlight the critical role of finishing procedures in clinical performance [20]. Among the polishing systems evaluated, OneGloss yielded higher microhardness values compared to OptiDisc and the Mylar strip (control), particularly after aging, while the control group consistently produced the lowest surface roughness and highest gloss values immediately post-curing. This result is consistent with findings by St-Pierre et al. [7], who demonstrated that multi-step systems such as OptiDisc provide more controlled abrasion and smoother surfaces compared to single-step approaches, although some single-step systems like OneGloss may enhance microhardness through denser matrix compaction during polishing. Moreover, Shimokawa et al. [5] reported that surface gloss can deteriorate if abrasive particles in polishing systems are not well matched to the resin composite’s filler hardness, which might explain the relatively lower gloss values observed with OneGloss. Importantly, one of the most decisive parameters influencing surface roughness is the abrasive nature of the polishing system and its application protocol. Previous studies have shown that multi-step polishing systems provide the smoothest surfaces by abrading both the organic resin matrix and the inorganic filler particles in a balanced manner [7]. Lu et al. [25] further emphasized that aluminum oxide–coated discs are among the most effective systems, as the progressively finer abrasive particles in multi-step sequences eliminate scratches produced by the preceding discs, ultimately creating a highly smooth surface. In contrast, single-step systems are more prone to producing scratches when the abrasive particle size is not sufficiently fine [7].

In the present study, the interaction between polishing system and material composition appears to have played a decisive role, as materials with inherently smoother surfaces (e.g., FBF) were less affected by the type of polishing procedure. At both T1 and T2 time points, the lowest microhardness values were recorded in CME specimens finished with the Mylar strip. Consistent with these findings, Yıldırımoğlu et al. [26] evaluated highly esthetic resin composites (Estelite Asteria, Omnicroma, Clearfil Majesty Esthetic) polished with different systems (Mylar strip as control, Sof-Lex discs, Sof-Lex diamond spirals, Optragloss) and reported that both the control group and Clearfil Majesty Esthetic exhibited the lowest hardness values. Similarly, Erdemir et al. [27] compared the effects of Sof-Lex discs and PoGo on various resin composites (Filtek Supreme XT, Ceram-X, and Grandio) and concluded that although polishing generally improved microhardness, no statistically significant differences were observed among the systems (p > 0.05). Another study on highly esthetic resin composites (Tokuyama Asteria, Tokuyama Omnicroma, Clearfil Majesty Esthetic) confirmed that polishing procedures increased microhardness regardless of the system used [26]. In line with these observations, Alfawaz [28] examined three polishing methods (Mylar strip as control, single-step PoGo, and multi-step Sof-Lex) on two different resin composites (Z350 XT and Ceram-X), and reported that the control group consistently showed lower hardness compared with the polished groups, while no significant differences were found between the polishing systems. These findings support the notion that both the abrasiveness and elasticity of polishing systems should be carefully selected in harmony with the composite’s filler-resin characteristics to optimize esthetic and mechanical longevity. Therefore, from a clinical perspective, multi-step polishing systems such as OptiDisc are recommended for nanohybrid and bulk-fill resin composites as they provide superior surface smoothness and gloss retention, whereas single-step systems may be sufficient for esthetic microhybrid resin composites that are less susceptible to surface degradation.

Artificial aging procedures demonstrated a significant impact on the surface characteristics of resin composites, with the extent and nature of degradation varying across aging protocols [29]. In the current study, 300 h of UV aging [23] and 10.000 thermocycling procedures between 5 °C and 55 °C were applied to the materials to simulate 1 year of clinical aging [22]. Thermocycling caused the most pronounced deterioration in gloss and microhardness, likely due to repetitive contraction–expansion stress cycles that damage the resin–filler interface—a mechanism well-documented in previous studies [30]. In contrast, UV aging led to a notable increase in surface roughness, particularly in SF and CME specimens, while unexpectedly preserving or slightly increasing microhardness in certain subgroups. This phenomenon may be attributed to post-curing polymer network densification under prolonged UV exposure, as previously observed by Unalan Degirmenci et al. [16] and Fidan & Dereli [31]. The distilled water storage protocol caused the least adverse effects, confirming earlier findings that passive water sorption alone is less aggressive than photothermal stressors [5]. Furthermore, the interaction between aging procedures and resin composite composition was crucial. Resin composites with hydrophobic matrices and optimized filler-resin coupling (e.g.. FBF) exhibited greater resistance to aging-induced degradation, likely due to reduced water absorption and improved network integrity [6, 16]. These findings underscore the clinical importance of considering not only the initial surface properties but also the long-term aging behavior of restorative materials when selecting products for high-stress intraoral environments.

Sequential evaluation of the resin composites at baseline (T0), after polishing (T1), and post-aging (T2) revealed material- and procedure-specific surface alterations. GU values showed a significant decline after polishing, particularly in groups where abrasive instruments disrupted the resin-rich superficial layer formed by Mylar strips. This finding supports the conclusions of Janus et al. [32], who noted that the unpolished surface initially appears smooth but lacks mechanical integrity due to a higher organic content. Surface roughness increased markedly following polishing, with one-step systems producing rougher surfaces compared to multi-step systems—a result consistent with the work of St-Pierre et al. [7], who demonstrated that one-step polishers generate more irregular topographies and less gloss. As aging progressed (T2), thermocycling and UV exposure further exacerbated surface degradation. These changes likely reflect hydrolytic degradation and thermal fatigue at the matrix–filler interface, as extensively described in the literature on resin composite durability [15]. Interestingly, while a general decrease in microhardness was expected post-aging, some resin composite–aging combinations (e.g., FBF under UV) exhibited marginal increases. This may be due to light-induced post-curing and polymer densification effects, as previously observed by Alshali et al. [33], who reported improved crosslinking in composites following prolonged UV exposure. Such responses highlight the complex interplay between environmental stressors and resin composite formulation over time.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was incorporated into this study to provide high-resolution qualitative insights into the surface morphology of resin composites after exposure to various polishing and aging conditions. The AFM findings are in line with recent studies. Kim et al. [34] showed that thermomechanical cycling induces pronounced surface degradation—characterized by microcracking and filler dislodgement—in CAD/CAM composite materials, matching the AFM observations in SonicFill and Opus APS after thermocycling aging. Similarly, UV aging has been reported to oxidize the resin matrix, leading to surface embrittlement and roughness [35]; this aligns with the prominent topographic changes seen in UV-aged, OneGloss-polished specimens. Conversely, AFM confirmed that Filtek Bulk‑Fill retained a smoother, more homogeneous surface even after aging, supporting findings by the previous study indicating that bulk-fill resin composites with optimal filler distribution exhibit superior structural stability under stress [36]. Finally, polishing with OneGloss consistently resulted in more irregular surface topographies, a result also reported in recent evaluations of reduced-step systems [13]. In summary, AFM not only validated the quantitative data from gloss and roughness measurements but also provided high-resolution confirmation of material- and procedure-specific surface degradation, reinforcing its utility in comprehensive in-vitro analysis.

The correlation analysis demonstrated a strong inverse relationship between surface roughness and gloss at all time points, consistent with recent evidence showing that smoother surfaces provide more uniform light reflection and thus retain higher gloss levels. Szczepaniak et al. [37] confirmed this link by showing that polishing systems producing lower Ra values significantly preserve gloss on nanocomposites. This underscores the necessity for meticulous polishing protocols to ensure long-lasting esthetic outcomes. Conversely, the observed correlation between surface roughness and microhardness was weak, indicating that increased surface irregularities are not directly predictive of changes in material hardness. A previous study [38] noted that microhardness remains relatively unaffected by surface-level degradation, even when Ra increases materially after aging. Similarly, a previous study found that although roughness increased significantly, microhardness values remained stable across different resin composite–polishing combinations [39]. Clinically, these findings imply that while gloss and surface smoothness are tightly coupled and crucial for maintaining immediate esthetic quality, microhardness—a sub-surface property—does not necessarily decline alongside surface roughness. Nevertheless, roughness exceeding 0.2 µm remains a risk factor for bacterial adhesion and staining, reinforcing the clinical imperative of achieving smooth surfaces even if hardness is unaffected [40].

Despite the comprehensive nature of this in-vitro investigation, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of intraoral conditions such as enzymatic activity, pH fluctuations, salivary proteins, and mechanical wear limits the generalizability of the findings to clinical scenarios. Although artificial aging procedures such as thermocycling and UV exposure are widely accepted surrogates for simulating oral degradation, they do not fully replicate the complex biochemical interactions present in-vivo. Moreover, the specimens used were flat and standardized, which does not mimic the anatomical curvature or occlusal load distribution of actual restorations. Another limitation lies in the short-term evaluation of aging effects. While the protocols applied simulate approximately one year of clinical function, long-term degradation patterns, particularly in terms of filler leaching or deeper matrix breakdown, remain unexplored. Additionally, the study was confined to a limited number of polishing systems and resin composite types, potentially excluding alternative materials or techniques that may perform differently under similar conditions. Future studies should incorporate in-situ or clinical follow-up models to validate the current findings under functional and microbiologically active environments. The integration of multi-modal aging, including brushing abrasion, pH cycling, and biofilm exposure, would also enhance ecological validity.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in-vitro study, the surface properties of resin composites were significantly influenced by material type, polishing protocol, and aging procedure. FBF consistently showed superior gloss, microhardness, and smoothness across all conditions, whereas SF exhibited the most pronounced degradation. CME demonstrated moderate results, while O-APS, despite its advanced polymerization system, provided no clear long-term advantage. Mylar strips produced the smoothest initial surfaces, but clinically relevant systems such as OptiDisc achieved better gloss retention, and OneGloss offered a faster, acceptable alternative with improved hardness. Aging procedures, particularly thermocycling and UV exposure, reduced gloss and increased roughness, with material-dependent effects on hardness. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that bulk-fill resin composites such as FBF may be preferred where long-term esthetic and mechanical stability are critical, while SF may require additional polishing or maintenance. Overall, disc-based systems outperformed single-step rubbers in gloss preservation, underscoring the need to align composite selection with appropriate finishing protocols to ensure durable esthetic and functional outcomes.
