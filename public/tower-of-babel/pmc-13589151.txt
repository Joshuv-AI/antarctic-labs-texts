Dietary betaine alleviates corticosterone-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying hens possibly by promoting tight junction protein expression via m6A methylation-mediated inhibition of the NF-κB pathway
Authors: Yulin Wu, Fanchi Meng, Yuxin Yang, Chunyu Cao, Yangyang Hu, Xuanze Ling, Liyang Zhang, Xiaoyan Cui, Tingting Li, Shengchen Wang, Xugang Luo, Yun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive corticosterone (CORT) exposure impairs intestinal barrier function in laying hens by decreasing tight junction (TJ) proteins expression, whereas betaine has been known to exert protective effects on gut health. However, it is unknown whether and how betaine can mitigate intestinal barrier dysfunction caused by CORT in laying hens. A total of 72 laying hens were randomly allocated to three groups: control (CON), CORT, and CORT+betaine groups. The duration of the experiment was 35 d. Chickens in the CON and CORT groups received only the basal diet, whereas those in the CORT+betaine group were provided with the same diet plus 0.1% betaine throughout the 35-d period. Beginning on d 28 of the study, the CORT and CORT+betaine groups were treated with subcutaneous CORT (4 mg/kg BW daily for 7 d), and the CON group was handled identically but received the solvent vehicle in place of CORT. The results showed that CORT injection elevated plasma diamine oxidase (DAO) activity and endotoxin concentration. This was accompanied by the downregulated mRNA or protein abundances of Claudin-1 (CLDN1), Occludin (OCLN), JAMA, ZO-1, and Claudin-5 (CLDN5) in the duodenum; CLDN1, OCLN, JAMA, ZO-1, ZO-2, and Claudin-3 (CLDN3) in the jejunum; and CLDN1, OCLN, JAMA, ZO-1, and CLDN5 in the ileum. Betaine supplementation remitted (P< 0.05) CORT-induced partial downregulation of TJ proteins abundances in small intestine. Moreover, betaine supplementation remitted (P< 0.05) CORT-induced activation of the Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) subunit p65 signaling pathway, which coincided with modifications of RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation on p65 mRNA. Furthermore, chromatin immunoprecipitation assays revealed that betaine suppressed the CORT-induced increase in p65 binding to the promoter regions of CLDN1 and OCLN. These results indicate that dietary betaine alleviates intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying hens, possibly through enhanced TJ protein expression associated with modulation of m6A methylation and inhibition of the NF-κB pathway.

Introduction

In modern intensive farming, the rapid development of the poultry industry has exposed laying hens to various stressors, such as high temperature, high stocking density, and imbalanced feed composition, which can trigger stress responses (Bilal et al., 2021). Stress prompts the synthesis and release of glucocorticoids from the adrenal cortex (Huang et al., 2024). Corticosterone (CORT) is the main glucocorticoid and a critical systemic stress biomarker in chickens. Chronic stress or prolonged CORT treatment can lead to disruption of the intestinal barrier (Vandana et al., 2021; Li et al., 2026). Impairment of the intestinal barrier function compromised nutrient absorption, induced inflammation and gut dysbiosis, and ultimately impaired the productive performance of laying hens, leading to substantial economic losses (Gao et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024).

The intestinal mucosal barrier is primarily composed of physical, chemical, microbial, and immune barriers (Hao et al., 2022). Among them, the physical barrier is integral to the maintenance of the intestinal mucosal barrier, serving as the foundation for the proper functioning of the other barriers. It not only selectively absorbs nutrients, electrolytes, and water but also effectively blocks the intrusion of harmful substances such as pathogens and foreign antigens into the intestinal cavity (Zhang et al., 2020). Tight junction (TJ) proteins, which serve as the core components of the physical barrier, are present at the apical aspect of epithelial cells and include claudins (CLDN), junctional adhesion molecule A (JAMA), and Occludin (OCLN). They contribute critically to maintaining cell polarity and controlling mucosal permeability through their interactions with zonula occludens (ZO) proteins (Heinemann and Schuetz, 2019; Liu et al., 2023a). The disruption of TJ structure impairs intestinal mucosal integrity, increases intestinal permeability, and ultimately allows endotoxin to translocate from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream (Ivanov, 2012; Shu et al., 2023). A prior study revealed that chronic water avoidance stress impaired intestinal barrier function, partly due to decreased TJ proteins expression (Zong et al., 2019). Furthermore, it has previously been reported that chronic stress suppressed TJ proteins expression and was intimately linked to the activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway (Zhao et al., 2023). Therefore, screening for dietary additives that can effectively inhibit NF-κB signaling pathway and promote the expression of TJ proteins under stress conditions, thereby alleviating intestinal barrier dysfunction, is of paramount importance.

Betaine is a naturally occurring quaternary ammonium alkaloid that functions as a dietary supplement, offering osmoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant benefits (Alagawany et al., 2022). Prior researches have demonstrated that betaine can ameliorate intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by lipopolysaccharide or heat stress via enhancing TJ proteins expression (Shin et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020). Furthermore, Zhao et al. (2022a) reported that betaine supplementation could mitigate the downregulation of OCLN and ZO-1 expression induced by dextran sulfate sodium by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. However, how betaine affects the NF-κB signaling pathway remains unclear. Prior studies have demonstrated that betaine can affect DNA methylation through the one-carbon metabolic pathway and thereby regulate the expression of related genes at the transcriptional level (Idriss et al., 2017, 2020). Notably, one-carbon metabolism is involved not only in supplying methyl groups for DNA methylation but also in regulating RNA methylation modification. Yang et al. (2025) demonstrated that supplemental betaine suppressed SREBP1 protein expression by enhancing the m6A methylation levels within the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of SREBP1 mRNA in the liver of hens. Wu et al. (2025b) found that betaine enhanced overall hepatic mRNA m6A methylation levels in mice and specifically increased the m6A abundance on Trub2 mRNA, thereby enhancing its mRNA stability and increasing its protein abundance. It is noteworthy that the NF-κB signaling pathway has been confirmed to be regulated by m6A methylation (Ruan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). In previous experiments from the same batch, it has been found that betaine could alleviate CORT-induced hepatic cholesterol deposition in laying hens via DNA 5-methylcytosine (5mC) methylation-mediated regulation of cholesterol metabolism genes (Wu et al., 2024); at the same time, it could also decrease hepatic triglyceride accumulation by modulating the expression of fatty acid metabolism genes through DNA 5mC and RNA m6A methylation (Yang et al., 2025). However, whether betaine can relieve CORT-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying hens by modulating the NF-κB pathway through m6A methylation remains unclear.

We hypothesize that betaine could ameliorate CORT-induced impairment of intestinal barrier function in laying hens through m6A methylation-dependent modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Hence, this study sought to evaluate the effect of dietary betaine on plasma endotoxin content and diamine oxidase (DAO) activity, mRNA and protein abundances of relevant TJ proteins, p65 in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum, and the enrichments of p65 binding to the DNA promoter regions of these TJ proteins, and the m6A modification level of p65 in the jejunum of laying hens exposed to CORT.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All protocols for animal experiments received ethical approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University, Yangzhou, China (permit number: SYXK(Su)2021-0027).

Experiment design, animals and diets

The laying hens and diets used in this experiment were the same as those used in our previous study (Wu et al., 2024). Briefly, seventy-two Rugao yellow-feathered laying hens (130 days old, weighing 1.25 ± 0.01 kg on average) were sourced from the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Sciences, Yangzhou. A completely randomized design was used to assign the chickens to three treatment groups: control (CON), CORT, and CORT+betaine. Each treatment included eight replicate cages, with three hens per cage. The experiment lasted 35 d. Birds in the CON and CORT groups were fed the basal diet without betaine, and those in the CORT+betaine group received the same diet with 0.1% betaine supplementation, all for 35 d. And birds in the CORT and CORT+betaine groups were subcutaneously administered CORT (C2505, Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 4 mg/kg BW during the last 7 d of the experiment. At the same time, an equivalent volume of the vehicle was administered to those in the CON group.

Sample collections and preparations

At 165 d of age, all chickens were fasted for 12 h, while water was provided ad libitum. Fasting for 12 h prior to sampling, which is a common procedure in such studies (Sarker et al., 2025), was aimed at standardizing the metabolic status of all chickens, thereby reducing intragroup variability, enhancing comparability among treatment groups, and preventing feed components from interfering with subsequent assays. In this study, since all chickens were fasted synchronously under identical conditions, the resulting variation was a systematic factor that did not significantly affect the detection of intergroup differences. From each replicate cage, two chickens with average BW were selected and anesthetized via wing-vein injection of propofol (10 mg/kg BW). Blood samples were then drawn from the wing vein into vacuum tubes containing heparin. Plasma separated from the centrifuged blood samples was used to determine DAO activity and endotoxin level. Afterward, the chickens underwent cervical dislocation for euthanasia, followed by the dissection and isolation of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. After rinsing with ice-cold physiological saline to remove intestinal contents, the mucosa was scraped using sterile glass slides. The harvested mucosal tissues were placed into sterile centrifuge tubes and frozen at −80°C pending further assays. For each replicate cage, equal portions of all samples taken from the two chickens were composited into a single sample for later analysis.

Determination of DAO activity and endotoxin level in plasma

Measurements of plasma DAO activity (BC1285, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and endotoxin level (EC80545S, Bioendo, Xiamen, China) were performed with commercial assay kits following the manufacturers’ guidelines.

RT-qPCR

The isolation of total RNA from mucosal tissues was achieved with TRIzol reagent. Following the determination of RNA concentration with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, 2 μg of RNA were subjected to reverse transcription with a reagent kit obtained from Vazyme (R223-01, Nanjing, China) to synthesize cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed using diluted cDNA, gene-specific primers, and the AceQ SYBR Green master mix (Q412-02; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Relative mRNA abundances were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), with 18S rRNA serving as the endogenous control for data normalization. Western blotting

The isolation of total protein from mucosal tissues was achieved by homogenization with RIPA buffer. Following centrifugation of the homogenates, the supernatants were harvested and their protein content was assessed via Beyotime’s BCA protein assay kit (P0011, Shanghai, China). Protein samples were adjusted to a uniform concentration of 4 mg/mL, and then 10 μL (equivalent to 40 µg of total protein) were loaded per lane. Proteins were separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (ET10010Gel, ACE, Changzhou, China) using the MOPS-SDS running buffer provided with the precast gel kit. Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for 30 min, after which the voltage was increased to 120 V for an additional 60 min. The separated proteins were then electrotransferred onto a PVDF membrane (E801-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) via wet transfer in Towbin buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol) at a constant current of 200 mA for 60 min. After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were then exposed overnight at 4°C to the primary antibodies as specified below: CLDN1 (Wanlei, WL03073; 1:1000), OCLN (Invitrogen, 331500; 1:1000), NF-κB p65 (Affinity, AF5006; 1:1000), phosphorylated-NF-κB p65 (p-p65) (CST, #3031, 1:1000) and β-tubulin (Proteintech, 10094-1-AP, 1:10000). Following washing, the membranes were exposed to the secondary antibody for a duration of 2 h. Visualization and subsequent quantification of protein bands were performed using the ECL system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) and Tanon Gis 1D software, respectively. β-Tubulin served as the internal control in this research.

Select assay for site-specific m6A modification levels

The NF-κB p65 mRNA sequence was analyzed using the SRAMP tool, revealing a high-confidence m6A modification peak near the 3′UTR region. The adenosine (A) at this site was designated as the X-site. For internal reference, another A residue with no significant m6A modification potential was selected and named the N-site. Upstream and downstream probes (Up/Down Probe) were designed specifically for these two sites. Total RNA was combined with these probes, dNTP (N0447S, NEB), and CutSmart buffer (B7204S, NEB), and then subjected to annealing and cross-linking. The annealed product was incubated at 40 °C with an enzyme mixture containing Bst 2.0 DNA polymerase (M0537S), SplintR ligase (M0375S), and ATP (P0756L), all sourced from NEB. It was then denatured and stored at 4 °C. Finally, the ligated DNA from the up and down probes was used as the template for RT-qPCR. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were carried out in accordance with previously established protocols (Hu et al., 2018). Briefly, frozen jejunal tissues (300 mg) were crushed under liquid nitrogen and subsequently resuspended in PBS buffer mixed with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat# 11697498001, USA). The samples underwent a 10-min exposure to 1% formaldehyde to achieve cross-linking, and the reaction was subsequently quenched by adding 2.5 M glycine for 10 min. After rinsing the pellets with PBS, they were homogenized in SDS lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The chromatin was then fragmented by sonication to achieve sizes between 300 and 700 base pairs. Following removal of non-specific binders using salmon sperm DNA/protein A agarose beads, the sonicated chromatin was combined with 2 μg of an antibody targeting NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, Cat# 80979-1-RR) and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Normal IgG was employed as a negative control. Immune complexes were pulled down using 40 μL of 50% protein A/G agarose beads. The beads were then heated at 65 °C for 5 h to reverse cross-linking, freeing the DNA fragments for subsequent purification. The purified DNA served as the RT-qPCR template for quantifying chicken CLDN1 and OCLN fragments, employing the specific primers listed in Table 1.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using the GLM procedure in SAS 9.4. Significant differences were identified via the LSD test. Each replicate functioned as an individual experimental unit, and statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Discussion

In laying hen production, various stressors such as mycotoxins, oxidized diets, and high temperatures can cause intestinal barrier dysfunction, an effect mediated by the suppression of tight junction proteins expression and the elevation of intestinal permeability (Basiouni et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2026). While our previous studies have demonstrated the hepatoprotective effects of betaine under stress conditions, its potential to alleviate stress-induced intestinal dysfunction remains unclear. This study suggests that dietary betaine alleviates intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying hens, possibly through enhanced TJ protein expression associated with modulation of m6A methylation and inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. These findings confirm our hypothesis and provide a scientific foundation for using it as a dietary supplement to combat stress-induced intestinal barrier function dysfunction in poultry production.

Previous studies have shown that, in both the hyperuricemia mouse model and the L-arginine-induced acute pancreatitis mouse model, when intestinal barrier integrity is compromised, increased intestinal permeability often occurs, resulting in the appearance of DAO and endotoxin in the circulation (Guo et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2024). In addition, based on a mouse model of colitis induced by dextran sulfate sodium, Liu et al. (2023b) proposed that the plasma DAO activity and endotoxin level could serve as indicators of the extent of intestinal mucosal dysfunction and increased permeability. Moreover, both chronic unpredictable mild stress in mice and long-term multiple nonsocial stress in rats have been shown to impair intestinal barrier function, as evidenced by increased DAO activity and elevated endotoxin levels (Wang et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2023). Supplementation with betaine has been found to mitigate the heat stress-induced rise in intestinal permeability, as well as the elevated DAO activity and endotoxin content in broilers (Alhotan et al., 2021). The present study demonstrated that CORT administration led to increased plasma DAO activity and elevated endotoxin level, while dietary betaine supplementation mitigated the rise in plasma DAO activity and endotoxin content in laying hens. Together with previous studies, this study further demonstrated that dietary betaine could partially alleviate markers of intestinal barrier dysfunction.

The maintenance and regulation of intestinal barrier function depend on the dynamic balance of intestinal epithelial TJ proteins and their interactions with intracellular signaling molecules (Amasheh et al., 2002). Yumoto et al. (2025) definitively demonstrated that intestinal epithelial-specific deletion of OCLN increased gut permeability and impaired survival during sepsis in mice. Saeedi et al. (2015) reported that hypoxia-inducible factor1β depletion driven CLDN1 downregulation directly caused barrier defects in T84 cells. Importantly, this barrier defect was rescued by CLDN1 overexpression. Collectively, the above studies confirmed that reduced expression of tight junction proteins is a key driver of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Previous study has shown that chronic heat stress impaired intestinal barrier integrity in broilers, which was manifested as reduced expression of CLDN1, OCLN, and ZO-1 (Shaukat et al., 2025). In this research, CORT treatment significantly downregulated mRNA and/or protein expression of CLDN1, OCLN, JAMA, ZO-1, and CLDN5 in the duodenum; CLDN1, OCLN, JAMA, ZO-1, ZO-2, and CLDN3 in the jejunum; and CLDN1, OCLN, JAMA, ZO-1, and CLDN5 in the ileum of laying hens, and this downregulation was associated with impaired intestinal barrier function. Furthermore, Wu et al. (2020) demonstrated that dietary betaine attenuated the LPS-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by increasing the protein abundances of CLDN1 and OCLN in IPEC-J2 cells. Consistent with earlier work, the present study showed that dietary betaine alleviated the CORT-induced downregulation of mRNA or protein abundances of CLDN1 and OCLN in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum of hens. Notably, the effects of CORT and betaine were less pronounced in the ileum than in the duodenum and jejunum. For instance, in the ileum, mRNA expression levels of p65, ZO-2, and CLDN3 were unaffected by treatment, and betaine did not restore OCLN and ZO-1 to control levels. These differential impacts on TJ protein mRNA abundance across intestinal segments may reflect tissue and gene specificity.

In chronic restraint stress-induced mouse models, a previous study found that chronic stress can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is associated with reduced expression of TJ proteins (Zhao et al., 2023). As examined by western blot in this study, CORT administration resulted in increased both p65 and p‑p65 protein abundances in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum of laying hens, suggesting activation of the NF-κB p65 pathway. Furthermore, it has previously been reported that betaine could inhibit the NF-κB signaling (Shi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022b; Zhang and Jia, 2023). Consistent with these findings, in the present study, supplemental betaine mitigated the CORT-induced upregulation of both p65 and p‑p65 protein abundance. Previous study has indicated that p65 can act as a transcription factor to regulate the expression of TJ proteins (Wu et al., 2025a). In the present study, ChIP assays demonstrated that CORT injection significantly enhanced the binding of p65 to the promoter regions of CLDN1 and OCLN in the jejunum, which was associated with reduced expression of these two TJ proteins. In contrast, dietary supplementation with betaine significantly suppressed this binding. Therefore, the NF-κB signaling pathway may serve as a key regulator of TJ protein expression, representing an important therapeutic target for chronic stress-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction. However, the specific mechanisms by which betaine regulates the NF-κB pathway remain poorly understood.

Numerous studies have established that the biological functions of betaine are driven by its role as a methyl donor in epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA and RNA methylation (Yan et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Among them, 5mC stands out as one of the most prevalent forms of DNA methylation and is instrumental in regulating gene expression, primarily at the transcriptional level (Liu et al., 2025). RNA m6A methylation, a prevalent and extensively investigated RNA modification, operates primarily at the post-transcriptional level, controlling gene expression by modulating mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and subcellular localization (Sendinc and Shi, 2023). In this study, the treatment with either betaine or CORT did not affect the transcript level of p65, but significantly modulated its protein expression in the jejunum and ileum, suggesting that this process might be regulated at the post-transcriptional level. Moreover, m6A methylation has been reported to regulate the post-transcriptional expression of NF-κB p65 (Zhang et al., 2025). In this work, CORT significantly reduced the m6A methylation level near the 3′UTR region of p65 mRNA. This reduction was effectively counteracted by betaine treatment. Existing evidence indicated that m6A modifications occurring near the 3′UTR predominantly influence gene expression by modulating mRNA transcript stability (Aluru and Karchner, 2021). Based on this mechanism, the observed reduction in CORT-induced upregulation of p65 protein expression by betaine in this study may be attributed to increased m6A methylation around the 3′UTR, leading to decreased stability of p65 mRNA.

Conclusion

Dietary betaine supplementation mitigates CORT-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying hens possibly through the promotion of TJ protein expression via RNA m6A methylation-mediated suppression of the NF-κB pathway.
