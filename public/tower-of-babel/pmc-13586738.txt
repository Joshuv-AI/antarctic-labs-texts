In Memory of Professor Charles Mayaud
Authors: Muriel Fartoukh
Published: 2026

We have lost a remarkable physician, a great teacher, and, for many of us, a mentor and a friend.

Charles Mayaud, Professor of Pulmonology and Head of the Pulmonology and Intensive Care Department at Tenon Hospital, Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris, Sorbonne University, was a man of light. Brilliant, curious and demanding, he had an extraordinary mind and an endless appetite for medicine, science and life. He worked hard, thought deeply, questioned everything, and never gave up easily. He was rigorous and proud of what he believed in.

He built bridges within medicine. He instinctively brought together clinicians, scientists and different medical subspecialties, convinced that the best ideas often emerge when boundaries disappear.

He played an active role in the early stages of the AIDS epidemic in France. As early as 1982, he was part of the first group of French physicians to confront this then-unknown disease and co-authored a paper in The Lancet—one of the first French accounts of opportunistic infections in a patient suffering from what would later be identified as AIDS. A few years later, he contributed to major research in pulmonary immunopathology, notably a study published in Nature demonstrating the presence of HIV-specific cytotoxic lymphocytes in the lungs. This dual approach—starting with the patient and then seeking to understand the mechanisms of the disease—remained a constant throughout his career. His expertise in pulmonary infections affecting immunocompromised patients led him to play a significant role in organizing the French national response to HIV care. He served on the expert panels for the Dormont and subsequently the Delfraissy reports, notably leading the 1996 workshop dedicated to the prophylaxis of opportunistic infections. He also participated in numerous projects conducted under the auspices of the ANRS, both in France and in resource-limited countries. For many years, Pneumocystis pneumonia, tuberculosis, bacterial infections, and respiratory complications associated with immunosuppression were among his primary areas of expertise. He also contributed to the researches on severe community-acquired pneumonia, life-threatening hemoptysis, drug-induced lung diseases, pulmonary immunopathology, and thoracic oncology. He left a profound mark on generations of pulmonologists, intensivists, infectious disease specialists, internists, and physicians across all subspecialties through his "Admirable Intelligence" when faced with complex clinical cases.

Above all, he was open, open to people, to ideas, to other countries and other cultures. From Paris to Cambodia, he built bridges, shared knowledge and created lasting friendships. From the 1990s onwards, he contributed to training generations of pulmonologists, structuring hospital departments, and developing clinical research there. The CAMELIA program, focused on the care of patients co-infected with HIV and tuberculosis, garnered particular international acclaim.

He had an inexhaustible desire to learn, to understand and to transmit. His interests remained remarkably broad throughout his life, driven by a true appetite for concepts; but concepts always rooted in clinical experience, observation and intuition.

Charles taught us much more than medicine. He taught us how to look at a patient, how to think, how to question, and how to remain curious. He shared his knowledge generously and believed deeply in passing it on to others. Many of us will carry with us something of the “Mayaud method”—a blend of rigor, curiosity, reasoning, and humanity.

We will remember his intelligence, his energy, his laughter, his determination, his immense curiosity, and the light he brought into our lives. We will never forget his unpretentious nature, his humor, and the joy he took in the department’s social gatherings, where his wonderful speeches could move us from deep emotion to uproarious laughter

Charles will be deeply missed.

Our thoughts are with Anne, his children, his grandchildren, and all those who loved him.

On behalf of the many physicians from several subspecialties who had the privilege of learning from him.
