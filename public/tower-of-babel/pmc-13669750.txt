Putting the mouth back into the body
Authors: Andrew Duncombe
Published: 2026

Throughout their history, the professions of Medicine and Dentistry have remained distinct. Recent insights into the relationship between gum disease, the oral microbiome and physical health suggest that this dichotomy is no longer valid. All physicians now need to be aware of the impact of oral health on organ systems and systemic disease. So I am delighted to welcome Jasmine Ho as our Guest Editor for this themed issue on oral and dental health. Jasmine is extremely well placed to address the issues around this dichotomy having trained both as an oral and maxillofacial surgeon as well as a dentist.

Shockingly, the commonest cause of accident and emergency attendance in children is dental disease. Much of this could be prevented on a population wide basis by equitable access to preventative dentistry. Yet access to NHS dentistry in the UK is increasingly difficult. NHS directory tracking data show that only around one third of NHS dental practices in England are currently accepting new patients with the BBC reporting this year that hundreds of people in Bristol had been forced to queue for hours just to register with an NHS dentist in the city. I strongly commend Jasmine’s guest editorial in which she introduces our expert contributors and discusses these issues in more depth.

Elsewhere in this issue, we demonstrate the breadth of content of Future Healthcare Journal with submitted articles on multimorbidity, the use of artificial intelligence in pharmacy services, attitudes to voluntary assisted dying, the invisibility of digital clinical work, making diagnostic doubt visible, assessing efficacy of cash releasing interventions in healthcare and how to lead healthcare innovation.

Following on from our themed issue in March of this year on topics related to end-of-life care, we feature a potentially relevant article. Although the recent private members bill to introduce voluntary assisted dying in England failed through time constraints, there is no doubt that this issue remains active and polarising and it seems likely that further attempted legislation will be put forward. Over their career, the group of clinicians who are most likely to be affected by this are currently medical students, so the national study of medical student attitudes to voluntary assisted dying published here1 is extremely timely. This national survey of nearly 1,000 medical students conducted in 2025 assessed both their ethical views as well as their confidence and education in this area. Nearly two-thirds believed that assisted dying could be ethically justified but they reported both minimal knowledge and lack of formal education around the subject. Concerns about coercion and inequalities were common. The authors conclude that should such legislation be passed in the future, there will need to be structured education programs starting at undergraduate level.

With increasing healthcare demand being driven by multimorbidity, the multidisciplinary paper from Alison Mackay et al2 about creating a system architecture for these patients is prescient. They used a systems engineering process to derive stakeholder needs from multimorbidity case studies. Their model places patients at the centre of the design process considering both their physical and mental needs and involving all those responsible for service provision. They suggest that a delivery engine of integrated multidisciplinary teams is better able to provide holistic and personalised care than the current system of diagnosis-based pathways and services.

Pharmacy services would seem to be an appropriate area for integration of artificial intelligence (AI) systems. Al-Taie et al3 present a PRISMA compliant scoping review of studies published in the last 15 years where AI has been utilised. They found that most pharmacy studies used AI for detection of drug interactions and adverse effects as well as looking for medication errors and inappropriate medications. However, they caution that further research is needed to demonstrate the added value that AI could bring to pharmacy services.

Given the unprecedented financial pressures facing the NHS, the importance of cash releasing interventions has been stressed by government. Authors from the health economics unit in London4 provide an evidence review of the efficacy of this strategy over the last seven years focusing on delivery against the Government’s three key health policy shifts viz: the desired transitions from treatment to prevention, hospital to community, and analogue to digital. They found that of nearly 200 eligible papers, no sources contained enough information to determine whether they were actually cash releasing by a strict definition of the term though many demonstrated potential to do so. They highlight that the potential cash savings and cost efficiencies described in many papers could only be realised if other permanent changes to inpatient capacity, staffing and commissioning were actioned.

Do you know how all the work that you do remotely or virtually is counted by healthcare systems? The answer, according to the interesting opinion piece by Al Fannah,5 is that it largely isn’t. The author describes neatly that performance measurement frameworks were designed on the assumption that medical work happens in a room at a specific time with a patient physically present. The revolution in healthcare delivery that was accelerated by Covid means this is clearly no longer appropriate and greatly underestimates the work carried out. The author’s thesis is that even if some digital work is counted, it is not done equitably and even when done equitably, it’s rarely connected to the outcomes. It gives rise to a healthcare system that appears busy but fails to capture the outcomes it aims to deliver. The paper makes a compelling case that we must redesign the way we count healthcare work for the digital age.

Another thought-provoking opinion piece is provided by Chel et al,6 about making diagnostic doubt more visible in healthcare. We all encounter patients on a regular basis that present with debilitating symptoms largely without abnormal test results for whom we struggle to find a diagnostic category. While terms such as medically unexplained symptoms, persistent physical symptoms, or system-based disorder have been adopted by clinicians in these circumstances, none map to a legitimised Nomenclature of Medicine such as the ICD classification. The authors argue that an official classification of ‘uncertain diagnosis’ would capture this cohort more effectively, recognise the uncertainty surrounding these conditions and keep therapeutic options open while stimulating our further curiosity to better understand these conditions. We could perhaps then be more comfortable telling patients ‘I don’t know yet’.

Janjua et al7 remind us that innovation alone does not transform healthcare. People do. Through the example of modern cataract surgery, the authors demonstrate how two individuals created a new global standard of care. Using a metaphor for change as the difficulties to be overcome in crossing a canyon, they utilise Kotter’s eight-step model to demonstrate this. These are: creating a sense of urgency, building a guiding coalition, forming a strategic vision, engaging others, removing barriers, showing proof of principle, sustaining acceleration and formalising professional and institutional guidance.
