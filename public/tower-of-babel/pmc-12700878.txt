Virological characterization of SARS-CoV-2 BA.2.86 variants by assessing antiviral susceptibility, in vivo infectivity and replicative fitness
Authors: Emilie Giraud, Géraldine Piorkowski, Jean-Sélim Driouich, Ornéllie Bernadin, Cyrielle Durand, Magali Gilles, Rayane Amaral, Sylvie van der Werf, Xavier de Lamballerie, Fabrice Agou, Antoine Nougairède, Franck Touret
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn late 2023, the BA.2.86 and EG.5.1 SARS-CoV-2 variants co-circulated globally, raising concerns about enhanced immune evasion and altered replicative capacity. In this study, we assessed their virological characteristics using live viruses. We evaluated sensitivity to therapeutic monoclonal antibodies and antivirals targeting NSP5 and NSP12, and performed direct competition assays. BA.2.86 displayed a complete loss of neutralization by Sotrovimab, consistent with the presence of the K356T escape mutation, whereas EG.5.1 retained similar susceptibility to its XBB ancestor. Both variants remained sensitive to antiviral compounds, with no evidence of resistance-linked mutations. In vivo, BA.2.86 showed reduced replication in hamster lungs (3–4 log10 reduction) and a moderate reduction in the upper respiratory airways, compared to an ancestral reference strain (B.1 D614G). Direct in vitro competition, in primary nasal cells, further confirmed the reduced fitness of BA.2.86 relative to EG.5.1. Altogether, these results underscore the limited replicative potential of BA.2.86 despite immune escape and emphasize the value of assessing emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants using native viruses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27481-0.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Omicron, Antivirals, Replicative fitnessSubject terms: Antivirals, SARS-CoV-2, Viral evolution

Introduction

Since its emergence at the end of 2019, SARS-CoV-2, the causative agent of COVID-19 disease, responsible for a major pandemic, has been circulating continuously with no defined seasonality so far. This circulation, has been characterized since its second year by successive epidemic waves linked to replacement of the circulating strain by new ones, called variants. These had acquired several mutations, most notably in the Spike protein involved in attachment of the virus to its receptor, the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2(ACE2), and the main neutralizing antibody target. The earliest variants, such as the Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Delta variants were associated with increased viral fitness and infectivity compared to the previous strain1–4. This replicative fitness included, but was not restricted to, an enhanced affinity of the virus for its receptor, the human ACE24. A major antigenic shift occurred with the emergence in late 2021 of the first Omicron variant, a variant which combined higher immune escape with newly acquired biological properties, including increased cell tropism for the upper respiratory tract5,6. Since then, many sub-variants that directly descended from the first Omicron variants have appeared, causing massive epidemic waves worldwide. All of these Omicron sub-variants, although derived from different evolutionary strategies - including recombination - shared common biological characteristics, notably a strong ability to evade the immune response. This has contributed to enable reinfections, as well as reducing the efficacy of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies and vaccines6–10.

Unexpectedly, in late July 2023, almost a year and a half after the disappearance of its direct progenitor, one of the first Omicron variants (BA.2), a new variant BA.2.86 emerged. This variant rapidly replaced the EG.5.1 variant11 dominantly circulating at the time. Variant EG.5.1 retained most of the Spike mutations found in its XBB.1 progenitor but acquired additional substitutions such as F486P, which contribute to improved immune evasion12. In contrast, BA.2.86 attracted attention due to its unusual Spike protein profile, with 58 different amino acid changes relative to the ancestral B.1 BavPat (D614G) strain13, including 26 mutations unique to this lineage. Many of these are clustered in the N-terminal domain (NTD) and receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the Spike protein, regions critical for viral entry and immune recognition14. In particular, the BA.2.86 Spike protein exhibits key substitutions, such as K356T, R346T and S371F that have been associated with escape from neutralizing monoclonal antibodies14. While preliminary studies highlighted the antigenic divergence of BA.2.86, little was known about its replicative capacity, transmissibility or in vivo fitness, particularly in direct comparison to EG.5.1.

In the present study, we conducted a comprehensive virological characterization of the BA.2.86 variant and its descendant JN.1 using live virus isolates obtained from clinical samples. First, we performed in vitro inhibition assays to evaluate their sensitivity to therapeutic monoclonal antibodies to draw a quick escape map, but also to antiviral compounds currently used in the clinic targeting the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, NSP12) and 3CLpro (NSP5) proteins. Second, we investigated the replication dynamics and pathogenicity of BA.2.86 in vivo in the Syrian hamster model (Mesocricetus auratus), using the well-characterized ancestral B.1 strain as a reference. Finally, we evaluated the replicative fitness of BA.2.86 relative to EG.5.1 through direct competition experiments in primary human nasal epithelial cells. Our results offer new insights into the biological behavior of BA.2.86 and contribute to a better understanding of its successful emergence and spread.

Results

BA.2.86, JN.1 and EG.5.1 susceptibility to antiviral agents

Firstly, we sought to determine the biological characteristics of the variants by assessing their sensitivity to both therapeutic monoclonal antibodies that have been used clinically and antivirals targeting RdRp (NSP12) or 3CLpro (NSP5). We studied closely related Omicron strains, an early variant BA.2.86 isolated in August 2023, and a later variant JN.1, collected in late September 2023, after the lineage had begun circulating more widely and diversifying. For this analysis, we compared these with the parental strain BA.2 and the EG.5.1 variant, descendant from XBB (dominant when BA.2.86 appeared). We also added one of the first SARS-CoV-2 strains from lineage B.1 (D614G) as a reference for calculating fold change, as most of the therapeutics analysed here were originally developed against this strain. To this end, we applied a standardised protocol for the evaluation of antiviral compounds based on RNA yield reduction, that we previously implemented for the evaluation of antivirals and therapeutic monoclonal antibodies for SARS-CoV-215,16. This test enables us to swiftly incorporate any SARS-CoV-2 variant, regardless of its cytopathic effect in cell culture. While the maximum reduction in viral yield may vary depending on the specific strain and test conditions, the 50% effective concentration (EC₅₀) should remain unaffected and depend solely on the efficacy of the antiviral agent. The assay, based on authentic and replicating viruses, was performed in VeroE6 or Vero E6 TMPRSS2 cells and viral RNA in the supernatant medium was quantified by qRT-PCR at the logarithmic phase post-infection to determine the (EC50).

Regarding monoclonal antibodies, we evaluated Sotrovimab which was the last one to maintain in vitro and in vivo activity against the subvariants from the second Omicron wave15,17–19. We also added Cilgavimab from the Evusheld cocktail as it exerts in vitro and in vivo activity against BA.2, the progenitor of BA.2.8620,21. We first observed a complete loss of detectable neutralizing activity for the BA.2.86 lineage variants with Sotrovimab (vir-7831) (Table 1). This result is in line with previous reports describing the Spike K356T mutation as a Sotrovimab escape mutation17. For EG.5.1 the activity is maintained like for its progenitor XBB albeit slightly reduced compared to the ancestral strain B.1 as we previously described15. From a mechanistic perspective, the loss of activity of Cilgavimab against the BA.2.86 and JN.1.1subvariants may be attributable to the G446S mutation, which has previously been described in decreasing Cilgavimab neutralising activity. For EG.5.1 the same mutation is combined with R346T another critical residue. Moreover in the three variants, these mutations are added with other critical residues also mutated and involved in Cilgavimab binding to the RBD namely V445P/H, N440K, E484K/A22. When we examined the sensitivity of EG.5.1, BA.2.86 and JN.1 to antiviral molecules used in the clinic, we find no significant differences between strains. This result is not surprising given the absence of known mutations that could be involved in resistance on NSP523 or NSP1224 proteins.

BA.2.86 infectivity in the hamster model

Secondly, we evaluated the infectivity of the early BA.2.86 strain using a previously described Syrian hamster model25–27. As we have already described, certain Omicron variants have a reduced infection capacity in this model compared to the pre-Omicron variants28,29. Moreover it has been recently shown that BA.2.86 has similar infectivity and replication as BA.5 and EG.5.1 in the hamster model30. In order to generate new data and reduce the number of animals in line with the 3Rs principle, we evaluated BA.2.86 only in comparison with the ancestral BavPat strain (B.1), which we and others have characterized several times in this model. After infecting the hamsters, we examined three parameters: the viral load in the lungs at day-3 post-infection (dpi) (time of sacrifice), measured either as the amount of viral RNA or as the TCID50 (50% Tissue Culture Infectious Dose) infectious viral load. We also examined viral shedding in the upper airways by nasal lavage at 2 and 3 dpi with measurement of the viral RNA load (Fig. 1). With the Omicron variant BA.2.86, we observed a significant and substantial reduction in viral replication in the lungs, both in terms of viral RNA yield and infectious titres, compared with variant B.1. We recorded a decrease of around 3 and 4 logs respectively with these read-outs (Fig. 1a and b).

Interestingly, when we compared the viral excretion in the upper respiratory airway through nasal washes performed at 2 and 3 dpi, we also observed a significant reduction in viral RNA yields for BA.2.86 (Fig. 1c and d). However, this reduction was less pronounced than in the lungs, with an approximate decrease of 1 log and 1.5 log at 2 and 3 dpi respectively. These findings suggest that, relative to the ancestral B.1 variant, BA.2.86 shows less attenuation in the upper respiratory tract than in the lower respiratory tract.

BA.2.86 replicative fitness in human primary nasal cells

Finally, the replicative fitness of BA.2.86 was evaluated and compared with variant EG.5.1 using a previously described model of a human airway epithelium reconstituted (HAE) with primary nasal cells31. The epithelia were inoculated through their apical side at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 in order to simulate the natural route of infection. Subsequent monitoring was conducted between 1 and 4 dpi to observe the excretion of new virions at the apical side, with viral RNA yields (Fig. 2a) and infectious titres measured (Fig. 2b) to analyse the infection. The results obtained revealed that the viral RNA yield and its kinetic were almost similar for both variants. In contrast, infectious titres exhibited a marginal increase for EG.5.1, along with its estimated relative virion infectivity (i.e., the ratio of the number of infectious particles over the number of viral RNA copies) (Fig. 2c). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Subsequently, we conducted competition experiments, which have previously been demonstrated to be effective in detecting replicative fitness differences, particularly for pre-Omicron variants3,4,32,33. We inoculated epithelia with both viruses simultaneously, as described above, and sampled the apical side between 1 and 4 dpi. Three infection inoculum ratios (BA.2.86 : EG.5.1 10/90, 50/50 and 90/10) were tested. Deep sequencing was then utilized to estimate the proportion of each viral genome in the viral population from days 1 to 4 (expressed as the BA.2.86: EG.5.1 ratio in Fig. 2d-f). The results showed that, whatever the initial ratio, a comparable pattern was observed, in which variant BA.2.86 was outcompeted by variant EG.5.1. At an unfavourable ratio of 10:90 (Fig. 2d), the BA.2.86 variant was no longer detected from day 3. At the 50:50 ratio (Fig. 2e), EG.5.1 took over on the first day post-infection, and in the following days BA.2.86 decreased further to around 10%. In the 90:10 ratio (Fig. 2f), the BA.2.86 variant exhibited a decline from day 1 to almost 50% by day 4 post-infection. These competition data suggest a reduced replicative fitness of the BA.2.86 variant in comparison with the EG.5.1 variant within the reconstituted human nasal epithelium model.

Discussion

In this study, the susceptibility of the Omicron variant of interest BA.2.86 to antiviral agents was evaluated, and it was demonstrated that this variant evades neutralization by monoclonal antibodies previously used in patients. Furthermore, this strain, like contemporary EG.5.1 and JN.1 variants, exhibited sensitivity to direct-acting antivirals employed in clinical settings as expected due to the absence of resistance mutations. These results are in line with previous reports34,35. We also showed that BA.2.86 is able to infect the hamster model, albeit replicating to much lower viral titers than the ancestral B.1 strain. Finally, a comparison was made between BA.2.86 and the concurrent EG.5.1, in primary nasal cells, which revealed that BA.2.86 exhibits inferior replicative fitness.

The continuous emergence of new variants that evade neutralization by existing therapeutic antibodies, both marketed and under development36, has highlighted the limitations of this approach to address COVID-19 in the long term. In contrast, the use of small molecules targeting highly conserved regions that have undergone minimal change over time has proven to be effective against these new variants. As a result, these small molecules are considered to be a preferred therapeutic approach, although they do not have the characteristics suitable for long-term preventive use in the most fragile patients that could be offered by effective therapeutic antibodies. This discrepancy underscores the relative merits of therapeutic antibodies, which are well-suited for prompt responses and prevention, and small molecules, which demonstrate greater efficacy over time.

Prior to the emergence of the Omicron variant, pre-Omicron variants demonstrated a high degree of infectivity in the Syrian hamster model, leading to lung damage similar to that observed in humans25,26. This capacity was reduced since the emergence of the Omicron variants, which have demonstrated a reduced rate of replication and an attenuated pathogenicity27,29,37. This phenomenon may be attributable to changes in the virus’s biological properties, resulting in a reduced affinity for the ACE2 receptor and the use of an alternative entry pathway, along with decreased utilization of TMPRSS26,38. Indeed It has been demonstrated that the ancestral strain and the first variants employed a TMPRSS2-dependent entry mechanism39, resulting in direct fusion to the plasma membrane of the target cell. Starting with the first Omicron variant and conserved by its descendant, this entry route changed, appearing to rely less on TMPRSS2 and more on the use of cathepsin L trough endocytosis6. However, studies have shown that the BA.2.86 variant has restored the use of TMPRSS2 and the ancestral entry route22. This finding suggests that certain ancestral biological properties may have been reinstated22. Given these observations, a comparative analysis between the ancestral strain B.1 and the BA.2.86 variant was deemed appropriate to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying these biological properties. One notable observation was the similarity in the infection process in hamsters compared to previous Omicron strains18,28, suggesting that restoring the entry route does not improve the replicative ability in this hamster model nor its tropism to the lower respiratory tract as described here and in another study40. Additionally, when compared to the contemporary XBB descendant EG.5.1, the BA.2.86 variant shows a similar replication ability in the hamster model, despite having different biological properties30. These findings indicate that a variant’s capacity to cause severe infection in hamsters is independent of the entry route and ACE2 binding.

The present study also evaluated the replication ability of BA.2.86, both alone and in competition with the XBB descendant, EG.5.1, in primary nasal cells. The results demonstrated that BA.2.86 exhibited a slightly lower replication ability when compared to EG.5.1, although the difference was not statistically significant. This aligns with the previous findings, which indicated that BA.2.86 replication is neither enhanced nor drastically reduced in this tissue34,41. In contrast, under competitive conditions, BA.2.86 was systematically outperformed by EG.5.1. This observation is unexpected, as it contradicts current epidemiological data indicating that the EG.5.1 variant has been supplanted by BA.2.86 and its subvariant JN.111. In this particular case and similarly to what was observed during the transition from Delta to Omicron, the variant showing superiority over the circulating variant is not necessarily the one with higher intrinsic viral fitness, but rather the one with the optimal combination of replicative fitness and immune evasion capacity in its target population, which is the main characteristic of BA.2.86/JN.111,34,42,43. It has been demonstrated that limited viral fitness can be offset by enhanced immune escape, leading to variant replacement. This dynamic has been documented both in vivo and in real-world epidemiological settings during the Delta to Omicron BA.1 shift27. Interpretation of these observations should also take into account the fact that SARS-CoV-2 variants since B.1 have been circulating massively in the human population, which has been largely vaccinated, and that other factors, such as escape from the innate human response, may be at play. A limitation of the present study pertains to its exclusion of the infection history and immune profile of the general population, which would be necessary to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of variant replacement at the global level.

In conclusion, the comprehensive analysis of BA.2.86 primarily reveals reduced viral fitness in the primary nasal cavity. Our findings highlight the critical need to integrate phenotypic assays, animal models and real-world epidemiological and immunological data to assess the threat posed by emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants. In the case of BA.2.86, limited replicative capacity was counterbalanced by pronounced immune evasion, which alone was sufficient to drive variant replacement, underscoring a shift in selective pressures within an immunized population previously exposed to infection and/or vaccination.

Methods

EC50 determination at UVE-Marseille

One day before infection, 5 × 104 VeroE6/TMPRSS2 cells per well were seeded in 100µL of 2.5% FCS containing assay medium in 96 well culture plates. The next day, antibodies were diluted in PBS with eleven 1:2 dilutions from 5000 to 4.8 ng/ml for Sotrovimab and Cilgavimab. Then, 25µL/well of the serial dilutions of antibodies were added to the cells in triplicate. Antiviral compounds were added using the D300e dispenser (TECAN) with eight 1:2 dilutions. Then, 25µL/well of a virus mix diluted in medium was added to the wells. Each well was inoculated with 100 TCID50 of virus which corresponds here to a MOI at 0.00244. Prior to the assay it was verified for each variant that with this MOI, viruses in the cell culture supernatants were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase of viral replication at 48 h post infection45,46. Four virus control wells were supplemented with 25µL of assay medium. Plates were first incubated 15 min at room temperature and then 2 days at 37 °C prior to quantification of the viral genome by real-time RT-PCR. To do so, 100µL of viral supernatant was collected in S-Block (Qiagen) previously loaded with VXL lysis buffer containing proteinase K and RNA carrier. RNA extraction was performed using the Qiacube HT automate and the QIAamp 96 DNA kit HT following manufacturer instructions. Viral RNA was quantified by real-time RT-qPCR (GoTaq 1-step qRt-PCR, Promega) using 3.8µL of extracted RNA and 6.2µL of RT-qPCR mix and standard fast cycling parameters, i.e. 10 min at 50 °C, 2 min at 95 °C, and 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 3 s followed by 30 s at 60 °C). Quantification was provided by four 2 log serial dilutions of an appropriate T7-generated synthetic RNA standard of known quantities (102 to 108 copies/reaction). RT-qPCR reactions were performed on QuantStudio 12 K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) and analyzed using QuantStudio 12 K Flex Applied Biosystems software v1.2.3. Primers and probe sequences, which target SARS-CoV-2 N gene, were: Fw: GGCCGCAAATTGCACAAT; Rev: CCAATGCGCGACATTCC; Probe: FAM-CCCCCAGCGCTTCAGCGTTCT-BHQ1. Viral inhibition was calculated as follows: 100* (quantity mean VC- sample quantity)/ quantity mean VC. The 50% effective concentrations (EC50 compound concentration required to inhibit viral RNA replication by 50%) were determined using logarithmic interpolation after performing a nonlinear regression (log(agonist) vs. response --Variable slope (four parameters)) as previously described15,16,46. All data obtained were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (Graphpad software).

EC50 determination at Institut Pasteur-Paris

Clear bottom 384-well black plates were seeded with 3 × 103 Vero E6 cells per well in 20 µl of DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. The next day, 20 µl of serially diluted antibodies or compounds were added to each well 2 hours prior to infection. Each plate included internal controls: 0.5% DMSO, PBS (2µL) and Remdesivir (25 µM; SelleckChem). After the pre-incubation period, the virus inoculum (MOI 0.05 to 0.25 PFU/cell depending on the viral strains) was added to the cells. Prior to screening, a range of MOIs was tested for each viral variant to determine the optimal MOI for each viral strain by selecting conditions yielding the highest Z’-factor under our assay parameters. Following a one-hour adsorption at 37 °C, the supernatant was removed and replaced with 2% FBS/DMEM medium containing the individual antibodies at the indicated concentrations. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 or 3 days. Viral supernatants were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase of replication and heat-inactivated at 80 °C for 20 min. Detection of viral genomes from heat-inactivated samples was performed by RT-qPCR using the Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs) with SARS-CoV-2 specific primers targeting the N gene region (5′-TAATCAGACAAGGAACTGATTA-3′ and 5′-CGAAGGTGTGACTTCCATG-3′) and with the following cycling conditions: 55 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 1 min, for 1 cycle, followed by 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 1 min, for 40 cycles in an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 thermocycler. Data were normalized to internal negative (0%) and positive (100%) controls and expressed as percentage inhibition of viral replication. EC50 values were calculated using non-linear regression with a variable Hill slope in GraphPad Prism 10 software (Graphpad software).

HAE experiments

After a gentle wash with pre-warmed OPTI-MEM medium ( Life technologies), epithelia were infected with individual SARS-CoV-2 variants EG.5.1 or BA.2.86 or a mix of both viruses (competition experiments) on the apical side using a MOI of 0.01 as previously described3,16,31. For competition assays, ratios were calculated based on TCID50 measurement and the final MOI was 0.01. At day 1, the apical side of the epithelium was washed with pre-warmed OPTI-MEM in order to eliminate the viral inoculum. Samples were collected at the apical side by washing with 200µL of pre-warmed OptiMEM medium: 100µL was collected in S-Block (Qiagen) previously loaded with VXL lysis buffer containing proteinase K and RNA carrier for RNA extraction; the remaining quantity was used to perform a TCID50 assay.

Animal experiments

In vivo experiments were approved by the local ethical committee (C2EA—14) and the French “Ministère de l’Enseignement Supérieur, de la Recherche et de l’Innovation” (APAFIS#35014). All experiments were performed in accordance with with French and European legislation. This study was conducted according to ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org/). All experiments were conducted in BSL 3 laboratory.

Three-week-old Syrian hamsters (Janvier labs) were maintained in ISOcage P - Bioexclusion System (Techniplast) with unlimited access to water/food and 14 h/10 h light/dark cycle. Animals were monitored and weighed daily throughout the duration of the study to detect the appearance of any clinical signs of illness/suffering. Group size was calculated with an effect size of 2 and a power of 80%, resulting in 6 animals/group. Groups of 6 animals were intranasally infected under general anesthesia (isofluorane) with 50µL containing 2.104 TCID50 of virus diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride solution.

Nasal washes were performed under general anesthesia (isoflurane). with 150 µl 0.9% sodium chloride solution which was transferred into 1.5mL tubes containing 0.5mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, then centrifuged at 16,200 g for 10 min and stored at -80 °C.

Blood and organs were collected immediately after euthanasia at 3 dpi (cervical dislocation; realized under general anesthesia (isofluorane)). Left pulmonary lobes were washed in 10mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, blotted with filter paper, weighed and then transferred into 2mL tubes containing 1mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution and 3 mm glass beads. They were then crushed using a Tissue Lyser machine (Retsch MM400) for 20 min at 30 cycles/s and centrifuged 10 min at 16,200 g. Supernatant media were transferred into 1.5mL tubes, centrifuged 10 min at 16,200 g and stored at -80 °C.

The weight monitoring of all hamsters during the experiment is available in Table S1.

Quantification of the viral genome by real-time RT-qPCR

To avoid contamination, all experiments were conducted in a molecular biology laboratory that was specifically designed for clinical diagnosis, and which includes separate laboratories for each step of the procedure. RNA extraction was performed using the Qiacube HT automat and the QIAamp 96 DNA kit HT following manufacturer instructions. Total intracellular RNA of each well was extracted using RNeasy 96 HT kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer’s instructions. Viral RNA was quantified by real-time RT-qPCR (GoTaq 1-step qRt-PCR, Promega) using 3.8µL of extracted RNA and 6.2µL of RT-qPCR mix and standard fast cycling parameters, i.e., 10 min at 50 °C, 2 min at 95 °C, and 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 3 s followed by 30 s at 60 °C). Quantification was provided by four 2 log serial dilutions of an appropriate T7-generated synthetic RNA standard of known quantities (102 to 108 copies/reaction).

RT-qPCR reactions were performed on QuantStudio 12 K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) and analyzed using QuantStudio 12 K Flex Applied Biosystems software v1.2.3. Primers and probe sequences, which target SARS-CoV-2 N gene, were: Fw: GGCCGCAAATTGCACAAT ; Rev : CCAATGCGCGACATTCC; Probe: FAM-CCCCCAGCGCTTCAGCGTTCT-BHQ1.

Tissue-culture infectious dose 50 (TCID50) assay

To determine infectious titers, 96-well culture plates containing confluent VeroE6 cells were inoculated with 100µL per well of ten-fold dilutions serial dilutions of each sample in sextuplicate. Plates were incubated for 5 days and then read for the absence or presence of cytopathic effect in each well. Infectious titers were estimated using the method described by Reed & Muench47 and expressed as TCID50/ml.

Sequencing (competition experiment)

Three overlapping amplicons were produced (7500pb in total from position 19725–27298: spike protein) using the reverse transcriptase Platinum® Taq High Fidelity polymerase enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and specific primers described elsewhere48. PCR products were pooled in equimolar proportions. After Qubit quantification using Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit and Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (ThermoFisher) amplicons were fragmented (Bioruptor, Diagenaode) into fragments 200 bp long. Libraries were built adding barcodes, for sample identification, and primers to fragmented DNA with Ion Plus Fragment Library Kit using AB Library Builder System (ThermoFisher). To pool equimolarly the barcoded samples a quantification step by real time PCR using Ion Library TaqMan™ Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was realised. An emulsion PCR of the pools and loading on 520 chips were realised using the automated Ion Chef instrument (ThermoFisher). Sequencing was performed using the S5 Ion torrent technology (Thermo Fisher) following manufacturer’s instructions. Consensus sequence was obtained after trimming of reads (reads with quality score < 0.99, and length < 100pb were removed and the 30 first and 30 last nucleotides were removed from the reads) and mapping of the reads on a reference (EG.5.1 ref GISAID PI_ISL_18472861) using CLC genomics workbench software v.21 (Qiagen). Parameters for reference-based assembly consisted of match score = 1, mismatch cost = 2, length fraction = 0.5, similarity fraction = 0.8, insertion cost = 3, and deletion cost = 3. A de novo contig was also produced to ensure that the consensus sequence was not affected by the reference sequence. Quasi species with frequency over 3% were studied.

Regarding in vivo and HAE experiments all data were directly transformed in log prior analysis. The data obtained were subsequently analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (Graphpad software). Statistical tests are described in the figure legend.
