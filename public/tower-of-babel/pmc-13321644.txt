The ARTEMIS trial identifies immune activation as a key predictor of neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in triple-negative breast cancer

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelecting treatments for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains challenging due to its high molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity. To maximize response rates, nearly all patients are treated with an aggressive combination of chemo-immunotherapy. However, since half of patients respond to chemotherapy alone and about a third do not respond at all, the majority of patients would benefit from alternative regimens. Thus, there is a pressing need to better personalize or de-escalate therapy using response-based biomarkers.MethodsTo address this need, we conducted the ARTEMIS randomized trial (NCT 02276443) to test whether a molecular classifier could predict response to treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone in early-stage TNBC, and to understand the correlates of response.ResultsThis study found that integrating a molecular classifier did not result in a statistically significant improvement in responses to doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide (AC), which increased from 33% pCR to 41% (p = 0.43). To identify more robust correlates of response, we found that the strongest predictors were related to the immune system. Using immune cell markers, tumors could be categorized into three subtypes linked to chemoresponse, “immune hot sensitive,” “immune hot resistant,” and “immune cold.” Notably, 50% of the immune hot tumors exhibited a pathological complete response, compared to only 8% of the immune cold tumors. The immune cold tumors represented a group of TNBC tumors characterized by chemoresistance, a mesenchymal phenotype, and signatures of FGF and TGF-β signaling, suggesting potential therapeutic strategies. Among the immune hot tumors, the sensitive subtype showed a stronger immune response and distinct spatial organization between cancer and immune cells compared to the resistant subtype despite their largely similar gene expression profiles.ConclusionsThese data indicate that the immune contexture is a critical factor in the response to AC chemotherapy and provides a framework for selecting more effective and less toxic therapies for patients with TNBC.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT02276443 2014-10-24.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02290-z.

Background

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype of breast cancer that accounts for 15–20% of breast cancer cases diagnosed [1]. It lacks the targets seen in other types of breast cancer, such as estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression or human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) overexpression or amplification, and thus a combination of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) and immune checkpoint inhibitors is the standard of care for the majority of patients with early stage disease [2–4]. On completion of NACT, pathologic complete response (pCR) is associated with excellent prognosis in TNBC and appears to be independent of the regimen used to achieve pCR [5–7]. Approximately 51% of patients with TNBC respond to NACT; the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy increases responses to 65% and improves event-free and overall survival [4, 8–12]. These response rates show that half the patients will respond to chemotherapy alone, and about a third of patients do not respond at all. Thus, immunochemotherapy is the optimal treatment option for only ~ 15% of patients, and there is a critical clinical need to personalize treatments by (1) determining which chemoresistant tumors would respond to the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors [13, 14], (2) identifying tumors that will not respond to NACT or immune checkpoint inhibitors [15], and (3) identifying new treatments that will be effective against the tumors resistant to the current regimen.

In the clinic, response to NACT for breast cancer is predicted using criteria including ER/PR/HER2 status, tumor grade, and age. A number of more effective markers have been proposed (reviewed in [16]), including Ki-67 expression, genetic mutations, androgen receptor status, immune markers including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) [17], immunoglobulin G evenness [18], and gene expression signatures [19]. Gene expression signatures can also distinguish sensitive from resistant tumors with a clinically meaningful difference in survival outcomes [19], and thus, there is great interest in implementing them in the clinic. However, they are not widely used in treatment decisions for patients with early-stage TNBC.

In addition to an accurate predictor of response, improved therapies are needed to treat chemoresistant tumors. To help identify and apply such therapies, a framework has emerged where TNBC tumors are divided into subtypes that are more homogeneous and share targetable vulnerabilities that may be exploited by following a therapeutic decision tree [20, 21]. Seven TNBC subtypes were found from the gene expression of 130 tumors treated with NACT using the TNBCtype algorithm [22]. These subtypes were significantly associated with pCR status and better predicted pCR compared to the PAM50 classification of basal-like versus non-basal-like. However, the positive and negative predictive values for predicting pCR were too low to be clinically useful as a predictor of NACT sensitivity [23, 24].

To develop improved strategies for selecting therapies for TNBC, we focused on resistance markers for NACT without immunotherapy, as this remains the optimal treatment for most patients. To that end, we conducted the ARTEMIS trial (A Robust TNBC Evaluation fraMework to Improve Survival, NCT02276443). Specifically, ARTEMIS was designed to improve the rates of pathologic response in patients with localized (stage I–III) invasive TNBC by directing them to either standard NACT or a therapy targeted to the biology of the tumor based on molecular profiling and clinical assessment. We leveraged the data collected from the trial to understand the performance of the chemosensitivity predictions, to develop more effective approaches of predicting response, and to molecularly characterize resistant populations. We discovered that the state of immune activation was the strongest predictor of treatment response. This identified a novel cohort of patients whose tumors exhibited little immune activation and were resistant to chemotherapy, and also suggested a new framework for predicting response and selecting therapeutic strategies in TNBC.

Methods

Clinical trial

The ARTEMIS trial (NCT02276443) was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center and informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. All patients were enrolled from November 2015 to January 2018 (for randomized patients) at M.D. Anderson, including M.D. Anderson satellite locations. Key inclusion criteria included patient age ≥ 18 years, ability to undergo pre-treatment biopsy, a primary tumor size of ≥ 1.5 cm, proven to have TNBC [defined from standard pathologic assays as negative for ER and PR (< 10% tumor staining) and negative for HER2 (IHC score < 3 + and gene copy number not amplified)], cardiac ejection fraction > 50% by MUGA (multigated acquisition scan) or echocardiogram, and adequate organ and bone marrow function as measured by laboratory parameters. Key exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of stage IV disease or found to have stage IV disease prior to randomization, prior history of invasive cancer within 5 years of study entry or history of metastatic cancer (exceptions included non-metastatic, curatively treated basal and squamous cell carcinoma of the skin), prior excisional biopsy of the primary invasive breast cancer, presence of hematomas or biopsy site changes that limit response assessment of the primary tumor by diagnostic imaging, being not eligible for chemotherapy with taxane and/or anthracycline-based chemotherapy regimens, prior anthracycline use, grade 2 or higher neuropathy, Zubrod performance status of > 2, and patients with history of serious cardiac events.

The primary objective was to determine whether the inclusion of molecular (transcriptomic) testing and diagnostic imaging could improve the pCR/RCB-I rate from 50 to 64.2%. A maximum of 360 patients were planned for 2:1 randomization to the experimental arm (i.e., patient and clinician know the results of molecular testing) or the control arm (i.e., patient and clinician do not know the results of the molecular testing). Patients randomized to the know arm and their treating physicians were notified of the molecular diagnostic results within 12 weeks of the start of treatment.

The trial followed a group sequential design with one-sided O’Brien-Fleming boundaries, with up to two equally spaced binding interim tests for both futility and superiority and one final test, having an overall Type I error 0.05 and power 0.80 to detect a response rate improvement from a null rate of 0.50 to a target value of 0.642. Randomization was performed using the MD Anderson Clinical Oncology Research (CORe) system. This trial was designed using East 5.4.2 (Cytel Inc., Cambridge, MA) with an expected sample size of 220 patients given the null hypothesis is true.

After 4 cycles of anthracycline-based chemotherapy, all patients had the option of continuing with standard taxane (paclitaxel or docetaxel) ± platinum (carboplatin or cisplatin) chemotherapy or participation in clinical trials. Recommendations were made by the treating physician and a trial-dedicated Tumor Board based on diagnostic imaging and clinical exam. For patients randomized to the experimental arm, predictions from the GES-CS (Gene Expression Signature for ChemoSensitivity) were considered.

Upon completion of their second phase of neoadjuvant therapy, either with a taxane-based regimen or targeted therapy, patients underwent surgical resection, and residual cancer burden (RCB) was determined by board-certified breast pathologists.

Ultrasound imaging

All patients (including those randomized to the control arm) were assessed for response using diagnostic imaging at baseline prior to therapy, then after 2 cycles of anthracycline-based chemotherapy (± 1 week, optional) and after 4 cycles of anthracycline-based chemotherapy (± 1 week, required per protocol). Whole-breast ultrasound [25] was performed on all patients using Epiq 5G scanners with 12- to 18-MHz high-frequency linear array transducers (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA) by eleven dedicated breast sonographers, and findings were evaluated by fourteen breast fellowship-trained radiologists.

The transverse and longitudinal images of the index lesion were captured. The measurements of the index lesion in 3 dimensions were obtained. The regional nodal basins, including the axillary level I-III nodes, and internal mammary chain, were examined by ultrasound. For patients with abnormal findings in the axillary region, the supraclavicular lymph nodes were assessed. The index lymph node in the highest involved nodal chain was targeted for fine-needle aspiration biopsy using a 21-gauge needle under sonographic guidance. The disease was staged according to the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 7th edition [26].

Tumor biopsy collection and storage for molecular testing

Direct ultrasound-guidance was used for all biopsies. Core needle biopsy (CNB) was performed using three commercially available disposable 14-gauge biopsy devices: Marquee® (Bard Biopsy systems, Tempe, AZ, USA), MaxCore® (Bard Biopsy systems, Tempe, AZ, USA) and Achieve® (CareFusion, San Diego, CA, USA), with similar needle throws (18 mm or 25 mm for Marquee®, 22 mm for MaxCore®, and 25 mm for Achieve®). Fine needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) was performed using commercially available 21-gauge hypodermic needles. Biopsy specimens were transferred to 2 mL cryopreservation tubes and stored in RNAlater at -80 °C until extraction of DNA and RNA analytes.

Gene expression-based chemosensitivity signature (GES-CS)

The chemosensitivity gene expression signature requires gene expression levels estimated from an Affymetrix microarray. Therefore, the tumors were profiled by microarray as well as RNA-Seq described below. The MD Anderson’s Molecular Diagnostic Lab (MDL) followed their standard operating procedures for microarray-based gene expression profiles of breast cancer, quality control, and reporting of results. Briefly, RNA was extracted using silica columns (Qiagen kit), and assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (concentration, 260:280 OD ratio) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer (RNA integrity number (RIN) and RNA 28S:18S ratio). A 1 μg aliquot of RNA is reverse transcribed to cDNA, and subsequently transcribed from double-strand cDNA to labeled cRNA (cRNA yield is recorded), prior to labeling, fragmentation, and hybridization to an Affymetrix U133A microarray. The array is scanned, and the data are normalized using the MAS5.0 algorithm, and then scaled to our reference distribution of 1322 breast cancer genes. The research database coordinator for the pharmacogenomics laboratory in MDL directly entered all sample information, quality control information, and prediction results into the protected laboratory research database for this study, reported the prediction results and the summary quality control information for each sample directly to the clinical study coordinator and the study chair.

Germline blood collection and DNA extraction

Whole blood was collected via antecubital venipuncture and collected in a 10 ml K3-EDTA spray-dried vacutainer. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (A1620). Briefly, whole blood was placed in cell lysis buffer and centrifuged to separate the white blood cells. White blood cells were incubated in nuclear lysis buffer followed by protein precipitation solution, centrifuged and supernatant separated. Genomic DNA was isolated with isopropanol and washed in 70% ethanol. After the DNA pellet was air dried, it was rehydrated and stored at − 80 °C for future use. Extracted DNA was measured utilizing a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer as per standard manufacturer’s instructions.

Genomic DNA, library prep, and capture

Genomic DNA was quantified by Picogreen (Invitrogen) and quality was accessed using Genomic DNA Tape for the 2200 Tapestation (Agilent). DNA from each sample (100–500 ng of genomic DNA) was sheared by sonication with the following conditions: Peak Incident Power 175, Duty Cycle 20%, Intensity 5, Cycles per Burst 200, and 120 s using Covaris E220 instrument (Covaris). To ensure the proper fragment size, samples were checked on TapeStation using the DNA High Sensitivity kit (Agilent). The sheared DNA proceeded to library prep using KAPA library prep kit (KAPA) following the “with beads” manufacturer protocol. Briefly, this protocol consists of 3 enzymatic reactions for end repair, A-tailing and Adaptor ligation, followed by barcode insertion by PCR using KAPA HiFi polymerase (6 cycles). PCR primers were removed by using 1.8 × volume of Agencourt AMPure PCR Purification kit (Agencourt Bioscience Corporation). At the end of the library prep, samples were analyzed on TapeStation to verify correct fragment size and to ensure the absence of extra bands. Samples were quantified using KAPA qPCR quantification kit. Equimolar amounts of DNA were pooled for capture (2–6 samples per pool). We used whole exome biotin labeled probes from Roche Nimblegen (Exome V3) and followed manufacturer’s protocol for the capture step. Briefly, DNA was pooled (2–6 samples), dried out and after addition of the capture reagents and probes, samples were incubated at 47 °C on thermocycler with heated lid (57 °C) for 64–74 h. The targeted regions were recovered using streptavidin beads, and the streptavidin–biotin-probe-target complex was washed. Another round of PCR amplification was performed according to manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of each captured sample was analyzed on TapeStation using the DNA High Sensitivity kit and the enrichment was accessed by qPCR using specific primers designed by Roche Nimblegen. The cutoff for the enrichment was 50-fold minimum.

RNA extraction and quality control

RNA was extracted by NORGEN Total RNA Purification Kit (Cat. 37,500) (NORGEN BIOTEK CORP). The extracted RNA was treated with DNase I to get rid of genomic DNA residues. The treated RNA then was purified using the AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences) and eluted into 1 × TE buffer. The purified RNA was quantified using Quant-iT™ RiboGreen™ RNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC) and the RNA quality was accessed using Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit and the 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent Technologies). The cDNA was prepared from the extracted total RNA using Ovation® RNA-Seq System V2 (NuGEN). Amplification is initiated at the 3’ end as well as randomly throughout the transcriptome in the sample. The prepared cDNA was quantified using Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC) and quality was accessed using Genomic DNA ScreenTape and Reagents on the Tapestation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). Up to 200 ng of each cDNA sample based on the PicoGreen quantification was sheared (mechanically fragmented) using the E220 Focused-ultrasonicator Covaris (Covaris). The sonication was performed under the following conditions: Peak Incident Power 200, Duty Cycle 25%, Cycles per Burst 50, and duration 10 s for 120 iterations. To ensure the proper fragment size, samples were examined on TapeStation 4200 using the DNA High Sensitivity kit (Agilent Technologies). The sheared cDNA was proceeded to library preparation using SureSelect XT Low Input Reagent Kit with indexes 1–96 (Agilent Technologies) as automated method on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation (PerkinElmer, Inc.). This protocol consists of 3 enzymatic reactions for end repair, A-tailing and Adaptor ligation, followed by barcode insertion by PCR using Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (8 to 14 cycles, based on input DNA quality and quantity). PCR primers were removed by using 1 × volume of Agencourt AMPure PCR Purification kit (Agencourt Bioscience Corporation). The quality and quantity of the prepared libraries were evaluated using TapeStation 4200 and the DNA High Sensitivity kit (Agilent Technologies) to verify correct fragment size and to ensure complete removal of primer dimers. Subsequently, the prepared libraries were individually hybridized to Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon v.4 probes (Agilent Technologies). The hybridization steps were automated on the Sciclone G3 NGSx Workstation (PerkinElmer, Inc.). Agilent captures were hybridized as single sample reactions using 500—1000 ng of prepared library as input. All Hybridization and Post-hybridization capture & washes were performed according to Agilent’s protocol. Briefly, the capture reagents and probes were added to the prepared libraries, the mixture was incubated at 65 °C on thermocycler with heated lid on for up 24 h. The targeted regions were captured using streptavidin beads, and the streptavidin–biotin-probe-target complex was washed. The captured libraries were enriched by PCR amplification according to manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of each captured sample was analyzed on TapeStation 4200 using the DNA High Sensitivity kit. The captured libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform for 2 × 150 paired end reads with an 8nt read for indexes using Cycle Sequencing v3 reagents (Illumina).

RNA-Seq analysis

Raw RNA Sequence data was processed by an in-house RNA-Seq data analysis pipeline, which uses the STAR aligner [27] to align raw reads to hg19 version of Human reference genome, featureCounts [28] to quantify aligned reads with to produce raw counts, and FastQC [29] and QualiMap [30] to evaluate quality of raw reads and feature counts. After assessment for sequencing quality control metrics, RNA-Seq yielded data for downstream analysis in 270 tumors.

TPM counts from RSEM [31] were used to extract expression for protein-coding genes only. Data was normalized using VST [32] and further batch correction was applied using ComBat [33]. Batch corrected data was used for ssGSEA analysis, which was run using the R package GSVA separately over sets of mSigDB gene sets, including Hallmark, Reactome and KEGG. Immune deconvolution was carried out on the batch corrected matrix using CIBERSORT, MCPCounter EPIC, xCell, and quanTIseq using default parameters. We used a bioconductor package DESeq2 (with a false discovery rate (Benjamini & Hochberg) < 0.05) to identify differentially expressed genes between pCR and non-pCR, and subtype comparisons [32, 34].

Whole exome sequencing analysis

The captured libraries were sequenced with Illumina sequencing technology on a version 3 TruSeq paired end flowcell according to manufacturer’s instructions at a cluster density between 700 and1000 K clusters/mm2. Sequencing was performed on a HiSeq 4000 for 2 × 100 paired end reads with a 7 nt read for indexes using Cycle Sequencing v3 reagents (Illumina). The resulting BCL files containing the sequence data were converted into “.fastq.gz” files and individual libraries within the samples were demultiplexed using CASAVA 1.8.2 with no mismatches. All regions were covered by > 20 reads. For data analysis, we aligned the WES capture deep-sequencing data to human reference assembly hg19 using BWA [35] and removed duplicated reads using Picard. After assessment for sequencing metrics, high quality DNA data was available for 285 tumors.

Consensus somatic variant calling

To generate a consistent set of somatic mutation calls we used multiple mutation callers. We used MuTect and Mutect2 for all samples, using default parameters and a pooled normal. MuSE does not support a tumor only mode, therefore it was run for all samples where either GB or GC normal was available. Mutations were annotated using ensemblVEP with reference databases including COSMIC, ExAC, gnomAD and various mutation pathogenicity predictors. Somatic mutations were filtered using a tiered approach, where calling by at least two callers was required in case of an available reference. We selected variants supported by at least 9 reads with > = 2% variant allele frequency (VAF) in the tumor sample. To remove potential germline variants, we discarded variants found with > = 0.005% allele frequency in the gnomAD database [36]. A small fraction of pathogenic or hotspot mutations known cancer-genes were recaptured in case they were only called using a single caller. Mutsig2CV was used to extract significantly mutated genes, using consensus SNV calls [37].

We calculated tumor mutation burden as the number of non-synonymous mutations per captured megabase using the tmb function in maftools [38]. We annotated mutations using VEP [39] and PCGR [40], and included additional annotations from databases including COSMIC [41] and ClinVar [42].

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using R (predominantly with version 4.0.3). We used two-sided Fisher’s exact tests to compare two categorical variables. Student’s t-tests, two-sided with unequal variances, were used to compare continuous variables (immune estimates, ssGSEA scores) and pCR. We used the R survival package to perform Kaplan–Meier survival analysis to compare the time to outcome (recurrence, distant relapse, or death) between different groups of patients (e.g., those whose tumors are predicted to be chemosensitive vs insensitive, and those whose tumors harbor TP53 mutations vs those whose tumors are wild-type for TP53).

Results

Clinical Trial

The ARTEMIS trial (NCT02276443) was first designed to test the hypothesis that the use of a chemotherapy response predictor improves the pCR rate of patients with Stage I–III TNBC (Fig. 1A), and second, to determine whether molecular profiling can identify alternative treatment approaches. To test the hypothesis, we predicted response using a CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments)-certified gene expression signature (GES-CS, gene expression signature for chemosensitivity) [41] from the pre-treatment biopsy. We randomized patients 2:1 to either know (experimental arm) or not know (control arm) based on the results of their signature and provided results only to the patients in the know arm. For the not know arm, the patients, physicians, and study team were all blinded to the results, and thus the results were not released into their medical records. All patients received NACT (neoadjuvant chemotherapy) with a planned four cycles of AC (doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide). After two cycles (optional) and four cycles (mandatory) of therapy, we assessed the change in the tumor size by ultrasound (US). Then, based on clinical exam, ultrasound assessed volumetric reduction, and (for the know arm) the GES-CS, we recommended the patient to either continue on standard taxane-based therapy, or to enroll in one of a portfolio of single-arm phase II trials of combined chemo- and targeted therapy using a second-hit strategy to overcome chemo-resistance (Supplementary Figure S1) [43].Fig. 1Overview of ARTEMIS clinical trial. After considering our recommendation, the treatment for both arms was ultimately determined jointly by the patient and physician.

To guide selection for the trials, primary tumor samples underwent H&E (hematoxylin and eosin) evaluation of stromal TILs and immunohistochemistry to measure androgen receptor (AR), vimentin, and PD-L1 (Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1). Using this information, a multidisciplinary board selected trials generally using the following hierarchy: patients whose tumors had TILs ≥ 10–20% were selected for a PD-L1 inhibitor, vimentin > 50% or metaplastic histology for mTOR (mammalian Target of Rapamycin) inhibitor, AR ≥ 10% for AR inhibitor, and if no biomarker was present, for EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) inhibitor. At the end of the second phase of treatment, tumors were surgically resected unless they progressed to unresectable or metastatic disease. We evaluated response using RCB (Residual Cancer Burden) [42].

Ultimately, 219 patients (n = 146 in the know arm and n = 73 in the not know arm) completed the randomized study (Fig. 1B). 41% of the patients in the know arm demonstrated no residual disease (pCR or RCB-0) compared with 33% in the not know arm (p = 0.43, two-sided Fisher’s exact test, pCR vs RCB I-III) (Table 1). The response rates for patients with either no or minimal residual disease (pCR/RCB-I) was 57% in the know arm compared with 44% in the not know arm (p = 0.17, two-sided Fisher’s exact test, pCR + RCB I vs RCB II-III). For the 146 patients in the know arm, the predictions were largely correlated with the reduction in tumor size, where the patients with tumors exhibiting at least 70% reduction after four cycles of chemotherapy were more likely to be predicted to be sensitive (Fig. 2B). The treatment closely followed the algorithm recommendations (assessed by clinical exam, ultrasound, and GES-CS) where 97% of the patients predicted to be chemosensitive (n = 87) received standard of care therapy, and 76% of the patients predicted to be chemoresistant (n = 42) were enrolled in a trial (Fig. 2C). Responses were unavailable for the remaining 17 patients because their surgery was done outside of MD Anderson (for 15 patients), or they developed metastatic disease and no surgery was done (two patients).

For the 94 patients in the know arm that received standard of care treatment, the recommendations predicted pCR with a positive predictive value of 0.54, comparable to the overall pCR rate in this cohort of 0.51. The negative predictive value was 0.70. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.94 and 0.15, respectively, indicating that this recommendation strategy did enrich for chemosensitive tumors, but many of the tumors predicted to be sensitive did not respond.

Because continued enrollment was deemed unlikely to lead to rejection of the null hypothesis, the randomized study was halted after review by the Data Safety and Monitoring Board at MD Anderson and the GES-CS was discontinued. ARTEMIS was redesigned as a single-arm unblinded umbrella protocol, and patients deemed to have chemoresistant disease by physical exam and imaging were still offered enrollment on biomarker-guided rescue continuation neoadjuvant trials designed for ARTEMIS trial participants.

Throughout all phases of the ARTEMIS protocol, comprehensive molecular profiling (using either WES (whole exome sequencing), RNA-Seq, or both) was performed on tumors from a total of 293 patients. Among those, WES was available for 285 (97%) tumors, RNA-Seq for 270 (92%) tumors, and both WES and RNA-Seq for 264 (90%) tumors. Cases selected for molecular profiling were enriched for chemoresistant tumors, resulting in a lower pCR rate of 40%. A fraction of these tumors were engrafted in mice and established as PDX models as described [44].

The ARTEMIS clinical trial generated a cohort of tumor samples treated under a common protocol, annotated with clinical covariates and outcomes, and accompanied with molecular profiling that could be leveraged to investigate the correlates of response.

Chemosensitivity is correlated with markers of immune activity

To identify correlates of response to chemotherapy, we focused on the 86 tumors treated with chemotherapy. We separated them into a sensitive group (46 tumors that achieved a pCR) and a resistant group (40 that did not achieve pCR). To increase sample numbers, we also included in the resistant group the tumors that were resistant to chemo + targeted therapy (assuming that the addition of targeted therapy would not convert a chemosensitive tumor to be resistant). Our final study cohort consisted of 113 tumors treated under the same clinical protocol, but differing in therapeutic responses, where 46 were sensitive and 67 resistant.

We started by comparing the transcriptomic profiles of the resistant and sensitive tumors using a GSEA analysis (Fig. 3A) [45]. We found that the chemosensitive tumors were enriched in multiple pathways indicative of activation of the immune system, including IFN-γ response (FDR [False Discovery Rate] = 1.3 × 10−39), IFN-α response (FDR = 2.4 × 10−23), allograft rejection (FDR = 2.6 × 10−31), inflammatory response (FDR = 4.2 × 10−10), NF-κB (FDR = 0.001), and IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling (FDR = 1.6 × 10−7). Next, as another measure of immune activation, we compared the proportion of sTILs (stromal tumor infiltrating lymphocytes) as assessed by H&E and found that they were indeed lower in the resistant tumors (p = 0.0002, two-sided Mann Whitney Rank Sum test; Fig. 3B). Although the chemosensitive tumors were correlated with strong markers of immune activity, the resistant tumors could not be characterized as clearly. Resistant tumors were distributed across the entire range of TNBC subtypes from the TNBCtype algorithm (Supplementary Figure S2A). The GSEA analysis showed enrichment of an EMT signature (FDR = 9.5 × 10–9), and these tumors were overrepresented in the mesenchymal subtype, approaching statistical significance (p = 0.06). This data suggested that resistance may arise from a mesenchymal phenotype, but the fact that these tumors were seen across all subtypes suggested that other non-mesenchymal resistance mechanisms may also be engaged.

Finally, we noted that the pathway analysis also identified an association between a gene expression signature for TP53 activation and chemosensitivity [46]. However, we found no association between the presence of somatic mutations in TP53 or PIK3CA, the only genes with frequent somatic mutations, with response (Supplementary Figure S2B).

Immune markers separate tumors into three cohorts with distinct responses to chemotherapy

Although the sensitive tumors were enriched in immune-related gene expression signatures (Fig. 3A), the tumors in the immunomodulatory (IM) subtype spanned both resistance phenotypes (Supplementary Figure S2A) with 74% being sensitive and 26% resistant. We reasoned that this split may have been due to heterogeneity in the immune response. To unravel those differences, we focused on additional immune markers. We deconvoluted the immune cell types seen in the gene expression profiles of the tumors using the MCPCounter [47], quanTIseq [48], EPIC [49], xCell [50], and CIBERSORT [51] algorithms. Each algorithm provided predictions of (different, but overlapping) subsets of the immune cell populations of the tumors based on their gene expression profiles. As validation of these predictions, we observed that the immune cell predictions correlated well with those measured by multiplex immunofluorescence on the Vectra platform [51] (Supplementary Figure S3). Then, we correlated the immune cell predictions from these algorithms with response and found that 73 signatures for multiple immune cell types, including T cells, B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, were associated with resistance at FDR < 0.05 indicating a broad association between immune activity and response (Supplementary Table S1).

To better dissect the biology underlying the immune response, we divided the tumors from our study cohort into an immune hot group, harboring an immune response of any type, and an immune cold group, showing no evidence of immune activation. To do this, we selected three immune markers: one for lymphocytes (we used the proportion of stromal tumor infiltrating lymphocytes assessed by H&E, as it is a direct measure of a component of the immune system used for clinical decisions), one for myeloid cells (we used the macrophage signature from xCell based on its strong correlation with macrophage counts from Vectra imaging, seen in Supplementary Figure S3), and a general marker of immune activity (the IFN-γ signature [52] from Fig. 3A). The lymphocyte and macrophage markers were not significantly correlated with each other, (r = 0.16, p = 0.10), and the interferon marker was significantly correlated with both the lymphocyte (r = 0.50, p = 2.4e−8) and macrophage markers (r = 0.52, p = 3.8e−9), suggesting that the immune responses of the tumors in this cohort could be driven by different cell types. Next, we discretized these continuous measures into binary indicators of sensitivity or resistance by selecting cutoffs that best distinguished the sensitivity of the tumors. After discretizing tumors by immune status, we examined their association with responses to chemotherapy (Fig. 4B). We found that only 8% of the immune cold tumors achieved PCR, while 50% of the immune hot tumors did. This data crystallized the observations that (1) markers of immune activation could discretize tumors based on chemosensitivity, (2) nearly all tumors that achieved a pCR had an activated immune system (96%), but (3) even with an activated immune system, a tumor could achieve a pCR only about half the time. Based on the immune status and pCR status, we grouped the tumors into three immuno-response subtypes: immune hot sensitive (39%), immune hot resistant (39%), and immune cold (22%). While two of the immune cold tumors were chemosensitive, the numbers were too low to determine whether they represented a distinct subtype with robust clinical or biological characteristics. Therefore, we grouped all the immune cold tumors into a single subtype, recognizing that future studies may find additional distinctions.

Next, we examined the association between the immuno-response subtypes of the tumors and the percent tumor growth that was used for the treatment recommendations. We looked at the tumor size growth in the three categories and found that the immune hot sensitive tumors indeed had greater reductions in tumor size after four cycles of AC than the other two groups (q < 0.0001, Dunn’s test with multiple hypothesis correction) (Fig. 4C). However, the immune hot resistant tumors had similar growth as the immune cold ones (q = 0.30), demonstrating the fact that the tumor size growth did not fully capture the biology represented in these subtypes. In addition, the GES-CS predicted comparable rates of sensitivity across the three subtypes (p = 0.33, two-tailed Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 4D). Thus, the immune cold classification represented a response criterion independent of tumor size reduction and GES-CS.

Finally, we investigated whether the three immuno-response subtypes were correlated with survival times and found that the immune hot sensitive tumors were associated with longer overall survival (p = 0.02, Log-rank test) and longer metastasis free survival (p = 0.006) (Fig. 4E). Among the resistant tumors, there was no significant difference in the survival times between the immune hot and cold tumors, which is consistent with our earlier observation from this trial that, among patients that did not achieve a pCR, tumor infiltrating lymphocytes were not associated with survival time [53]. This delineates a cohort of immune hot tumors whose response and survival times more closely resemble the immune cold ones.

To interrogate the differences in the phenotypes between the immune hot and cold tumors, we looked at the Hallmarks pathways and saw that the immune hot tumors were enriched with multiple immune related pathways, while the cold tumors exhibited an EMT (epithelial to mesenchymal transition) signature (FDR = 0.001, calculated using Benjamini and Hochberg method [54]) (Fig. 5B). To determine whether the immune cold tumors had undergone a complete EMT, we examined the expression of established markers for epithelial (CDH1, CLDN1) and mesenchymal cells (VIM, CDH2, SNAI1, ZEB1) (Fig. 5C) [55]. We saw no differences in the expression of the epithelial markers across the three immune types, but two mesenchymal markers, CDH2 (p = 0.02, two-sided Student’s t-test) and ZEB1 (p < 0.0001) were elevated in the immune cold tumors. This suggested that the cells in these tumors may have mesenchymal characteristics, but had not completed an EMT that was marked by loss of epithelial markers.

A mesenchymal gene expression signature was frequently seen in metaplastic tumors, a rare subtype of (frequently) triple-negative breast cancer known to be chemoresistant [56]. To determine whether the immune cold subtype expressed a mesenchymal signature due to enrichment of metaplastic tumors, we found that 24% of the immune cold tumors were metaplastic, while 11% of the immune hot tumors were metaplastic. However, this difference did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.19, two-sided Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 5D). We investigated whether the EMT signature in the immune cold tumors were driven by metaplastic tumors and found no significant difference in the EMT scores in the metaplastic and non-metaplastic tumors (p = 0.60, two-sided Student’s t-test) (Supplementary Figure S4). Finally, while it was possible that the immune response of metaplastic tumors may vary based on their metaplastic subtype, we lacked the sample numbers to investigate this possibility [57].

To further characterize the immune cold tumors, we looked more broadly for additional pathways associated with this group by leveraging the Canonical pathways, a more comprehensive set of molecular signatures than the Hallmarks signatures we used above. We compared the scores of the Canonical pathways between the immune hot and cold tumors using GSEA and saw that the immune hot tumors were enriched with a range of immune signatures (Supplementary Figure S5, Fig. 5E). The most significant pathways in the immune hot tumors were related to the TNF (tumor necrosis factor) cytokines (FDR = 3.8 × 10−9), which were inducers of a pro-inflammatory immune response and the adaptive immune system [58, 59]. For the cold tumors, the most significant pathway elicited activin activity (FDR = 6.6 × 10−7), which is a TGF-β family member and has been shown to induce mesenchymal and stem cell phenotypes [60]. This was supported by other signatures for TGF-β (BIOCARTA_TGFB_PATHWAY FDR = 7.3 × 10−5, KEGG_TGF_BETA_SIGNALING_PATHWAY FDR = 8.8 × 10−5) and BMP (REACTOME_SIGNALING_BY_BMP FDR = 0.0001), a TGF-β family member (Fig. 5F, top panel).

In the immune cold tumors, the most significant pathways also included a signature for FGFR (fibroblast growth factor receptor) signaling (FDR = 4.2 × 10−5) (Fig. 5F, bottom panel). In these tumors, there was no association between the FGFR pathway score and metaplastic histology (Supplementary Figure S6). FGFR has previously been shown to regulate cell proliferation, resistance, and tumor progression in breast cancer, including triple-negative [61]. We looked for correlation between the expression of FGFR family members and the immune cold tumors and found that the expression of FGFR1 was higher (p = 0.02, two-sided Student’s t-test, Supplementary Figure S7).

The fact that the FGFR signature was more strongly associated with the immune cold tumors than FGFR1 gene expression suggested that the pathway may also be activated through mechanisms outside the gene expression of FGFR1. The activation of FGFR1 has been reported to be controlled by post-translational mechanisms, including stabilization and reduction of internalization and degradation by CDH2 [62], one of the two mesenchymal markers that were associated with the immune cold tumors. Hypothesizing that CDH2 may regulate the activation of FGFR1 in these tumors, we compared CDH2 expression with the scores for the FGFR signature and found a significant positive correlation (R = 0.35, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 5G, top panel). The other gene whose expression was significantly correlated with the immune cold tumors, ZEB1, has also been related to FGFR signaling. The association is complex, with reports suggesting both that ZEB1 can regulate FGFR1 expression [63, 64] and that FGFR1 can induce ZEB1 expression [65, 66] in a positive feedback loop [67]. In any case, the FGFR signature was significantly positively correlated with ZEB1 expression (R = 0.62, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5G, bottom panel). Finally, we looked at the correlation between the FGFR1 signature and the IFN-γ response pathway and saw a negative correlation (R = − 0.29, p = 0.002) (Fig. 5H).

To determine whether these findings could be replicated in independent data sets, we predicted the immune status of TNBC tumors from the Fudan University study [68] and also from the TCGA [69]. Both these data sets confirmed our observation that the immune cold tumors are enriched in TGF-β and FGF signaling pathways, and that FGF signaling was correlated with CDH2 and ZEB1 expression (Supplementary Figure S8). The negative correlation between FGF signaling and IFN-γ response was replicated in both data sets, but the association in the TNBC tumors from the TCGA was not statistically significant, potentially due to lower sample numbers there.

Taken together, these results characterized a cohort of immune cold tumors that were enriched for gene expression signatures of TGF-β and FGF signaling, which may predict a therapeutic vulnerability.

The degree of immune activation is correlated with response to chemotherapy

We next focused on understanding the biology underlying the disparate chemoresponses of the immune hot tumors. Although these all showed evidence of an activated immune system, they had a diverse response to chemotherapy where approximately half were sensitive and half were resistant. All markers were elevated in the sensitive (immune hot) tumors relative to the resistant, although the differences were relatively modest compared to the differences with the cold tumors. To understand why the immune hot sensitive and resistant subtypes may have different immune responses, we compared the predicted immune cell types and found no significant differences. The ability to elicit an immune response had also been linked to the tumor mutation burden and expression of neoantigens. However, we saw no difference in the tumor mutation burden between the two groups (Supplementary Figure S9A). Finally, the expression of PD-L1 has been linked to CD8 + infiltration in TNBC [70, 71], and we saw that the chemosensitive tumors had a higher expression of the PD-L1 gene (p < 0.0001), although the protein levels (IHC) were not significantly elevated (p = 0.10) (Fig. 6B).

Seeing that general markers of immune activation were correlated with response, we next asked whether response was associated with the spatial architecture of the tumor microenvironment. We started by using gene expression signatures that predicted the spatial orientation of lymphocytes relative to cancer cells [72]. We found that immune hot resistant tumors were less likely to exhibit an Inflamed phenotype that was indicative of a mixed distribution of cancer cells and lymphocytes (p = 0.01, two-sided Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 6C).

Next, to more directly assess whether spatial architecture was related to response, we leveraged the multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF) data that we previously generated on a subset of these tumors [44]. These analyses revealed that the distance between cancer cells and CD3+CD8+ cells (representing CD8+ T cells) differed across the three immune subtypes (p = 3 × 10−8, ANOVA test), as did the distance between the cancer cells and CD68+ cells (predominantly macrophages) (p = 0.006) (Fig. 6D). A closer examination revealed that the CD3+CD8+ cells were closer to the cancer cells in the immune hot tumors compared to the immune cold tumors (Fig. 6E). While these distances were also shorter in the immune hot sensitive tumors compared to the immune hot resistant ones, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09, Student’s t-test). In contrast, CD68+ cells were significantly closer to the cancer cells in the immune hot resistant tumors relative to the sensitive ones (p = 0.001).

In addition to differences in the immune contexture, we looked for other correlates of response. The proliferation rate of cells had been investigated as a correlate for sensitivity to chemotherapy [73–75], although the relationship is controversial [76]. In our analysis, we found only a modest difference between the Ki-67 IHC marker and chemosensitivity (p = 0.02) and limited differences in expression signatures for proliferation (Supplementary Figure S9B). Consistent with this, the gene expression profiles of the two cohorts were highly similar and could not be distinguished in a principal components analysis (p = 0.32, Maximal mean discrepancy K-sample test) (Fig. 6F).

Finally, we looked for correlates between the immune hot and cold tumors and clinical covariates and found an association with clinical staging. Immune hot resistant (36% Stage III) and immune cold (28% Stage III) tumors presented at a more advanced stage than the sensitive ones (9% Stage III) (sensitive vs resistant p = 0.009; two-sided Fisher’s Exact Test) (Fig. 6G). Given that clinical staging incorporated lymph node status, we also observed that the immune hot resistant tumors were most likely to have lymph node involvement (50% in immune hot resistant, 36% in immune cold, and 27% in immune hot sensitive; sensitive vs resistant p = 0.048) (Fig. 6H). Taken together, the immune hot and cold tumors exhibited broadly similar molecular profiles, and the strongest distinctions between these groups were their immune signatures and clinical and treatment response phenotypes.

Discussion

Using the molecular profiling of the tumors collected from the ARTEMIS trial, we discovered that the strongest distinction between the sensitive and resistant tumors was seen in their immune markers. When the trial was conceived, the relationship between chemoresponse and the activity of the immune system in TNBC was not yet widely recognized, and neither the GES-CS nor tumor size reduction criteria explicitly included immune markers. In future studies, stratifying the patients based on immune activity would lead to cohorts that are more homogeneous in chemoresponse and enriched in potentially targetable resistance mechanisms.

While chemotherapy has classically been regarded as immunosuppressive, its ability to exert toxicity by stimulating an immune response is increasingly being recognized [77, 78]. In breast cancers treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, an immune gene expression signature [79] and T cell markers [80] were correlated with pCR as well as improved overall survival [81]. Macrophages also appeared to be important, as chemotherapy promotes the recruitment of tumor associated macrophages, promoting a poor outcome response [82]. Similar results are seen in TNBC where higher TILs correlate with increased pCR to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and overall survival [18, 83–85]. Increasing evidence is establishing an immune response as an important component of chemotherapy efficacy [16], and our findings reveal its critical role in distinguishing chemoresistant tumors in TNBC.

In this study, we focused on the patients who were treated with doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel (AC-T). It is not known whether our findings are generalizable to other regimens, as chemotherapies vary in the degree and mechanism by which they induce immunogenicity [86]. In particular, anthracycline treatment can induce the production of IFN-α [87] and stimulate an immune response [88, 89]. With anthracycline-based therapies, lymphocytic infiltration has been associated with less relapse in ER-/HER2- lymph node-positive breast cancers [90] and more frequent response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in unselected breast cancers [80]. Further, attempts to substitute anthracyclines with safer chemotherapies have shown reduced efficacy [91, 92], possibly due to attenuated recruitment of the immune system. De-escalation strategies that incorporate alternative mechanisms of immune stimulation should be explored.

The available data suggests that chemotherapy induces a stepwise attack on the cancer cells, where, in the first phase, it exerts toxicity directly on the cancer cells by targeting cycling cells, resulting in an immunogenic cell death that triggers an immune response in the second phase. In the ARTEMIS trial, predictions of response were influenced by the regression in tumor size two months after the initiation of chemotherapy. While chemotherapy can directly induce cell death relatively rapidly, regression from immune attack occurs more gradually. In a clinical trial of pembrolizumab in advanced TNBC, the median time to response was 18 weeks [93], highlighting a delay between the stimulation of the immune system and the reduction in tumor size. Therefore, the tumor size reduction after eight weeks likely reflects the direct cytotoxicity from the chemotherapy drug and incompletely captures the ability of the immune system to target the tumor.

Our analysis characterizes, for the first time, a cohort of patients that have chemoresistant immune cold tumors. Although these tumors have not been previously characterized molecularly, an immune cold microenvironment has been associated with poor outcomes in the prior literature. A correlation between a low immune TNBC subtype and short recurrence free survival has been previously reported [68]. However, that study lacked endpoints measuring response to chemotherapy. Another study focused on cancer cell intrinsic rather than microenvironment determinants of chemoresponse in TNBC and did not identify a correlation with immune activation [55]. Importantly, it did reveal a negative correlation between the immunomodulatory tumors and mesenchymal phenotype, which is consistent with our findings.

As a potential therapeutic strategy to target the immune cold tumors, we observed that they are enriched with a mesenchymal phenotype concomitant with gene expression signatures for TGF-β and FGF signaling. Both pathways have been linked to chemoresistance and EMT (reviewed in [94, 95]), itself a driver of resistance and immune escape [96]. One of these pathways, FGFR, has been shown to suppress IFN-γ signaling [97–100], and we see a negative correlation between FGFR and IFN-γ in the ARTEMIS and Fudan University data sets. Therefore, it is possible that FGFR suppresses the immune response in the immune cold tumors, which must be further investigated. Nevertheless, inhibition of FGFR family members has been shown to enhance sensitivity to gemcitabine in cholangiocarcinoma [101, 102] and cisplatin in ovarian cancer [103]. While there are currently no FGFR inhibitors approved to treat breast cancer, pre-clinical studies have shown efficacy in selected cohorts [104].

In contrast to the immune cold tumors that were almost entirely chemoresistant, the immune hot tumors showed substantial heterogeneity in their response, where approximately half were resistant and half were sensitive. The resistant hot tumors were phenotypically distinct from the immune cold tumors, showing a relatively low level of EMT and FGF phenotypes, and thus, these subtypes were likely to harbor distinct resistance mechanisms. Unexpectedly, among the hot tumors, the resistant and sensitive tumors exhibited no clear differences in their signaling as determined by transcriptomic profiles. While larger cohorts might uncover further distinctions in gene expression, the differences are unlikely to be adequate for clinical utility.

In contrast to the modest difference in overall gene expression, analysis of immune markers revealed clear distinctions. Immune hot resistant tumors were characterized by signatures of less immune engagement, fewer infiltrated lymphocytes, and shorter distances between cancer cells and CD68+ cells. The correlation between infiltrating lymphocytes and response is consistent with a prior study leveraging spatial transcriptomics [105]. Finally, the fact that resistant tumors were also more likely to be accompanied with lymph node metastases was also suggestive of an attenuated immune response since lymph node metastasis has been found to promote immune suppression [106, 107].

The spatial organization of the cancer to CD68+ cells suggest that, in immune hot tumors, macrophages may contribute to resistance to chemotherapy through immunosuppression or secretion of chemoprotective factors [108–111]. Interestingly, the close spatial relationship between cancer cells and CD68+ cells was seen specifically in the immune hot resistant tumors and not in the immune cold resistant tumors. This suggests that macrophage-dependent mechanisms of chemoresistance may be modulated by the presence of lymphocytes, highlighting the importance of cancer cell–myeloid–lymphoid relationships rather than the activity of any individual cell type.

Our data demonstrates that tumor immune contexture is a key factor in chemoresistance and that deeper interrogation of the immune cell composition, localization, and function will be necessary to dissect the mechanisms that drive response in these tumors. Indeed, we recently showed in an unselected subset of this cohort that the spatial proximity of T cells to cancer cells is predictive of pCR [44]. This is in line with studies that have shown heterogeneity in the spatial mixing between cancer and immune cells [72, 112], providing further evidence of complexity in the immune ecosystem. Conclusions

We conducted the ARTEMIS clinical trial and found that including molecular profiling did not help in predicting the response of patients with triple-negative breast cancer to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Instead, signatures of immune activation were the strongest predictor. 50% of immune hot tumors were responsive, compared to only 8% of the immune cold tumors. Immune cold tumors were enriched in mesenchymal features and FGF and TGF-β pathway activation. These results highlight the need for immune-based biomarkers to guide treatments and de-escalate when not needed, and also the need for new strategies to target immune cold tumors.
