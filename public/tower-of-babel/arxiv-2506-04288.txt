Backbone Augmented Training for Adaptations
Jae Wan Park, Junhyeok Kim, Youngjun Jun, Hyunah Ko, Seong Jae Hwang

Abstract
Adaptations facilitate efficient training of large backbone models, including diffusion models for image generation and transformer-based language models. While various adaptation techniques enhance performance with minimal computational resources, limited adaptation data often lead to challenges in training. To address this, we focus on the enormous amount of backbone data used to pre-train the backbone models. We propose B ackbone A ugmented T raining (BAT), a method that leverages backbone data to augment the adaptation dataset. First, we formulate and prove two mathematical key propositions: one establishes the validity of BAT, while the other identifies a condition under which BAT benefits adaptation. Furthermore, we introduce an advanced data selection scheme that satisfies these propositions and present AL gorithm for BAT (ALBAT) to implement this approach. ALBAT efficiently enhances adaptation training in both personalization and language generation tasks with scarce data.

Adaptations.
Fine-tuning a large-scale model to solve a downstream task is extremely expensive. To mitigate this challenge, many have come up with methods that train a small portion of parameters, also known as adaptations. Adaptation methods are widely distinguished as additive fine-tuning

LoRA.

Despite utilizing only a small set of parameters, LoRA achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning, and in certain benchmarks, even surpasses it. Based on the strong performance of LoRA, several variants including DoRA

DreamBooth.
DreamBooth
[

aims to generate images of a specific subject only with the reference images of that subject, using text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models.
Concretely, given images of a subject, DreamBooth learns to bind that specific subject with a unique token identifier. Not only to generate the target subject effectively but also to preserve the knowledge of the pre-trained model, DreamBooth utilizes synthesized images as a regularization dataset.
However, for specialized tasks, gathering the adaptation dataset required to train Dreambooth is extremely challenging, placing a significant burden on users.

3.1
Mathematics on Adaptations

Every adaptation method begins by initializing parameters from its backbone model.
Let
\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}}\in\Theta^{\text{B}}
represent the backbone model parameters, and
\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}\in\Theta^{\text{A}}
encompass all parameters from both the backbone model and adapter.
Then, loading an initialized adapter over the backbone model can be expressed using a continuous function
g
, such that
\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}:=g(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})\in\Theta^{\text{A}}
. That is, denoting
\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}\backslash\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}}
as the parameters exclusive to the adaptation, note that
0<\text{dim}(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}\backslash\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})<\text{dim}(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})
holds.
Typically, the backbone model itself is frozen, allowing only a small subset of parameters to be updated.
Thus, as the training step
n
progresses and the
\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{n}^{\text{A}}
are updated to reach their optimal values
\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}^{*}}
, the parameter training is described as
\textstyle{(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{A}}\backslash\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})}_{n+1}={(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{A}}\backslash\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})}_{n}+\Delta(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}\backslash\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}})_{n+1}
.

We now define risk functions. To begin, let the backbone model be pre-trained with the dataset
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}
via empirical risk minimization. Suppose dataset
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}
is a training set for adaptation, usually constructed by the user. The size of each dataset is noted as
\displaystyle N^{B}:=|\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}|
and
\displaystyle N^{A}:=|\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}|
, respectively, where
\displaystyle n\ll N
is common due to the nature of adaptation.
Recall that backbones and adaptations commonly share the loss function.
We set the backbone risk
R^{\text{B}}_{N}
as

\displaystyle R^{\text{B}}_{N^{B}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N^{B}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},f^{\text{B}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}}))+\lambda\Omega(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}}),

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}^{*}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\Theta^{\text{B}}}{\argmin}~R^{\text{B}}_{N^{B}},

with the regularizer function
\displaystyle\Omega:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and constant
\displaystyle\lambda
to balance the training. On the other hand, adaptation risk
R^{\text{A}}_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle R^{\text{A}}_{N^{A}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{{N^{A}}}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},f^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}))+\lambda\Omega(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}}),

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}^{*}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\Theta^{\text{A}}}{\argmin}~R^{\text{A}}_{N^{A}}.

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}\cap\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}=\varnothing
, the adaptation risk possesses independent characteristics from the backbone risk. Thus, combining both risks does not necessarily ensure convergence.

3.2
Definitions of Backbone Augmented Training

Now, we construct the essential definitions to mathematically formalize Backbone Augmented Training.

Definition 3.1

Backbone augmented training risk on an adaptation is defined as

R^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},f^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}))+\lambda\Omega(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}),

for some
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}=\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}
where
\varnothing\neq\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}.
Here,
k=|\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}|
denotes the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}},
and
\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}=\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1}^{\text{A}}
is assumed for the first training step.

The notation
\text{bat}|\text{A}
in Def.
3.1
stands for the application of BAT to the adaptation
A
. Specifically, the adaptation’s training set
\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}}
incorporates not only the original adaptation dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}
but also a portion of backbone dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}^{\prime}}
).
Meanwhile,
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}=\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1}^{\text{A}}
indicates that when BAT training for an adaptation begins, the adaptation with BAT is initialized with the same weights as the standard adaptation.
We demonstrate in the following section that this BAT risk always increases the performance of adaptations when specific condition (Proposition
4.2
) is satisfied, unlike other random mixture of backbone and adaptation risks.

Definition 3.2

Backbone augmentation ratio is denoted as
\displaystyle n/k\rightarrow\gamma\in(0,1).

This ratio essentially shows the proportion of adaptation data and backbone data used in our method. In this definition, we use convergence to derive the ratio and adopt it in our proposition based on asymptotic.

Lastly, following the format of prior studies on estimators
[
21
,
22
]
, we extend our discussion by introducing asymptotic error coefficients.
We begin by defining the weighted quadratic error as
\displaystyle||\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}-\mathbf{\theta}^{*}||^{2}_{\mathbf{S}}:=\langle\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}-\mathbf{\theta}^{*},\mathbf{S}(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}-\mathbf{\theta}^{*})\rangle
, for some
\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\text{dim}(\Theta)\times\text{dim}(\Theta)}
. Notably, setting
\displaystyle S=\mathbf{I}
gives a simple Euclidean inner product when
\displaystyle R_{N}
is twice differentiable.
Additionally, choosing
\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{\theta}}R_{N}
results in the total risk achieved from the iteration through entire epoch of
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}}
(please refer to
[
22
]
for more detailed structure).
Finally, we define the asymptotic error coefficient as
\displaystyle\rho_{{}_{\text{B}}}(\mathbf{S}):={p\!\!-\!\!\lim}_{{N^{B}}\rightarrow\infty}N||\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{B}}-\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}^{*}}||^{2}_{\mathbf{S}}
.

Definition 3.3

The backbone augmentation coefficient on an adaptation is defined as

\rho_{{}_{\text{bat}|\text{A}}}(\mathbf{S}):={p\!\!-\!\!\lim}_{k\rightarrow\infty}k||\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}-\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{A}^{*}}||^{2}_{\mathbf{S}}.

This coefficient represents the convergence rate toward the optimal adaptation.
Its convergence depends on the limit of the estimator. If the coefficient remains a real value, the estimator is guaranteed to converge to the optimal parameters.

We further define the conditional Hessian matrix for parameters of the backbone risk as
\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{\text{B}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{B}^{*}}}|\mathbf{x}]
. This notation is useful in representing the parameter update in optimization with respect to related variables. If the notation
B
is replaced, then the matrix is associated with another model and its empirical risk.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{n}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}\in U^{n}

(Validity of BAT)

\mathbf{H}^{\text{bat}}=\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}|\mathbf{x}]

\displaystyle\iff(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle||(\mathbf{H}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}})^{-1}\sum_{\mathcal{D}^{\text{bat}}}\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}||\leq||(\mathbf{H}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}-\mathbf{H}^{\text{bat}})^{-1}\sum_{\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}}\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}||+o_{P}(1),

\mathcal{D}^{\text{B}^{\prime}}
in Proposition
4.2

\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}

4

Normalized Loss Sampling Data Selection.

Additionally, we propose a method called
normalized loss sampling data selection
to improve the accuracy of biased scheme results for nondeterministic models. For instance, when a diffusion model generates an image, it iteratively adds and removes noise
\epsilon
, sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
, where
\dim(\mathbf{I})
corresponds to the dimension of the image’s latent representation. To mitigate the model’s nondeterministic behavior, we simply sample the noise
\delta
times. Unless specified, we fix
\delta=3
in all subsequent experiments.

Conclusion

This study addresses the challenges posed by limited adaptation data through the introduction of Backbone Augmented Training (BAT). First, we present two well-founded propositions that provide the theoretical rationale (Proposition
4.1
) and necessary conditions (Proposition
4.2
) for BAT, supported by rigorous proofs. Building on these mathematical foundations, we propose an efficient BAT algorithm, ALBAT (Alg.
1
), designed to operate within practical time complexity. Finally, we validate the effectiveness of BAT through experimental results, demonstrating its capability to enhance adaptation performance.

Limitations and Future Works.
For Sec.
6.5
, our study only presents the empirical results of the alternative of backbone data. Though it is out of our study’s scope, more rigorous mathematical investigation is required in utilizing the data outside of adaptation and backbone data. This will open broader applications for data augmentation for adaptations.

A.1
Basic Notations

For standard notations, we denote consistency of random variables as
\textstyle X_{n}\overset{P}{\rightarrow}X
. Next, using the notation
\displaystyle{p\!\!-\!\!\lim}
, we define probabilistic asymptotics as

X_{n}=o_{P}(a_{n})\iff\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{p\!\!-\!\!\lim}\frac{|X_{n}|}{a_{n}}=0,

(6)

and denote almost sure convergence as

X_{n}\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\iff\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}P(X_{n}=X)=1.

(7)

Lastly, for some matrices
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}
, we denote
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\succeq\mathbf{Y}
if
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}
is positive semi-definite, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\succ\mathbf{Y}
if it is positive definite.

Now, for a parameter space
\Theta
and an estimator
\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}_{n}\in\Theta
, we define an empirical risk function
R_{n}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

R_{n}(\mathbf{\theta}_{n}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathbf{\theta}}\iff R^{\mathbf{\theta}}_{n}=R_{n}(\mathbf{\theta}_{n}),

(8)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\mathbf{\theta}}:=\mathcal{L}(Y_{i},f(X_{i};\mathbf{\theta}_{i})).

Here,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the loss function of the parameters, where
\displaystyle f(\cdotp;\mathbf{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
refers to the model.
Additionally,
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
represent the sampled input and label at training step
i
.
In the case of deterministic sampling, these are denoted using lowercase notations
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

Now, by the law of large numbers, we can define some
\displaystyle R
satisfying
\displaystyle R_{n}\xrightarrow{P}R
. We set
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{n}
to be the nearly minimizing estimator that satisfies the following condition:

R_{n}(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{n})\leq\inf_{\mathbf{\theta}\in\Theta}R_{n}(\mathbf{\theta}_{n})+o_{P}(1).

(9)

Recall that every risk in this study uses sampled sets to optimize their corresponding models. To discuss convergence across the entire sample, we must define the total risk. We can achieve this with a simple expectation as follows:

R(\mathbf{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y},f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\theta})),

(10)

where
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to some distribution
P(\cdot)
.

A.3
Lemma 1

Assume that the map
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}):\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous for almost all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
which is any input data of the estimator. Then, for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\leq\underset{\mathbf{\theta}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{\theta}}{\liminf}\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\textit{almost surely.}

(11)

Proof of Lemma 1.

We begin by recalling the definition of lower semi-continuity. A function
f:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous at
\theta
if:

\liminf_{\theta_{n}\to\theta}f(\theta_{n})\geq f(\theta).

(12)

This property ensures that the function does not suddenly drop in value near
\theta
. Formally, for any sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta
, we have:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(\theta_{n})\geq f(\theta).

(13)

Given that
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
is lower semi-continuous for almost all
x
, we can apply the definition of lower semi-continuity. Specifically, for any
\theta\in\Theta
and any sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta
, it follows that:

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)\leq\liminf_{\theta_{n}\to\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{n}}(x).

(14)

This inequality holds because
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
is assumed to be lower semi-continuous.

The term
almost surely
means that the inequality holds for almost all values of
x
. In other words, there may be a set of measure zero where the inequality does not hold, but this set is negligible.

Thus, for almost every
x
(except on a set of measure zero), the following inequality holds:

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)\leq\liminf_{\theta_{n}\to\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{n}}(x),\quad\text{almost surely.}

(15)

By combining these observations, we conclude that since
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
is lower semi-continuous for almost all
x
, for any sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta
, the lemma is proven.
\Box

A.4
Lemma 2

For any sufficiently small neighborhood
\displaystyle U\subset\Theta
around
\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}

\displaystyle{\inf}_{\mathbf{\theta}\in U}\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the condition of Theorem 1, then the map is measurable and
\displaystyle R(\mathbf{\theta})>-\infty
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}
that satisfies
\displaystyle{\inf}_{\mathbf{\theta}\in U}\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{\theta}}

Proof of Lemma 2.
Using Lemma 1 (Sec.
A.3
), we know that if
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
is lower semi-continuous, then for any
\theta\in\Theta

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)\leq\liminf_{\theta_{n}\to\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\theta_{n}}(x)\quad\text{almost surely}.

(16)

Analyzing the map
\inf_{\theta\in U}\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
, which is the infimum of
\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
over a neighborhood
U\subset\Theta
around
\theta

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}(x)
is assumed to satisfy the lower semi-continuity condition of Lemma 1 (Sec.
A.3

We now show that the map

Proposition 1 for LoRA.
Similar to the case of DreamBooth showing LoRA
[
6
]
continuity will be sufficient to justify Backbone Augmented Training (BAT). Given a pre-trained weight matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, LoRA decomposes the weight update
\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
into the product
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
to get the adapted matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\Delta\mathbf{W}
. Here,
\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
with
r\ll\min\{d,k\}
.
To prove that LoRA is continuous, we need to show that the function
\displaystyle g(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
is continuous.
A function
\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}\text{ and }\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is continuous at
(\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{0})
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that:

\|(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{0})\|<\delta\quad\text{implies}\quad\|f(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})-f(\mathbf{A}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{0})\|<\varepsilon.

(33)

The function
g(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
involves matrix multiplication, which is continuous. The addition of
\mathbf{W}_{0}
is constant and does not affect continuity. Hence, we need to show that the mapping
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\mapsto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
is continuous. Given small perturbations
\Delta\mathbf{A}
and
\Delta\mathbf{B}
, we have:

g(\mathbf{A}+\Delta\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{W}_{0}+(\mathbf{A}+\Delta\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{B}).

(34)

We expand the expression:

\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}+\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}.

(35)

The term
\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}
represents the change in
\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}
due to small perturbations in
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

The perturbation
\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}=\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\Delta\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{B}
can be bounded as:

\|\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}\|\leq\|\mathbf{A}\|\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|+\|\Delta\mathbf{A}\|\|\mathbf{B}\|+\|\Delta\mathbf{A}\|\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|.

(36)

As
\|\Delta\mathbf{A}\|\to 0
and
\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|\to 0
, the perturbation
\|\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}\|\to 0
. Therefore, for any
\epsilon>0
, we can find a
\delta>0
such that if
\|\Delta\mathbf{A}\|<\delta
and
\|\Delta\mathbf{B}\|<\delta
, then
\|\Delta\mathbf{W}_{\text{LoRA}}\|<\epsilon
.

A.9
Derivation of Score Function

Recall the general formula (Eq.
29
) for the asymptotic error coefficient
\rho(\mathbf{S};\mathbf{Q})
. In a non-reweighting scheme with selection probability
\pi
, we write
\rho(\mathbf{S};\mathbf{Q})
as
\rho(\pi;\mathbf{Q})
, with an abuse of notation. Explicitly, we have the following expression for
\rho(\pi;\mathbf{Q})
:

\rho(\pi;\mathbf{Q})=\text{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{G}(x)\right]\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right]\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right]^{-1})

(37)

Notice that this is defined for
\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right]>0
, and we extend it to the case where
\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right]\succeq 0
by introducing a limiting process, as shown in the following equation:

\rho(\pi;\mathbf{Q})=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\text{Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{G}(x)\right](\lambda\mathbf{I}+\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right])\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(x)\mathbf{H}(x)\right])^{-1}

We now aim to minimize this function over
\pi
subject to the convex constraints
\mathbb{E}\pi(x)=\gamma
, and
\pi(x)\in[0,1]
for all
x
.

We claim that a minimizer
\pi_{\text{nr}}
always exists. To prove this, we view
\rho(\pi;\mathbf{Q})=F(\nu)
as a function of the probability measure
\nu(dx)=\pi(x)\mathbb{P}(dx)/\gamma
. In other words,
F
is a function defined on the space of probability measures, where the Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
\mathbb{P}
is upper bounded by
1/\gamma
. This space of probability measures is uniformly tight. Furthermore, if
\nu_{n}
is a sequence in this space and
\nu_{n}\to\nu_{\infty}
weakly, it follows by the Portmanteau’s theorem that
\nu_{\infty}
also has a Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
\mathbb{P}
, which is upper bounded by
1/\gamma
. Hence, this domain is compact by Prokhorov’s theorem.

Finally, since the mappings
\nu\mapsto\int\mathbf{G}(x)\nu(dx)
and
\nu\mapsto\int\mathbf{H}(x)\nu(dx)
are continuous in the topology of weak convergence (because
\mathbf{G}(x)
and
\mathbf{H}(x)
are continuous by assumption), the function
F(\nu)
is lower semi-continuous. Therefore, a minimizer
\nu_{\text{nr}}(dx)=\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)\mathbb{P}(dx)/\gamma
exists, with
\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)\in[0,1]
.

Next, we consider any minimizer
\pi_{\text{nr}}
and any other feasible
\pi
. Let
\pi_{t}:=(1-t)\pi_{\text{nr}}+t\pi
. By assumption,
\mathbf{H}_{\pi_{\text{nr}}}>0
strictly. Then we have:

\rho(\pi_{t};\mathbf{Q})=\rho(\pi_{\text{nr}};\mathbf{Q})+t\int\left(\pi(x)-\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)\right)Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})\mathbb{P}(dx)+o(t)

This leads to the conclusion that for any feasible
\pi
, the following condition must hold:

J(\pi;\pi_{\text{nr}}):=\int\left(\pi(x)-\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)\right)Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})\mathbb{P}(dx)\geq 0

(38)

Let
Q_{\epsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)\in(\epsilon,1-\epsilon)\right\}
. The claim (4.10) is implied by the following statement:
Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})
is almost surely constant on
Q_{\epsilon}
for each
\epsilon>0
. Assume by contradiction that such constancy does not hold. We then define:

Q_{+}:=\{x\in Q_{\epsilon}:Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})\geq z_{0}\},\quad Q_{-}:=\{x\in Q_{\epsilon}:Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})<z_{0}\}

Define
\pi(x)
as:

\pi(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)-p_{\epsilon}&\text{if }x\in Q_{+},\\
\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)+p_{\epsilon}&\text{if }x\in Q_{-},\\
\pi_{\text{nr}}(x)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is easy to check that
\pi
is feasible and:

J(\pi;\pi_{\text{nr}})=-p_{\epsilon}\int_{Q_{+}}Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})\mathbb{P}(dx)+p_{\epsilon}\int_{Q_{-}}Z(x;\pi_{\text{nr}})\mathbb{P}(dx)

which leads to a contradiction with Eq.
38
. Finally, we conclude that the stated form of
\rho_{\text{nr}}
follows from Eq.
37
.

\displaystyle\square

A.10
Proof of Theorem 5.1

Since the assumption states that the empirical risk is a logarithmic function, we can apply Bartlett’s second identity so that all
\mathbf{H}
is now
\mathbf{G}
in the calculation of
Z
. However, a deep learning model’s
\mathbf{G}
is often not invertible as
n\ll D
. By using damping Hessian
[
39
]
that regularizes the Hessian with a very small number as the dimension of the matrix added to be invertible, this problem can be solved. Also, to deal with many layers of a deep learning model, one can define the model as
f_{\theta}(x)=f_{\theta_{L}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\theta_{1}}(x)
according to
[
40
]
. Then, one can show that each layer,
\mathbf{G}_{l}(\theta):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}^{T}
when
\mathbf{G}
is now
\text{diag}(\mathbf{G}_{1}(\theta),\dots,\mathbf{G}_{L}(\theta))
for
l\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
.

Combining both approaches,
\mathbf{G}
, the second moment of first gradients, becomes
\left(\mathbf{G}_{l}(\theta^{*})+\lambda_{l}I_{d_{l}}\right).
Recall that

\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})^{\top}|x]\approx\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}^{T}+\lambda_{l}I_{d_{l}}\right).

(39)

Then, by the damping factor and Taylor’s approximation, the summation can be swapped and by Sherman Morrison’s formula, the following holds:

\mathbf{G}^{-1}=(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}^{T}+\lambda_{l}I_{d_{l}}\right))^{-1}=\frac{1}{n\lambda_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(I_{d_{l}}-\frac{\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}^{T}}{\lambda_{l}+\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}^{T}\nabla_{\theta_{l}}\ell_{i}}\right).

(40)

This derivation is from
[
23
]
and concludes the time complexity for
\mathbf{G}=O(nDL)
. Now, by the fact that
n,L\ll D
, the time complexity for
Z
is also bounded by
O(nDL).

A.11
Justification of Surrogate Model using Minimax Theorem.

First, assume the worst case of the surrogate model,

\sup_{\mathbf{y}}\inf_{\Theta^{A}}R_{N}(\mathbf{\theta}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}};\mathbf{y}).

(41)

Then, from the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we know that

\sup_{\mathbf{y}}R(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}_{\text{surrogate}};\mathbf{y})\leq\sup_{\mathbf{y}}\inf_{\Theta^{A}}R(\mathbf{\theta}^{A};\mathbf{y})+\epsilon,

(42)

for some surrogate model with a well designed adaptation. By the assumptions from Sec.
4.1
, Minimax theorem can be applied so that a single worst case comparison can be generalized as

\sup_{\mathbf{y}}R(\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\text{bat}|\text{A}}_{\text{surrogate}};\mathbf{y})\leq\inf_{\Theta^{A}}\sup_{\mathbf{y}}R(\mathbf{\theta}^{A};\mathbf{y})+\epsilon=\sup_{\mathbf{y}}R(\mathbf{\theta}^{A^{*}};\mathbf{y})+\epsilon.

(43)

C.1
Metrics

Using DINOv2
[
52
]
, cosine similarity is used to measure the similarity between two feature vectors, often extracted from image representations. Given two vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}
, their cosine similarity is computed as:

\text{Cosine Similarity}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|\|\mathbf{v}_{2}\|}.

(44)

The centroid represents the mean vector of a set of feature vectors. The squared centroid is the square of the distance between the centroid and each data point. Suppose we have
N
data points
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The centroid
\mathbf{c}
is given by:

\mathbf{c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

(45)

The squared centroid distance for each point
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is:

\text{Squared Centroid Distance}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{c}\|^{2}.

(46)

Where
\|\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{c}\|^{2}
is the squared Euclidean distance between each point and the centroid. Lower centroid score shows that the output is more consistent with lower variance which infers better generalization.

CLIP uses cosine similarity to compare text and image embeddings. The model learns to maximize the similarity between matching text-image pairs while minimizing the similarity between non-matching pairs.
Let
\mathbf{t}
be the text embedding and
\mathbf{i}
be the image embedding. The similarity score between them is calculated as:

\text{CLIP Similarity}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{i})=\frac{\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{i}}{\|\mathbf{t}\|\|\mathbf{i}\|}.

(47)

As
\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{i}
is the dot product between the text and image embedding, and
\|\mathbf{t}\|
and
\|\mathbf{i}\|
are the norms of the text and image embeddings. The cosine similarity is maximized for relevant text-image pairs and minimized for irrelevant pairs.

The Vendi score is a metric used to quantify similarity across multiple domains or datasets. It measures the overlap between sets of embeddings from different modalities (e.g., vision, text). Mathematically, Vendi score uses the concept of overlapping support across distributions.

Given two distributions of feature vectors
P
and
Q
, the Vendi score can be formulated as:

\text{Vendi Score}(P,Q)=\int\min(P(x),Q(x))dx.

(48)

This score evaluates how much of the support of one distribution is shared by the other, effectively measuring their similarity. Higher Vendi scores indicate greater overlap between distributions. Therefore, in the case of adaptations, lower Vendi scores implies the concentration of identity.
