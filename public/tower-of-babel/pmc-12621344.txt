Revealing Non-Conventional and Thermotolerant Yeasts from Tropical Regions: Diversity and Potential Traits for Bioproducts
Authors: Mochamad Nurcholis, Salsabilla Izzah Nurheibah, Adhiskaprillia Nur Anissa, Jaya Mahar Maligan, Suprayogi, Sudarma Dita Wijayanti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diminishing reserves of fossil fuels have spurred a global search for sustainable energy alternatives. Bioethanol, a renewable fuel derived from biomass, represents a promising alternative, particularly in tropical regions. Yeasts, key agents in ethanol fermentation, have attracted substantial attention for their ability to convert sugars into ethanol. However, the commonly used mesophilic yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae is temperature-sensitive, limiting its effectiveness in tropical climates. Tropical countries such as Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, and Laos, harbor abundant non-conventional and thermotolerant yeast species. These yeasts offer multiple advantages, including reduced contamination risk, lower cooling requirements, and decreased production costs. Moreover, they can utilize complex substrates, such as sucrose, starch, xylose, and lignocellulose biomass, while tolerating high temperatures, ethanol stress, pH fluctuations, and various inhibitors. This review highlights the diversity, physiological traits, and dual industrial potential of thermotolerant and non-conventional yeasts isolated from tropical environments. These yeasts demonstrate robust substrate utilization (including pentoses), high-temperature tolerance, and resilience under stress, positioning them as promising candidates for second-generation bioethanol production as well as for producing value-added aroma compounds. Key species discussed include Ogataea polymorpha, Candida tropicalis, Kluyveromyces marxianus, Pichia kudriavzevii, Meyerozyma caribbica, Pichia stipitis, and Spathaspora passalidarum.

Introduction

Bioethanol fermentation in tropical regions faces significant challenges, primarily due to high ambient temperatures that impair the performance of conventional fermentation yeasts, particularly mesophilic Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Bioethanol, a renewable fuel produced from biomass rich in sugars, starches, or cellulose, has experienced increasing global demand driven by diminishing fossil fuel reserves and increasing environmental concerns [1]. According to the 2017 National Energy General Plan (RUEN), the projected demand for fuel oil in 2020 reached 1.5 million barrels per day, while domestic petroleum production was limited to 520.3 thousand barrels per day, with 30% exported-resulting in a substantial energy deficit [2].

Despite its potential as an eco-friendly alternative, bioethanol production still faces key hurdles, including high production costs, the need for complex pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass, and the temperature sensitivity of commonly used yeast strains [3,4]. Industrial-scale ethanol production is typically conducted at elevated temperatures to reduce contamination risks and cooling costs [5]. Therefore, the development of efficient, cost-effective fermentation process is crucial.

One promising strategy involves the exploration and utilization of thermotolerant microorganisms, particularly yeasts, capable of efficiently fermenting biomass under tropical conditions. Yeasts are unicellular fungi characterized by spherical, oval, or cylindrical morphologies, typically ranging from 3 to 5 micrometers in size [6]. More than 2000 yeast species have been described, with applications spanning food and beverage production, bioethanol synthesis, biomass conversion, and glycerol generation [7–9].

S. cerevisiae has traditionally dominated fermentation processes due to its robust ethanol-producing capacity, ethanol tolerance, adaptability to pH variations, and generally recognized as safe (GRAS) status [10]. However, its limited thermotolerance restricts its application in tropical environments. In contrast, thermotolerant yeasts offer several advantages, including accelerated metabolism, higher fermentation rates, reduced oxygen solubility, lower energy requirements for agitation and cooling, and decreased risk of contamination-all contributing to lower production costs [11].

Several thermotolerant yeasts, including Candida tropicalis, Pichia sp, Pichia chibodasensis, and Kluyveromyces marxianus have been identified [12]. These yeasts predominantly belong to the phylum Ascomycota and the class Saccharomycetes [13] are frequently isolated from diverse tropical ecosystems such as soils, fruits, flowers, and fermented foods [14]. While S. cerevisiae remains the primary organism in industrial ethanol production, non-conventional yeasts possess unique physiological and metabolic traits that position them as strong candidates-particularly for fermenting lignocellulosic biomass. This review comprehensively explores the diversity, physiological traits, and biotechnological potential of non-conventional thermotolerant yeasts isolated from tropical resources. By highlighting their resilience to environmental stressors and ability to ferment complex substrates, we emphasize their suitability for sustainable, cost-effective industrial bioethanol and aromatic compound production in tropical climates.

Diversity of non-conventional yeast

Non-conventional yeasts are defined as any yeast species outside the Saccharomyces genus. Although some are associated with food spoilage, many exhibit significant biotechnological potential [15]. Globally, approximately 2000 yeast species have been identified, representing only about 1% of the estimated total yeast biodiversity [8]. This vast, largely untapped diversity suggests the existence of unique metabolic capabilities and environmental adaptations. Many non-conventional yeasts have evolved independently from S. cerevisiae, resulting in distinct physiological traits. Some species exhibit extraordinary abilities to survive in extreme environments, making them highly valuable for industrial applications [15]. Tropical regions, in particular, offer diverse ecological niches that foster the emergence of thermotolerant and stress-tolerant yeasts. This diversity reflects the adaptability of non-conventional yeasts to various environmental conditions and highlights their potential as industrial biocatalysts, particularly in bioethanol production under tropical conditions. Characteristics of non-conventional yeast

Non-conventional yeasts possess distinctive physiological and metabolic traits that differentiate them from S. cerevisiae. One of their most notable characteristics is the ability to ferment a broad spectrum of sugars, making them strong candidates for second-generation bioethanol production, particularly from lignocellulosic biomass. Lignocellulosic biomass-composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin-requires pretreatment to release fermentable sugars such as glucose and xylose. While S. cerevisiae readily ferments glucose, it struggles to metabolize xylose, limiting its effectiveness in lignocellulosic ethanol production [35]. In contrast, several non-conventional yeasts – such as P. stipitis, M. caribbica, and K. marxianus – demonstrate the ability to ferment multiple sugars, including pentoses like xylose and arabinose. Yeasts generally ferment sugars via the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas (EMP) pathway, producing pyruvate under either aerobic or anaerobic conditions. Pyruvate is subsequently converted to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase [40]. During fermentation, yeast cells undergo distinct growth phases (lag, log, and stationary), converting substrates into carbon dioxide, ethanol, and various secondary metabolites, including lipids, amino acids, nucleotides, and esters. Yeasts have a long-standing history of use in alcoholic fermentation processes. Successful fermentation depends on factors such as nutrient composition, pH (typically between 3.5 and 6.0), and temperature (ranging from 28 °C to 35 °C) [41,42]. Many non-conventional yeasts can maintain fermentative activity even under challenging conditions, such as high sugar concentrations or oxygen-limited environments. In high-sugar media, 3–20% of glucose may be assimilated, while the remainder is utilized for ethanol production [6]. This resilience is often attributed to stress-response mechanisms such as trehalose accumulation, redox balance regulation, and enhanced activity of sugar transporters and alcohol dehydrogenases [43–45].

In addition to their broad sugar utilization profiles, non-conventional yeasts exhibit impressive tolerance to environmental stressors, including elevated temperatures, high ethanol concentrations, acidic or alkaline pH, and fermentation inhibitors. These traits are critical for achieving robust fermentation under industrial conditions. Such tolerance is typically mediated by genetic and biochemical adaptations, including expression of heat shock proteins (e.g., HSPs) [36], antioxidant defenses (e.g., SOD) [44], osmoprotectants (e.g., trehalose) [45], and detoxification enzymes (e.g., aldehyde reductases) [46]. The resilience of these yeasts to various stresses is crucial for achieving efficient, large-scale fermentation processes. For instance, the ability to tolerate high ethanol concentrations helps maintain cell viability during fermentation, while thermotolerance reduces the need for cooling systems, thereby lowering overall production costs. Moreover, some non-conventional yeasts can synthesize valuable aromatic compounds, such as esters and higher alcohols, which enhance their potential for applications beyond bioethanol production, including the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries. In the following section, we further examine the specific traits and industrial potential of these non-conventional, thermotolerant yeast species for sustainable development of bioproducts.

Each species offers distinct advantages, such as xylose fermentation, ethanol and acid tolerance, and efficient substrate utilization. Their traits make them suitable for second-generation bioethanol production, especially from lignocellulosic biomass. Overall, these yeasts represent valuable alternatives to traditional strains for sustainable and cost-efficient bioethanol processes in tropical regions.

Key traits and industrial potential of non-conventional yeasts

The superior characteristics outlined in Tables 3 and 4 are crucial for efficient second-generation bioethanol fermentation from lignocellulosic biomass. In addition to ethanol production, non-conventional yeasts also possess the potential to synthesize aromatic compounds. Lignocellulosic biomass requires pretreatment to facilitate hydrolysis and release fermentable sugars; however, chemical pretreatment methods involving strong acids or bases often generate toxic byproducts, creating a stressful environment for yeast cells. Therefore, selecting yeast strains with traits such as osmotolerance to high sugar concentrations and resistance to toxic compounds is essential for successful fermentation [15].

Moreover, the ability to simultaneously hydrolyze multiple substrates and ferment under anaerobic conditions can significantly reduce both fermentation time and operating costs. Thermotolerance is another desirable trait, as it helps lower cooling costs and minimize contamination risks [55]. Ethanol tolerance is equally important, since ethanol toxicity can damage yeast membranes, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and triggering stress responses such as the expression of heat shock protein and accumulation of trehalose [14,43–45].

Aromatic compounds, which are cyclic structures containing six carbons atoms, are highly valued in the chemical, food, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries. Traditionally, they are produced through chemical synthesis from petroleum derivatives or extracted from natural sources. Microbial production-particularly by non-conventional yeasts-offers a more sustainable and renewable alternative for aromatic compound synthesis [46].

Non-conventional yeasts isolated from Southeast Asia exhibit thermotolerant properties, attributed to the tropical climate characterized by moderate to high temperatures, high humidity, and evenly distributed rainfall. These conditions create ideal ecological niches for a wide range of yeast species. Studies conducted in various tropical countries have revealed unique species characteristics and diverse isolation sources (Table 5).

The potential of thermotolerant non-conventional yeasts from Southeast Asia for high-temperature fermentation has been well documented. Yeasts isolated from fermented fruits and foods in Indonesia, in particular, exhibit greater thermotolerance compared to those isolated from soil and water. Most isolates were able to grow at approximately 40 °C when using glucose, xylose, and sucrose as substrates, although growth declined at 45 °C [14]. Further studies in Indonesia have identified yeasts from various sources, including water, soil, vegetables, fruits, and fermented foods. Most of these isolates demonstrated robust growth at temperatures exceeding 37 °C and exhibited ethanol production capabilities, with the exception of C. ethanolica 3-1-19, which showed sensitivity to temperature above 35 °C [14,45,57–59].

Thailand also displays a comparable diversity of yeasts, with Pichia strains being particular abundant in soil, in contrast to their predominance in fruits observed in Indonesia [25]. The K. marxianus strain DMKU 3–1042, isolated in Thailand, demonstrated remarkable growth and fermentation capacity at elevated temperatures, producing between 28 and 67.8 g/L of bioethanol from lignocellulosic biomass or sugarcane [5]. This strain further exhibits efficient xylose conversion, resistance to 2-deoxyglucose, and tolerance to multiple environmental stressors [14]. K. marxianus has similarly been isolated in Vietnam and other regions, underscoring its remarkable adaptability to diverse environmental conditions [49].

In Vietnam, yeasts have been sourced from various substrates, including ripe flowers, fruit trees, cocoa, fermented products, alcoholic fermentation starters, sugarcane, molasses, sawdust, agricultural byproducts, and soil samples. Common species identified include C. tropicalis, S. cerevisiae, P. kudriavzevii, and Candida glabrata. While most isolates were capable of growing at 37 °C, approximately 80.45% could grow at 40.43 °C and 10% at 45 °C. In Laos, yeast isolates have mainly been obtained from fruits, vegetables, leaves, and soil. These isolates were categorized into two groups: those capable of high-temperature fermentation using glucose, sucrose, sugarcane juice, and molasses; and those capable of converting xylose to ethanol at 37 °C. Representative species include C. tropicalis, P. kudriavzevii, and K. marxianus.

Ethanol yield is a critical parameter in the selection of yeast strains for industrial applications. Among the species reviewed, P. stipitis exhibits significant promise, attributed to its superior ability to ferment xylose-rich substrates without requiring additional vitamins or supplements [68]. The non-conventional yeasts discussed are capable of fermenting various substrates, particularly lignocellulosic biomass, making them suitable candidates for second-generation bioethanol production. In terms of thermotolerance, O. polymorpha and K. marxianus demonstrate the most promising performance at elevated temperatures, offering the advantage of reduced cooling costs during industrial fermentation processes. In contrast, S. cerevisiae, Candida sorboxylosa, and Candida apicola exhibit optimal growth at ambient temperatures, typically not exceeding 37 °C [71,72]. For industrial-scale ethanol production, yeasts that combine high-temperature tolerance with strong ethanol tolerance are particularly desirable. Such strains can be developed through natural evolutionary processes or by applying genetic engineering approaches.

Comparative overview of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and non-conventional yeasts

Although extensive research has been conducted on thermotolerant yeasts, much of the focus has centered on the genetic modification of S. cerevisiae. However, Table 7 highlights several other yeast species that exhibit notable thermotolerance. S. cerevisiae has historically served as the primary yeast for industrial ethanol fermentation due to its high ethanol yield, robust glucose metabolism, and well-characterized genetic toolkit [10,72]. However, its application in tropical regions is constrained by limited thermotolerance – optimal growth occurs between 28 °C and 35 °C with inhibition above 38 °C – and by its inability to ferment pentose sugars such as xylose and arabinose, which are prevalent in lignocellulosic biomass [3,4,29]. In contrast, non-conventional yeasts such as K. marxianus, P. kudriavzevii, O. polymorpha, and S. passalidarum offer broader substrate utilization – including both hexoses and pentoses – and maintain fermentative activity at higher temperatures (up to 42–45 °C) [5,24,36,39]. These species also exhibit enhanced tolerance to ethanol, low pH, and fermentation inhibitors, making them more resilient under industrial stress conditions [25,44,45]. Furthermore, non-conventional yeasts can produce diverse aroma compounds (e.g., esters, higher alcohols) under thermophilic conditions, offering added value for the food, beverage, and fragrance industries [39,73,74]. Although genetic tools for these yeasts are still emerging, platforms for K. marxianus, P. kudriavzevii, and O. polymorpha have progressed significantly, enabling targeted strain development for bioethanol and bioproduct synthesis [39,49].

In addition to their ability to withstand elevated temperatures, these non-conventional yeasts possess additional traits that enhance their suitability for ethanol fermentation. Pichia kudriavzevii and Issatchenkia orientalis demonstrate multi-stress tolerance, thriving in environments with high ethanol concentrations and low pH, while maintaining metabolic activity under stress conditions [25]. Table 7 summarizes the ethanol production capacities of selected thermotolerant yeasts isolated from tropical environments. K. marxianus is recognized for its ease of cultivation and rapid growth rates [75]. M. caribbica shows moderate ethanol production capability under high-temperature fermentation conditions, with reported yields of 19.87 g/L [28]. In contrast, the highest ethanol yield reported in the screening study-57.10 g/L-was achieved by K. marxianus, underscoring its industrial potential for thermotolerant bioethanol production [28].

These findings emphasize the importance of accurate species-specific screening and validation, as broad yield ranges may reflect interspecies variation rather than within-species performance. The ability of these thermotolerant yeasts to perform well under elevated temperatures makes them suitable candidates for industrial-scale bioethanol production, particularly in tropical climates where high ambient temperatures prevail.

Sources and isolation of bioethanol-producing thermotolerant yeast

Tropical countries, characterized by high plant biodiversity, moderate temperatures, suitable humidity, and evenly distributed rainfall, offer ideal habitats for diverse plants and microbial life [76]. This rich environment supports a broad range of yeast and fungal species. Research indicates that tropical regions-particularly Southeast Asia-are rich sources of thermotolerant yeasts, owing to these favorable climatic conditions. Yeasts have been isolated from various plant parts, including flowers, stems, fruits, and surrounding soil [77]. Tropical fruits and flowers, especially those cultivated in lowland areas, typically contain higher sugar concentrations due to increased sunlight exposure and extended photosynthesis periods. The abundance of sugars creates optimal conditions for yeast proliferation and fermentation [78].

Studies have shown that yeasts isolated from tropical fruits and fermented foods in countries such as Indonesia, Thailand, and Vietnam exhibit greater thermotolerance than those isolated from soil and water, often thriving at temperatures around 40 °C [14]. This enhanced thermotolerance is partly attributed to the high glucose content in tropical fruits, which supports vigorous yeast growth [40]. Species distribution and isolation sources vary across tropical regions. In Indonesia, common yeast species include S. cerevisiae, Pichia spp., and Candida spp. The former two are predominantly isolated from fruits, flowers, fruit waste, and dairy products, while Candida spp. are frequently isolated from soil samples at approximately 30 °C [56]. In Thailand, Pichia strains are predominantly recovered from soil samples [25], whereas in Ethiopia, yeast isolates are mainly derived from fruits, with Candida spp. being the most prevalent [71]. K. marxianus is widely distributed in Vietnam and neighboring regions, attributed to its high adaptability to diverse environmental conditions [46]. These favorable environmental and biological conditions have stimulated extensive research efforts aimed at isolating thermotolerant yeasts from tropical regions. Aroma compound production by thermotolerant yeasts

Beyond bioethanol production, non-conventional yeasts contribute significantly to aromatic compound synthesis. These yeasts enhance aroma complexity in alcoholic beverages by increasing acetate ester yields and producing fruity notes [73,74,86]. Similar enhancements have been observed in bakery products, where non-conventional yeasts improve the diversity and richness of aroma profiles, meeting the evolving demands of global markets.

Thermotolerant and non-conventional yeasts such as K. marxianus, C. tropicalis, and P. kudriavzevii have been shown to naturally produce aromatic volatiles even at elevated temperatures, making them suitable for simultaneous ethanol and aroma production in tropical or thermophilic bioprocesses. The biosynthesis of aroma compounds is commonly linked to the Ehrlich pathway, where amino acids, such as phenylalanine, leucine, and valine are catabolized into fusel alcohols (e.g., 2-phenylethanol) through transamination, decarboxylation, and reduction steps. Another major route is the de novo lipid biosynthesis and acetyl-CoA metabolism, which produce esters such as ethyl acetate.

Biotechnological applications and recent innovations

Lignocellulosic biomass generally requires pretreatment to enhance hydrolysis efficiency and increase the availability of fermentable sugars. Common pretreatment methods involve the use of acidic or alkaline chemical reagents. However, these processes often generate toxic byproducts that can inhibit microbial fermentation. Moreover, ethanol – the principal product of anaerobic yeast fermentation – is inherently toxic to yeast cells, compromising membrane integrity, promoting the generation of ROS, and inducing stress responses such as heat shock protein expression and trehalose accumulation [15,43,44,55]. Consequently, maintaining yeast viability and ensuring high ethanol tolerance are critical for sustaining production performance.

While many yeast species are suitable for bioethanol production, thermotolerant yeasts often exhibit lower ethanol tolerance compared to their mesophilic counterparts [71]. Conversely, yeasts with high ethanol tolerance may lack adequate thermotolerance. Therefore, enhancing ethanol tolerance and other key physiological traits in thermotolerant yeast strains is essential for optimizing industrial bioethanol production. Conclusions and perspectives

Tropical regions, characterized by warm and humid climates, harbor a rich diversity of non-conventional and thermotolerant yeasts with significant biotechnological potential. These yeasts exhibit superior traits, including the ability to ferment a wide range of substrates, tolerate high temperatures, and resist environmental stresses such as elevated ethanol concentrations and toxic fermentation byproducts. Their resilience under extreme conditions positions them as promising candidates for sustainable second-generation bioethanol production, particularly from lignocellulosic biomass. Furthermore, non-conventional yeasts offer added value through the synthesis of aromatic compounds, contributing to industries beyond biofuel, such as food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. The adaptability and metabolic versatility demonstrated by species such as K. marxianus, P. kudriavzevii, C. tropicalis, and O. polymorpha underscore the importance of continued exploration and development of yeast biodiversity in tropical environments. Notably, several species also possess the ability to synthesize industrially relevant aroma compounds – such as esters and fusel alcohols – under thermophilic conditions, making them attractive for integrated biorefineries focused on both biofuel and bioflavor production. Their ecological origins – from fruits, flowers, soils, and agro-industrial byproducts – highlight their adaptive capacity and suitability for low-cost bioprocesses. Future directions should prioritize the integration of systems and synthetic biology tools, including genome-scale metabolic modeling, CRISPR/Cas9 engineering, and adaptive evolution, to enhance key traits such as thermotolerance, pentose fermentation, and aroma compound synthesis. Leveraging such strategies will expand the utility of tropical yeast biodiversity in developing sustainable and multi-functional biotechnological platforms.
