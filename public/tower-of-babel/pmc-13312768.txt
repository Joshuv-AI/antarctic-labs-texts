CRAK-Velo: chromatin accessibility kinetics integration improves RNA velocity estimation
Authors: Nour El Kazwini, Mingze Gao, Idris Kouadri Boudjelthia, Fangxin Cai, Yuanhua Huang, Guido Sanguinetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA velocity has recently emerged as a key tool in the analysis of single-cell transcriptomic data, yet connecting RNA velocity analyses to underlying regulatory processes has proved challenging. Here, we propose CRAK-Velo, a semi-mechanistic model that integrates chromatin accessibility data in the estimation of RNA velocities. CRAK-Velo provides biologically consistent estimates of developmental flows and enables accurate cell-type deconvolution, while additionally shining light on regulatory processes at the level of interactions between genes and chromatin regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04086-y.Keywords: RNA velocity, Chromatin accessibility, Region kinetics

Background

Recent advances in single-cell sequencing technologies are leading to unprecedented insights in the molecular biology of cells [1–10]. However, the intrinsically destructive nature of sequencing creates a significant limitation in using such technologies to elucidate the dynamics of cellular processes. To circumvent this problem, La Manno et al. [11] proposed the concept of RNA velocity, a ground-breaking method which exploits splicing information to determine whether individual genes are being up-regulated or repressed at the time the sequencing happened. When aggregated at the cell level, this information provides valuable insight into the developmental or adaptation processes happening through the population of cells.

The RNA velocity concept has been extended and refined in a number of recent papers [12–17], yet all of these methods need to specify or learn from data the transcription rate of individual genes in each cell. This either imposes significant constraints on the models (when the transcription rate is postulated to be e.g. a binary switch), or greatly expands the number of parameters required by the model. Alternatively, one might use additional epigenomic information, such as chromatin accessibility, to explain transcription rates; such information is increasingly becoming available through multi-omic single-cell techniques, including SNARE-seq [18], SHARE-seq [15], and the 10x Genomics Multiome commercial kit. This idea has been recently exploited by MultiVelo [19], an elegant model which aggregates chromatin accessibility and expression data through a differential equations latent variable model.

Here, we present CRAK-Velo (ChRomatin Accessibility Kinetics integration in RNA Velocity), a simpler model that directly integrates chromatin accessibility data in the estimation of individual gene transcription rates.

Results

CRAK-Velo draws inspiration from UniTVelo [12], a recently proposed RNA velocity model which estimates transcription rates using a parametric approximation, but expands it by explicitly connecting transcription rates to observed open region probabilities (quantified using a probabilistic topic model [20, 21]; see Methods for the mathematical formulation of the model). This achieves two main goals: it regularizes the estimation of transcription rates, ensuring consistency with chromatin information, and it quantifies the impact of each regulatory region during transcriptional dynamics. In the following, we demonstrate CRAK-Velo on two challenging developmental data sets, as well as on a data set included in the additional files (Additional file 1: Table S2). We report run-time comparisons (Additional file 1: Table S1) between the three methods, and focus on the ability of CRAK-Velo to extract biological insights from complex data; technical specifications, including robustness, are discussed as Supplementary Material (Additional file 2: Figs. S1, S2).

We then considered the usefulness of the CRAK-Velo representation to distinguish cell types. Figure 1c shows the standard RNA velocity phase portraits (spliced vs unspliced read counts) for selected genes within the first row, while the second and third rows show unspliced reads vs chromatin effect for the same genes for CRAK-Velo and MultiVelo, respectively. Different cell types are coloured differently; visually, using chromatin accessibility shows a better separation of cell types compared to using only un/spliced reads. This can be quantified by evaluating the accuracy of a nearest neighbour classifier on these two-dimensional data sets, as proposed in [21] (see Methods). The accuracy achieved by the classifier is reported in the inset in the individual panels in Fig. 1c, showing a stronger performance of CRAK-Velo in all the cases. Figure 1d shows histograms of the accuracy of such a classifier on all genes for the different methods. Methods using chromatin information retrieve in general a higher accuracy, but while this effect is modest for MultiVelo, CRAK-Velo provides a much greater accuracy for a very large number of genes.

While the previous results demonstrate the ability of CRAK-Velo to extract meaningful information at the cell level, we now examine its ability to recover meaningful biological information at the level of individual genes and gene-region interactions. In order to do so, we propose a novel ranking of genes based on their interactions with nearby accessible regions. We reason that, while individual genes may be associated with multiple nearby cis-regulatory regions, in any particular biological condition only few regions are likely to control the expression dynamics of a gene. We therefore use the distribution of inferred region-gene interaction weights to define an entropy metric which we use to rank genes (see Methods for mathematical details). This novel entropy metric explicitly integrates both expression and chromatin information, and can be used to identify key genes and pathways involved in the biological process under study. Figure 1e reports the results of a GO analysis summarizing functional enrichment among low-entropy genes. Several pathways involved in hematopoietic and lineage-specific biological processes Fig prominently, indicating that CRAK-Velo preferentially identifies genes whose function is consistent with the differentiation trajectories inferred earlier. Next, we investigated whether inferences from CRAK-Velo can also be used to detect signals about the regulators of these genes. To do so, we interrogated ChIP-ATLAS, a large database of ChIP-seq experiments [22], for transcription factor binding assayed in relevant cell types (more details in data availability section). Figure 1f shows the enrichment of transcription factor (TF) binding within the high-weighted chromatin regions inferred by CRAK-Velo, demonstrating that the model not only captures transcriptional dynamics but also recovers biologically meaningful regulatory elements. The significant log-enrichment values suggest that CRAK-Velo’s weighting scheme effectively prioritizes regions that correspond to genuine TF binding sites, confirming the model’s interpretability in linking accessibility features to gene regulation. The enriched transcription factors include well characterized TFs like IKZF1 (Ikaros), key in lymphoid lineage commitment and chromatin organization, and MEIS1 an essential for HSC self-renewal, once again highlighting the biological relevance of our findings. Finally, we illustrate the power of CRAK-Velo in deciphering the mechanisms of RNA expression kinetics on an example. Figure 1g further illustrates this for the KLF1 gene, a key regulator in erythroid differentiation. The plot shows in blue the average unspliced read counts as a function of pseudotime, while coloured traces represent the activity of individual chromatin regions, defined as the product of the open chromatin probability and the inferred regional weight. It shows a clear temporal coupling between chromatin accessibility and unspliced RNA abundance across pseudotime. For KLF1, it is evident that the increasing transcriptional trajectory is driven by an accessible region-proximal region with high accessibility that later declines, producing a transient expression pattern. Notice that the expression of the gene follows a delayed pattern with respect to the region’s activity, as implied by the dynamical system of CRAK-Velo. The final decrease in activity of the region is only partly compensated by the increase in activity in other more distal regions which are assigned lower weight by the model. The accompanying arrow plot visualises the weight associated with each regulatory region and its genomic distance from the transcription start site, revealing that accessible regions-proximal sites dominate regulatory influence during activation. We also consider the intersection of chromatin loci with TF binding sites; regions showing both high intersection counts and strong CRAK-Velo weights are likely to act as major control points for gene activation, as shown in the bar plot of Fig. 1g. Together, these analyses validate that CRAK-Velo integrates chromatin accessibility in a way that reflects real regulatory hierarchies and transcriptional control mechanisms.

Next, we applied CRAK-Velo to another challenging data set, detailing mouse embryonic brain development at day 18 using 10X Multiome technology. While all three methods obtain broadly similar flows (Fig. 2a), a detailed observation reveals subtle but relevant differences, particularly regarding the dynamics at the boundary between the terminally differentiated states of the Upper and Deeper Layer. We use the PAGA graph approach [23] to summarize transitions in Fig. 2b. Both MultiVelo and UniTVelo recover a spurious flow from Upper Layer to Deeper Layers, while CRAK-Velo correctly recognises these cell states as independent terminally differentiated states.This is appreciated more clearly in Fig. 2c, where the inset shows in detail the bifurcating flow at the boundary of subplate and Upper and Deeper Layer. An analysis of the inferred latent time (Fig. 2a) also confirms this observation, as both MultiVelo and UniTVelo predict the deeper layers to develop after the upper layers, while CRAK-Velo assigns them both a similar pseudotime, consistent with biological knowledge. Limitations nevertheless remain, as all three methods were not able to identify ependymal cells as a terminal state. Next, we quantified the ability of the various methods to deconvolve cell types based on their inner representation. Figure 2f and h provide a second, independent demonstration of how CRAK-Velo recovers meaningful gene–regulatory structure, which shows the model’s reliability across distinct biological contexts. In Fig. 2f, the enrichment of TF binding within high-weighted chromatin regions implies that CRAK-Velo assigns weights to regulatory elements that are likely to be biologically active. The transcription factors enriched in the Fig collectively regulate neural stem cell maintenance, neurogenesis, and neuronal differentiation. For example, the most enriched TF, Pou3f2, is a central regulator of neuronal differentiation and identity, particularly important for cortical and hippocampal neuron development. The next most enriched factor, Sox2, helps maintain neural stem cell self-renewal and prevents premature differentiation. Additionally, Junb acts as an activity-responsive transcription factor that adjusts gene expression in response to neuronal stimulation.

Using the entropy-based ranking introduced earlier, a similar theme emerges in the enrichment analysis of Fig. 2g, where low-entropy genes—those regulated by a concentrated subset of regions—cluster within pathways fundamental to cortical development, especially neuronal projection morphogenesis and synaptic organization. These specific enriched processes prove that CRAK-Velo reliably identifies genes whose regulatory profiles are tightly coupled to differentiation. Thus, CRAK-Velo can capture important genes that are relevant to the developmental trajectories captured by the velocity field.

The regulatory dynamics of the Jag2 locus in Fig. 2h deepen this validation. Jag2, an important ligand in Notch-mediated neuronal specification, exhibits a tightly orchestrated coordination between chromatin accessibility and unspliced RNA accumulation. As pseudotime progresses, distinct regulatory regions modulate their contributions, with proximal elements dominating precisely when Jag2 expression rises. This structured sequence of region activity resembles what was previously observed for KLF1 in hematopoiesis: thus, the method detects differential regional engagement that not only accompanies transcriptional change but anticipates and shapes it.

Conclusion

Taken together, these analyses demonstrate that CRAK-Velo consistently uncovers a coherent regulatory architecture across diverse tissues, species, and developmental processes. By tightly integrating chromatin accessibility with RNA kinetic information, the method reliably recovers known biology, highlights lineage-specific regulatory programs, and resolves the temporal structure of gene activation with fine granularity—all while maintaining strong performance at the systems level. Across benchmark datasets, CRAK-Velo accurately reconstructs complex dynamic flows and outperforms existing approaches in distinguishing cell types, underscoring the practical utility of its unified representation. In particular, compared to the recent method MultiVelo, CRAK-Velo offers both a simpler (and faster, see Supplementary Table 1) approach and a clear interpretation of how individual chromatin regions shape transcriptional trajectories, providing mechanistic insights that can be translated into testable hypotheses. As such, CRAK-Velo offers an interpretable framework for extracting dynamic regulatory information from high-throughput single-cell multi-omic data.

Methods

Data preprocessing

For the HSPC dataset, the same cells and their corresponding annotations in MultiVelo are used. For the embryonic E18 mouse brain we followed the cell annotations of MultiVelo. All cells that belong to the cell types list chosen by MultiVelo (‘RG, Astro, OPC’, ‘IPC’, ‘V-SVZ’, ‘Upper Layer’, ‘Deeper Layer’, ‘Ependymal cells’, ‘Subplate’) are kept for downstream analysis.

All the scRNA-seq part of the multi-omics datasets are preprocessed using the same approach as UniTVelo: The highly variable genes (HVGs), which contribute to cell-cell variation, were selected (default 2,000). Further selected informative genes with similar settings in scVelo.

For the scATAC-seq data part we chose to keep regions that are captured in more than 800 cells in the HSPC dataset and 400 cells for the embryonic E18 mouse brain dataset. Then, regions that are within 104 upstream and downstream from the gene starting site are kept.

The final HSPC dataset is composed of 11605 cells, 2000 genes, and 3939 regions. For the embryonic E18 mouse brain dataset, we ended up with 3365 cells, 2000 genes, and 4002 regions.

For the human cerebral cortex, we used the same scRNA-seq dataset obtained by the MUltiVelo preprocessing and kept the regions within a distance of 10kb from the genes. The final input data has the dimensions of 4693 cells, 954 genes, and 844 regions.

Low entropy genes

To identify genes with preferentially utilized chromatin regions, we quantified the entropy of accessibility weights across a window (104bp) for each gene. For every gene, the distribution of accessibility weights within its associated regions was compared against the entropy of a uniform distribution over the same number of regions. Because the number of accessible regions is gene-specific, the uniform entropy was computed separately for each gene as a baseline.

We then calculated the difference between the observed entropy and the corresponding uniform entropy. Genes for which this difference ranked in the top 20% were classified as low-entropy genes. A low entropy score indicates that accessibility is not evenly distributed across a gene’s regions but is instead concentrated in a subset of preferred binding sites. This property highlights regulatory specificity: while some genes display broadly distributed accessibility across many potential sites, low-entropy genes exhibit stronger localization of chromatin opening, which may correspond to key regulatory elements driving transcriptional dynamics.

Region kinetics

We monitored the accessibility kinetics of regions around a chosen gene by dividing the timeline (between 0 and 1) into intervals of width 0.05 and computing the average accessibility crg per interval. We showed in parallel the change in the unspliced reads with cell pseudotime by also averaging the normalised unspliced reads over an interval of 0.05.
