Surgical Options for Retained Gallstones After Cholecystectomy
Authors: Ali Mohtashami, William A Ziaziaris, Chris SH Lim, Nazim Bhimani, Steven Leibman, Thomas J Hugh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Retained gallstones are the most common cause of postcholecystectomy pain. They may be in a long remnant cystic duct (CD), the common bile duct (CBD), or within a remnant gallbladder (GB) post subtotal cholecystectomy. Although endoscopic management is often attempted, occasionally surgical clearance is required. Rates of preoperative surgery to remove stones in a remnant GB are rising due to the increased practice of subtotal cholecystectomy during a problematic laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This study aims to review the surgical management of symptomatic retained stones at a tertiary hepatobiliary referral center in Sydney, Australia.Patients and Methods:This retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data was performed on patients who underwent an operation for retained stones after a previous cholecystectomy over 18 years (2004-2022). All of the patients with residual CBD stones had failed endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatogram (ERCP) attempts or could not have an ERCP because of prior foregut surgery. All patients underwent a systematic preoperative workup confirming the biliary anatomy and pathology.Results:Fourteen patients had surgical intervention for retained stones. All cases were attempted laparoscopically and were successful in 11/14 patients (78%). Retained stones were found within a remnant GB (29%, n=4), a remnant CD (36%, n=5), or the CBD (36%, n=5). Conversion to an open procedure in 3 patients was for dense fibrosis associated with a long low-insertion of the CD, necessitating a hepatico-jejunostomy, failure to delineate the biliary anatomy, and inability to clear CBD stones, respectively. One patient developed a minor postoperative complication (superficial wound infection), and all patients were free of symptoms at a median follow-up of 33.5 months.Conclusion:This study demonstrates favorable outcomes in patients undergoing laparoscopic intervention for retained gallstones. A systematic approach to the workup and surgical management of patients with retained stones is essential.Key Words: gallbladder, retained stones, cholecystectomy, pain, postoperative, remnant gallbladder

Retained gallstones are the most common cause of postcholecystectomy pain in the early and late postoperative setting.1 Asymptomatic choledocholithiasis at the time of cholecystectomy ranges from 5% to 20%,2 and the incidence of retained gallstones postoperatively is ∼0.2% to 2.4%.3 Late symptoms may develop because of missed stones in the cystic duct (CD) or the common bile duct (CBD). Endoscopic management is possible in many patients. However, this may not be straightforward or feasible for multiple large stones or if there are anatomic access issues related to a long remnant CD or altered anatomy from prior foregut surgery. Retained stones may also be present in a remnant gallbladder (GB) after a previous partial or subtotal cholecystectomy, and these can cause recurrent biliary symptoms.4,5

Subtotal cholecystectomy is promoted as an operative strategy to protect critical structures during a problematic dissection of the Calot triangle.6 The most common indication for doing this is severe acute or chronic long-standing cholecystitis.4,7–9 Incomplete resection of the GB may be intentional due to inadequate visualization as a cause of adhesions, inflammation, excessive bleeding, and confounding anatomic or pathologic morphology.7,10,11 Although most surgeons accept that subtotal cholecystectomy may be helpful in certain circumstances, this operative strategy may also result in morbidity.12 This includes the short-term risk of bile leak from the remnant GB and the long-term risk of retained or recurrent stones that cause further biliary events requiring endoscopic, percutaneous, or operative intervention.

Pain following cholecystectomy is often attributed to the postcholecystectomy syndrome. However, with improved imaging, it is clear that a proportion of patients have symptomatic retained calculi either in the extra-hepatic biliary tree or within a remnant GB.1,13 In our study, all the patients with residual CBD stones had failed endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatogram (ERCP) attempts or could not have an ERCP because of prior foregut surgery. Despite adhesion formation in the subhepatic space, a laparoscopic approach is often feasible and is considered the gold standard. Results from multi-institution and single-institution studies show that this is associated with low morbidity and avoids the potential complications of open or complicated endoscopic or percutaneous approaches.14–18

This study aims to review the surgical management and outcomes of patients with symptomatic retained gallstones after a previous cholecystectomy who received surgical treatment in a single center in Sydney, Australia, over 18 years.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

A retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data was performed on patients who underwent surgical intervention for retained stones after a previous cholecystectomy between 2004 and 2022. This study was considered low-risk, and ethics approval was obtained by the Northern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (2021_ETH12453).

All patients underwent a systematic preoperative assessment and workup to confirm the biliary anatomy and pathology. All the patients with retained stones had failed ERCP attempts or could not have an ERCP because of prior foregut surgery. Clinicopathologic assessment and treatment details included the presenting problem (biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, or cholangitis) and the results of sequential imaging, including ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT) cholangiography, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), and ERCP when used. Information regarding the operative approach for the initial cholecystectomy (open, laparoscopic, subtotal, total) and the symptoms prompting re-referral was investigated. The operative details (technique, gross operative findings, and cholangiography findings) and the postoperative outcomes were recorded and analyzed for the surgical clearance of stones.

A laparoscopic approach was attempted in all patients, and adhesions were divided using the hook diathermy on a low-power setting combined with the Ligasure (Minneapolis, USA). Fixed anatomic landmarks (eg, Rouviere’s sulcus and segment 4b) and surgical landmarks (eg, previous metal clips or suture material) were used to guide safe dissection around the CBD. Needle aspiration of the biliary tree and operative cholangiography were used liberally to confirm the correct anatomy, and choledochoscopy was done depending on the preoperative and operative findings. Early in the series, T-tubes were placed routinely after choledochotomy and CBD exploration. This practice was discontinued in the mid-2000s because of studies citing high complications, frequent dislodgement, increased costs, and prolonged hospital length of stay.18,19

Laparoscopic suture closure of the CD stump was preferred rather than using clips or end loops, and most patients had abdominal drains in the subhepatic space postoperatively. Follow-up was done through physical examination as part of a routine postoperative review and then review in a clinic in 3 to 72 months. Dichotomous variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as a median and range. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Version 28 (SPSS Statistics for Windows, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, 2017).

RESULTS

Fourteen patients underwent surgical intervention for retained stones during the study period. The demographic data, preoperative workup, and surgical outcomes are summarized in Table 1. The most common symptom prompting presentation to the emergency department or elective surgical referral was abdominal pain. The median age of the cohort was 63.5 years, and 64% of patients were female. The initial cholecystectomy was done laparoscopically in 9 patients (64%) and converted to open in 5 patients (36%). Before surgical intervention for retained stones, 9 patients (64%) had an unsuccessful ERCP where it was not possible to retrieve either CD stones (n=3) or CBD stones (n=6).

The median time between the first and definitive operation to remove the retained stones was 15 months (1 to 360 mo). A laparoscopic approach was successful in 11/14 patients (78%). Reasons for conversion to an open procedure in 3 patients included dense fibrosis associated with a long low-insertion of the CD necessitating a hepatico-jejunostomy (n=1), failure to delineate the biliary anatomy (n=1), and inability to laparoscopically clear CBD stones (n=1). Four patients had stones within a remnant GB (29%), 5 patients had stones within a remnant CD (36%), and 6 patients had retained CBD stones (36%). One patient had a concomitant cholecysto-duodenal fistula, which was successfully repaired laparoscopically. Eight patients underwent an intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC), and 9 patients underwent choledochoscopic exploration of the CBD (with a clearance rate of 89%). In 1 patient with a remnant GB and a CBD stone, the choledochoscope could not be advanced into the distal CBD, and a postoperative ERCP was required to remove the stone. Before 2006, a T-tube was used in 4 patients after choledochotomy and CBD exploration, and there were no complications associated with this management.

All patients in this series had an uneventful postoperative course except for 1 patient (7%) who developed a superficial wound infection that resolved with oral antibiotics (Clavien-Dindo grade 2). The median follow-up time was 33.5 months; all patients reported resolved preoperative symptoms. Two patients required further biliary intervention postoperatively. In the immediate postoperative period, 1 patient required percutaneous lithotripsy and extraction through a radiologically placed biliary stent for a distal CBD stone that ERCP could not retrieve could not retrieve. Another patient on long-term imatinib presented with a new CBD stone that ERCP successfully removed.

DISCUSSION

The short-term and long-term outcomes of patients who underwent surgical intervention for retained stones after a previous cholecystectomy are as follows: 9 patients (64%) had stones in either a remnant GB or long cystic duct, and the remainder had residual CBD stones that could not be removed endoscopically. The median time between the initial cholecystectomy and the reoperation was 15 months. This period is the same interval reported by Cawich et al19 but shorter than the interval reported by Gadiyaram et al20 and Kumar et al.21 A third of patients had an initial open cholecystectomy, which is consistent with the study period dating back to 2004. Patients were investigated using multiple imaging modalities, including MRCP, to confirm the biliary anatomy.

Multiple single-center studies and one multicentre study describe outcomes after surgical management of retained stones. Most describe the management of either GB remnant stones14,15,17,19,20,22–26 or CD stones,13,21,27 although some studies include the management of both GB remnant and CD stones13,16,18,28,29 as was the case in the present series. All studies describe relatively small patient numbers (<25) except for 2 studies from India [Parmar et al14 (n=40) and Singh et al15 (n=93)].

A previous open cholecystectomy should not rule out the possibility of a minimally invasive approach for retained stones. A laparoscopic approach was successful in 78% of patients in the current study. The laparoscopic approach has advantages over an open approach, including lower morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and earlier return to normal activities. The likelihood of success of a laparoscopic approach depends on the extent of subhepatic adhesions, the presence of residual chronic inflammatory change around the CBD, and the technical expertise and experience of the operating surgeon. As a result of adhesion formation, there is a risk of vascular or biliary injury during these operations. These risks can be minimized preoperatively by clarifying the exact remnant biliary anatomy in imaging studies and intraoperatively by using anatomic and previous surgical material landmarks to guide the dissection. Careful dissection is essential to avoid a vasculo-biliary injury.

To date, there are no consensus protocols for managing retained gallstones postcholecystectomy. In our experience, obtaining the original cholecystectomy’s operative record helped determine whether a cholangiogram was done and whether there were any specific anatomic or pathologic variants. In the present series, the critical view of safety was only possible in 50% of patients, but a successful and safe laparoscopic approach was achieved in most patients. Conversion to an open procedure was necessary for 2 patients to remove remnant stones in a long CD with a low insertion point into the CBD. This anatomic variation may require dissection into the head of the pancreas, which can be technically challenging when removing retained stones. Intraoperative ultrasound was not used in the current series, and, in retrospect, this may have been helpful in these cases.

Rates of successful laparoscopic removal of retained stones vary widely in reported series (0% to 100%).13–21,23–27,29–31 This is likely due to differences in case selection and presenting pathologies, as well as differences in technical expertise and experience. When excising a remnant GB, Parmar et al14 stress the importance of blunt instrument dissection and minimal use of electrosurgery in the subhepatic space to avoid injury to critical structures. A combination of blunt and sharp dissection using the diathermy on a low setting was used in the present series, and there were no vascular or biliary injuries in any patient. Minor bile leaks and even a significant bile duct injury have indeed been reported following surgical intervention for retained stones.14,16–19,24

Long-term follow-up in these patients is essential because of the potential for recurrent stone formation if there has been chronic damage to the biliary tree. In the current series, all patients were free of symptoms and had no further biliary-related treatments or hospital admissions at long-term follow-up.

Limitations

While the present study provides some insights, the limitations should be acknowledged, including the study’s retrospective nature and the relatively small sample size. In addition, the study was conducted at a single center, which may not represent a broader patient population or surgical practice. This series did not use robotic surgical or combined radiologic and endoscopic approaches. The participation of experienced invasive radiologists can be extremely helpful, and further work is needed to determine any advantages of these treatment options. Future studies with larger sample sizes, prospective designs, and multicenter collaborations are necessary to address these limitations and provide more robust evidence on managing retained stones after cholecystectomy.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this retrospective analysis highlights favorable outcomes in patients undergoing surgical intervention for retained stones after a previous cholecystectomy. A systematic approach to the workup and surgical management of patients with retained stones can result in symptom resolution and complete stone clearance. Laparoscopic removal of retained stones is a feasible approach for most patients, with a success rate of 78% in this study. Ultimately, individualized treatment decisions should consider patient characteristics, surgical expertise, and anatomic factors to optimize outcomes in this patient population.
