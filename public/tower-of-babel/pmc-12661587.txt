Modified electroconvulsive therapy for severe malnutrition secondary to obsessive–compulsive disorder: A case report
Authors: Masaya Miyazaki, Junko Yamamoto, Maiko Tsutsui, Emu Obara, Yuji Takamaru, Yoshihisa Sasagawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic psychiatric condition marked by intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviors. In rare cases, severe contamination or defecation fears lead to extreme dietary restriction and life‐threatening malnutrition. Although a handful of cases have been reported, none describe the use of modified electroconvulsive therapy (mECT) in this setting. To avoid perceived fecal contamination, she progressively restricted her diet, losing weight from 52 to 32.1 kg (body mass index [BMI] 13.1) and escalating to a Yale‐Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale (Y‐BOCS) score of 39/40. BACKGROUND

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) affects approximately 1%–3% of the population and is marked by persistent intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors (compulsions) performed to relieve anxiety. 1 Standard treatments include pharmacotherapy with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and exposure‐and‐response‐prevention (ERP)‐based cognitive‐behavioral therapy (CBT), leading to remission in many patients; however, up to 40% of cases remain refractory and require alternative interventions. 2

In rare instances, contamination or defecation fears drive extreme dietary restriction. 3 Patients may restrict caloric intake to avoid the perceived need to defecate, leading to severe malnutrition with body mass index (BMI) values below 15. Such cases pose significant challenges: pharmacotherapy may be poorly tolerated, and CBT cannot proceed effectively without adequate physical reserves. A few reports have described life‐threatening weight loss secondary to OCD. These reports concern adult cases in which obsessions and compulsions, such as scrupulosity, contamination fears, including refusal of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) feeds, nonstructural dysphagia, or avoidance related to fear of constipation, led to severe undernutrition. Management relied on SSRIs, sometimes with antipsychotic augmentation, as well as CBT/ERP and nutritional support. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) was not used, and the outcomes were variable. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

ECT is an established treatment for refractory mood disorders and has shown benefit in select cases of treatment‐resistant OCD, 10 yet major guidelines do not recommend it as routine therapy for core OCD. The American Psychiatric Association (APA) guideline notes that the available evidence is limited and does not support ECT for treatment‐resistant OCD in the absence of comorbidity; the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline concludes that there is insufficient evidence on which to base a recommendation for the use of ECT in OCD; and the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) guideline notes one randomized controlled trial (RCT) and suggests that modified ECT (mECT) may be tried in carefully selected refractory cases before more invasive options such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) or neurosurgery. 11 , 12 , 13 ECT has been demonstrated to alleviate obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviors, reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms, and support structural and functional brain changes that improve quality of life. 10 Modified ECT, which employs brief‐pulse stimuli under anesthesia and muscle relaxation, offers a rapid intervention that may bypass barriers posed by malnutrition and treatment refusal. To date, mECT has not been reported in the context of critical malnutrition secondary to OCD. We present the first such case, highlighting clinical features, treatment protocol, and outcomes.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 54‐year‐old Japanese woman was referred to our department for evaluation of excessive weight loss. She was diagnosed with OCD at age 33, presenting with contamination obsessions and associated compulsive rituals, including frequent hand‐washing and avoidance behaviors. Over two decades, she received intermittent treatment with duloxetine (40 mg/day) and escitalopram (10 mg/day), as well as ERP‐based CBT, achieving only transient improvement.

Approximately 12 months before referral, her symptoms acutely worsened following the death of her primary psychiatrist. Her compulsive behaviors centered increasingly on defecation, which she perceived as contaminated. To avoid this perceived fecal contamination, she severely restricted her diet. During this period, her weight fell from 52 to 32.1 kg (BMI 13.1), and her Yale‐Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale (Y‐BOCS) score increased to 39/40, indicating severe symptomatology. Outpatient SSRI/serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) adjustments and nutritional supplementation were poorly tolerated, and ERP‐based CBT could not be effectively implemented due to physical fragility and treatment refusal.

The patient was admitted under medical protection for both psychiatric and medical management. Final diagnosis at admission was DSM‐5 OCD with poor insight (Y‐BOCS insight item = 3), characterized by a firm conviction that defecation would cause contamination and an inability to question this belief despite preserved reality testing. There was no comorbid major depressive disorder (MDD). Baseline depressive severity on the 17‐item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM‐D‐17) was 4, consistent with no depression. On initial assessment, she exhibited cachexia, hypotension, muscle wasting, and minimal subcutaneous fat. Laboratory tests revealed albumin 4.1 g/dL, cholinesterase 227 U/L, and an absolute lymphocyte count of 990/µL, with mild hypokalemia (K 3.4 mmol/L). The 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed left anterior fascicular block with a normal QT interval (QTc), and echocardiography revealed no structural abnormalities. Nutrition was provided exclusively by mouth; no nasogastric or other enteral tube feeding was used. Intake was consistently very low, and on most days it did not exceed 800 kcal/day, with several days of near‐complete refusal. Electrolytes were monitored and repleted as needed, and no clinical or laboratory evidence of refeeding syndrome was observed. Despite supportive nutritional care and close monitoring, her weight declined further by Day 7, reaching 30.4 kg.

Given the failure of nutritional management and the impracticality of SSRI titration or ERP in the acute setting, the indication for mECT was reviewed by several psychiatrists within psychiatry, with input from nursing, pharmacy, and clinical nutrition, and consultations with cardiology and anesthesiology. A cautious approach was advised in view of severe underweight and an abnormal ECG. After these consultations and adoption of a risk‐mitigation plan focused on refeeding precautions, mECT was recommended, with close inpatient observation and frequent monitoring of vital signs and electrolytes. Although admission was initially involuntary, decision‐making capacity for mECT was reassessed immediately prior to treatment using a focused clinical interview addressing understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and expression of a choice; capacity was judged intact. After a structured discussion covering the indication, procedure, expected benefits, and risks, including cognitive and anesthetic risks, reasonable alternatives, and the urgent medical context, the patient provided written informed consent, which was witnessed by a nurse, and her family was informed and agreed to proceed. After obtaining informed consent, mECT was initiated on hospital Day 14. Modified ECT was administered as bilateral brief‐pulse treatment using a Thymatron System IV (Somatics LLC, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) in the LOW 0.5 program. All antidepressant medications were discontinued. Bitemporal electrodes were applied, and ictal activity was monitored with a two‐lead electroencephalogram (EEG) using the frontomastoid montage. The skin was prepared per routine to optimize contact. Stimulation parameters were fixed at a pulse width of 0.5 ms and a current of 0.9 A. In this program, the percent energy setting scales pulse frequency and train duration according to the device algorithm, thereby determining total charge, resulting in delivered charges ranging from 125 mC at 25% to 455 mC at 90%. Treatments were scheduled twice per week at approximately 72‐h intervals. The initial stimulus dose was set at 25% using the half‐age method, and when ictal quality was suboptimal, the charge was increased by approximately 1.5 times the previous session in accordance with standard principles for bilateral ECT. Each session was performed under general anesthesia with propofol 50–70 mg and succinylcholine 20 mg, using mask ventilation with 100% oxygen, a soft bite block, and continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring; anesthetic practice followed standard protocols. A total of 19 treatments were delivered; adequate generalized seizures were confirmed in 14 sessions with stable hemodynamics. Decision‐making capacity for mECT was reconfirmed before each session. By the 13th session, the patient had demonstrated marked symptom improvement, with her Y‑BOCS score falling to 18/40 and her oral intake returning to consistent, adequate levels. mECT concluded after 19 sessions, after which paroxetine was titrated to 40 mg daily and ERP‐based CBT sessions were reinstated. The patient was transitioned to voluntary admission for rehabilitation and discharged with a weight of 50.3 kg and Y‐BOCS 19/40. At the 10‐month follow‐up, she maintained stable weight and psychiatric control (Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

This case, to our knowledge, is the first to describe mECT in a patient whose life‐threatening malnutrition was primarily driven by OCD. Contemporary models implicate dysregulation of cortico‐striatothalamo‐cortical (CSTC) loops, notably characterized by orbitofrontal‐striatal hyperactivity, impaired thalamic gating, and habit circuitry that sustains intrusive thoughts and ritualized behaviors, together with alterations in serotonergic, glutamatergic, and dopaminergic signaling and a substantial genetic contribution. 14 , 15 Clinically, a meaningful subset of patients remain refractory despite guideline‐concordant first‐line treatments such as CBT with ERP and SSRIs, which highlights the need for approaches that can directly modulate pathologic network activity. 16 , 17 Although major guidelines do not recommend ECT for core OCD symptoms, converging neuromodulation evidence, such as circuit‐targeted DBS and clinical series, suggests that direct network modulation can benefit extreme, treatment‐resistant presentations. 11 , 15 , 18 Within this framework, mECT offers theoretically grounded multi‐scale mechanisms. Acutely, the induced generalized seizure can desynchronize pathological oscillations and restore inhibitory–excitatory balance across fronto‐striatal networks, attenuating the affective arousal and urge amplification that drive compulsions. Over weeks, ECT has been associated with neuroplastic adaptations at multiple levels, including increases in neurotrophin signaling, modulation of glutamatergic transmission, and reorganization of large‐scale network connectivity, as well as broader neurochemical effects involving monoaminergic and GABAergic systems, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and inflammatory cytokine pathways. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 In medically fragile contexts where oral agents cannot be reliably tolerated or absorbed and rapid stabilization is required to enable refeeding and re‐engagement with behavioral therapy, this mechanistic profile provides a clinically coherent rationale for considering mECT. 11 , 16 , 17

In this patient, the indication for mECT was OCD‐driven malnutrition with imminent medical risk, not depression. At baseline, the HAM‐D‐17 score was 4, and the clinical interview did not support a diagnosis of MDD. The rationale for mECT was the need to achieve rapid stabilization in the setting of profound undernutrition and failure of standard care. With a BMI near 13 and persistent meal refusal driven by a fixed fear of defecation, low‐dose SSRI trials were not tolerated, and exposure‐based CBT was ineffective while starvation impaired cognition and stamina. After supervised nutritional rehabilitation failed to reverse weight loss, a multidisciplinary discussion incorporating perspectives from psychiatry, anesthesiology, and cardiology reviewed the options. While initial concerns were raised regarding the patient′s profound undernutrition and cardiometabolic risk, these were addressed through pre‐ECT optimization, including electrolyte correction, cardiac evaluation, and anesthetic perioperative risk assessment, together with careful cardiopulmonary monitoring, and the team ultimately reached consensus to proceed with mECT to provide an opportunity for refeeding and behavioral work.

To substantiate this decision, risk assessment focused on cardiac and metabolic safety. Hyponatremia was absent, and mild hypokalemia (K 3.4 mmol/L) was corrected before mECT with ongoing monitoring of magnesium and phosphate given refeeding risk. The 12‐lead ECG showed left anterior fascicular block, QTc was within the reference range, echocardiography was reassuring, and cardiology judged no contraindication to proceed. Under these safeguards, mECT was delivered with standard cardiopulmonary monitoring, and no adverse events occurred, which in turn allowed oral intake to increase steadily, leading to nutritional stabilization and facilitating transition to maintenance pharmacotherapy alongside renewed behavioral interventions.

Even in medically fragile patients, mECT can be delivered safely when implemented with stringent periprocedural management. In the literature on patients with low body weight, including those with anorexia nervosa, ECT is generally well tolerated under appropriate monitoring, has been delivered without immediate complications, and low body weight alone is not a contraindication. 23 , 24 , 25 In our patient, despite severe underweight (BMI approximately 13 kg/m²) and an abnormal ECG (left anterior fascicular block with normal QTc), mECT was performed in a controlled inpatient setting after cardiology and anesthesiology evaluated the patient and found no contraindication to proceed. The treatment was well tolerated under standard anesthetic monitoring, with no cardiopulmonary or cognitive adverse events. By hospital Day 14, we began twice‐weekly mECT, and her Y‐BOCS score fell dramatically from 39/40 to 18/40 by the 13th session, allowing her to maintain reliable oral intake. After 19 sessions, she was eating normally and had regained weight, allowing us to transition her to maintenance SSRI therapy and behavioral intervention.

In the only six adult cases of OCD‐induced malnutrition reported to date, treatment depended on SSRIs, antipsychotics, psychotherapy, and tube feeding, yet outcomes were inconsistent (Table 1). 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In our patient, mECT broke the cycle of malnutrition driven by OCD, produced rapid clinical improvement, and made safe nutritional rehabilitation possible. This case demonstrates that mECT can serve as a practical and effective alternative when medical instability rules out standard OCD therapies.

In summary, this case highlights mECT's life‑saving role in medically unstable, treatment‑refractory OCD. By swiftly modulating dysfunctional CSTC circuits and augmenting neurotrophic support, mECT achieved rapid symptom relief, enabling safe nutritional rehabilitation alongside the resumption of standard therapies. Despite profound malnutrition, mECT was administered without incident under close monitoring. In situations where pharmacotherapy may be too slow to address urgent medical needs, targeted neuromodulation, such as mECT and, when appropriate, complementary techniques like transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) or DBS, should be considered on an individualized basis. Future case series and multicenter registries will be essential to delineate mECT's safety profile, refine treatment protocols, and assess long‐term outcomes in this vulnerable population, further establishing its role in bridging psychiatric and medical stabilization.

CONCLUSION

Modified ECT represents a viable and rapid option for treatment‐refractory OCD with critical malnutrition. It can be administered safely in severely malnourished patients, delivering swift symptom relief while simultaneously enabling nutritional recovery.
