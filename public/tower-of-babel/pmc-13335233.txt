Strategies to support Internationally Educated Nurses from non-English speaking backgrounds transition into the Western healthcare workforce: a systematic review
Authors: Cath Connolly, Jacalyn Mcdonald, Lou Unger, Damon Williams, Grace Cains, Kate Laver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe globalisation of the healthcare workforce has led to a growing proportion of Internationally Educated Nurses within health care organisations. While their contributions are invaluable, many face challenges in adapting to new professional environments, highlighting the urgent need for organisations to implement evidence-based strategies to support their successful integration.ObjectiveThe aim of this review was to identify and describe workplace support strategies shown to be helpful in supporting Internationally Educated Nurses from non-English-speaking backgrounds transitioning into the Western healthcare workforce.Study design and methodsA systematic review was conducted including primary research studies reporting qualitative and/or quantitative data. The population of interest was Internationally Educated Nurses from non-English speaking backgrounds who were employed in Western health care settings including Australia, New Zealand, Canada, United States of America and the United Kingdom. The intervention of interest was strategies that could be put in place by the workplace to improve outcomes for the nurse or the workplace. Three databases (Medline, PsycInfo, CINAHL) and grey literature were searched in November 2024. Risk of bias was assessed using corresponding tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute. Results were tabulated and presented in a narrative synthesis.ResultsA total of 20 studies were included with most (n = 13) being qualitative. Studies were conducted in a range of countries with nurses from a range of different cultural backgrounds. Nine key approaches were identified to support successful integration, spanning organisational strategies (e.g., anti-racism policies and equitable career pathways), educational programs on cultural competence for all nurses, and structural supports such as tailored orientation and mentoring.ConclusionsWe were unable to identify any experimental studies which provided quantitative data on the effects of strategies to support Internationally Educated Nurses. The included studies provided information about a range of different ways to enhance transition.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04654-4.

Background

The world population is rapidly ageing and by 2030 it is estimated that one in six people will be aged 60 years or over [1]. The growing proportion of older people, many of whom live with multiple chronic conditions, is driving an increased demand for a larger workforce. Globally, nurses make up the most substantial proportion of healthcare professionals and numbers continue to rise. For example, the number of nurses employed in Australia has increased from 295,000 in 2013 to 372,000 in 2022, representing an increase of 26% over 10 years [2]. In the United Kingdom, the number of nurses registered has increased 18% over the last five years [3]. Yet, despite increased numbers of nurses employed, workforce shortages in many countries persist [4, 5].

Many Western countries rely heavily on the international recruitment of nurses who obtained their training and qualifications abroad to address nursing shortages. The literature adopts a range of terms to describe this workforce, including ‘overseas qualified nurses’, ‘foreign nurses’, and ‘internationally trained nurses’. For consistency, this paper will use the term ‘Internationally Educated Nurses’ (IENs) to refer to nurses who completed their initial nursing in a country other than the one in which they currently practice or are seeking licensure.

In 2022, 20% of the nursing workforce in Australia completed their qualification in a different country and this is expected to rise to 28% by 2035 [6]. This is not unique to Australia with other countries also dependent on IENs to meet health workforce requirement [7, 8].

Internationally Educated Nurses in Australia have reported several challenges working within the Australian healthcare system. These include language barriers, cultural differences, limited access to support and limited recognition of the person’s existing professional skills [9]. Nursing administrators have also reported challenges including clarification of the person’s career path, language proficiency and additional time require to provide support [10]. An integrative review by Chun Tie et al. (2017) categorised challenges into three main themes [11]. The first theme was the process of transition, whereby nurses were required to complete complex processes including orientation to a new workplace. The second theme was adjusting to local context where nurses often had to adjust their usual scope of practice for their new work environment and modify communication styles to adjust to English language and Australia’s model of person-centered practice. The third theme related to experiences of prejudice where nurses reported experiencing nurse-to-nurse racism and patient-to-nurse racism [11].

Countries and workplaces across the world have implemented a range of strategies to help support the successful integration of IENs. Overall, research suggests that a multifaceted approach is required for successful integration [9, 12]. These strategies should be intra-organisational (e.g. policies, employee conditions and environment), sociocultural (e.g. cultural training, learning, support, personal development) and related to professional development (e.g. skill development, workplace support) [13]. Several recent systematic reviews have provided important information about this topic. Correa-Betancour et al. reported that discrimination and racism continue to be major barriers to integration for IENs and that better incident reporting and training programs in intra-professional cultural competence are required [14]. They reported that a more culturally competent workforce is required to enable more inclusive and supportive environments for all nurses, regardless of their cultural background. Thomas et al. examined transitions of nurses in the United States and found that the quality of orientation and organisational support were positively associated with job satisfaction and turnover [8]. Primeau et al. reported that the personal characteristics of the IENs played a role in outcomes with older and more experienced nurses and women more likely to be satisfied with their careers compared to younger or less experienced or male nurses. Furthermore, those who worked full time had significantly higher satisfaction levels than those who worked part time [15].

Existing reviews either focus on specific countries [8, 14, 16] or are limited to exploring individual perspectives of nurses [11] or barriers to integration [10]. We were interested in the range of strategies that could be applied by healthcare organisations within Western healthcare services to support IENs and their effectiveness.

The aim of this systematic review was to identify strategies that can be implemented by health services within Western Countries to support successful transition resulting in improved outcomes for the IENs and/or the workplace and to determine their effectiveness.

Methods

Design

A protocol for this systematic review was developed a priori and registered with Open Science Framework [17]. The review is reported according to the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines [18].

Eligibility criteria

Types of studies

Research studies of any design were eligible if they were peer-reviewed and presented primary data. This included qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods studies. Reviews of published literature were not included. We excluded studies published in languages other than English and restricted our search to studies published in the last ten years (≥ 2014) to ensure we captured contemporary literature.

Population

We included studies involving IENs from predominantly non-English speaking backgrounds with no restrictions on age or gender. We included health settings in English speaking countries comprising Australia, New Zealand, United Kingdom, Canada or the United States. We excluded studies where the population of interest was nursing students and non-healthcare settings (e.g. aged care homes).

Intervention

We included studies which described the effect of workplace support strategies to support IENs. These strategies needed to be implementable by the workplace and included approaches like education, peer support, mentorship and cultural sensitivity training. We excluded studies which described recruitment strategies or programs that were not implemented by the employing health service (e.g. third party provided bridging programs).

Outcomes of interest

We were interested in outcomes for the nurse (e.g. work satisfaction, quality of life) and the organisation (e.g. patient care, quality of care).

Data sources and search strategy

We searched MEDLINE, CINAHL and PsycInfo on the 20th of November 2024 using a combination of MESH headings and key word terms. The MEDLINE search strategy, which was adapted for CINAHL and PsycInfo, is provided in Supplementary file 1. To identify additional relevant studies, we searched Google and Google Scholar for papers meeting our inclusion criteria that may not have been captured in our database searches. We also conducted citation chasing of included studies to identify further relevant literature. All search results were exported into Covidence for screening.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were reviewed in Covidence by two members of the research team independently with a third researcher helping to make decisions where there was disagreement. Full text papers were also reviewed by two researchers independently and a third person making the final decision around eligibility when required.

Quality appraisal

Study quality was assessed by two people using the applicable quality appraisal tool from the Joanna Briggs Institute. The Checklist for Qualitative Research was used to appraise qualitative studies [19], and the Checklist for Analytical Cross Sectional Studies for quantitative studies [20]. If mixed methods studies included both quantitative and qualitative results relevant to the research objective, authors completed quality appraisal using both tools. Mixed methods studies with only quantitative results or qualitative results relevant to the research objective were only appraised using the relevant tool (Checklist for Analytical Cross Sectional Studies and Checklist for Qualitative Research, respectively).

Data extraction

Two tables were developed for data extraction. The first table captured study characteristics for each included study, organised by study type (qualitative, mixed methods, quantitative). Details extracted included: author, year, country, setting, study aim, methods, sample size and type of program implemented. Where authors used alternative terms (such as Overseas Qualified Nurses or Culturally and Linguistically Diverse Nurses) we used the authors term in the data extraction phase but treated these terms as equivalent to Internationally Educated Nurses which is the term used consistently throughout this review.

The second table summarised relevant study results and the implications of findings, as reported by study authors. Specifically, data were extracted on workforce support strategies and their impact on outcomes of interest, including effects on nurses (e.g. work satisfaction, workforce integration, quality of life) and on the organisation (e.g. patient care, quality of care). Results were presented both narratively and in tabular form, highlighting key themes arising from qualitative or quantitative data. Implications of findings were synthesised narratively, drawing on content from discussion, conclusion or specific subheadings (e.g. ‘recommendations’ or ‘implications for practice’). Data extraction was performed by one reviewer and independently checked for accuracy and completeness by a second reviewer.

Data synthesis

The extracted data results and implications were used to identify workplace support strategies for IENs (e.g. tailored orientation, peer support). These most common strategies were presented in a tabular format, linking specific strategies to relevant studies. Authors then further elaborated on strategies narratively. We had planned to undertake meta-analysis, however, we were unable to identify studies providing experimental data (e.g. randomised controlled trials).

Results

Study selection

Our search identified a total of 2843 citations identified in the search of the three database and an additional 34 citations from grey literature searching and reference lists. After removal of duplicate and screening, a total of 94 studies were reviewed in full text. Of these, a total of 20 studies were found to meet the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (Fig. 1). Characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1.

Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart

PRISMA flowchart

Most studies (n = 13) used qualitative methodology, conducting interviews and/or focus groups with IENs. Some of these qualitative studies also included the perspectives of peers and managers. Sample sizes of qualitative studies ranged from five to 171 participants. Five studies used mixed methods with focus groups and surveys providing both qualitative and quantitative data. The remaining two studies used surveys with large sample sizes (n = 2280; n = 360) to elicit quantitative data. Unfortunately, we were unable to find any experimental studies providing quantitative data related to the evaluation of workplace strategies to support nurses. Most studies involved participants identifying interventions or workplace characteristics that had enhanced their transitions rather than specific structured programs.

Most studies were conducted in Australia (n = 9), followed by the United Kingdom (n = 5), Canada (n = 4) and the United States of America (n = 2). The studies included IENs from a wide range of countries.

Quality appraisal

A total of 18 studies were appraised using the JBI checklist for Qualitative Research [19], comprising of five mixed methods, and 13 qualitative studies (Table 2). Two qualitative studies [21, 22] met al.l appraisal criteria. In contrast, one mixed methods study [23] met only a single criterion (question 9, relating to ethical conduct).

Questions six and seven, which assess researcher positioning and reflexivity, were consistently under addressed. Only 15% (n = 2) of qualitative studies met the criterion for question six (statement locating the researcher culturally or theoretically), and 31% (n = 4) addressed question seven (influence of the researcher on the research and vice versa). None of the mixed methods studies met either criterion.

Some concerns were noted regarding question one, which evaluates the congruency between philosophical perspective and the research methodology, with only 20% (n = 1) mixed method study sufficiently addressed. However, this question was more consistently addressed by qualitative studies, with 77% (n = 10) meeting the criterion.

Strategies to support the successful transition of IENs from non-English speaking backgrounds into the Western healthcare workforce

IENs: Internationally Educated Nurses

Support from peers at the ward level

Nine studies reported the importance of support provided at the ward-level [21–33]. A ward environment that was supportive where other staff demonstrated patience was important [21]. IENs also reported that it was helpful when other nurses on the ward understood their own intercultural communication needs [21]. Several studies identified the potential benefits of a more formal support structure on the ward through a buddy system or ward-based mentor who was directly accessible for support and advice on a day-to-day basis [28, 30, 31]. In addition, direct clinical supervisors who were understanding and supportive was considered important [28].

Characteristics of a positive workplace

Several studies highlighted organisations features that promoted successful integration [21, 22, 27, 28, 30–35]. An organisation with a lack of hierarchy was seen as a positive attribute of the workplace for IENs [21]. Nurses also valued workplaces that provided awards and recognition for staff [34]. In terms of the physical environment of the workplace, Xiao et al. highlighted the importance of an accessible and welcoming staff room as this was a key space for interaction between nurses [30].

Support from management and the organisation

Leaders and management within the organisations were able to significantly influence the success of integration [22–30, 35], 33– [25]. Leadership commitment to equity [34] and continuous advocacy and support was reported to be important [28]. It was also critical for organisations to have a well-considered plan with pre-determined supportive strategies in place to support IENs [29]. Joseph et al. reported that health service managers can play a pivotal role in addressing racism and discrimination, when the organisation is supported by targeted policies and actions [38]. One study suggested that a case management approach may be beneficial to provide individualised support to IENs [24]. Flexible work arrangements, including part-time options [35] enhanced inclusivity and demonstration of the value of IEN staff, through equitable treatment and recognition, including remuneration [27] was essential to retention.

Opportunities for career progression and professional development

Several studies [21, 24, 26, 31, 32, 34, 36–39] reported the importance of offering IENs the same career development opportunities that other non-IEN nurses received. This enabled them to equally build their skills, knowledge and progress their careers. Nurses valued being offered informal encouragement and support for career progression [21] as well as practical support (time, funding, opportunity) for learning and career development opportunities [31, 34].

Tailored orientation program specifically for IENs

Many studies spoke of the benefits of orientation programs specifically designed for IENs addressed to meeting their unique needs [21, 23, 25, 29–32, 37, 39]. For example, Smith described how IENs needed additional information and training about using local equipment and technologies (e.g. call bells, automated medication dispensing machines) as well as additional context-specific content on local practice and education models within the setting [29]. It was acknowledged that the role and scope of nurses differs globally and so orientation programs should provide information about what to expect in terms of local role and scope [32]. It was reported that Western countries tend to have a stronger emphasis on both evidence-based care and team-based care and so IENs required support to adjust to these expectations [35]. Salami and colleagues reported the benefits of supporting entry level skills first (to help gain skills in dealing with families, doctors and allied health) before taking on more complex skills and roles [37].

Support before and on arrival

IENs highly valued organisations and staff that provided additional support upon employment with the organisation [22–26, 37]. For example, the allocation of a specific support person on arrival to the organisation, a meet and greet session with other staff were suggested as beneficial [23]. Furthermore, IENs valued assistance which helped them to integrated within the wider community; this included provision of an arrival manual, information on local transport options, a guide to local community amenities (banks, shops, primary care practices) and advice on accommodation were thought to be helpful [23]. Pressley et al. reported that offering a tour of local amenities was beneficial in supporting community integration [26].

Building communication and language confidence and competence

Adjustment to a different language and communication style was identified as a major challenge and an area where more support was required [21, 28, 29, 32, 39, 40]. It was suggested that formal language development programs were beneficial with consideration of access to accent reduction programs which could improve articulation and build confidence in nurses [29]. Within the organisation it was suggested that IENs try to mirror the way that their local colleagues communicated [21] and to learn from other nurses about the mannerisms, communication styles and body language used within the health care setting [30]. Specifically it was important for IENs to build conversational competence with patients and other health professionals [32]. While working with other IENs provided a range of benefits, it was recommended that IENs communicate with each other in English to help with skill building and practicing the language in a safe space [29].

Cultural awareness, competency and respect

Studies reported how employing organisations could provide training and support for non-IEN staff which would help to improve workplace experiences for IENs [22, 28–30, 32, 34, 39, 40]. Most suggestions related to educating and informing staff about difference cultures. This included information on different ways of living, food and beliefs [29] and resources on cultural differences [30]. Studies highlighted the importance of building cultural humility and cultural tolerance in staff within the organisation [32], the need for education on anti-racism [28] and to view cultural diversity as an asset [39].

Community and cultural integration beyond the workplace

Several studies highlighted opportunities to support IENs outside of the workplace with the aim of improving social and community connections [22–24, 26, 29, 39]. Covell et al. suggested that organisations try and develop the IENs social capital with a range of different opportunities [24] and Dywili et al. recommended that workplaces arrange social functions to help bring employees together to form connections and get to know each other outside of the work environment [22].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to identify the type of strategies that could be implemented by the health care organisation to support IENs and to evaluate their effectiveness. Unfortunately, we were unable to identify any studies which provided objective evaluations of the benefit of different strategies. For example, none of the studies applied objective measures such as workplace satisfaction surveys to evaluate specific initiatives or explore retention data. None of the studies compared the benefits of different approaches (for example, comparison of a peer mentor system with tailored orientation programs). As such, we relied on data from qualitative studies or survey data which provided insight into the strategies which IENs and other stakeholders believed to be beneficial. This included a large range of approaches and strategies which can provide useful information for organisations either reviewing existing programs or considering introduction of new programs.

Our review showed that support for IENs stems from the top with responsibility on health service leaders to ensure that policies are in place to provide equitable access to career opportunities, flexible working arrangements (e.g. allowance for part time work) and prevent and address racism from both staff and patients. Interestingly, a mixed methods study conducted with hospital equity officers found that most respondents (84%) felt that the organisation’s Chief Executive Officer was supportive of advancing health equity goals whereas clinical leaders were less likely to be supportive [41]. The same study reported that greater diversity within hospital staff members was more likely to lead to better health equity for hospital patients.

Several studies highlighted the importance of overcoming language barriers. This related not just to the IEN’s competence in English but also acknowledging different styles of communication. For example, the Western style was considered ‘direct’ and addressing colleagues by their first names was not usual practice for some IENs. Registration requirements in most Western countries require English proficiency (as demonstrated on a recognised English language test) but this review suggests that additional support is required to build knowledge and skills in cultural norms when communicating.

This review also highlighted actions that can be taken to educate members of the workforce (who work with IENs) about different cultures and the value of a diverse workforce. Recommendations included building awareness of different religious beliefs, food and approaches to health care as well as developing interpersonal skills to support IENs (such as patience and approaching interactions in an encouraging manner). These skills (patience, empathy, honesty) are valued in nurse-patient interactions [42] but also need to transfer to peer interactions.

Unfortunately, none of these studies were experimental in nature, meaning that none of the studies tested or compared the efficacy of strategies and measure objective outcomes. Studies such as this would help to ensure that healthcare organisations can ensure evidence-based practice and could also be beneficial in determining the cost-benefit of different approaches given that some strategies will be more resource intensive than others.

Our review synthesises the findings of individual research studies in this field and our findings echo findings from similar reviews. However, the findings of this review provide a valuable contribution to the field as we bring together the most recent research findings on this topic across countries and study types. A recent scoping review of strategies to support international medical graduates similarly failed to identify experimental studies [43]. This review by Nwankwo and colleagues found that support strategies mostly involved education and social support to facilitate transition and that tailoring of strategies to individual needs, continual re-assessment and ongoing assistance were beneficial [43]. A Discussion Paper published by the Centre for Cultural Diversity in Ageing (Australia) examining wellbeing in the aged care workforce suggested a range of support strategies including clearly articulated organisational values, thorough workplace orientation, opportunities to improve English language skills and social support networks and connections. These strategies align with those identified in our review of scientific literature [44].

This review involved a comprehensive search of three databases and grey literature, with several of our included studies being identified in our grey literature search. Limitations of this review include exclusion of studies published in languages other than English and limiting our search to studies published in the last ten years as older studies may also have provided valuable information. A further limitation was that we excluded studies which explored the benefits of bridging programs that were offered by third parties rather than the employing health organisation.

Conclusions

In the absence of studies demonstrating the efficacy of interventions, health care organisations should consider implementing support strategies which have been reported to be beneficial in creating a supportive, inclusive environment by internationally educated nurses. Strategies include peer and managerial support, tailored orientation, cultural and language integration support and ongoing professional development.

Implications

Based on our review results, several implications can be recommended. First, organisations need to review existing approaches and put a range of strategies into place to support IENs. Secondly, strategies need to be applied at a range of different levels; from organisational policies, to cultural education and peer support at the ward level. Further studies should evaluate the effectiveness of interventions on objective outcome measures and compare the efficacy and cost-benefit of different approaches.
