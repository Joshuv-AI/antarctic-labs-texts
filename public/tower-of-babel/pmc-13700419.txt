Development of a multi-parameter MRI-based habitat radiomics model for differential diagnosis of vestibular schwannoma and meningioma in the cerebellopontine angle: a multi-center study
Authors: Xiyuan Tang, Xueyun Deng, Yanfen Zhao, Shuang Li, Yuanchuan Wang, Bo Tan, Xudong Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVestibular schwannomas (VS) and meningiomas (MM) are the most common benign tumors in the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), often presenting with similar clinical symptoms. Introduction

The cerebellopontine angle (CPA) is a vital anatomical region located in the posterior cranial fossa, where tumors constitute approximately 5%-10% of all intracranial neoplasms. Vestibular schwannomas (VS) and meningiomas (MM) are the most common benign tumors in this region, accounting for approximately 85%-90% of CPA tumors [1]. Although both VS and MM are benign and share similar clinical manifestations, such as hearing loss, tinnitus, headache, and gait instability, they differ markedly in their growth patterns, treatment strategies, and prognoses [2–4]. Therefore, accurate preoperative differential diagnosis is essential for developing personalized treatment plans.

Currently, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the primary modality for screening, diagnosing, and monitoring CPA tumors. As surgical objectives increasingly prioritize the preservation of neurological function and the improvement of quality of life [5], there is a rising demand for noninvasive preoperative differential diagnostic methods. Radiomics (Rad) addresses this need by extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from regions of interest (ROI) in medical images, using machine learning or deep learning algorithms to reveal hidden imaging information. This offers a novel, noninvasive approach for disease differentiation, grading, prognostication, follow-up, and treatment evaluation [6]. However, traditional radiomics often treats the tumor as a homogeneous entity, potentially overlooking its inherent temporal and spatial heterogeneity [7–9]. Recently, habitat analysis has emerged as a technique that maps macroscopically observable heterogeneous regions on imaging to microscopically distinct cellular subpopulations with similar gene expression profiles and biological phenotypes within the tumor [10]. This approach has shown promise in predicting patient survival, molecular subtyping of tumors, differential diagnosis, treatment response assessment, and recurrence prediction [11–13].

This study aims to develop an MRI-based habitat radiomics model for the differential diagnosis of VS and MM in the CPA region. We compare the diagnostic performance of various machine learning algorithms and assess the feasibility and performance differences between single-parameter MRI habitat models, multi-parameter MRI habitat models, and a combined model integrating habitat radiomics features with clinical characteristics.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 176 patients diagnosed with CPA vestibular schwannoma (VS) or meningioma (MM) based on clinical or postoperative pathology across three centers: Guangyuan Central Hospital (Center 1), Nanchong Central Hospital (Center 2), and Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (Center 3) between January 2019 and September 2025. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 101 patients (68 with VS, 33 with MM) were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

(1) First-time patients with no prior treatment before their initial MRI; (2) Complete preoperative MRI images, including T2-weighted and CE-T1WI sequences, with the lesion visible on at least 3 consecutive slices; (3) Diagnosis of VS or MM confirmed by pathology or clinical evaluation.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Incomplete imaging data or significant artifacts; (2) History of brain trauma, other brain tumors, or infectious diseases; (3) Preoperative treatment with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other therapies.The patient enrollment flowchart is shown in Fig. 1a. Using random sampling, eligible patients were divided into a training set and a test set in a 7:3 ratio. To address the class imbalance issue, we applied the SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) oversampling method to augment the minority class samples in the training set. The overall study workflow is depicted in Fig. 1b.

Instruments and methods

MRI images were acquired as follows: Center 1 and Center 2 each used a Siemens 1.5 T MRI scanner. Center 3 used GE Signa Excite 1.5 T and Discovery MR 750 3.0 T MRI scanners with 8-channel and 32-channel phased-array head coils, respectively, along with standard quadrature head coils. CE-T1WI and T2WI images were included for subsequent analysis. The axial T2WI and CE-T1WI sequences were acquired with a slice thickness of 5 mm and an interslice gap of 0.5 mm. For contrast-enhanced scans: Center 1 and Center 2 used gadopentetate dimeglumine injection (manufacturer: Guangzhou Consun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.); Center 3 used gadobenate dimeglumine injection (manufacturer: Shanghai Bracco Sine Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.). The contrast agent was administered at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg body weight via intravenous injection at a rate of 2 ml/s.

Image preprocessing and segmentation

Original preoperative DICOM-format MRI images were copied from the hospital Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Prior to segmentation, N4 bias field correction was applied using Python (version 3.7.0, https://github.com/aim-Harvard/pyRadiology) to correct inhomogeneous signal intensity for accurate subsequent analysis. Blinded to clinical and pathological information, a junior neurosurgeon manually performed image segmentation using 3D Slicer software (version 5.2.2, http://www.slicer.org/). ROIs encompassing the entire tumor were delineated slice-by-slice on T2WI and CE-T1WI sequences to generate a 3D tumor volume. A senior neurosurgeon reviewed and confirmed all segmentations. To evaluate the robustness of segmentation, two neurosurgeons with different years of experience independently performed tumor segmentation on randomly selected cases and calculated the Dice similarity coefficient. The results showed an overall Dice coefficient of 0.928 ± 0.169, with Dice coefficients of 0.986 ± 0.026 for the T2WI sequence and 0.986 ± 0.032 for the CE-T1WI sequence, indicating high consistency in segmentation. Tumor masks were generated from the segmentation results and paired with their corresponding MR images. To extract local radiomics information within the tumor, a sliding window method was employed: a 5 × 5 × 5 cubic window centered on each pixel within the tumor mask was defined, and radiomics features were extracted from each window using the PyRadiomics toolkit (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/2.1.2/). All feature values were then normalized to a range of 0 to 1.

To reduce computational complexity, five key texture features from the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) were selected: contrast, difference entropy, correlation, joint energy, and joint entropy. GLCM effectively captures subtle texture variations reflecting tumor heterogeneity and complexity [14] Thus, each pixel’s local radiomics information was represented as a 5-dimensional feature vector.

Pixels with analogous radiomics features indicate relative tissue homogeneity. To identify similar intratumoral subregions, a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) was employed for pixel-level clustering across the cohort, where the cluster number k regulates the analytical resolution. Clustering was performed at the cohort level (not per patient) to ensure consistency. Cluster centers derived from the training cohort were propagated to the test cohort. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined using the Silhouette coefficient. Based on K, the tumor was partitioned into corresponding habitat subregions. This habitat generation process was completed using the PixelMed AI platform.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Prior to feature extraction from the whole tumor and subregions, MR images were preprocessed to minimize inter-scanner variability: (1) All images and segmentation masks were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³; (2) Voxel intensity values were discretized using a fixed bin width of 25 before normalization. Eight types of filters were applied: Wavelet, Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG), Gradient, LocalBinaryPattern3D, Exponential, Square, SquareRoot, and Logarithm. Radiomics features were extracted from both original and filtered images using the open-source PyRadiomics Python package (version 3.0.1, https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/, Computational Imaging Biomarkers Lab, Harvard Medical School). An independent samples t-test was initially used to filter features, excluding those with p > 0.05 to ensure statistical significance. For highly reproducible features, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. Only feature pairs with a correlation coefficient > 0.9 were retained to reduce redundancy. Further feature optimization was performed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. Stable radiomics features were incorporated into LASSO analysis with a penalty parameter α, which shrinks some regression coefficients to zero. The optimal α value was determined via 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum criterion. Features with non-zero coefficients in the model corresponding to the optimal α were selected, yielding an independent and stable radiomics feature set.

Finally, based on the features selected by LASSO and their importance, a feature importance histogram was plotted to visually represent each feature’s contribution to the model.

Model construction

After feature selection and fusion with clinical features, four models were established: a T2WI-based habitat radiomics model (T2), a CE-T1WI-based habitat radiomics model (T1C), a T2WI & CE-T1WI fused habitat radiomics model (T2&T1C), and a combined model integrating habitat radiomics features with clinical features (Combine). In the binary classification task, meningioma (MM) was designated as the positive category (coded as 1), while vestibular schwannoma (VS) was designated as the negative category (coded as 0). Machine learning classification models were built using the scikit-learn library, including: Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Extremely Randomized Trees (ExtraTree), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). To mitigate overfitting, 5-fold cross-validation was used to select optimal hyperparameters for each classifier. Model performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.

To interpret model decisions, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated for each prediction sample. SHAP, derived from cooperative game theory, quantifies the contribution of each feature to increasing or decreasing the output probability for an individual prediction, revealing feature impact and interactions [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.2; https://www.r-project.org) and Python (version 3.8; https://www.python.org). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD); categorical variables are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed quantitative data, with Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for non-normally distributed quantitative data across multiple groups. The Chi-square test was used for categorical data comparisons. DeLong’s test was used to compare ROC curves between models. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to compare the clinical net benefit of models across threshold probabilities. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess model calibration (goodness-of-fit between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

After inclusion and exclusion, 101 patients were enrolled (31 male, 70 female), with a mean age of 56.90 ± 10.41 years. Based on clinical or postoperative pathology, 68 patients (67.33%) were diagnosed with VS and 33 (32.67%) with MM. Among these cases, a total of 97 cases (96.04%) were confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination, including 65 vestibular schwannomas (accounting for 95.59% of VS cases) and 32 meningiomas (accounting for 96.97% of MM cases). The remaining 4 cases (3 VS and 1 MM) were diagnosed based on typical imaging features and clinical follow-up. Patients were randomly split 7:3 into a training set (n = 70; 50 VS, 20 MM) and a test set (n = 31; 18 VS, 13 MM). Comparison of baseline clinical characteristics between the training and test sets showed no significant differences in age, gender, or tumor location (all p > 0.05), as detailed in Table 1.

Results of habitat radiomics feature extraction and selection

Prior to habitat feature extraction, GMM clustering was applied to tumor ROIs to segment habitat subregions. The optimal cluster number “K” was identified via the silhouette coefficient. The K value with the highest silhouette coefficient and marked slope change was selected (K = 3 for this study; Fig. 1c), and each tumor was thereby partitioned into three habitat subregions.

For each subregion, 1561 features were initially extracted, broadly categorized into: first-order features (n = 306), shape features (n = 14), and texture features including Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM, n = 374), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM, n = 272), Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM, n = 272), Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM, n = 238), and Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM, n = 82).

Following Z-score normalization, independent samples t-test, and Pearson correlation analysis to remove low-reproducibility and highly redundant features, LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used for further dimensionality reduction and feature selection for T2WI, CE-T1WI, and T2WI&CE-T1WI habitat radiomics features. The feature selection process, optimal regularization parameter α selection (optimized α values: 0.054, 0.012, 0.003, respectively), and feature importance histograms are shown in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Finally, 5, 7, and 8 features were retained for the T2WI, CE-T1WI, and fused models, respectively.

Discussion

VS and MM in the CPA share similarities in clinical presentation and imaging features. Typical MRI findings for VS include hypointensity on T1WI, isointense or slightly hyperintensity on T2WI, and heterogeneous marked enhancement on CE-T1WI, often accompanied by internal auditory canal (IAC) widening and thickening of the vestibular nerve root. Meningiomas typically appear isointense on T1WI, hyperintense on T2WI, show homogeneous marked enhancement on CE-T1WI, and may exhibit a “dural tail sign” [16]. However, in clinical practice, some meningiomas may be located near or grow into the IAC, while some VS may show signal heterogeneity due to cystic degeneration or micro-hemorrhage, further complicating differentiation. Differences in the relationship with neurovascular structures, histology, and potential invasiveness also influence surgical approach selection [17]. Traditional imaging diagnosis relies heavily on subjective visual assessment by neurosurgeons or radiologists based on experience. In contrast, methods based on objective quantitative indicators offer greater reliability, reproducibility, and clinical value.

Radiomics employs algorithms to extract high-dimensional quantitative information from medical images, providing a novel noninvasive tool for tumor screening, diagnosis, treatment planning, and outcome prediction [18]. Patel et al. [19] noted that radiomics is commonly applied in skull base meningiomas, VS, and sellar/parasellar tumors for detection/segmentation, classification, grading, feature detection, and prognosis, with CE-T1WI-based MRI radiomics models being the most frequently developed. Chakrabarty et al. [20] trained a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to classify six intracranial tumor types (acoustic neuroma, high-grade glioma, low-grade glioma, brain metastasis, meningioma, pituitary adenoma) and healthy tissue using MRI T1-enhanced images, achieving internal and external test accuracies of 93.35% and 91.95%, respectively. This demonstrates that advanced CNN algorithms can enhance diagnostic performance for multiple pathologies. However, most radiomics studies extract features from the entire tumor region, which may be confounded by factors like hemorrhage, necrosis, or peritumoral edema, potentially masking true heterogeneity and reducing model precision [21]. Notably, solid tumors exhibit inherent temporal and spatial heterogeneity; observable local or global differences on MRI may stem from variations in tumor cell composition or properties [22]. Habitat imaging visualizes this intratumoral heterogeneity, distinguishing it from whole-tumor radiomics analysis.

Habitat Analysis and Habitat Radiomics, emerging fields at the intersection of radiomics and tumor ecology, enable quantitative MRI-based analysis of spatial-temporal heterogeneity within tumors, revealing the dynamic interaction mechanisms between the tumor microenvironment (TME) and host microenvironment (HME) [10]. The core technique involves partitioning tumors into biologically distinct subregions (habitats), each characterized by shared imaging features due to unique intrinsic cell populations and/or TME. By combining radiomics features (e.g., T2-FLAIR heterogeneity, perfusion parameters) with host clinical parameters (e.g., age, immune status), multidimensional diagnostic models are built, enabling interpretable quantification of tumor heterogeneity distribution and evolution [23]. For instance, in glioma, Beig et al. [24] used habitat radiomics features from glioblastoma (necrosis, enhancing tumor, edema) to develop a radiomics risk score (RRS) predicting progression-free survival on pretreatment MRI. 18 F-FDG PET/CT-based habitat radiomics outperformed conventional radiomics in predicting overall survival in hepatocellular carcinoma, with further improvement after integration with a clinical model [25]. Habitat analysis is increasingly applied to predict molecular subtypes, treatment response, survival, etc., in neuro-oncology [26–28], but has not been previously applied to differentiate CPA VS from MM.

In this study, to prevent information leakage, all steps related to model construction were strictly confined to the training set. The detailed workflow is as follows: (1) Sliding window-based radiomic feature extraction was performed exclusively on the training set data. (2) A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) was fitted solely using the feature vectors of all pixels from the training set, and the optimal number of clusters K was determined within the range of K = 2–10 using the Silhouette coefficient. This process was entirely completed within the training set. (3) Based on the optimal K value determined from the training set and the corresponding GMM parameters (i.e., cluster centers and covariance matrices), the final clustering model was constructed. (4) For the test set data, no re-fitting or re-training of the clustering model was performed. Instead, the GMM model learned from the training set was directly applied to the pixel-wise features of the test set to assign the corresponding sub-region (habitat) labels. Through the aforementioned steps, strict independence between the training and test sets was ensured. ROIs were manually delineated on T2WI and CE-T1WI MRI sequences, followed by preprocessing. To objectively extract intratumoral subregions, five selected GLCM texture features were computed for each pixel within the tumor using a sliding window. GLCM effectively captures subtle texture variations reflecting image irregularity and complexity, which is significant for studying tumor heterogeneity. Furthermore, most extracted features were derived from wavelet transforms, which capture heterogeneous information at multiple scales and orientations, providing a more comprehensive description of complex image structures [29]. Subsequently, GMM clustering generated three distinct subregions for habitat radiomics feature extraction, followed by feature dimensionality reduction and selection. Based on this, MRI-based habitat radiomics models were constructed for VS vs. MM differential diagnosis in the CPA. Comparing models based on T2WI features, CE-T1WI features, and fused T2WI&CE-T1WI features revealed that the multi-parameter fused feature model outperformed single-parameter models, with a training set AUC of 0.987 (95% CI: 0.9030–0.9985) and test set AUC of 0.974 (95% CI: 0.7697–0.9916). Our finding that multi-parameter models outperform single-parameter ones is consistent with results from Panahi et al. [30].

Subsequently, a combined model integrating subregional features from different sequences with clinical features was constructed. This combined model showed significantly improved predictive performance compared to habitat radiomics models alone, achieving AUCs of 0.987 (95% CI: 0.9030–0.9985) in the training set and 0.974 (95% CI: 0.7697–0.9916) in the test set. Compared to existing conventional radiomics models, our clinical-habitat radiomics model demonstrated markedly enhanced performance for CPA VS and MM differential diagnosis, offering a novel, objective, quantitative, and potentially monitorable tool for noninvasive preoperative differentiation. Moreover, DCA indicated greater clinical utility for the combined model. Its calibration curve also closely approximated the ideal fit line, indicating predictions were closer to actual diagnoses. SHAP analysis, which quantifies the contribution of each individual feature to the final prediction for every observation, enhances model interpretability [31]. Results showed that habitat radiomics features contributed substantially to model performance, while age, gender, and tumor location had lower weights, suggesting limited contribution within this specific model.

Study limitations

(1) Retrospective three-center study design may have selection bias: retrospective data is susceptible to the impact of uneven case screening, and prospective data from more centers can be supplemented in the future; data heterogeneity: differences in data collection standards and equipment among different centers may lead to result bias. (2) Relatively small sample size with class imbalance (fewer MM cases) may affect results. Although the SMOTE oversampling technique was applied during model training to mitigate the class imbalance, future work should further expand the sample size, balance the proportions of the two tumor types, and incorporate an independent external validation cohort to enhance model generalizability. (3) Although this study employed a multi-center design, the limited sample size precluded more rigorous cross-center validation strategies, such as leave-one-center-out cross-validation. Consequently, the cross-scanner and cross-center robustness of the model remains to be further validated in future studies using large-scale, independent external cohorts. (4) Manual tumor ROI segmentation may introduce subjective bias. Although supplementary inter-rater agreement analysis demonstrated good reproducibility, future integration of increasingly sophisticated deep learning algorithms for automated, sub-regional tumor segmentation could improve the reproducibility and transferability of the model across different centers and imaging platforms. (5) The novelty is primarily in the methodological combination (habitat radiomics analysis + clinical features). Included clinical features were limited; the potential value of integrating molecular biomarkers or genomics was not explored. Subsequent research could integrate tumor genomic data (e.g., NF2 gene mutation) to develop multi-omics diagnostic models. Independent analysis of the two tumor types to assess specific clinical risk factors (e.g., tumor diameter, intratumoral cystic/calcified component ratio, specific symptom combinations, degree of cranial nerve encasement) followed by SHAP-based contribution quantification could further enhance the model’s biological interpretability. (6) The present cohort included a subset of pediatric patients. Although current results suggest that the model exhibits a certain degree of stability when applied to data encompassing individuals of varying ages, the pediatric sample size is limited. This study is therefore insufficient to fully evaluate the model’s applicability across different age subgroups, particularly in the pediatric population. Future investigations with larger sample sizes and stratified designs are warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, habitat analysis is an emerging technique that quantifies and visualizes intratumoral subregions. Combined with radiomics, it can provide a more accurate basis for formulating precise, personalized treatment strategies. This study successfully established a preoperative multi-parameter MRI-based habitat radiomics model for differential diagnosis of vestibular schwannoma and meningioma in the cerebellopontine angle. Furthermore, the combined model integrating clinical features with habitat radiomics features further improved diagnostic performance for CPA VS vs. MM.
