Association between seasonal influenza vaccination and neonatal outcomes in Shanghai, China
Authors: Lei Lei, Ling Yang, Dong-Mei Feng, Yan Yan, Qing-Quan Cai, Hong Pang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile international and domestic recommendations advocating for influenza vaccination during pregnancy, the vaccination rate among this demographic in China remains low, mainly due to concerns about fetal safety. This retrospective cohort enrolled mothers who registered and delivered between August 2020 and July 2023. The relationship between maternal influenza vaccination and adverse neonatal outcomes was assessed using Poisson regression models and logistic regression analyses. The adverse neonatal outcomes under evaluation included small for gestational age (SGA), large for gestational age (LGA), preterm birth (PTB), low birth weight (LBW), low Apgar score, congenital anomalies, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission or referral, as well as stillbirth or neonatal death. Of the 2,517 women, 203 (8.1%) had received influenza vaccination during their pregnancies. The incidence rate of gestational complications in the vaccinated group being significantly higher than that in the unvaccinated group. In comparison with no vaccination, there was no statistically connection between vaccination and an increased risk of any adverse neonatal outcomes. The adjusted risk ratios (95% CIs) were: SGA 1.354 (0.842–2.177), LGA 1.504 (0.808–2.798), PTB 0.639 (0.257–1.590), LBW 0.294 (0.072–1.211), low Apgar score 0.918 (0.213–3.958), congenital anomaly 0.676 (0.087–5.247), and NICU admission or referral 0.151 (0.021–1.089). In this study, there was only 1 stillbirth and 1 neonatal death in the unvaccinated group, resulting in a cumulative incidence of 0.1%. No stillbirths or neonatal deaths occurred in the vaccinated group. These findings endorse public health initiatives aimed at enhancing vaccination rates among pregnant women.KEYWORDS: Pregnancy, influenza vaccine, small/large for gestational age, stillbirth/neonatal death, congenital anomaly, preterm birth, low birth weight, low Apgar score

Introduction

Seasonal influenza imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide and affects 5–15% of the global population annually,1 disproportionately impacting high-risk groups such as pregnant women who face elevated risks of severe illness, hospitalization, mortality, and adverse pregnancy outcomes following influenza infection.2–4 The World Health Organization (WHO) data indicate that pregnant women experience influenza-related hospitalization rates of 0.04–7.7 per 10,000 and mortality rates of 0.003–0.69 per 10,000.5 Influenza vaccination decreases infection risk by 40–60%, alleviates disease severity, and lowers maternal influenza-related hospitalizations by 40%.6–8 Importantly, maternal immunization confers neonatal protection, reducing infant influenza risk by 33% and hospitalizations by 40%.9 Robust evidence from international clinical trials, observational studies, and clinical guidelines confirms its safety during pregnancy,10–13 this has prompted the WHO, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Chinese CDC to recommend prioritization for pregnant women for influenza vaccination.14–17

Despite recommendations, Concrens from fetal safety are the main reason for the low influenza vaccination rate among pregnant women in China.18–20 This study aims to assess the effects of influenza vaccination during pregnancy on the fetus, to establish a scientific foundation for evaluating the safety of implementing this practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study with a population-based design enrolled all women who registered for and delivered at Shanghai United Family Hospital (SUFH) from August 2020 to July 2023. Established in 2004, SUFH is a private international comprehensive hospital situated in Shanghai, China, providing obstetrical and vaccination clinics. Between 2020 and 2023, the annual average number of deliveries at this hospital exceeded 800 cases per year. Beginning in 2019, obstetricians at SUFH have started broadly recommending flu vaccines to pregnant women during flu season that defined as November 1st of a given year to March 30th of the following year. Pregnant women who are willing to receive the vaccine can conveniently do so at the hospital’s vaccination clinic, providing an opportunity for us to investigate the relationship between maternal flu vaccination and neonatal outcomes. No sample size was calculated in advance，as the number of participants in the cohort was determined by study duration. Approval for the study was obtained from the ethics committee of the Changning District Center for Disease Control and Prevention in Shanghai (approval number 2023–11).

Data collection

All mothers information we collected during this study period was obtained from the electronic obstetric outpatient and inpatient registration system of the hospital, including maternal sociodemographic characteristics, pre-pregnancy weight and height, gravidity, parity, history of adverse perinatal outcomes in a previous pregnancy, expected date of birth, the timing of initiating prenatal care, pregnancy induced complications, delivery mode, the date of delivery and pregnancy results. Subsequently, body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight by the square of height (kg/m2). We have also gathered information on fetal and neonatal outcomes, including infant sex, gestational age, weight and length at birth, 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores, occurrence of stillbirth or neonatal death, congenital malformations, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or referral to a tertiary hospital. Influenza vaccination information (vaccine brand and date of administration) for all mothers is sourced from the Shanghai Immunization Program Information Management System, a government-mandated electronic registry that contains vaccination data from all vaccination clinics in Shanghai. The follow-up duration was defined as the period from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP) to the end of pregnancy. We excluded 129 pregnancies from all women who delivered at SUFH throughout the study duration, based on the following categories: deliveries before 24 weeks’ gestation or beyond 42 weeks’ gestation, and pregnancies lacking documented records of prenatal care. After these exclusions, we had a cohort of 2,517 women (Figure 1).

Enrollment of pregnant women at Shanghai United Family Hospital from August 2020 to July 2023.

Exposure

We classified women as exposed if they had received an influenza vaccine during pregnancy. Those who had not received an influenza vaccine during pregnancy were classified as unexposed. No vaccines other than the influenza vaccine were administered to pregnant women during their pregnancy, including the COVID-19 vaccine and tetanus vaccine. Gestational age is typically determined by calculating from the first day of the LMP to estimate the trimester for vaccination. In the event of uncertainty LMP date or discrepancy of more than seven days between the LMP-based estimationand the ultrasound assessment conducted during the booking visit. When arises regarding the uncertainty LMP date or discrepancy of more than seven days between the LMP-based estimation and the ultrasound assessment conducted during the booking visit, the ultrasound findings take precedence. The first trimester is defined as weeks 0–12 of gestation, the second trimester as weeks 13–26, and the third trimester as weeks 27 until the end of pregnancy.

Outcomes

Delivery mode included spontaneous vaginal delivery (SVD), lower segment cesarean section (LSCS), forceps, and ventouse. Preterm birth (PTB) was identified as delivery at <37 weeks of gestation. Small for gestational age (SGA) and large for gestational age (LGA) were classified as weight-for-gestational age <10th percentile and >90th percentile, respectively, using the Fenton infant growth chart as the reference standard.21 Low birth weight (LBW) was identified as a birth weight of less than 2500 g. Low Apgar scores were identified as scores <7 at either 1 or 5 minutes after birth. Congenital anomalies were identified through electronic records documenting any anomalies detected by midwifery or pediatric staff in the delivery suite. Stillbirth was defined as delivery of an infant showing no signs of life at 24 weeks of gestation or later. Neonatal death was classified as the death of an infant within the first seven days following birth. NICU admission or referral was defined as either admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or transfer to a tertiary hospital.

Statistical analysis

We compared continuous characteristics using Student’s t-tests and categorical characteristics using χ2 tests between the vaccinated and unvaccinated cohorts. Statistical significance was established with a two-sided P < .05. In our analysis of delivery mode and neonatal outcomes, we focused on singleton births and excluded women who had received the influenza vaccine within 7 days prior to delivery. For the delivery mode outcome, we applied univariable logistic regression models to derive unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) alongside 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all delivery modes. We subsequently employed multivariable logistic regression to evaluate ORs and 95% CIs, with adjustment for potential confounding variables. For birth outcomes, we estimated crude risk ratios (cRRs) alongside 95% CIs and adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) alongside 95% CIs using Poisson regression models without and with adjustment for confounding variables. The following confounders were adjusted for all models: infant sex, maternal nationality, maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, parity, gestational complications, previous pregnancy loss, gestational age at first exam, and season of delivery. Continuous variables such as maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI were categorized for descriptive purposes but retained as continuous in regression models.

We performed several sensitivity analyses to evaluate the reliability of the primary results. First, we replicated the analysis by restricting the cohort to nulliparous women due to the recurrence of certain outcomes between pregnancies. Second, to account for the influence of race on outcomes, we reevaluated our hypotheses by excluding foreign women. Third, some studies indicate that infants delivered during peak influenza periods have poorer birth outcomes.22 Therefore, we repeated the analysis after excluding deliveries that took place in the peak influenza months. Stata version 15 was used to carry out all analyses.

Results

Among the 2517 women who gave birth to either a liveborn or stillborn infant during the study period, 203(8.1%; 95% CI 7.0–9.3) received Sanofi Pasteur’s trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine during their pregnancy. Of those, 21.7% (44) received the vaccination in the first trimester, 40.4% (82) in the second trimester, and 37.9% (77) in the third trimester.

Table 1 displays the demographic features and clinical features of the cohort categorized by vaccine receipt status. Compared to unvaccinated individuals, those who received the vaccination exhibited a greater tendency to be Chinese citizens and in the 30–35 age range. They were also more likely to have enrolled for prenatal care in their first trimester, be multiparous women, have a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes (previous spontaneous abortion, previous elective termination and stillbirth), and deliver during influenza peak seasons (November–March). Additionally, the distribution of gestational complications differed significantly between vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals (P < .05), with the incidence rate of gestational complications in the vaccinated group being significantly higher than that in the unvaccinated group. Further comparison of individual complication types revealed that the difference in the incidence of Hypothyroidism between the two groups was particularly prominent: the incidence of Hypothyroidism in the vaccinated group was significantly higher than that in the unvaccinated group, and this difference was statistically significant (P < .05).

There was 1 congenital anomaly among 195 pregnancies in vaccinated women and 19 among 2286 pregnancies in unvaccinated women (0.5% vs. 0.8%; aRR 0.676, 95% CI 0.087–5.247). The congenital anomaly recorded for the 1 neonate was an atrial septal defect. Among the 19 neonates with recorded congenital anomalies, there were 8 cases of congenital heart disease, 3 of polydactyly, 3 of renal agenesis or hypospadias, and 1 case each of left-sided polycystic dysplastic kidney, nasal septum perforation, duodenal atresia, zygomatic arch syndrome, and bilateral clubfoot varus deformity. Notably, in this study, only 1 stillbirth and 1 neonatal death occurred in the unvaccinated group, for a cumulative incidence of 0.1%, and none occurred in the vaccinated group.

The sensitivity analyses revealed that restricting the dataset to primigravidas, Chinese women, and those who delivered outside influenza peak seasons (April-October) yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, supporting similar conclusions regarding the safety of administering influenza vaccine to pregnant women. The detailed statistical results for each subgroup (primigravidas, pregnant women of Chinese nationality, and women who delivered outside influenza peak seasons) are available in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Discussion

Influenza vaccination rate in pregnancy was 8.1% at the study hospital in Shanghai. While the vaccination rate in this study considerably exceeded the 0.04% rate observed in Suzhou, China (2018–2023)19 and Gansu, China (2019–2021),23 it remains worryingly low in comparison to findings from developed countries. For instance, the influenza vaccination coverage reached approximately 50% in Australia (2016–2018),24 the UK (2018–2019),25 the USA (2019–2020)26 and Switzerland (2020–2021).27 This suggests that improving obstetricians’/gynecologists’ recommendations and increasing accessibility to vaccines alone may not suffice in overcoming barriers to influenza vaccination in pregnancy. China still encounters major challenges in policy, health systems, and patient perceptions when it comes to increasing seasonal influenza vaccination coverage among pregnant women.28

Adverse neonatal outcomes, such as LBW, PTB, and SGA, serve as major contributors to neonatal mortality and early childhood morbidity.29–31 The study calculated the overall prevalence rates of LBW, PTB, and SGA to be 3.0% (75/2481), 3.6% (88/2481), and 8.5% (212/2481), respectively. These rates closely align with the overall prevalence rates in Shanghai between 2004 and 2020, which were 2.9%, 4.7%, and 9.3%, respectively.32

In this study, no correlation was found between influenza vaccination in pregnancy and the risk related to delivery mode or adverse neonatal outcomes, including SGA, LGA, LBW, PTB, NICU admission, referral to a tertiary hospital, congenital anomalies, or low Apgar scores after adjusting for several potential confounders. This finding aligns with previous studies in other countries. Most retrospective and prospective cohort studies and case-control studies suggest comparable rates of adverse neonatal outcomes, such as LBW, PTB, SGA, LGA, spontaneous abortion, NICU admission, miscarriage, stillbirth, low Apgar score, respiratory distress syndrome and congenital malformations, between vaccinated and unvaccinated pregnant women.11,12,33–36 On the other hand, numerous other studies have linked influenza vaccination to a decreased risk of PTB, LBW, SGA, stillbirth, neonatal death, and NICU admission.22,37–43 A limited number of randomized controlled trials have indicated no increase in adverse outcomes in the influenza vaccination group in contrast to the placebo group. A phase 4, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in Nepal from 2011 to 2013 showed that maternal influenza immunization decreased the rates of LBW by 15% (95% CI 3–25) while the rates of PTB and SGA were not affected by immunization. The number of adverse events, including miscarriage, stillbirth, congenital defects and infant deaths at age 0–6 months, was similar regardless of immunization status.44 Two double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trials in South Africa during 2011–2012 found that there were no significant differences between the rates of miscarriage, stillbirth, or PTB or LBW in the influenza vaccine recipients and the placebo recipients.45 A prospective, active-controlled, observer-blind, randomized phase 4 trial in Mali in 2011–2013 also showed that no severe adverse events among infants were found to be related to maternal influenza vaccination, including stillbirth, LBW/SGA and major congenital malformations.46 Differences in study methodologies, participant demographics, and circulating influenza virus strains present challenges for comparing results.

This study has some limitations to note. First, the relatively small number of deliveries observed precluded analyses of uncommon outcomes like fetal and neonatal death; meanwhile, it may also result in a small sample size for subgroups in the sensitivity analysis, which may limit the generalizability of subgroup-specific conclusions. Second, this observational study is subject to residual confounding, as data on additional risk factors associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, such as maternal education,47 smoking habits,48 alcohol consumption during pregnancy,49 and exposure to fine particulate matter,50 were not collected. These unmeasured factors may well have confounded the correlation of influenza vaccination with pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, detailed information regarding the timing of gestational complications was not gathered, so the relationship between vaccination and pregnancy complications cannot be definitively established. Third, the study was conducted in a single private international hospital in Shanghai. The deliveries at this hospital are primarily from high-income groups, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other regions of China.

This is the first study conducted in China, specifically in Shanghai, that explores the link between influenza vaccination during pregnancy and both delivery and neonatal outcomes. Our study was strengthened by our access to comprehensive electronic immunization and medical records, encompassing details of all deliveries, demographic information, birth outcomes, and vaccination history.

Conclusion

The results of this retrospective cohort study indicate that administering inactivated influenza vaccine during pregnancy did not correlate with a heightened risk of predetermined pregnancy outcomes. These findings endorse public health initiatives aimed at enhancing vaccination rates among expectant mothers. Subsequent research endeavors would gain from utilizing multicenter cohorts and undertaking prospective investigations to broaden the assessment of influenza vaccine safety during pregnancy in China.
