Towards omission of drainage in distal pancreatectomy—the PANDRA II study: another brick in the wall!
Authors: Emmanuel Devant, Mircea Chirica
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the randomized controlled trial PANDRA II, conducted at Heidelberg University between 2017 and 2023, aimed to evaluate the relevance of omitting prophylactic drainage after distal pancreatectomy (1). A total of 246 patients were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis (125 with drainage, 121 without drainage). The primary endpoint was overall postoperative morbidity, assessed by the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), which integrates all complications according to their severity. The no-drain group demonstrated non-inferiority compared to the drain group in terms of CCI (13.90±16.51 vs. 19.43±16.92; P<0.001 for non-inferiority). Moreover, it exhibited a significantly lower overall complication rate (50% vs. 78%; P<0.001). Specific complications, such as postoperative pancreatic fistula (15% vs. 21%; P=0.23) and post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (4.96% vs. 4.80%; P>0.999), were comparable between groups. However, the incidence of chyle leaks was higher in the drain group (18.4% vs. 0.83%; P<0.001). Operative time (212±64 vs. 205±62 min; P=0.373), length of hospital stay (12.01±10.84 vs. 12.91±11.53 days; P=0.53), and readmission rate (19.83% vs. 27.20%; P=0.17) did not differ significantly. These findings indicate that omission of drainage is not inferior to the conventional strategy and is associated with a significant reduction in overall morbidity.

Discussion

Prophylactic abdominal drainage after distal pancreatectomy has long been considered a safety measure to prevent severe complications such as postoperative pancreatic fistula or hemorrhage (1,2). However, recent data suggest that drains may themselves contribute to postoperative morbidity (3). The results of PANDRA II confirm the conclusions of other randomized trials, representing a paradigm shift in pancreatic surgery (2,4,5). They demonstrate that omitting the drain does not compromise postoperative safety and may reduce overall morbidity.

The literature on the topic is still scarce, with only two other randomized trials published to date (2,5). In 2017, van Buren et al. (5) conducted a multicenter trial (Canada, USA) including 344 patients undergoing distal pancreatectomy with (n=174) or without drainage (n=170). Rates of major complications (26% vs. 29%), clinically significant pancreatic fistula (18% vs. 12%), and mortality (0% vs. 1%) were comparable. Omission of drainage was associated with a higher incidence of intra-abdominal collections (22% vs. 9%) without affecting percutaneous drainage, reoperation, readmission, or quality of life. In 2024, the international PANDORINA trial, conducted in 12 centers in the Netherlands and Italy, compared distal pancreatectomy with or without intraoperative drainage in 282 patients (2). Drain omission reduced the incidence of pancreatic fistulas (12% vs. 27%) and overall complications (33% vs. 51%), without affecting major morbidity (15% vs. 20%). The authors concluded that drain-free surgery is safe and associated with improved clinical outcomes.

The PANDRA II trial, with its non-inferiority design and relevant primary endpoint (CCI), adds further evidence to the fact that distal pancreatectomy, whether open or minimally invasive, can be safely performed without drainage (1). The no-drain group was non-inferior to the drain group in terms of overall morbidity, with very low postoperative mortality. Drainage was associated with increased overall morbidity, while specific complications (pancreatic fistula, hemorrhage, gastroparesis) remained comparable. Chyle leaks were more frequent in the drain group, likely due to detection bias related to the presence of the drain. The study has several limitations. The high rate of splenectomy, reflecting institutional practice and patient profile (high proportion of cancers), may limit the generalizability of results, as several studies have suggested that spleen preservation reduces the risk of pancreatic fistula after distal pancreatectomy (6,7). Additionally, no subgroup analysis based on POPF risk [e.g., using the Distal Fistula Risk Score, D-FRS (8)] was performed, limiting risk stratification and comparison with other studies. According to recent literature, systematic omission of drainage after distal pancreatectomy could become a standard of care, although translation of these recommendations into clinical practice may require a prolonged implementation period (9,10).

Nevertheless, previous experience with drain omission in pancreaticoduodenectomy warrants caution before generalization (11-13). The first randomized study from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center suggested feasibility of a drain-free approach after pancreatic resection; although the final analysis of the study did not distinguish between distal pancreatectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy, the majority of patients (139/179) underwent a pancreaticoduodenectomy (11). Omission of drains did not increase postoperative morbidity or mortality: the overall complication rate was 63% with drains versus 57% without drains, and the incidence of clinically relevant pancreatic fistula was low and comparable between groups. Importantly, patients without drains had fewer drain-related complications and a trend toward faster recovery. However, years later, updated results showed that nearly half of the surgeons at the same center continued to use drainage (14,15). Subsequent randomized trials have reported conflicting results, with some even suggesting increased mortality in the absence of drainage.

Although postoperative complications after distal pancreatectomy are generally less severe than after pancreaticoduodenectomy, identifying high-risk patient subgroups (high D-FRS score, clinical frailty, severe comorbidities) for whom drainage may remain beneficial is likely a key issue for the future. Therefore, despite the current trend favoring drain omission, a personalized strategy remains the more prudent approach, with drainage retained for selected high-risk situations.

Conclusions

The PANDRA II trial demonstrates that omission of abdominal drainage after distal pancreatectomy is non-inferior to conventional practice according to the CCI and significantly reduces overall morbidity. These results support reevaluation of standard practice and advocate for a more restrictive and individualized approach. The study makes a significant contribution to the evolution of pancreatic surgery practices.
