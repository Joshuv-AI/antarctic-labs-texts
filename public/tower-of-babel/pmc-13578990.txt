Return Migration and Contraceptive Use in Mexico
Authors: Ana P. Canedo, Manon Tremouille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReturn migration, whether voluntary or forced, can disrupt individuals’ lives as they navigate life in a country that may feel unfamiliar. This disruption often extends to healthcare, including reproductive care. The migration experience, combined with differences between healthcare systems—in terms of availability, accessibility, and affordability—may create barriers to contraceptive access. This study utilizes data from the 2014, 2018, and 2023 National Survey of Demographic Dynamics (ENADID) surveys to analyze contraceptive use and access among female Mexican returnees. Findings indicate that returnees are more likely to use contraception than nonmigrants, particularly short‐acting hormonal methods (SACs), while being less likely to rely on permanent methods. These patterns suggest that both migrant selectivity and adaptation shape contraceptive behavior. However, important differences emerge by time since return. Women who recently returned are less likely to use SACs and long‐acting contraceptives and are more likely to obtain contraception from private clinics and pharmacies, compared to those with longer residence in Mexico. At the same time, there is little evidence that these patterns reflect shifts in fertility preferences. Instead, findings point more strongly to disruption and barriers to public healthcare following return. Overall, results highlight the need for reproductive healthcare interventions that better support return migrants during reintegration.

INTRODUCTION

International migration is a disruptive life event that often requires individuals to navigate unfamiliar settings under challenging circumstances. For those forcibly displaced or with limited time to prepare, this experience can be especially difficult. Return migrants, although reentering their country of origin, often face similar challenges, especially when their return is sudden and unplanned, such as in cases of deportation, or driven by family reunification or personal crises (Cassarino 2014; Dingeman‐Cerda 2018). While they return to somewhat familiar surroundings, reassimilating after years abroad can be as complex as emigration itself. Securing employment, navigating unfamiliar bureaucratic systems, and meeting basic needs such as education, housing, and healthcare, for oneself and one's family, can pose significant obstacles.

Building on recent scholarship documenting barriers to accessing reproductive healthcare among resettled refugees and vulnerable migrant populations (Tapales, Douglas‐Hall, and Whitehead 2018; Chalmiers et al. 2022; Rivillas‐Garcia et al. 2021; Flippen and Schut 2022), this study examines patterns of contraceptive use and access among return migrant women of childbearing age, an understudied area in demographic research. The focus is on Mexico, which saw a significant wave of return migration between 2005 and 2018, with over 3 million migrants returning from the United States (Gonzalez‐Barrera 2015). While women represent only about 10 percent of all returnees to Mexico, their share is increasing and, unlike men, they are more likely to return not by their own preference but for family reunification purposes or to accompany deported family members (Issacs and Preston 2023).

Several studies have documented the wide array of challenges that Mexican return migrants face in their reintegration process (Wassink 2018; Silver 2018; Jacobo Suárez and Cárdenas Alaminos 2018; Gomberg‐Muñoz 2021; Canedo 2025a; among many others). Return migration, particularly when unplanned or driven by external factors, can be costly and difficult. Beyond economic hardship and the need to find employment, return migrants may struggle to navigate complex and unfamiliar health systems, in part because obtaining the official documentation required for enrollment often involves lengthy administrative processes. This can limit their access to services and healthcare, including family planning and reproductive healthcare (Wassink 2018; Dominguez‐Villegas and Bustamante 2021). In addition, contraceptive availability and affordability can vary greatly across countries.

Several factors suggest potential differences in contraceptive access and use between return migrants and nonmigrants residing in Mexico. On the one hand, adaptation‐based theories of immigrant assimilation suggest that return migrant women may have adopted new fertility preferences and social norms, particularly if they spent several years in the host country and had access to more comprehensive and higher quality healthcare (Parrado and Morgan 2008; Alba and Nee 2009). Studies indicate that migrants’ contraceptive behaviors often align more closely with those of women in their destination country rather than in their origin country (Georgiadis 2008; Behrman, Eilers, et al. 2022; Behrman, Buyukakbas, et al. 2022). Furthermore, preferences for certain methods could be the result of selective migration in the first place. Binational research on Mexican migrants indicates that Mexican migrants are positively selected on education within households and rural municipalities relative to those who remain in Mexico (Chiquiar and Hanson 2005; Kaestner and Malamud 2014; Hamilton and Huang 2020), which could explain differential preferences for certain contraceptives.

On the other hand, return migration may entail a process of disruption and reintegration, during which returnees often face significant knowledge gaps and encounter resource, employment, and institutional barriers, which can limit their access to reproductive care (Silver 2018; Jacobo Suárez and Cárdenas Alaminos 2018; Gomberg‐Muñoz 2021). Similar patterns have been observed among displaced women and refugees in host countries, who often resort to less‐preferred, short‐acting, or traditional contraceptive methods due to restricted access to healthcare providers (Chalmiers et al. 2022; Davidson et al. 2022).

Thus, whether returnees use more modern or longer‐term contraceptive methods depends not only on their preferences and reproductive intentions but also on whether they encounter barriers to accessing these methods. While individuals may be mobile, welfare and healthcare systems are typically not, and the complexity of the process of readjustment often mirrors the differences in institutional and social conditions across the U.S.–Mexico border. This study contributes to the migration and return migration literatures by analyzing how changes in contraceptive preferences and barriers to access interact at different stages of resettlement (e.g., six months, up to a year, and over a year since return). We also investigate how key migration characteristics, such as duration of stay abroad, place of destination, and age at migration, affect contraceptive use among Mexican women returnees of childbearing age.

BACKGROUND

Mexican Return Migration and the Challenge of Reintegrating

Mexican–U.S. migration patterns have shifted dramatically over the last two decades. While migration to the United States has generally been cyclical since the early 1940s, the early 2000s saw a substantial number of Mexican immigrants leaving the United States and returning to Mexico. This shift was largely driven by the rise in unemployment associated with the recession and increased interior enforcement measures (Amuedo‐Dorantes, Puttitanun, and Martinez‐Donate 2013; Gonzalez‐Barrera 2015). Between 2010 and 2019, the number of Mexican immigrants residing in the United States decreased by nearly 780,000. At the same time, data from the Mexican Census and the National Survey of Demographic Dynamics (ENADID) show that approximately 3 million individuals returned from the United States to Mexico between 2005 and 2018 (Gonzalez‐Barrera 2015; Alba 2013; Hamilton, Orraca‐Romano, and Valle 2023). Although return migration rates have since stabilized with the recovery from the Great Recession, many Mexicans continue to be forcibly detained and repatriated. Estimates from U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement indicate that between 2017 and 2018 alone, the United States deported roughly 180,000 Mexicans (ICE 2019). Collectively, these figures suggest not only a significant increase in return migration to Mexico but also that those returning during this period tended to do so more permanently, aiming to reintegrate into Mexican society (Alba 2013).

Recent studies highlight the numerous challenges that Mexican migrants face upon returning, particularly in terms of reintegrating successfully, which, according to the International Organization for Migration, includes not only economic self‐sufficiency but also social stability within their community, and psychosocial well‐being. Research has documented that returnees, particularly those who are forcibly returned or spent much of their formative years abroad, encounter several difficulties and structural barriers upon return. These include discrimination and stigma (Silver 2018; Gomberg‐Muñoz 2021; Canedo 2025b), difficulty in finding formal and stable employment (Hagan and Wassink 2016), bureaucratic impediments in obtaining essential identity documents (Gomberg‐Muñoz 2021; Canedo 2025a), securing adequate housing (Gomberg‐Muñoz 2021), enrolling in school and higher education (Medina and Menjivar 2015; Gándara 2020; Zúñiga and Hamann 2021), and accessing public healthcare (Aguila and Zissimopoulos 2013; Wassink 2018; Canedo and Angel 2019; Dominguez‐Villegas and Bustamante 2021), among many other challenges reflecting disruption and subsequent reintegration.

The Mexican Public Healthcare System

Access to a comprehensive range of sexual and reproductive health services is essential for individual well‐being. In Mexico, contraceptive awareness and access have steadily increased over the past two decades. However, despite nationwide policies that allow certain forms of birth control to be available over the counter for women, regardless of their health insurance status, significant barriers to accessing reproductive care persist (Juarez, Gayet, and Mejia‐Pailles 2018). These challenges are particularly evident when it comes to long‐acting contraceptives that require professional insertion, limiting adequate family planning options for specific populations. Previous research has documented that the unmet need for contraception remains a significant health concern for women in Mexico, closely linked to restricted healthcare access and discriminatory practices by health providers, especially toward young unmarried women (Juarez, Gayet, and Mejia‐Pailles 2018). This gap in access contributes to Mexico's status as having the highest adolescent pregnancy rate among OECD countries (OECD 2017).

Access to adequate contraceptives through the Mexican healthcare system can be particularly challenging for newcomers and returnees. The public health system comprises two different types of public agencies. First are the public social security institutions that cater to salaried employees in the formal sector, including the Mexican Social Insurance Institute (IMSS), which accounts for half of all public health coverage,1 the Services for State Employees (ISSSTE), the social security for the oil company Petróleos Mexicanos (PEMEX), and the Mexican Social Insurance Institute for Armed Forces (ISSFAM). Access to these services is funded through mandatory salary contributions, covering private and public sector employees, respectively. Second, there is the IMSS‐Bienestar (formerly INSABI, and previously to 2020 known as Seguro Popular), which serves as the only publicly funded option for those lacking access to employment benefits and protections associated with formal employment.

Accessing healthcare through the primary social insurance programs, including contraceptives and reproductive services, can be extremely challenging for newly arrived returnees, particularly those unable to secure salaried employment. Studies on Mexican return migrants indicate consistently high rates of informal sector employment among this population (Sheehan and Riosmena 2013). Meanwhile, informal employment, which accounts for about 54 percent of all employment in Mexico, is a common precursor to initial U.S. migration (Villarreal and Blanchard 2013). Thus, it is rather unlikely for returnees to readily secure public health insurance through formal sector channels, particularly in the immediate months following their return.

For returnees who are excluded from salaried or formal employment and the unemployed, the IMSS‐Bienestar program acts as a safety net to access health services. The IMSS‐Bienestar program is designed to be universal, fully state subsidized, and free of co‐pays or other charges for users. However, the program's rollout has faced several hurdles. As of April 2024, IMSS‐Bienestar was operational in only 23 of Mexico's 32 states and had been affected by severe budget cuts and abrupt changes to government procurement practices for medical supplies (Unger‐Saldaña, Lajous, and Reich 2023; CONEVAL 2024). Both newcomers and return migrants have reported difficulties accessing IMSS‐Bienestar, as was previously the case with INSABI and Seguro Popular. Beyond physician shortages and limited infrastructure, enrollees must provide a voter ID or the Instituto Nacional Electoral card, the Clave Única de Registro de Población (CURP), and a birth certificate to access care. Obtaining these documents can be challenging for many returnees and may take up to several months, especially those who spent extended periods abroad or migrated as children (Jacobo Suárez and Cárdenas Alaminos, 2019; Canedo 2025a).

Finally, for individuals unable to access public care in Mexico, private care is an option—provided they can afford it. In Mexico, private health services are available on a fee‐for‐service basis, with private health insurance covering only about 8 percent of the total population. These tend to be predominantly high‐income individuals who also have access to IMSS or ISSSTE through their employers. The private healthcare sector is highly fragmented and can be extremely costly, making it financially inaccessible for much of the population.

Consequently, contraceptive access in Mexico is expected to vary with time since return. Recently returned migrants must navigate a complex public health system in which access is frequently tied to formal labor market attachment and involves bureaucratic hurdles that can delay enrollment. As a result, returnees may rely disproportionately on private healthcare services, incurring substantial out‐of‐pocket costs. Using ENADID 2009 and 2014 data, Wassink (2016) documents significant disparities in insurance coverage between returnees and nonmigrants, with uninsurance rates of 54 percent among returnees compared to 35 percent among nonmigrants. He later shows that return migrants often experience prolonged delays in enrolling in public schemes linked to salaried employment, instead relying on private clinics, pharmacies, or self‐medication. These dynamics heighten both health and financial vulnerability (Wassink 2018). Similarly, Martinez‐Donate et al. (2014) demonstrate that reintegration into healthcare systems can delay or constrain contraceptive access, while cross‐border differences in prescription requirements and method availability further complicate continuity of care (Flippen and Schut 2022). Therefore, access to preferred contraceptive methods should improve over time as returnees secure formal employment, enroll in formal public coverage, and learn to navigate institutional requirements.

At the same time, not all differences in contraceptive use can be attributed to barriers to access. Migration selectivity and assimilation processes may shape contraceptive preferences. Exposure to contraceptive norms and practices abroad may influence method familiarity and continuation upon return (Georgiadis 2008; Behrman, Eilers, et al. 2022; Behrman, Buyukakbas, et al. 2022). Moreover, reproductive preferences may shift as part of the broader reintegration process, as individuals reassess partnership stability, legal certainty, family support networks, and economic prospects.

Guided by these theoretical considerations, our analysis is organized around the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 1Mexican return migrants will exhibit contraceptive use patterns distinct from nonmigrants. Hypothesis 2aRecent return migrants will exhibit contraceptive use patterns that differ from those of those who have been residing in Mexico for a longer period. Hypothesis 2bReturn migrants with a longer duration of residence since return will be more likely to access contraceptives through the public health system (both employment‐based public insurance and subsidized care), as they gradually reintegrate into the Mexican labor market and society.

Mexican return migrants will exhibit contraceptive use patterns distinct from nonmigrants.

Recent return migrants will exhibit contraceptive use patterns that differ from those of those who have been residing in Mexico for a longer period.

DATA

This study utilizes data from the 2014, 2018, and 2023 iterations of Mexico's ENADID. We focus on these waves of the ENADID as they coincide with a period of elevated return migration flows to Mexico. The ENADID employs a multi‐stage probability sample to generate nationally representative estimates of various population dynamics. It collects information on respondents’ place of residence one and five years prior to the survey, which facilitates the identification of return migrants who returned within the last five years. Additionally, it includes migration‐specific questions regarding respondents’ location while abroad, as well as the year and month of their initial migration and their return. In addition to the migration module, the survey contains comprehensive data on the fertility and pregnancy history of women aged 15–54, including births, abortions, stillbirths, and neonatal deaths, and contraceptive use and preferences.

Following the literature, we first restrict our analysis to Mexican women of reproductive age (18–50) residing in the country at the time of the survey. Our initial analytical sample includes 259,292 nonmigrant women and 411 women returnees. The ENADID contraceptive use module is administered to women who report knowing about and having ever used a contraceptive method. Women meeting these criteria are subsequently asked about current contraceptive use (ENADID 2014). For the purposes of our analysis, return women in this sample universe with missing information on current contraceptive method use were coded as missing (24 percent, n = 97/411). Of the female returnee analytic sample (n = 411), 206 respondents (50 percent) were contraceptive users, while those who reported not using any contraceptive method, including those who were pregnant, were coded as nonusers (n = 108, 26 percent).

An important advantage of this study is its use of the full breadth of ENADID's rich data and the approach, as taken by Wassink (2018), of categorizing women returnees by the time they have spent in Mexico. Specifically, being able to compare those who returned within the last six months, within the last year, and those who have been residing in Mexico over a year. While it is not feasible to explore detailed patterns of contraceptive use based on monthly changes due to the small size of our returnee sample, we can analyze disparities in contraceptive access and use by grouping returnees at different cutoffs, such as those with less than six months, up to a year, and more than a year since their return. This approach allows us to uncover differences not only between return migrants and nonmigrants but also within the returnee population as they progress through various stages of reintegration. We have information on the exact month and year of return for 405 out of 411 returnee women.2 Of these, about 57 percent (n = 230/405) had been residing in Mexico for over a year at the time of the survey while the remaining had only been residing in Mexico for less than a year (n = 175), the majority of whom had been in Mexico for less than six months at the time of the survey (n = 119, or 68 percent of women who had returned for less than a year). (See Online Appendix Figure A1 for a flowchart of sample selection and key analytical groups.)

Measures

Dependent Variable

Our primary outcome is contraceptive method type among current users. Respondents were asked the following: What are you (or your partner) currently using to avoid having children? Based on existing literature (Behrman, Eilers, et al. 2022; Behrman, Buyukakbas, et al. 2022), we categorize these methods as follows and construct a binary variable for each category: coital short‐acting contraceptives (coital SACs, such as condoms, diaphragms, cervical caps, and jellies), hormonal short‐acting contraceptives (hormonal SACs, including oral contraceptive pills, patch, and injectables), long‐acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs, such as implants and intrauterine devices), and permanent methods (male and female sterilization). We do not include other “traditional” methods in our analysis, such as withdrawal or rhythm, as a very small share of participants (only 2 percent, n = 8) reported using them.

In addition to the type of methods currently used, a different question asked women whether they had ever used emergency contraception. As with the other contraceptive methods, we categorized this outcome as binary and analyzed it separately. Our final outcome examines how respondents reported accessing the contraceptive method they currently use. The precise phrasing of the question stated the following: Where did you go to have (“NAME OF METHOD”) administered? or Where did you obtain (“NAME OF METHOD”) when you started using it? Responses were categorized into accessed via public health services (e.g., IMSS, ISSSTE, PEMEX, ISSFAM, INSABI (or Seguro Popular), or IMSS‐Bienestar), accessed via private health services, which require out‐of‐pocket payment (e.g., private clinics, midwife, or pharmacy), and other. This question is different from the one on healthcare coverage used in previous studies by Wassink (2018) and Dominguez‐Villegas and Bustamante (2021) to measure barriers to healthcare among returnees, as it specifically asks about how the contraceptive method that is being used was accessed.

Independent Variable

For the first part of the analysis, we classify as return migrants those Mexican women who responded living abroad either for a year or five years before the survey, and as nonmigrants those women who responded not having lived abroad in either question. For the second part of the analysis, we focus on women returnees and dichotomize the variable measuring the time since return in months into women who returned to Mexico within the last six months and those who have been residing in Mexico for longer. We later perform sensitivity analyses by modifying our definition of recent returnee to those who returned within the last year.

Control Variables

Our analysis incorporates key demographic characteristics that are likely to influence migration and contraceptive use: a continuous variable with regard to respondents reported age, a dichotomous variable showing self‐identification as indigenous, educational attainment (which was recoded as dichotomous for individuals with at least a high school degree), ENADID's computed household socioeconomic status (SES) (which was recoded as dichotomous for individuals with high SES), whether the respondent works for pay or not, and a binary indicator for those uninsured. This measure was constructed following Wassink (2018), distinguishing between those without coverage (uninsured) and those with coverage through employment‐based public insurance (IMSS, ISSSTE, PEMEX, SEDENA, or SEMAR), social program coverage (Seguro Popular), or other forms of coverage (private or other). We also control for fertility‐related factors such as partnership status (a recoded dichotomous variable for married or cohabitating individuals), parity in the form of past live births (as reported in the ENADID), and a control for low sexual activity (recoded as 1 if the respondent's most recent sexual intercourse occurred more than three months prior to the survey and zero otherwise).

When we subset our data to include only return migrants, we also include migration‐specific variables that capture exposure to and potential assimilation of social norms and reproductive preferences abroad, including age at migration and time spent abroad. To maintain parsimony and avoid overfitting, our preferred specification controls for urban–rural residence and survey‐year fixed effects. Although including region fixed effects could further account for geographic heterogeneity in access to reproductive healthcare in Mexico, we exclude them from the main models to preserve a more conservative specification. Models that additionally incorporate region fixed effects are presented in the Online Appendix and yield substantially similar point estimates and statistical significance.

METHODOLOGY

To examine differences in contraceptive use and access between nonmigrants and returnees, our initial analytical sample includes all Mexican‐born women aged 18–50 residing in Mexico at the time of the survey. Because migration is a selective process, return migrants may differ systematically from nonmigrants in observable characteristics associated with contraceptive behavior. If unaddressed, these compositional differences could bias estimates of the association between migration experience and contraceptive use. To account for selection on observables, in this part of the analysis, we employ entropy balancing (EB) following recent applications (see Behrman, Eilers, et al. 2022).

Although EB does not address selection on unobservable characteristics, like propensity score matching, it improves balance across observed covariates by reweighting the comparison group. Specifically, EB assigns weights to nonmigrants such that the distribution of selected covariates matches that of return migrants, thereby directly achieving covariate balance without requiring specification of a propensity score model (Hainmueller 2013). This approach has been shown to outperform alternative matching methods in achieving covariate balance, as it does not rely on logit‐linear extrapolation, which can be less reliable when overlap between groups is limited due to high selectivity, as is often the case in studies of migrant populations. We implement EB using Hainmueller (2013)’s “ebalance” command and balance covariates on their first moments (means), which is the standard approach in most applications. While higher‐order moments could be incorporated, doing so may increase weight variability. As a check, we assess the sensitivity of the weights to alternative covariate specifications, examine common support across groups, and replicate the analysis using both Jann's (2021) “ebalfit” command and inverse probability weighted regression adjustment (IPWRA).

Table 1 presents the means and differences in observed characteristics between female return migrants and nonmigrant women in our initial sample. As expected, these two subgroups differ significantly. On average, return migrants are younger, more educated, and a higher proportion are employed and receive remittances. Reflecting current trends in return migration, nearly all returnees relocated to urban areas, with only 11 percent residing in rural localities, compared to a much higher share (22 percent) among nonmigrants. Furthermore, despite significantly lower levels of high school completion, a higher share was classified as high SES (28 percent vs. 9 percent), most likely due to the economic benefits of migrating. Regarding fertility‐related characteristics, a smaller proportion of returnees are partnered (41 percent vs. 62 percent), and they reported lower levels of sexual activity.

As a second step, we narrow our analysis to include only female return migrants residing in Mexico at the time of the survey. Because selection on observables is no longer a major issue when focusing on the returnee analytic sample, we employ logistic regression to examine differences in contraceptive use and access among recent returnees, specifically those who have spent less than six months (and less than a year) in Mexico to those returnees who have been in Mexico longer (up to five years). The descriptive statistics, presented in Table 2, confirm this expectation as they show minor significant differences in background characteristics between recent returnees and those who have resided in Mexico longer, as confirmed by the joint F‐test. Modest differences do emerge in indigenous identity and high SES; recent returnees tend to have lower SES upon return, given the barriers to reentering the Mexican labor market mentioned earlier. We also find significant differences in private insurance coverage with those recently returned having lower private coverage than those who have been in Mexico for longer. Duration abroad varies very slightly between groups, and differences are nonsignificant. Significant differences in age at migration indicate that groups that have been in Mexico longer tend to have migrated at a younger age. Our analysis accounts for these factors.

When focusing on the returnee sample, our objective is to assess whether barriers to accessing contraceptive care after return shape the type of contraceptive method used. Accordingly, we exclude permanent contraceptive methods from this analysis. Given their irreversible nature, sterilization procedures are unlikely to be responsive to post‐return changes in contraceptive access. We also exclude ever‐use of emergency contraception, as the data do not allow us to determine whether it was used before or after returning to Mexico. Additionally, our models incorporate both background and migration‐specific characteristics, including time spent abroad and age at migration, as captured in the survey's Migrant Module; however, models that do not control for these variables yield very similar estimates.

RESULTS

Do Return Migrants Show Patterns of Contraceptive Use That Differ From Those of Nonmigrants?

In Figure 1, we show descriptively how the use of contraceptives differs between nonmigrants and returnees in our sample, as well as differences in how they reported accessing this method. Without the inclusion of controls, we can see that the permanent method was nearly twice as prevalent among nonmigrants as among returnees and a lower share (17 percent vs. 20 percent) used long‐acting reversible contraceptives or LARCs. In contrast, more than double the number reported using hormonal short‐term contraceptives (hormonal SACs), and reliance on coital SACs was also approximately doubled. With regard to how they responded they had accessed such methods, we see noticeable differences between returnees and nonmigrants. Public provision is markedly lower among returnees (47 percent vs. 78 percent), while reliance on private clinics is nearly twice as high. In addition, returnees are almost three times as likely to obtain contraceptives through midwives or pharmacies.

To further explore whether observed characteristics explain differences in contraceptive use between return migrants and nonmigrant women, we reweighted nonmigrant women to resemble return migrants based on their background characteristics using EB. As reported previously in the right panel of Table 1, this reweighting results in both groups appearing virtually identical across background characteristics (excluding the outcome variables of interest to our analysis), notably age, educational attainment, indigenous identity, employment for pay and receipt of remittances, share uninsured, as well as partnership status, parity (number of live births), sexual activity frequency, and urban/rural residence. Covariates included in the reweighting are selected based on their relevance for both migration status and contraceptive outcomes (see Behrman, Eilers, et al. 2022; White and Potter 2013). As mentioned, we further evaluate common support by examining the distribution of estimated propensity scores across groups (see Online Appendix Figure A2). The distributions show substantial overlap, indicating that the reweighting is not driven by extrapolation, and standardized differences in means are significantly reduced for most covariates. Third, we examine the distribution of EB weights for the reweighted comparison group. The distribution is tightly concentrated, with no evidence of extreme or highly influential weights (Online Appendix Figure A3).

The results of our multivariate EB analyses are shown in Table 3 as odds ratio (OR). Results indicate that return migration is associated with some noteworthy differences in contraceptive use when compared to reweighted nonmigrant women. First, return migrants are practically twice as likely to report contraceptive use than nonmigrants (OR of 1.97; statistically significant at the 0.001 level).3 Second, the types of methods used differ between both groups. Specifically, return migrants report nearly double the likelihood of using hormonal SACs such as contraceptive pills, patches, and injectables (OR of 1.85; statistically significant at the 0.01 level). They also exhibit lower odds of using permanent contraception (OR of 0.61; p < 0.01). Taken together, these patterns are consistent with prior evidence showing both higher overall contraceptive use and greater reliance on hormonal SACs among women in the United States, including those of Mexican origin, compared to those in Mexico (Guttmacher Institute 2024; White and Potter 2013), suggesting that return migrants may adopt predominant contraceptive practices while abroad.

Recent research found that, in 2015, Mexico had the lowest prevalence of hormonal and coital SACs (14 percent) among 23 Latin American countries and was the only country where LARCs (17 percent) were more commonly used than SACs. Mexico's predominant method of contraception was permanent methods (32 percent) (de Leon et al. 2019). In contrast, 2018 National Center for Health Statistics data from the United States show that among contraceptive users aged 15–49, female permanent contraception was the most common method (28 percent), followed by pills (21 percent) or hormonal SACs, male condoms (coital SACs), and intrauterine devices (LARCs) (both at 13 percent) (Guttmacher Institute 2024).

However, these differences may also reflect initial migrant selectivity and shifts in fertility intentions following return, or difficulty in accessing certain methods. For example, it is possible that once resettled in their home country, returnees may favor methods that offer greater flexibility, such as hormonal SACs, particularly if returning to Mexico provides social support through family networks or reunification with partners, which may encourage plans for childbearing. The higher use of hormonal SACs among returnees may also partly reflect disruption primarily driven by limited access to public reproductive healthcare, as these methods are relatively affordable and available in Mexican pharmacies without physician intervention. At this stage, the analyses do not allow us to attempt to differentiate among these mechanisms, an issue we explore further below. We additionally find that returnees have a higher and statistically significant likelihood of having ever used emergency contraception. This pattern may reflect episodes of unplanned sexual activity or challenges in maintaining consistent access to contraception during or after migration and return.

Notably, return migrants are disproportionately more likely to access contraceptives through private clinics and pharmacies instead of Mexico's public healthcare system (OR of 1.79; p < 0.001). As detailed in Online Appendix Table A1, these results remain substantively similar across specifications, including those based on reduced and expanded covariate sets for balancing weights and those with further covariate adjustment, indicating limited sensitivity to covariate selection. Estimates are somewhat more conservative in the IPWRA estimations, with smaller coefficient magnitudes and reduced statistical significance, but remain consistent in direction. Findings are broadly similar when using Jann's (2021) “ebalfit” command in Stata4 (Harvey et al. 2017). A particularity of Jann's (2021) “ebalfit” command versus Hainmueller (2013)’s approach is that it takes into consideration both the survey weights provided by ENADID and the balance weights from the EB procedure (Jann 2021).

Overall, these results support Hypothesis 1, as the analysis revealed clear differences in contraceptive use patterns between returnees and nonmigrants in the sample, as well as a higher likelihood of accessing contraceptives privately.

Do Recent Returnees Experience Barriers to Contraceptive Care?

We now turn to the returnee population in our sample to examine differences in contraceptive use based on the timing since return. As shown earlier in Table 2, there were no substantial differences in background characteristics among returnees analyzed by the duration of their stay in Mexico, as confirmed by the joint F‐test. Modest differences do emerge in indigenous identity and high SES; recent returnees tend to have lower SES upon return, given the barriers to reentering the Mexican labor market mentioned earlier. We also find significant differences in private insurance coverage, with those recently returned having lower private coverage than those who have been in Mexico for longer. Duration abroad varies very slightly between groups, and differences are nonsignificant. However, differences in age at migration indicate that groups that have been in Mexico longer tend to have migrated at a younger age. Our analysis accounts for these factors.

Table 4 presents the OR from our multivariate analysis of contraceptive method used by length of residence in Mexico. The results indicate that individuals who had recently returned, particularly those who had been in Mexico for less than a year at the time of the survey, were less likely to employ long‐acting contraceptive methods (LARCs), marginally significant when including all controls, and less likely to use hormonal SACs (OR of 0.04; statistically significant at the 0.01 level). No significant differences were observed in the use of coital short‐acting methods or in the prevalence of contraceptive use.

Furthermore, when in the third column of each modeled outcome, we replace contraceptive‐specific private mode of access with a binary variable measuring whether an individual has public healthcare coverage or not, the substantive conclusions remain the same albeit in reverse. Public coverage is positively and significantly associated with higher LARC use, and the coefficient for recent return migration becomes only marginally significant in the LARC model. In contrast, public coverage is not significantly associated with hormonal short‐acting method use, and the coefficient for recent return remains statistically significant, although slightly reduced in magnitude. Importantly, the coefficient for public healthcare coverage is also significant in the model for contraceptive prevalence. Overall, this alternative specification addresses the concern that most coital SAC methods, such as condoms are inherently obtained through private channels so that in reality we are capturing availability rather than institutional barriers and reinforces the interpretation that publicly funded access plays an important role in LARC use but does not fully account for differences in hormonal method use (see Online Appendix Table A3 for sensitivity analysis using a six‐month cutoff).

Taken together, these results suggest that disparities in LARC use among recent returnees are largely explained by differential reliance on private provision, indicating that institutional access plays an important role in shaping the use of provider‐dependent methods that require medical insertion and are considerably more costly outside the public system. In contrast, differences in hormonal method use are not fully attributable to the mode of access. Unlike LARCs, hormonal short‐acting methods such as pills and patches are available in Mexico at relatively low cost and without a prescription, reducing formal institutional barriers to access. This suggests that the lower likelihood of hormonal use among very recent returnees may reflect disruption and reintegration factors beyond simple availability. Furthermore, age at migration and duration abroad are significantly associated with a lower use of coital SACs, a pattern broadly consistent with assimilation‐based explanations, as coital SAC use is considerably less common in the United States. However, we do not observe the same pattern for hormonal methods. In the case of hormonal SACs, only older age at migration is associated with higher use.

A possible explanation for the pattern observed with regard to hormonal SACs use is that, once resettled in their origin country, returnees may prefer methods that offer greater flexibility. To assess whether these patterns reflect changes in reproductive intentions, we conduct ancillary analyses (see Online Appendix Table A4), modeling childbearing‐related nonuse (defined as reporting in the ENADID nonuse due to pregnancy, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding; to capture those who recently had a child), and we again find no statistically significant association with timing of return. These results suggest that shifts in hormonal contraceptive method use are unlikely to be driven by immediate changes in fertility intentions following return.

The ENADID also provides information on the month and year respondents began using their current method, allowing us to examine whether post‐return method adoption is associated with the use of a particular method. However, missing timing information and the small number of women reporting adoption of a new method after return limit statistical power, and we do not observe statistically significant associations.5 Nonetheless, descriptive patterns indicate that adoption of a current method after return becomes more common with longer time since return and is least prevalent among those who are uninsured. Moreover, the observed transitions between previously used and current contraceptive methods do not suggest a pronounced shift toward more flexible methods that would signal imminent fertility intentions. Instead, many transitions occur toward less flexible and more provider‐dependent methods. For example, women who previously relied on coital methods frequently report switching to hormonal, LARCs, or permanent methods, while transitions toward coital methods are comparatively rare (see Online Appendix Figure A4 for contraceptive method transitions). Among the smaller subset of women who reported changing methods after returning to Mexico (n = 51); however, the pattern is somewhat less pronounced: although transitions still tend to move away from coital methods, they concentrate more heavily in hormonal methods and less in long‐acting or permanent options. Taken together, these descriptive patterns are more in line with the interpretation that contraceptive behavior among returnees reflects gradual adjustment to contraceptive access rather than an immediate shift in fertility intentions.

Thus, another possible explanation for the lower utilization of hormonal SACs upon return is that, despite acquired preferences for such methods, contraceptive behavior immediately following return is shaped by the broader process of reintegration. Individuals may be preoccupied with employment, documentation, housing, or family reunification, and contraceptive management may not be an immediate priority. Over time, as returnees stabilize, navigate the local healthcare environment, re‐partner, or experience increased sexual frequency, hormonal method use may rise accordingly.

Overall, the findings are broadly consistent with Hypothesis 2a: recent return migrants exhibit contraceptive use patterns that differ from those of returnees who have resided longer in Mexico, particularly in their lower use of long‐acting reversible methods and hormonal contraceptives, and lend support to Hypothesis 2b: longer duration since return is associated with greater reliance on public healthcare for contraceptive access, a pattern that aligns with prior evidence on gradual reintegration into Mexico's health system in general (Wassink 2018). The attenuation of the association between recent return and LARC use after accounting for private versus public modes of access also suggests that institutional access may play a role in shaping method choice in the period immediately following return. Nonetheless, it is not possible to completely rule out changes in fertility intentions or other potential explanations, as the data do not allow us to disentangle the multiple processes that may shape contraceptive behavior after return, particularly the lower prevalence of hormonal contraceptive use among recent returnees, which may arise from a combination of institutional, relational, and experiential factors.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, our methods cannot account for selection on unobservable characteristics. While the analysis identifies differences in contraceptive use patterns between returnees and nonmigrants, these findings should not be interpreted as causal. Second, although EB substantially improves covariate balance, some residual differences remain, and overlap across groups is not perfect for all characteristics. While sensitivity analyses indicate that results are not driven by covariate selection or extreme weights, the approach relies on balancing first moments and cannot fully eliminate differences in higher‐order distributions. Third, the ENADID survey only captures place of residence within the last year and the last five years, limiting our ability to distinguish long‐term returnees from nonmigrants. However, it is reasonable to expect that most individuals residing in Mexico for over five years have largely reintegrated and assimilated. Fourth, the ENADID does not differentiate between return and seasonal migrants. Since the survey is conducted in June and July, it likely captures few seasonal migrants, suggesting that most returnees in the sample are individuals intending to remain in Mexico rather than return to the United States. Lastly, while the ENADID asks about current, former, and first contraceptive method, we do not have longitudinal information on method use as to better disentangle how access, continuity, and adaptation interact before, after return, and over time.

CONCLUSION

Our findings indicate systematic differences in contraceptive use between return migrants and nonmigrant women in Mexico. Overall, returnees have a higher contraceptive prevalence and are more likely than nonmigrants to use hormonal SACs and less likely to use permanent methods. They are also substantially more reliant on private clinics and pharmacies to obtain their current method. These associations persist after adjusting for socioeconomic, demographic, and geographic characteristics, indicating that return migration status is independently associated with contraceptive behavior. The higher prevalence of hormonal method use among returnees may also reflect familiarity with contraceptive practices in the United States, where hormonal methods are comparatively more common, suggesting that prior exposure abroad may shape contraceptive preferences upon return.

At the same time, important heterogeneity emerges when distinguishing recent returnees from those who have resided in Mexico longer. Although returnees overall exhibit higher hormonal use than nonmigrants, the association between recent return and hormonal use is negative: the more recent the return, the lower the likelihood of hormonal use relative to longer‐term returnees. In other words, hormonal use appears to increase with time since return. Notably, this association persists even after controlling for obtaining contraception through private clinics and pharmacies. By contrast, the association between recent return and LARC use attenuates and becomes statistically insignificant once controls for modes of access and coverage are introduced into the models.

These patterns help hint at some of the mechanisms behind the observed differences. For LARCs, it seems that lower use among recent returnees operates largely through institutional barriers. Given that LARCs require trained insertion and are considerably more costly in private settings, disruptions in attachment to public healthcare systems appear central in shaping LARC use among recent returnees. Upon return, migrants are more likely to rely on private providers, and private access is strongly associated with lower LARC use and greater reliance on short‐acting and coital methods. In contrast, the persistence of the association between time since return and hormonal SAC use, even after controlling for private access, suggests that hormonal method choice is not fully explained by institutional access alone and that other disruption‐related mechanisms may be at play. Additional analyses indicate that the observed patterns do not reflect an immediate shift toward childbearing. Rather, the increase in hormonal use with time spent back in Mexico may reflect gradual reintegration into the healthcare system, method continuation, or adaptation processes shaped by prior exposure abroad. The associations between age at migration, duration abroad, and method choice further suggest that migration experiences may influence familiarity with specific contraceptive methods, even if access constraints shape which methods are feasible upon return.

Taken together, these findings indicate that contraceptive behavior among return migrants reflects the interaction between migration‐shaped experiences and the institutional constraints encountered after return, while also evolving through the dynamic process of reintegration into Mexico's healthcare system. These results carry important policy implications. As Mexico continues to receive return migrants, particularly in the context of potential increases in deportations, facilitating rapid enrollment in public healthcare programs and reducing bureaucratic barriers to coverage will be essential to ensure timely access to reproductive healthcare among women returnees. More broadly, this study underscores that return migration, much like initial migration, carries implications beyond economic reintegration. Interruptions in healthcare coverage and access can reshape reproductive healthcare trajectories and affect broader dimensions of well‐being.
