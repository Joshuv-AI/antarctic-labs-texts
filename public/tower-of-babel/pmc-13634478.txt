Structural Control of Stromal‐Immune Coupling by COL24A1 Underlies Pregnancy Maintenance
Authors: Teng Wu, Lingling Xie, Wenyi Liu, Hanjie Mo, Chengshu Xie, Jing Tang, Zhaorui Wang, Peixuan Lan, Jianping Zhang, Jie Gao, Qionglan Tang, Chunwei Cao, Hui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSuccessful pregnancy requires dynamic extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling at the maternal‐fetal interface, yet whether individual collagen subtypes actively regulate this process remains unknown. Here, collagen type XXIV alpha 1 chain (COL24A1), a uterine‐enriched fibrillar collagen, is identified as a regulator of pregnancy‐associated ECM‐immune homeostasis. Col24a1‐deficient mice exhibit increased embryo resorption, fetal growth impairment, and pregnancy‐dependent uterine collagen accumulation and inflammation. Mechanistically, Col24a1 loss promotes expansion of a maladaptive Timp1‐high stromal state, accompanied by reduced matrix metalloproteinase 2 activity, impaired ECM degradation, and altered stromal‐immune communication and inflammatory immune‐cell states. COL24A1 silencing in human endometrial stromal cells similarly impairs decidualization and increases TIMP1 and fibrillar collagen gene expression. Hydroxychloroquine partially rescues embryo resorption and attenuates uterine inflammation, indicating that these pregnancy‐associated abnormalities are pharmacologically modifiable. Moreover, a patient with 12 consecutive unexplained early pregnancy losses carrying rare heterozygous COL24A1 variants shows a concordant decidual phenotype with reduced COL24A1 and increased TIMP1, inflammation, and collagen deposition. These findings identify COL24A1 as a collagen subtype‐specific regulator of maternal‐fetal ECM‐immune homeostasis and nominate it as a candidate susceptibility gene for unexplained recurrent pregnancy loss.

Introduction

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), defined as two or more failed clinical pregnancies before 20–24 weeks of gestation, affects ∼1%‐5% of couples attempting to conceive and carries substantial psychological and physical burdens [1, 2]. Despite standardized endocrine, cytogenetic, and uterine evaluations, the etiology remains unidentified in approximately half of cases, leaving unexplained RPL without mechanism‐based diagnostics or targeted therapy [2, 3].

Early pregnancy success requires a receptive endometrium and tightly coordinated cellular programs at the maternal‐fetal interface [4, 5, 6]. Beyond a structural scaffold, the decidual extracellular matrix (ECM) provides biochemical and biomechanical signals that shape stromal differentiation, regulate trophoblast invasion, and influence immune‐cell positioning and tissue adaptation [4, 5, 7, 8]. Extensive ECM remodeling, including dynamic changes in collagen composition and organization, is a well‐established feature of decidualization in rodents and human endometrium [9, 10, 11]. As dominant ECM constituents, collagens organize matrix architecture and turnover; however, mechanistic work has largely centered on major fibrillar collagens (e.g., types I and III) [7, 12, 13], while many fibril‐associated or non‐fibrillar collagen isoforms remain poorly defined in decidual biology, representing a key gap for unexplained RPL.

The COL24A1 gene, located on chromosome 1, encodes the collagen type XXIV α1 chain, a large fibril‐associated collagen comprising 1,714 amino acids [14] and primarily characterized in developmental contexts [15]. Emerging human genetic data revealing that the maternal SNP rs11161721 synergizes with pre‐pregnancy obesity to elevate preterm birth risk [16] now thrust it onto the pregnancy stage, while its robust expression in uterine fibroids hints at active participation in ECM remodeling [17]. Yet whether COL24A1 is required for pregnancy maintenance and how it contributes to maternal‐fetal interface homeostasis remain unknown.

Here, by integrating a Col24a1‐null mouse model with bulk and single‐cell transcriptomics, pharmacological rescue, and human evidence from a patient with idiopathic RPL, we identify COL24A1 deficiency as a driver of maternal‐fetal interface dysfunction. Loss of COL24A1 reshapes uterine stromal cell states towards a maladaptive phenotype characterized by impaired ECM remodeling and altered stromal‐immune communication. These findings nominate COL24A1 as a candidate susceptibility gene for unexplained RPL and implicate stromal‐immune dysregulation as a potentially tractable mechanism underlying pregnancy failure.

Human study protocols were approved by the ethics committee of Sun Yat‐sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University (2017‐BA‐04), and all participants provided written informed consent. Animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Sun Yat‐sen University (SYSU‐IACUC‐2022‐001356 and SYSU‐IACUC‐2022‐001220), and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines on the Care and Use of Animals.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (v8.4.3; GraphPad Software). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. For normally distributed data, two‐group comparisons used a two‐tailed unpaired Student's t test, with Welch's correction when variances were unequal. Multiple‐group comparisons used one‐way or two‐way ANOVA, followed by the appropriate post hoc test (eg, Dunnett's or Tukey's). For non‐normally distributed data, the Mann‐Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal‐Wallis test (>2 groups) with Dunn's post hoc test was used. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In figures, significance is denoted as *p <0.05, **p <0.01, and ***p <0.001.

Results

Col24a1 Is Highly Expressed in the Uterus

COL24A1 encodes collagen XXIV, a fibrillar collagen component of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Analysis of the Expression Atlas database revealed relatively high uterine COL24A1/Col24a1 expression in the uterus of humans, rats, and mice compared to other tissues (Figure 1A). qPCR analysis of tissue expression profiles further confirmed high Col24a1 expression in the uterus of both mice and rats (Figure 1B,C). Additionally, the collagenous “Gly‐X‐Y” repeat region of COL24A1 was highly conserved across humans, rats, and mice (Figure 1D). Together, these data indicate that Col24a1 exhibits relatively high uterine expression and suggest a potential role in female reproductive function.

Col24a1 Knockout Induces Embryo Loss in Mice

Given that Col24a1 showed relatively high expression in the mouse uterus, we investigated the effects of Col24a1 loss of function on reproductive outcomes. To this end, we generated a Col24a1 knockout (Col24a1‐KO) mouse model using CRISPR/Cas9 (Figure 1E–G and Figure S1A,B). Col24a1‐KO mice exhibited no obvious gross abnormalities and showed no significant differences in body weight compared with wild‐type (WT) mice (Figure 1H). Female WT and Col24a1‐KO mice were subsequently mated with healthy WT males and euthanized at gestational day 13.5 (GD13.5) (Figure 2A). Col24a1‐KO mice exhibited a significantly higher embryo resorption rate than WT controls (Figure 2B,C). Notably, age‐stratified analysis showed that the increased embryo resorption associated with Col24a1 deficiency was already evident in younger females and was further exacerbated in older females (Figure S2A,B). In addition, Col24a1‐KO mice showed a reduced number of embryos per dam and lower fetal and placental weights (Figure 2D–F). Further analysis revealed no significant differences in serum reproductive hormone levels between WT and Col24a1‐KO mice in the non‐pregnancy state or during pregnancy (Figure 2G–I). Likewise, no significant alterations in estrous cyclicity were observed between the two groups (Figure 2J). These findings indicate that the pregnancy phenotype associated with Col24a1 deficiency was not accompanied by major alterations in the measured serum reproductive hormone levels or estrous cyclicity. Taken together, these results demonstrate that Col24a1 loss compromises pregnancy maintenance and promotes embryo loss.

Col24a1 Knockout Promotes Uterine Collagen Deposition and Inflammation During Pregnancy

To investigate the pathological changes associated with Col24a1 knockout‐induced pregnancy loss in mice, we further examined collagen deposition and inflammation in uterine tissue. Histopathological analysis revealed significantly increased uterine collagen deposition in Col24a1 knockout mice compared with WT mice at GD 13.5 (Figure 3A). Hydroxyproline assays further confirmed the increase in total uterine collagen content (Figure 3B). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that uterine collagen fibrils in Col24a1 knockout mice were more densely packed and less regularly organized than those in WT mice at GD13.5 (Figure 3C). At the molecular level, key collagen and inflammatory markers were examined in uterine tissue. Immunoblotting showed that Col24a1 knockout significantly increased the protein levels of COL1a1, COL3a1, IL‐6, and IL‐1β at GD 13.5 compared with WT controls (Figure 3D). Immunohistochemistry further showed increased COL1a1 and IL‐1β staining in the uteri of Col24a1 knockout mice at GD 13.5 (Figure 3E,F).

To determine whether these pathological changes were also present in the nonpregnant state, we further examined uterine tissues from nonpregnant WT and Col24a1 knockout mice. Masson's trichrome staining revealed no significant difference in uterine collagen deposition between the two groups (Figure 3G). Similarly, immunoblotting showed no significant differences in the protein levels of COL1a1, COL3a1, IL‐6, or IL‐1β between WT and Col24a1 knockout uteri in the nonpregnant state (Figure S2C). Immunohistochemical analysis further showed no significant difference in uterine COL1a1 staining between the two groups (Figure 3H). These data indicate that Col24a1 deficiency does not produce an overt fibrotic or inflammatory uterine phenotype under nonpregnant conditions.

Collectively, these findings indicate that Col24a1 deficiency is associated with pregnancy‐dependent increases in uterine collagen deposition and inflammation, which may contribute to the adverse pregnancy phenotype observed in Col24a1 knockout mice.

Col24a1 Knockout Upregulates TIMP1 and Suppresses Uterine Extracellular Matrix Degradation During Pregnancy

Given that Col24a1 knockout induces pregnancy loss in mice, we sought to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms. To this end, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of uterine tissues from WT and Col24a1 knockout mice at GD13.5 (Figure S3A). PCA showed that samples from the two groups clustered separately (Figure 4A), indicating distinct global transcriptional profiles between WT and Col24a1 knockout uteri. Differential expression analysis identified 452 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Col24a1 knockout uteri compared with WT controls, including 295 upregulated and 157 downregulated genes (Figure 4B). Functional enrichment analysis showed that the downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in “Negative regulation of cell adhesion” and “Degradation of the extracellular matrix” (Figure 4C). Consistently, GSEA further showed enhancement of cell‐cell adhesion and suppression of ECM degradation (Figure S3B). Furthermore, the upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in “positive regulation of cell‐substrate adhesion” and “positive regulation of cell junction assembly” (Figure S3C).

Based on the observation that Col24a1 knockout promotes uterine collagen deposition during pregnancy (Figure 3), we hypothesized that Col24a1 deficiency may contribute to collagen accumulation by impairing ECM degradation. We therefore examined key components involved in ECM turnover, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), and ubiquitin‐proteasome‐related genes. The analysis revealed no significant differences in the expression of Mmp2, Mmp9, Mmp14, Uba52, Ube2i, or Ube2n between WT and Col24a1 knockout uteri (Figure 4D). Notably, Timp1 was significantly upregulated in Col24a1 knockout uteri compared with WT controls (Figure 4D). Immunohistochemistry (Figure 4E) and immunoblotting (Figure 4F) further confirmed increased TIMP1 protein levels in Col24a1 knockout uteri at GD 13.5. In contrast, TIMP1 protein levels did not differ significantly between WT and Col24a1 knockout uteri in the nonpregnancy state (Figure S3D).

TIMP1 inhibits the activity of multiple MMPs by interacting with their catalytic domains and has important roles in physiological processes including pregnancy, embryonic development, angiogenesis, and wound healing [18]. Immunoblotting revealed no significant differences in MMP2 and MMP9 protein levels between WT and Col24a1 knockout uteri at GD 13.5 (Figure 4G). However, gelatin zymography showed that MMP2 activity was significantly reduced in Col24a1 knockout uteri compared with WT controls, whereas MMP9 activity was unchanged (Figure 4H).

Taken together, these findings indicate that Col24a1 deficiency is associated with increased TIMP1 expression in the uterus, reduced MMP2 activity, and impaired ECM degradation during pregnancy. These alterations may contribute to excessive collagen deposition in Col24a1‐knockout uteri.

ScRNA‐seq Reveals That Col24a1 May Act as a Buffering Factor That Maintains Homeostasis at the Maternal‐Fetal Interface During Pregnancy

To characterize the early cellular changes associated with Col24a1 deficiency and pregnancy loss, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) of uterine tissues from WT and Col24a1‐KO mice at GD 5.5 (Figure S4A). Based on canonical marker genes and cluster‐specific expression patterns, we identified 19 major uterine cell types, including stromal, immune, epithelial, endothelial, smooth muscle, pericyte, and mesothelial populations (Figure 5A,B and Figure S4B,C). Col24a1 transcripts were preferentially expressed in stromal population 3 (Str_3_Tenm3), whereas Timp1, which was also identified in our bulk RNA‐seq analysis, was predominantly expressed in stromal population 4 (Str_4_Clec3b) (Figure 5C). Notably, Col24a1‐KO uteri exhibited a disproportionate expansion of str_4_Clec3b relative to WT uteri (Figure 5B), implicating this subset as a potential contributor to early pathological alterations in the uterine microenvironment.

To infer stromal‐state dynamics associated with Col24a1 deficiency, we integrated stromal cells from WT and Col24a1‐KO uteri and reconstructed pseudotime trajectories. This analysis revealed distinct state distributions between the two genotypes (Figure 5D and Figure S4D). Stromal cells from Col24a1‐ KO uteri were distributed across two representative transcriptional states, designated adaptive and maladaptive states (Figure 5D), suggesting that Col24a1 loss alters stromal‐state dynamics during early pregnancy. Genes exhibiting similar dynamic expression patterns along the pseudotime trajectories were subsequently grouped into four gene clusters (clusters 1–4) (Figure 5E). Compared with the adaptive state, the maladaptive state was characterized by increased expression of fibrillar collagen genes, including Col1a1 and Col3a1, together with adhesion‐associated and matrix‐interacting genes such as Icam1, Dag1, and Htra3 (Figure 5E). Consistently, Str_4_Clec3b cells were preferentially distributed along the maladaptive branch (Figure S4D), in agreement with the histological phenotype of increased collagen accumulation and suggesting an association of this stromal subset with altered ECM remodeling and adhesion programs. Gene set enrichment analysis further showed enrichment of collagen‐containing extracellular matrix and cell adhesion molecule binding pathways in Str_4_Clec3b cells (Figure 5F).

Given that the expansion of the maladaptive Str_4 population was associated with altered ECM and cell adhesion programs, we hypothesized that this stromal shift may disturb stromal‐immune coordination at the maternal‐fetal interface. We therefore examined predicted intercellular communication between Str_4 cells and immune populations (Figure 5G). CellChat‐based inference suggested extensive alterations in ligand‐receptor communication in Col24a1‐KO uteri, involving signaling programs related to ECM remodeling, adhesion and cellular recruitment, and immunomodulation (Figure 5G). ECM remodeling‐ and adhesion‐associated networks involving collagen, tenascin XB (Tnxb), fibronectin 1 (Fn1), midkine (Mdk), complement C3 (C3), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (Icam1) were prominent and predicted to originate predominantly from Str_4 cells. These signals were predicted to engage immune populations expressing syndecan 1 (Sdc1) and multiple integrin receptors, consistent with altered ECM‐receptor communication that may influence immune‐cell adhesion, migration, and spatial organization (Figure 5G). In parallel, immunoregulatory signaling involving C‐C motif chemokine ligands (Ccls), complement components, macrophage migration inhibitory factor (Mif), galectin 9 (Lgals9), C‐C motif chemokine receptor 5 (Ccr5), Cd74, and Cd44 formed an altered communication network, suggesting altered immune recruitment and activation states at this stage of gestation (Figure 5G). Consistently, genes associated with inflammation, ECM organization, and adhesion were expressed at higher levels in Str_4 cells from Col24a1‐KO uteri than in WT controls (Figure 5H and Figure S4E).

We further quantified the relative abundances of major immune‐cell populations, including uNK cells, T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and plasma cells. Unexpectedly, their relative proportions were either reduced or largely unchanged in Col24a1‐KO uteri compared with WT controls (Figure 5B). Given that uNK cells are the predominant immune population at the maternal‐fetal interface and play critical roles in decidualization and pregnancy maintenance, we further examined their heterogeneity and identified five transcriptionally distinct uNK states: Gzma+Cma1+ effector uNK, Gzmc+Lgals3+ inflammation‐associated uNK, Tcf7+Tcf12+ immature uNK, Klra7+Cd2+ resting uNK, and Stmn1+Birc5+ cycling uNK (Figure S5A–C). Notably, the relative proportion of Gzmc+Lgals3+ inflammation‐associated uNK cells was increased in Col24a1‐KO uteri, accompanied by increased expression of inflammatory genes and stronger inflammatory‐response signatures (Figure S5D–F).

Collectively, these findings indicate that Col24a1 contributes to maintaining stromal homeostasis during early pregnancy. Col24a1 deficiency was associated with expansion of a maladaptive stromal subset characterized by altered ECM remodeling and adhesion programs, accompanied by changes in stromal‐immune communication and immune cell states rather than a generalized increase in immune cell abundance.

COL24A1 Silencing Impairs In Vitro Decidualization and Alters ECM‐Associated Gene Expression

To determine the effect of COL24A1 downregulation on decidualization, we transfected human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs) with COL24A1‐targeting siRNAs and subsequently induced decidualization in vitro. COL24A1 expression was reduced by approximately 70% after siRNA transfection (Figure S6A). On day 6 of decidualization, COL24A1 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of decidualization markers compared with the siNC control (Figure S6B). Notably, COL24A1 mRNA levels progressively increased during decidualization in control cells (Figure S6C).

Given that Col24a1 deficiency was associated with abnormal ECM remodeling in the pregnant uterus, we next examined ECM‐related gene expression during HESC decidualization after COL24A1 knockdown. TIMP1 mRNA levels increased during decidualization and were further elevated in COL24A1‐silenced cells (Figure S6D). Likewise, COL1A1 and COL3A1 mRNA levels increased during decidualization and were further increased following COL24A1 knockdown (Figure S6E,F).

Together, these findings show that COL24A1 knockdown impairs HESC decidualization and is accompanied by increased expression of TIMP1 and fibrillar collagen genes.

Hydroxychloroquine Sulfate Reduced Embryo Resorption in Col24a1 Knockout Mice

Given that Col24a1 deficiency was associated with uterine inflammation, fibrosis, and pregnancy loss in mice, we next investigated whether pharmacological intervention could ameliorate the adverse pregnancy outcomes. Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is an antimalarial drug with well‐established immunomodulatory and anti‐inflammatory effects and is widely used to treat rheumatic autoimmune diseases, particularly systemic lupus erythematosus [19, 20]. We therefore assessed the effects of HCQ treatment on pregnancy outcomes in Col24a1‐deficient mice.

To evaluate the effects of HCQ treatment in vivo, Col24a1 knockout mice received HCQ‐containing or regular drinking water beginning at 7 weeks of age, were mated with healthy WT males at 8 weeks of age, and were euthanized at GD 13.5 (Figure 6A). HCQ treatment significantly reduced the embryo resorption rate in Col24a1 knockout mice compared with water‐treated controls and improved fetal and placental growth (Figure 6B–E). Notably, HCQ did not significantly affect pregnancy outcomes in WT mice under the same experimental conditions (Figure S7A–D). In addition, HCQ concentrations were markedly increased in the serum and uterine tissues of HCQ‐treated Col24a1 knockout mice compared with water‐treated controls, confirming systemic and uterine tissue exposure to the drug (Figure 6F).

Consistent with its known anti‐inflammatory properties, HCQ treatment significantly reduced uterine IL‐1β expression in Col24a1‐deficient mice at GD13.5 (Figure S7E). Masson's trichrome staining further showed reduced uterine collagen deposition after HCQ treatment (Figure 6G). Immunoblotting demonstrated reduced levels of collagen‐ and inflammation‐associated proteins in HCQ‐treated Col24a1‐knockout uteri (Figure 6H). Notably, HCQ treatment did not significantly alter the elevated TIMP1 protein levels (Figure 6H).

Taken together, these findings show that HCQ treatment reduced embryo resorption and was accompanied by decreased uterine inflammation and collagen accumulation in Col24a1‐knockout mice.

Human Genetic Evidence Links COL24A1 Variants to Severe Unexplained RPL

Building on our experimental findings showing that Col24a1 deficiency promotes pregnancy loss in mice, we next explored the potential relevance of COL24A1 to human RPL. We screened whole‐exome sequencing (WES) data from patients in our institutional unexplained RPL cohort (n = 193) and identified a patient with severe RPL who had experienced 12 consecutive spontaneous early pregnancy losses with the same partner. The patient had no notable gynecological disorders, relevant family history, or other significant medical history. Comprehensive clinical evaluation identified no recognized cause of RPL, including immune disorders, thrombophilia, uterine structural abnormalities, or chromosomal abnormalities in either partner, and no consanguinity was reported.

We identified two rare heterozygous COL24A1 variants (chr1:86581010 G>A and chr1:86453324 T>G) in this patient (Figure 7A) and independently confirmed both variants by Sanger sequencing (Figure 7B). Both were missense variants resulting in nonconservative amino acid substitutions, p.Arg515Trp and p.Asp775Ala. SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org) and PolyPhen‐2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2) predicted both substitutions to be deleterious (Figure 7A). These substitutions involve physicochemical changes from polar (hydrophilic) to nonpolar (hydrophobic) residues that may affect protein structure or function (Figure S8A).

We next examined COL24A1 expression in endometrial and decidual tissues from the patient and control subjects by immunohistochemistry. In controls, we observed relatively weak COL24A1 staining in the nonpregnant endometrium, including the superficial, glandular, and basal regions (Figure 7C,D, upper left half), whereas the superficial decidua showed stronger COL24A1 staining during pregnancy (Figure 7C,D, lower left half and Figure S8B). The patient showed a staining pattern comparable to controls in the nonpregnant endometrium, whereas the miscarriage decidua retained relatively weak COL24A1 staining compared with control decidua (Figure 7C,D, right panel). We also observed stronger TIMP1 and IL‐1β staining in the patient's decidual tissue than in control decidua (Figure 7E,F and Figure S8C). In addition, Masson's trichrome staining showed greater collagen deposition in the patient's decidual tissue than in control decidual tissue (Figure 7G).

Collectively, this severe unexplained RPL case carrying two rare COL24A1 variants showed reduced decidual COL24A1 expression and increased collagen deposition. These tissue features paralleled key phenotypes observed in Col24a1‐deficient mice, providing cross‐species support for further investigation of COL24A1 as a potential susceptibility factor for RPL.

Discussion

This study identifies COL24A1, a uterine‐enriched fibril‐associated collagen, as a previously unrecognized regulator of pregnancy maintenance (Figure 8). Genetic ablation of Col24a1 in mice caused a marked increase in embryo resorption at GD 13.5, accompanied by reduced fetal number and fetal weight, whereas estrous cyclicity and circulating sex hormone levels remained largely unchanged. These findings suggest that the pregnancy phenotype is unlikely to result primarily from endocrine dysfunction and instead point to abnormalities in the local uterine microenvironment. Supporting this view, histopathological and ultrastructural analyses of pregnant Col24a1‐deficient uteri revealed excessive collagen deposition together with inflammatory changes at the maternal‐fetal interface. In line with these structural abnormalities, bulk RNA‐seq and enzymatic analyses showed increased TIMP1 expression and reduced MMP2 activity, consistent with impaired extracellular matrix turnover. Single‐cell transcriptomic profiling further revealed expansion of a maladaptive Timp1‐high stromal cell subpopulation at the maternal‐fetal interface. This shift was accompanied by changes in extracellular matrix remodeling and stromal‐immune communication, suggesting that defective matrix remodeling may contribute to inflammatory dysregulation and pregnancy loss. Notably, analogous alterations were also observed in a patient with unexplained RPL, providing supportive human evidence for the potential relevance of COL24A1‐associated matrix abnormalities to RPL.

Successful pregnancy depends on tightly regulated microenvironmental homeostasis at the maternal‐fetal interface. Within this niche, ECM remodeling, trophoblast invasion, vascular transformation, and immune adaptation must be coordinated spatially and temporally. Single‐cell and spatial multi‐omics studies indicate that such coordination emerges from precisely orchestrated interactions among decidual stromal cells, endothelial cells, diverse immune populations, and trophoblast lineages [4, 21, 22]. Disruption of these interaction networks is an important feature of pregnancy complications [23, 24], and disturbances in intercellular homeostasis may precede overt histopathological changes. Our findings extend this framework by identifying COL24A1 as a collagen‐associated regulator of maternal‐fetal interface homeostasis and as an upstream contributor to defective tissue remodeling that becomes evident under the increased remodeling demands of pregnancy. In our single‐cell analyses, Col24a1 ablation increased the abundance of a maladaptive Timp1‐high stromal subpopulation and was associated with impaired ECM turnover and altered stromal‐immune signaling networks at the maternal‐fetal interface, potentially promoting a proinflammatory microenvironment. In turn, inflammatory signaling may further reinforce a TIMP1‐high state associated with reduced MMP2 activity and restricted matrix degradation [25, 26]. These changes may establish a self‐reinforcing cycle that shifts the interface toward a low‐proteolytic, fibrosis‐like remodeling state, promoting collagen persistence and progressive tissue dysfunction. Together, these findings integrate defective ECM turnover and altered stromal‐immune communication into a unified pathological axis at the maternal‐fetal interface and support the concept that ECM abnormalities may act as upstream contributors to immune dysregulation and pregnancy loss.

Pregnancy places the uterus under substantial remodeling demands. In this setting, COL24A1 appears to help maintain ECM homeostasis. Col24a1 deletion produced few detectable changes in the nonpregnant uterus, whereas gestation revealed marked collagen accumulation and inflammatory activation. This pregnancy‐dependent phenotype also makes a primary endocrine mechanism less likely, as estrous cyclicity and circulating sex hormone levels remained largely unchanged. Instead, ECM degradation appears to be impaired in Col24a1‐deficient uteri, as indicated by increased TIMP1 expression and reduced MMP2 activity. Such an imbalance may favor collagen accumulation, potentially alter tissue stiffness and mechanical properties, and perturb the mechanical and biochemical cues required for coordinated cell migration, vascular remodeling, and immune homeostasis [12, 24, 27]. Thus, as remodeling demands increase during gestation, loss of COL24A1 may amplify these pathological changes, whereas they may remain largely subclinical under the comparatively lower remodeling demands of the nonpregnant state. Together, our findings suggest that COL24A1 is particularly important for maintaining uterine ECM homeostasis during pregnancy, when remodeling demands are substantially increased.

Our findings also have potential clinical and translational relevance. Identification of rare heterozygous COL24A1 variants in a severe unexplained RPL case, together with reduced decidual COL24A1 expression and increased TIMP1 and fibrosis‐associated features, provides preliminary cross‐species concordance and supports COL24A1 as a candidate susceptibility gene for a potential mechanistic subset of unexplained RPL—an area in which current guidance emphasizes the need for genetics‐informed and mechanism‐based stratification [1, 28]. However, we did not functionally validate the patient‐specific variants; variant‐level knock‐in models and complementary rescue experiments will therefore be important to establish causality and define allele‐specific mechanisms. In parallel, HCQ partially attenuated embryo resorption and improved fibrosis and inflammation‐related measures in the Col24a1 knockout model, suggesting that the ECM‐immune coupling unmasked by COL24A1 deficiency may be amenable to pharmacological modulation. Recent studies have also suggested that HCQ may have therapeutic potential for alleviating intrauterine fetal growth restriction [29]. Nevertheless, clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of HCQ for RPL remains inconclusive [30, 31], underscoring the need for prospective studies incorporating mechanistic enrichment and biomarker‐guided stratification. Our findings provide a rationale for such an approach, and future studies could prioritize individuals exhibiting decidual ECM‐turnover defects together with a TIMP1‐high inflammatory signature.

Several limitations define important priorities for future investigation. First, the global knockout design cannot fully exclude contributions from extra‐uterine tissues or cell populations; uterine cell‐type‐specific deletion and rescue experiments will therefore be required to define the cellular origin of the phenotype. Second, the molecular mechanism linking COL24A1 ablation to TIMP1 upregulation remains unresolved. Our single‐cell transcriptomic data nevertheless provide a framework for further mechanistic investigation by localizing TIMP1‐high states to defined cell populations and identifying inflammatory and stress‐response programs associated with TIMP1 expression. These analyses can prioritize candidate regulators and ligand‐receptor circuits for subsequent causal testing. Third, and most importantly, the current human genetic and phenotypic evidence derives from a single informative case. Larger independent cohorts will therefore be required to establish frequency of COL24A1 variants, validate genotype‐phenotype associations, and determine their contribution to unexplained RPL.
