BPM Exposure Induces Pulmonary Dysfunction in Adult Mice and Adverse Intergenerational Pulmonary Effects in Offspring
Authors: Jie Zhao, Zehong Wu, Kelei Pan, Chenming Yi, Liuyan Fu, Wenjuan Zhang, Jie Zhang, Yong Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bisphenol M (BPM), a structural analogue of bisphenol A, has become increasingly prevalent in the environment, raising concerns about its potential health impacts. However, the pulmonary toxicity of BPM, particularly with regard to direct exposure in adults and potential intergenerational respiratory effects, is not well understood. This study systematically investigates the mechanisms of BPM-induced pulmonary dysfunction in adult mice using integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses while also evaluating intergenerational respiratory effects in the offspring of a pregnant mouse. In adult mice, BPM exposure impaired pulmonary function and induced histopathological alterations including inflammatory infiltration and early-stage fibrotic changes. At the molecular level, BPM disrupted pulmonary immune-inflammatory homeostasis by synergistically activating the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, while suppressing T-cell receptorand Fc receptormediated immunoregulatory signals. Notably, maternal BPM exposure resulted in pulmonary impairment in female offspring, as evidenced by altered lung function parameters and the corresponding histopathological abnormalities. In summary, these findings demonstrate that BPM induces pulmonary dysfunction in adult mice by disrupting immune-inflammatory homeostasis while also conferring sex-specific intergenerational respiratory health risks.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases are a significant and pervasive challenge to global health, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) emerging as a leading contributor to this burden. Specifically, COPD accounts for 81.7% of all deaths stemming from chronic respiratory conditions, culminating in a staggering 3.2 million deaths on an annual basis worldwide. In China, approximately 100 million cases of COPD result in 1 million deaths annually, making it one of the most prevalent chronic diseases alongside hypertension and diabetes. This widespread burden is closely linked to environmental triggers, particularly airborne pollutants. , In view of the significant prevalence of respiratory diseases, the evaluation of lung toxicity engendered by environmental pollutants, notably emerging chemicals, is of paramount public health significance.

Bisphenol M (BPM), an industrial compound widely used as a substitute for bisphenol A (BPA) in plastic products and food packaging materials, is frequently detected in environmental and biological samples, raising serious public health concerns. Environmental monitoring studies have detected in 77% of indoor dust samples from 13 Canadian cities, with an average concentration of 28 ng/g. Additionally, BPM has been identified in carbonated beverages and infant feeding bottles. Human biomonitoring data further indicated a detection rate of 65.8% in blood samples from Polish adults, and 25% in infant urine in Switzerland, , confirming multiroute exposure via ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact. These findings underscore the critical need for a comprehensive toxicological assessment of this emerging contaminant.

The widespread environmental presence and human exposure to BPM necessitate an urgent investigation into its toxicological profiles, particularly given its structural similarity to BPA and emerging evidence of potent endocrine-disrupting effects. Growing evidence indicates that BPM exhibits endocrine-disrupting properties comparable to its structural analog BPA, with potential adverse effects on reproductive, nervous, and immune systems. Notably, BPM exposure was shown to adversely affect female reproductive potential by compromising oocyte quality and meiotic maturation in mice. In vivo toxicity studies have demonstrated that BPM exposure induces severe developmental impairments in zebrafish embryos, including inhibited yolk sac absorption, altered heart rate, and teratogenic effects. Furthermore, BPM exhibits stronger acute toxicity than BPA in a zebrafish embryo model combined with molecular docking analysis. Mechanistic studies reveal that BPM has a higher binding affinity for human nuclear receptors than BPA, particularly for estrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ). , Intriguingly, coexposure of BPM with estrogen induces pronounced antiestrogenic activity. , Crucially, our previous studies revealed that the antiestrogenic compound tris­(2,3-dibromopropyl) isocyanurate triggers COPD-like phenotypes via ERα signaling, suggesting BPM may impair respiratory health. However, the pulmonary toxicity of BPM remains largely uncharacterized, and its underlying molecular mechanisms require further elucidation.

To address this critical knowledge gap, this study systematically investigates the pulmonary toxicity and intergenerational respiratory effects of BPM, while elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms of BPM-induced pulmonary injury through integrated multiomics profiling (RNA sequencing/proteomics). This work provides essential evidence for evaluating the safety of BPM as a BPA substitute in consumer products and provides novel mechanistic insights into the etiology of respiratory diseases caused by environmental pollutants. These findings will form the basis for establishing safer chemical regulatory standards and preventing environmentally triggered respiratory pathologies.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

BPM (purity ≥98%) and corn oil (pharmaceutical grade) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Co Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 4% paraformaldehyde fixative was purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade.

BPM was dissolved in corn oil, and the two solutions of 0.2 and 2 mg/mL were prepared and stored at 4 °C.

Animal Experimentation

To control for sex-related variables and minimize potential confounding effects of endogenous sex hormone fluctuations during the initial mechanistic exploration, the aforementioned experiments were initially conducted in male mice. Balb/c mice (5–6 weeks old, 18 ± 1 g) were procured from Beijing Viton Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50% ± 5% relative humidity) with ad libitum access to standard chow and distilled water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jianghan University (Reference Number: JHDXKJLL2025-203). After a 7-day acclimatization period, mice were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 10/group): vehicle control (corn oil), low-dose group (1 mg/kg/day BPM, BPM-1), and high-dose group (10 mg/kg/day BPM, BPM-10). BPM was administered via oral gavage for 14 consecutive days. Body weight, food intake, and water consumption were measured at 48-h intervals. After the exposure period, mice were tested for respiratory function using pulmonary function testing equipment (EMMS, U.K.) with 5 min recording sessions and then fasted for 12 h. Animals were humanly euthanized, and lung tissues were harvested and weighed to determine the lung coefficient (lung-to-body weight ratio × 100%).

Following the establishment of BPM-induced toxic effects in male mice, parallel experiments were performed in female mice under identical exposure and detection conditions to further evaluate sex-specific differences in BPM-induced pulmonary toxicity. The female mouse cohort was matched to the male cohort with respect to strain, age, housing conditions, experimental grouping, exposure protocol (dose, route, duration), and all detection parameters and methodologies.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson Trichrome Staining

Fresh lung tissue was fixed in 4% neutral paraformaldehyde for more than 24 h and stored at room temperature. The fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and prepared into sections 4-μm thick. After deparaffin of the sections, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson trichrome staining was performed, , followed by digital imaging using an EVOS M7000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lung injury scores were determined using a semiquantitative scoring system, assessing the extent of injury based on key features such as inflammation and congestion (alveolar and interstitial cell infiltration and hemorrhage), alveolar wall thickening, and hyaline membranes formation. Collagen deposition in lung tissue was quantitatively analyzed on Masson-stained sections using Image Pro-Plus software (version 6.0, Media Cybernetics) to determine the collagen-positive area percentage.

Transcriptome Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher), and quality was assessed via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RNA integrity number, RIN > 7.0). Subsequently, mRNA was enriched with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. Sequencing libraries were constructed through reverse transcription (Super Script TM II) and double-stranded cDNA synthesis (Escherichia coli DNA polymerase I/RNase H), with subsequent end repair, adapter ligation, and UDG treatment. After polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, the library yielded an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (LC-Bio). Raw data underwent quality control using Cutadapt to remove low-quality reads (defined as containing adapters, poly-A/G sequences, >5% unknown bases, or >20% low-quality bases). Filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, followed by transcript assembly and quantification via StringTie in FPKM units. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2/edgeR (thresholds, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and log2 FC ≥ 1), while functional enrichment analysis was carried out for gene ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using hypergeometric tests (p < 0.05).

Proteomics Analysis

Tissue samples underwent protein extraction using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer, with protein concentration quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. After acetone precipitation, protein samples were sequentially processed with dithiothreitol (DTT) reduction and iodoacetamide (IAA) alkylation followed by overnight trypsin digestion. For tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling experiments, digested peptides were labeled with TMT reagents and fractionated via high-pH reverse-phase chromatography; for data-independent acquisition (DIA) and label-free experiments, peptides were directly analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Mass spectrometry detection adopts the data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode, with a first-stage scan resolution of 120,000, a second-stage scan resolution of 30,000, and a high-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation energy of 32%. The 400–1200 m/z range was divided into 53 continuous acquisition Windows. The original data were analyzed by DIA-NN software (v1.8), with the Trypsin/P enzyme digestion set, allowing for two missed digestion sites, fixed modification to cysteine alkylation, variable modification to methionine oxidation, and FDR threshold of 1%. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (v4.0) for differential protein screening (p < 0.05, FC > 1.2) and functional enrichment analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Integrated transcriptomic-proteomic profiling identified spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), AKT serine/threonine kinase 3 (AKT3), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) as key targets from enriched immune-inflammatory pathways, with concurrent validation of estrogen signaling components ERα and cytochrome P450 17A1 (CYP17A1, the rate-limiting enzyme for estrogen biosynthesis) via immunohistochemistry. Tissue sections were processed through paraffin embedding, microtome sectioning (4 μm), and stepwise dewaxing to hydration. Antigen retrieval was performed according to antibody requirements using a citrate or Tris–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer solution. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, followed by blocking of nonspecific binding with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Anti-SYK Rabbit pAb (14858-1-AP, 1:1000; PTGLAB, Wuhan, China), anti-AKT3 Rabbit pAb (GB115583, 1:500; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-p38 Rabbit pAb (GB154685, 1:500; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-ERα Rabbit pAb (GB111843, 1:1000; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), anti-CYP17A1 Rabbit pAb (14447-1-AP, 1:200; PTGLAB, Wuhan, China), these antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. The DAB chromogenic reaction was monitored microscopically to determine the optimal termination point after which the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The sections were then dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. All antibodies underwent rigorous validation for specificity and batch consistency. Protein expression and localization were quantified using an EVOS M7000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Image-Pro Plus software for intensity analysis.

Intergenerational Effects Analysis

Several studies have indicated that prenatal exposure to bisphenol compounds is associated with an increased risk of respiratory disorders in offspring. To investigate the potential long-term health risks of BPM, pregnant mice (gestational day (GD) 1) were exposed to 0.1 mg/mL (0.5 mg/kg/day) BPM during the gestation and lactation periods (postnatal day (PND) 21). After the exposure period, F1 offspring mice were maintained under standard conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50% ± 5% humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to irradiated rodent chow and autoclaved water until they were 6 weeks old. Then, lung function of F1 offspring mice was assessed using the pulmonary function testing equipment (EMMS, U.K.). Following functional assessment, the animals underwent a 12-h fast prior to being humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia (isoflurane 5%). Lung tissues were immediately collected, weighed, and processed for histopathological analysis (H&E staining).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS18.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with intergroup differences denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Results

BPM Exposure Induced Lung Dysfunction in Adult Mice

Lung function assessment, reflecting ventilatory capacity, airway resistance, and gas exchange efficiency, revealed dose-dependent impairments in key dynamic pulmonary function parameters. Compared to the control group, minute volume (MV) decreased by 7.9% in the BPM-1 group and by 8.5% in the BPM-10 group, peak inspiratory flow (PIF) decreased by 9.9% in the BPM-1 group and by 9.6% in the BPM-10 group, peak expiratory flow (PEF) decreased by 7.4% in the BPM-1 group and by 10.4% in the BPM-10 group, and tidal volume (TV) decreased by 8.4% in the BPM-1 group and by 5.9% in the BPM-10 group (Figure a). Histopathological analysis further confirmed that BPM induced pulmonary damage, characterized by alveolar septal thickening, inflammatory cell infiltration, and perivascular hemorrhage, particularly in the BPM-10 group (Figure b). Consistently, the injury scores of lung tissues were significantly increased in both BPM-1 and BPM-10 groups compared with the control group (Figure c). Masson trichrome staining showed a dose-dependent increase in collagen deposition (blue signal) within the pulmonary interstitium of BPM-exposed mice compared with that in the control group (Figure b,c). Additionally, the lung organ coefficient (lung-to-body weight ratio) showed no significant elevation in the BPM treatment groups, compared to the control group (Figure S1). Besides the lung injury observed in male mice, our results showed that BPM exposure caused similar pulmonary damage in female mice (Figure S2). These findings indicate that BPM exposure induces structural damage and functional impairment in the lungs of mice.

Transcriptomic Profiling Revealed Activation of Immune-Inflammatory Pathways in the Lungs of BPM-Exposed Mice

The results of transcriptomics identified that 190 and 383 genes were upregulated and downregulated, respectively, in the BPM-10 group compared to the control group (Figure a). Hierarchical clustering of DEGs indicated that significantly upregulated genes (Fkbp5, Hif3a, Adamts9) primarily mediated cellular stress response and hormone signal regulation, while key downregulated genes (Nr4a1, Bmp10, Dusp10, Dusp2) functioned as critical modulators of inflammatory progression and stress adaptation (Figure b). The top 20 KEGG pathways enriched with DEGs were selected for functional annotation. KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated DEGs involvement in immune-inflammatory pathways, including cytokine–chemokine receptor interaction, hematopoietic cell lineage regulation, NF-κB signaling pathway and Th17 cell differentiation, and metabolic reprogramming events: tryptophan catabolism and fatty acid metabolism (Figure c). GO analysis further indicated that DEGs were predominantly localized to the plasma membrane and extracellular matrix, with predominant roles in biological processes including innate immune response, adaptive immune response, and inflammatory response; and the molecular functions: the activities of signaling molecules such as cytokines and chemokines, as well as the binding activities of immune-related receptors (e.g., antigen and chemokine receptors) (Figure d). These findings collectively indicate that BPM exposure significantly activates the immune-inflammatory networks through dysregulation of core immunomodulatory genes and signaling factors in the lungs of mice.

Proteomic Profiling Revealed BPM-Induced Dysregulation of Pulmonary Growth Signaling and Immune Activation Networks in Mice

The results of proteomic analysis demonstrated that most proteins clustered along the identity line (Figure a), whereas 57 proteins exhibited significant differential expression versus controls (23 upregulated, 34 downregulated; Figure b). Among these, the significantly upregulated proteins AP5B1, H2-AB1, and RBPJ were primarily associated with immune response and cell transport; downregulated proteins Ifi203, ERBB3, AKT3, CYP2F2, and HMGCS1 modulated immune activation, growth signal inhibition, and metabolic processes (Figure c). The GO enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were mainly localized in the cytoplasmic matrix and cytosolic compartments, implicating their involvement in intracellular trafficking. Molecular functions were significantly enriched in protein homodimerization activity, isomerase activity, and guanylate nucleotide exchange factor activity (Figure d). The KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in the Fc epsilon RI (FcεRI) signaling pathway (critical for immune response), B cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway, and metabolic pathways including d-amino acid metabolism and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Figure e). Therefore, BPM exposure induces pulmonary injury by interfering signal transduction, immune response, apoptosis, and metabolism in lungs of mice.

Integrated Multiomics Analysis Identified Core Signaling Hubs in BPM-Induced Pulmonary Injury

To elucidate the molecular concordance between transcriptomic and proteomic responses following BPM exposure, a comprehensive multiomics integration was performed. A nine-quadrant plot visualized coordinated alterations in transcriptomic and proteomic expression profiles (Figure a), revealing a significant positive mRNA–protein correlation. A core subset of regulators’ directional consistency at mRNA and protein levels indicates their functional involvement in a BPM-induced pathophysiological response. Integrated pathway analysis via Venn diagrams revealed 40 KEGG pathways coenriched in transcriptomic and proteomic data sets (Figure b), including pathways grouped according to biological functions, immune-inflammatory response pathways, cell proliferation/survival pathways, infection and pathogen response pathways, and endocrine regulatory pathways. Notably, immune-related signaling cascades were prominently represented among these coenriched pathways. Of particular significance, the FcεRI signaling pathway and the BCR signaling pathway emerged as top-ranking hits, demonstrating robust enrichment at both the transcriptional (mRNA) and translational (protein) levels (Figure c), thereby reinforcing their central role in orchestrating BPM-triggered pulmonary immunotoxicity. Chord diagram analysis was employed to delineate the interconnectivity of perturbed pathways and identify critical regulatory nodes (Figure d), revealing two pivotal molecules exhibiting high connectivity across multiple immune and inflammatory pathways, SYK (the main signaling kinase of BCR and Fc receptors) and AKT3 (a key effector within the PI3K-Akt signaling axis). These findings suggest that their potential as key regulatory molecules in BPM-induced pulmonary immune-inflammatory injury.

Verification of Key Signaling Proteins by Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to validate key signaling molecules identified in our proteomic study. The results demonstrated that BPM exposure induced significant differential regulation of key signaling proteins (SYK, p38 MAPK, and AKT3) in the BPM-10 group (Figure a). Quantitative analysis revealed that BPM significantly upregulated SYK and p38 MAPK expression by 96.5 and 50.3%, respectively, while AKT3 expression was markedly downregulated by 81.4% compared to the control group (Figure b). The significant dysregulation of key factors SYK, p38 MAPK, and AKT3 indicates aberrant activation in their implicated signaling pathways. These findings are highly consistent with the proteomics data and provide direct evidence in support of the core mechanism by which BPM disrupts pulmonary immune-inflammatory pathways.

BPM Exposure Induced Intergenerational Adverse Effects in Mice

To investigate the intergenerational effects of BPM, pregnant mice were continuously exposed to BPM during gestation and lactation (Figure a). Pulmonary function assessment revealed significant sex-specific impairments in offspring, and female offspring exhibited marked functional deficits, including 21.8% reduction in tidal volume (TV), 14.1% decrease in peak expiratory flow (PEF), 18.3% decline in pause enhancement (Penh), and 17.0% shortening of inspiration time (TI) (Figure b). Male offspring displayed only a 15.9% increase in TI, with significant alterations in other parameters (Figure c). Histopathological analysis further demonstrated severe pulmonary inflammation in BPM-exposed offspring. Compared with the control group, BPM-exposed offspring exhibited significant histopathological alterations, including alveolar wall thickening, hemorrhage, and pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure d,e). Lung injury scores were significantly elevated by 350% in female offspring and 266.7% in male offspring (Figure f,g). Additionally, there were no significant differences in lung coefficients between BPM-exposed offspring of both sexes and sex-matched controls (Figure S6). Collectively, these results demonstrate that maternal BPM exposure during gestation and lactation induces adverse intergenerational effects, characterized by impaired pulmonary function and inflammatory lung injury in the offspring of mice.

Discussion

The lung serves as a dynamic interface between the organism and airborne pollutants (e.g., PM2.5, dust), with established etiological roles in respiratory pathogenesis. , Beyond these ambient pollutants, epidemiological evidence links BPA exposure to elevated respiratory disease risk. Multiple studies have revealed that BPA exposure could cause severe pulmonary inflammation and histological damage in experimental animals, including airway inflammation and fibrosis. , Consistent with this evidence, our study demonstrates that BPM exposure induces structural lung damage characterized by alveolar wall thickening, inflammatory cell infiltration, hemorrhage, and focal collagen deposition. These pathological alterations confirm BPM-induced lung injury and further suggest its potential to trigger a pathological repair process. The confluence of qualitative similarity between BPM and BPA effects, combined with the prevalent use of BPM as a “safer alternative”, underscores an urgent need to re-evaluate the pulmonary safety profiles of bisphenol substitutes.

Pulmonary inflammation is collectively governed by immunoregulatory gene networks. In this study, BPM exposure induced significant upregulation of Fkbp5 and Hif3a in the lung tissue of mice, along with marked downregulation of Nr4a1, Dusp10, and Dusp2. Functionally, Fkbp5 serves as a pivotal regulator of NF-κB activation, thereby promoting inflammatory responses, with its upregulation further exacerbating inflammatory stress via AKT/FOXO1 signaling. Conversely, suppressed Nr4a1 (an essential negative feedback regulator) attenuates NF-κB inhibition, facilitating pro-inflammatory cytokines release. Similarly, reduced expression of Dusp10 and Dusp2 attenuates their endogenous inhibition of MAPK signaling, leading to diminished p38/JNK activities through dephosphorylation-mediated inactivation, thereby constraining excessive inflammation. Collectively, these dysregulated genes substantiate pulmonary inflammatory activation in the lungs of BPM-exposed mice. These findings were consistent with the known pathophysiological mechanisms of exogenous compound-induced lung injury. Moreover, downregulation of MHC Class II molecules (H2-Ob, H2-M2) and TCR signaling effectors (Cd3d, Zap70, Lat) directly impairs antigen presentation and T-cell activation, resulting in compromised immunoregulation that propagates chronic inflammatory cycles. −

BPM-induced immune inflammation extends beyond transcriptional dysregulation to proteomic perturbations. Specifically, BPM exposure activated multiple immune-related pathways, with the key effectors AKT3 and SYK exhibiting pronounced directional dysregulation and being coenriched across these pathways, indicating their central roles within the BPM-perturbed immune signaling network. AKT3 functions as a kinetic switch in the PI3K-Akt cascade, regulating cell survival, proliferation, and immune signal transmission. SYK functions as a proximal regulatory hub for FcεRI and BCR signaling, driving myeloid cell activation and migration, while modulating diverse immune processes. , Furthermore, the immunohistochemical analysis of BPM-exposed lung tissue confirmed SYK upregulation alongside concurrent AKT3 downregulation. These findings reveal that BPM induces lung injury by disrupting the immune regulatory networks. Integrated multiomics analysis further reveals that BPM exposure dysregulated ERα and CYP17A1 (a critical enzyme for estrogen synthesis), the key components of the estrogen signaling pathway. Given the critical role of local estrogen metabolic balance in pulmonary immunomodulation, , ERα mediates the protective effects of estrogen against pulmonary inflammatory responses. In this study, we observed BPM exposure downregulated the protein expression of ERα and CYP17A1 (Figure S5). This downregulation likely attenuates estrogen-dependent anti-inflammatory mechanisms while reducing local estrogen production, thereby synergistically exacerbating pulmonary inflammation.

Given the high susceptibility of developmental stages to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), , we established an early life exposure model to investigate the potential long-term health risks of BPM. The results indicate that maternal BPM exposure induces significant pulmonary damage in offspring, with female progeny exhibiting more severe pathological alterations and impaired lung function. This sex-specific phenotype aligns with prior reports on other bisphenol-endocrine disruptors. For instance, intrauterine BPA exposure induces sex-dependent airway and pulmonary inflammation in offspring. Recent studies also indicate that gestational–lactational exposure to bisphenol F specifically induces pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress in female offspring mice. Notably, based on the mechanism elucidated in our adult exposure model, where the estrogen signaling pathway (ERα) mediates BPM-induced pulmonary inflammation, we propose a plausible and testable hypothesis. Maternal BPM exposure may indirectly induce pulmonary injury in offspring by disrupting endocrine homeostasis. This sex-differential phenotype not only provides an entry point for further investigation into the developmental toxicity of BPM but also serves as a further warning regarding the potential risks of BPM as a BPA substitute during early life windows of vulnerability.

This study delineates the core mechanism of BPM-induced pulmonary toxicity, which involves the synergistic disruption of pulmonary immune-inflammatory homeostasis and local estrogen signaling. Specifically, BPM promotes a pro-inflammatory microenvironment by concurrently upregulating key inflammatory mediators and downregulating inhibitory factors, leading to synergistic activation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, while suppressing TCR and Fc receptor-mediated immunoregulatory signaling. Additionally, BPM disrupts local estrogen activity by downregulating ERα and CYP17A1, thereby attenuating endogenous anti-inflammatory protection. In the adult exposure model, these molecular perturbations collectively resulted in decreased pulmonary function, inflammatory infiltration, and early fibrotic changes. In the early life exposure model, BPM induced more severe lung damage in female offspring, suggesting that developmental exposure may precipitate long-term pulmonary effects via endocrine-mediated programming. Thus, BPM-induced pulmonary toxicity stems from its dual interference with immuno-inflammatory balance and estrogen signaling, exhibiting a dependence on developmental timing. However, it is important to note that the present study represents an initial step toward characterizing the molecular and endocrine underpinnings of BPM-induced intergenerational pulmonary effects. Future investigations can build upon this foundation by integrating multiomic analyses and targeted endocrine endpoint assessments in progeny to fully delineate the complex intergenerational toxicity mechanisms of BPM. Such mechanistic insights will be critical for translating these preclinical findings to human health risk assessment. Additionally, our future research will conduct chronic low-dose BPM exposure studies to evaluate progressive respiratory dysfunction and extrapolate safe exposure thresholds for human populations and perform comprehensive pharmacokinetic investigations to elucidate tissue-specific distribution, bioaccumulation kinetics, and clearance pathways of BPM under both acute and chronic exposure paradigms.
