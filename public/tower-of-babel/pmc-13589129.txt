Identification of novel linear antigenic epitope of IBDV VP3 recognized by monoclonal antibodies
Authors: Chengyu He, Zhixuan Xiong, Huiping Liang, Zheng Chen, Xiangdong Wu, Huali Xie, Huansheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to prepare specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) of IBDV VP3. We purified the recombinant VP3 protein to immunize the BALB/c mice for subsequently cell fusion. Following three rounds of sub-cloning, we successfully obtained three hybridoma cell lines (3B, 5 G, 6A) capable of stably secreting anti-VP3 mAbs with highly specificity and sensitivity. Preliminary identification using truncated Western blotting indicated that the mAbs primarily recognized the antigenic epitope in the C-terminus of VP3. Then, we employed alanine-scanning mutagenesis to precisely identify the key amino acid residues of this linear epitope as G243, R244, G246, W248 and I249. This discovery offers a new insight on the immunological characteristics of IBDV VP3 and might lay a solid basis for the advance of novel diagnostic tools based on C-terminal epitopes.Keywords: Infectious bursal disease virus, VP3, Monoclonal antibody, Antigenic epitope

Introduction

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) represents a highly transmissible as well as immune depression, which caused by IBDV, constituting a severe menace to the worldwide poultry-producing industry. This disease predominantly targets the Fabricius bursa in chicks aged 3 to 5 weeks. Resultant loss of lymphocytes and profound immunosuppression give rise to high illness and death rates (Zhang et al., 2022). IBDV is a dsRNA virus, and comprises segment A and segment B. A polyprotein, consisting of pVP2, VP4, and VP3, is encoded by segment A; after cleavage, it yields the structural polypeptides VP4, VP2, and VP3, plus the nonstructural polypeptide VP5 (Zhang, Wang et al., 2022). Effective diagnostic and vaccination strategies are crucial for controlling the spread of IBD and reducing economic losses.

Among the structural proteins, VP2, as a capsid protein, is responsible for inducing viral neutralizing antibodies. However, the high variability of VP2 undermines the broad-spectrum protective efficacy of existing vaccines, posing a significant challenge to the prevention and control of IBD (Chen, Zhou et al., 1998). In contrast, VP3 accounts for approximately 40% of the total protein mass of the viral particle (approximately 32 kDa) and is highly conserved across different IBDV strains (Deng, Gao et al., 2007). Furthermore, VP3 is deeply involved in all stages of the viral replication cycle, including the regulation of the host’s innate immune response, the protection of the double-stranded RNA genome, the recruitment of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (VP1) and the regulation of its activity, as well as the promotion of genomic replication within host cells (Gimenez, Issa et al., 2021). These multifunctional roles further highlight the central role of the VP3 protein in the IBDV infection process, making it a key target for the design of antiviral strategies.

Although some progress has been made in previous studies on the antigenic epitopes of IBDV VP3, these have mainly focused on its N-terminal and central regions, such as amino acids 109-119aa, 177-190aa, 29-43aa, and the 150-164aa region containing the conserved motif ELDKRA, evidence regarding linear antigenic epitopes in the C-terminal region remains unclear (Deng, Gao et al., 2007). Gap in this research constrains our comprehension of the immunological landscape of the VP3 protein and may result in the overlooking of potential targets for diagnostic and vaccine development.

This study produces mAbs against the IBDV VP3 and determine its novel linear antigenic epitopes. The innovation of this study lies in the fact that it is the first to experimentally identify immune dominant epitopes in the C-terminus of VP3 protein, thereby providing new molecular targets for preventing IBDV.

Materials and methods

Viruses, cells and key reagents

The IBDV JX strain (GenBank: OR743230 and OR743231) (Zeng, Huang et al., 2025), SP2/0 mouse myeloma cells, and the DF-1 cell line were held by the Institute of Preventative Vet Medicine in Jiangxi Agriculture University. For immunization, 6-week-old SPF BALB/c mice were obtained from Henan Sikebas Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Restriction endonucleases EcoRI and SalI, together with T4 ligase, were supplied by Takara, Japan. Isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), Freund's complete and incomplete adjuvant (FCA and FIA), peritoneal fluid immune adjuvant, HAT selective medium, HT supplement medium, mAb isotyping kit, and growth factors were from BioDragon (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China); HRP-conjugated and FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody were supplied by Proteintech (Wuhan, Hubei, China); The NP-40 lysis buffer used in immunoblotting assays was acquired from Beyotime (Shanghai, China).

Vector construction

The VP3 gene was amplified via RT-PCR from IBDV-infected cells. Amplicons were subsequently ligated into pET-28a vector. All of the mutant plasmids were constructed using the site-mutation technologies.

Preparation of anti-IBDV VP3 monoclonal antibodies

Recombinant VP3 protein was expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells harboring pET-28a-VP3 upon IPTG induction (1 mmol/L, 37°C, 6 h). After cell harvest and ultrasonic disruption, inclusion bodies were solubilized in 8 mol/L urea, and the protein was purified by Ni-NTA affinity (GE Healthcare, United States) (Hu, Wu et al., 2026). The purified VP3 was verified by anti-His immunoblotting and concentrated by ultrafiltration. For immunization, mice received a primary subcutaneous dose (400 μg) emulsified in Freund’s complete adjuvant, followed by two subcutaneous boosts (200 μg each in incomplete adjuvant) at 14-day intervals. Serum antibody titers were measured by indirect ELISA; mice with titers ≥1:100,000 were selected for fusion, and a final intraperitoneal booster (100 μg, no adjuvant) was given three days before spleen removal. Spleen cells were fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using PEG 4000 (Sigma, USA 95904), and hybridomas were selected in HAT medium. Supernatants were screened by ELISA and immunofluorescence, and positive clones were subcloned by limiting dilution. This resulted in three stable hybridoma cell lines that continuously secreted VP3-specific monoclonal antibodies; these cells were expanded and cryopreserved. To induce ascites, each cell line (1 × 10⁶ cells) was injected intraperitoneally into BALB/c mice pre-treated with adjuvant 10-15 days prior; 10-15 days after injection, when typical symptoms of ascites appeared, ascites fluid was collected via paracentesis and its titer was determined by ELISA (Hu, Wu et al., 2026).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The purified VP3 protein (5 µg/mL) was coated onto an ELISA plate at 4°C overnight and blocked with 5% skimmed milk at 37°C. The plate was then incubated with mAb at various dilutions at 37°C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, with three washes with PBST between each step. After adding the TMB substrate, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm (Zeng, Huang et al., 2025). IgG subtype was determined through a mouse mAb subtype identification kit.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from the cells using NP-40 lysis buffer, followed by SDS-PAGE and transfer to an NC membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk, then incubated overnight at 4°C with diluted mAb (1:5000). The samples were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody and visualized using the Tanon 5200 multi-imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) (Zeng, Huang et al., 2025).

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

DF-1 cells infected with IBDV or uninfected were fixed, then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 and incubated with mAbs at various concentrations as primary antibodies. Following incubation with FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody, the samples were examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Epitope conservation in mAbs and molecular docking

The sequence of VP3 in various IBDV strains, classical, variant, very virulent, attenuated, and novel variant, were retrieved from GenBank. A precise comparison was carried out of the core residues G243, R244, G246, W248 and I249 of the IBDV VP3 protein. AlphaFold was used to predict the three-dimensional structure of the IBDV protein; the sequences were input into the AlphaFold server to obtain structural models in PDB format. Based on the wild-type structure, point mutations were introduced using PyMOL’s Mutagenesis Wizard tool, sequentially replacing the G243, R244, G246, W248 and I249 residues with alanine. The mutant and wild-type structures were displayed in cartoon mode to show the protein backbone, with the side chains at the mutation sites shown as ball-and-stick models.

Results and discussions

Analysis of anti-VP3 monoclonal antibodies and preliminary epitope identification

After immunizing mice with the recombinant His-VP3 protein until the desired titer was achieved, through cell fusion and sub-cloning selection, three hybridoma cell lines stably producing VP3 specific mAbs were successfully generated, named mAb 3B, mAb 5 G and mAb 6A. Indirect ELISA results showed that three mAbs reached as high as 1:2,048,000. (Fig. 1A). Antibody subtype identification indicated that they primarily belonged to the IgG1 and Kappa subtypes (Fig. 1B). IFA confirmed that all mAbs recognize IBDV in infected cells at dilutions ranging from 1:500 to 1:5000. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that these mAbs react specifically to IBDV infected cells (Fig. 1C). Samples were collected 0, 6, 9, 12 and 18 hours after infection with IBDV; WB analysis revealed that all mAbs specifically recognized viral VP3. (Fig. 1D). The three successfully prepared monoclonal antibodies against IBDV VP3 (3B, 5 G and 6A) exhibit high specificity and affinity, providing a crucial foundation for the identification of linear antigenic epitopes. Then, we performed preliminary linear antigen epitope mapping on the three mAbs obtained (Fig. 1E); the preliminary screening involved expressing VP3 as two fragments, each comprising approximately 150aa. The first fragment, comprising 1-147aa, was designated VP3α, while the second fragment, comprising 148-258aa, was designated VP3β. Second truncation, the third fragment, comprising 148-201aa, was named VP3γ, and the fourth fragment, comprising 202-258aa, was named VP3δ. Following further truncation, the VP3ε fragment was located at 202-230aa, while the VP3σ fragment was located at 231-258aa.

Identification of C-terminal epitopes of VP3

Western blot analysis was performed on the initially truncated VP3 fragments; the results showed that all three mAbs, 3B, 5 G and 6A, recognized VP3β (Fig. 2A). To achieve more precise localization, the VP3β was further divided into two fragments. The results showed that all three mAbs recognized VP3γ (Fig. 2B). Following further truncation, western blot analysis revealed that all three mAbs recognized VP3σ (Fig. 2C). The results clearly demonstrate that all mAbs bind specifically to C-terminus of VP3. Subsequently, using an alanine mutagenesis screen, the antigenic epitope sites for the three mAbs were precisely identified as G243, R244, G246, W248, I249 (Fig. 2D). Sequence analysis revealed that 28-amino acid region of VP3 exhibits 100% sequence conservation across a wide range of IBDV strains (Fig. 2E). In the wild-type, residues such as G243, R244, G246, W248 and I249 possess large and structurally complex side chains, including the absence of a side chain in glycine, and the branched side chain of isoleucine (Fig. 2F). Upon substitution with alanine, these sites all become simple, small methyl side chains, thereby enabling the assessment of the specific roles of each residue within the protein structure (Fig. 2G). These residues are located in key regions on the protein surface and are likely involved in maintaining the overall folding and three-dimensional conformation of the protein.

In this study, three highly specific monoclonal antibodies against IBDV VP3 were successfully prepared, and a novel linear antigenic epitope located in the C-terminal region (G243, R244, G246, W248 and I249) was precisely identified. Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on the N-terminal or central regions, our findings provide the first direct experimental evidence of a conserved epitope within the C-terminal oligomerization domain (Maraver, Oña et al., 2003). Given that this domain is crucial for VP3-VP1 interactions and capsid assembly, this epitope is not only of significant value for immunodiagnostic but may also provide important clues for elucidating the relationship between the structure and function of the VP3 protein (Zhang, Wang et al., 2024). The 100% conservation of this epitope across different IBDV strains highlights its potential importance as a DIVA diagnostic marker and for the development of broad-spectrum epitope-based vaccines (Bortolami, Donini et al., 2021). Although VP3 is an internal capsid protein and the mAbs generated in this study are unlikely to directly neutralize IBDV, the high conservation of this C‑terminal epitope across diverse IBDV strains renders it a promising candidate component for epitope‑based vaccine design. In particular, this epitope can bind to the neutralizing epitope derived from VP2, thereby offering the potential to broaden protection against emerging variant strains. Further research is required to assess the immunogenicity of this epitope within the VP2-VP3 combined antigen delivery system.

Conclusion

In this study, three highly-selective mAbs targeting the VP3 protein were produced. Employing truncation together with alanine-scanning mutagenesis, previously uncharacterized linear antigenic determinants (G243, R244, G246, W248, I249) of VP3 were identified. The findings not only deepen our understanding of the immunological characteristics of IBDV VP3 protein but also lay a solid foundation for the future development of novel diagnostic reagents based on C-terminal epitopes.
