Advanced Gastric Cancer Presenting as a Giant Subepithelial Lesion: A Case Report
Authors: Ji Sun, Qian Wang, Yuemei Hu, Ping Yin, Ziming Zhou, Hui Chen, Xiaoming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGastric cancer may occasionally present with a subepithelial lesion‐like appearance. When a gastric gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) is suspected preoperatively without definitive pathological confirmation, intraoperative frozen section analysis is recommended to prevent inappropriate surgical intervention.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a malignant epithelial tumor, predominantly associated with Helicobacter pylori infection. Subepithelial lesions (SELs) are lesions originating from the muscularis mucosae, submucosa, or muscularis propria [1]. We report a rare case of moderately to highly differentiated advanced gastric cancer that manifested as a subepithelial lesion (SEL) in a patient considered to have undergone spontaneous eradication of H. pylori .

Case History/Examination

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his family for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

The patient was a 69‐year‐old male with a history of hypertension who was prescribed amlodipine besylate tablets (5 mg daily). He had not undergone H. pylori eradication treatment, reported no significant family history, occasionally consumed alcohol, and was a nonsmoker. Recent physical examination was unremarkable. Abdominal ultrasonography (US) and chest computed tomography (CT) scans were normal. In December 2024, he underwent an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) due to a week‐long retrosternal discomfort. EGD showed a giant gastric SEL in the cardia and gastric fornix. The lesion lacked central depression but showed evidence of prior bleeding; it was firm on palpation with biopsy forceps and prone to bleeding (Figure 1a,b). Image‐enhanced endoscopy with linked color imaging (LCI, Figure 1c) and blue light imaging (BLI, Figure 1d) revealed that the surface of the lesion exhibited granular alterations, and the gastric area appeared enlarged.

The EGD diagnosis was a suspected GIST. A biopsy of the mass was not performed. Instead, a random biopsy was performed from the gastric antrum. Furthermore, a mucosal bulge located in the gastric body was identified and subsequently resected using biopsy forceps. Histopathology of the antral biopsy specimens confirmed mild chronic nonatrophic gastritis, whereas biopsy specimens from the gastric body revealed hyperplastic polyps. Notably, histological examination of both biopsy specimens was negative for H. pylori infection. The patient was admitted to the department of gastrointestinal surgery. On admission, laboratory investigations revealed the following: an elevated serum gastrin‐17 level of 9.840 pmol/L; a pepsinogen I (PGI) concentration of 73.18 ng/mL; an elevated pepsinogen II (PGII) level of 73.83 ng/mL; and a decreased PGI/PGII ratio of 0.99. Among the tumor markers, neuron‐specific enolase (NSE) was above the detection range. Abdominal enhanced CT scans (Figure 2) revealed a slightly hypoattenuating, round‐shaped lesion measuring 29 × 31 mm in the fundus of the stomach, with indistinct margins and heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration. The preoperative diagnosis was a suspected GIST. Laparoscopic proximal gastrectomy was initially performed.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

The majority of SELs exhibit histopathological features consistent with stromal tumors, leiomyomas, lymphomas, neuroendocrine tumors, and schwannomas. Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) represents the most prevalent malignant lesion type among gastric SELs. However, intraoperative rapid frozen‐section pathology confirmed the lesion as adenocarcinoma, prompting a radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer. The postoperative pathological report indicated moderately to well‐differentiated adenocarcinoma of the proximal stomach, protruding type, measuring 4.5 × 3.5 × 2 cm, with indistinct margins. The tumor exhibited infiltrative growth, extending through the gastric serosa and invading the surrounding striated muscle tissue. Additional features included neural bundle invasion and vascular tumor thrombi. Of the 16 perigastric lymph nodes examined, one demonstrated metastatic involvement (1/16). The special staining results revealed Masson stain (indicating collagen fiber hyperplasia), elastic staining (negative), and reticulin stain (negative for intercellular staining). These features suggested a diagnosis of moderately to highly differentiated advanced SEL‐like gastric cancer (pT4aN1M0) (Figure 3). The patient tested negative for serum antibodies against H. pylori. A total of 13 genes were evaluated (HER2, MSI, PD‐L1, EGFR, MET, FGFR2, KRAS, PIK3CA, EBV, NTRK, CDH1, BRCA1, BRCA2), all of which were found to be negative.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Postoperatively, the patient completed six cycles of the mFOLFOX6 regimen, after which follow‐up examination showed abnormally elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). The treatment was adjusted to a combination of mFOLFOX6 and camrelizumab. The patient has now completed all 12 planned cycles of chemotherapy, with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Gastric cancer is a malignant epithelial tumor, predominantly associated with H. pylori infection. In this case, the tests for H. pylori were all negative and the patient had not undergone eradication therapy. However, on white light endoscopy, the extent of gastric mucosal atrophy was classified as C2 according to the Kimura–Takemoto classification. Histopathological examination of the background mucosa from the resected specimen revealed atrophy and intestinal metaplasia (Figure 4). Based on these findings, this case may have had a prior H. pylori infection that was spontaneously eradicated. Whether the unusual subepithelial appearance is associated with this condition remains unclear. Possible mechanisms underlying SEL‐like gastric cancer include lymphoid follicular formation, lymphocyte infiltration, and interstitial connective tissue proliferation, predominant in the submucosal layer [2]. It has been hypothesized that the decrease in inflammation following H. pylori eradication may facilitate stromal invasion in gastric cancer [3]. Following the eradication of H. pylori , a shift of the Ki‐67‐labeled zone of mucosal hyperplasia from the upper to the middle region and a gradual reduction in surface epithelial atypia have been described [4]. Similar findings were observed in the current case (Figure 5). Therefore, we hypothesize that H. pylori eradication status might have contributed to the endoscopic and histopathological features of this lesion.

In this patient, the gastric diameter was > 2 cm, surgical intervention was indicated, abdominal enhanced CT suggested a probable GIST, and R0 resection was deemed achievable. Therefore, surgical resection of the lesion was performed. The primary limitation in the preoperative assessment of this case was the absence of endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) and the lack of a histopathological diagnosis prior to surgical intervention. This was due to two factors: the patient declined a second EGD, and only a 12 MHz catheter probe EUS was available at the time. Considering that the lesion was larger than 2 cm, we deemed that the diagnostic information obtainable through catheter probe EUS was limited. Based on contrast‐enhanced CT and endoscopic evaluation, we initially considered a diagnosis of GIST; however, subsequent histopathological analysis did not confirm this. Although imaging differential diagnosis strategies for SELs vary slightly depending on tumor size, symptoms, and other factors worldwide, EUS is regarded as having excellent diagnostic capabilities for both lesion detection and histological assessment [5, 6, 7, 8]. Prior studies have confirmed that EUS is superior to computed tomography (CT) in diagnostic accuracy [9, 10] and is regarded as the gold standard imaging modality owing to its accessibility, detectability, and minimal invasiveness [11]. Given that preoperative EUS can provide valuable diagnostic information and facilitate better characterization of such lesions, greater emphasis should be placed on its integrating into the diagnostic workup of similar cases in clinical practice.

Direct lesion‐targeted biopsy was not performed during the examination because subepithelial lesions (SELs) are generally difficult to definitively diagnose via simple mucosal biopsy, and the lesion exhibited a tendency to bleed upon contact with the biopsy forceps. There is no consensus on the optimal timing for endoscopic tissue acquisition in SELs. Decisions should be individualized based on EUS findings, patient symptoms, clinical background, and institutional resources and expertise [10]. The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) recommends tissue diagnosis for SELs with GIST features if they are > 20 mm, present with high‐risk stigmata, or require surgical or oncology evaluation. For lesions ≥ 20 mm, either EUS‐FNB or MIAB is equally recommended [5]. Japanese guidelines recommend that for resectable, localized gastrointestinal submucosal tumors, endoscopic biopsy should be performed to rule out epithelial tumors. For lesions measuring 2–5 cm with irregular margins, ulceration, pitting, or enlargement, EUS‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (EUS‐FNA) is recommend [6]. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) suggests that (1) bite‐on‐bite biopsies should be avoided before EUS; (2) EUS with tissue acquisition—either FNB alone or FNA with ROSE—should be performed to improve diagnostic accuracy compared with FNA without rapid on‐site evaluation (ROSE); and (3) if these approaches are nondiagnostic but a definitive diagnosis is still required, an unroofing technique should be used [7]. The Chinese consensus states that preoperative biopsy for SELs is invasive and not mandatory because it may increase surgical complexity and increase risks such as bleeding, perforation, and tumor seeding. However, when preoperative pathology is lacking, intraoperative frozen section is essential to exclude other tumors and guide the correct surgical approach [8, 12].

In conclusion, the identification of a gastric SEL should raise suspicion for gastric cancer. When SELs are detected, EUS should be the imaging method of choice, and pathological diagnosis should be obtained whenever possible to guide appropriate treatment. For lesions with surgical indications but without a confirmed pathological diagnosis, intraoperative frozen section is indispensable.
