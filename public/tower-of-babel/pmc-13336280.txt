Specific and shared mechanisms mediating pain intensity and functional outcomes in chronic neck pain
Authors: Chad E Cook, Bryan O’Halloran, Derek Clewley, Frank Keefe, Cindy Green, Amy McDevitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo date, very few studies have used mediation analysis to explore the reasons why people with chronic neck pain improve after physical therapy intervention involving manual therapy or exercise.ObjectivesTo evaluate which specific mechanisms (mobility, pain modulation and endurance) and shared mechanisms (self-efficacy, working alliance, engagement, fear, and positive coping) mediated the effects of treatment on clinical outcomes at 4-weeks and 6-months in individuals with chronic neck pain.MethodsThe study incorporated planned indirect‑only mediation analyses to evaluate both specific and shared mechanisms as mediator using data from a mechanistic clinical trial comparing manual therapy and exercise interventions, which demonstrated no between‑group differences. Baseline pain intensity was modeled as the independent variable, and pain interference and physical function at four-weeks and six-months were specified as outcomes. Direct, indirect, and total effects were estimated across four mediation models.ResultsNone of the mediation analyses found specific or shared mechanisms that mediated the relationship of baseline pain intensity to long-term pain interference and physical function. Discussion/Conclusion: Our findings suggest that the absence of significant mediation implies that commonly targeted mechanisms may not be the primary drivers of the relationship of pain intensity to longer-term pain interference or physical function.Clinical trial registrationwww.clinicaltrials.gov identifier is NCT05940012.KEYWORDS: Manual therapy, physical therapy, mediation analysis, mechanisms, neck pain

Introduction

Evaluating the effectiveness of interventions requires not only determining whether they produce clinically meaningful improvements but also understanding why they work [1]. These second-order questions focus on the mechanisms through which treatments exert their effects, offering insight into the processes that connect therapeutic inputs to patient outcomes [2]. Mechanisms may be psychological (e.g., changes in fear-avoidance beliefs), behavioral (e.g., increased adherence to exercise), or physiological (e.g., improved cervical proprioception) [3]. They can also be divided into “specific” and “shared” categories, with specific mechanisms reflecting a type of treatment that provides a unique set of physiological/psychological processes within the body. In contrast, shared mechanisms are common underlying processes through which different treatments produce therapeutic change, even if the treatments themselves look very different on the surface [2–5]. Identifying these pathways is critical for refining interventions, tailoring them to individual needs, and advancing theoretical models of chronic pain management.

Mediation analyses are a family of statistical approaches that quantify how an independent variable exerts its influence on an outcome through one or more intervening mechanisms, allowing researchers to disentangle direct effects from indirect, pathway‑specific effects [6,7]. By formally modeling these intermediary processes, mediation frameworks help clarify not only whether an effect exists but how it unfolds, offering richer theoretical insight than simple associations alone. This added nuance is especially valuable when evaluating complex behavioral or biological systems, where multiple, interrelated pathways may jointly contribute to observed outcomes [8]. Mediation analyses help researchers to distinguish how therapeutic benefits unfold across short- and longer-term (6 months) follow-up periods.

Recent mediation analyses in chronic neck pain (CNP) and related conditions have begun to illuminate these pathways. For instance, a recent study [9] used repeated-measures mediation analysis to show that reductions in pain catastrophizing and improvements in muscle function mediated the short-term effects of dry needling in chronic neck pain. This adds to a recent systematic review [10], which found that pain intensity alone does not fully explain disability; psychological factors, specifically fear of movement/avoidance behaviors and pain catastrophizing, were consistent mediators between pain and disability, much more so than self‑efficacy and coping strategies, which displayed variability across studies. Broader chronic pain research has also highlighted behavioral mechanisms, such as reductions in avoidance and improvements in coping, as mediators of both mental and physical health outcomes in digital interventions [11]. Collectively, these findings suggest that both psychological and physical mechanisms contribute to the translation of baseline pain intensity into functional improvements and reduced interference.

Mediation analyses are important in disentangling the complex interplay of factors that drive recovery in CNP, setting the stage for mechanism-informed treatment development. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have comprehensively examined the role of both specific and shared mechanisms underlying chronic neck pain. Investigating these pathways is essential for advancing our understanding of why certain treatments are effective and whether targeted interventions are required to optimize outcomes [5]. The objective of this study was to evaluate the extent to which specific (tissue mobility, pain modulation and endurance) and shared (self-efficacy, therapeutic alliance, engagement, fear, and coping) treatment mechanisms mediate clinical outcomes at 4 weeks and 6 months in individuals with CNP. We hypothesized that shared mechanisms will mediate improvements in pain interference and physical function to a greater extent than specific mechanisms across both time points.

Methods

Study registration

This study was registered with clinicaltrials.gov (#NCT05940012), and a protocol paper describing the parameters of the study has been previously published [5]. A Guideline for Reporting Mediation Analyses (AGReMA) statement [12], a guideline for reporting mediation analyses was used to standardize publishing for the results of this present study.

Study design and setting

The study included a planned secondary data analysis using data from a randomized, parallel-arm mechanistic clinical trial [5]. The mechanistic trial was originally powered to examine differences between manual therapy (MT) and strengthening/endurance groups. The multi-site trial (Duke University, University of Colorado at Anschutz, Saint Josephs University and the Medical University of South Carolina) found no differences in clinical outcomes (pain interference, pain intensity or physical function) across two comparative groups at any follow-up point [13].

Participants

The secondary data included adults (≥18 years) with self‑reported CNP, defined as pain rated ≥3/10 on most days over the past 3 months, with symptoms primarily localized to the cervical region. Individuals with suspected serious pathology or underlying cervical radiculopathy were excluded.

Interventions

Interventions have been comprehensively defined elsewhere [5], but consisted of a baseline visit followed by 4 weeks of treatment (five total sessions, one per week), with equal time and support provided across the MT and exercise groups. Participants were informed that both approaches had comparable evidence of efficacy. Four physical therapists with advanced training delivered prescriptive exercise or manual therapy protocols, with flexibility to tailor treatment to individual impairments, and reinforced group‑specific home exercise programs each week to promote adherence and effectiveness. In the mechanistic clinical trial, comparative analyses found no differences at 4 weeks or 6 months between the two groups for pain interference, pain intensity or physical function.

Sample size

Because this project was a secondary analysis of an existing trial, sample size was determined by the parent study. The original trial was powered to detect differences in the targeted mechanisms between MT and resistance exercise. Assuming a medium cumulative effect size across mechanisms, 80% power, a two‑tailed α of 0.05, three measurement occasions, two treatment groups, and a correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures, linear mixed‑effects modeling indicated that 86 participants were required. To account for anticipated attrition exceeding 30% across sites, the parent study increased the target enrollment by 40 participants, yielding a planned sample size of 126.

Effects of interest

This study evaluated to the direct effect (the effect not operating through the mediator or X to Y), the indirect effect (the effect transmitted through the mediator or X on Y operating through M) and the total effect (defined as the overall causal effect of changing X from the reference to the exposure level): this effect captures both the portion transmitted through the mediator M (indirect effect) and the portion operating through all other pathways (direct effect).

Assumed causal model

The mediation analysis involved an indirect-only mediation analysis of specific and shared mechanisms (Figure 1). An indirect‑only mediation analysis is useful because it allows researchers to identify and quantify mediating pathways even when the direct effect (X to Y) of the exposure (X) on the outcome (Y) is weak or non‑significant [8]. This makes indirect‑only mediation particularly valuable for uncovering subtle or mechanism‑driven effects that may be obscured in traditional analyses focused solely on total or direct associations.

Measurements

Measurements included independent variables (X), outcome measures (Y) and potential mediators (M) captured at several points over 6 months (Figure 2). Independent Variable (X): Baseline pain intensity was the independent variable (“X”) selected for the mediation analysis. We selected baseline pain intensity because it represents a primary clinical driver of disability, serving as the initial exposure that influences downstream behavioral and psychological mechanisms [1,10]. In the trial, pain intensity was gathered at baseline prior to randomization and initiation of treatment.

Timing of data capture for the mediation analyses.

Outcome measures (Y)

For the mediation analysis, the outcome variables included the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) pain interference and physical function measures. The Pain Interference measure assesses the extent to which pain hampers engagement in daily activities and social, cognitive, and emotional functioning. The Physical Function measure evaluates an individual’s capability to perform a range of physical activities, providing a standardized assessment of functional limitations and overall physical health. Both measures were captured at baseline (at the same time as pain intensity), 4 weeks and 6 months (via an emailed survey link). The two PROMIS measures, which each included four items, were adopted from the PROMIS 29.2 scale [14].

Potential mediators (M)

Candidate specific and shared mechanisms mediators included the constructs of tissue mobility, pain modulation, and endurance (specific mechanisms), and self-efficacy, fear, engagement, coping, and working alliance (shared mechanisms). Specific mechanisms were measured using a Cervical Range of Motion device (CROM) (Fabrication Enterprises, 64 Mountainview Boulevard, Wayne, New Jersey 07470, United States) (for mobility) [15], pain pressure threshold (for pain modulation) using algometry testing (Wagner Pain Test FPX, Greenwich, CT 06836–1217 USA) at the first point of pain on the left (L) and right (R) zygapophyseal joints, L and R at the midpoint between the tip of the acromion and the C5 spinous process [16], and timed endurance testing for anterior neck flexion, left and right lateral flexion, and neck extensors [17–19].

Shared mechanisms were measured using the Working Alliance Inventory [20], selected items from the Optimal Screening for Prediction of Referral and Outcome-Yellow Flag (OSPRO YF) [21], the UW Pain-Related Self Efficacy Scale [22], and the Clinician Rated Patient Engagement [23] (Supplementary Table S1). These tools were selected to represent key mechanistic constructs: therapeutic alliance, fear, coping, self‑efficacy, and engagement. Our multidisciplinary research team identified each as a plausible mediator of treatment outcomes, given their relevance to both biomechanical and psychological processes, and because several have been evaluated previously in mediation analyses [24,25]. Each measure was captured at baseline at the same time as pain intensity and again at the end of the third week of treatment. The one exception was the working alliance inventory [20], which reflected a change in therapeutic alliance from week 2 to week 3 (a baseline therapeutic alliance would be inappropriate to measure since no therapeutic interaction has occurred). Composite measures were calculated into change scores which reflected the change from the baseline measures.

Statistical methods

Analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro for IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0 (Model 4), which estimates mediation effects through ordinary least squares regression with bootstrapping procedures. Baseline pain intensity was specified as the independent variable (X), while pain interference and physical function served as dependent variables (Y). Candidate mediators included measures of mobility, pain modulation, endurance, self-efficacy, fear, engagement, coping, and working alliance.

Four separate indirect-only mediation models were run (16 total) that targeted 1) mobility, 2) pain modulation, 3) strengthening/endurance and 4) shared mechanisms were estimated for each outcome at two follow-up time points (4 weeks and 6 months). Indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples, with statistical significance determined by intervals that did not cross zero. For each model, “group” was used as a covariate, despite the null findings of the trial. The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated to evaluate the overall model fit and the proportion of variance explained, and p-values were reported for significance testing. Standardized beta coefficients and 95% confidence intervals were examined for direct effects, indirect effects through each mediator, and total effects.

Results

Participants

Participants averaged 38.3 ± 16.5SD years of age, were mostly female (72.4%) and were representative of the race and ethnicity proportions for the recruitment sites. On average, at baseline, participants reported nearly 76 months of neck pain (total range: 1517 months) and scored nearly one-half of a standard deviation above/worse (55.2; SD = 6.4) the population mean in pain interference (50.0). Mean pain intensity at baseline was 4.5/10 (SD = 2.1) whereas the PROMIS physical function average was 50.0 (SD = 6.6).

Tissue mobility

Mediation analyses examining range of motion yielded R2 values between 0.02 and 0.08, with p‑values that did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). None of the range of motion change scores significantly mediated the association between baseline pain intensity and subsequent pain interference or physical function at either 4 weeks or 6 months. These findings indicate that the models accounted for only a small portion (2–8%) of the variance in the outcomes, with the direct effect of baseline pain intensity on the outcomes comprising the majority of the total effect (although not statistically significant). The proposed mediators, which were not statistically supported, explained only a small proportion of the relationship between the exposure and the outcomes.

Discussion

The study included a planned indirect-only mediation analysis of specific and shared mechanisms. We hypothesized that shared mechanisms would mediate outcomes more than specific mechanisms, but found that only two of our shared mechanisms (OSPRO Fear and OSPRO coping) mediated the relationship of baseline pain intensity to four-week PROMIS physical function. There were no specific or shared mechanism changes scores that mediated the relationship of baseline pain intensity to PROMIS pain interference at 4 weeks and 6 months or physical function at 6 months. These unexpected findings deserve further discussion.

Early improvements in OSPRO‑assessed fear avoidance and coping responses mediated the relationship between pain intensity and physical function at 4 weeks, supporting our hypothesis that shared psychological mechanisms would exert a stronger influence on outcomes than specific, impairment-related factors. Although the OSPRO measures have not previously been incorporated into mediation models, they have been widely used for predicting clinical outcomes such as pain, disability, and recovery trajectories; for risk stratification and phenotyping; for identifying psychosocial profiles; and for efficiently estimating full‑length psychological questionnaire scores [26–29]. Our findings extend this body of work by demonstrating that the OSPRO‑YF tool, designed to capture multiple psychosocial risk factors in a brief, clinically feasible format, may also function as a meaningful mediator of early functional improvement. This aligns with a growing body of evidence suggesting that reducing fear‑related cognitions and enhancing adaptive coping early in care can accelerate recovery and improve short‑term functional outcomes.

In musculoskeletal pain research, it is not unusual for mediation analyses to yield null results, with proposed mediators failing to explain the relationship between pain intensity and disability outcomes. This pattern has been observed across multiple trials and reviews, underscoring the complexity of biopsychosocial mechanisms in chronic pain. For example, a systematic review of mediation studies in back and neck pain [10] found that while psychological factors such as fear‑avoidance and catastrophizing were often tested, many analyses did not identify significant mediating effects. Similarly, others [9] noted in their meta‑analysis of psychologically based interventions for chronic musculoskeletal pain that mediation findings were inconsistent, with several plausible mechanisms showing no significant explanatory role. These results highlight that null mediation findings are common and emphasize the need for broader mechanistic models and more refined and/or responsive measurement approaches in pain research, including moving beyond broad, single‑timepoint self‑report scales toward methods that capture mechanisms with greater precision, timing, and validity.

Another potential explanation to our findings is that there may be insufficient data spread across our sample in the selected mediator variables; this generated little variation in our sample. When little variation exists, it is difficult to identify statistically significant differences when mediating X to Y [30]. This was highly reflective in our mobility scores and pain pressure threshold mediation values (most participants had similar scores), which was compounded further when converted to change scores (scores ranged from −3 to 6 points). We also experienced this with the working alliance inventory and clinical engagement tools, both of which exhibited ceiling effects, findings that have been reported previously [31–34].

Further, we selected PROMIS pain interference and physical function measures as these are a recommended framework for clinical research because of its psychometric robustness, cross‑condition applicability, and ability to improve comparability across studies [35]. However, PROMIS values have shown poor responsiveness and may too include very little spread [36,37]. PROMIS tools are designed as generic health status instruments. This broad applicability means they may miss nuances of specific conditions (e.g., cervical spine disorders, inflammatory arthritis). Disease specific measures often capture subtle changes more sensitively [37]. PROMIS pain interference and physical function are excellent general health status instruments, but their non-disease specificity can reduce responsiveness and clinical sensitivity compared to condition specific measures. Future studies should consider capturing legacy measures concurrently.

It is also important to recognize that our chosen mediators may not be the true mechanisms, which influence our longer-term outcomes. Our specific measures were indirect mechanisms, which are clinical findings that serve as proxy indicators of an underlying contrast (in our case, tissue mobility, strength and pain modulation). Indirect mechanism measures may lead to incorrect findings when the proposed mediators lack sufficient variability or reliability, resulting in spurious or attenuated estimates of indirect effects. In addition, unmeasured confounding or misspecification of the causal pathway can bias mediation models, producing misleading conclusions about how an exposure influences clinical outcomes [1,30]. This can lead to an overestimation or underestimation of recorded values because improvements could stem from better efficiency, reduced discomfort, or improved aerobic capacity rather than true increases in muscle strength [38].

Although a simple, indirect-only mediation models have lacked initial historical support, it is an acceptable modern way of looking the relationship between X and Y variables [39]. This approach is particularly useful in clinical and behavioral research, where interventions may exert their influence primarily through mediators such as coping, self‑efficacy, or fear‑avoidance rather than through a large direct effect [40]. Its strengths include providing mechanistic insight, clarifying how treatments work, and informing refinement of interventions to target the most influential pathways. However, weaknesses include the potential for over‑interpretation of small indirect effects, susceptibility to measurement error, and the risk of missing unmeasured or alternative mediators, which can limit explanatory power. Transparent reporting of indirect effects, confidence intervals, and theoretical justification is therefore essential.

Limitations

Our sample size was reflective of the power estimates of the mechanistic clinical trial. Although our sample size (n = 124) is only slightly smaller than other indirect‑only mediation studies (n ≈ 145) reported in the literature, a larger sample may tighten confidence intervals and improve the reliability and interpretability of findings [41]. Importantly, however, our mediation models were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples, which provides effect estimates and confidence intervals that are robust to non‑normality and small‑sample variability. This resampling approach reduces the likelihood that the observed indirect effects are artifacts of sampling fluctuations. Additionally, we assessed change scores in mechanisms using changes from baseline to 3 weeks; although this timeframe may have been too short to capture the full extent of change in both specific and shared mechanisms, such a limitation would be expected to influence the precision of estimates rather than generate spurious mediation pathways. Taken together, while these factors may introduce some uncertainty around the exact magnitude of effects, they are unlikely to account for the overall pattern of findings, which aligns with our theoretical model and prior mechanistic work.

Conclusion

Across all mediation models, including those assessing range of motion, pain modulation, strengthening and endurance, as well as shared psychological mechanisms such as self‑efficacy, working alliance, engagement, fear avoidance, and coping, most proposed mediators did not significantly account for the relationship between baseline pain intensity and subsequent pain interference or physical function at either 4 weeks or 6 months. The only individual mediators demonstrating significant associations were changes in OSPRO fear‑avoidance and coping, both of which were linked to improved PROMIS Physical Function at 4 weeks. Taken together, the absence of significant mediation through the remaining mechanisms (e.g., range of motion, pain modulation, strengthening, self‑efficacy, alliance, engagement) suggests that these factors may not be the primary pathways through which baseline pain intensity influences longer‑term pain interference or physical function in this cohort.
