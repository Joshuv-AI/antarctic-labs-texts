Unpacking the association of activities of daily living on self-care ability in China’s rural empty-nest older adults: the chain mediation of social alienation and psychological resilience
Authors: Zhaoquan Jiang, Lan Zhang, Qi Sun, Zhaoxu Xu, Mingyue Zhou, Xiaoyan Zhang, Tao Liu, Shixue Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the associations among Activities of Daily Living (ADL), social alienation, psychological resilience, and self-care ability in rural empty-nest older adults. It further examines the chain mediating roles of social alienation and psychological resilience in the relationship between ADL and self-care ability.MethodsBetween February 2022 and March 2023, a survey was conducted among 1,025 rural empty-nest older adults in Chaoyang, Liaoning Province, China. A general information questionnaire, the Activities of Daily Living Scale, the Social Alienation Scale, the Psychological Resilience Scale, and the Self-Care Ability Scale were used as research instruments. Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and the PROCESS v4.1 macro for correlation, regression, and mediation effect analyses.ResultsThe mean scores for ADL, social alienation, psychological resilience, and self-care ability were 21.01 ± 9.51, 42.30 ± 14.05, 129.41 ± 22.79, and 143.54 ± 19.24, respectively. ADL scores were positively correlated with social alienation scores (r = 0.113, p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with both psychological resilience (r = -0.120, p < 0.001) and self-care ability (r = -0.170, p < 0.001). Social alienation was negatively correlated with psychological resilience (r = -0.535, p < 0.001) and self-care ability (r = -0.738, p < 0.001). Psychological resilience was positively correlated with self-care ability (r = 0.780, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis indicated that social alienation and psychological resilience partially mediated the effect of ADL on self-care ability. Specifically, a chain mediation effect of social alienation and psychological resilience was confirmed in the relationship between ADL and self-care ability.ConclusionsADL directly predicts self-care ability among rural empty-nest older adults. Additionally, ADL can indirectly affects self-care ability through the separate mediating effects of social alienation and psychological resilience, as well as through their combined chain mediation effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07707-0.Keywords: Rural empty-nest older adults, Activities of daily living, Social alienation, Mediation effect, Psychological resilience, Self-care ability

Background

The rapid aging of the global population presents a significant public health challenge, particularly in China, where urbanization has led to a substantial increase in “empty-nest” older adults in rural areas [1–3]. This group, comprising individuals aged 60 and over whose adult children have migrated away, faces unique difficulties in maintaining health and independence [4–6]. Central to their well-being is their self-care ability—the capacity to independently manage their health and daily needs. Understanding the factors that influence this ability is crucial for developing effective support systems. This study investigates the association between ADL, social alienation, psychological resilience, and self-care ability among China’s rural empty-nest older adults. We propose and test a serial mediation model where the effect of ADL on self-care ability is sequentially mediated by social alienation and psychological resilience.

The association between ADL and self-care ability

ADL encompass fundamental tasks essential for independent living, such as dressing, bathing, and eating. A decline in ADL is directly linked to a diminished capacity for self-care, increased dependency, and a lower quality of life [7]. This link is particularly critical for rural empty-nest older adults who often lack immediate family support [8]. From a theoretical standpoint, Dorothea Orem’s Self-Care Theory posits that self-care is a deliberate, learned practice aimed at maintaining life, health, and well-being [9]. Within this framework, ADL constitutes the foundational capability upon which self-care regimens are built. An individual’s ability to perform these daily tasks is a direct prerequisite for engaging in more complex health management behaviors. Furthermore, Home-Based Care Theory emphasizes the importance of enabling older adults to remain in their familiar environments, where maintaining ADL is key to preserving autonomy and dignity [10]. Research consistently shows that older adults with stronger ADL are better able to manage their daily lives and maintain a higher level of self-care, thereby reducing their reliance on others [11–13]. Therefore, we propose our first hypothesis: H1: The ability to complete ADL is positively correlated with self-care ability.

The mediating role of social alienation

While the direct link between ADL and self-care is clear, psychosocial factors may explain the underlying mechanisms. Social alienation—an individual’s feeling of disconnection from society, often accompanied by a lack of social support—is a significant issue for rural empty-nest older adults [14]. A decline in ADL can exacerbate this issue; as physical limitations increase, older adults may find it harder to participate in community activities, leading to social withdrawal and heightened feelings of isolation [15, 16]. This alienation, in turn, can negatively impact self-care [17, 18]. Environmental Gerontology provides a lens for understanding this, suggesting that an unsupportive environment—one lacking social networks and accessibility—acts as a barrier to health and independence [19, 20]. When older adults feel disconnected from their community, their motivation and capacity for self-care can diminish. This is supported by Self-Determination Theory, which highlights the fundamental human needs for belonging, competence, and autonomy [21]. Social alienation directly undermines the need for belonging, which can reduce an individual’s intrinsic motivation to engage in self-care practices.

The mediating role of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience—the ability to adapt and recover from stress and adversity—is another critical factor [22]. Strong ADL capabilities can foster resilience [23]. According to Self-Efficacy Theory, successfully performing daily tasks enhances an individual’s belief in their own competence (self-efficacy), which is a cornerstone of psychological resilience [24, 25]. Older adults who feel capable and in control of their daily lives are better equipped to cope with health challenges. This heightened resilience has a direct, positive effect on self-care. Resilient individuals are more likely to actively manage their health, seek information, and adhere to treatment plans, thereby demonstrating greater self-care ability. Conversely, a decline in ADL can erode self-efficacy, leading to lower resilience and, consequently, poorer self-care outcomes [26, 27].

The proposed serial mediation model: the study’s novel contribution

Previous research has examined the links between these variables independently. However, there is a gap in understanding how these factors interact within a comprehensive framework, particularly for the vulnerable population of rural empty-nest older adults. This study proposes a novel serial mediation model to address this gap. We hypothesize that ADL’s effect on self-care ability is not only direct but also flows through a sequential psychological pathway.

Specifically, we argue that a decline in ADL initially leads to increased social alienation. This alienation, characterized by a loss of social connection and support, then erodes an individual’s psychological resilience [28, 29]. According to Social Support Theory, the lack of social and emotional resources inherent in alienation depletes one’s capacity to cope with adversity [30]. This weakened psychological resilience ultimately results in a diminished ability to perform self-care. This study is the first to conceptualize and test a model where social alienation and psychological resilience act as serial mediators in the association between ADL and self-care ability in this specific context. By doing so, we aim to provide a more nuanced understanding of the complex mechanisms that shape the well-being of China’s rural empty-nest older adults and offer a theoretical foundation for designing more effective, multi-faceted interventions. Based on this, we propose our second hypothesis: H2: Social alienation and psychological resilience play a serial mediating role in the association between ADL and self-care ability. Methods

Between February 2022 and March 2023, this study employed purposive and convenience sampling to recruit rural empty-nest older adults. Prior to the formal survey, the research team contacted township and village leaders to facilitate participant recruitment based on the established inclusion criteria.

Given the challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic, a hybrid data collection approach was adopted. Data were primarily collected through in-person interviews with appropriate safety precautions. However, in regions with strict lockdown measures or geographical constraints, telephone interviews were conducted as an alternative. This approach ensured the inclusion of participants from remote areas and maintained study continuity.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years as of January 2022; (2) holding rural household registration and residing in the area for at least six consecutive months; (3) living alone or only with a spouse, without co-residing children, and receiving visits from non-co-residing children less than once a week; (4) capable of normal communication and independent survey completion; (5) provided informed consent to participate.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of serious organic diseases (e.g., malignant tumors, major cardiovascular diseases); (2) presence of psychiatric disorders that could compromise study participation; (3) having non-co-residing children who live nearby and can provide immediate care; (4) refusal to cooperate or submission of a low-quality questionnaire; (5) being childless, as this group’s social support structure is distinct and beyond the scope of this study.

A total of 1,100 questionnaires were distributed, and 1,025 valid responses were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 93.2%. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University (Review Number: JZMULL2022017). All participants provided verbal informed consent.

The Activity of Daily Living Scale (ADLS)

The ADLS, adapted from the scale developed by Lawton and Brody in 1969, was used to assess the daily living abilities of participants [31]. The scale comprises two subscales: the Physical Self-Maintenance Scale (PSMS; 8 items) and the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL; 6 items), totaling 14 items. Each item is scored on a 4-point scale from 1 (independent completion) to 4 (completely restricted), with total scores ranging from 14 to 56. Higher scores indicate greater impairment. Based on established cutoffs, scores are categorized as normal (≤ 16), mild decline (16 to < 22), or significant impairment (≥ 22) [32]. The scale has shown good reliability in Chinese populations (Cronbach’s α = 0.811). In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.824, confirming its reliability in our sample.

Social Alienation Scale (SAS)

The 15-item SAS was used to measure feelings of alienation. The scale is divided into four dimensions: social alienation, self-alienation, meaninglessness, and powerlessness. Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 15 to 60. Higher scores indicate greater social alienation.

The SAS has been validated in Chinese samples [33–35]. In our study, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.878. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the subscales were 0.892 (social alienation), 0.912 (self-alienation), 0.860 (meaninglessness), and 0.815 (powerlessness).

Psychological Resilience Scale (PRS)

We used the Chinese version of the PRS, originally developed by Wagnild and Young and subsequently translated and validated by Huang [36, 37]. The scale contains 25 items across five dimensions: equanimity, persistence, self-reliance, meaningfulness, and existential aloneness. Items are rated on a 7-point scale from 1 (complete disagreement) to 7 (complete agreement), with total scores ranging from 25 to 175. Higher scores indicate greater resilience. Scores are categorized as low (< 125), moderate (125–145), or high (> 145). The Chinese version has demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.943).

Self-care Ability in Old Age Scale(SAIOAS)

Self-care ability was measured using a 45-item Chinese scale developed by Pang et al. [38]. The scale was informed by previous work on life satisfaction and has been psychometrically validated in Chinese populations [39–41]. It comprises three dimensions: economic independence, daily living skills, and health self-maintenance. Items are rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all applicable) to 5 (completely applicable), with total scores ranging from 45 to 225. Higher scores indicate stronger self-care ability.

In this study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (overall Cronbach’s α = 0.952). The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.876, 0.922, and 0.939, respectively. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.936, indicating good sampling adequacy for factor analysis.

Covariates

The following variables were included as covariates: gender, age, education level, monthly income, frequency of contact with children, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, self-reported health, and number of chronic diseases. The overall missing data rate for these covariates was 4.5%. The pattern of missingness was assessed and determined to be missing at random (MAR).

Missing data were handled using imputation. For categorical variables (e.g., gender, education), missing values were imputed using the mode. For continuous variables (e.g., age, income), missing values were imputed using the mean. Descriptive statistics for all covariates are provided in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were calculated, with continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the associations among ADL, SA, PR, and SAIOA.

To test the hypothesized chain mediation model, we used the PROCESS macro for SPSS (v4.1, Model 6) developed by Hayes [42]. In the model, ADL was the independent variable (X), SAIOA was the dependent variable (Y), and SA and PR were the first (M1) and second (M2) mediators, respectively. All covariates were included in the model to control for their potential confounding effects.

Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05. Mediation effects were tested using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. A 95% bootstrap confidence interval (CI) that does not contain zero was considered indicative of a significant indirect effect.

To ensure the robustness of our results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. We compared the findings from the primary analysis (using mean/mode imputation) with results from analyses using multiple imputation and listwise deletion. The results were consistent across all methods, suggesting that the findings were not substantially affected by the missing data handling technique.

Results

Common method bias test

To test for common method bias (CMB), we conducted Harman’s single-factor test by performing an unrotated exploratory factor analysis on all items from the four main study variables [43]. The results showed that the first unrotated factor accounted for 11.2% of the total variance, which is below the 40% threshold, suggesting that CMB was not a major concern in this study.

Additionally, to assess multicollinearity among the predictor variables, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each predictor. All VIF values were below the common threshold of 5.0, indicating that multicollinearity was not a significant issue.

Demographic and social characteristics of participants

The demographic characteristics of the 1,025 participants are presented in Table 1. The sample was predominantly female (66.5%). The age distribution showed that 36.9% of participants were over 70 years old, and 24.9% were between 80 and 85 years old. A large proportion of participants had a low level of education, with 46.9% having completed middle school or less. In terms of monthly income, 61.5% earned between 1,000 and 2,000 CNY. Most participants (53.7%) had weekly contact with their children. The majority were married (81.4%), while 18.6% were widowed. Regarding health behaviors, 21.0% of participants were current smokers.

Discussion

This study’s findings confirm that a decline in ADL is significantly associated with reduced SAIOA among rural empty-nest older adults. More importantly, our results reveal that this relationship is not merely a direct consequence of physical decline. Instead, it is substantially channeled through a sequential psychological pathway, where SA and PR act as powerful chain mediators. This discussion will interpret these findings, situating them within existing theoretical frameworks and prior research to elucidate the complex mechanisms at play and provide a foundation for targeted interventions.

The nuanced association between ADL and SAIOA

Our study first confirmed the foundational hypothesis that declining ADL is detrimental to SAIOA. This aligns with a large body of literature that establishes physical independence as a cornerstone of successful aging and self-management [45–47]. When older adults struggle with basic tasks, their capacity to manage their own health is inherently compromised. As noted by Katz et al., the inability to perform daily activities can erode confidence and foster a sense of being overwhelmed, directly diminishing self-management capabilities.

However, a key finding of our study is that the direct association of ADL on SAIOA, while significant, is less pronounced than its indirect association through psychosocial factors. This does not diminish the importance of physical function; rather, it suggests that the primary harm of declining ADL on SAIOA manifests through the resulting social and psychological distress. The physical limitations appear to trigger a cascade of negative psychosocial consequences that are ultimately more damaging to an individual’s capacity for self-care than the physical decline itself. This insight shifts the focus from a purely functional perspective to a more holistic, biopsychosocial model.

The mediating role of SA

Our results identify social alienation as a critical first step in the pathway from ADL decline to poor self-care. A loss of physical ability often restricts social participation, leading to withdrawal and isolation [48, 49]. This finding is particularly salient for rural empty-nest older adults in China, a demographic uniquely vulnerable due to systemic factors such as mass youth migration, weaker community support systems, and limited access to transportation and social activities [50]. In this context, physical decline acts as a catalyst for profound social disconnection.

Theoretically, this finding supports the principles of Environmental Gerontology, which posits that an individual’s environment—including its social dimensions—is a key determinant of health and well-being [6]. A socially impoverished environment, lacking in meaningful interaction and support, becomes a significant barrier to maintaining health behaviors. When older adults feel marginalized or disconnected, their motivation and perceived ability to engage in self-care diminish. This process of alienation, therefore, serves as a powerful mechanism through which physical limitations are translated into a reduced capacity for self-management.

The mediating role of PR

The study also confirms that PR is a significant mediator in the association between ADL and SAIOA. A decline in ADL can directly erode psychological resilience by undermining an individual’s sense of mastery and independence [51]. This aligns with Self-Efficacy Theory, which suggests that confidence in one’s ability to perform tasks is a critical component of resilience [23]. As older adults lose the ability to manage their daily lives, their self-efficacy wanes, leaving them more vulnerable to stress and less capable of adapting to health challenges [52, 53].

Conversely, individuals with higher PR are better equipped to navigate the difficulties associated with aging and health problems, demonstrating greater self-management and proactive health behaviors. Our findings underscore that resilience is not merely an innate trait but a dynamic capacity that is influenced by an individual’s functional abilities. It serves as a crucial psychological resource that, when depleted by physical decline, directly impairs the ability to practice self-care.

The chain mediation: from SA to depleted resilience

The most novel contribution of this study is the validation of the chain mediation model, which demonstrates that SA and PR operate in sequence. The data suggests a compelling narrative: declining physical function leads to SA, which in turn depletes PR, ultimately resulting in diminished SAIOA. This sequential process highlights a critical interaction between social context and individual psychology.

This finding provides strong empirical support for theories such as Thoits’s work on the interplay between social support and psychological well-being. The lack of social integration and support inherent in alienation starves individuals of the external resources needed to bolster internal resilience. For rural empty-nest older adults, who already face a deficit in social capital, this pathway is particularly destructive. They become trapped in a vicious cycle where physical decline leads to SA, and that isolation cripples their psychological ability to cope, leaving them unable to care for themselves effectively. This is further compounded by a loss of life satisfaction, which is closely linked to resilience and proactive coping.

In conclusion, the association of ADL on the SAIOA of rural empty-nest older adults is complex and deeply interwoven with psychosocial factors. Understanding this chain mediation mechanism is crucial for developing effective interventions. Simply addressing physical limitations is insufficient. A comprehensive approach must also focus on mitigating SA and fostering PR. Interventions should aim to rebuild social support networks and enhance coping skills to interrupt the destructive chain that links physical decline to a loss of independence and well-being.

Policy implications

The findings of this study offer several key implications for policy and practice aimed at enhancing the SAIOA and overall well-being of rural empty-nest older adults.

Strengthening Rural Healthcare Infrastructure: Improving access to and quality of rural healthcare is fundamental. This includes not only expanding services through mobile clinics and telemedicine but also ensuring that healthcare professionals receive specialized training in geriatric care. Such measures can directly address physical health limitations and improve ADL, forming the bedrock of self-care.

Fostering Community-Based Support Systems: To mitigate social alienation, it is crucial to develop robust community-based care and support models. Initiatives such as local activity centers, volunteer networks, and intergenerational programs can create a supportive social fabric, providing essential interaction and reducing feelings of isolation.

Enhancing Social Connectivity and Support: Beyond formal programs, efforts should target the enhancement of informal social support networks. This can be achieved by promoting digital literacy to connect older adults with family and peers, facilitating regular social events, and ensuring accessible transportation to reduce geographical barriers to social participation.

Building Psychological Resilience: Given its strong mediating role, interventions designed to build psychological resilience are vital. This includes providing access to mental health services, such as counseling and peer support groups, and promoting wellness programs focused on stress management and positive coping strategies. Empowering older adults with these skills can significantly improve their capacity to maintain self-care.

Addressing Cultural Barriers to Care: Finally, policy interventions must be culturally sensitive. Public awareness campaigns are needed to address the potential stigma associated with seeking formal help, framing these services as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, traditional values of self-reliance.

By adopting a multi-faceted approach that integrates health, social, and psychological support, policymakers can create an enabling environment that empowers rural empty-nest older adults to maintain their independence and enhance their quality of life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. While our findings reveal significant associations, the directionality of these effects cannot be determined. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore the causal pathways among ADL, SA, PR, and SAIOA over time.

Second, the sample was drawn from a single province in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The results may not be representative of all rural empty-nest older adults in China, as regional variations in socioeconomic conditions, cultural norms, and local policies could influence the observed associations.

Third, data collection occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic. This unique context may have influenced the study’s variables. For instance, pandemic-related social restrictions and heightened psychological stress could have temporarily altered participants’ typical levels of social alienation and resilience. Consequently, the findings might reflect circumstances specific to the pandemic era and should be interpreted with this context in mind.

Finally, the reliance on self-report measures means that responses could be subject to social desirability bias or influenced by participants’ transient emotional states. While standard scales were used, future research could benefit from incorporating objective measures or multi-informant reports to corroborate the findings.
