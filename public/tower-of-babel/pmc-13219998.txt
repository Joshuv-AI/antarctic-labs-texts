Early Structural Healing and 12-Month Clinical Outcomes of Atelocollagen Patch Augmentation for Achilles Tendon Repair: A Prospective Cohort Comparison
Authors: Young Hwan Park, Woon Kim, Jeong Ho Son, Hak Jun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchilles tendon rupture (ATR) is a common injury, yet achieving optimal biological healing remains a challenge despite various surgical techniques. This study aimed to investigate the effect of atelocollagen patch augmentation on ATR repair. We hypothesized that atelocollagen patch augmentation would show superior structural and clinical outcomes compared with the standard repair group.MethodsIn this prospective cohort comparison, 17 patients were enrolled to undergo Achilles tendon repair with atelocollagen patch augmentation, while 18 patients receiving standard open ATR repair served as the control group. A comparison of outcomes between the 2 groups was performed. Outcomes were assessed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively via ultrasonography and clinical measures, including the Achilles Tendon Total Rupture Score (ATRS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and isokinetic muscle strength. Ultrasonographic evaluation focused on the cross-sectional area (CSA) and intratendinous morphology of the repaired tendon.ResultsUltrasonographic evaluation revealed that the atelocollagen patch group had a significantly higher CSA ratio at 1 and 3 months postoperatively (p = 0.026 and p = 0.043, respectively), though this difference was not significant at 6 and 12 months. Morphology scores were also higher in the patch group at 3 and 6 months (p = 0.009 and p = 0.027, respectively). Clinical outcomes, including ATRS, VAS pain scores, and isokinetic muscle strength, showed no significant differences between groups at all follow-up points. No complications, such as rerupture, infection, or allergic reactions, were observed in either group.ConclusionsAtelocollagen patch augmentation provides early structural benefits in tendon healing after ATR repair; however, these benefits did not translate into superior clinical functional outcomes within the 12-month follow-up period. Further large-scale studies are warranted to confirm the long-term clinical efficacy and safety of this technique.

Achilles tendon rupture (ATR) is the most common tendon injury involving the ankle joint, with surgical repair being the treatment of choice.12) Reported outcomes after ATR repair vary across studies and are influenced by patient age, rupture site, chronicity, and comorbid conditions.345) Despite advances in surgical techniques and postoperative rehabilitation, some patients with ATR still experience suboptimal outcomes.67)

The percentage of patients with ATR who were unable to return to their prior level of activity is approximately 20%, with reported rates ranging from 0% to 72%.8) Additionally, rerupture rates following surgical repair of acute Achilles Tendon Rupture (ATR) have been observed in 3%–8% of cases.9) These challenges in achieving complete recovery have spurred research into biological and bioengineered scaffolds to improve tendon healing and enhance the effectiveness of ATR repair.

Collagen scaffolds used for tendon injuries provide a supportive environment for cell attachment, migration, differentiation, proliferation, matrix remodeling, and regeneration.10) Among various collagen materials, atelocollagen—a form of collagen treated with proteolytic enzymes to remove terminal telopeptides, reducing immunogenicity and antigenicity—has demonstrated beneficial effects on tendon healing.111213) In a rat ATR model, the atelocollagen sponge patch accelerated tendon healing and showed potential as an option for tendon augmentation during Achilles tendon surgery.14) However, to our knowledge, no clinical studies have evaluated the effectiveness of atelocollagen patch augmentation for ATR.

In this study, we performed a prospective cohort comparison to clinically and ultrasonographically evaluate the effect of atelocollagen patch augmentation in patients undergoing ATR repair. We hypothesized that atelocollagen patch augmentation would show superior clinical and structural outcomes compared with the standard repair group.

METHODS

Study Design

This prospective cohort comparison was conducted on patients included in 2 prospective studies, as the surgical technique, postoperative rehabilitation, ultrasonography examination, and outcome measurement protocols were identical. The study employed a sequential enrollment design; the first 18 patients, treated from October 2017 to July 2018, participated in an ultrasound observation study on tendon recovery after Achilles tendon repair. The subsequent 18 patients, treated from August 2018 to May 2020, participated in a study where an atelocollagen patch was applied during Achilles tendon repair.

Both studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of Korea University Guro Hospital (IRB No. 2017GR0265 and 2018GR0207), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All study protocols adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.15)

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Patients with acute ATR requiring surgical repair were included. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years or > 65 years, previous Achilles tendon surgery, open or bilateral ATR, significant injuries around the ankle joint combined with tendon rupture, and coexisting chronic Achilles tendinopathies. These conditions included those associated with previous quinolone therapy, renal transplantation, steroid use, alcohol abuse, or diabetic polyneuropathy. Patients with peripheral vascular disease, those participating in other studies, and individuals with cognitive disorders that prevented adherence to postoperative rehabilitation protocols or patient-reported outcome assessments were also excluded.

Preoperative Survey

Patient characteristics were assessed prior to surgery, including age, sex, body mass index, injured side, diabetes status, smoking status, preinjury activity level, injury mechanism, and the interval from injury to surgery. Preinjury activity level was evaluated using the ankle activity score, which ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater physical activity.16) Originally developed to assess changes in ankle-related activity in patients with ankle instability, this score has also been applied to evaluate sports activity levels in cases of acute ATR.17)

Interventions

All surgeries were performed under general or spinal anesthesia. After applying a pneumatic tourniquet, patients were positioned in the prone position. A longitudinal skin incision was made over the midline of the ATR site, and the paratenon was carefully dissected while preserving adjacent neurovascular structures. Tendon repair was performed using the Krackow suture technique in a 2-stranded single configuration, with 3 locking loops made along each side of the tendon. Once the core suture was applied, the repair site was augmented with interrupted circumferential sutures.

In the control group, the paratenon was closed, and the skin was sutured and sealed with cyanoacrylate topical skin adhesives. In the atelocollagen patch group, a 50 × 50 × 0.5 mm atelocollagen sponge patch (RegenPatch, Sewon Cellontech Co., Ltd.) was placed and wrapped over the repair site. The patch was secured along its proximal and distal borders with sutures, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The paratenon and skin were then closed in the same manner as in the control group. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon (YHP) throughout the study period.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

A standardized postoperative regimen was applied to all patients, and patient compliance with the protocol was closely monitored and documented by the treating surgeon during each follow-up visit. Immediately after surgery, a short-leg splint was applied with the ankle positioned at 20° of plantar flexion, and patients maintained a non-weight-bearing status for 2 weeks. After this period, partial weight-bearing was allowed with a controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot set at 20° of plantar flexion, which was progressively adjusted to neutral over 6 weeks. By 9 weeks after surgery, the CAM boot angle was increased to 10° dorsiflexion. At this stage, patients gradually discontinued crutch use and wore the CAM boot only for daily activities. At 12 weeks, the CAM boot was removed, and patients began a home exercise program that included double and single heel-raise exercises.

Ultrasonographic Evaluation

Patients were prospectively evaluated at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively by an author (HJK) with 8 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasonography. Using a 9–15-MHz linear-array probe (LOGIQ, GE Healthcare), longitudinal and axial images of the repaired and contralateral tendons were acquired. The maximal sagittal dimension of the tendons was recorded on both sides in the axial plane. Ultrasonographic evaluation of the repaired tendon focused on the cross-sectional area (CSA).18) The CSA ratio at the rupture site was calculated using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PiViewSTAR, Infinitt Co., Ltd.) as the ratio of the CSA of the affected side to the unaffected side, expressed as a percentage (Fig. 2): CSA (affected side) / CSA (unaffected side) × 100 (%).

In addition, the intratendinous morphology of the repaired tendon was assessed using a scoring system for ultrasonographic evaluation of Achilles tendon healing.1819) This scoring system has been previously validated and has demonstrated high reliability and clinical relevance in assessing the structural quality of the repaired Achilles tendon. Residual anechoic tendon defects were categorized as 0%–1% (2 points), 1%–50% (1 point), and 50%–100% (0 points). The intratendinous hyperechoic area at the rupture site was categorized as 0%–25% (3 points), 25%–50% (2 points), 50%–75% (1 point), and 75%–100% (0 points). The continuity of intratendinous fibrillar echo texture was assessed as complete (3 points), incomplete (1 point), or absent (0 points). Peritendinous reactions, such as local edema or effusion, were scored as present (2 points) or absent (0 points) (Fig. 3). Each parameter was assigned a specific score, with a maximum possible score of 10 points, indicating a fully healed tendon. The overall healing status was evaluated based on this cumulative score.

Clinical Outcome Assessment

The primary outcome measure was the Achilles Tendon Total Rupture Score (ATRS), a validated tool specifically designed to assess outcomes in patients with ATR.2021) The ATRS is a patient-reported outcome measure that evaluates challenges experienced during various physical activities due to symptoms. Higher scores indicate better physical function and fewer limitations, with a range from 0 to 100. Additionally, the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain was used as a secondary outcome measure.22) Pain experienced during daily activities was quantified by measuring the distance (in millimeters) between the “no pain” point and the patient-indicated “pain” level on a 100-mm scale, with a range of 0 to 100. Both ATRS and VAS for pain were assessed at 6 months and 12 months postoperatively. Isokinetic muscle strength of ankle plantar flexion was evaluated using a computer-based Cybex dynamometer (CSMI HUMAC Norm) at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. Both ankles underwent 5 cycles of isokinetic plantar flexion at velocities of 30°/sec and 180°/sec. Peak torque and total work were recorded, and the relative deficit in peak torque and total work was calculated for further analysis:23) (Unaffected side − affected side) / unaffected side × 100 (%).

Statistical Analysis

The normality of data distributions was examined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. The CSA ratio and intratendinous morphology scores during follow-up visits were compared using a 2-way analysis of variance with repeated measures for time, and p-values adjusted using the Greenhouse–Geisser epsilon. A per-protocol analysis was conducted. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics for Windows version 23.0 (IBM Corp.), with significance defined as p < 0.05. Post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software, version 3.19 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf). It should be noted that the study was not specifically powered to evaluate differences in complication rates, such as rerupture or infection.

RESULTS

Following the withdrawal of 1 patient from the atelocollagen patch group, 35 patients (17 in the atelocollagen patch group and 18 in the control group) completed the 12-month follow-up period. No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in terms of age, sex, affected side, body mass index, smoking status, diabetes, preinjury activity levels, injury mechanism, or the time interval between injury and surgery (Table 1).

Ultrasonographic Outcomes

The CSA ratio in the atelocollagen patch group was significantly larger than that of the control group at 1 month and 3 months postoperatively; however, no significant differences were observed at 6 months and 12 months postoperatively (Fig. 4). Analysis of the CSA ratio revealed a significant group effect (F[1,33] = 2.94, p = 0.005) and a significant group-by-time interaction (F[2.46, 81.3] = 2.74, p = 0.048).

The initial morphology scores in the atelocollagen patch group were similar to those in the control group. However, at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, morphology scores in the atelocollagen patch group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 5). Analysis of the morphology score revealed a significant main effect of group (F[1,33] = 6.15, p = 0.018) and a significant group-by-time interaction (F[2.27, 74.9] = 3.64, p = 0.026).

Clinical Outcomes

The ATRS and VAS pain scores showed no significant differences between the atelocollagen patch group and the control group at 6 and 12 months postoperatively (Table 2). Similarly, isokinetic muscle strength assessments at 3 and 6 months postoperatively revealed no significant differences in plantar flexion strength deficits between the 2 groups in terms of both peak torque and total work at test speeds of 30°/sec and 180°/sec (Table 3).

Complications

No complications, including rerupture, surgical site infection, or allergic reactions related to surgical repair of ATR or atelocollagen patch augmentation, were observed in either group during the study period.

Post Hoc Power Analysis

A post hoc power analysis was conducted for the CSA ratio and morphology scores to evaluate significant intergroup differences. The analysis indicated a power of 0.99 to detect an effect size of 0.88 for the CSA ratio and 0.74 for the morphology score between the 2 groups, with a type I error probability of 0.05.

DISCUSSION

While there is growing interest in using atelocollagen for ATR repair, there is a lack of clinical studies evaluating the effectiveness of atelocollagen patch augmentation for ATR. To address this issue, we performed a prospective cohort comparison using data from patients who underwent ATR repair with atelocollagen patch augmentation and compared the outcomes with those of a cohort control group. The primary findings of this study indicate that the use of an atelocollagen patch for ATR enhances ultrasonographic evidence of early tendon recovery. However, this study alone cannot determine whether these early ultrasonographic improvements translate to better clinical outcomes. Therefore, although the atelocollagen patch appears promising as an adjunctive treatment to enhance Achilles tendon healing, further clinical evidence is necessary before widespread adoption.

Our findings indicate that atelocollagen patch augmentation provided early improvements in tendon structure, as evidenced by significantly greater morphology scores at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. The atelocollagen patch group also demonstrated a significantly larger CSA ratio in the early postoperative period (1 and 3 months). While an enlarged CSA can sometimes be interpreted as edema or scar formation, in the context of our study, it likely reflects the scaffold effect provided by the atelocollagen patch, which supports early cellular activity and extracellular matrix remodeling. Preclinical studies have shown that atelocollagen enhances cell attachment, migration, and differentiation, which are essential processes for tendon healing.10131424) By creating a scaffold that supports cellular activity and extracellular matrix remodeling, atelocollagen may accelerate the early stages of healing, leading to the improved ultrasonographic parameters observed in this study. However, despite these promising short-term benefits, the CSA ratios and morphology scores between the atelocollagen and control groups did not differ significantly at 12 months, suggesting that the early structural advantage of atelocollagen may diminish over time.

Interestingly, the observed structural improvements in the atelocollagen group did not correspond to significant differences in clinical outcomes, including ATRS and VAS for pain, or isokinetic muscle strength. This discrepancy raises questions regarding the clinical relevance of early structural changes. It is possible that the assessment of ATRS and VAS only at 6 and 12 months may have missed potential short-term functional benefits that might have been more apparent in the earlier stages (e.g., 3 months), correlating with the peak ultrasonographic improvements. Similar findings have been reported in a prospective randomized trial of atelocollagen injection for rotator cuff tears, which demonstrated accelerated healing at the surgical site but no significant differences in functional clinical scores.25) However, another randomized trial for rotator cuff tears reported that atelocollagen injection improved both functional outcomes and tendon integrity.26) These mixed findings suggest that while atelocollagen may contribute to structural tendon improvements, its impact on functional recovery remains uncertain. Further research is warranted to clarify the relationship between structural and clinical outcomes and determine the conditions under which atelocollagen might most effectively enhance both tendon integrity and functional performance.

Despite the clinical results of this study, and in light of the positive results from previous animal experiments, we believe that atelocollagen has potential applications. First, as atelocollagen appears to promote early structural healing, it may be beneficial for high-demand individuals requiring quicker tendon remodeling. Second, given that functional recovery is a multifaceted process, a more comprehensive approach that incorporates various forms or proportions of atelocollagen as an adjunctive treatment may be required to achieve meaningful improvements in both structural and functional outcomes. Lastly, the significant improvements in CSA ratio and morphology scores raise the possibility that atelocollagen might be more beneficial for patients with complex tendon injuries or comorbidities affecting tendon healing, such as diabetes or chronic tendinopathy, where healing capacity may be compromised.

A notable strength of this study is that it is the first to evaluate the effect of atelocollagen patches on human ATR. Additionally, the study utilized ultrasonographic evaluations to monitor structural changes in tendon healing over time. Ultrasonography enabled the assessment of both CSA and morphological characteristics, providing valuable insights into the dynamics of tendon healing. The scoring system employed allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of tendon integrity, revealing early distinctions between the atelocollagen and control groups.

This study has several limitations. First, the study design was non-randomized and utilized sequential patient enrollment, which may introduce potential temporal and performance bias, such as changes in surgical experience or perioperative management over time. Second, it did not include a comparison of postoperative complications, particularly rerupture and infection. These complications, which can occur after surgical repair of ATR, are serious and can lead to poor outcomes and long-term sequelae. Since one of the primary reasons for using biological or bioengineered scaffolds in ATR repair is to prevent rerupture, it is reasonable for surgeons to question whether an atelocollagen patch can lower the rerupture rate. Reported rerupture rates following surgical repair of ATR are approximately 4%–6%, and the incidence of infection is reported to be 2%–3%.2728) Given these low rates, hundreds of patients in each group would be needed to demonstrate a statistically significant difference. Consequently, our study was underpowered to detect meaningful differences in complication rates or clinical outcomes, and the absence of complications in our small cohort should be interpreted with caution. Third, ultrasonographic evaluation of the Achilles tendon was performed by a single examiner without blinding, raising concerns about potential bias. The absence of blinding could lead to subjective interpretation, especially with operator-dependent imaging techniques like ultrasonography. However, the evaluations were conducted by an orthopedic surgeon with expertise in musculoskeletal ultrasonographic imaging, likely minimizing this risk.2930) Additionally, the examiner had no conflicts of interest related to the products used in the study, further reducing the potential for bias. Despite these mitigating factors, future studies could benefit from incorporating blinding and multiple evaluators to enhance the reliability and generalizability of the findings.

In conclusion, while the atelocollagen patch offered an early structural advantage in tendon healing following ATR repair, it did not significantly enhance the functional outcomes within the 12-month follow-up period. Although atelocollagen shows promise as a scaffold for tendon repair, the clinical relevance of early structural improvements remains to be fully elucidated. Further large-scale, randomized controlled trials are required to determine if this adjunct treatment can achieve more evident clinical benefits and to confirm its long-term safety profile.
