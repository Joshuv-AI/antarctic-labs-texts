Causal relationship between gut microbiota and rheumatoid arthritis: A bidirectional 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Shulan Zhang, Yuanzhe Li, Hui Cheng, Yabin Gao, Huan Zhao, Woxing Ren, Wei Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the bidirectional causal relationship between gut microbiota and Rheumatoid arthritis using Mendelian randomization (MR). Using gut microbiota data from the IEU OpenGWAS database (n = 5959, Finnish cohort) and rheumatoid arthritis genetic data from the Finnish database (6236 cases and 1,47,221 controls), forward and reverse MR analyses were conducted with gut microbiota and rheumatoid arthritis as exposure factors and outcomes, respectively. The significance threshold for forward MR was set at 1 × 10−5, and the significance threshold for reverse MR analysis was set at 5 × 10−8. The analysis primarily used inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) methods, supplemented by MR-Egger regression, weighted median methods, simple mode methods, and weighted mode methods. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the reliability of the results. The forward MR analysis indicated that increased abundances of Bacilli (CAG-345 sp000433315; PIVW = .040), Eubacterium_F sp000434115 (PIVW = .031), Fimbriimonadia (PIVW = .022), Christensenellales (GCA-900199385 sp900320755; PIVW = .006), Muribaculaceae (PIVW = .046), Lachnospira rogosae (PIVW = .023), Leuconostoc (PIVW = .025), Provencibacterium massiliense (PIVW = .021), Provencibacterium (PIVW = .039), and Rubneribacter sp002159915 (PIVW = .016) elevated the risk of rheumatoid arthritis, while higher abundances of Actinobacteriota (PIVW = .028), Bifidobacterium breve (PIVW = .007), Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense (PIVW = .009), Oscillospirales (CAG-448; PIVW = .029), and Syntrophomonadia (PIVW = .015) were protective against rheumatoid arthritis. The reverse MR analysis showed that rheumatoid arthritis was associated with increased abundances of 15 types of gut microbiota, including Blautia_A sp900066145 (PIVW = .025), Lachnospiraceae (PIVW = .041), Lactococcus lactis (PIVW = .026), Terrisporobacter (PIVW = .013), and Veillonella (PIVW = .046), and decreased abundances of 17 types of gut microbiota, including Alistipes shahii (PIVW = .032), Alistipes (PIVW = .010), Barnesiellaceae (PIVW = .008), Desulfovibrionaceae (PIVW = .029), and Parabacteroides (PIVW = .027). Cross-analysis did not reveal significant shared gut microbiota between forward and inverse MR analysis. Bidirectional sensitivity analysis validated the reliability of the study results. This study highlights the potential significance of specific gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis, providing new insights for the development of prevention, diagnosis, and therapeutic strategies for rheumatoid arthritis.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a common chronic autoimmune disorder primarily affecting the joints, causing pain, swelling, stiffness, and loss of joint function. It is characterized by aggressive, polyarticular synovitis, typically symmetrically affecting small joints such as the proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal joints. Early symptoms may include joint redness, swelling, warmth, and pain. In advanced stages, it may cause varying degrees of joint deformity and restricted mobility, significantly impairing patients’ physical function and quality of life.[1] The pathogenesis of RA is complex, involving multiple factors including genetics, environment, and autoimmunity.[2,3] Interactions between the immune system and cells exacerbate inflammation and bone destruction.[4] As the exact mechanisms remain unclear, current treatment primarily focuses on symptom control and disease progression delay, without achieving complete cure. Consequently, research into the pathogenesis and treatment of RA remains one of the most pressing challenges in managing this condition.

In recent years, research into the gut microbiota has garnered significant attention. Increasing evidence indicates that gut microbes play a pivotal role in immune regulation and the development of inflammatory diseases.[5] Alterations in the gut microbiota are directly linked to the progression of RA. Compared to healthy controls, RA patients exhibit marked differences in their gut microbiota composition, potentially related to the autoimmune mechanisms underlying RA. In early-stage RA patients, Prevotella abundance is significantly elevated, while Bacteroides and Clostridium species abundance is reduced.[6] During active RA, the abundance of Collinsella and Akkermansia increases, while that of Bifidobacterium decreases.[7] These studies collectively indicate that alterations in gut microbiota are associated with RA pathogenesis. Prevotella species have been identified as a potential trigger for RA and may also influence genetic susceptibility in affected individuals. An interaction exists between the gut microbiome and inflammation in distal joints; gut dysbiosis can modulate inflammatory responses in these joints by affecting immunoregulatory pathways, with this effect being particularly pronounced in RA patients. Gut microbes may directly or indirectly affect joint health by producing inflammatory mediators such as lipopolysaccharides. Lipopolysaccharides generated by certain gut bacteria can reach joints via the bloodstream, activating immune cells like macrophages and T cells, and promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines.

The onset and progression of RA exhibit a close correlation with the gut microbiota. Dysbiosis of the intestinal microbial community may contribute to the pathological process of RA by influencing immune system function.[8] With advances in microbiome research, investigators have increasingly focused on the potential link between the gut microbiome and RA. As studies deepen, scholars are progressively elucidating how the gut microbiome participates in the pathogenesis of RA. Research has demonstrated[9] that the presence of the Prevotella spp. genus within the gut correlates positively with RA risk alleles in individuals without RA manifestations. That is, individuals genetically predisposed to RA may harbor higher levels of Prevotella spp. in their gut microbiota prior to the onset of RA symptoms, suggesting an association between host genotype and pre-disease microbial characteristics. This research underscores the relationship between the gut microbiome and genetic susceptibility to RA, suggesting a potential role for the microbiome in the pre-onset phase of the disease.

Furthermore, RA patients exhibit increased intestinal permeability, which correlates positively with disease severity. This suggests that alterations in intestinal permeability may represent an early event in the pathogenesis of RA and are associated with disease progression. Altered intestinal permeability leads to sustained immune system activation, modifying the immune environment within RA patients. This results in reduced responsiveness of immune cells to treatment, particularly therapies aimed at mitigating inflammatory responses. Concurrently, levels and types of inflammatory mediators may shift due to altered intestinal permeability. These mediators can interfere with drug efficacy, preventing certain medications from effectively suppressing inflammatory reactions.[10] Evidently, gut microbiota and their metabolites exert immunoregulatory effects through multiple pathways, not only modulating intestinal inflammatory responses but also influencing systemic immune status.

Gut microbiota and their metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), play a crucial role in immune regulation and exert significant influence on the pathophysiology of RA. SCFAs modulate the immune system via specific G protein-coupled receptors and by inhibiting histone deacetylases. Activation or inhibition of these receptors and enzymes promotes the proliferation and function of regulatory T cells while suppressing the activity of helper T cells 17. This regulation reduces inflammatory responses in RA.[11] Research indicates that SCFAs also enhance the barrier function of intestinal epithelial cells, reducing the permeability of pathogens and pro-inflammatory molecules.[12] This action is crucial for preventing systemic inflammation and immune activation. The revelation of the gut microbiota and its metabolites’ pivotal role in regulating inflammatory and immune responses further supports the potential for treating RA by modulating the gut environment. However, the relationship between gut microbiota and RA remains largely based on observational studies or animal experiments, making it difficult to establish clear causality.[13] Specifically, observational studies are susceptible to residual confounding and reverse causation. Moreover, there is a lack of systematic investigations covering the entire spectrum of gut microbial taxa. Given the potential of the gut-joint axis for therapeutic intervention, robust causal evidence from human genetic studies is urgently needed to validate specific microbial targets.

Mendelian randomization (MR), as an analysis method based on genetic variation, effectively overcomes confounding factors and reverse causality interference in traditional observational studies, providing a powerful tool for exploring potential causal relationships.[14] By employing genetic variants associated with gut microbiota as instrumental variables, MR analysis holds promise for revealing whether a genuine causal link exists between gut microbiota and RA.

This study employs MR analysis to comprehensively investigate potential causal relationships between 473 gut microbial species and RA. This research not only contributes to a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of RA but may also provide crucial theoretical foundations for developing novel diagnostic methods and therapeutic strategies for the condition.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

In this study, a 2-sample bidirectional MR analysis method was used to assess the causal association between gut microbiota and RA using pooled statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). First, gut microbiota was used as the exposure factor and RA as the outcome for forward MR analysis. Subsequently, RA was used as the exposure factor and gut microbiota as the outcome for reverse MR analysis. Sensitivity analysis was implemented to ensure the reliability of the analysis results. Finally, through cross-analysis, gut microbiota with bidirectional effects were identified. This study must satisfy the following 3 core instrumental variable assumptions: the instrumental variable is significantly correlated with gut microbiota; the instrumental variable is unrelated to confounding factors; the instrumental variable is unrelated to the outcome variable. 2.2. Data sources

The aggregated data on the human gut microbiota were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database, which includes 473 gut microbiota species, a total of 5959 samples, and 79,37,635 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).[15] The genetic data for RA comes from the Finnish database Finn-B-M13_Rheuma. This dataset contains a total of 1,53,457 samples, including 6236 patients with RA and 1,47,221 controls, with a total of 1,63,80,169 SNPs detected.[16] This study was a 2-sample MR analysis based on publicly available GWAS summary statistics. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained in the original studies from which the data were derived. Hence, further ethical review or informed consent was not applicable for the current secondary analysis.

2.3. Instrumental variable screening

Gut microbiota data are publicly available in the IEU OpenGWAS directory, with ID numbers ranging from GCST90032172 to GCST90032644. Among these, ebi-a-GCST90032348, ebi-a-GCST90032412, ebi-a-GCST90032474, ebi-a-GCST90032510 do not exist, so a total of 469 gut microbiota were included. The screening criteria were P = 1 × 10−5, r2 = 0.001, and Distance = 10,000 kb. The screening criteria for the reverse MR analysis were P1 = 5 × 10−8, r2 = 0.001, and distance = 10,000 kb.

To eliminate bias caused by weak instrumental variables, this study set the F-statistic of instrumental variables to be >10 (F > 10), where the F-statistic is used to quantify the strength of the association between instrumental variables and the exposure factor. A higher F-value indicates a stronger correlation of the instrumental variable, a lower possibility of weak instrumental variables, and can effectively reduce MR analysis bias caused by the weak association between instrumental variables and the exposure.

2.4. Mendelian randomization analysis

MR analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR software package. For proteins with a single instrumental variable, the Wald ratio method was used to estimate the change in log odds of RA risk per one-standard-deviation increase in gut microbiota levels represented by the instrumental variable.[17] For gut microbiota with multiple instrumental variables, the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was used to obtain the MR effect estimates.[18] To account for potential pleiotropy, additional analyses were conducted, including simple models, weighted models, weighted medians, and MR-Egger regression. MR-Egger results were only used when the MR-Egger intercept indicated the presence of level multieffectivity.[19] P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Reverse MR analysis was also conducted using the aforementioned methods. To control for type I error inflation due to multiple testing in the large-scale analysis, false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to the nominal P-values from MR analyses.[20] A significant causal association between gut microbiota and RA was defined as an FDR < 0.1, while a suggestive association was defined as a nominal P-value < .05 from the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method despite an FDR ≥ 0.1.[21]

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using MR-Egger regression; a significant intercept term indicates the presence of horizontal pleiotropy.[22] Heterogeneity among the SNPs was assessed with Cochran’s Q test; a statistically significant result (P ≤ .05) indicates substantial heterogeneity.[23]

We further evaluated horizontal pleiotropy using the MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) test, identified and excluded outliers, and then repeated the analysis with the remaining instrumental variables.[24] Using the leave-one-out method, assess whether causal associations are determined by a single SNP.[25]

The strength of association between human gut microbiota and RA is expressed as an odds ratio (OR) with its 95 % confidence interval (CI); a P-value < .05 is considered indicative of a potential causal relationship.

2.6. Statistical software versions

All MR analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) with the TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO packages.

4. Discussion

4.1. Correlation between gut microbiota diversity and susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis

This study evaluated the association between gut microbiota and susceptibility to RA using a 2-sample bidirectional MR analysis. Positive MR analysis identified 15 gut microbiota species: increased abundance of Bacillales, Eubacterium, Fimbriomonas, Christensenellales, Muribaculaceae, Leptospira, Mycobacterium, Provencibacterium massiliense, Provencibacterium, and Rubnerella spp. increases the risk of RA; An increase in the abundance of Actinobacteria, Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense, Vibrionaceae (CAG-448), and Syntrophomonadia has a protective effect against the onset of RA. Reverse MR analysis identified 32 gut microbes: RA is associated with increased abundance of the genera Mucorales, Mucorales (CAG-180 sp000432435), Spirochaetales, Spirochaetales (CAG-882 sp003486385), Streptococcus family, Peptostreptococcus family (GCA-900066495 sp900066495), Peptostreptococcus family (GCA-900066495), Lactococcus lactis, Massiliomicrobiota sp002160815, Massiliomicrobiota, Terrisporobacter othiniensis, Terrisporobacter, Ruminococcus (UBA7177 sp002491225), Ruminococcus family (UBA7177), and Veillonella genus increased in abundance; while the abundance of Shigella, Anameconia genus, Barnesiidae family, Barnesiidae genus, Butyricibacter genus, Vibrionales order (CAG-448 sp000433415), Spirillaceae (CAG-81 sp000435795), Secondaria, Desulfovibrionaceae, Desulfovibrionales, Desulfovibrio, Bacteroides, Phocea massiliensis, Thermophiles, Bacteroidetes (UBA11471 sp000434215), and Bacteroidetes (UBA11471) decreased.

4.2. Causal relationship between specific gut microbiota and rheumatoid arthritis risk

The gut microbiota can influence host immunity, while the immune system can in turn shape the gut microbiota through maintenance of the intestinal barrier and immune exclusion.[26] In both pre-clinical individuals and patients with established RA, the composition of the gut microbiota is markedly altered, suggesting that the gut microbiome may serve as a pivotal trigger for RA onset.[27] The forward MR analyses in this study demonstrated suggestive associations between specific gut microbes and the risk of RA, while the reverse MR analyses revealed that RA may, in turn, exert reciprocal effects on the gut microbiota.

The interplay between the gut microbiota and RA is intricate. Studies have found that the abundance of Eubacterium is increased in RA patients, potentially influencing the pathological course of RA through its involvement in key amino-acid metabolic pathways and immunomodulatory actions.[28,29] Alterations in the abundance of Muribaculaceae are also related to the pathological status of RA. Arsenic trioxide can partially reverse the gut-microbiota composition: Muribaculaceae included – and modulate its metabolic functions in RA mouse models, thereby exerting therapeutic effects on RA.[30] Notably, case reports have documented that the prolonged coexistence of RA and tuberculous arthritis may induce gastrointestinal amyloidosis, thereby compromising the intestinal barrier and increasing the risk of Leuconostoc-mediated infection.[31] Moreover, a consistent finding in the gut microbiota of RA patients is a marked reduction in Bifidobacterium abundance, an alteration that may be mechanistically linked to the pathogenesis of RA.[32] In addition, a reduction in the number of bifidobacteria (Bifidobacterium) is commonly observed in the gut microbiota of RA patients, which may be related to the pathogenesis of RA. Bifidobacterium, a member of the phylum Actinobacteria, is widely regarded as a probiotic that contributes to intestinal health and immune regulation.[33] Bifidobacterium breve, a species within the Bifidobacterium genus, exerts several potentially beneficial effects on RA through mechanisms that include immunomodulation, anti-inflammation, antioxidation, and bone protection, all of which may help alleviate the symptoms and slow the progression of the disease.[11,34]

On the other hand, RA may itself influence the gut microbial community. Evidence indicates that the abundance of Blautia_A (formerly known as mucin-degrading Eubacterium) is significantly higher in RA patients compared with healthy controls.[35] Blautia_A (formerly mucin-degrading Eubacterium) abundance correlates with disease activity, being significantly higher in ACPA-positive RA patients.[36]As key metabolites of gut microbiota, SCFAs are critical for immune regulation and profoundly impact RA pathophysiology. By activating specific G protein-coupled receptors and inhibiting histone deacetylases, SCFAs modulate the immune system-promoting regulatory T cell proliferation and function while suppressing helper T cell 17 activity-to alleviate RA-related inflammation.[11] They also strengthen the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, thereby mitigating the penetration of pathogens and pro-inflammatory molecules-an effect critical to averting systemic inflammation and immune activation.[12] In patients with autoimmune diseases such as RA, the relative abundance of specific taxa – including Eubacterium-may be altered; these bacteria participate in short-chain fatty acid production and subsequently influence disease progression.[37,38] The role of Lachnospiraceae in disease is complex, and its altered abundance in RA patients may be influenced by multiple factors. Some studies have observed an increased abundance of Lachnospiraceae in the gut microbiota of RA patients, which may be associated with the inflammatory milieu and immune dysregulation.[39-41] However, in some cases RA patients exhibit reduced Lachnospiraceae abundance, and this decrease has been linked to potential anti-inflammatory effects.[42] Studies also suggest that Peptostreptococcaceae may be involved in the development and progression of several autoimmune diseases, with shifts in its abundance associated with alterations in specific metabolites.[43] Previous studies have shown that RA can induce a marked increase in the abundance of Ruminococcaceae, a change that may be linked to the inflammatory status associated with the disease.[44] Elevated levels of Veillonella in RA patients have been associated with pro-inflammatory responses.[45-47] Lactococcus lactis, a probiotic species, holds promise for both preventing and treating RA; it attenuates arthritis progression by strengthening intestinal mucosal immunomodulation,[48] and its relative abundance in the gut is likewise altered in RA. In addition, studies have reported that the abundance of Alistipes may be reduced in RA.[49] Evidence from Pianta et al further links specific gut microbes to RA pathogenesis: peptides derived from Butyricimonas share sequence homology with the RA autoantigen FLNA, and epitopes within sulfatase proteins of Parabacteroides exhibit significant homology with peptides from the RA autoantigen GNS. These molecular mimics may activate T cells and thereby promote autoimmune responses implicated in RA development.[50] A recent MR study also revealed inverse associations between RA and both Desulfovibrionaceae and Desulfovibrionales, suggesting that their abundances are likely diminished in RA patients.[51]

Recent 2-sample MR studies have explored the causal links between gut microbiota and RA. Zhou et al[52] analyzed 211 gut microbiota and identified 13 gut microbiota associated with RA, while Wu et al[53] further performed subtype-stratified MR and revealed RA subtype-specific microbial associations. Our findings are consistent with these studies in supporting a causal gut-RA axis, but differ in critical aspects. Notably, no complete overlap was observed in specific significant microbial taxa (genus/species) between our study and the 2 published reports, with only taxonomic associations at higher ranks shared across studies. Besides, our study implemented a bidirectional MR framework, providing reverse causal evidence that RA influences gut microbial composition. The discrepancies in specific associated taxa may be attributed to several factors. First, our study included 473 gut microbial taxa, considerably more than the 211 gut microbial taxa analyzed in previous reports, enabling detection of more granular species- or genus-level taxa not identified in earlier investigations. Second, differences in study populations and microbial annotation pipelines across studies may also contribute to the observed inconsistencies.

4.3. Strength and limitations

The strength of this study lies in the assessment of bidirectional causality between gut microorganisms and RA using MR methods. A notable advantage is the high homogeneity of the study population: both the gut microbiota GWASdata and the RA outcome data were derived from the Finnish population, which effectively minimizes population stratification bias and significantly enhances the reliability and validity of our causal estimates. By applying multiple analytical approaches and rigorous sensitivity analyses, we identified statistically significant associations between specific gut microbial taxa and RA, which provides a valuable genetic basis for further exploring the functional role of the gut microbiota in RA pathogenesis.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations remain. First, although our MR analysis supports a potential causal link between gut microbiota and RA, we acknowledge that the observed associations cannot fully exclude the influence of alternative biological pathways or unmeasured confounders beyond the core exposure-outcome relationship. While we performed MR-Egger regression, leave-one-out analysis, and weighted median methods to minimize residual bias, these approaches cannot entirely rule out non-genetic confounding factors or indirect effects through intermediate biological pathways. Further experimental and mechanistic studies are therefore required to validate these findings and clarify the precise biological processes underlying the observed gut-RA relationships. Second, the GWAS data used in this study were primarily derived from European populations, and the generalizability of our results to other ethnic populations remains to be determined.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study explored the causal relationship between gut microbes and RA using a 2-sample bidirectional MR approach. The forward MR analysis revealed a suggestive association between specific gut microbes and RA, indicating that certain gut microbial taxa may be associated with an increased or decreased risk of RA. In contrast, the inverse MR analysis identified a potential causal effect of RA on gut microbial composition, suggesting that RA may actively modulate the gut microbiome. Collectively, these findings not only provide novel insights into the bidirectional interactions between the gut microbiome and RA but also offer new perspectives for the etiological research and clinical treatment of RA.
