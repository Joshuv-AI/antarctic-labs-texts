Addressing bioenergetic deficits and restoring mitochondrial health in transplanted islets using mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Rosita Primavera, Abantika Ganguly, Reza Yarani, Lili Bettencourt, Shobha Regmi, Shashank Chetty, Jing Wang, Michele Guindani, Ramasamy Paulmurugan, Avnesh S Thakor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the process of islet transplantation, islets undergo isolation and are then introduced into a new microenvironment where they need to adapt and revascularize. This transition imposes substantial stress on islets, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, adversely affecting islet function and vitality. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) offer a promising therapy to mitigate these adverse effects by restoring their bioenergetic capacity and overall functionality of islets. Our study investigates how human MSCs (hMSCs) from different sources—adipose tissue (AD), bone marrow (BM), and umbilical cord (UC)—can restore the bioenergetic capacity of inflamed pancreatic islets. We assess how hMSCs can improve islet survival, function, and mitochondrial health, by analyzing key mitochondrial oxidative stress-related genes. In addition, this study examines the functional effect of co-transplanting islets with hMSCs in vivo in diabetic mice. hMSCs, particularly those from UC and BM, can effectively sustain islet viability, enhance mitochondrial function, and alleviate oxidative stress, as evidenced by increased expression of key mitochondrial markers such as PPARGC1A, TOMM20, and Sod2. Furthermore, co-transplantation of hMSCs with sub-therapeutic islet numbers leads to long-term improvements in glucose regulation, with UC-hMSCs showing better short-term efficacy (100% of diabetic mice receiving islets with UC-hMSCs recovered their blood glucose levels back to normal at 4 weeks post-transplantation, vs. 80% of those transplanted with BM-hMSCs, 60% with AD-hMSCs, and 16% for islets alone). These findings highlight the potential of hMSC-therapies for islet transplantation, underscoring the need to select the optimal MSC source to maximize the therapeutic outcome of transplanted islets.

Islet transplantation can restore glycemic control in diabetes, but early graft failure remains common because isolated islets experience inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction before revascularization. Here, we show that co-culture and co-transplantation with human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) improves islet survival, preserves function, and restores mitochondrial health under inflammatory stress. Importantly, therapeutic efficacy depends on hMSC source: umbilical cord– and bone marrow–derived hMSCs most effectively reduced oxidative injury, increased mitochondrial markers, and accelerated restoration of normoglycemia in diabetic mice (100% normoglycemia by 4 weeks with UC-hMSCs vs 16% with islets alone). These results support rational selection of MSC source to optimize cell-based adjunct therapies for islet transplantation.

Introduction

Pancreatic islets are clusters of endocrine cells (α, β, δ and PP cells) that continuously produce hormones.1 Given their high metabolic activity for protein synthesis, processing (ie, translocation to the ER, folding, and quality control), and secretion (ie, vesicle transport, fusion, and exocytosis), islets have high bioenergetic requirements compared to other cells. In islet β-cells, mitochondria play a pivotal role in regulating glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)2–4; as glucose uptake increases, mitochondrial metabolic pathways are activated leading to enhanced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation.5,6 Islet transplantation involves the infusion of pancreatic islets into the portal vein of the liver to preserve glucose homeostasis.7 This can be done in the allogeneic setting for the treatment of diabetes or in the autologous setting for patients with chronic pancreatitis (CP) who have severe refractory abdominal pain and impaired quality of life, and require a total pancreatectomy (TP).8–10 Unlike allogenic transplants where islets are retrieved from a healthy pancreas, patients with CP have pancreases that are very scarred and fibrotic from being constantly inflamed. As a result, islets from these pancreatic glands have compromised health and function at baseline and are thus even more susceptible to further injury from the isolation process (ie, use of proteolytic enzymes and multiple rounds of centrifugation).11,12 Moreover, islets in both settings must engraft in the liver, which has a relatively hypoxic microenvironment that puts further stress on islets, as well as exposing them to an instant blood-mediated inflammatory reaction (IBMIR), involving the activation of thrombotic and complement pathways.13–15 Taken together, it is not surprising that islets isolated for transplantation have metabolic and physiological dysfunction, that ultimately affects their long-term function (ie, impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion)2 and viability.

A promising strategy for supporting and restoring dysfunctional islets in the context of islet transplantation involves the use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as a cell-based therapy.16–18 MSCs are self-renewable, multipotent, and non-hematopoietic progenitor cells with a favorable clinical safety profile and no reported risk of tumor formation. This safety profile is attributed to their tendency not to differentiate following administration, but rather to persist in situ, where they can regenerate surrounding tissues and cells through the release of anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, anti-fibrotic, pro-angiogenic, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory factors into their surrounding microenvironment.19 Recently, MSCs have been shown to possess the capability to rescue dysfunctional islets by restoring the bioenergetic capacity of islets via the transfer of healthy mitochondria between donor MSCs and recipient stressed islets.20 The process of mitochondrial transfer is believed to be an active response triggered in MSCs by damage signals, potentially involving changes in cell surface phosphatidylserine domains or other unidentified soluble factors.21–23

MSCs can be sourced from various tissues, including bone marrow (BM-MSCs), adipose tissue (AD-MSCs), and umbilical cord (UC-MSCs).24 Numerous pre-clinical studies have demonstrated the beneficial use of MSCs in both cell culture and animal models of islet transplantation.25–29 In addition, several clinical trials are registered to investigate the safety and efficacy of using MSCs in the context of islet transplantation (www.clinicaltrials.gov, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00646724, NCT03835312, NCT06751199) and even in the setting of patients with chronic pancreatitis (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05095532, NCT02384018). While numerous pre-clinical studies and clinical trials have shown promising results with MSCs in islet transplantation, the overall findings are inconsistent, and there is no clear evidence to support a single optimal source of MSCs. Continued research and exploration are therefore necessary to elucidate the most effective approach for utilizing MSCs in the context of islet transplantation.

Hence, the goal of this manuscript is to evaluate the capacity of MSCs from different sources and assess their ability to restore the bioenergetic capacity of inflamed/stressed islets. Here, we have examined the impact of hMSCs on pancreatic islet survival, function, and mitochondrial health under inflammatory conditions in vitro. Additionally, we have explored whether different sources of hMSCs exhibit varying effects on enhancing mitochondrial health in islets exposed to such cytokines, especially with respect to analyzing the expression of genes related to mitochondrial function, including PPARG coactivator 1 alpha (PPARGC1A), human translocase TOMM20 (TOMM20), as well as genes associated with oxidative stress, such as superoxide dismutase 2 (Sod2). Lastly, we have assessed the functional impact of hMSCs on restoring blood glucose homeostasis after their co-transplantation with islets in diabetic mice. In a syngeneic transplantation model, we investigated the expression of Ppargc1a in the explanted graft tissues at 7- and 90- days post-transplantation to understand the support provided by hMSCs to transplanted islets.

Methods

Reagents

Histopaque (R)-1077, histopaque (R)-1119, heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), propidium iodide (PI), fluorescein diacetate (FDA), D-glucose, collagenase from clostridium histolyticum, penicillin/streptomycin, accutase, and streptozotocin (STZ) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, Missouri, USA). Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) without phenol red and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from GIBCO (Invitrogen Corporation, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) without calcium and magnesium, and low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Minimal Essential Medium (DMEM) were obtained from Hyclone (Logan, Utah, USA). All other products and reagents were of analytical grade.

Animals

All animal studies were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC 33855 - Regenerative Effect of MSCs or MSCs-Derived Extracellular Vesicles in the Treatment of Type-1 Diabetes (T1D)) of Stanford University. Healthy, 8-10 weeks old, male C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, USA) were used as donors of pancreatic islets. Diabetic, 6-8 weeks old, male C57BL/6 mice, were used as recipients. Animals were made diabetic via an intraperitoneal injection of STZ at the single dose of 180 mg/kg to induce diabetes.30 Recipient animals were considered diabetic and ready for islet transplantation when their non-fasting blood glucose levels (BGLs) were higher than 350 mg/dL, for 3 consecutive days post-STZ injection. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment at the animal facility at the Canary Center at Stanford University, California.

Pancreatic islet isolation

Pancreatic islets were isolated following established procedures.28,31 Briefly, mice were initially anesthetized with isoflurane (3%-4% in oxygen via inhalation) and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, an abdominal incision was made to expose the pancreas and common bile duct. Hemostat clamps were applied to either side of the small intestine, and the pancreas was infused with 3 mL of cold collagenase solution via the bile duct. After removal from the body, the pancreas was immersed in a vial containing 2 mL of collagenase for 10 min before termination of enzymatic action using HBSS with 10% FBS. Islets were then filtered through a 500-mesh strainer and purified via centrifugation (1000 ×g, 30 minutes, 4 °C) using Histopaque solutions at two density gradients (1.077 g/mL and 1.119 g/mL). Following centrifugation, the islet layer was carefully identified and separated to obtain pure islets, discarding any remaining debris. Purified islets were subsequently centrifuged in media to obtain a pellet. Culturing of the islets was performed in RPMI-1640 medium without phenol red, supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 2 mmol/L L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, under humidified conditions with 20% O2 and 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Individual islets were then manually counted and picked under a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ745, California, USA) for both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Human MSC culture and co-culture with pancreatic islets

Human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) used in this study were obtained from StemBioSys, USA (https://www.stembiosys.com/), at passage (P)1 and characterized by the minimal criteria defined by the International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT).32 MSCs were obtained from three different sources: adipose (AD), bone-marrow (BM) and umbilical cord (UC) tissue. hMSCs at low passages (P3-P6) were used for all in vitro and in vivo experiments. All hMSCs were expanded in vitro using the same culture conditions to ensure standardization. In brief, hMSCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% (v/v) FBS, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The medium was changed every other day, with removal of non-adherent cells. When hMSCs reached 80% of confluency, the cells were detached using accutase and pelleted down by centrifugation (300 ×g for 5 min). Cells were then counted using the trypan blue exclusion method and added to mouse pancreatic islets (1 islet:1000 hMSCs). All experiments were performed using a trans-well system to co-culture islets with hMSCs that were seeded onto the top insert of the culture plate forming a confluent monolayer of cells within 12 h. Islets were collected by handpicking and then seeded on the bottom chamber of the trans-well system. Medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 without phenol red, supplemented with L-glutamine, 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin, and 2% (v/v) of FBS, with or without the cocktail of pro-inflammatory cytokines for 48 h. The cocktail of pro-inflammatory cytokines included 156 ng/mL interferon-γ (IFN-γ), 25 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and 0.046 ng/mL of interleukin 1β (IL-1β).28,33 After 48 h, islets were collected and subjected to the subsequent experiments outlined below.

Islet function

Islet function was assessed using a glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) assay, following previously established protocols.28 In brief, islets were pre-incubated for 48 h with or without hMSCs (± cytokines) as outlined above. Subsequently, 25 islets were transferred into 0.6 mL microfuge tubes and incubated at 37 °C in Krebs buffer (137 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4-7H2O, 2.5 mM CaCl2-2H2O, 24.9 mM NaHCO3) supplemented with bovine serum albumin (0.05% w/v) and containing 2.8 mM (D)-glucose for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Following this, the buffer was replaced with Krebs buffer containing either 2.8 mM (low glucose) or 28 mM (high glucose). The islets and buffer were separated by centrifugation (100 ×g for 3 min), and the supernatant was assayed for insulin content using an ELISA immunoassay (Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For normalization, the total DNA content of the islets was quantified using a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Molecular Probes, Eugene, USA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Stimulation indices were calculated as the ratio of insulin secretion in response to high glucose concentrations (28 mM glucose) to that in response to low glucose concentrations (2.8 mM glucose).

Metabolic assessment

Reactive oxygen species production

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using a fluorometric assay employing 2′,7′-Dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA; Sigma–Aldrich). DCFH-DA is a cell-permeable non-fluorescent probe which is hydrolyzed by intracellular esterases, thus becoming trapped within the cell. Upon oxidation by ROS, DCFH-DA is converted into fluorescent dichlorofluorescin (DCF). The intracellular fluorescence intensity is directly proportional to the amount of intracellular ROS production within a linear dynamic range. Islets pre-incubated for 48 h, with or without hMSCs (± cytokines), were treated with DCFH-DA (20 µM final concentration) and incubated for 1 hour. The fluorescence emitted by DCF was subsequently measured using a microplate spectrophotometer system with excitation (ʎex) and emission (ʎem) wavelengths at 485 nm and 535 nm, respectively. Fluorescence intensities were normalized to the respective DNA contents, which were quantified using a Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Molecular Probes, Eugene, USA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Nitrite production

Media was collected from islets pre-incubated for 48 h with or without hMSCs (± cytokines). Nitrite levels within the media were measured using the nitric oxide assay kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Nitrite levels were detected as a colored azo dye product of the Griess reaction that absorbs visible light at 540 ± 20 nm.

ATP production

ATP content in islets pre-incubated for 48 h, with or without hMSCs (± cytokines), was quantified using CellTiter-Glo assay kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA). The experiment was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, islets were seeded in opaque-walled 96-well plate (25 islets/well) in 100 µL of culture medium. An equal volume of CellTiter-Glo reagent was then added to each well. The plate was mixed for 2 min on an orbital shaker to induce cell lysis. Luminescence was recorded using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M1000) after 10 min incubation period to stabilize luminescence signal.

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit

MSCs (2.0 × 10^5 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber of a 96-well transwell plate (pore size: 8 µm) in 100 µL of DMEM supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution and 10% (v/v) FBS. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C in humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Once the desired cell density was achieved, mouse islets were added to the lower compartment at a ratio of 1:1000 in an inflammatory cocktail (RPMI media supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution and 2% (v/v) FBS containing cytokines: 156 ng/mL of IFN-γ, 25 ng/mL of TNF-α, and 0.046 ng/mL of IL-1β) and incubated for 48 h. Two control groups with healthy islets and inflamed islets alone were also assessed. After the incubation period, the upper wells were removed, and the islets were stained using the following protocol: the culture media was removed and replaced with the JC-1 reagent working solution, which was then incubated in a 37 °C cell culture incubator for 20 minutes. Following incubation, the reagent was removed, and the wells were washed once with culture media. The red and green fluorescence intensities were measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 550/600 nm and 485/535 nm, respectively, using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M1000). Specifically, green fluorescence indicates low mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) when JC-1 exists as monomers, suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction. In contrast, red fluorescence occurs when JC-1 aggregates in healthy, polarized mitochondria, indicating proper mitochondrial function and a high ΔΨm. Finally, the ratio of red fluorescence to green fluorescence was calculated.

RT-qPCR

mRNA expression levels of the target genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis function and transfer such as mouse and human PGC1alpha (Ppargc1a and PPARGC1A), mouse SOD2 (Sod2), and human TOMM20 (TOMM20) were measured by RT-qPCR of the homogenized islet samples. Islet samples were obtained after pre-incubation of the islets, with or without hMSCs (± cytokines), for 48 h. Specifically, mRNA was extracted using RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, Germany) and RT-qPCR for gene expression analysis was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit. Primers were procured from Integrated DNA Technologies, and GAPDH was used as an internal control in mice while GUSB was used as internal control for humans. Primer sequences are given in Table S1.

In vivo pancreatic islet transplantation

Diabetic mice were randomly assigned into 4 experimental groups, with each receiving a transplant of 175 islets only (ie, a sub-therapeutic dose of islets)34,35 or with 1 million hMSCs into the subcapsular space of the left kidney [group1: islets only (islets only); group 2: islets with AD-hMSCs (islets + AD); group 3: islets with BM-hMSCs (islets + BM); group: 4 islets with UC-hMSCs (islets + UC)]. Diabetic mice that did not receive a transplant, as well as those receiving only transplant of 1 million hMSCs were included as additional controls [refer to Supporting information for more details]. For this experiment, we used 5 animals for each experimental group.

Blood glucose, body weight monitoring, and fasting intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test

Following islet transplantation, non-fasting BGLs and body weight measurements were performed in conscious mice daily for 90 days. BGLs were measured with a glucose meter (Bayer Contour Glucose Meter, USA) from a drop (∼ 3 µL) of blood collected from the tail vein of the mice. In addition, an Intraperitoneal Glucose Tolerance Test (IPGTT) was performed at 30, 60, and 90 days post-transplant. Specifically, mice were fasted overnight (approximately 14 h) and then BGLs determined before a solution of glucose (2 g/kg) was administered by intra-peritoneal injection. After glucose injection, BGLs were measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. The results were reported as the BGLs at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min or AUC0-120min, which indicates the area under the curve from time 0 to 120 min.

Histology and molecular analysis of tissue and serum

Grafts (kidney containing islets with and without hMSCs) were excised at 7- and 90-days post-transplantation. Sections of the grafts were harvested and then fixed in 10% formalin to preserve their structure and prevent degradation. After fixation, the grafts were dehydrated with graded ethanol solutions and embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissues were then sliced into thin sections using a microtome (6-8 µm). Some of the tissue sections were used for histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses to determine islet structure and viability (Hematoxylin and Eosin [H&E], and insulin staining) via standard procedures. For H&E staining, the nuclei of the tissues were stained with hematoxylin (purple/blue), and the cytoplasm and the extracellular matrix were counterstained with eosin Y (pink), providing general tissue morphology. Anti-insulin antibody (Polyclonal Guinea Pig Anti-Insulin antibody, supplied by Dako) and goat anti-Guinea Pig (supplied by abcam) were used as primary and secondary antibodies for insulin staining. DAB staining was used to enhance the visualization of insulin-positive cells, providing a brown chromogenic reaction at the site of antibody binding. The stained sections were imaged using a NanoZoomer slide scanner 2.0-RS (Hamamatsu, Japan) using FIJI Image J software. The remaining grafts excised at 2-, 7-, and 90-days post-transplantation were collected and stored at −80 °C before being homogenized for RT-qPCR analysis of the target genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis (ie, Ppargc1a), as well as cytokines involved in the inflammatory response triggered by tissue injury during the transplantation process.

Lastly, blood was collected from the tail vein of mice at 90 days post-transplantation. After collection, whole blood was left undisturbed for 20 min at room temperature to allow the blood to coagulate. Serum was then isolated via centrifugation at 2400 x g for 3 min. The insulin level in serum was determined using a mouse insulin ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden). Additionally, serum samples were utilized to evaluate the immunoreactivity of hMSCs post-transplantation in vivo. Briefly, cell membranes were extracted from hMSCs derived from various sources, including AD-hMSCs, BM-hMSCs, and UC-hMSCs. These extracted cell membranes were subsequently exposed to the serum of diabetic mice that had received transplants of islets combined with hMSCs. A detailed experimental outline of the immunoreactivity assessment and the corresponding results are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S10).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis for this study was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.1) and R (version 4.4.0). Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Ordinary one-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) was performed to analyze the differences among groups and a post-hoc Tukey test for evaluating pairwise comparisons between groups. Additionally, for analyzing data with repeated measures, a mixed-effects model with linear splines was employed to account for the temporal correlation within the data. Linear splines allow for different slopes for different time intervals of the data, capturing changes in trends more flexibly, also as a function of each experimental condition (effect modification). This approach effectively captures the dynamics of the system being studied by incorporating both fixed and random effects and allowing for changes in trends at specified points.36,37

Results

Source-dependent effects of hMSCs in facilitating islet survival and function

To explore the efficacy of hMSCs, pancreatic islets were exposed to inflammatory cytokines for 48 h, both with or without hMSCs. Using a live/dead assay, we assessed islet viability and observed a higher number of irregular inflamed islets in control conditions (ie, inflamed islets alone) compared to those islets co-cultured with hMSCs. Interestingly, when inflamed islets were cultured with hMSCs, the majority of cells within the islets exhibited green fluorescence, indicating viability, while only a few displayed red fluorescence (Figure 1A). This suggests a significantly higher survival rate of inflamed islets in the presence of hMSCs from the three different sources tested, compared to inflamed islets alone (84.6 ± 3.2% for inflamed islets with AD-hMSCs, 90.0 ± 5.9% for inflamed islets with BM-hMSCs, and 91.8 ± 3.0% for inflamed islets with UC-hMSCs vs. 69.2 ± 11.2% for inflamed islets alone) (Figure 1A and B). In addition, insulin release in response to high glucose levels was substantially higher in inflamed islets cultured with BM-hMSCs and with UC-hMSCs compared to the same islets exposed to low glucose levels (stimulation index: 5.1 ± 1.8 and 5.0 ± 1.6 for inflamed islets cultured with BM-hMSCs and with UC-hMSCs, respectively vs. 1.6 ± 0.8 for inflamed islets alone). On the contrary, AD-hMSCs were not able to fully rescue islet function (stimulation index: 2.6 ± 0.8) (Figure 1C). These findings suggest that hMSCs can enhance islet viability and function in inflammatory conditions, particularly when using BM-hMSC and UC-hMSC as sources, which have a greater protective effect.

Source-dependent effects of hMSCs in reducing oxidative stress and re-energizing islets

To investigate the potential impact of inflammatory conditions on oxidative status in islets and the protective effect of hMSCs, the generation of total ROS by inflamed islets with or without hMSCs was assessed. A significant increase (1.9 ± 0.5-fold change) in total ROS levels was observed in islets exposed to cytokines compared to healthy islets. However, when hMSCs were co-cultured with islets, they exhibited a suppressive effect on the total ROS level in inflamed islets. This effect was significant when islets were cultured with BM-hMSCs and UC-hMSCs (Figure 2A). To investigate whether co-culture of islets with hMSCs affect inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induction, nitrite levels—the end product of iNOS-derived nitric oxide—were analyzed. Nitrite levels in islets increased with cytokines and were reduced by co-culture with AD-, BM-, or UC-hMSCs (Figure 2B). The ATP levels were also recovered in inflamed islets cultured with hMSCs, whereas a significant drop in ATP levels was seen in inflamed islets cultured alone (Figure 2C). Our results indicate that the reduction in ROS and nitrite levels in islets appear to have an impact on ATP levels, specifically they are inversely correlated (Figure 2D). Additionally, direct measurement of the JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential in islets reveals a reduced ratio of red to green fluorescence in inflamed islets compared to healthy islets. This effect is less pronounced in inflamed islets that are co-cultured with hMSCs suggesting the protective hMSC effect (Figure 2E).

Source-dependent effects of hMSCs for enhancing mitochondrial health in islets exposed to pro-inflammatory cytokines

To delve deeper into the mechanisms underlying the role of MSCs in islet physiology and pathophysiology after exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines, we investigated the expression of key regulators of mitochondrial function and energy metabolism in pancreatic islets. Inflamed islets cultured with UC-hMSCs and BM-hMSCs showed significantly higher level of PGC1alpha and TOMM20 mRNAs compared to islets treated with inflammatory cytokine cocktail (no signal detected) (Figure 3A and B). Likewise, Ppargc1a mRNA level increased significantly when islets were cultured with BM-hMSCs and with UC-hMSCs compared to inflamed islets alone (Figure 3C). Furthermore, no significant increase was observed when islets were cultured with AD-hMSCs. In addition, to confirm the pro-oxidative shift in islets induced by cytokines and the hMSC response, we assessed the islet antioxidant response following cytokine exposure. Our findings revealed that cytokine exposure induced a significant overexpression of Sod2 mRNA in inflamed islets cultured with UC-hMSCs and with BM-hMSCs compared to inflamed islets cultured alone. Notably, this effect was particularly pronounced with BM-hMSCs, showing a statistically significant increase compared to inflamed islets alone (Figure 3D). Our transcriptomic analysis also confirms that BM-hMSCs and UC-hMSCs have greater gene expression of TOMM70A, TOMM5, TOMM20, TOMM40, TOMM22 and COX5A, COX7A1, COX7B, COX7C, COX8A compared to AD-hMSCs, which, in turn, are essential for the import of proteins into mitochondria and are crucial for mitochondrial function and cellular metabolism (Figure S4).38

Source-dependent effects of hMSC on supporting transplanted islets

The impact of hMSCs on islet function and survival in vivo was then assessed using an STZ-induced diabetic mouse model, following the experimental timeline outlined in Figure 4A. Representative images captured at the time of the graft removal (ie, 90 days post-transplantation) depict islets (visible as circular white spots) distributed within the sub-capsule space of kidneys, the designated site of transplantation in this model. Notably, the pictures show better survival of islets co-transplanted with hMSCs compared to those transplanted without hMSCs (Figure 4B). Non-fasting blood glucose levels (BGLs) in mice receiving islets co-transplanted with hMSCs were significantly lower compared to those transplanted with islets alone, with efficacy varying depending on the hMSC source (Figure 4C-G, Table S12). Specifically, in animals co-transplanted with islets with UC-hMSCs, BGLs were restored more rapidly compared to those transplanted with BM-hMSCs or with AD-hMSCs (observed at 10, 36, and 73 days post-transplantation, respectively). At 4 weeks post-transplantation, all diabetic mice receiving a transplant of islets with UC-hMSCs had BGLs restored to normal levels (100%), whereas only 80% of those transplanted with BM-hMSCs, 60% with AD-hMSCs, and only 16% of mice transplanted with islets alone achieved normal level (Figure 4H, Table S14). These percentages increased over time, reaching 96%, 92%, and 40% of animals receiving a transplant of islets with BM-hMSCs, with AD-hMSCs, and islets alone, respectively, becoming normoglycemic at 12 weeks post-transplantation. Similarly, an increase in blood serum insulin levels was found in mice transplanted with islets with hMSCs compared to mice transplanted with islets alone (Figure S6). In addition, all animals showed body weight gain over the 90 days post-transplantation period (Figure 4I).

Accordingly, to study the ability of mice transplanted with islets in regulating BGLs in response to a glucose challenge, IPGTTs were performed on short- and long-term islet recipients at 7, 30, 60, and 90 days post-transplantation. Mice co-transplanted with islets and hMSCs displayed enhanced responses following intraperitoneal glucose bolus injection (2 g/kg), similar to profiles observed in healthy mice (Figure 4J). The elevated blood glucose concentrations in recipients were restored to the normal range within 2 h. Similarly, the AUC0-120min of mice receiving a co-transplant of islets with hMSCs was lower compared to diabetic mice transplanted with islets only (Figure 4K). Notably, a more robust response was evident with UC-hMSCs at 7, and 30 days post-transplantation, while the response became less dependent on the cell source at longer time points (60 and 90 days) (Figure S7, Figure 4K).

Representative H&E and insulin IHC images showed the arrangement and distribution of transplanted islets within the subcapsular space of the kidney at 7 and 90-days post-transplantation (Figure 5A). Islets transplanted alone displayed an elongated morphology and smaller size within the kidney sub-capsule at 7 days post-transplantation. Conversely, pancreatic islets co-transplanted with AD-hMSCs and with BM-hMSCs sources appeared with a more round or oval shape. Also, islets appeared pale under DAB staining for insulin. In contrast, pancreatic islets co-transplanted with UC-hMSCs demonstrated a more clearly delineated circular or oval morphology, as early as 7 days post-transplantation. To elucidate the source-dependent effect of hMSCs on supporting transplanted islets, we investigated the expression of Ppargc1a (PGC1alpha) within homogenized explanted graft tissues at 2, 7 and 90 days. In line with BGLs data, a significant upregulation of Ppargc1a expression was observed within the graft tissue of mice receiving the transplant of islets with UC-hMSCs at 2 days, and with BM-hMSCs and with UC-hMSCs at 7 days compared to those receiving islets alone, or islets with AD-hMSCs. Notably, grafts of islets transplanted with UC-hMSCs showed higher expression of Ppargc1a compared to grafts transplanted with BM-hMSCs. (Figure 5B, and 5C) However, at 90 days post-transplantation, significant overexpression of Ppargc1a was detected within the graft tissue of mice receiving transplants of islets with AD-hMSCs, BM-hMSCs and UC-hMSCs compared to those receiving the transplants of islets alone, with BM-hMSCs showing the strongest effect among the sources (Figure 5D).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether hMSCs from different origins have differential responses in supporting the function and survival of transplanted islets. Our findings reveal that UC- and BM-hMSCs effectively sustain islet viability and insulin secretion in vitro by mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and enhancing antioxidant defenses. In addition, co-transplantation of hMSCs with a sub-therapeutic dose of pancreatic islets improve islet engraftment and survival in vivo, with these animals demonstrating improved glucose regulation over the long-term and enhanced responses following an intraperitoneal injection of glucose. Notably, a more robust response was evident with UC-hMSCs in the shorter-term (≤ 30 days), while the response became less dependent on the cell source in the longer term (≥ 60 days).

The bioenergetic homeostasis of pancreatic islets in their normal physiological state involves intricate processes that facilitate glucose sensing and insulin secretion. In this state, islets efficiently utilize glucose to generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria, which ensures proper insulin secretion to maintain glucose homeostasis.39 However, in conditions of pathophysiological stress such as inflammation, hypoxia, or isolation, mitochondrial dysfunction occurs which results in reduction of the cellular bioenergetic capacity and the ability to produce ATP at basal levels (ie, reduced ability to maintain glucose homeostasis) and when stimulated (ie, reduced ability to respond to glucose fluctuations). Hence, if not enough ATP is produced by these mitochondria to meet the energy requirements of islets, this subjects cells to experience an ATP crisis which ultimately results in cell death.5,40 Furthermore, dysfunctional mitochondria impair the regulation of intracellular calcium levels (which are critical for insulin secretion) and increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which, in turn, poses challenges for β-cells that are highly susceptible to oxidative stress.41,42 As a result, islet isolation and further transplantation can cause metabolic dysfunction, leading to reduced insulin sensitivity and impaired response to glucose changes. This is demonstrated by the attenuated insulin secretion observed upon exposure of inflamed islets to elevated glucose levels in vitro, as well as the lack of a response following intraperitoneal administration of a glucose bolus in mice transplanted with islets alone.

MSCs offer a promising avenue for restoring islet bioenergetics through various mechanisms. Primarily, their paracrine effects involve the secretion of factors such as growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which promote islet survival, function, and regeneration.27,43 Second, MSCs can donate mitochondria to pancreatic islets through tunneling nanotubes, gap junction signaling, and EV secretion, which will bolster their bioenergetic capacity.20 Our data are consistent with a model in which hMSCs improve islet bioenergetics by modulating the oxidative environment and mitochondrial pathways, as indicated by upregulation of PPARGC1A (PGC-1α) and reductions in ROS and nitrite, together with increased ATP in islets. Ppargc1a is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism; its upregulation can promote mitochondrial activity and ATP production, while MSCs’ anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects can further support energy production and islet viability. However, these findings are correlative and rely on indirect measures of mitochondrial function. Definitive demonstration of restored mitochondrial health will require high-resolution bioenergetic profiling (ie, multi-condition Seahorse respirometry), which we plan to pursue in future studies.

The source of MSCs strongly influences their ability to restore islet bioenergetics, due in part to differences in their secretome, immunomodulatory properties, tissue-specific, and proliferative characteristics.24,44 Our data indicate that when islets were cultured or transplanted with UC-hMSCs or BM-hMSCs, there was a notable enhancement in mitochondrial function as evidenced by the increased expression of mouse Ppargc1a. In addition, higher levels of human PPARGC1a and TOMM20 in mouse islets suggest donor-derived mitochondrial components being present in these islets, though this observation is qualitative and does not allude to the transfer mechanism or functional incorporation of these components in the host cells (Figure 3); future studies employing time-lapse imaging with labeled donor mitochondria and tracer-based approaches will be able to provide direct evidence of donor-to-host mitochondrial transfer to further validate this finding. Nevertheless, a reduction in oxidative stress, indicated by decreased ROS and nitrite levels, and an increase in Sod2 mRNA expression, was observed (Figure 3). Overall, these results suggest that UC-hMSCs and BM-hMSCs exhibit superior efficacy in improving islet bioenergetics compared to AD-hMSCs.

Our transcriptomic analysis (Figure S4)38 confirmed that BM-hMSCs and UC-hMSCs have greater expression of COX and TOMM genes compared to AD-hMSCs. Specifically, TOMM70A, TOMM5, TOMM6, TOMM40, TOMM20, and TOMM22 are all genes associated with mitochondrial import machinery, particularly in the translocation of proteins into the mitochondria. COX4, COX5, COX6, COX7, and COX8 are integral components of cytochrome c oxidase, which is also known as complex IV in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Additionally, the NDUFA4 gene exhibits higher expression levels in UC-hMSCs compared to both AD-hMSCs and BM-hMSCs. NDUFA4, also known as NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] 1 alpha subcomplex subunit 4, is a gene that encodes a subunit of NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase, which is also known as complex I and is another component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (Figure S4). Overall, these genes are essential for efficient mitochondrial function and cellular energy production.

Moreover, hMSCs also exert immunomodulatory effects, suppressing inflammation and immune responses that can be detrimental to islet function in both autologous and allogeneic islet transplantation, potentially improving graft survival by suppressing immune responses.45–47 Transplantation of islets with UC-hMSCs resulted in a more rapid restoration of BGLs compared to those transplanted with BM-hMSCs and AD-hMSCs, with diminishing dependence on the cell source over time. This favorable outcome for UC-hMSCs at shorter time points may be attributed to their stronger immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties compared to those of BM-hMSCs or AD-hMSCs (Figure S9). Furthermore, our immunogenicity data indicate that UC-hMSCs have lower immunogenicity compared to AD-hMSC and BM-hMSC, despite their high proliferation rate (Figures S1 and S10).

Lastly, in our study, we employed a syngeneic animal model, transplanting islets between genetically identical animals. While this model offers advantages, including reproducibility and controlled genetic backgrounds, it also presents limitations in accurately reflecting human disease conditions, transplant site, and integrating human cells effectively. For instance, we chose the kidney capsule as transplant site for our study,48 due to the fact that this location facilitates hMSCs to be in close proximity with islets. The close proximity between islets and hMSCs allow interactions, potentially mediated by mechanisms like tunneling nanotubes or gap junctions, along with paracrine modulation of the microenvironment, are pivotal for favoring islet survival. However, translating this setup to humans presents significant challenges, as the kidney capsule is unlikely to be used as a transplant site in humans.49 The liver is the conventional site for islet transplantation due to its robust vascularization, accessibility, immune privilege, and supportive environment for islet survival and function, crucial for efficient insulin release. However, ensuring close proximity between islets and MSCs after injection into the portal vein remains challenging and hence for these effects to remain, islets would need to be co-transplanted with hMSCs. Future investigations will aim to explore alternative transplant sites,50 such as the omentum or subcutaneous space, in animal models given these sites can facilitate close interaction between islets and MSCs, potentially through bioengineered platforms or through co-encapsulation strategies, thereby optimizing the beneficial effects of hMSCs. Additional assessments will test MSCs from multiple donors of the same source to account for potential donor variability given the inherent variability in MSC populations, stemming from donor heterogeneity and isolation methodologies. Overall, these endeavors aim to refine and optimize MSC-based therapies for enhanced efficacy and reliability as findings are translated from preclinical investigations to clinical implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, these findings highlight the potential of MSC therapies, particularly UC-hMSCs and BM-hMSCs, as a promising avenue to modulate islet bioenergetics and oxidative stress, which may contribute to improved islet resilience in the transplantation setting; however, definitive clinical implications will require additional mechanistic validation in translational studies that specifically examine direct assessment of mitochondrial transfer coupled with high-resolution bioenergetic profiling.
