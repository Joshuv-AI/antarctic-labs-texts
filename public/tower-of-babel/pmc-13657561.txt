Introduction

Pure gonadal dysgenesis (PGD) is a rare condition characterized by phenotypically female individuals with nonfunctional gonads, leading to primary amenorrhea and absent secondary sexual characteristics[1]. While 46,XX PGD is less commonly reported than the 46,XY type, which accounts for more than one-third of cases in clinical practice, it is crucial to recognize the condition[2,3]. Although gonadal malignancies such as gonadoblastomas and dysgerminomas are frequently observed, the development of malignant germ cell tumors, such as yolk sac tumors (YSTs), in PGD is exceedingly rare, with a risk of less than 5% in 46,XX PGD and 30–40% in 46,XY PGD[4,5]. Case presentation

History and examination

A 27-year-old unmarried female presented with a 2-week history of acute lower abdominal pain and an enlarging abdominal mass. She had primary amenorrhea, with no symptoms of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism or acne. The patient had no significant past medical or surgical history and no history of galactorrhea, visual disturbances, or pituitary disorders. There was no family history of delayed puberty, gonadal disorders, or malignancies. No consanguinity was reported. The patient had primary amenorrhea since adolescence but had not undergone prior endocrine evaluation. No hormonal therapy, including estrogen replacement or cyclic progesterone therapy, had ever been initiated. She had not received any prior imaging or gynecologic assessment before the current presentation. She had never been started on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) before this presentation. Physical examination revealed underdeveloped breast tissue (Tanner stage II) (Fig. 1) and normal axillary and pubic hair distribution. Abdominal examination revealed a firm, non-mobile, non-tender mass corresponding to a 24-week gestation size. Investigation, diagnosis, and management

Laboratory investigations showed elevated serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, consistent with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism and low estrogen levels. Serum testosterone was within the normal female range, and thyroid function tests were unremarkable. Karyotyping revealed a 46,XX karyotype (Fig. 2). Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was >2000 ng/mL, CA-125 was >1000 U/mL, the LDH level was 555 U/L, and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin was within normal limits. An abdominal and pelvic ultrasound confirmed a well-defined, solid-cystic adnexal mass with heterogeneous echogenicity and internal vascularity. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a large, heterogeneous, predominantly solid right ovarian mass (Fig. 3). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a large abdominopelvic mass measuring 10.7 × 17 × 10 cm, with areas of hemorrhage abutting surrounding structures, and a thickened mesentery and omentum with nodular deposits (Fig. 4). A small, infantile uterus measuring 4 × 1 × 1.8 cm was noted, and bilateral ovaries could not be visualized. Ascitic fluid was negative for malignant cytology. Based on the clinical presentation, imaging findings, and tumor markers, an initial diagnosis of gonadal dysgenesis with a mixed germ cell tumor was made, with the major components suspected to be YST and dysgerminoma.

The patient underwent a staging laparotomy with a total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Intraoperative findings confirmed a large right ovarian tumor measuring 20 × 20 cm that had ruptured on its inferior and posterior aspects, releasing a fleshy, friable mass into the abdominal cavity (Fig. 5). The tumor was adherent to the omentum, sigmoid colon, pelvic portion of the ureter, and the infundibulopelvic ligament (Fig. 6). Multiple variable-sized deposits were noted on the omentum (the largest measuring 3 × 2 cm), and a 4 × 2 cm mass was infiltrating the sigmoid colon (Fig. 7). The uterus was infantile, and the left ovary was very small (less than 1 cm) and fibrous. Figure 5.Intraoperative findings of a ruptured ovarian tumor. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of a YST with the presence of typical Schiller-Duval bodies and a dysgenetic gonad (Fig. 8). The patient expressed significant anxiety about the abdominal mass and her fertility potential prior to surgery. Postoperatively, following counseling about the diagnosis, expected treatment outcomes, and long-term hormonal therapy, her psychological status improved, with better acceptance of the condition and adherence to follow-up. The postoperative period was uneventful. A pelvic drain was placed and removed on postoperative day 3. Postoperative analgesia included intravenous opioids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). No intraoperative or postoperative complications were noted. The patient was discharged on the 10th postoperative day. She received four cycles of the bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin (BEP) chemotherapy regimen, initiated 4 weeks after surgery. At follow-up, tumor marker levels showed a downward trend, and the patient’s condition had significantly improved. Figure 8.Histopathological report of the YST showing a Schiller–Duval body (arrow).

The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting comprising gynecologic oncology, pathology, radiology, endocrinology, and medical oncology specialists. Treatment decisions, including the surgical approach and adjuvant chemotherapy, were guided by MDT consensus. The patient remained under MDT follow-up during treatment.

Discussion

YSTs arising in 46,XX PGD are extremely rare, with fewer than 20 cases reported in the literature to date. Most reported cases present with smaller tumors and intact gonads, whereas our patient presented with a massive 20 cm tumor with rupture and omental deposits. Diagnostic challenges in resource-limited settings such as Nepal include limited access to advanced genetic testing, delayed presentation due to poor health-seeking behavior, and restricted availability of fertility preservation options. These factors contribute to diagnostic delays and more advanced disease at presentation.

Genetic and molecular mechanisms in PGD

The genetic basis of PGD is complex, involving numerous genes critical for early gonadal development[7,8]. Mutations can disrupt this process, leading to the formation of nonfunctional streak gonads. Genes implicated in 46,XX PGD include FOXL2, NR5A1, WNT4, BMP15, and BRCA2, among others[7,9,10]. While sporadic cases occur, a significant percentage of 46,XX PGD cases are associated with autosomal recessive inheritance[11]. The absence of functional gonads results in elevated gonadotropins (FSH and LH) due to a lack of ovarian feedback, a hallmark of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, which in turn leads to failed development of secondary sexual characteristics[1].

Yolk sac tumors in PGD

YSTs are aggressive malignancies typically arising in dysgenetic gonads, which are prone to neoplastic transformation[5,12]. However, most germ cell tumors arising in PGD are gonadoblastomas or dysgerminomas; YSTs are exceptionally rare in this context[5,13]. The median age of diagnosis for YST is around 19 years[14]. The pathogenesis of YST in PGD remains unclear, but prolonged, unopposed gonadotropin stimulation may promote abnormal germ cell proliferation. In this patient, markedly elevated gonadotropins (FSH and LH) with low estradiol confirmed hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, consistent with previous reports of 46,XX PGD. Furthermore, molecular studies have identified specific genetic and epigenetic alterations in YSTs, such as hypermethylation of the RUNX3 gene promoter and expression of the transcription factor GATA-4, although their specific roles in the context of PGD are still under investigation[15,16].

Imaging and diagnostic challenges

Imaging plays a key role in diagnosing both the extent of gonadal dysgenesis and any associated tumors. In this case, ultrasound and MRI were critical in identifying the large adnexal mass and the associated anatomical features of PGD, such as an infantile uterus and non-visualized ovaries. Despite underdeveloped breast tissue, the patient had normal axillary and pubic hair, reflecting preserved androgen function of adrenal origin. These findings, combined with tumor markers, established the diagnosis. According to the FIGO 2014 staging system for malignant ovarian germ cell tumors, the presence of omental deposits and tumor extension to the sigmoid colon correspond to Stage IIIC disease. The significantly elevated AFP level is a hallmark of YSTs and was instrumental in differentiating this malignancy from other gonadal tumors[14].

Management and treatment

The cornerstone of treatment for malignant germ cell tumors in PGD patients is surgical resection[13]. A staging laparotomy with hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy provides both diagnostic and therapeutic confirmation. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Total operative time was approximately 140 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 350 mL. A pelvic drain was placed and removed on postoperative day 3. Postoperative analgesia included intravenous opioids and NSAIDs. No intraoperative or postoperative complications were noted. Postoperatively, adjuvant chemotherapy with the BEP regimen is standard of care for YSTs and has shown favorable outcomes[14,17,18]. Following cancer treatment, long-term HRT is essential to induce and maintain secondary sexual characteristics, support bone and cardiovascular health, and improve overall well-being[1].

Prognosis and long-term follow-up

The prognosis for YST has improved significantly with effective chemotherapy[17]. However, long-term surveillance is crucial and involves regular clinical evaluation, imaging, and monitoring of serial tumor markers, such as AFP. Higher-stage tumors and elevated AFP levels are associated with a poorer prognosis[14]. Management must also include fertility counseling and psychological support.

The prognosis of malignant ovarian germ cell tumors, including YSTs, has significantly improved in recent decades due to the introduction of centralized multidisciplinary team (MDT) care. MDT management ensures standardized staging, coordinated surgical and chemotherapeutic planning, and structured long-term surveillance, all of which contribute to improved survival outcomes. Regular follow-up with tumor markers such as AFP, along with radiological monitoring, is essential because recurrence often occurs within the first 2 years. Long-term endocrine follow-up and lifelong HRT are also required in patients with 46,XX PGD to optimize bone, cardiovascular, and reproductive health.

Limitations

This report is limited by short-term follow-up and the lack of fertility-preserving options due to advanced disease at presentation. Hormonal and psychological long-term outcomes could not be assessed within the available follow-up period.

Conclusion

This rare case of a YST in a patient with 46,XX PGD highlights the importance of considering malignancy in individuals with PGD who present with abdominal masses. Following the completion of chemotherapy, long-term estrogen-progesterone HRT is essential in 46,XX PGD to maintain bone health, cardiovascular protection, and the development of secondary sexual characteristics. Early diagnosis, followed by surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy, is critical for a favorable outcome. Continued research is needed to better understand the pathophysiology of germ cell tumor development in PGD to refine management strategies for these complex cases.
