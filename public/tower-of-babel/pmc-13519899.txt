Sex and Chronic Stress Alter the Distribution of Corticotropin‐Releasing Factor Receptor in Rat Hippocampus Following Oxycodone Conditioned Place Preference
Authors: Dana N. Silberstein, Zoha Baig, Batsheva Rubin, Fangmin Yu, Joshua Kogan, Jason D. Gray, Yan Zhou, Teresa A. Milner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCorticotropin‐releasing factor receptor 1 (CRFR1) plays a role in stress‐induced hippocampal plasticity that can affect learning and memory processes including those important for addiction. Our prior work revealed sex differences in the distribution of CRFR1 within CA3 pyramidal cells and hilar interneurons in rats that could differentially affect associative learning processes in females and males, especially in response to chronic immobilization stress (CIS). Here, we examine the effect of oxycodone (Oxy) conditioned place preference (CPP) in unstressed and CIS adult female and male rats on the subcellular distribution of CRFR1 in CA3 pyramidal cells and dentate gyrus (DG) hilar interneurons using immuno‐electron microscopy. After Oxy CPP, males compared to females had greater plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR1 in CA3 pyramidal cell and DG interneuron dendrites, consistent with a greater binding capacity for corticotropin‐releasing factor (CRF) in males. CIS alone resulted in increases in corticotropin‐releasing hormone receptor 1 (Crhr1) mRNA in the pyramidal cell layer and stratum oriens of CA3 in males. Following CIS and Oxy CPP behavior, only females acquired CPP. Oxy CIS females compared to Oxy CIS males had more total CRFR1 in DG interneuron dendrites. Moreover, CRFR1 trafficked from the cytoplasm toward the plasmalemma in DG interneuron dendrites in CIS Oxy females, indicating a potential greater binding capacity for CIS Oxy females in this neuronal population. These findings along with prior studies suggest that changes in CRFR1 and opioid receptor trafficking in CIS females contribute to mechanisms that are associated with the development of Oxy CPP in CIS females, but not males.

Introduction

Genetics, environmental contributions like stress, and multidrug use are all major factors in the development of opioid dependence and addiction in people (Sinha 2007; Strang et al. 2020), making it difficult to isolate the influence of sex on opioid addiction processes. The rate of overdose involving heroin and synthetic opioids such as fentanyl has risen dramatically in both sexes, though rates have risen faster in women (Scholl et al. 2018; Strang et al. 2020; VanHouten et al. 2019). Women are more prone to relapse due to stress or depression (Becker et al. 2017) and show varying sensitivity to morphine through the menstrual cycle (Ribeiro‐Dasilva et al. 2011). Therefore, sex‐specific neurobiological factors may differentially contribute to opioid addiction vulnerability.

Neural circuits critical for associative memory formation and motivational incentives are primary targets of drugs of abuse, with hippocampal connections to the mesolimbic reward system being heavily involved (Koob and Volkow 2010). The hippocampus is a site of contextual associative learning important in drug craving and relapse (Hyman and Malenka 2001; Nestler et al. 2002; Volkow et al. 2006), where spatial memory is influenced by opioid signaling in the CA3 region (Kesner and Warthen 2010; Meilandt et al. 2004).

Besides clinical studies, animal studies demonstrate that females express higher sensitivity to craving and rates of relapse than males (Elman et al. 2001; Zlebnik 2019). To further support the involvement of hormonal state, estrogen fluctuations through the estrous cycle affect heroin self‐administration behaviors in rats (Lacy et al. 2016). Additionally, a form of opioid‐mediated long‐term potentiation (LTP) in mossy fiber (MF)–CA3 pyramidal cell synapses is present in female rats in proestrus (i.e., high estrogen states) but not in male rats or female rats in diestrus (i.e., low estrogen states) (Harte‐Hargrove et al. 2015).

Prior work from our lab shows that naïve female and male Sprague–Dawley rats acquire conditioned place preference (CPP) to mu opioid receptor (MOR) agonist oxycodone (Oxy) (Randesi et al. 2019; Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018). With these behavioral findings, anatomical analyses primarily utilizing immuno‐electron microscopy revealed sex differences in the distribution of opioid‐related peptides, MORs, and delta opioid receptors (DORs) within the hippocampal circuits in a way that heightens female sensitivity to excitation and opiate associative learning processes more than in males (Bellamy et al. 2019; Chalangal et al. 2022; Dolgetta et al. 2022; Mazid et al. 2016; Ryan et al. 2018). Both endogenous enkephalins and opiates like Oxy inhibit gamma‐amino butyric acid (GABA)‐ergic interneurons of the MF pathway projecting from the dentate gyrus (DG) hilus to CA3 through the activation of MORs and DORs (reviewed in Chalangal et al. (2022)). This signaling disinhibits CA3 pyramidal neurons, affecting their intrinsic excitability important for promotion of LTP (Drake et al. 2007; Harte‐Hargrove et al. 2015; McEwen and Milner 2017; Williams et al. 2011). Sex‐specific patterns also emerge in the expression and protein density of plasticity and stress markers, as well as related kinase activity (Randesi et al. 2019; Reich et al. 2019).

Chronic immobilization stress (CIS) primes the hippocampal opioid system for Oxy‐associated learning in female, but not male rats (Reich et al. 2019). CIS females, but not CIS males, acquired Oxy CPP and exhibited a rearrangement of opioid peptides and receptors in a manner that could promote opioid‐related learning (Reich et al. 2019). Moreover, after chronic stress, male rodents exhibit impaired spatial learning and memory (Luine et al. 2007; McEwen 1999; McEwen and Milner 2007; Sousa et al. 2000) and dendritic retraction in CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites (McEwen et al. 2016). These findings suggest differences in hippocampal adaptation to chronic stress between the sexes.

Corticotropin‐releasing factor (CRF)‐mediated hippocampal neuroplasticity processes are important for learning and memory (Blank et al. 2002). CRF released in the hippocampus excites synapses and enhances synaptic efficacy (Chen et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2000; Wang et al. 1998). However, chronic stress can impair this CRF‐mediated plasticity and memory function (Chen et al. 2012; Regev and Baram 2014; Smith and Vale 2006). CRF also is deeply implicated in craving and relapse following prolonged drug abstinence (Brown et al. 2009; Logrip et al. 2011; Shalev et al. 2010), processes which involve the hippocampus (Almela et al. 2018). Thus, CRF can contribute to driving addiction process (Haass‐Koffler and Bartlett 2012; Koob and Zorrilla 2010; Williams and Milner 2011).

Within the hippocampus, opioid and CRF systems are interwoven and can be modified by hormonal factors and chronic stress (Haass‐Koffler and Bartlett 2012; Williams and Milner 2011). CRF and DORs are colocalized in interneurons throughout the hippocampus, especially those containing somatostatin (SOM) (Haass‐Koffler and Bartlett 2012; Williams and Milner 2011). Although CRF/DOR interneurons occur with equal frequency in proestrus females and males, proestrus (high estrogen levels) females have greater numbers of CRF terminals that lack DORs (Williams and Milner 2011). These findings suggest that DORs are positioned to regulate CRF peptide release from interneurons, but their ability to exert such regulation may be compromised in females with elevated estrogen states.

CRF receptor (CRFR) signaling can be modulated by DORs through cyclic AMP‐phosphokinase A pathways (Williams et al. 2011). In rat hippocampus, CRFRs are positioned to interact with DORs in a sex‐specific manner in several opioid circuits. Additionally, CRFRs and DORs are colocalized in CA1 and CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites (McAlinn et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2011). In proestrus females compared to males, CRFR/DOR dual‐labeled CA1 pyramidal cell dendrites are more numerous and have greater amounts of plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR (McAlinn et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2011). Conversely, females compared to males have lower plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR in hilar interneurons and CA3 pyramidal cells (McAlinn et al. 2018). These findings suggest that activation of corticotropin‐releasing factor receptor 1 (CRFR1) could differentially impact DOR‐containing pyramidal cells and interneurons in males and females. In support, our studies have shown that CIS affects DOR and CRF systems in the hippocampus in male and female rats differently. Following CIS, gene expression for opioid receptor delta 1 (Oprd1), Opr nociceptin 1 (Oprl1), and corticotropin‐releasing hormone receptor 1 (Crhr1) in the CA3 region is downregulated in males, but not females (Randesi et al. 2018). Additionally, CIS decreases plasmalemmal‐associated DORs and CRFRs in CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites in males (Mazid et al. 2016; McAlinn et al. 2018). Concomitant with these changes, reserved pools of CRFRs in the cytoplasm of CA3 pyramidal cells are decreased in males (McAlinn et al. 2018). The altered ratio of CA3 plasmalemma‐associated CRFR1 to DORs males may position them to be vulnerable to hyperexcitability following CIS (Elliott‐Hunt et al. 2002; Hollrigel et al. 1998).

However, whether there are sex differences in the subcellular distributions of CRFR1 in the MF opioid–containing pathway following Oxy‐associated learning and if these are affected by CIS are unknown. Thus, this study seeks to determine differences in the subcellular distributions and trafficking of CRFR1 in CA3 pyramidal cells and DG interneurons following Oxy CPP in naïve and CIS female and male rats.

Experimental procedures

Animals

CPP studies utilized tissues from two cohorts of adult male and female (275–325 and 225–250 g at the time of arrival, respectively) Sprague–Dawley rats (N = 48; Charles River) collected for prior publications (Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018). Cohort 1 consisted of saline (Sal) or Oxy unstressed (US) females and males subjected to CPP (N = 6/group) (Ryan et al. 2018). Cohort 2 contained CIS Sal or CIS Oxy females and males also subjected to CPP (N = 6/group) (Reich et al. 2019) (Figure 1A). In situ hybridization studies utilized tissues collected from female and male rats subjected to CIS (N = 18; Charles River) in prior studies (Johnson et al. 2021). Rats were either single‐housed (CPP studies) or pair‐housed (in situ studies) in R20 cages (10.5 in. × 19 in. × 8 in.; Ancare, Bellmore, New York, USA) with a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on 06:00–18:00) and ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Weill Cornell Medicine and Rockefeller University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees and were in accordance with the 2011 eighth edition of the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Handling and estrous cycle determination

Animal handling can cause changes in stress and corticosterone levels (Collins et al. 2016; Deutsch‐Feldman et al. 2015). To reduce handling stress, rats were acclimated to the animal facilities for 1 week and then gently handled 3–5 min per day for 5 days prior to experimentation. Rats in each cohort were handled by the same experimenters for the CPP behavior (Cohorts 1 and 2) and CIS procedure (Cohort 2; in situ studies). Behaviors were performed between 9:00 a.m. and 1:00 p.m. for all rats and perfusions were conducted immediately after the last CPP session for Cohorts 1 and 2 (Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018) or 1 day after the last CIS session for in situ studies (Johnson et al. 2021).

Prior studies have shown that associative memory is affected by regular estrus cycle monitoring using repetitive vaginal swabbing or lavage in cocaine CPP (Van Kempen et al. 2014; Walker et al. 2001; Walker et al. 2002). For this reason, terminal estrus cycle using vaginal smear cytology (Turner 1948) was assessed only at the time of euthanasia. Estrous cycles were classified as proestrus (elevated estrogen levels), estrus (declining estrogen levels), or diestrus (low estrogen and progestin levels) (Turner 1948). Most of the female rats used in this study were in the estrus phase (elevated estrogen) of the estrous cycle (Johnson et al. 2021; Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018) on the day of perfusion.

CIS

Rats in Cohort 2 destined for the CPP studies (Reich et al. 2019) (Figure 1A) or rats destined for the in situ studies (Johnson et al. 2021) were subjected to CIS for 10 consecutive days. Each day for 30 min, the rats were placed in plastic cone‐shaped polyethylene bags with a small apical hole. The bags were sealed with tape near the tail and a Kotex mini‐pad underneath them for urine collection. Cohort 2 CIS rats began CPP training 2 days following the last stress period.

Oxy CPP

A 14‐day protocol of 3 mg/kg (i.p.) Oxy (Sigma–Aldrich #O1378) CPP was performed as previously described (Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018). The CPP apparatus (product # MED‐CPP‐013, Med Associates Inc., Fairfax, Vermont, USA) consisted of three compartments (black, white, and central gray) separated by removable doors. Locomotor activity and time spent in each compartment were monitored using infrared photobeams. Day 1 (preconditioning): Rats were given free access to the entire CPP apparatus for 30 min. During conditioning, Oxy was injected in the non‐preferred side because rats in each cohort had a preferred side (Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018). Days 2–9 (conditioning): Rats had four training sessions of 2 days in length. On the 1st day of each session, the rats were administered Oxy (3 mg/kg, i.p.) and restricted to the non‐preferred compartment of the apparatus (e.g., black) for 30 min. On the 2nd day of each session, the rats were administered Sal and restricted to the other compartment for 30 min. Control rats were injected with Sal on each day of the conditioning session. Day 14 (CPP test): The doors separating the compartments were removed. The rats were not injected and were placed in the central neutral gray compartment, and their behavior was monitored for 30 min. Rats were immediately euthanized following the CPP behavioral test.

Time spent in the Oxy‐paired compartment was expressed as time spent in the Oxy section divided by the total time spent in the CPP apparatus. Preference score was calculated as a ratio of time spent in the Oxy‐paired chamber to combined time spent in Sal‐ and Oxy‐paired chambers. Preference change was determined by subtracting preconditioning time spent in the Oxy CPP chamber from post‐conditioning time. A two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed an effect of treatment (Oxy CPP vs. Sal) (F 3, 18 = 50.234, p < 0.0001), and sex (F 3, 18 = 4.974, p < 0.039) as well as a significant interaction of sex × condition (F 3, 18 = 6.422, p = 0.021). Post‐hoc revealed that both female (p < 0.0001) and male (p < 0.0224) rats demonstrated CPP to Oxy (Figure 1A) as shown by significant increases in percent time spent in the Oxy‐paired compartment. Females showed significantly more percent time spent on the Oxy‐paired side than male rats (p = 0.0222) (Ryan et al. 2018).

Our prior studies showed sex differences in the acquisition of CPP in the two cohorts of rats used in these studies. The naïve male and female rats (Cohort 1) both acquired Oxy CPP (Ryan et al. 2018). However, only CIS females, but not CIS males, acquired Oxy CPP (Reich et al. 2019). Two‐way ANOVA showed an effect of treatment (F 3, 19 = 10.6507, p = 0.0041) and post hoc revealed an increased percent change of preference score for the Oxy‐paired compartment in CIS female rats (p = 0.0147), not CIS males (Reich et al. 2019).

Antibody characterization

For the immuno‐EM experiments, a goat polyclonal antibody raised against the 425–444 amino acid sequence of the carboxy terminus of the CRF receptor precursor of human origin, identical to the corresponding sequence in the rat (C‐20 Cat# sc‐1757, RRID:AB_673600; Santa Cruz Biochemical, California, USA [discontinued]) was used. This antibody recognizes one band at 80 kD on Western blots of hypothalamus from wild‐type mice (Chen et al. 2000). The specificity of this antibody has been shown by absence of labeling in dorsal raphe sections from CRFR1 knockout mice fixed with acrolein and paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Waselus et al. 2009). Moreover, preadsorption with the antigenic peptide sequence produced no labeling in immunoblots (Bangasser et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2000) and tissue sections (Reyes et al. 2006; Sauvage and Steckler 2001). This antibody was used in our previous EM studies (McAlinn et al. 2018; Williams and Milner 2011).

Electron microscopic immunocytochemical procedures

Section preparation

Details of brain section preparation and dual labeling immunocytochemistry were described previously (Milner et al. 2011). The rats were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) i.p. and perfused through the ascending aorta sequentially with (1) 10–15 mL 2% heparin in 0.9% Sal, (2) 50 mL 3.75% acrolein and 2% PFA in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB; pH 7.4), and (3) 200 mL 2% PFA in PB. Coronal sections (40 µm thick) through the hippocampus were cut on a vibratome (VT1000X Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, Illinois, USA) and stored in cryoprotectant solution (30% sucrose, 30% ethylene glycol in PB) at −20°C until use. From the six rats per group that underwent behavioral testing, dorsal hippocampal sections (−3.5 to −4.2 mm from Bregma) (Swanson 1992) were selected from three rats from each experimental condition from Cohorts 1 and 2. Female rats selected for analysis were all in estrus. Sections were coded by hole‐punches in the cortex. Tissue from Cohorts 1 and 2 were processed at the same time under identical conditions. For each cohort, one section/group was processed in a single container. All crucibles were processed simultaneously throughout immunocytochemical procedures to ensure identical labeling conditions between groups. Sections were incubated in 1% sodium borohydride in PB for 30 min to neutralize reactive aldehydes then rinsed in PB ∼10 times until gaseous bubbling ceased.

CRFR1 immunocytochemistry

Sections were rinsed 2 times in 0.1 M tris‐buffered saline (TS; pH 7.6) followed by a blocking step in 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TS for 30 min to reduce nonspecific labeling. Sections then were incubated in goat polyclonal CRFR1 (1:100) in 0.1% BSA in TS for 24 h at room temperature and 48 h at 4°C. Sections were rinsed in TS followed by washing buffer (0.1 M phosphate‐buffered saline [PBS] with 2% gelatin and 0.1% BSA), and incubated overnight at 4°C in a 1:50 dilution of donkey anti‐goat IgG conjugated to 1‐nm gold particles (Electron Microscopy Sciences [EMS] Cat# 25800, RRID: AB_2631210) in 0.01% gelatin and 0.08% BSA. Antibody incubations were carried out at 145 rotations per minute (RPM) and rinses were conducted at 90 RPM on a rotator shaker.

Sections were rinsed in PBS, postfixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 10 min, then rinsed in PBS and in 0.2 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 7.4). The conjugated gold particles were enhanced using silver solution (IntenSE; Amersham Biosciences, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA; Cat# RPN491 [discontinued]) for 6 min.

Sections were fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations to propylene oxide and embedded in EMBed 812 (EMS) between two sheets of Aclar plastic (Honeywell, Pottsville, Pennsylvania, USA). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) of the CA3 and DG were cut with a Diatome diamond knife (EMS) on a Leica UCT6 ultratome and were collected on 400 mesh thin‐bar copper grids (T400‐Cu, EMS). The grids were counterstained with uranyl acetate (EMS 22400) and Reynold's lead citrate (lead nitrate, EMS, 17900–25) (Cohort 1) or Uranylless (catalog #22409, EMS) and lead citrate (catalog #22410, EMS) (Cohort 2). The appearance of the cell membranes did not differ with the two counterstains.

Electron microscopic analysis of CRFR1 localization

Experimenters who performed data collection and analyses were blind to experimental conditions. Cohort 1 images were taken on Tecnai transmission electron microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, Oregon, USA), and Cohort 2 images were taken on Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope, both equipped with an Advanced Microscopy Techniques digital camera (Danvers, Massachusetts, USA). Images from the ultra‐thin sections were collected at a magnification of 5000× and 8000× (Cohort 2) or 18,500× (Cohort 1) from the tissue–plastic interface where immunoreagent access is maximal (Milner et al. 2011); therefore, particle counts reflect relative rather than absolute receptor abundance. Profiles were identified based on standard morphological criteria (Peters et al. 1991). Dendritic profiles contained regular microtubular arrays and were usually postsynaptic to axon terminals. In CA3, pyramidal cell dendrites were identified by the presence of spines or spine protuberances. In the DG, interneuron dendrites were often contacted by multiple terminals. Dendritic profiles were categorized as large (average diameter > 1.0 µm) and small (average diameter < 1.0 µm), corresponding to proximal and distal, respectively, to the cell body. MF terminals were identified by their large size (1–1.5 µm in diameter), irregular contour, and the presence of numerous small synaptic vesicles (Amaral and Dent 1981). Silver‐intensified immunogold (SIG) labeling for CRFR1 appeared as black electron‐dense particles.

Analysis 1: CRFR1–SIG labeling in dendrites from CA3 and DG

Micrographs were taken of CRFR1–SIG–labeled dendrites in the stratum radiatum (SR) of CA3 and in the hilus of the DG (Figure 1C). Photographs were taken of 50 random dendritic profiles in each region for each animal. One to three ultrathin sections were examined for one block from nonoverlapping regions. Microcomputer Imaging Device software (MCID Analysis, RRIS:SCR_014278) was used to determine perimeter (i.e., plasma membrane), area, average diameter, and major and minor axis lengths for all labeled dendrites. MCID corrects for the differences in the magnifications from the two transmission electron microscopes. Dendrites with abnormal shape (form factor value < 0.5) were excluded from analysis. Average diameter measures were used to classify dendrite size. CRFR1–SIG particle distribution and morphometry data were used in statistical comparisons.

The density of CRFR1–SIG particles in the dendrites were classified by their subcellular distribution (Figure 1D): (1) number of SIG particles on the plasmalemma (On/µm), (2) number of SIG particles within 50 nm of the plasmalemma (Near/µm) (1 and 2 sometimes described together as plasmalemmal‐associated), (3) number of SIG particles localized in the cytoplasm (Cyto/µm2), and (4) the total number of SIG particles (Total/µm2). CRFR1–SIG particle densities within were calculated within subregions (CA3 or DG), and across sex, Oxy, and CIS conditions. Subcellular CRFR1 distribution was further categorized into small and large dendrites. No dendrites had a form factor value < 0.5 and thus all dendrites were included in the analysis.

Localization of SIG‐labeled CRFR1 in different subcellular compartments indicates different functions for the receptor. Plasmalemmal SIG‐labeled receptors identify receptor‐binding sites (Boudin et al. 1998). Receptors near the plasmalemma constitute a reserve pool from which receptors can be inserted into or removed from the plasmalemma. Receptors in the cytoplasm are either stored during transfer to a different cellular compartment or are awaiting being degraded or recycled (Fernández‐Monreal et al. 2012; Pierce et al. 2009). When stimulated by an agonist, the ratio of plasmalemmal receptors to those in the cytoplasm declines as receptors internalize as a desensitization response (Haberstock‐Debic et al. 2003).

Analysis 2: CRFR1–SIG labeling in CA3 pyramidal cell dendritic spines

For each rat, 50 random spines containing CRF1 labeling were counted from micrographs collected in SR of CA3 (Analysis 1). CRFR1–SIG particles in labeled spines were classified as on the synapse, plasmalemmal, or in the cytoplasm.

Analysis 3: CRFR1–SIG labeling in CA3 pyramidal cell dendritic spines contacted by MF

Fifty MF profiles per rat were randomly imaged from the tissue–plastic interface of CA3 stratum lucidum (SLu). The number of unlabeled and SIG‐labeled dendritic spines was determined. SIG particles in labeled dendritic spines were classified as on the synapse, plasmalemmal, or in the cytoplasm.

In situ hybridization for Crhr1

A separate cohort of female and male CIS rats (N = 5/group) and their corresponding control rats (N = 4/group) were euthanized 1 day after the last stress session. Rats were deeply anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and perfused through the ascending aorta sequentially with (1) 10–15 mL 0.9% Sal and (2) 200 mL of 4% PFA in 0.1 M PB. After removing from the skull, brains were cut into coronal blocks (5 mm thick) and postfixed in 4% PFA with 10% sucrose in PB for 24 h at 4°C and then transferred into 30% sucrose in PB for an additional 24 h. The brains were then frozen on dry ice and stored in a −70°C freezer.

Sections (30 µm thick) through the dorsal hippocampus (−3.5 to −4.2 mm from Bregma (Swanson 1992)) were cut on a cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, Illinois, USA) and collected into 24‐well plates containing cryoprotectant solution. The trays were stored at −20°C. Hippocampal sections were coded with hole punches and then mounted in 1× sterile PBS on Superfrost Plus slides (Cat. # 48311–703 Fischer Scientific) and air‐dried overnight at room temperature. Prior to probe labeling, slides were baked at 60°C in a circulating oven for 1 h.

Expression of mRNA for Crhr1 in the hippocampus was assessed using the RNAScope 2.5 BROWN Kit (ACD, Hayward, California, USA, Cat. # 322300) as previously described (Johnson et al. 2021). Tissue was pretreated with target retrieval buffer (ACD, Cat # 322000) at 40°C in HybEZ oven (ACD) for 12 min. Protease Plus was applied (ACD, Cat. # 322330), then slides were incubated in Rn‐Crhr1‐Channel 1 (Cat. # 318911, ACD) for 2 h at 40°C. In addition, positive control (Rn‐PPIB; Cat. # 313921) and negative control (DapB; Cat. # 310043) probes were run with each experiment. Following the probe incubation, the sections underwent six amplification steps. Tissue was then reacted for 10 min in diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Cat. # 322310, ACD) to develop peroxidase staining. Sections were counterstained using 25% Gill's Hematoxylin No. 1 (Cat. # GHS132; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA, GHS132), dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanols, and coverslipped from xylene using DPX (Cat. # 06522, Sigma–Aldrich).

Light microscopic image acquisition and analysis

Positive and negative control probes verified optimal RNA exposure and detection by RNAScope. One section per animal was utilized for the final analysis. In CA3b, stratum oriens (SO), pyramidal cell layer (PCL), and SR were examined. As little Crhr1 was detected in the DG, this region was not examined. Regions of interest were imaged at 20× on a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope using a Micropublisher 5.0 digital camera (Q‐imaging, British Columbia, Canada) using IP Lab software (Scanalytics IPLab, RRID: SCR_002775). As Crhr1‐labeled cells in CA3b were sparse, images were placed in PowerPoint and cells were counted manually. Crhr1‐labeled cells were analyzed in the whole CA3b image and then within individual lamina. Nissl staining was used to identify the cell layers.

Figure preparation

Adjustments to brightness, contrast, and sharpness were applied to the whole image using PowerPoint 2024. After adjustments, images were aggregated and annotated in PowerPoint 2024. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 software (RRID:SCR_002798).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted on JMP 12 Pro software (JMP, RRID:SCR_014242). Significance was set to an alpha < 0.05. Statements such as “trending interactions” and “verging on significance” are used to describe data with an alpha of 0.06–0.08. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. The distribution of SIG particles in dendrites was calculated using a two‐way ANOVA followed by a Tukey post hoc. Multiple t‐tests with Welch's correction were used for analysis of SIG density in Sal rats from Cohort 1 and 2 (Analysis 1). A Student's t‐test was used to compare the number of CRFR1‐labeled spines (Analysis 2) between groups. Two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc was used to determine differences in Crhr1 expression (Analysis 3).

Results

Cohort 1: CRFR1 distribution in CA3 and DG from US rats following Oxy CPP

In CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites, no baseline sex differences in CRFR1 were observed, however males had more plasmalemmal CRFR1 following Oxy CPP

Representative electron micrographs showing CRFR1–SIG labeling in CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites for US Sal and Oxy CPP groups are shown in Figure 2A–D. Consistent with prior studies (McAlinn et al. 2018), CRFR1–SIG particles were found on the plasmalemma, near the plasmalemma, and in the cytoplasm of CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites. Two‐way ANOVA indicated a significant effect of sex observed in CRFR1 distribution in CA3 dendrites of US rats (F 3, 599 = 8.30, p = 0.0041). No differences in the subcellular distribution of CRFR1–SIG particles in any subcellular compartment of pyramidal cell dendrites were seen between Sal females and males (Figure 2E,F). However, Oxy males had more plasmalemmal CRFR1–SIG on CA3 dendrites than Oxy females (p = 0.041) (Figure 2E,F).

Crhr1 mRNA

Given that previously published data showed dendritic retraction in CA3 in males following CIS (McEwen et al. 2016), and sex differences in the expression of opioid‐related genes throughout the hippocampus after CIS (Johnson et al. 2021), we quantified Crhr1 mRNA expression from the dorsal hippocampus in US and CIS rats by in situ hybridization. This is a separate cohort of animals that underwent CIS but not Oxy injections or CPP behavior. Sparse Crhr1 expression was found in SO, the PCL and SR of CA3b in US and CIS females and males. A significant effect of condition (F 3, 10 = 7.738, p < 0.01) was discovered for Crhr1+ cells in CA3 PCL. CIS males compared to US males had more (p < 0.01) Crhr1+ cells in CA3 PCL (Figure 8A–C). Moreover, an effect of condition trending on significance (F 3, 10 = 3.037, p = 0.0796) was noted for Crhr1+ cells in CA3 SO. CIS males trended toward greater Crhr1+ cells in CA3 SO (p = 0.059) than US males (Figure 8D). Finally, a significant effect of condition was noted for the total count of Crhr1+ cells/µm2 in CA3 (F 3, 10 = 7.677, p < 0.01). CIS males compared to US males had more total Crhr1+ cells in CA3 (p < 0.01). No significant differences were seen in the distributions of Crhr1+ cells in any subregions of CA1 in any groups (not shown). Few Crhr1+ cells were detected in the DG and thus these were not quantified.

Discussion

This study demonstrates sex‐dependent, cell‐type specific changes in CRFR1 density and trafficking in hippocampal neurons following Oxy CPP shaped by prior chronic stress exposure. In US rats, Sal females had more plasmalemmal CRFR1 on DG interneuron dendrites than males suggesting greater baseline sensitivity of interneurons to CRF in females. Following Oxy CPP, near plasmalemmal CRFR1s increased in DG interneuron dendrites in males, suggesting increased binding capacity for CRF. Following CIS, plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR1s decreased in pyramidal cell dendrites in both males and females. Additionally, plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR1s in DG interneuron dendrites decreased in CIS females. Following Oxy CPP behavior in CIS animals, only females acquired CPP. Oxy CIS females compared to Oxy CIS males had more total CRFR1 in DG interneuron dendrites. Moreover, CRFR1 trafficked from the cytoplasm toward the plasmalemma in DG interneuron dendrites in CIS Oxy females may indicate a greater binding capacity for CIS Oxy females in these neuronal populations. The present study uncovered region‐specific sex differences in the density of CRFR1s following Oxy CPP and CIS (Figure 9), contributing to our understanding of previous findings (Chalangal et al. 2022) that these changes may lead to diminished plasticity of the hippocampal opioid system in males (Figure 10).

Methodological considerations

Tissues from Cohorts 1 and 2 (Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018) were processed together to allow for comparisons between US and CIS rats that underwent either Sal or Oxy CPP. The tissue used for the mRNA experiments came from a different cohort of US and CIS rats that did not undergo behavior testing nor were they injected (Johnson et al. 2021). Compared to unhandled controls, Sal injections in male rats are associated with increased spine density of CA1 pyramidal cells (Horner et al. 1991). Additionally, repeated intraperitoneal Sal injections shift the CRF dose–response curve in the locus coeruleus of males (Curtis et al. 1995; Curtis et al. 1999), highlighting the impact of stress caused by injections. Thus, it is possible that the Crhr1 mRNA expression deviates from changes in Crhr1 mRNA that occur in the cohorts of CPP rats. Likewise, the findings in the current EM study may vary from our prior immuno‐EM study examining the effect of CIS on CRFR1 distributions in the hippocampus (McAlinn et al. 2018) as this study did not include behavior or injections.

As consistent estrus cycling could impact Oxy CPP and other behaviors (Van Kempen et al. 2014; Walker et al. 2002), estrus cycle was only determined at time of euthanasia. All rats used in the final experiments (Dolgetta et al. 2022; Reich et al. 2019; Ryan et al. 2018) were identified as being in estrus. Though we note a single vaginal cytology sample serves as an estimation of estrous stage (Cora et al. 2015), consistency in estrus‐only inclusion minimizes cycle‐related variance.

No Sal‐ or Oxy‐injected rats without CPP training were included in the present study. However, our prior studies (Ashirova et al. 2021) have shown that rats injected with Oxy but not subjected to behavior had almost no changes in the redistribution of MORs and DORs in CA3 pyramidal cell neurons and DG interneurons.

At baseline, Sal females had more plasmalemmal CRFR1 than sal males in DG interneuron dendrites

At baseline, there were no sex differences in the subcellular localization of CRFR1s in CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites or dendritic spines. However, Sal females had a greater density of plasmalemmal CRFR1s in hilar interneurons than Sal males, suggesting these neurons have a greater basal binding capacity for CRF in females. In support, studies of the locus coeruleus have demonstrated elevated plasmalemmal CRFR1 in male rats is associated with elevated beta arrestin 2 which is necessary for the internalization of CRFR1 (Bangasser et al. 2010; Reyes et al. 2008).

Most DG interneurons containing CRFR1 were in the central hilus where the SOM/NPY interneurons reside (Chalangal et al. 2022). SOM/NPY interneurons colocalize DORs (Commons and Milner 1996; Mazid et al. 2016; Williams and Milner 2011). Moreover, like CRFR1s, Sal females compared to Sal males have more plasmalemmal DORs on GABA dendrites (Ryan et al. 2018). The concurrent elevation of CRFR1s and DORs on the plasmalemma of hilar interneurons in females could promote hippocampal circuits important for the promotion of opioid‐associated learning. Hilar SOM/NPY interneurons project to granule cell dendrites where they converge with entorhinal afferents (Milner and Bacon 1989; Milner and Veznedaroglu 1992). Activation of CRFR1 on inhibitory neurons promotes long‐lasting potentiation of DG neurons (Wang et al. 2000). Additionally, activation of DORs on NPY‐containing interneurons is known to promote lateral perforant path LTP (Sperk et al. 2007).

Following Oxy CPP, males had greater Plasmalemmal‐Associated CRFR1 in CA3 and DG dendrites than females

Oxy males compared to Oxy females exhibited more plasmalemmal CRFR1 on CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites. Concurrent with these changes, DORs redistribute from the plasmalemma and toward the cytoplasm in CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites in Oxy CPP males (Ryan et al. 2018). Furthermore, expression of Arc, an immediate early gene that regulates synaptic plasticity (Bramham et al. 2010) and important for LTP (Plath et al. 2006), increases in the CA3 region in males following Oxy CPP (Randesi et al. 2019). Taken together, increased CRFR1 (Figure 9) and decreased DOR availability (Figure 10) may bias CA3 pyramidal cells toward a state of heightened CRF modulation of plasticity processes in male rats following Oxy CPP.

The Oxy CPP–driven upregulation of interneuronal CRFR1 in both sexes, with male‐specific trafficking toward the plasmalemma, would maintain the heightened CRF sensitivity seen in male DG interneurons at baseline. Accompanying these interneuron changes in CRFR1 trafficking, Oxy CPP in males increases mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) expression in the DG and phosphorylated MAPK (pMAPK) immunoreactivity in the central hilus (Randesi et al. 2019). MAPK integrates membrane receptor signals to the nucleus and affects many plasticity processes (Murphy and Blenis 2006; Zheng et al. 2005) like NMDA phosphorylation and LTP in the hippocampus (Bi et al. 2000). CRFR1 and MAPK signaling are closely linked in modulating stress responses, neuronal plasticity, and affective behaviors (Hauger et al. 2009). Activation of CRFR1 triggers the MAPK/extracellular‐regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) cascade in the hippocampus, amygdala, and striatum (Refojo et al. 2005; Vasconcelos et al. 2020). In the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, prior work has shown CRFR redistribution following chronic morphine administration in male mice (Jaferi et al. 2009).

Following CIS, Plasmalemma‐Associated CRFR1 is reduced in CA3 pyramidal cells, while cytoplasmic and total CRFR1 is decreased in DG interneurons in sal males

Prior studies have shown that CIS in males, but not females, results in downregulation of gene expression for stress gene arginine vasopressin receptor 1A (Avpr1a) as well as several plasticity genes (Arc, Cdh2, Ntrk2) and kinase/signaling genes (Akt1 and Arrb) (Randesi et al. 2018). The elevated proportion of plasmalemmal‐associated CRFR1 in CA3 dendrites along with these other changes in gene expression may position the CIS males to be more vulnerable to CRF‐induced cyclic AMP‐dependent hyperexcitability (Elliott‐Hunt et al. 2002; Hollrigel et al. 1998) and perhaps contribute to processes that promote spine loss and dendritic retraction observed in CIS males (McEwen et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2013).

Following CIS, Sal females had reduced plasmalemma‐associated and total CRFR1s on DG interneuron dendrites compared to US Sal females (Figure 9). This indicates that CIS in Sal females downregulates CRFR1 and traffics CRFR1 away from the plasmalemma in interneurons. In contrast, CIS increases plasmalemmal DORs on hilar interneurons in females (Figure 10) (Mazid et al. 2016). Data suggest that DORs are well positioned to influence CRF release from interneurons of males and normal cycling females (Williams and Milner 2011).

Following CIS and Oxy CPP behavior, only females exhibited changes in CRFR1 density in CA3 and DG neurons

Our prior studies showed that only the CIS females acquired CPP to Oxy (Reich et al. 2019). Consistent with prior studies (Reich et al. 2019) (Figure 10), changes in CRFR1 densities in hippocampal neurons after Oxy CPP behavioral conditioning were only observed in CIS females (Figure 9). In CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites, total CRFR1 was increased in Oxy CIS females compared to Sal CIS females. In DG hilar interneuron dendrites, CRFR1s were increased near the plasmalemma in Oxy CIS females, where they may be more available for plasmalemmal insertion. In parallel with these changes, Oxy CIS females exhibit increases in total and cytoplasmic DORs in CA3 dendrites and in total and cytoplasmic MORs in parvalbumin (PARV) interneurons in the DG hilus (Reich et al. 2019). Furthermore, the density of DORs in MF–CA3 synapses remains elevated in Oxy CIS females and low in Oxy CIS males.

The findings suggest a coordinated shift across the dentate–CA3 circuit in Oxy CIS females. In DG hilar interneurons, CRFR1 trafficking toward the plasmalemma may enhance CRF‐driven modulation of granule cell excitability, consistent with plasmalemmal CRFR1 on interneurons promoting LTP in DG (Wang et al. 2000) and DOR activation on NPY interneurons facilitating perforant path LTP (Sperk et al. 2007). DORs can modulate CRFR1 signaling through cyclic AMP‐phosphokinase pathways and DORs and are colocalized with CRFR1 in hippocampal pyramidal cell dendrites. This functional and anatomical relationship allows for interpretation of coordinated receptor changes found in CIS Oxy females. Downstream, the rise in CA3 pyramidal CRFR1 alongside elevated DOR density at MF–CA3 synapses (Reich et al. 2019) (Figures 9–10), imperative for opioid‐mediated LTP (Harte‐Hargrove et al. 2015), positions the CA3 to receive and further potentiate this dentate signal. The convergence of interneuron and pyramidal cell receptor tracking in CIS females may be a candidate circuit mechanism for their selective vulnerability to Oxy CPP acquisition after CIS.

In contrast to Oxy CIS females, Oxy CIS males compared to Sal CIS males have increased GluN1 and GluA1 in most compartments of CA3 pyramidal cell dendrites (Dolgetta et al. 2022). The increased levels of glutamate receptors in pyramidal cells in Oxy CIS males would make them more excitable to glutamate (Pál 2018). Importantly, CRF‐mediated neuroprotection has been shown to decrease susceptibility to glutamate excitotoxicity in the hippocampus (Dolgetta et al. 2022; Elliott‐Hunt et al. 2002; Hollrigel et al. 1998). Thus, our result that CRFR1 increased in CA3 and DG neurons in Oxy CIS females but not Oxy CIS males would support these findings.

Conclusion

Changes in CRFR1 density and Crhr1 expression may contribute to reported sex differences in hippocampal learning and memory processes basally and after both Oxy CPP and CIS. Prior work indicates Oxy CPP and CIS redistribute opioid receptors in the female hippocampus to promote opioid‐related learning processes (reviewed in Chalangal et al. (2022)). Males, unable to acquire CPP after CIS, lack this plasticity. Sex differences in the redistribution of CRFR1 in CA3 dendrites may contribute to observed dendritic retraction in males after chronic stress (McEwen et al. 2016). Thus, after chronic stress, hippocampal mechanisms in promoting and disrupting drug‐associative learning differ across males and females (Chalangal et al. 2022). Given known sex differences in the presentation of addiction (Becker et al. 2016) and the capacity of chronic stress to impair CRF‐mediated plasticity and memory function (Chen et al. 2012; Regev and Baram 2014; Smith and Vale 2006), continued investigation of how contextual factors like stress confer sex‐specific vulnerability to opioid addiction is necessary to inform prevention and treatment strategies.
