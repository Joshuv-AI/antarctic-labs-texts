Association between healthy lifestyle score and risk of multiple myeloma: a case–control study
Authors: Reza Amiri Khosroshahi, Forough Shahmohammadi, Sara Ebrahimi-Mousavi, Nazanin Zamanian, Mohammad-Reza Jowshan, Abbas Hajifathali, Hoda Zahedi, Mahdi Shadnoush
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple myeloma (MM) is a type of blood cancer that has both genetic and environmental causes. However, there is limited information available on how lifestyle factors affect MM risk. The current study looked at the relationship between a Healthy Lifestyle Score (HLS) and MM risk in Iranian adults. This is a hospital case-control-based study from Aug 2020 to March 2024, where 149 patients were newly diagnosed with MM and 359 hospital-based controls without MM. The four components of HLS (0–4) were related to diet, physical activity, BMI, and smoking. The HLS distribution among the control groups was divided into tertiles (T1- 0-1, T2-2, T3-3-4). Using multivariable binary logistic regression to estimate OR’s and 95% CI’s for MM within HLS tertiles and per 1-point increase in HLS using crude, demographic adjustment (including energy intake), and 3 models for full adjustment. HLS levels were observed to have lower odds of MM. When adjusted, results demonstrate that the odds ratio (OR) between T1 and T2, as well as T1 and T3, are 0.48 (95% CI = 0.25–0.90) and 0.20 (95% CI = 0.07–0.45). The trend was statistically significant with a P-value < 0.001. A 1-point increase in HLS was associated with 49% decreased odds of MM when considering all lifestyle factors (OR = 0.51; 95% CI = 0.36–0.72; P < 0.001). When examining the results separately by individual components of HLS, the associations between smoking and body mass index (BMI) were consistent with the risk of MM. However, when adjusting for other variables considered in the overall HLS analysis, neither physical activity nor adherence to the Mediterranean Diet was found to be independently associated with the risk of MM. Higher HLS has been shown to correlate negatively on an individual basis with odds of developing MM in a dose-response manner. Additional studies to assess potential exposure of patients prior to the development of disease will help determine whether or not the association is true and, if so, to clarify potential causality.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, Life style, Diet, Case-control studies, Primary preventionSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a neoplastic disorder characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal plasma cells in the bone marrow, leading to complications such as bone lesions, anemia, immune dysfunction, and renal impairment1,2. This is the most common form of primary blood cancer, representing 10% of all blood malignancies3. According to national registry reports for 2014, Iran’s age-standardized incidence rates (ASRs) for multiple myeloma were 1.97 per 100,000 in women and 2.66 per 100,000 in men4. More recent national data from the Iranian National Cancer Registry (2016) confirm a continued cancer burden in Iran, with age-standardized incidence rates for all cancers showing an increasing trend over time5. Regional registry data from Golestan province also reported age-standardized incidence rates for multiple myeloma through 20176. This condition is more common in men than women and usually appears in middle age (over 40)6. Although therapeutic advancements have improved patient outcomes, MM remains an incurable condition, with a median survival period ranging between five and seven years7.​ Over 83% of MM patients who receive a diagnosis pass away within ten years8. Growing evidence suggests that environmental and lifestyle-related factors such as diet, physical activity, smoking, alcohol use, and exposure to environmental toxins may significantly contribute to MM risk9.

Among modifiable risk factors, obesity has been strongly associated with a higher likelihood of developing MM10,11. Meta-analyses of observational studies suggest that individuals who are overweight or obese are at an elevated risk of developing MM compared to those with normal weight12. Other contributing risk factors include family history of hematologic malignancies, autoimmune diseases, chronic antigenic stimulation, ionizing radiation, and pesticide exposure13,14. These highlight the need to identify modifiable lifestyle factors that influence the risk of MM15.

Research shows that body weight, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking, and diet quality affect cancer risk by altering immune function and inflammation16. To assess the combined impact of lifestyle behaviors, composite indices such as the Healthy Lifestyle Score (HLS) have been developed17.

The HLS incorporates several low-risk lifestyle characteristics, including ideal body mass index (BMI), consistent physical activity, non-smoking status, moderate alcohol use, and nutritious dietary habits18. For the present study in Iran, alcohol consumption was excluded from the HLS due to unavailable data. Higher HLS has been associated with reduced incidence and mortality for several cancers, including colorectal, breast, and pancreatic cancers19. These associations are thought to be mediated by common biological processes such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and dysregulation of cellular proliferation pathways20.

Lifestyle behaviors in Iran are influenced by the rapid urbanization of the country and the continuing shift in nutrition toward a more traditional, westernized diet, which has been well documented by many researchers21. In addition to these changes, national-level STEPs surveys have identified a significant burden associated with modifiable lifestyle factors, such as being overweight/obese22, using tobacco products23, and being physically inactive24. Thus, an assessment of a composite score representing a healthy lifestyle can provide contextualized evidence that can be used to develop population-specific strategies for the prevention of MM in Iran.

Although composite healthy lifestyle scores have been inversely associated with the risk of various cancers in prospective studies and cohort analyses (e.g., overall cancer incidence, lymphoma)25,26, data specifically examining the association between HLS and multiple myeloma are scarce, particularly in non-Western populations such as Iran. Given this gap, the present case–control study aimed to investigate the association between HLS and the risk of multiple myeloma in an Iranian population, providing region-specific evidence to inform potential lifestyle-based prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This hospital-based case-control study was conducted between August 2020 and March 2024 at Shahid Taleghani Hospital, Tehran. The study population included 149 newly diagnosed MM cases and 359 controls. Eligible participants were aged 20–75 years. MM diagnosis was confirmed according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD C90.0), and cases were restricted to newly diagnosed patients interviewed within 3 months of diagnosis.

Control subjects were selected from healthy individuals accompanying patients at the hospital. Exclusion criteria included a history of cancer, advanced-stage cancer with no survival chance, ongoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy, mental or cognitive impairments, or daily energy intake below 500 kcal or above 5000 kcal.

Assessment of lifestyle behaviors

The HLS used in this study is based on previously established composite lifestyle indices that have been widely used in international research17,18,27. Minor adaptations were made to accommodate local dietary data, such as excluding alcohol consumption from the Mediterranean Diet Score (MDS). The HLS estimates the number of participants who follow a low-risk lifestyle and are therefore at lower risk of developing MM. The HLS is composed of four components: diet quality (assessed using the Mediterranean Diet Score [MDS]), physical activity, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status. The MDS is a 9-item questionnaire28,29, although wine consumption was excluded as this information was unavailable. Participants could score between 0 and 8 based on the remaining items. For health-promoting items, participants scoring at or above the sex-specific median received one point; all others received zero. For items considered detrimental (meat and dairy), participants scoring below the sex-specific median received one point; others received zero. Participants in the top 40% of the MDS were classified as having a “healthy diet” and assigned one point for this component. Energy adjustment of the MDS was performed using the residual method before inclusion in the HLS to control for total energy intake. Lifestyle behaviors used to construct the HLS were assessed at the time of enrollment, reflecting the period around diagnosis for cases and a comparable reference period for controls.

Physical activity was assessed using the validated Iranian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which is reliable and valid for assessing physical activity levels in Iranian populations. The questionnaire covers physical activity over the past 7 days, which is a standard recall period for IPAQ. This version of IPAQ has been previously validated in the Iranian context30.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was collected using a validated 154-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)31. Consumption frequencies (daily, weekly, monthly, and annually) were converted to grams per day. Energy and nutrient intake were calculated using Nutritionist IV software (version 7.0; N-Squared Computing, Salem, OR, USA)32.

Assessment of other variables

Anthropometric measurements, including weight and height, were taken using standardized equipment, a stadiometer (accuracy 0.1 cm) and a digital scale (accuracy 0.1 kg, Seca725 GmbH & Co., Hamburg, Germany). BMI was subsequently calculated. The validated Iranian version of the IPAQ30 was used to assess the degree of physical activity. Metabolic equivalents (METs) of less than 600, 600–3000, and more than 3000 METs/min/week were considered low, moderate, and strenuous activities, respectively.

Details about age, sex, marital status, residence, education, employment, use of cell phones, history of X-ray exposure, family history of cancer, and the presence of conditions like allergies, hepatitis, hypertension, anemia, stroke, diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, anemia, blood transfusions, and eating disorders. Based on a prior study33, farming was categorized as high-risk employment for MM.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics)34, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Quantitative data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were assessed for all variables included in the analyses. The proportion of missing data was minimal, and analyses were conducted using available data for each variable. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Independent-sample t-tests were used to compare normally distributed continuous variables between MM patients and controls, while the Mann-Whitney test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Chi-square tests were used for categorical comparisons. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences in normally distributed continuous variables across HLS tertiles, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed variables; chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables. The association between HLS and the odds of MM was assessed using binary logistic regression analysis. HLS was analyzed both categorically (in tertiles) and continuously. For categorical analyses, HLS was divided into tertiles to facilitate comparison between participants with low, moderate, and high adherence to a healthy lifestyle. This approach ensures approximately equal sample sizes across groups and is consistent with previous studies evaluating lifestyle indices. For the categorical approach, tertiles were defined based on the distribution in the entire study population: scores 0–1 (T1, reference category), scores 2 (T2), and scores 3–4 (T3). Crude and multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated across tertiles, using T1 as reference, together with p-values for trend. Additionally, to obtain a more statistically efficient estimate independent of cut-point choice, HLS was modeled as a continuous variable, yielding ORs per 1-point increment. Three sequential adjustment models were used. Confounders were selected based on prior epidemiologic evidence and biological plausibility.

Sample size considerations

We determined the sample size largely by assessing how many newly diagnosed MM cases were identified within our enrolment time frame, as well as how many controls were eligible and subsequently recruited at the same time (149 cases, 359 controls; control-to-case ratio approximately 2.4). As post hoc power calculations made using the observations of this study are not suitable for interpretation35, we report here the minimum detectable effect (MDE) based on standard methods for unmatched case-control studies36. We believe that, given the sample size and assuming a two-tailed α = 0.05, and having at least 80% power to determine an OR of approximately 0.50 based on cases and controls with an exposure prevalence between 33% and 48% for the controls, it is reasonable to assume that our sample size will have sufficient power to detect this level of effect. However, the power of this study may not be adequate to detect smaller effect sizes or less prevalent exposures.

Across HLS tertiles, participants in the highest tertile (T3) were younger, had lower BMI, and were more likely to be female and have higher educational attainment compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1) (all P < 0.001). 3.2 Energy-adjusted dietary intakes across tertiles of the HLS

There were differences in energy-adjusted dietary intake between MM patients and controls (Table 2). Cases had significantly lower total energy intake and carbohydrate intake than controls and tended not to eat many of the foods classified as health promoting (such as dairy products, vegetables, any kind of fruit, nuts, and fish), but did, however, consume more total fat, SFA, monounsaturated fats, and meat than controls (all P < 0.01). There were no significant differences in protein, whole grains, or legumes between the two groups. Higher HLS tertiles were associated with a better dietary profile based on HLS (Table 2), which were characterized by higher dairy, vegetables, fruits, and nuts, as well as slightly less total energy and total fat than lower HLS tertiles. There were no significant differences between HLS tertiles for carbohydrate, protein, MUFA, SFA, meat, legumes, whole grains, or fish.

3.3 OR for MM by HLS tertiles and per 1-point increase

In unadjusted analyses, higher HLS was strongly and inversely associated with MM risk, with a clear dose–response pattern across tertiles and when modeled continuously (Table 3; P for trend < 0.001). This inverse association remained robust after sequential adjustment for demographic factors and energy intake (Model 1), additional socio-demographic and clinical covariates (Model 2), and medication use (Model 3). Across all models, participants in the highest HLS tertile consistently exhibited substantially lower odds of MM compared with those in the lowest tertile, and each one-point increase in HLS was associated with a markedly reduced odds of MM (Table 3).

OR for MM per 1-unit increase in each HLS component

As continuous variables, the various components of the HLS have shown different patterns of association with MM risk when examined independently. Both smoking and BMI were consistently associated with increased odds of developing MM, regardless of the analytical model (i.e., crude vs. multivariable adjusted). Conversely, there was an inverse but not significant association between physical activity and MM risk following multivariable adjustment, and the energy-adjusted Mediterranean diet component was not significantly associated with risk of developing MM regardless of the model used. Overall, results from this analysis suggest that the composite HLS’s overall association with MM risk is primarily due to the contribution of some dominant components (such as smoking and BMI), while other components either add less to the overall association, are subject to measurement error, or are associated with limited statistical power (Table 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates a strong inverse association between adherence to a healthy lifestyle and the risk of MM. Individuals in the highest tertile of the HLS had substantially lower odds of MM compared with those in the lowest tertile, and this relationship remained robust after adjustment for a wide range of demographic, clinical, lifestyle, and medication-related factors. Modeling HLS as a continuous variable further confirmed a clear dose–response pattern, with each 1-point increase in the score associated with nearly 50% lower odds of MM. Overall, these findings suggest that adherence to a healthier lifestyle is associated with lower odds of MM.

Our results align with new research findings from Iran that indicate that dietary inflammatory potential may contribute to the risk of myeloma development. For example, an Iranian case-control study has assessed and found a positive association between the total Dietary Inflammatory Index and increased risk of myeloma37. Evidence from large cohorts across Asia has further demonstrated that key aspects of lifestyle, particularly excessive body fat, are associated with myeloma-related health outcomes38.

Adopting a comprehensive healthy lifestyle has been consistently linked to lower incidence rates of various chronic non-communicable diseases39 and overall mortality40. Several studies have shown its positive impact on conditions such as DM41, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease42, CVD43,44, and stroke45. Cancer remains a leading cause of death globally46, prompting various treatment approaches like surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy47. Importantly, adherence to a healthy lifestyle has been widely associated with lower risk profiles for multiple types of cancer in cohort studies48. Consistent with this evidence, our findings showed a strong inverse association between the HLS and the odds of MM, with a clear dose–response pattern across HLS tertiles that remained significant in both crude and multivariable-adjusted models. Additionally, when modeled continuously, each 1-point increase in HLS was associated with significantly lower odds of MM.

In line with the results of this investigation, a study by Ghosn et al. demonstrated that those with high HLS were less likely to get breast cancer (BC). Moreover, analysis of each component of HLS revealed that individuals who followed healthy eating had a reduced likelihood of developing BC49. The findings of three observational studies involving patients with pancreatic cancer are consistent with this study’s results, highlighting the substantial positive impact of a healthy lifestyle50–52. In accordance with our findings, a prospective cohort research showed that persons who followed four or five low-risk lifestyle behaviors at age 50 saw markedly increased life expectancy devoid of major chronic diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. In contrast, the shortest spans of disease-free life were observed among male smokers consuming 15 or more cigarettes per day and among those classified as obese (BMI ≥ 30)18. Similarly, in our data, the inverse association between overall HLS and MM remained robust across crude and fully adjusted models, and component-wise analyses indicated that the smoking- and BMI-related components were the most consistently associated with MM risk. In contrast, physical activity and energy-adjusted Mediterranean diet demonstrated non-significant trends toward lower MM odds. Likewise, research by Buckland and colleagues demonstrated that sustained engagement in healthy lifestyle behaviors was strongly associated with a decreased incidence of gastric cancer (GC)53.

The evidence on the correlation between a healthy lifestyle and leukemia risk is inconclusive. As opposed to the results of the present study, Parodi et al. reported no inverse association between lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking and the risk of leukemia and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. However, lymphoid and myeloid malignancies are heterogeneous diseases encompassing various subtypes, each with potentially distinct etiologies that may impact the study’s validity54. Similarly, the EPIC cohort study found no overall link between healthy lifestyle adherence and lymphoma or non-Hodgkin lymphoma risk but observed an inverse association with Hodgkin lymphoma, mainly related to smoking. However, the study did not consider lifestyle changes during follow-up, potentially biasing the results25. In a prospective cohort study of 13 cancer types, adherence to lifestyle recommendations was not associated with the risk of melanoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, ovarian cancer, or lymphocytic leukaemia, consistent with the lack of conclusive evidence linking individual lifestyle factors to these malignancies55. In our analysis, the overall HLS showed a clear inverse association with MM across models, and the smoking- and BMI-related components demonstrated the most consistent associations with MM risk, supporting the possibility that specific lifestyle domains may differentially influence risks across hematologic malignancy subtypes. However, Antwi et al. reported no significant association between self-reported BMI and pancreatic cancer risk56. Other components, such as physical activity and energy-adjusted Mediterranean diet, showed non-significant inverse associations with MM risk across all models.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the observed associations between lifestyle factors and MM risk. MM is a multistage disease that requires the development of genetic mutations and the creation of a microenvironment in the bone marrow (BM), both of which are essential to the development of the disease57. Excess body fat has been associated with increased MM risk through a supportive BM environment for plasma cells. Chronic low-grade inflammation, the disruption of adipokine signaling, and the activation of insulin/IGF-1 pathways contribute to an obesogenic BM58. The influence of dietary quality on the development of MM through inflammatory and insulinemic pathways has been examined. In studies involving large, prospectively gathered data, dietary patterns that have a higher potential for creating inflammation/insulinemia have been positively associated with the increased risk of MM59; whereas healthful, plant-based patterns have shown inverse associations60. Physical activity has been linked to potentially lower MM risk by positively influencing insulin sensitivity, decreasing systemic inflammation, and altering the function of the immune system and the tumor microenvironment61. Finally, reactive oxygen species (ROS) from tobacco products or other sources can cause oxidative injuries and damage to the macromolecules of B cells, including DNA, and thus, increase the accumulation of mutations in B-cell neoplasms such as MM62.

Cancer arises through a multistep carcinogenic process involving the progressive accumulation of genetic mutations and epigenetic alterations, encompassing several essential biological hallmarks influenced by environmental, nutritional, and genetic factors63. Obesity and elevated BMI are major etiological factors in cancer development, second only to smoking64. Increased adherence to a healthy lifestyle was associated with lower BMI. Excess adiposity impairs adipose tissue function, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor, which facilitate DNA damage, mutations, aberrant cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and tumor invasiveness65. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress induced by obesity further increase cancer risk, compounded by abnormal hormone production (e.g., estrogens, androgens, and leptin)66,67. In contrast, compliance with nutritious dietary patterns, particularly the Mediterranean diet abundant in fruits, vegetables, and fiber, correlates with decreased cancer incidence owing to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory bioactive substances that impede tumor formation68,69. The data from this study indicated that individuals in the case group, compared to the control group, who consumed higher quantities of wholegrains, vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, seeds, and fish, achieved a greater HLS. Regular moderate physical activity also lowers cancer risk by enhancing immune function, reducing inflammation, and limiting fat accumulation70. In the present research, higher physical activity levels were associated with higher HLS scores. Additionally, alcohol consumption contributes to carcinogenesis via oxidative stress and DNA damage from reactive oxygen and nitrogen species generated during metabolism71. In this study, the percentage of alcohol usage showed a decreasing trend from T1 to T3, indicating that participants in T3 had better HLS. Smoking promotes cancer through direct DNA damage by carcinogens and oxidative stress-induced inflammation72,73. Considering the substantial health and financial impact of cancer globally, implementing thorough prevention measures is crucial for successful disease management and control74.

The limitations of this research include:1 the inability to make causal inferences because of the retrospective nature of the study (i.e., hospital-based case–control design);2 selection bias is a concern in hospital-based case-control studies, as controls may not represent the true source population at risk for MM. Healthy controls seeking care at the same hospital might differ systematically from community controls regarding health-seeking behaviors, socioeconomic status, or undiagnosed subclinical conditions. Cases and controls were recruited concurrently from the same facility with identical eligibility criteria (age 20–75 years, no prior cancer); however, hospital controls may overestimate protective lifestyle factors compared to population-based controls3. Recall bias and differential misclassification represent major limitations of retrospective FFQ methodology. Myeloma cases may systematically over- or under-report past dietary and lifestyle exposures due to heightened diagnostic awareness or cognitive rumination following diagnosis, thereby distorting OR estimates. Non-differential misclassification among both cases and controls would bias associations toward the null, potentially obscuring genuine component-specific effects. Implausible energy intakes (< 500 or > 5000 kcal/day) were excluded to partially mitigate this concern;4 reverse causation remains a concern; however, as MM typically progresses through an asymptomatic precursor phase (MGUS), systematic lifestyle changes before diagnosis are unlikely, though prodromal symptoms such as fatigue or bone pain may have influenced some exposures before formal diagnosis. No sensitivity analyses excluding participants with recent unintentional weight loss or prodromal symptoms were performed, which should be addressed in future studies;5 residual confounding may still be present despite adjustment for demographic, clinical, occupational, and medication-related variables, due to unmeasured or insufficiently measured confounders;6 equal weighting of HLS components was consistent with previously validated composite lifestyle indices; however, differential weighting and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses should be considered in future studies;7 formal collinearity diagnostics such as variance inflation factors were not reported; however, multicollinearity concerns are minimized given that HLS was entered as a single composite score in regression models, individual components were modeled independently in secondary analyses, and energy intake was adjusted using the residual method prior to HLS construction.

Future research should validate these findings through prospective data collection with lifestyle exposures assessed prior to diagnosis. Large-scale population-based cohorts in Iran with longitudinal collection of dietary behaviors, physical activity levels, weight trajectories, and smoking history are needed, complemented by objective measures such as accelerometry and direct anthropometric assessments, and supported by biomarker analyses related to inflammation and metabolic impairment.

Conclusion

In this hospital-based case–control study of Iranian adults, higher adherence to HLS was associated with lower odds of MM. After adjustment for multiple potential confounders, higher HLS scores were associated with substantially lower odds of MM compared with lower scores (Overall OR = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.07–0.45). A clear dose-response gradient was evident across HLS tertiles in all multivariable models. Additionally, higher continuous HLS scores showed consistent inverse associations with MM odds. These findings provide support for a potential association between healthy lifestyle factors and lower MM odds, warranting confirmation through additional prospective cohort studies and intervention trials.
