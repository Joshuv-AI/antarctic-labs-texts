Behavioral telehealth in low-resource primary care settings for anxiety and depression in youth: protocol for a randomized effectiveness-implementation study
Authors: Francesca Rodriguez, Frances L Lynch, Araceli Gonzalez, Michelle Rozenman, John Dickerson, Maureen O’Keeffe-Rosetti, Judy Donald, Brigit Hatch, Gordon Barker, Michelle Henninger, Meagan Shaw, Katherine A Vaughn, V Robin Weersing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety and mood disorders in youth are prevalent and impairing, with high current and lifetime comorbidity. Untreated, these disorders lead to sustained functional impairment and increased risk for recurrent disorder and suicide. In addition, there are notable disparities in care, with both families experiencing increased social risk and youth of color significantly less likely to receive mental health services. In previous research, a primary-care-based transdiagnostic brief behavioral therapy (BBT) was developed and found to be effective for a broad population of youths with anxiety and depression. To increase dissemination potential to settings that serve low-resource families (e.g., community health centers [CHCs]), the intervention, now called “STEP-UP,” has been expanded to support a digital health framework and delivery in Spanish. We describe the protocol for a hybrid type 1 randomized-effectiveness trial to test the effects of STEP-UP in a community sample of youths with anxiety and/or depression.MethodYouths (age = 8–16, N = 220) will be randomized to (a) STEP-UP or (b) assisted referral to treatment as usual (TAU+). Clinical effectiveness will be assessed by masked independent evaluators at posttreatment (week 16) and at follow-up (week 32). Implementation data will be pulled from the following: (a) Surveys of health system leaders, (b) surveys of and interviews with STEP-UP clinicians, and (c) electronic health record (EHR) and health system administrative data. Specific aims include testing the clinical effectiveness of STEP-UP and engagement of the intervention mechanism (Aim 1), probing cost-effectiveness (Aim 2), testing social determinants of health (SDOH) predictors and moderators to evaluate robustness of effects (Aim 3), and identifying target mechanisms for future implementation trials using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR, Aim 4).DiscussionResults of the randomized trial may help move effective treatments for youth anxiety and depression problems into widespread community practice.Trial registrationNCT06273982. Introduction

Anxiety and mood disorders in youth are prevalent [1] and impairing [2–7], with a high degree of current and lifetime comorbidity, due in part to shared etiologic factors [8]. Both disorders are markedly undertreated, with only 1 in 5 anxious youth and 2 in 5 depressed youth reporting any lifetime mental health service use, the lowest rates for any youth mental health condition [9]. Further, there are notable disparities in care. Youth experiencing greater risks associated with social determinants of health (SDOH; e.g., housing insecurity) and youth of color are both at higher risk for disorder [9–13]. Despite higher risk, these youth are significantly less likely to receive mental health services compared to similarly affected, non-Hispanic white (NHW) youth, and those with greater access to resources [9–13]. Low-resource families, such as those served by community health centers (CHCs), often experience the burden of multiple intersectional risks and difficulty accessing evidence-based mental health care [14]. The development of an effective primary-care-based treatment has been proposed as a key tool to (1) broadly increase access to quality care for all youths with anxiety and depression [15–21] and (2) to reduce disparities in mental health care, helping to improve the public health of the population as a whole [13, 22, 23].

In this protocol paper, we describe the rationale, aims, design, and methods of a randomized clinical trial (RCT) to test the clinical effectiveness and probe the implementation readiness of a primary-care-based transdiagnostic intervention for anxiety and depression in underserved lower-income youths. Recent findings support the exploration of transdiagnostic interventions as a promising treatment option for youths with anxiety and depression. These interventions target the underlying mechanisms and symptoms shared across disorders to treat them in a single, streamlined approach [24–26]. A growing body of evidence from RCTs suggests that transdiagnostic interventions are effective treatments for anxiety and depression in youth, demonstrated by the medium to large effect sizes of these interventions compared to waitlist and active treatment control conditions [27–29].

A transdiagnostic approach may be particularly well-suited for settings that serve low-resource families, such as CHCs, and be capable of mitigating known challenges in these settings, including high diagnostic comorbidity rates, clinician burden of needing to learn multiple single-disorder treatments, and infrequent therapy visits by families [30–32]. Reflecting this potential, there is growing interest in testing transdiagnostic models in community settings both nationwide and globally [33–36]. In a recent investigation, researchers tested the effects of the Unified Protocols for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders in Adolescents (UP-A) in community mental health settings [33]. However, despite showing strong positive effects in previous investigations [28, 37, 38], the researchers did not find significant differences between the UP-A and comparison conditions for their primary clinician-rated outcome metric when disseminated into this active practice setting [33]. The investigators note that these findings may reflect implementation challenges such as high clinician turnover and a potential mismatch between the intervention’s demands and the level of organizational support [33]. Thus, implementation-focused research is still needed to understand how to best integrate transdiagnostic interventions into real-world clinical contexts.

Transdiagnostic brief behavioral therapy

As part of this broader research effort, Weersing and colleagues [29, 39] developed brief behavioral therapy (BBT), a primary-care-based transdiagnostic intervention for youth anxiety and depression. BBT targets the core mechanism of behavioral avoidance and withdrawal in the face of negative affect that is common to both anxiety and depression. The BBT model does not rely on cognitive restructuring, a central ingredient in other evidence-based transdiagnostic models for anxiety and depression. In contrast, BBT combines the more action-oriented techniques of exposure and behavioral activation, both identified as key elements for treatment response [40, 41], and shown to be less challenging for clinicians to learn and deploy [42] and easier for youth to assimilate [43]. These techniques are considered particularly critical given the deployment focus of BBT in primary care settings, where a broader set of providers may implement BBT in practice compared to those in specialty mental health.

The BBT intervention was tested in a multi-site face-to-face (F2F) effectiveness trial that recruited a large (N = 185) sample of youths, most of whom were covered by health insurance [29]. In this trial, youth who received BBT demonstrated greater clinical improvement, better functioning, and less severe anxiety as rated by clinicians posttreatment and at long-term follow-up, compared to youths referred to community mental health [29, 44]. These positive effects were significantly associated with a reduction in behavioral avoidance, the intervention’s hypothesized target mechanism [45]. In addition, a neuroimaging study conducted in a subsample of youth who received BBT suggests that the intervention may also increase activity in reward-processing centers of the brain [46]. BBT has also shown strong positive effects on non-targeted clinical outcomes, including reductions in behavioral problems, hyperactivity, and suicidality per parent report [47]. Notably, the BBT effectiveness trial demonstrated that the intervention was cost-effective [48] and may have reduced disparities in care, with Hispanic/Latine youths faring particularly well compared to their outcomes in community services [22, 29].

The breadth of positive outcomes associated with BBT serves as compelling evidence for testing the scalability of the intervention in broader settings and populations. In the current study, the core therapeutic content of BBT remains unchanged [29, 39]. However, the delivery method expands from an in-person F2F modality to include live, video-based telehealth delivery with shared content hosted online. This adapted BBT program is called “STEP-UP.” The STEP-UP program comes as health systems increasingly turn to using technology to improve the efficiency of care delivery and to extend behavioral health capacity. In addition, telehealth and digitally supported treatments may be particularly effective in expanding mental health access for traditionally underserved populations, including youth experiencing SDOH risks and children of color [14]. Given the need to increase capacity, many virtual behavioral health programs have emerged, but few involve evidence-based practices (EBPs), especially for youth [49]. In this protocol report, we detail how the current STEP-UP trial aims to build an evidence base for a promising EBP by conducting the study in settings with a broad population of youth who have been historically underserved by mental health services. Notably, the current study is designed to collect data relevant for future large-scale implementation and dissemination trials should the STEP-UP program prove effective. EBPs often take decades to enter practice, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and others have called for methods to accelerate this translation [50, 51]. One approach is hybrid effectiveness–implementation designs, which provide a road map to address implementation [52]. Table 2 summarizes this progression and situates the current study on the “translational highway” [53] from efficacy to implementation. STEP-UP builds on a multi-year program of deployment-focused [29] research; however, there has not yet been a randomized-effectiveness trial for low-resource vulnerable youth, especially those served in “safety-net” settings. By “safety net,” we refer to providers and agencies that accept uninsured families or families with public insurance (e.g., Medi-Cal, Medicaid) and may have substantially fewer resources to provide care. To address this gap in the literature, the current study is a type 1 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial that adopts the dual focus of testing the effectiveness of STEP-UP in this new sample and setting while strategically collecting data for future implementation efforts.

Aims of the current research

The study aims the following:

Test the clinical effectiveness of STEP-UP and engagement of the target mechanism. We hypothesize that STEP-UP will have superior effects on youth functioning [54], anxiety [55], and depression [56] than assisted referral to community treatment as usual (TAU+). We hypothesize that STEP-UP will engage its target mechanism of action, and that reduction in avoidance [57] will mediate the effects of STEP-UP. Methods

Design

The STEP-UP study is funded by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) (R01MH131698). Collection of data related to gender identity is supported by San Diego State University (SDSU) Research Support Funds. In a type 1 hybrid effectiveness–implementation design, STEP-UP will be compared to assisted referral to traditional outpatient services in the community. In addition, surveys will be collected from health care leadership and providers, and interviews will be conducted with a subsample of providers and patients to gain their perspective on the program and the manner in which it was implemented. Youths (ages 8–16; N = 220) will be identified through EHR and clinician referral for study recruitment. Eligible youths who consent to the study will be randomized 1:1 to (a) STEP-UP or (b) TAU+. Data will be collected from participating families during four assessment windows: baseline interview (week 0), during the STEP-UP active intervention phase (week 8), at the close of the active STEP-UP phase (week 16), and long-term follow-up (week 32). Reflecting health system priorities, families in both arms will be screened for SDOH risk at intake and provided community resource navigation support. The study will follow the CONSORT statement [60] and the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) guidelines for reporting [61]. First, we present the randomized trial design and methods for Aims 1–3. Next, we present the design and methods for Aim 4, the implementation analysis.

Randomized trial methods

Participants and study setting

Participants will include youths (age = 8–16 at initial screen) drawn from primary care/behavioral health settings that serve low-resource youth, such as CHCs. We will partner specifically with OCHIN, Inc. member CHCs, which provide a range of low-cost primary health care services (e.g., behavioral health, school-based services) in rural and medically underserved communities. As a national nonprofit health information technology consultancy, OCHIN provides a fully hosted and shared EHR platform with tailored tools and expertise that are currently used by more than 33,000 providers to support connected, whole-patient care for more than 6 million people nationwide and hosts one of the nation’s largest practice-based research networks (PBRN) that serves patients in safety-net settings.

Sample size

Over a 5-year period, we aim to recruit and randomize 220 youths. The sample size was calculated in relation to planned analyses of clinical effectiveness. See power for clinical aims below. Our power estimates use a conservative 30% attrition rate to ensure that the investigation will be able to provide a rigorous test of hypotheses.

Eligibility criteria

Clinics will be identified that have a substantial number (e.g., > 100) of youth meeting EHR screening criteria (e.g., age 8–16, target anxiety/depression diagnoses on an encounter or problem list within the last 2 years, was not prescribed antipsychotic and/or mood stabilizer medication within the last year). Participants will be interviewed by masked independent evaluators (IEs) and then selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) A current diagnosis of a target anxiety (social anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, separation anxiety disorder) and/or mood disorder (major depressive disorder [MDD], persistent depressive disorder [PDD]) on the Mini‐International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents (MINI-KID) [62] or (2) scores in the clinically elevated range on the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Disorders (SCARED-P, SCARED-C) [63] and/or the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI-2 P, CDI-2 C) [64]. Youths must live with a legal consenting parent or guardian (hereafter referred to as “parent”) and be able to complete study assessments and intervention sessions in English. Parent participants can complete study assessments and intervention sessions in English or Spanish.

To enhance generalizability, we will exclude only those youths at baseline who (1) may benefit from a higher intensity of treatment, or different treatment focus, as indexed by (a) current clinically significant symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (MINI-KID) [62], (b) suicidal ideation with plan or suicidal behavior within the last 6 months, (c) serious and/or frequent non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) within the last 3 months, (d) current substance abuse, (e) report of youth physical or sexual abuse within the 6 months prior to baseline, and (f) parent/guardian does not identify anxiety and/or depression as a treatment priority, (2) have received other mental health treatment for anxiety or depression within the past six months, or (3) are receiving lithium or an antipsychotic medication at baseline. Youths receiving other medications may be included if management has been stable at current dose for at least 8 weeks or if medication was discontinued for at least 4 weeks prior to enrollment. Youths will not be precluded from beginning medication after enrollment if recommended by their medical provider.

Recruitment and enrollment

We will adopt a multistage recruitment and screening process to (1) identify partner clinics serving our target population (e.g., CHCs in the OCHIN PBRN), (2) generate a recruitment pool of potential participants using EHR data and clinical referral, and (3) assess participant study eligibility (see Fig. 1). To address implementation questions (Aim 4), clinic providers and staff will be consented later in the investigation and invited to complete surveys and/or qualitative interviews on program acceptability and readiness for broad implementation of STEP-UP .

Recruitment flow chart

After a clinic is identified, families from the health system will be recruited either through (a) identification of youths with target diagnoses in the EHR, (b) direct clinical referral from care providers, or (c) family self-referral (e.g., referring a sibling of a child identified through [a] or [b] above). Potentially eligible families will be contacted by research staff via mail and telephone outreach to assess interest and screen for eligibility (see inclusion criteria). At baseline, recruitment staff will seek verbal consent from parents and assent from youths. The informed consent document will be made available upon request. Following consent, masked IEs will conduct the baseline assessment via phone or video to determine eligibility. Table 3 describes the schedule of enrollment, interventions, and assessment.

Randomization

Participants meeting eligibility criteria will be randomly assigned at the family level to STEP-UP or TAU+, following a 1:1 allocation ratio. A research staff member will conduct the randomization live with participants using a computer-generated algorithm embedded within the study’s secure tracking platform to ensure allocation concealment. We will balance randomization on Hispanic/Latine status (Hispanic/Latine vs. non-Hispanic/Latine), the presence of clinically significant depression (current major depressive episode on the MINI-KID [62] and/or Children’s Depression Rating Scale-Revised (CDRS-R) [56] score ≥ 41), and age (child [8.0–11.9] vs. adolescent [12.0–17.3]) using stratified varying block size design.

Treatment orientation

At the time of randomization, parents in both arms will receive a pre-treatment orientation session to address potential obstacles to and enhance engagement with pursuing mental health care. This session is modeled on evidence-based practices for reducing mental health no-show rates [65, 66] and guidelines for SDOH resource navigation [67–70]. There are three key steps to this approach. In Step 1, the research coordinator will host a video visit with parents of eligible youths following the baseline assessment to (a) welcome them to the trial, (b) reveal randomization outcome, and (c) provide feedback from the assessment. We will couple this feedback with information on the course of untreated anxiety and depression and a discussion of the potential positive effects of mental health care. In Step 2, we aim to enhance parent efficacy by making the experience of connecting with care more personal and concrete. While screen sharing, the research coordinator will log in to the project website and demonstrate navigation (e.g., to STEP-UP or clinic-specific TAU+ page). Parents will then practice logging in and navigating the site, with technical assistance. In Step 3, the research coordinator will problem-solve barriers to care associated with SDOH risk endorsed by parents during the baseline interview. To do this, the research coordinator will validate parent concerns and problem-solve options to address needs, supported by a clinic-specific SDOH resource page, and information on any clinic navigation services, such as those increasingly provided by CHCs in the OCHIN network [69–73].

Interventions

STEP-UP

STEP-UP is a brief (8–12 sessions), transdiagnostic behavioral intervention that targets the core mechanisms of avoidance and withdrawal in the face of negative affect common to both anxiety and depression. Conventional cognitive behavioral (CBT) interventions include many techniques designed to impact a wide range of central and associated deficits in cognitive and behavioral skills [74, 75]. In the development of STEP-UP, we winnowed the broad array of available CBT techniques for anxiety and depression to focus on graded engagement— an integration of exposure (anxiety) and behavioral activation (depression) techniques [29, 44]. As shown in Table 4, during the first 25% of treatment, youths and parents (a) learn relaxation techniques (e.g., deep breathing, mental imagery) and problem-solving skills to help manage stress, (b) identify areas where anxiety/depression interferes with functioning, and (c) collaborate with their provider to develop personalized plans to engage or reengage with these critical life tasks. The remaining sessions (75% of treatment) consist of active behavioral practice of these plans within sessions and between sessions as homework. STEP-UP has a minimum dose of eight sessions, with flexibility to add four additional sessions of extended behavioral practice and to deliver all sessions within 16 weeks. This dosing was designed to encourage frequent practice while accommodating practical barriers to weekly treatment that may be more frequently encountered by families facing adverse SDOH and by racially/ethnically minoritized youths.

While the core content is unchanged from the previous efficacious in-person version of the intervention (BBT) [29, 44], STEP-UP has been adapted to a scalable, digital platform. This adaptation includes an English- and Spanish-language intervention website and a protocol for telehealth sessions with the STEP-UP provider. To enhance stability across devices, operating systems, and generations of technology, we will use a web browser interface for STEP-UP content, decouple the website from the video visit tool, and use the preferred HIPAA-compliant telehealth platform for each health system. To encourage parent involvement in sessions, we will also add scalable supports such as text appointment reminders and brief after-visit text/email summaries, in line with best practices for parent treatment engagement interventions (e.g., accessibility promotion, use of technology to enhance communication in and out of session) [66, 76–78]. While the STEP-UP model is designed as a telehealth protocol, different practice structures may face varying constraints or have preferred methods of care delivery. For this reason, intervention sessions also may occur in person to avoid overburdening a participant or provider. For example, this may occur if the participant has a medical appointment directly before or after a scheduled STEP-UP session or if a participant is seen during a school health clinic visit.

Assisted referral to treatment as usual (TAU+)

The comparison condition in this project is the most common mental health treatment option in primary care—referral to outpatient community mental health (TAU+). TAU+ provides a useful public health benchmark for STEP-UP and enables us to collect meaningful cost-effectiveness data. In addition, we view TAU+ as a more ethical comparison condition than other options, such as a no-treatment control, waiting list, or “weak” intervention (e.g., psychoeducation). Youths referred to TAU+ have the universe of potential mental health interventions available to them, and their families are not restricted by this study in their choice of treatments. As such, TAU+ also serves as a conservative test against a range of potentially active treatments (e.g., SSRIs).

Given the barriers to accessing care in our target population, we will design a facilitated version of TAU+, with static and dynamic supports to assist families in accessing mental health care. As a static support, we will collaborate with individual health system representatives to build clinic-specific webpages for TAU+ families that include information on accessing local community mental health services, customized for each clinic’s geographic location. As a dynamic support, TAU+ parents will receive treatment orientation from the research coordinator as part of the randomization procedure described above. Following orientation, two supportive follow-up emails will be sent to parents to encourage connecting with care.

Training and consultation

Once a health system agrees to participate in the study, clinical providers from the system will be trained as STEP-UP interventionists by the treatment developer and the intervention team. Staff are not required to be licensed clinicians and may hold a variety of roles in the clinic (e.g., social worker, psychologist, nurse), as long as deemed appropriate by the clinic for the provision of short-term mental health interventions in their clinical setting. Training procedures were developed in prior studies and consist of the equivalent of two half days of training, plus weekly consultation for the duration of study participation. Training workshops will be held when (a) clinics are recruited into the study and (b) in response to staff turnover at existing sites. In training, the study website and parallel therapist manual will be reviewed, and sessions will be role-played. Following didactic training, the first two cases for each clinician will be reviewed in detail by developers of the treatment program (VRW, AG). Additional recordings will be reviewed for consultation at the interventionist’s request. Clinical group consultation is designed to occur weekly throughout the project per site. STEP-UP sessions will be recorded using native health system platforms for secure telehealth (e.g., HIPAA-compliant Zoom) or secure digital voice recorders if sessions take place in person. Providers who are observed as deviating from protocol through the consultation groups will be provided opportunities for retraining and support. Data from all providers will be included in intent-to-treat (ITT) analyses, while providers with low adherence may be removed from per protocol analyses.

Trial oversight and monitoring

Ethics and data safety monitoring

The study protocol and activities are reviewed, approved, and monitored by the Kaiser Permanente Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (IRB no. 00000405). A Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) monitors this investigation and reports to NIMH and the study’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). The primary role of the DSMB will be to monitor the safety of participants in both the STEP-UP and TAU+ referral comparison group, report all adverse effects in a timely and consistent fashion to NIMH and IRB, and check the validity and integrity of the data. The DSMB will be composed of three members, none directly involved in the project or employed by the participating institutions. Note all changes to the protocol will be reviewed by the IRB and, as deemed necessary, communicated to participants. Further, substantive changes to inclusion/exclusion criteria or primary outcomes will be reviewed by the study’s DSMB and funder and communicated on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Structure of the study team

The project will be led by the trial steering committee (TSC), headed by the multiple Principal Investigators (mPIs) team. The TSC will meet weekly to review overall study operations, including progress in recruitment and enrollment, retention of participants in assessment and the intervention arms, technical issues with study infrastructure (e.g., video visits), data accrual, and adverse events and regulatory matters. Project investigators and staff will organize into teams focusing on (a) study coordination (leadership team, as above), (b) recruitment and enrollment of participants, (c) assessment quality assurance for baselines and follow-up assessments, (d) intervention supervision and maintenance of the intervention website, (e) data management and analysis, and (f) implementation data collection and analysis.

Data management

Data from research measures (e.g., clinical assessments, surveys, interviews) will be retained by the study data coordination center, Kaiser Permanente Northwest Center for Health Research (KPCHR), and SDSU. Data collected for all participants during the recruitment process will be kept strictly confidential, except as may be required in the case of a psychiatric or medical crisis (reporting abuse, imminent danger to self, or others). All research files will be kept on a secure server that uses multiple layers of data protection.

Risk management

Several measures will be taken to protect participants in the study. Our Data Safety Monitoring Plan provides definitions of serious adverse events (SAEs) and adverse events (AEs) and details the triggers and timeline of reporting these events to the investigators, DSMB, and IRB. Throughout the study, both intervention and control participants will be assessed regularly for evidence of clinical deterioration. If a problem is detected of imminent and crisis status, we will take appropriate immediate action, including calling emergency services to perform a health and welfare check. Youths may be formally withdrawn from the STEP-UP study intervention if they (a) develop a condition that meets criteria for exclusion (e.g., psychosis) or (b) show evidence of functional deterioration that clinically indicates alternate services incompatible with continued participation (e.g., inpatient hospitalization). Youths or families also may choose to withdraw from the study intervention or from study procedures (including assessments) of their own accord.

Measures and schedule of assessments

Youth and parents

Reliability and quality of assessments

All major assessments will feature a combination of self-report surveys completed electronically in our data management platform, REDCap [79, 80], and measures administered by IEs masked to participant randomization status via video visit or telephone. The interim week 8 assessment will consist of online self-report surveys for the youth and parents. All parent assessments will be available in both English and Spanish. When available, we will use the official translated version. Measures not available in Spanish will be translated via a certified translator. All members of the TSC will be unmasked to randomization status. If masking of the IE is unintentionally compromised, the IE will notify the mPI team, and a new outcome assessor will conduct future assessments. IEs will also be unmasked if necessary for participant safety management. Training in assessments and crisis management procedures will be provided to recruitment staff and IEs in year 1.

Note that data analysts will have access to randomization status, but this status will not be stored with the data files and will not be accessed during data quality assurance or building base analytic files containing the clinical outcome measures. Randomization status will be entered into final data analytic models to perform analyses and interpret outcomes. The objective nature of outcome assessments (i.e., rated by blinded IEs) limits any impact of unblinding of analysts at the moment of interpretation.

Retention plan

We will seek to retain families by (a) obtaining comprehensive contact information, (b) providing adequate compensation for study assessments to offset the time and hassle involved in completing assessments, (c) engaging in additional periodic warm contacts to promote engagement, and (d) texting and emailing to remind participants to complete surveys, answer questions, and check in. In addition, we have designed our study follow-up procedures to be as convenient and low-burden as possible.

Measures of sample characteristics

The presence of target diagnoses

The MINI-KID [62] is a brief, structured diagnostic interview for DSM-IV and ICD-10 disorders. The MINI-KID was designed to have a median administration time of 15 min and has been used successfully in youth with anxiety and depression [81, 82], demonstrating sound validity and reliability [83].

SDOH factors

The general information sheet (GIS) includes age, sex assigned at birth, race/ethnicity, languages spoken/preferred, school placement, parental education, employment status, and income. In addition, data on gender identity will be collected with nonfederal funds. Familial disadvantage will be assessed with an SDOH tool adapted from the Accountable Health Communities Health-Related Social Needs Screening Tool [84]. Questions were selected to be similar to those built into the OCHIN Epic EHR system and collected in routine care. The SDOH tool can be analyzed as a single score (i.e., any risk factor present?) or individual factors probed (e.g., food insecurity, housing instability, financial stress). Community-level SDOH are assessed with the Child Opportunity Index (COI) [85], a geocoded resource using census data to map neighborhood disadvantage. Parents will complete the Barriers to Treatment Participation Scale (BTPS) [86]; the practical barriers subscale summarizes resource-relevant obstacles to engaging in mental health care (e.g., no transportation to mental health site). We also developed the Parent Expectations of Treatment Scale (EOTS) to probe parents’ willingness to engage in various therapy tasks (e.g., ability/willingness to attend weekly telehealth sessions); questions were designed to be parallel to the posttreatment assessment of engagement [87].

Comorbidity in youth and parents

The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [88] is a youth-report (SDQ-C) and parent-report (SDQ-P) assessment of child behavioral and emotional problems with extensive reliability and validity data. The Patient Health Questionnaire Somatic Anxiety and Depressive Symptoms (PHQ-SADS) [89] is a well-validated self-report measure of internalizing symptoms in adults. We will use the PHQ-SADS to characterize parental psychopathology.

Measures of clinical effectiveness (Aim 1)

Primary outcome measures

The Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS) [54] is a widely used measure of functional impairment, rated at the end of each interview as a summary judgment by the IE. The CGAS demonstrates good reliability and was sensitive to treatment in the F2F STEP-UP RCT [29]. The Pediatric Anxiety Rating Scale (PARS) [55] and CDRS-R [56] are IE-rated measures of anxiety and depression, respectively, that integrate youth and parent report. Both have strong reliability and validity and are sensitive to treatment.

Secondary outcome measures

The SCARED [63] is a youth-report (SCARED-C) and parent-report (SCARED-P) measure of anxiety symptoms. It has been validated in diverse samples [90, 91] with good internal consistency, has shown sensitivity to treatment effects [92], and is available in Spanish [93]. The CDI2 [64] is a youth (CDI2-C) and parent report (CDI2-P) of youth depressive symptoms with sound psychometric properties in English- and Spanish-speaking versions [94, 95].

Target mechanism

We will assess engagement of treatment mechanism through self-report measures completed at major interviews and during the active phase of treatment (week 8). The Child Avoidance Measure for self (CAMS) and parent (CAMP) query youths’ avoidance behaviors in response to negative affect [57]. The CAMS and CAMP have strong psychometric properties, with sensitivity to treatment response in exposure therapy [57].

Measures for the cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA, Aim 2)

The primary effectiveness measure for the cost-effectiveness analyses (CEA) is the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (Peds-QL) [58]. The Peds-QL is a brief, standardized assessment of youths’ health-related quality of life by either youth or parent report. The measure has high reliability and validity, is responsive to clinical change, and is commonly used to construct QALYs [48, 96, 97]. In addition, we will explore incremental cost-effectiveness using anxiety-free day and depression-free day as secondary clinical effectiveness metrics [48, 98].

Use of non-step-up services will be assessed with the Child and Adolescent Services Assessment (CASA) [99]. The CASA measures health service utilization and has good reliability and validity. Youth and parents will separately report services that the youth received. This method allows us to gather information on the variety of services that youths are likely to use (e.g., health system, school, and social welfare) without having to obtain data from multiple systems. Given that youths came from a wide range of community clinics and geographic areas, this method is the most practical for obtaining comparable data for all youths. To create TAU+ costs, we will apply nationally representative unit costs developed for previous studies to youth and parent report of services obtained on the CASA [99]. The TAU+ arm will also include costs associated with referral, such as telecalls by project staff to support families’ connection with care.

To calculate the costs of the STEP-UP intervention, we will use records from the RCT about activities, facilities, and overhead costs. We will use a micro-costing approach. We will include costs of STEP-UP sessions or time therapists and staff spent with youths and parents outside of visits, as well as time spent in clinical training for STEP-UP, supervision, and materials.

Note that research-specific costs will be excluded. Thus, the cost associated with research assessment interviews, randomization reveal, or data management will not be included in either the STEP-UP or TAU+ arm.

Measures of engagement in care (Aim 3)

Engagement in mental health care

In both STEP-UP and TAU+, youth and parents will complete My Thoughts about Therapy (MTT-Y, MTT-CG) [87], a 35-item measure assessing engagement in treatment. We will also examine if treatment dose (e.g., total no. of sessions, receipt of > 1 session, receipt of > 8 sessions) varies by SDOH risk.

STEP-UP specific measures

Clinicians will be prompted each session to provide therapist ratings of engagement in STEP-UP sessions, including parental attendance at sessions, parental engagement in sessions, and youth homework completion. In addition, we will explore website utilization data from the STEP-UP intervention pages to explore process factors only available within the STEP-UP arm.

Analytic plan for Aims 1, 2, and 3

Across all analyses, treatment dropouts and subjects withdrawn from the study will be encouraged to continue participating in study assessments to allow for ITT analysis of primary outcomes [100]. We will conduct a sensitivity analysis, using different methods of imputation to arrive at our conclusions based on the results and our assessment of the missingness mechanisms. Such an approach is feasible because of software (e.g., STATA [101]) that contains the necessary programs for completer analysis, last observation carried forward (LOCF), and multiple imputation. If, however, we suspect that the missingness is informative (that is, not missing at random), we will write programs to address biases using weighted distributions.

We anticipate higher attrition than the previous multi-site RCT that demonstrated the effectiveness of the transdiagnostic behavioral intervention that formed the basis of STEP-UP [29, 44] due to increased SDOH barriers experienced by low-resource patients, such as holding multiple jobs and a lack of stable housing. We will strive to maximize follow-up data collection using a variety of methods. We have also accounted for attrition (30%) in our power calculations.

Clinical outcome analyses (Aim 1)

We will test whether there are differences in the trajectories of scores (CGAS, PARS, CDRS-R) across time and between arms with two-level hierarchical linear models (HLMs) in a growth curve framework [102–104]. The first level of the model will include time as a predictor, modeling within-person variation. The second level will include a dummy variable for arm (1 = STEP-UP, 0 = TAU+) as the predictor variable for both the random effects of the intercept and the time slope. We will probe any significant arm × time interactions by graphing the simple-effects equations to determine whether the observed pattern is consistent with the hypotheses. Compared to the TAU+ condition, a pattern in which the combined STEP-UP condition demonstrates a greater decrease over time in PARS and CDRS-R scores and an increase over time in the CGAS score supports intervention effectiveness.

We will evaluate the mediation of STEP-UP effects by testing whether change in participant-rated avoidance behavior assessed at week 0 and week 8 statistically mediates the effects of STEP-UP on our clinical outcomes (CGAS, PARS, CDRS-R) at week 16; we will also probe the influence of change in avoidance in weeks 8–16 to evaluate impact on clinical outcomes at week 16. Analysis will be repeated twice for two measures of avoidance, once for youth ratings (CAMS) and once using parent ratings (CAMP). We will regress the appropriate follow-up measure of avoidance on avoidance at baseline and a dummy variable representing randomization arm (1 = STEP-UP, 0 = TAU+), and we will regress the appropriate measure of follow-up on our clinical outcomes (CGAS, PARS, CDRS-R) on the follow-up measure of avoidance and study condition. We will assess the indirect effect using the product coefficients method [105–107], estimating standard errors using bias-corrected bootstrapping [108].

Power for clinical aims

Power analyses for the primary outcomes were conducted using a combination of PASS software (Version 20) [109] and STATA (Version 16.1) [101]. For Aim 1, we estimate that we have 91% power to detect a small effect size (Cohen’s d < 0.5) or larger between STEP-UP and TAU+ groups (assuming STEP-UP n = 110, TAU+ n = 110, at baseline). Overall, the study is very well positioned to provide a strong test of the central aim of the effectiveness of intervention on clinical outcomes. Power analyses for the mechanistic role of reduction in avoidance on our clinical outcome metrics (CGAS, PARS, CDRS-R) were also strong. For mediation analysis using CAMS and CAMP, we will be able to detect an indirect effect of 0.06 or larger with 80% power using a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval in both sets of analyses, which is considered a small effect. We have conservatively estimated that 30% of the enrolled youth will be lost to follow-up by the final 32-week assessment. We also probed a range of possible attrition scenarios, and we are well powered (> 86%) to assess the effectiveness of STEP-UP relative to TAU at even higher rates of attrition (e.g., 40%).

Cost-effectiveness analyses (Aim 2)

We will conduct patient-level ITT incremental CEA of delivering STEP-UP in the clinic compared to TAU+. Based on promising cost-effectiveness findings in the F2F RCT [48], we hypothesize that STEP-UP will be cost-effective compared to TAU+ in terms of cost per QALY [58]. We will also explore incremental cost-effectiveness using two other clinical outcomes that may be relevant to health system decision-makers, cost per anxiety-free day, and cost per depression-free day [48, 98]. We anticipate that costs will not be normally distributed; thus, we plan to use the net benefit regression method with ordinary least-squares regression analyses. The robustness of the parametric tests will be confirmed using nonparametric bootstrapping with a single model with 1000 replications using the bias corrected and accelerated method. Adjusted differences in costs and clinical effects between the intervention arms will be estimated using ordinary least-squares regression models with bootstrap interval estimates, and all analyses will be adjusted for baseline characteristics.

A major component of the CEA, the costs, will be assessed by the following: (a) calculating direct costs of the STEP-UP inputs (e.g., labor, equipment, supplies, facilities, management), (b) measuring the total other TAU service use for each participant (including costs related to assisted referral to TAU mental health for the control group), and (c) applying a costing algorithm to translate TAU service use into TAU costs. We will use a micro-costing approach that we have used in prior studies [48, 110]. We will use staff reports of time spent in STEP-UP-related tasks to calculate the time of different components of STEP-UP. The direct costs included are staff salary and fringe benefits (e.g., STEP-UP clinicians) and general administration and overhead. We will also track and include costs related to training and supervision for the STEP-UP providers. Purchased inputs will be valued at their invoiced cost. Professional labor inputs will be valued using unit cost estimates developed from prior studies [48, 110]. It is possible that participation in STEP-UP will change the total amount of TAU services used by the participants in key service sectors (e.g., school health). For instance, if STEP-UP works well, STEP-UP participants may be less likely to need additional care for anxiety from the primary care provider. We capture this possibility by including the difference in total TAU service costs between the treatment and control groups. Comprehensive service utilization profiles will be developed on participants using the CASA at baseline and each follow-up. The CASA collects comprehensive data on all types of health and other services received by the participant in multiple service settings relevant to youth. To assign costs to TAU utilization, we will use unit cost coefficients developed for previous studies [48, 110]. We will describe and compare the average cost per participant and average cost per unit change in QALYs. The timeframe of the analysis is less than 12 months, so we will not need to use discounting [111].

We will conduct cost-effectiveness analyses from several perspectives including the societal perspective which attempts to include costs and outcomes for all groups impacted by the intervention (e.g., patient, health system) and analyses focused on the perspective of decision-makers in health systems relevant to implementation of the intervention in a broader range of community health care systems. We will also conduct sensitivity analyses to examine uncertainty around key variables, such as unit cost estimates. Missing data will be imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Sample sizes comparable to ours have been sufficient for CEA using similar techniques [48, 112].

In addition, we will collect data from key informants (patients, providers, leadership) using brief surveys and semi-structured qualitative interviews to identify mechanisms likely to influence future implementation. Process data collection

We will collect process data throughout the study. We will examine administrative records from the research team regarding barriers to participation in the STEP-UP study, and using electronic records, we will gather data on how often providers are using the EHR tool and the completeness of the data collected with the tool (e.g., information on progress through STEP-UP sessions). Similarly, we will examine electronic data on family utilization of the STEP-UP website.

Survey data collection

We will conduct brief REDCap surveys of health system leaders and providers to explore health system readiness for broad implementation of STEP-UP. We will use several standardized measures (the Acceptability of Intervention Measure [AIM], Intervention Appropriateness Measure [IAM], Feasibility of Intervention Measure [FIM], the Implementation Leadership Survey [ILS]) [113, 114] as part of these surveys. For the providers delivering STEP-UP, we will use the Evidence Based Practice Attitude Scale (EBPAS) [115]. We also include questions specific to STEP-UP implementation (e.g., potential barriers to implementing STEP-UP). Across all health systems, we aim to include the following: (a) all participating STEP-UP clinicians and (b) other health system stakeholders not directly involved in providing STEP-UP. These other stakeholders include health system leadership (e.g., medical directors) and clinicians who may refer to STEP-UP but are not providers of the model (e.g., primary care clinicians).

Qualitative data collection

We will aim to conduct individual semi-structured qualitative interviews with all participating STEP-UP clinicians and youth/parents (n = 20) to provide a more nuanced assessment of implementation readiness from the provider and patient perspectives. The focus of the qualitative portion of the study will be to identify specific workflow issues or youth/parent barriers to participation in STEP-UP to aid in future implementation.

Procedures for implementation data collection

All STEP-UP providers and other health system stakeholders will be consented prior to data collection on implementation constructs. We will recruit approximately 35 leaders/non-STEP-UP (NSU) clinicians who will be asked to fill out a brief REDCAP survey regarding the acceptability and fit of STEP-UP to their setting and other similar systems. NSU clinicians will be invited to participate through engagement of the leadership of participating clinics. They will receive an email invitation to participate, including informed consent language.

For the qualitative interviews, we will invite all providers who are trained in STEP-UP to participate in semi-structured interviews to help understand how well STEP-UP worked in their setting, any barriers they encountered, and their suggestions for improvements in the program and processes. We anticipate interviewing approximately 20 providers.

We will invite a subset of youth and parents who have completed participation in the STEP-UP intervention to participate in qualitative interviews. These semi-structured interviews are intended to help understand how well STEP-UP worked from the patient/parent perspective, any barriers they encountered, and their suggestions for improvements in the program and processes. We anticipate interviewing approximately 20 youth/parents. We will seek to recruit a range of youth/parents from different settings and different participation levels.

Interviews will be held by phone or video platform, conducted by study team members, and will last about 20–30 min.

Interviews will be digitally recorded using password-protected devices, professionally transcribed, and imported into software (e.g., Dedoose) for management of qualitative data. The data will then be coded by study team members, and we will conduct thematic analysis of the coded data [116].

Implementation analyses

Implementation analyses will focus on exploring readiness for broad implementation of STEP-UP, identifying barriers to STEP-UP implementation, and probing for needed modifications to intervention content or workflow to improve implementation and sustainment.

Analyses of the process data will be descriptive. We will examine the frequency and range of use for both the use of the EHR tool and the study website. We will also describe barriers to participation and use of STEP-UP that are collected during the conduct of the trial and recorded by research staff interacting with clinic staff and patients.

We will conduct descriptive analyses of the brief survey data to explore opinions and experiences and to generate hypotheses to be tested in future research. The data will then be coded by study team members, and we will conduct thematic analysis of the coded data. The focus will be identifying potential mechanisms of successful implementation and possible barriers to implementation in future research.

Interpretation of the pattern of implementation results

Given that this study is a hybrid type 1 design, our analyses will focus on exploring whether the pattern of results is supportive of STEP-UP implementation rather than testing specific hypotheses related to implementation strategies. The study team will harmonize data across quantitative and qualitative measures and methods. We will summarize our findings within each CFIR domain across the different data sources to present an overall picture of the support for implementation of STEP-UP for low-resource families in the settings that provide their care. We also will seek to identify weaknesses in STEP-UP implementation and modifications to our processes that should be addressed prior to a larger future implementation effort. We will seek to both compare within each data source the types of potential mechanisms that emerge and compare and expand on themes identified in other data sources. Our team will meet regularly to discuss and integrate analyses, to provide a more complete, nuanced understanding of the most salient potential mechanisms related to implementation, and to identify likely preconditions or moderators that also may influence the success of future implementation efforts. Once we have developed a draft of our implementation findings, we will present our preliminary results to OCHIN clinical and patient representatives and the OCHIN clinic oversight team to get feedback on our preliminary results. Based on this feedback, we will conduct additional analyses, if recommended, to provide a more complete, nuanced understanding of the most salient potential mechanisms related to implementation.

Dissemination plan

Per NIH policy, study information will be communicated through ClinicalTrials.gov. The study investigative team will work to disseminate trial information to the field and to clinical, health system, and family stakeholders through the following additional activities. At the conclusion of the study, results will be shared directly with participating health systems, their leadership, and the health networks to which they belong (e.g., OCHIN). In addition, we plan to disseminate results of the RCT with other collaborative networks that serve similar populations, such as the NIH-funded Mental Health Research Network (MHRN). We will present the results at scientific meetings spanning multiple academic domains, particularly those with connection to health system and patient stakeholders. The TSC will serve as the Publications Committee to vet and organize investigator, staff, and trainee proposals for analyses and papers in the STEP-UP trial dataset. Authors will be included who make substantive contributions to the design, conduct, and interpretation of the clinical trial results and who participate in the drafting or revision of manuscripts.

Discussion

This study compares STEP-UP, a brief transdiagnostic intervention to TAU+ for youths with anxiety and/or depression receiving care in low-resource settings. We hypothesize that STEP-UP will be more clinically effective and cost-effective than TAU+ and will engage its hypothesized target mechanism of action (reduction of avoidance). We will also examine whether SDOH factors predict or moderate response to STEP-UP versus TAU+. We will explore the readiness of clinics to implement STEP-UP if it should be found to be clinically effective in this population of low-resource families and the settings that serve them.

Several reviews have documented increasing rates of anxiety and depression amongst youth and the difficulty in obtaining timely evidence-based care for youth with these disorders [117–119]. In addition, multiple studies have documented how experiencing SDOH risks (e.g., housing insecurity) can exacerbate youth mental health concerns and complicate receipt of treatment. While evidence-based treatments such as CBT are effective for youth anxiety, there are multiple barriers to the receipt of these treatments in practice. These barriers are particularly acute for youth who receive health care in low-resource settings, such as CHCs. STEP-UP is distilled from evidence-based practices for youth anxiety and depression and has been translated into a scalable digital health framework that provides flexible delivery options to overcome many of these access barriers.

Few trials have tested digital interventions for youth anxiety in low-resource settings. Interventions supported by a digital health framework have the potential to reduce several barriers to care commonly experienced by youth receiving care in safety-net settings, such as CHCs. STEP-UP is a brief intervention and can be delivered at home via live video visit or phone call, thus reducing transportation barriers and making it more flexible to fit the needs of families. Use of native clinicians will provide a test of the fit with low-resource health care settings and will provide important new information about the level of training and supervision needed to deliver STEP-UP in this context. To our knowledge, no other study has tested a similar intervention in low-resource settings or has examined cost-effectiveness or the role of SDOH in intervention effectiveness. If the intervention is effective, this study will provide important information to help reduce key barriers for the treatment of vulnerable youth with anxiety and/or depression served in low-resource settings.
