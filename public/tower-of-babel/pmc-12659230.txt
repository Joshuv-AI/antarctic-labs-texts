Multidisciplinary management of intravenous leiomyomatosis extending to the inferior vena cava and heart: a case series
Authors: Zhongyi Gu, Jizi Shen, Shenglian Lu, Jun Chen, Rui Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study provides a concise review of the multidisciplinary management of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with inferior vena cava or intracardiac extension, based on cases from a tertiary teaching hospital in China.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed nine patients diagnosed with intravenous leiomyomatosis(IVL)extending to the inferior vena cava or cardiac chambers between January 2015 and February 2025. Data included patient demographics, imaging findings, multidisciplinary collaboration, surgical details, and follow-up outcomes.ResultsMost patients with intravenous leiomyomatosis(IVL)presented with nonspecific symptoms; 77.78% had a history of uterine leiomyoma, and 55.56% had undergone myomectomy or hysterectomy. Key imaging modalities included Computed Tomography venography, echocardiography, pelvic Magnetic resonance imaging, and pelvic ultrasound. Surgery was the primary treatment: eight patients underwent single-stage surgery, one had two-stage surgery (1/9). Six patients presented with lesions extending into the cardiac chambers (6/9), Of these, three underwent combined inferior vena cava and cardiac incision surgery, while the remaining three had tumors extracted solely via an inferior vena cava incision. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography was utilized for real-time monitoring to ensure complete tumor resection. Intraoperative blood loss ranged from 400 to 5000 mL (median: 1200 mL, interquartile range: 3075mL); six patients required allogeneic transfusion. No postoperative complications occurred. During the follow-up period, four patients experienced recurrence and all underwent reoperation.ConclusionIntravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with inferior vena cava or intracardiac extension is complex and requires multidisciplinary management.Computed Tomography venography effectively delineates intravascular lesions, and intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography provides real-time monitoring. Complete tumor resection and oophorectomy are critical for preventing recurrence, but even with these, there is still a high rate of recurrence, and most of the recurrence requires another surgery.

Introduction

Uterine myoma is the most common benign tumor of the female reproductive organs, composed of smooth muscle and connective tissue. When smooth muscle tumor tissue extends into the venous system, it is referred to as intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL). This rare smooth muscle tumor is characterized by growth within the venous system, typically without invading the venous wall [1].Due to the rarity of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL), its exact incidence remains unclear. However, with increasing awareness and understanding of the condition, the number of diagnosed cases has shown a significant upward trend in recent years. The mean age of onset for Intravenous leiomyomatosis(IVL) is 46.0 ± 9.3 years. Among patients with uterine leiomyoma, 0.25% are affected by intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL), which accounts for approximately 1% of surgical specimens of uterine leiomyoma. Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) involving the inferior vena cava and heart constitutes 66.7% of all intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) cases [2, 3].

Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) usually originates from the pelvic veins and extends upward through the venous system, potentially involving the iliac veins, ovarian veins, inferior vena cava, and heart. The condition was first described by Birch-Hirschfeld in 1896 [4]. In 1907, Durck reported the first case of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with cardiac involvement [5]. Due to the lack of specific clinical manifestations, intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) is prone to misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis, leading to delayed treatment and potentially life-threatening consequences for patients [6].

Complete surgical resection of the tumor is the optimal treatment for intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL). When the lesion is confined to the pelvic region, experienced gynecologic oncologists can typically achieve complete tumor excision. However, when the tumor extends to the inferior vena cava or involves the heart, the condition becomes more complex, requiring multidisciplinary collaboration to develop a treatment strategy. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data and treatment outcomes of nine cases of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with extension to the inferior vena cava or intracardiac involvement, with the aim of enhancing clinicians’ understanding of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL), guiding its diagnosis and treatment, and standardizing comprehensive multidisciplinary management.

Methods

Patients

Between January 2015 and February 2025, a total of 12 patients were histopathologically diagnosed with intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University.

Patient Inclusion and Exclusion: Among these 12 patients, 2 were diagnosed with intravascular leiomyomatosis only after postoperative pathological examination, with lesions confined to the pelvic cavity and without undergoing the multidisciplinary team (MDT) process; another patient was lost to follow-up after surgery, and thus was excluded from the study. The remaining 9 patients were included in this study. All 9 patients had involvement of the inferior vena cava or heart and underwent surgical treatment. Comprehensive medical records were reviewed, and follow-up was conducted for each patient. Data collected included past medical history, demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, imaging findings, surgical details, postoperative recovery, histopathological results, and follow-up outcomes. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board.

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) and roles

Patients presented to the gynecology or vascular surgery department of our hospital with symptoms, signs, or abnormal findings related to intravenous leiomyomatosis. Detailed medical histories were obtained, physical examinations were performed, and preliminary diagnostic tests were conducted. When intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) was strongly suspected, particularly with evidence of extrapelvic venous involvement, the gynecology department formed a temporary multidisciplinary team for further evaluation and to collaboratively develop a treatment plan. Informed consent was obtained from patients prior to multidisciplinary surgery. The roles and responsibilities of the MDT members are detailed in Table 1.

Preoperative evaluation

Primary imaging modalities include computed tomography venography (CTV), ultrasonography (pelvic ultrasound, echocardiography), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Surgical treatment

The surgical plan is formulated following a discussion by the multidisciplinary team (MDT). Whenever possible, single-stage surgery is preferred, aiming for complete tumor resection in a single procedure. Two-stage surgery, which involves tumor resection in two stages, is reserved for patients with a poor general condition, large tumor size, or other factors that preclude simultaneous multi-site surgery.

For patients with extensive tumor involvement of extrauterine major veins and the heart, the standard surgical approach includes a total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and intravascular tumor resection. In cases of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) affecting the heart, preparation for cardiopulmonary bypass is mandatory. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), performed by the anesthesiologist, enables real-time monitoring of tumor mobility within the heart and inferior vena cava. This facilitates the prevention of cardiac or vascular injury and aids in assessing the presence of residual tumor or tumor dislodgement.

Statistical analysis

Due to the small sample size (n = 9), no formal statistical testing was performed in this case series. Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistical methods (such as means and medians) in Microsoft Excel (version 2016). No inferential analyses (such as hypothesis testing or regression modeling) were conducted, as these methods are neither feasible nor appropriate for such limited data.

Surgical details

Of the nine patients, eight underwent single-stage surgery (8/9), and one underwent two-stage surgery (1/9). Three patients underwent thoracoabdominal incision surgery (3/9), while six patients underwent abdominal incision surgery alone(6/9).

One patient (Case 2) had the uterus and bilateral adnexa preserved due to fertility requirements (1/9 cases). Four patients with a history of hysterectomy and/or salpingo-oophorectomy (unilateral or bilateral) for uterine fibroids underwent resection of the remaining ovary during the current surgery for intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL). The remaining four patients, without a prior history of hysterectomy, underwent hysterectomy and salpingo-oophorectomy during the current treatment.

Tumor involvement extended to the heart in six patients (6/9). Of these, three underwent combined thoracoabdominal incision surgery to remove tumors from the inferior vena cava and heart (3/9), while the other three underwent laparotomy with tumor removal via inferior vena cava incision (3/9). Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography was used to dynamically monitor and ensure complete tumor resection.

Intraoperative blood loss varied from 400 to 5000 mL, with significant differences among patients (median: 1200 mL; interquartile range: 3075 mL). Two patients (Cases 1 and 7) experienced severe bleeding, which resulted in the premature termination of surgery due to insufficient blood reserves and challenges in achieving hemostasis, leading to incomplete tumor resection. Complete tumor resection was accomplished in seven patients (7/9). Six patients received allogeneic blood transfusions during surgery (6/9). No patients experienced severe postoperative complications. In Case 6, a transient intestinal obstruction occurred postoperatively, which resolved rapidly with conservative measures including fasting, gastrointestinal decompression, and nasogastric administration of paraffin oil. In Case 9, the patient developed fever and lumbar discomfort post-surgery, with urinalysis revealing significant leukocyturia, leading to a diagnosis of urinary tract infection. The infection improved following escalated antibiotic therapy. Notably, this patient had a significantly enlarged uterus, and bilateral ureteral stents were placed preoperatively to prevent ureteral injury, which likely contributed to the infection. The postoperative hospital stay ranged from 6 to 11 days, with a median of 7 days.Surgical details are outlined in Table 3.

Follow-up and recurrence outcomes

Eight patients in the study underwent total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (8/9). Postoperative anti-estrogen therapy was not administered. One patient (Case 2), who desired fertility preservation, retained the uterus and ovaries but declined anti-estrogen therapy post-surgery (1/9).

The follow-up duration for the nine patients ranged from 5 to 90 months, with a median follow-up of 49 months. During follow-up, four patients experienced recurrence (4/9), with recurrence occurring between 20 and 43 months post-surgery (mean recurrence time: 27.00 ± 9.30 months). Four patients (4/4) had recurrent lesions involving the pelvis and inferior vena cava, with one patient (1/4) showing cardiac involvement and one patient (1/4) presenting with pulmonary metastasis. Five patients showed no recurrence (5/9), with follow-up durations ranging from 5 to 67 months.(see Table 4 for details).

Discussion

Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) is characterized by the formation and growth of leiomyomatous tissue within the venous walls, representing a rare benign lesion that nonetheless exhibits biologically aggressive tumor features. The pathogenesis of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) remains unclear, with two prevailing hypotheses regarding its origin: one suggests that intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) arises from the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, while the other proposes that it originates from the invasion of uterine leiomyoma smooth muscle cells into the venous system [7]. Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) tumors can extend along the uterine veins to the internal and common iliac veins, inferior vena cava (IVC), or along the left ovarian vein to the left renal vein and inferior vena cava (IVC), or via the right ovarian vein to the inferior vena cava (IVC), potentially reaching the right atrium, right ventricle, and pulmonary artery [7–9]. In this report on nine patients, seven (77.78%) had a history of uterine leiomyoma, and five (55.55%) had previously undergone myomectomy. The lesion growth followed the venous return pathway: from pelvic veins to the inferior vena cava (IVC) to the right atrium, with continuous growth and no evidence of skip lesions. This supports the theory that intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) originates from the invasion of neoplastic smooth muscle cells from the uterus into the veins of the reproductive system.

How is Intravenous Leiomyomatosis (IVL) Diagnosed?

Patients with intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) often have a history of uterine fibroids [1].Clinical symptoms are primarily associated with the tumor’s location, with 20%–30% of patients being asymptomatic. The clinical presentation lacks specificity and may include abdominal pain, bloating, pelvic masses, and compressive symptoms such as urinary frequency or constipation. The varied morphology of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) tumors within the venous system determines the symptoms. For instance, slender tumors may be asymptomatic, whereas larger tumors can impair venous return. Mechanical obstruction of the heart may lead to dyspnea, heart failure, chest pain, or, in severe cases, sudden death [10, 11].In this study, most patients had a history of uterine fibroids and prior surgery, presenting primarily with pelvic masses, lower limb swelling, abdominal pain, and chest discomfort.

Diagnostic examinations

Ultrasound Examination is a convenient, non-invasive method commonly used to detect intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL). Pelvic ultrasound may reveal hypoechoic masses within or adjacent to the uterus, extending along the pelvic veins in a worm-like pattern with clear boundaries. Color Doppler imaging often shows abundant blood flow signals within the mass. Echocardiography can identify right heart occupation, typically presenting as hypoechoic or isoechoic masses in the right atrium, resembling a snakehead, continuous with the tumor in the inferior vena cava. These masses are generally mobile, exhibiting rhythmic movement with the cardiac cycle, occasionally protruding through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle or even the pulmonary artery [12, 13]. In this study, all patients underwent ultrasound examination for initial intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) detection, followed by further diagnostic evaluations. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) allows for dynamic observation of tumor mobility within the heart and the inferior vena cava, aiding in the prevention of cardiac or inferior vena cava injury. It also monitors for residual tumors or tumor embolization, offering real-time guidance during surgical procedures [14].In this study, all transesophageal echocardiography procedures were conducted by anesthesiologists.

Computed tomography venography (CTV) is primarily used to provide a three-dimensional visualization of the entire extent and course of intravenous tumors, as well as their relationship with the venous wall. When intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) involves the inferior vena cava (IVC), the lumen appears dilated with filling defects, presenting as soft tissue shadows. Contrast-enhanced CT shows heterogeneous, sieve-like enhancement. In cases where intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) extends to the heart, tumors in the right atrium or right ventricle may exhibit a characteristic “cane-head” or “snake-head” appearance, continuous with the inferior vena cava (IVC) tumor [15].

CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) can reveal filling defects in the pulmonary arteries and pulmonary metastatic nodules.

Under the recommendation of radiologists in the MDT team, most patients in this study underwent preoperative venous Computed Tomography venography(CTV) examination, with preoperative assessments largely consistent with surgical exploration findings.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior soft tissue resolution, enabling clearer visualization of intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) tumors, their relationship with the vascular wall, and adjacent tissues [16]. Gynecologists prefer pelvic MRI with contrast enhancement, as it provides a more intuitive assessment of uterine morphology, size, and other pelvic anatomical structures. This facilitates preoperative planning and enhances confidence in the surgical resection of pelvic lesions [17].

Multidisciplinary collaboration

Considering that intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) can affect large blood vessels and the heart, involving multiple systems and organs, and that tumor emboli can obstruct the inferior vena cava and heart, potentially causing systemic symptoms and various complications, patients necessitate comprehensive management in a tertiary hospital with a strong expertise in gynecologic oncology, vascular surgery, cardiac surgery, urology, and critical care [18, 19]. This encompasses preoperative evaluation, surgical resection, and postoperative rehabilitation. Medical institutions lacking such capabilities should promptly refer patients to appropriate centers. Our institution, a tertiary teaching hospital, established a gynecologic oncology multidisciplinary team (MDT) clinic several years ago, led by the gynecology department. This MDT has been operational long-term, primarily serving patients with gynecologic malignancies, particularly those with advanced ovarian cancer, and has accumulated extensive MDT experience. As IVL is a rare disease, no dedicated multidisciplinary team (MDT) has been established specifically for it. Instead, when patients with IVL extending to the inferior vena cava or heart are admitted, the gynecology department takes the lead in forming a temporary MDT team for collaborative management. The team comprises experts from relevant specialties with extensive clinical experience. The establishment of the MDT significantly optimizes medical resource integration, facilitating successful primary surgical intervention for patients.

Intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) generally does not infiltrate the vascular wall

For tumors involving the inferior vena cava and right atrium, the preferred approach is to incise the wall of the inferior vena cava, which typically allows for the simultaneous extraction of the tumor from both the inferior vena cava and the right atrium. Alternatively, a cardiac incision may be performed to dissect the intracardiac tumor, followed by the extraction of both the intracardiac and inferior vena cava tumors through the cardiac incision [10, 20].

In instances where the tumor adheres to the vascular wall or is excessively large, it is necessary to incise the corresponding vascular segment to free and remove the tumor. Smaller veins require ligation at the proximal end to prevent tumor extension along the vascular lumen. The surgical goal is complete tumor resection whenever possible [21]. In this study, six patients had lesions extending to the heart. Among them, three patients had tumors confined to the right atrium without adhering to the cardiac wall, allowing for complete tumor extraction through the inferior vena cava (IVC), thus avoiding the need for a cardiotomy. The tumors in the remaining three patients were larger, or the tumors had already extended into the right ventricle, making it unfeasible to fully extract the intracardiac tumor solely via an inferior vena cava incision.Consequently, in addition to tumor resection via inferior vena cava incision, cardiothoracic surgeons performed open-heart surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass assistance.All intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) patients with cardiac involvement were scheduled for procedures in cardiac operating rooms equipped with cardiopulmonary bypass facilities to ensure immediate availability if needed.

During the surgical resection of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) tumors, it is crucial to exercise caution to prevent damage to the venous wall when extracting the tumor, as this could result in significant hemorrhage. Rough and blunt dissection should be avoided. Intraoperatively, meticulous clamping and ligation of the tumor wall are essential, with vascular sutures used as needed to achieve hemostasis of the tumor wall and tumor bed. It is recommended to prepare an intraoperative autologous blood salvage device to reduce the volume of allogeneic blood transfusions [10].In this study, two patients with extensive tumor involvement of the pelvic veins experienced severe intraoperative bleeding. Due to insufficient blood reserves, the surgery was prematurely terminated, resulting in incomplete resection of the pelvic tumor.

Ovarian Preservation versus Removal in the Management of Intravenous

Leiomyomatosis.Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) is associated with elevated estrogen levels and is considered a hormone-dependent neoplasm. The recurrence rates following surgical interventions are as follows: 7.6% for patients undergoing total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and resection of all visible tumors, 25% for those undergoing total hysterectomy alone, 44% for those undergoing total hysterectomy with unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and 75% for those undergoing myomectomy with preservation of the uterus and adnexa [6, 22]. Consequently, for patients without fertility requirements, total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is recommended to minimize recurrence risk. In this study, only one patient, due to fertility preservation needs, retained the uterus and bilateral ovaries, while all other patients underwent hysterectomy and oophorectomy in prior or current surgical procedures.

Certain intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) tumors develop deep within the pelvic floor, posing challenges for complete surgical resection. In such cases, bilateral oophorectomy is particularly critical to prevent tumor progression in most patients, which could otherwise involve major blood vessels and necessitate reoperation [20]. In this study, two patients experienced significant intraoperative bleeding, leading to premature termination of surgery. Given that bilateral oophorectomy had been performed, the risk of progression or recurrence was deemed relatively low despite residual pelvic tumor tissue. Case 2, in which the uterus and bilateral adnexa were preserved and anti-estrogen therapy was declined, experienced recurrence 23 months post-surgery, requiring reoperation and eventual hysterectomy with adnexectomy.

Management of postoperative recurrence

In IVL patients who have undergone hysterectomy and bilateral oophorectomy, pelvic recurrence typically exhibits slow growth. In the absence of compressive symptoms, compromised quality of life, or involvement of major vessels or the heart, aggressive surgical resection is not recommended. Instead, long-term close follow-up is advised, with consideration of anti-estrogen therapy [23]. However, a retrospective study conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University followed up 166 patients with intravenous leiomyomatosis regularly. Univariate analysis revealed that age and surgical type were associated with recurrence, whereas multivariate regression analysis indicated that surgical type was the only independent risk factor. Bilateral oophorectomy and postoperative GnRHa therapy were not significantly associated with recurrence [24]. Among the nine patients in this study, four experienced recurrence. As noted, Case 2, with preserved uterus and bilateral adnexa and refusal of anti-estrogen therapy, recurred 23 months post-surgery, requiring reoperation and eventual hysterectomy with adnexectomy. The remaining three patients, despite prior hysterectomy and oophorectomy, also developed recurrence.Such a high recurrence rate can be largely attributed to the fact that all cases in our study involved invasion of the inferior vena cava or heart, with involvement of major veins representing a significant risk factor for postoperative recurrence [25].We did our utmost to remove the tumors, especially those invading into the veins and the heart, as well as the enlarged uterus. However, it must be acknowledged that the pelvic venous plexus is extensive and deeply located, making it difficult to thoroughly identify all microscopic lesions, let al.one achieve complete resection. It is highly likely that these residual lesions are responsible for the recurrence.In each of the four recurring instances, the tumors spread to the inferior vena cava or the heart, requiring additional surgical procedures.

The highlight of this study lies in its systematic summary of the multidisciplinary management of intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with inferior vena cava or intracardiac extension, covering the entire disease process. As a rare disease study, this article offers significant advantages by summarizing the clinical experience of nine cases. However, the limitations should be noted: Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with inferior vena cava or intracardiac extension is relatively rare. This analysis is based on retrospective data from a single-center study with a limited sample size, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the retrospective study design may introduce bias. Future validation through larger-scale or prospective studies is warranted to confirm these observations.

Conclusion

Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) with inferior vena cava or intracardiac extension is complex and requires multidisciplinary management. Computed Tomography venography(CTV) effectively delineates intravascular lesions.Surgery was the primary treatment, and intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography can provide real-time monitoring.Complete tumor resection and oophorectomy are critical for preventing recurrence, but even with these, there is still a high rate of recurrence, and most of the recurrence requires another surgery.
