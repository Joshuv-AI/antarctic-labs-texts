Improvement of PCR primers for detection of Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides, the causative agent of contagious bovine pleuropneumonia
Authors: Akari TACHIBANA, Rana OKAWAKI, Eiji HATA, Takashi MADA, Daisuke TAKAMATSU, Mariko OKAMOTO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP) is a severe respiratory disease of cattle caused by Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides (Mmm). A method combining PCR and restriction enzyme analysis (PCR-REA) is described in the World Organisation for Animal Health manual to differentiate Mmm from the related species Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. capri. However, the primers in the manual did not amplify the target region from an attenuated Mmm strain. Genome sequence comparison revealed mismatches between the primers and the target sequences of Mmm strains. Newly designed primers with corrected bases successfully amplified the target region from M. mycoides strains, and REA patterns distinguished the two subspecies. These results indicate that the new primers provide reliable Mmm detection and enhance preparedness for future CBPP outbreaks.

Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP) is a severe respiratory disease of cattle caused by Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. mycoides (Mmm) [10, 13, 17]. This disease is characterized by high morbidity and mortality due to the development of pneumonia and pleuritis [5, 21]. Because of its potential for rapid transmission and its serious socioeconomic and public health implications, CBPP is classified as a notifiable disease by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH; formerly the Office International des Epizooties, OIE) [22].

Historical records considered to be CBPP date back to documents from 16th to 18th centuries [5, 13, 17]. Over the following centuries, the disease spread to various countries worldwide, causing numerous devastating outbreaks. Today, CBPP has been eradicated from many countries, including Japan. However, CBPP remains endemic in several African countries, parts of Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, where it continues to cause significant economic losses in the livestock industry [11, 13, 14, 19]. Although no cases have been reported in Japan since 1940 [12], the possibility that this serious transboundary disease could enter the country through international livestock trade in the future cannot be ignored. Therefore, even though the disease has not occurred in Japan for decades, the diagnostic system needs to be further strengthened to ensure the stable health of livestock.

Mycoplasmas are known to be among the smallest self-replicating organisms [5]. They lack a cell wall, do not possess flagella, and exhibit a pleomorphic shape [5]. Mmm belongs to the Mycoplasma mycoides cluster. Mmm is genetically very similar to Mycoplasma mycoides subsp. capri (Mmc), which infects goats and other small ruminants, necessitating the use of molecular methods for accurate identification of Mmm [18].

PCR-based molecular identification methods for Mmm have been widely used for the diagnosis of CBPP [2, 3, 21] and are recommended as standard diagnostic techniques in the CBPP diagnostic manual published by the WOAH [21]. In Japan, the official diagnostic guidelines for livestock infectious diseases introduce a method combining PCR and restriction enzyme analysis (PCR-REA), one of the PCR-based methods recommended in the WOAH manual, to detect and differentiate Mmm and Mmc [2, 21]. However, during the PCR step of this test, amplification of the target region was not observed for the attenuated Mmm strain V, which is preserved for biological products at the National Institute of Animal Health in Japan. The same result was obtained for the Mmc strain Y-goat. These findings indicate that the current PCR is not necessarily capable of detecting all M. mycoides strains, highlighting potential limitations in diagnosing CBPP using the PCR-REA system. If Mmm were introduced into Japan, these detection limitations could allow the disease to spread nationwide. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the causes of PCR failures and to improve the current PCR-REA into a more accurate approach for identifying Mmm, in order to establish a reliable CBPP diagnostic system in Japan.

Genomic DNA of strains V and Y-goat was extracted for genome analysis and PCR-REA. Briefly, bacterial cells cultured in broth were collected by centrifugation and suspended in 550 µL of Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0). The cell suspensions were treated with 20 µL of sodium dodecyl sulfate (10% w/v) and 20 µL of Proteinase K (20 mg/mL) and incubated at 55°C for 1 hr. After incubation, the lysates were extracted with an equal volume of phenol, phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) (PCI), and chloroform once, three times, and once, respectively. After ethanol precipitation, the DNAs were further treated with 4 µL of RNase (100 μg/mL) (Nippon Gene Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 37°C for 2 hr. Following the extraction with PCI and chloroform twice and once, respectively, the DNAs were precipitated with ethanol. The purified DNAs were suspended in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0). Genomic DNA of the strains listed in Table 1, except for strains V and Y-goat, was also extracted using InstaGene™ Matrix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) to confirm the specificity of PCR-REA. The DNAs were stored at −20°C until use.

Comparative analysis of our sequencing data with reference genome sequences of Mmm and Mmc strains obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/) (Supplementary Table 1) confirmed that the target genomic region is completely conserved across all Mmm strains (Supplementary Table 2). Several nucleotide variations were identified within the amplified region in Mmc strains; however, these variations would not affect the REA pattern (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, mismatches were identified between the primer sequences and the primer-binding regions in all Mmm and Mmc strains analyzed (Fig. 2, and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Although all Mmm strains and most Mmc strains exhibited a single mismatch in each primer region (Fig. 2, and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3), several Mmc strains showed multiple additional mismatches in the primers MM451- and MM450-binding region (Supplementary Table 3). Since the genome sequences used in this study were derived from strains isolated between 1950 and 2021 (Supplementary Table 1), the observed mismatches are considered to be common among strains from a wide range of years. These results suggest that the primers MM451 and MM450 described in the official manual [2, 21] are not suitable for detecting strains of both Mmm and Mmc.

Furthermore, to identify which of the mismatches in primers MM451 and MM450 was responsible for PCR failure, additional PCR assays were performed on strains V, Y-goat, and PG3T using different primer combinations: MM451 with MM_R, and MM_F with MM450. PCR using MM451 and MM_R failed to amplify the target region, whereas PCR using MM_F and MM450 successfully produced 574-bp amplicons in all three strains (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results indicate that the mismatch between MM451 and the target genomic sequences was the primary cause of the PCR failure, while the mismatch involving MM450 had minimal impact on amplification efficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated that PCR efficiency is critically influenced by nucleotide complementarity at the 3’ end of primers [1, 9]. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that the mismatch near the 3’ end of MM451 was responsible for the PCR failure observed in this study.

The primer set MM451/MM450 was designed based on sequence data using the sequencing technology available before 1994. Subsequent advances in genome analysis technologies have markedly improved the accuracy and reliability of genomic sequence data [7, 15, 20]. Consequently, discrepancies can be observed between primer sequences designed using early sequence data and currently available high-quality genome sequences. Such differences are likely a contributing factor to the issues observed in the present study.

Depending on the types and combinations of PCR enzymes and thermal cyclers, MM451 and MM450 may still be capable of detecting M. mycoides strains; however, for highly accurate diagnostics using modern PCR systems where highly specific enzymes are commonly employed, primers must be designed based on correct and reliable nucleotide sequences. It should also be noted that similar issues are not limited to these primers for CBPP diagnosis, as mismatches may also be present in other primers for other diseases designed in earlier studies.

In this study, no nucleotide variation was observed in the target region among Mmm strains registered in the public database. However, additional mutations may arise in the primer-binding sites as the pathogen continues to evolve. Such mutations could compromise PCR efficiency and potentially lead to false-negative results in the future. Therefore, periodic updates of primer sequences may be necessary to maintain diagnostic reliability. In contrast, additional mismatches were identified in the primer-binding regions of some Mmc strains (Supplementary Table 3), which may limit the ability of the newly designed primers to detect all Mmc strains. However, since these primers are primarily intended for the detection of Mmm, they are considered suitable at least for the diagnosis of CBPP.

This study did not include an evaluation using field strains, including recent isolates. This is because CBPP has not occurred in Japan for over 80 years, making it impossible to obtain Japanese field strains. Therefore, further evaluation is needed to assess the effectiveness of the primers against currently circulating strains. In addition, validation efforts in countries where CBPP is endemic or at other diagnostic facilities would be particularly helpful in confirming the broader applicability of the primers.

Although CBPP has not occurred in Japan, establishing a diagnostic system is essential to prepare for potential future invasions. The combination of the newly designed primers and the REA technique is expected to facilitate more reliable detection of Mmm. Moreover, this approach may contribute to improving veterinary diagnostic systems and livestock health globally.
