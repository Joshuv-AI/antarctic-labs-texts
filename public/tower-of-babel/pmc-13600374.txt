Integrated Methylome and Transcriptome Analysis in Chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy Uncovers Alterations in Heart Development
Authors: Francisco Heredia-Fernández, Javier Martínez-López, Laura C Terrón-Camero, Desiré Casares-Marfil, Pau Bosch-Nicolau, Israel Molina, Javier Martín, Marialbert Acosta-Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChagas disease, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, remains a health concern worldwide. Its most severe clinical outcome, chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy (CCC), is marked by progressive cardiac dysfunction. Biological mechanisms underlying the differential development of CCC remain unclear. We hypothesized that alterations in the host DNA methylation and its influence on gene expression may contribute to this differential disease progression.MethodsWe analyzed whole-blood methylation data from 42 CCC patients and 23 individuals with the indeterminate form, followed by an integrated transcriptomic analysis in a 22 individuals subset. We performed transcription factor (TF) and functional enrichment analyses to identify biological mechanisms underlying CCC severity.ResultsSevere CCC patients exhibited higher methylation variability, particularly in immune regulation genes. Integrated analysis revealed dysregulation of genes regulating cardiac development, morphogenesis, and ion homeostasis, with GATA5 as a key TF regulating cardiac pathways. TF activity inferred from peripheral blood resembled cardiac signatures previously described, with RUNX TFs, key drivers of Th1 immune polarization, correlating strongly with disease severity.ConclusionsOur findings highlight a systemic epigenetic signature in blood that reflects cardiac pathology in CCC. These insights advance our understanding of CCC pathogenesis and facilitate novel blood-based biomarkers and therapeutic targets aimed at preventing disease progression.Keywords: epigenetics, chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy, transcription factors, biomarkers

Chagas disease is a parasitic infection endemic to Latin America caused by the protozoan Trypanosoma cruzi [1]. Due to migratory movements, it has now spread to several nonendemic industrialized countries, leading to its recognition as a global health problem [2]. After the infection, individuals develop an acute phase followed by an indeterminate phase where most patients remain asymptomatic (ASY) [2]. Nevertheless, around 30% of Chagas disease patients develop a chronic phase with cardiac involvement known as chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy (CCC), which in some cases can lead to severe inflammatory dilated cardiomyopathy [2]. Candidate genes and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) performed over the last years have shown a role of the host genetics in the development of CCC [3–6]. However, in spite of the recent advances in the genetic basis of Chagas disease, the mechanisms underlying the differential development and progression of CCC are yet scarce.

It is widely accepted that the interaction between genetic and environmental factors in the host response may contribute to the differential presentation of infectious diseases [7], making the study of epigenetics and its impact on gene expression particularly important. Previous epigenomic studies have reported a significant influence of DNA methylation in gene expression patterns of CCC patients [8–10]. They also demonstrated that the severe form of CCC is characterized by a stronger immune component than in other cardiac disorders, emphasizing the importance of studying immune pathways in the disease [9]. However, studies in cardiac tissue are constrained by the need for invasive procedures to obtain biopsies and the consequent limited sample sizes, while blood-based studies require validation in diverse Latin American populations due to genetic heterogeneity [11]. Therefore, further investigations in independent cohorts that integrate multiple molecular layers are needed to better understand the factors driving the progression from ASY infection to severe CCC.

Considering this, we hypothesize that the analysis of DNA methylation and its impact on gene expression in CCC patients will provide novel insights into disease pathogenesis and reveal molecular differences associated with disease progression. To this end, we aim to identify genes and pathways deregulated in CCC through an integrated methylome and transcriptome analysis of whole blood samples, with a focus on characterizing molecular signatures of the most severe form of CCC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Considerations, Study Populations, and Sample Collection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee from the Vall d'Hebron University Hospital, Barcelona, Spain (PR [AMI] 297/2016). Protocols used in the study complied with all ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all individuals included in the study signed written informed consents.

A total of 65 individuals from Bolivia were initially recruited in the International Health and tropical medicine unit of the Vall d'Hebron University Hospital, Barcelona, Spain. All patients were seropositive for T. cruzi and stratified by disease severity according to the Kuschnir classification (K0–K3), as shown in Table 1. Further details on the classification criteria are provided in the Supplementary Data.

DNA Methylation Analysis

DNA Methylation Data Processing and Quality Controls

DNA methylation was profiled from whole blood using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (Illumina). Quality control (QC), normalization, and statistical analyses were performed in R following standard pipelines from minfi (https://bioconductor.org/packages/devel/bioc/vignettes/minfi/inst/doc/minfi.html) and limma (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/limma.html) (Supplementary Figure 1). Briefly, samples failing bisulfite conversion and low-quality or potentially confounded probes (detection P > .01, sex chromosomes, or SNP-containing) were removed, and data were normalized using Noob + quantile normalization. Estimated blood cell-type composition was inferred using epiDISH R package (https://github.com/sjczheng/EpiDISH). The full preprocessing details are described in the Supplementary Data.

Differential Methylation and Variability Analyses

We compared CCC and indeterminate (ASY) groups to identify differentially methylated and variable positions (DMPs and DVPs, respectively), adjusting for potential confounding factors (age, sex, cell composition). Associations with covariates were assessed using Pearson or Wilcoxon tests, as appropriate, and significant covariates were included in the models. Differentially methylated positions were identified using Bayesian linear regression in limma, and DVPs were detected by testing variance differences using empirical Bayes moderation. For each analysis, CpG sites with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All the statistical workflow is detailed in the Supplementary Data.

Transcriptome Analysis

RNA Extraction Data Processing and Quality Control

RNA sequencing was performed on whole blood samples from a subset of 22 individuals using the Illumina HiSeq platform. Quality control, normalization, and statistical analyses were carried out following standard pipelines from miARma-seq (https://github.com/eandresleon/miARma-seq) and STAR software (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html) (Supplementary Figure 2). After QCs, only 16 individuals were included (9 CCC and 7 ASY) (Supplementary Figure 3). Details on preprocessing, normalization, and QC procedures are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Differential Expression Analysis

We compared CCC and ASY groups to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), adjusting for age and sex. Genes with an FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Further details on the statistical analyses are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Integrative Analysis of the Methylome and Transcriptome

In order to identify how methylation changes impact gene expression, we applied a Pearson correlation test by using the MatrixEQTL R package [12]. All the details of the workflow and applied filters are specified in the Supplementary Data.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To identify biological pathways relevant for CCC, functional enrichment of the results of DNA methylation and gene expression analyses was performed using the EWAS toolkit (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/ewas/toolkit) and the EnrichR R package (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr), respectively.

Transcription Factor Activity Analysis

We analyzed the enrichment of significant methylation alterations on transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) using the EWAS toolkit with a P < .05 threshold (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/ewas/toolkit). In addition, we used the differential expression data to calculate the activity of the transcription factors (TFs). This analysis was performed utilizing the DecoupleR R package using the human TF data from the colecTRI package (https://github.com/saezlab/CollecTRI). Transcription factors with an activity score greater than 1 in absolute value and a P < .05 were considered significant.

Assessment of Previous Chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy-Related DNA Methylation Changes and Cross-tissue Validation of Transcription Factor

We compared our methylation and TF activity results with previously published whole blood and cardiac tissue datasets from Brochet et al [9] through meta-analysis of the methylation results and cross-tissue correlation of TF activity. Methodological details and statistical parameters are provided in the Supplementary Data.

RESULTS

DNA Methylation Analysis: K3 Patients Show Increased Variability

After QC, 771 534 CpG sites from 23 ASY and 42 CCC, were analyzed. A significant association between sex, age, and CD8 cell proportion with disease status was revealed and was therefore included as a covariate in the statistical model. Differential methylation analysis revealed no significant DMPs in any comparison (FDR < 0.05). On the other hand, 46 DVPs in the CCC versus ASY comparison were identified, which were annotated to 38 genes (Figure 1A). K1 and K2 versus ASY showed no significant DMPs or DVPs; only the comparison of K3 versus ASY yielded significant results, namely 458 DVPsK3 that were annotated to 333 genes, indicating greater methylation variability in this group (Figure 1B) (Supplementary Tables 1-2). While DMPs could reflect uniform epigenetic shifts that consistently suppress or activate gene expression across patient cells, DVPs may indicate unstable epigenetic states, where methylation levels vary widely between cells or individuals. Given that the comparison with the K3 most severe cases was considered the most clinically relevant and showed the greatest differences from ASY, subsequent analyses were focused on this specific comparison.

Functional enrichment analyses of DVPs can be found in Supplementary Tables 5-7. DVPsK3 showed enrichment at positions associated with a high blood concentration of TNF-α (OR = 353.38 and P = 2.49e-5) (Supplementary Table 7). In addition, biological pathway enrichment analysis showed intestinal lipid and cholesterol absorption and digestion processes (Supplementary Table 6). In this regard, we observed a DVP located in the promoter of the Lysocardiolipin-acyltransferase 1 (LCLAT1) gene (FDR & 1.67e-2), which encodes for an enzyme participating in the synthesis of cardiolipin, an essential fatty acid in the mitochondrial membrane [13].

Gene Expression Analysis: K3-Associated Genes Are Related to Cardiac Processes

After QC, expression data of 20 706 genes was examined in a total of 9 CCC and 7 ASY individuals, with no significant genes found after correction by multiple testing (Supplementary Table 9). On the other hand, in the comparison of K3 versus ASY individuals, 24 085 genes were examined in 2 patients and 7 ASY individuals, resulting in 3 DEGs (Supplementary Table 10). Among the upregulated, we found GATA binding protein 5 (GATA5) (FDR = 6.49e-3 and logFC = 3.37) and Alpha Kinase 2 (ALPK2) (FDR = 3.44e-2 and logFC = 4.26), while T Cell Receptor Beta Variable 11-2 (TRBV11-2) (FDR = 3.44e-2 and logFC = -2.89) was downregulated (Figure 2A). GATA5 and ALPK2 genes are related to heart development and morphogenesis [14, 15]. Consistent with previous methylation analyses, the differential expression analysis showed that the K3 group exhibits more pronounced dysregulation, despite the lower sample size.

Integrative Analysis: Epigenetic Dysregulation of Cardiac Development-Related Genes

Given the potential influence of DNA methylation on gene expression, we aimed to investigate if methylation changes correlate with the gene expression patterns observed in CCC. Accordingly, an integrative study of methylation and expression data from the same patients was performed, focusing on those with the most severe stage of cardiomyopathy (K3), although the results in CCC overall can be found in Supplementary Table 11. Integrative analysis on K3 patients resulted in 482 significant K3 specific cis interactions with correlation (nominal P < .05 and R2 ≥ 0.8) (Supplementary Table 12). These interactions involved 450 CpG sites (nominal P-value of DVPs < 0.05) and 320 genes (nominal P-value of DEGs < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 12). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are associated with the regulation of apoptosis in cardiac and striated muscle cells, in cardiac valve and atrial morphogenesis, and in other relevant processes, including immune cell regulation and ion homeostasis (Figure 2B) (Supplementary Table 14). In line with this and relevant to the previous findings, GATA5 is involved in cardiac valve morphogenesis and was one of the DEGsK3 that also showed a significant correlation with the methylation levels at cg03945807 (Figure 2C). Additionally, we found an enrichment in potassium and calcium ion homeostasis (Figure 2B), including genes, such as potassium calcium-activated channel subfamily M alpha 1 (KCNMA1).

Transcription Factors Analyses: GATA5 and COUP-TFII, Key Transcription Factors in Severe Chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy Patients

One of the mechanisms by which DNA methylation can regulate gene expression is by altering the TFBS. We therefore investigated whether differential variability in methylation patterns occurs within TFBS and whether this might be relevant in the more severe form of cardiomyopathy. As a result, we observed that DVPs in K3 patients were preferentially located in the TFBS of 3 different TFs (nominal P < .05) (Figure 3A) (Supplementary Table 15). In particular, we highlight the Chicken ovalbumin upstream promoter transcription factor II (COUP-TFII) (nominal P & .01), a TF involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism [16], which has also been implicated in the development of dilated cardiomyopathy in mice [17].

Additionally, based on gene expression data, we calculated which TFs showed differential activity between K3 patients and ASY. This analysis revealed 96 TFs with differential activity (nominal P < .05). COUP-TFII, which was identified in the previous TFBS analysis (nominal P & .01), also presented increased differential activity in K3 patients (nominal P & .01) (Figure 3A). The TF GATA5 was among the top 10 TFs with the highest activity score (nominal P = 2.77e-8) (Figure 3B). Of note, GATA5 gene was also differentially expressed in the most severe patient group, also showing a significant correlation with the methylation levels in the integrative analysis.

Replication of Chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy-Associated Differentially Methylated Positions and Cross-tissue Correlation of Transcription Factor Activity

To ensure that potential methylation changes relevant to CCC were not overlooked, we carried out a meta-analysis combining our dataset with that of Brochet et al [9], aiming to increase statistical power and uncover consistent signals across 2 independent cohorts. Specifically, we integrated DNA methylation alterations in whole blood samples from Brochet et al and identified 35 DMPs associated with CCC and 67 DMPs with the severe CCC group (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5) (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Enrichment analysis of these 67 severe-associated DMPs revealed a significant enrichment of genes involved in multiple pathways, being the most significant the regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway (Figure 1C) (Supplementary Table 8). Notably, the inhibition of this pathway prevents the development of cardiac abnormalities in a Chagas disease model [18].

Several of our findings, particularly from the TF analyses, highlighted processes relevant to cardiac tissue. Therefore, we investigated if similar patterns could be observed directly in heart biopsies from cardiomyopathic patients. Using cardiac transcriptomic data from Brochet et al, we inferred TF activity and found that 12 TFs presented differential activity in both our whole blood dataset and the heart tissue of severe CCC patients. Notably, the activity scores of these 12 shared TFs showed a significant correlation (R2 & 0.76; P & 4e-3) (Figure 3D). Moreover, the TFBS enrichment analysis of the 67 severe-associated DMPs highlighted 2 of these 12 TFs, EBF and Sox10, as significantly enriched, reinforcing the common dysregulation observed in both whole blood and cardiac tissue (Supplementary Table 15).

DISCUSSION

In this work, we investigated the methylome and transcriptome of individuals with CCC compared to individuals in the indeterminate phase. Across all analyses, patients with severe CCC exhibited greater molecular dysregulation, with a higher number of alterations in DNA methylation and gene expression. Although no DMPs were detected, we identified DVPs that may reflect disrupted epigenetic regulation across cells, capturing pathogenic processes even without changes in mean methylation, as illustrated by the enrichment analysis. Several TFs also showed impaired activity in this group, including key regulators previously implicated in cardiac and immune processes. Moreover, a significant correlation in TF activity was observed between whole blood and heart tissue.

Of interest, the higher methylation variability in the comparison of CCC-K3 with ASY individuals revealed an enrichment of CpGs previously associated with elevated blood TNF-α concentrations in coronary heart disease [19]. In this sense, increased levels of this proinflammatory cytokine have been also reported in Chagas disease and is associated with its most severe forms [3]. Indeed, TNF-α is synthesized at increased levels in response to the persistent stimulus of T. cruzi infection [20]. It has also been reported that this proinflammatory cytokine together with IFN-γ can induce mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes [20]. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction in the myocardium of patients with CCC [20–22].

Remarkably, GATA5 emerged as a key molecule related to CCC, displaying consistent methylation variability that influences its expression levels and showing significant regulatory activity in our analyses. GATA5 encodes a TF that plays an important role in heart development and function, and mutations in this gene are linked to cardiovascular diseases, such as bicuspid aortic valve and atrial fibrillation [14, 23]. In addition, this TF is required for the differentiation of cardiogenic precursors into endocardial cells and, together with GATA4 and GATA6, control cardiac cell growth and function, including blood pressure regulation [24–26]. Interestingly, our results also revealed a significantly higher activity score of the TF GATA6 in CCC-K3 patients. In turn, GATA5 maintains the expression of NK2 homeobox 5 (NKX2-5), a critical gene for the regulation of heart development [27], whose expression is influenced by DNA methylation in CCC-K3 patients. This gene encodes the first TF expressed in cardiogenesis and is required for progenitor cell differentiation. In addition, it has also been implicated in the cellular response to myocardial injury in adult zebrafish [27–29]. Considering all of the above, our results led us to hypothesize that GATA5 plays a central role in the pathogenesis and progression of CCC, leading to structural and functional abnormalities in cardiac tissue.

Regarding the functional abnormalities in the heart, our integrative analysis showing the impact of methylation variability on gene expression further revealed genes enriched in calcium and potassium ion homeostasis, such as KCNMA1. This gene encodes calcium-activated potassium channels that play an essential role in cardiomyocytes and vascular smooth muscle, thereby contributing to cardiac and vascular regulation [30]. Alterations in KCNMA1 expression or function have been associated with several cardiac pathologies, including hypertension, heart failure, and arrhythmias [31]. Electrical and arrhythmic alterations are common in CCC, and previous studies have observed dysregulation of potassium channel-encoding genes in heart biopsies from both patients with severe CCC and T. cruzi-infected mice [8, 9, 32]. Indeed, it has been suggested that elevated TNF-α levels in the most severe CCC patients [20] may exacerbate ion channel dysfunction by inducing electrophysiological changes [9, 33]. Therefore, these results support the idea that ion transport dysregulation is crucial for the development of CCC [31].

In this study, we revealed significant alterations in the activity and regulation of multiple TFs. Notably, we identified that DVPs were enriched within the COUP-TFII DNA binding site in CCC-K3 patients, and gene expression data confirmed significantly higher activity of this TF in this group. Overexpression of this TF in the myocardium has been linked to impaired cardiac function and the development of dilated cardiomyopathy in a murine model [17] and also plays an essential role in cardiac development [34, 35]. Moreover, 12 TFs with differential activity in whole blood of CCC-K3 patients showed significant correlation with activity inferred from transcriptomic data of heart tissue obtained from patients with severe CCC from a previous study [9]. This cross-tissue concordance is further supported by a direct comparison of blood DNA methylation data with the dataset of Brochet et al, which identified overlapping DMPs associated with CCC and severe CCC that were enriched in pathways relevant to disease pathogenesis, including Wnt signaling. One of the identified TFs is RUNX1, which belongs to the Runt-related family of TFs that are highly relevant in blood cells function and differentiation [36]. This TF promotes differentiation into Th1 lymphocytes and is involved in the regulation of different types of immune response [36]. Notably, RUNX3, another member of this family, participates in the same pathway and was previously highlighted by Brochet et al, who suggested that it could contribute to the disease through its involvement in Th1– and IFNγ–mediated immune responses [9]. This regulation is particularly important in the context of this infectious disease, as patients with CCC develop an altered immune response and persistently produce proinflammatory cytokines [37]. Notably, while Brochet et al compared CCC patients with nonchagasic controls, our analysis using seropositive ASY individuals as the reference group reinforces the rationale that these regulatory changes are specific to chagasic cardiomyopathy progression rather than to cardiomyopathy in general. At the same time, differences in the genetic ancestry of the studied populations contribute to discrepancies between studies.

The main limitation of our work is the limited sample size in some of the comparisons, reducing our statistical power to detect subtle changes, as well as the lack of validation cohort. In addition, the absence of medication data prevented adjustment for treatments, which may represent a potential confounder. Differences in control definitions across studies may also limit direct cross-study replication. However, we have identified genes and biological processes strongly associated with CCC and have validated previous findings, thereby reinforcing the reliability of our analyses. Related to this, our study focused on epigenetic alterations exclusively in peripheral blood rather than affected heart tissue, as we believe blood represents a more accessible tissue for identifying potential biomarkers relevant to diseases. Nevertheless, the overlap of our results with previous studies on heart biopsies suggests that epigenetic alterations observed in cardiac cells are reflected at the systemic level in blood. This finding holds significant clinical potential for identifying molecular biomarkers, as blood samples are more accessible and less invasive than obtaining biopsies from the heart. Therefore, our work highlights molecular mechanisms in CCC that are consistent with findings from independent cohorts and may inform future longitudinal studies to develop blood-based biomarkers or therapeutic strategies while assessing causal relationships between these changes and disease progression.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study identified alterations in the methylation and expression patterns of CCC patients, particularly in those with the severe form of the disease. We also observed overexpression of key genes involved in cardiac development. Furthermore, we revealed an impairment of several TFs involved in the progression of the disease, including COUP-TFII, as well as GATA and Runt families, master regulators of cardiac processes and the immune response. Finally, the correlation between the activity of TFs in blood and heart biopsies of CCC patients suggests that whole blood could be reflecting the epigenetic dysregulation that occurs in the affected cardiac tissue. Taking into account the exploratory nature of this study, these results suggest a systemic epigenetic signature that may contribute to the development of potential biomarkers and novel strategies to guide the clinical management of the disease.
