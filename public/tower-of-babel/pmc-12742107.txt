Long-term prognostic impact of in-hospital serum albumin concentration changes in patients hospitalised for heart failure
Authors: Keiko Fukino, Satoshi Miyata, Tomomi Ide, Shouji Matsushima, Takeshi Tohyama, Nobuyuki Enzan, Hiroyuki Tsutsui, Fumitaka Nakamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition influences prognosis in patients with heart failure, but current nutritional evaluation methods are excessively complex for routine clinical use. The role of the serum albumin concentration, a widely used surrogate marker, in acute heart failure remains unclear. We assessed whether changes in serum albumin during heart failure hospitalisation can serve as a prognostic marker.MethodsAmong 14 847 patients in a nationwide heart failure registry in 2013, 5836 eligible patients (mean age: 77.7 years, 51.2% men) were categorised into two groups: the albumin-increase group (n=2048) and the no-albumin-increase group (n=3788). The incidences of primary (all-cause mortality) and secondary (a composite of cardiovascular death and rehospitalisation for worsening heart failure) outcomes were compared between the groups. The prognostic impact of serum albumin changes was also evaluated.ResultsThe median follow-up period was 1545 days (95% CI 1535 to 1557 days). Event-free survival rates for clinical outcomes were higher in the albumin-increase group than in the no-albumin-increase group (primary outcome: 54.5% vs 45.4%, 95% CI 51.4% to 57.6% vs 42.9% to 47.8%, p<0.005; secondary outcome: 40.5% vs 36.9%, 95% CI 36.4% to 44% vs 34.1% to 39.6%, p<0.005). A generalised additive model revealed a linear relationship between serum albumin changes and prognosis; declines had a stronger negative effect than the positive impact of increases, but the change rate better reflected the relationship with prognosis. Survival classification and regression tree analysis indicated that a 25% decrease in the serum albumin concentration identified the most vulnerable population; even a 3% decrease was associated with worse outcomes.ConclusionsSerum albumin changes during hospitalisation can predict prognosis in patients with heart failure, indicating potential as a target for interventions.

Introduction

The incidence of heart failure increases with age, with its prevalence surging in ageing populations.1 2 In older individuals, heart failure is historically associated with a preserved ejection fraction and greater comorbidity burden than in younger patients.3 Although its aetiology has evolved with lifestyle changes,3 the prognosis in older patients is more strongly influenced by overall health status, including comorbidities and frailty, than by heart failure itself.3 4 Malnutrition has been identified as a key factor influencing clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure.5

Several markers are used to assess nutritional status in patients with heart failure, including the Prognostic Nutritional Index,6 7 Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index5 8 and Controlling Nutritional Status Score.9 However, these scores require multiple laboratory parameters and complex calculations, limiting routine clinical use. Low serum albumin has been reported to predict the long-term prognosis of patients hospitalised for heart failure.10 11 However, the reliability of the serum albumin concentration as a surrogate nutritional marker in acute heart failure is controversial because it often fluctuates, particularly in the early phase.10 The prognostic impact of changes in serum albumin during hospitalisation remains unclear, with limited evidence suggesting that early increases may be beneficial12 and that an in-hospital albumin increase may improve the 1-year prognosis.13 Whether its prognostic significance varies according to patient characteristics, such as age, sex and comorbidities, and how the degree of change influences prognosis, also remains uncertain.

This study evaluated the clinical impact of changes in serum albumin between admission and discharge, representing the acute-to-chronic transition, as a simple and accessible marker for routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and data collection

The design and primary findings of the Japanese Registry of Acute Decompensated Heart Failure (JROADHF), a 2013 retrospective nationwide registry of patients hospitalised for worsening heart failure, have been reported previously.14 The JROADHF database was developed by linking the Japanese Registry Of All cardiac and vascular Disease to the Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination (JROAD-DPC), a nationwide claims database. Follow-up data were collected for up to 5 years postindex hospitalisation. Data unavailable through the JROAD-DPC or follow-up information was collected by independent investigators at each participating facility using medical records.

Study population

Of 14 847 patients in the JROADHF registry, we identified 5836 eligible patients with serum albumin concentrations measured at both admission and discharge. Patients receiving haemodialysis, including peritoneal dialysis, before or during the index hospitalisation (n=819) and those with malignancies at admission (n=1450) were excluded because these conditions substantially affect nutritional status. Patients with missing information regarding haemodialysis (n=2672) and/or malignancy (n=1609) were also excluded (figure 1). The mean age of the eligible patients was 77.7±12.9 years, and 51.2% were men (n=2988). We categorised the patients into two groups based on their serum albumin concentration changes during the index hospitalisation: the albumin-increase group and the no-albumin-increase group. The albumin-increase group comprised patients whose serum albumin at discharge was higher than at admission, whereas the no-albumin-increase group comprised those with serum albumin at discharge equal to or lower than at admission.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular death and heart failure rehospitalisation after admission. Event rates were analysed for the entire cohort as well as for subgroups stratified by age, sex and baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between the albumin-increase and no-albumin-increase groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and were compared using unpaired Student’s t-tests. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) and were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Event-free survival rates for the primary and secondary outcomes were estimated using Kaplan-Meier method, with group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. The follow-up duration was summarised using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. We primarily report 4-year survival and event-free survival estimates, while 5-year estimates are provided as exploratory. Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed to identify factors associated with clinical outcomes, with variables selected by the Akaike information criterion.15 To visually examine the relationship between the degree of change in serum albumin and clinical outcomes, a generalised additive model with a smoothing spline was used.16

For age subgroup analysis, the 80-year cut-off was derived from a survival classification and regression tree (CART) analysis17 (online supplemental figure 1). The CART analysis also identified the most vulnerable population stratified by the degree of serum albumin change, with results presented using a regression tree model. For subgroup analyses by LVEF, the patients were divided into three categories based on their baseline LVEF: <40% (heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)), 40%–<50% (heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF)), and ≥50% (heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)), in accordance with Japanese and other major guidelines.1820 All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.1.2 for Windows) with the tableone and RcmdrPlugin.EZR packages21 for descriptive analyses and the mgcv package for generalised additive models. P values of <0.05 (two tailed) were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study because, as stated, this was a retrospective cohort study using deidentified data from patients hospitalised for heart failure in 2013; thus, patient and public involvement was not feasible.

Results

Patient characteristics and comparisons between the albumin-increase and no-albumin-increase groups

Baseline patient characteristics and differences between the albumin-increase group and no-albumin-increase group are presented in table 1. Among 5836 eligible patients, 2048 (35.1%) exhibited an increase in their serum albumin concentration during the index hospitalisation. Compared with the albumin-increase group, the no-albumin-increase group was older and included a lower proportion of men (age: 79.26±11.89 years vs 74.78±14.08 years, p<0.001; men: 49.7% vs 54.0%, p=0.002). Compared with the albumin-increase group, the no-albumin-increase group had a higher mean baseline LVEF (47.88%±17.34% vs 45.16%±17.33%, p<0.001) and a greater proportion of HFpEF (47.7% vs 41.7%, p<0.001). Among major comorbidities, hypertension and chronic kidney disease were more prevalent in the no-albumin-increase group, whereas diabetes mellitus was more frequent in the albumin-increase group. Baseline albumin was higher in the no-albumin-increase group than in the albumin-increase group (3.64±0.50 g/dL vs 3.30±0.61 g/dL, respectively). Serum creatinine levels, potassium levels, haemoglobin levels, white blood cell counts and lymphocyte counts were also higher in the no-albumin-increase group. Medication use was generally similar, except for more frequent initiation of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in the albumin-increase group. Overall, initiation rates for standard heart failure therapy were relatively low in both groups within this cohort.

The 4-year survival rate of the entire cohort was 55.4% (95% CI 53.9% to 56.8%), and the event-free survival rate for the secondary outcome was 43.5% (95% CI 41.9% to 45.1%). For completeness, the 5-year exploratory estimates were 48.6% (95% CI 46.7% to 50.5%) and 38.2% (95% CI 35.9% to 40.5%), respectively (figure 2a,b).

For the primary outcome, survival curves differed throughout follow-up, with a 4-year survival rate of 61.1%±5.4% (95% CI 58.7% to 63.4%) in the albumin-increase group and 52.1%±5.4% (95% CI 50.3% to 53.9%) in the no-albumin-increase group (p<0.001) (figure 2a). For completeness, the 5-year survival rate was 54.5% (95% CI 51.4% to 57.6%) and 45.4% (95% CI 42.9% to 47.8%), respectively; these estimates are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution because follow-up was available up to 5 years.

For the secondary outcome, event-free survival also differed significantly according to serum albumin changes, with 4-year rates of 46.6% (95% CI 44.0% to 49.2%) vs 41.8% (95% CI 39.8% to 43.7%) in the albumin-increase and no-albumin increase groups (p<0.001) (figure 2b). For completeness, the 5-year rates were 40.5% (95% CI 36.4% to 44.6%) and 36.9% (95% CI 34.1% to 39.6%), respectively.

For each component of the secondary outcome, the risk of cardiovascular death was lower in the albumin-increase group, except in HFmrEF (p=0.321), older adults (p=0.054) and women (p=0.052), with the latter two showing a trend towards significance. Predictors of clinical outcomes

Cox proportional hazards analysis identified predictors of clinical outcomes (table 2). After variable selection, the baseline serum albumin and the rate of change in serum albumin, but not the absolute change, emerged as predictors. The serum albumin change rate remained significant in all subgroups analysed for the primary outcomes. For the secondary outcomes, the serum albumin change rate was not a predictor in patients aged<80 years, men, or patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF. However, when the secondary outcome components were analysed separately, the serum albumin change rate predicted cardiovascular death in all subgroups after variable selection, except in patients with HFmrEF and HFrEF. By contrast, the serum albumin change rate was not a predictor of heart failure hospitalisation in any subgroup. Outcome associations with serum albumin change

To assess whether the extent of serum albumin change influenced clinical outcomes differently, a generalised additive model with a smoothing spline was used to visualise associations between all-cause mortality and both the absolute difference (figure 7a) and the rate of change (figure 7b) in serum albumin from admission to discharge. The analysis revealed a consistently negative association between serum albumin change and outcomes, with distinct patterns for the absolute change and the change rate. The relationship between the rate of serum albumin change and all-cause mortality flattened near a HR of 1 and plateaued as the rate increased, whereas lower rates showed a steeper gradient, indicating a greater negative impact of decreases than the benefit of increases.

Associations between serum albumin changes and outcomes. Effect of the absolute difference in serum albumin concentration between admission and discharge on the primary outcome. Effect of the rate of serum albumin change, calculated relative to its value at admission, on the primary outcome. The relationship between (a) the absolute change and (b) the rate of change in the serum albumin concentration and all-cause mortality was consistently negative; however, the patterns differed. The rate of serum albumin change showed that the gradient became less steep as the HR approached 1, plateauing as the rate increased. This suggests that a decrease in serum albumin exerted a more pronounced negative impact on clinical outcomes than the positive effect of an increase.

Cut-off for predicting the most vulnerable population

Survival CART analysis identified the most vulnerable population for the primary outcomes in this cohort (figure 8). The first cut-off value was −0.257, indicating that patients with a decline in serum albumin of >25% during hospitalisation had a significantly worse prognosis than those without. The second cut-off for further stratification was 0.03, indicating that even a 3% drop in serum albumin during the index hospitalisation was linked to a worse prognosis (figure 8).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that an increase in serum albumin during heart failure hospitalisation significantly reduced the risks of both the primary and secondary outcomes. Additionally, the rate of serum albumin change, rather than the absolute change, influenced outcomes, along with baseline levels. While increases were associated with improved outcomes, decreases had a more pronounced adverse impact. A>25% drop from baseline identified the most vulnerable population. To our knowledge, this is the first study showing that short-term changes in serum albumin during acute heart failure hospitalisation can predict long-term outcomes, irrespective of baseline levels, in a large national cohort. The changes in serum albumin during hospitalisation may therefore serve as a practical and accessible prognostic marker and a potential short-term therapeutic target to improve outcomes.

Malnutrition is common in patients with heart failure and is associated with a poor prognosis.5 Multiple factors contribute to nutritional decline in these patients, including appetite loss, malabsorption, protein loss due to enteral oedema, hepatic dysfunction and hypercatabolism. Nevertheless, malnutrition remains amenable to appropriate interventions.22

The serum concentration of albumin, a key regulator of oncotic pressure and fluid distribution, is widely used as a surrogate nutritional marker and is linked to a poor prognosis in heart failure.10 11 However, its reliability as a nutritional marker in patients with acute heart failure is limited because it can be influenced by various factors.10 23 24 We compared serum albumin levels at admission and discharge to capture the transition of acute to chronic heart failure. In our national cohort, the rate of serum albumin change, not the absolute change, was more strongly associated with the long-term prognosis and better reflected the observed nonlinear association. Despite the acute-phase variability, the serum albumin change rate may serve as a reliable prognostic marker to guide postdischarge care.

Yoshioka et al25 recently reported that low serum albumin 1 year after acute myocardial infarction was associated with a worse prognosis. Patients who experienced a decline from baseline during the remote phase had an increased risk of a composite outcome of heart failure hospitalisation and cardiovascular death, as well as all-cause mortality, compared with patients who experienced an increase in serum albumin during the same period, regardless of baseline levels.25 In our study, even a short-term in-hospital increase in serum albumin was associated with improved long-term survival. These findings reinforce the previous reports13 25 and underscore the potential of in-hospital serum albumin monitoring as a therapeutic target.

A recent study showed that a rise in serum albumin within 7 days of hospitalisation was associated with improved survival and reduced risk of heart failure readmission, suggesting that early nutritional support and other interventions to prevent declines in serum albumin may improve outcomes.12 In our study, an in-hospital increase in the serum albumin concentration reduced mortality, but not rehospitalisation, consistent with a recent report.13 The reason for this discrepancy is unclear, but timing may be relevant: our data included laboratory values only at admission and discharge, with no intermediate information. Thus, the timing of the increase was unknown. If the increase occurred earlier in the hospitalisation period, it might have lowered the risk of rehospitalisation. Further prospective studies with large cohorts are needed to identify the optimal timing for serum albumin management in patients hospitalised for heart failure.

Subgroup analyses showed that the prognostic benefit of in-hospital increases in serum albumin was consistent across most subgroups, except for secondary outcomes in patients aged≥80 years and those with HFmrEF. This may reflect the higher hospitalisation rates in the elderly26 27 and the smaller HFmrEF sample size. While increases in serum albumin were beneficial, decreases showed a more substantial adverse impact on the patients’ prognosis. The most vulnerable patients were those with a >25% reduction in serum albumin, suggesting that preventing a >25% decline in serum albumin, and potentially increasing it during hospitalisation, could be a novel therapeutic target. Compared with the previous regional study showing a 1-year benefit of increased serum albumin,13 our nationwide cohort with longer follow-up showed that the rate of serum albumin change, rather than the absolute change, better reflects the nonlinear relationship—in which the detrimental effect of a decrease outweighed the benefit of an increase—and identified a possible cut-off for the highest-risk group. These findings extend previous evidence and provide new insights into the prognostic significance of in-hospital albumin dynamics in patients with heart failure.

Nutritional interventions, including dietary optimisation and individualised strategies, can improve outcomes in malnourished patients hospitalised for heart failure, regardless of the baseline serum albumin concentration.28 29 Even those with normal albumin levels could benefit,29 suggesting that baseline serum albumin alone is insufficient to identify high-risk patients. In our study, the rate of serum albumin change more accurately reflected the association between albumin dynamics and prognosis than did the absolute change, independent of baseline levels. Although mechanisms explaining why low serum albumin affects the prognosis have been proposed,30 it remains unclear whether changes in serum albumin directly influence outcomes or merely reflect the heart failure status. Serum albumin may increase during decongestion through haemoconcentration (plasma volume contraction) and improvement in hepatic congestion and its synthetic function, as well as changes in nutrition and inflammation. This mechanism could partly explain the observed association between increases in albumin and clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, alongside previous studies, our findings support the potential of serum albumin changes as a therapeutic target in heart failure, irrespective of nutritional status.

Despite growing recognition of nutrition in heart failure care, standardised methods for its assessment and management remain lacking. Because nutrition is influenced by many factors, effective strategies often require a multidisciplinary approach even during hospitalisation.28 29 Early and intensive interventions to prevent serum albumin decline may be a promising management strategy.

HFpEF was more prevalent in the no-albumin-increase group, along with hypertension and chronic kidney disease. HFpEF accounts for more than half of heart failure cases; it has a heterogeneous background,1820 its prevalence increases with age, and morbidity and mortality remain high.1820 Unlike HFrEF, no definitive therapies have been established, although recent evidence is emerging, and management still largely targets comorbidities.1820 In our cohort, although not statistically significant, in-hospital changes in serum albumin substantially influenced both primary and secondary outcomes in the HFpEF population, suggesting that multidisciplinary efforts to manage in-hospital serum albumin may improve the prognosis. HFpEF, together with hypertension and chronic kidney disease, may help identify patients who require closer attention.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it included only Japanese patients, limiting generalisability to other ethnicities. Second, as a subanalysis of a retrospective registry, time-dependent LVEF changes were unavailable, precluding assessment of their influence on the association between serum albumin changes and outcomes. Third, the LVEF was measured by echocardiography at the attending physician’s discretion, introducing potential variability. Fourth, because information on serial haematocrit and other direct indices of plasma volume change was unavailable, quantification of haemoconcentration was not feasible; therefore, residual confounding by decongestion cannot be excluded. Fifth, other nutritional markers (eg, transferrin) were not systematically collected, and the absolute lymphocyte count had almost 23% missing data; therefore, their independent contributions to outcomes could not be robustly assessed. Sixth, the data were exclusively collected from Japanese Circulation Society-certified hospitals, and hospitals with fewer than 11 annual heart failure hospitalisations were excluded; therefore, the results may not represent smaller or non-specialist centres. Seventh, although guideline-directed medical therapy is recommended, treatment strategies likely vary across sites, potentially affecting outcomes. Finally, the data were collected from records generated in 2013; guideline-directed medical therapy has since evolved, and outcomes might have differed under current treatment standards.

Conclusion

An in-hospital serum albumin increase reduced the risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death but not heart failure rehospitalisation. The degree of change in serum albumin strongly influenced survival in patients hospitalised for worsening heart failure. In-hospital serum albumin change could serve as a practical prognostic marker and a target for short-term nutritional interventions. HFpEF, hypertension and chronic kidney disease may help identify patients needing closer nutritional attention.
