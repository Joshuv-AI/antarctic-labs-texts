Schwann Cells in Physical Nerve Injury and Tumor Perineural Invasion: A Functional Overview and Comparison
Authors: Yanni Zheng, Xin Chen, Jiachen Liu, Jiayi Chen, Lei Tang, Wenjun Liu, Yuxin Lin, Xuedong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe nervous system exhibits remarkable stability, making its repair challenging. Compared to the central nervous system, peripheral nerve injuries demonstrate a greater potential for regeneration. Schwann cells, the unique myelinating glial cells of the peripheral nervous system, possess striking plasticity and support nerve regeneration through phenotypic transformation following injury. While Schwann cells are essential for nerve regeneration after injury, in the context of tumors, their reparative function is exploited to facilitate perineural invasion and the progression of cancer. However, the tumor‐promoting effect of Schwann cells is context‐dependent, and emerging evidence suggests that under specific conditions, Schwann cells may exert tumor‐suppressive functions. This article aims to explore the complex roles of Schwann cells in both physical nerve injury and tumor‐induced nerve damage, comparing their similarities and differences and seeking to provide insights for future research and potential therapeutic interventions.Keywords: cancer progression, nerve injury, peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells, tumor perineural invasion

Introduction

Cancer is the leading cause of death and a major obstacle to extending life expectancy worldwide [1]. Perineural invasion (PNI) has been recognized as an independent predictor of tumor recurrence, potentially linked to intraneural tumor remnants and micrometastases [2]. PNI has become a significant pathological feature of many malignancies, including cancers of the pancreas, colon, rectum, prostate, head and neck, biliary tract, and stomach. For these malignancies, PNI is a marker of poor prognosis and an indicator of reduced survival [3]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, cervical cancer, and colorectal cancer, Schwann cells (SCs) have been shown to undergo reprogramming, altering their phenotype to reshape the neural microenvironment, initiate PNI, and drive tumor recurrence, invasion, and metastasis. These processes lead to worsened patient outcomes and shorter survival times [4, 5, 6]. As highly plastic cell populations, SCs not only perform critical functions in maintaining neural homeostasis and promoting repair under normal physiological conditions but also play a pivotal role in tumor‐associated PNI. Unlike their beneficial role in nerve repair, Schwann cells may contribute to tumor progression in tumor‐associated PNI.

Functional Characterization of SCs in Peripheral Nervous System

SCs are the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) and serve as key structural and functional components of the PNS. They coordinate most pathophysiological processes during neural development and are uniquely aligned along axonal projections, acting as one of the critical safeguards of neural homeostasis. Derived from the neural crest, SCs exhibit a highly plastic differentiation state that depends heavily on external signals. Schwann cell precursors (SCPs) arise within early embryonic neural structures and differentiate into multipotent immature Schwann cells (iSCs). These cells initiate radial sorting and further differentiate into myelinating and non‐myelinating Schwann cells. Mature SCs retain the capacity to fundamentally alter their phenotype in response to external signals, including transitioning into repair Schwann cells that drive peripheral nerve regeneration [7].

SCs are indispensable to the PNS, serving critical roles in nerve repair, axonal maintenance, and homeostasis. Their principal functions include the following:

Coordination of nerve repair

Coordination of nerve repair

SCs play a critical role in peripheral nerve repair. They mediate the inflammatory response, clear cellular and myelin debris, and secrete various regeneration‐promoting factors. These functions allow SCs to orchestrate the multicellular microenvironment necessary for nerve regeneration in peripheral neuropathies [8, 9]. Following peripheral nerve injury, Schwann cells undergo a transcriptional reprogramming process controlled primarily by the c‐Jun/AP‐1 pathway [10]. Injury‐induced activation of MAPK signaling pathways, including JNK, ERK1/2, and p38, promotes c‐Jun upregulation, which suppresses the myelination program while activating repair‐associated genes. c‐Jun directly regulates genes involved in myelin clearance, immune recruitment, neurotrophic support, and regeneration track formation, thereby enabling SCs to transition into repair SCs [11].

2Maintenance of axonal integrity and function

Maintenance of axonal integrity and function

SCs sustain axonal integrity by facilitating neuron–glia communication, particularly through nutrient and metabolite transfer between SCs and axons. This interaction is crucial for the maintenance of axonal function and neuronal survival [12]. Moreover, SCs play a pivotal role in energy storage and transfer, ensuring adequate metabolic coupling to support neuronal activity and systemic homeostasis [13]. Axon–Schwann cell communication is largely mediated by the NRG1/ErbB signaling axis. Axonal NRG1 Type III activates ErbB2/ErbB3 receptors on Schwann cells, triggering downstream PI3K/Akt and MEK/ERK pathways that regulate Schwann cell survival, metabolic support, and myelin maintenance. In addition, metabolic coupling through AMPK/mTOR pathways contributes to axonal energy homeostasis [14].

3Secretion of neurotrophic factors

Secretion of neurotrophic factors

During development, SCs secrete essential neurotrophic factors such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF), insulin‐like growth factor (IGF), endothelin (ET), platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF), and neurotrophin‐3 (NT3). These factors not only support neuronal survival but also establish autocrine survival pathways critical for SCs' functionality [8]. These trophic signals act through Trk receptors, GFRα/RET complexes, and p75NTR‐dependent pathways to modulate neuronal growth, survival, and regeneration [15].

4Induction of myelination

Induction of myelination

SCs are integral to myelin formation in the PNS. The secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) represents a critical step in this process, and the transcription factor Krox20 (also known as Egr2) is a recognized master regulator required for the initiation and maintenance of myelination [8]. Schwann cell myelination is governed by a transcriptional hierarchy involving Sox10, Oct6/POU3F1, and Egr2/Krox20. Sox10 initiates the myelinating program, whereas Egr2/Krox20 acts as a master regulator controlling expression of myelin structural proteins including MPZ(P0), MBP, and PMP22. NRG1/ErbB signaling from axons regulates myelin thickness through PI3K/Akt and ERK pathways [16].

5Homeostasis and neuronal survival

Homeostasis and neuronal survival

SCs and their precursors secrete glial cell line‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and NT3, which activate RET/GFRα and TrkC signaling pathways to support sensory and motor neuron survival [17]. SCPs play a vital role in PNS development by supporting the survival of sensory and motor neurons and their axons through provision of survival signals and nutritional support [18].

6Plasticity of nerve regeneration

Plasticity of nerve regeneration

The plasticity of SCs is a critical aspect of their function in nerve regeneration. After injury, c‐Jun, Sox2, Notch, and NF‐κB promote dedifferentiation, while suppression of Egr2/Krox20, MPZ, MBP, and MAG facilitates conversion into repair SCs, characterized by a change in morphology to a longer, more branched appearance. During later remyelination, Zeb2 and Krox20 restore the myelinating phenotype [19].

These functions highlight the multifaceted role of SCs in maintaining PNS stability, promoting repair mechanisms, and supporting neuronal survival under both physiological and pathological conditions.

Reparative Role of SCs in Peripheral Nerve Injury

Compared to the central nervous system (CNS), damage to the PNS is more common yet remains challenging to repair. Peripheral nerve injury can result from various causes, including mechanical trauma from accidents or tools, iatrogenic injuries, and disease‐related complications. Such injuries often lead to persistent numbness, impaired motor and sensory function, neuropathic pain, and even paralysis [9]. SCs, as the myelinating glial cells of the PNS, possess unique characteristics such as plasticity, secretory capacity, proliferation, and migration. These attributes enable SCs to undergo reprogramming and remodeling after injury, thereby orchestrating and facilitating neuronal repair and regeneration processes [11]. Additionally, SCs act as biological micro‐pumps, secreting neuroactive substances that alleviate pain, which helps mitigate the discomfort caused by nerve damage or irritation [20]. Differentiation and Reprogramming of SCs

Before elaborating on the reprogramming of Schwann cells (SCs) following injury, it is essential to distinguish their two primary mature phenotypes: myelinating and non‐myelinating SCs. These phenotypes, which diverge during development, are structurally and functionally distinct. Myelinating SCs engage in a one‐to‐one relationship with large‐diameter axons, spirally enveloping them to form the multi‐lamellar myelin sheath. This structure is critical for saltatory conduction, drastically increasing the speed of nerve impulses. In contrast, non‐myelinating SCs do not form compact myelin; instead, they ensheath multiple small‐diameter axons within cytoplasmic troughs, collectively known as Remak bundles, providing crucial metabolic support and protection [11, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Molecularly, myelinating SCs are characterized by the expression of key transcription factors such as Egr2 (Krox20) and structural proteins including myelin protein zero (P0), myelin basic protein (MBP), and peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22) [24]. Non‐myelinating SCs, however, typically express markers like glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and the low‐affinity nerve growth factor receptor p75NTR [22]. Following peripheral nerve injury, both SC types undergo dedifferentiation, but their initial states influence their response. Myelinating SCs must first dismantle the myelin sheath, a process involving the downregulation of myelination genes, while non‐myelinating SCs, already in a more plastic state, can rapidly adopt a repair‐supportive phenotype [25, 26]. This fundamental understanding of SC heterogeneity provides the necessary context for appreciating their remarkable plasticity in the face of injury.

The injury response stimulates c‐Jun activation in Schwann cells, driving epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and cellular phenotypic changes, whilst inducing reprogramming to generate reparative Schwann cells that coordinate the peripheral nerve regeneration process [27, 28]. After peripheral nerve injury, both myelinating and non‐myelinating SCs undergo extensive reprogramming, which involves two overlapping processes: demyelination and transformation (or transdifferentiation) into repair SCs. Demyelination is characterized by the suppression of myelination‐promoting genes, including Egr2, Mbp, Pmp22, and Mag. Transformation entails the re‐expression or upregulation of developmentally active genes, particularly those encoding proteins that support axonal regeneration, such as L1‐CAM, neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM), p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) [29].

Differentiation of myelinating Schwann cells may undergo interference from inhibitory pathways that negatively control the expression of genes responsible for myelin sheath formation. NOTCH1 and JUN stand out among the negative regulators of the myelination program, in the same way as SOX‐2 and PAX‐3 [30]. This process involves downregulating myelination genes, upregulating regenerative genes, and activating diverse transcriptional mechanisms. SCs detach from distal axon stumps, demyelinate, and adopt a specialized repair phenotype to facilitate and guide axonal regeneration. The primary participant—the transcription factor c‐Jun—is regarded as the principal driver of SC‐dependent repair programmes and a key coordinator of reparative phenotypic differentiation. It governs the molecular reprogramming that transforms mature Schwann cells into a denervated phenotype following injury [10], while also regulating a series of reparative processes: demyelination clearance, neuronal survival, Büngner band formation, and axonal regeneration [8, 11].

Wallerian Degeneration

After nerve injury, distal nerve fibers are separated from the neuronal cell body, which serves as the center of nutrition and metabolism. The distal nerve fibers, including nerve terminals, begin to degenerate in a process known as Wallerian degeneration (WD). This process involves a series of changes, including axonal and myelin degeneration, SCs proliferation, infiltration of macrophages and mast cells, and the clearance of axonal and myelin debris [31]. In the PNS, injured axons distal to the lesion site rapidly degenerate after separation from the neuronal cell body. Within two days, most distal axons fragment into small pieces, prompting their swift removal and, together with demyelination, creating a favorable environment for axonal regeneration [32]. The most critical event in WD is the phagocytosis and clearance of axonal and myelin debris, an active process involving various cells and substances [33].

In the early stages of WD, SCs act as the primary phagocytes through a process known as myelin autophagy. In the later stages (starting around day 4 post‐injury), SCs release cytokines that serve as chemotactic signals to recruit large numbers of circulating macrophages, which then take over as the primary phagocytes until debris is completely cleared. This removal of cellular debris provides the necessary space for nerve regeneration and reduces inhibitors produced by myelin. Additionally, endothelial cells contribute to nerve regeneration by promoting angiogenesis, SCs' proliferation, and migration [34]. In injured nerves, SCs undergo demyelination, dedifferentiation, proliferation, and migration, ultimately forming Büngner bands to guide axonal regeneration. They secrete various neurotrophic factors to support neuronal survival and provide the nutrients required for regeneration while facilitating axonal growth [33].

Therefore, efficient clearance of myelin debris is considered a critical prerequisite for successful axonal regeneration [18]. WD is an essential step in ensuring axonal regrowth and functional recovery following nerve injury [31].

In addition to their intrinsic regenerative functions, Schwann cells (SCs) coordinate a multicellular repair microenvironment after peripheral nerve injury. Repair SCs interact with macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells to regulate debris clearance, extracellular matrix remodeling, and axonal regeneration. SC‐derived cytokines, including MCP‐1, IL‐6, and LIF, promote macrophage recruitment, while macrophages contribute to myelin debris clearance and regenerative niche formation. Fibroblasts participate in nerve bridge formation and extracellular matrix remodeling, whereas endothelial cells support SC migration and regeneration through angiogenic signals. These coordinated interactions are essential for successful nerve repair. Interestingly, similar regenerative programs may be exploited by tumor cells during perineural invasion, allowing SCs to promote tumor migration, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation [31, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37].

Axonal Regeneration

Once myelin debris is cleared, the proximal nerve stump begins to regrow [38]. Axonal sprouts rapidly emerge from the proximal stump, supported by local cytoskeletal proteins replenished through axonal transport [39]. Migrating nerve‐associated cells, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and SCs, form new tissue bridging the nerve gap, known as the “nerve bridge.” This structure serves as a substrate for regenerating axons to traverse the gap [35]. Repair SCs act as scaffolds for axonal reconstruction. In the distal stump, SCs elongate significantly, overlapping at their extended ends to form longitudinal columns called Büngner bands. These bands function as guidance cues and “cellular tracks” for axonal regrowth [18, 40]. SCs also secrete regeneration‐promoting factors, such as glial cell‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), artemin, and p75NTR. Additionally, repair SCs release interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), which attract macrophages. These macrophages produce vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) that targets endothelial cell receptors, promoting neurogenesis and angiogenesis [41]. The regenerating axon tip, called the growth cone, releases proteases under the influence of various factors to clear the path toward the target organ [42].

Myelin Regeneration

Once axons regenerate to their target locations via Büngner bands, repair SCs initiate remyelination [43]. Studies indicate that transcription factors such as c‐Jun and ZEB2 are essential for this process [44]. Krox20, considered a key regulator of myelination in the PNS, activates numerous myelin‐associated genes, suppresses myelination inhibitors, and maintains the myelinating state [45]. Finally, SCs wrap around the successfully regenerated axons and redifferentiate into myelinating SCs, returning to a quiescent state. However, compared to developmental myelination, the regenerated myelin sheaths are thinner and have shorter internodal lengths, leading to incomplete functional recovery of the nerve after injury [18].

The Role of Schwann Cells in Perineural Invasion of Tumors

Tumor‐Nerve Crosstalk

Neuronal activity governs organ development, systemic homeostasis, plasticity, and regeneration. The formation, growth, and progression of cancer frequently co‐opt and repurpose developmental and regenerative mechanisms, positioning the nervous system as a pivotal player in various aspects of cancer pathophysiology [46]. Similar to regenerative processes, neurotropism in cancer appears to involve dual mechanisms: on one hand, neurotrophic factors released by neurons act synergistically on both cancer cells and stromal cells [47]; on the other hand, neurotrophic factors released by cancer cells promote neural infiltration. Inflammatory mediators, in particular, can alter neural activity and increase neuronal excitability, potentially contributing to neurological manifestations such as epilepsy [48]. Its dependence is particularly evident in the peripheral and central nervous systems, synapses, neurotransmitters, and neurotrophic factors, which influence tumor progression and metastasis through multiple regulatory pathways and the tumor immune microenvironment [49]. Bidirectional signaling between the nervous system and tumors, particularly within the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulates key cancer hallmarks including proliferation, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis [50]. Tumors not only invade nerves but also further promote malignancy by remodeling nerves, establishing a bidirectional interaction. The immune system further mediates this communication as a crucial intermediary between cancer and the nervous system, forming a triangular relationship that influences primary tumor inflammation and the efficacy of immunotherapy [51]. PNI, a hallmark of neuro‐tumor interaction, manifests as cancer cells infiltrating nerve sheaths and establishing close contacts with axons and Schwann cells (SCs), thereby promoting local tumor spread and distant metastasis [52]. The formation and function of PNI are dual‐regulated by molecular (e.g., neurotrophins, cytokines, chemokines, and neurotransmitters), metabolic (e.g., serine metabolism), and cellular mechanisms (e.g., Schwann cells, stromal cells, T cells, and macrophages) [4, 53]. These integrated mechanisms of PNI not only support tumor development, growth, invasion, and metastasis but also mediate pain formation, all of which are closely associated with poor prognosis [4, 54, 55]. Current research indicates that the central nervous system plays a regulatory role in most neurogenic tumors while also influencing certain non‐neurogenic tumors, such as prostate and breast cancers. Conversely, the peripheral nervous system appears to exert regulatory effects on a broader range of non‐neurogenic tumors, including gastrointestinal, pancreatic, breast, and prostate cancers [56].

Although Schwann cells primarily promote perineural infiltration and tumor progression, their role in cancer is not unidirectional. Numerous studies have shown that, under specific conditions, Schwann cells can exert tumor‐suppressive effects. For example, repair‐associated Schwann cells have been shown to secrete EGFL8, a factor that induces neuronal differentiation in neuroblastoma cells and reduces their proliferative capacity [19]. Similarly, Schwann cell‐derived pigment epithelium‐derived factor (PEDF) has been identified as a mediator of anti‐tumor activity in neuroblastoma [57]. These findings emphasize that the functional outcomes of Schwann cell activation in the tumor microenvironment are highly context‐dependent and shaped by a variety of factors, including tumor type, stage, and the local microenvironment.

Schwann Cells in Tumor Microenvironment Formation

Ironically, this orchestrated repair program is paradoxically redeployed within the tumor microenvironment, yielding detrimental consequences. The following section delves into the mechanisms underlying this deleterious role of Schwann cells in perineural invasion. The TME consists of various components, including extracellular matrix (ECM) elements and different cell types, such as immunosuppressive immune cells, macrophages, regulatory T cells, cancer‐associated fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. Multiple cellular phenotypes have been demonstrated to foster tumor advancement via diverse pathophysiological pathways [37]. The neural microenvironment constitutes a crucial component within TME as well, and it exerts a substantial influence on both the initiation and progression of tumors. For instance, the presence of neural innervation within the TME has been associated with tumor invasiveness in diseases such as salivary gland cystadenocarcinoma [58], gastric cancer [59], prostate cancer [60], pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [61], and ovarian cancer [62]. The nervous system, as an early perceiver of tumorigenesis, is intricately entwined in tumor initiation and progression, its influence persisting from nascence to advanced tumor states. PNI enhances the spatial interaction between nerves and tumors and serves as an important mechanism supporting tumor cell migration, characterized by bidirectional chemotaxis between nerves and cancer cells [63]. SCs are instrumental in PNI and constitute one of the most significant cell types within the neural microenvironment of tumors.

SCs display significant plasticity, enabling them to respond to nerve injury signals through cellular reprogramming, which in turn supports the regeneration of peripheral nerves. Within the tumor environment, these attributes render SCs susceptible to manipulation by cancer cells, thereby facilitating tumor progression and metastasis [37]. Once tumor cells invade nerves, SCs interact with cancer cells, altering their phenotypes, inducing the expression of related factors, and remodeling the extracellular matrix, all of which create favorable conditions for cancer cell growth and invasion, thus influencing the prognosis of cancer patients [36]. SCs' reprogramming into a repair‐like progenitor cell state further indicates that their flexibility and regenerative capacity contribute to the plasticity of the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, the TME itself can potentially activate Schwann cells and increase their plasticity [37].

In direct regeneration, damaged SCs undergo an epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT)‐like process, which may enhance their migration and invasive capabilities. As part of indirect regeneration, SCs may release neurotrophic factors and inflammatory cytokines, recruiting immune cells such as macrophages to support the regeneration of damaged peripheral nerves [37]. Both EMT and the release of neurotrophic factors and inflammatory cytokines may promote cancer cell proliferation and dissemination, thus advancing the cancer process. For instance, SCs express neurotrophic factors like NRG1 and NGF, along with their receptors ErbB and p75, facilitating bidirectional communication with tumor cells. Additionally, SCs may directly interact with cancer cells via integrins, NCAM‐1, or MAG‐dependent mechanisms, promoting neuroinvasion in neurotropic tumors [36].

The TME also includes immune‐active neuropeptides/neurotransmitters, such as calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP), substance P, neuropeptide Y, and nerve growth factor (NGF). These neuropeptides and neurotransmitters have various roles within the tumor and can influence tumor growth and invasiveness [64]. As a key component of the nervous system, SCs may affect the TME by secreting these neuropeptides and neurotransmitters, thereby promoting or inhibiting cancer progression.

Interaction Between SCs and Tumor Cells

SCs are also implicated in the origin of certain tumors. Increasing evidence suggests that there is an interaction between the peripheral nervous system and the TME. SCPs play a crucial role in supporting tumorigenesis, contributing to the formation of TME in various tissues, and acting as early markers and drivers of tumor development [65, 66]. In pancreatic and colon cancers, SCs differentiate from nerves surrounding the tumor and migrate toward cancer cells, thereby promoting tumor progression and invasion [67]. In autologous cells, adaptive cellular reprogramming typically occurs following peripheral nerve injury. Similar changes are also observed during tumorigenesis and cancer progression, though the mechanisms differ. During PNI in tumorigenesis, SCs play a crucial role. SCs undergo adaptive reprogramming to acquire a repair phenotype and functions akin to those in nerve regeneration. This change is closely associated with the increased neural innervation observed in cancer [65]. Increasing evidence suggests that when cancer cells invade surrounding nerves, SCs interact with these cancer cells by degrading the surrounding matrix, thereby enhancing the invasive capabilities of the cancer cells [68]. Moreover, SCs can recruit and guide cancer cells during their invasion process and even insert between cancer cells, facilitating their dispersal. During nerve repair, SCs can form tracks and cover with adhesion proteins, which cancer cells may utilize to promote metastasis [36].

SCs and cancer cells have a reciprocal nourishing relationship that directly influences cancer progression. In melanoma, pancreatic cancer, colon cancer, and prostate cancer, SCs can migrate toward tumor cells and attract them, leading to PNI. Furthermore, SCs enhance the invasiveness of cancer cells, PNI, and support metastasis by inducing epithelial‐mesenchymal or mesenchymal‐epithelial transitions in malignant cells [67].

In vitro studies have shown that SCs exhibit strong adaptability toward tumor cells through the interaction of chemokines and their receptors. As a result, tumor cells are dispersed, labeled, and ultimately recruited into the nerve sheath through early physical contact with SCs [69]. When tumor cells invade the endoneurium and damage axons, it triggers a cascade of inflammatory cytokines, creating a unique cellular and biochemical microenvironment around the nerve. Within this microenvironment, the complex interplay between cells initiates a continuous multistep process that promotes tumor progression [67]. After direct physical contact with tumor cells, SCs can disrupt the cell–cell connections within tumor cell clusters, dispersing individual cells and promoting their migration from the cluster. Meanwhile, SCs secrete associated proteins that provide a path for cancer cell movement and facilitate the formation of directional protrusions, guiding the cells toward neurons [65].

After nerve injury, the repair process can induce myelinated SCs to convert into non‐myelinated repair SCs, guiding axonal regeneration. This transformation leads to a massive change in gene expression in SCs. Genes associated with myelinated SCs, such as the myelin transcription factor Egr2 (Krox 20), structural proteins like myelin protein zero (P0), myelin basic protein (MBP), and membrane‐associated glycoproteins (MAG), are downregulated, while genes related to non‐myelinated SCs, such as GFAP, NCAM, L1‐CAM, and p75, are upregulated. During this process, SCs interact with cancer cells and become activated GFAP+ SCs. Studies have shown that GFAP+ SCs are present in precancerous stages of pancreatic and colon cancers, where they not only attract tumor cells but also actively move toward them [70]. This process is similar to WD, but the extent of SC dedifferentiation depends on the cancer cell type and the level of interaction with the cells. In nerve repair, Schwann cells induce axonal sprouting, and upon contact with cancer cells, they can induce the cancer cells to extend protrusions and guide them toward the SCs [36].

Research has also shown that SCs in the TME attract different subgroups of immune regulatory factors and enhance their ability to suppress T cells [68]. SCs promote tumor progression by mediating neurotrophic factors, glial cell‐derived neurotrophic factors (GDNF) family members, guidance molecules, and other molecules. Tumor‐activated SCs produce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), secreted proteolytic enzymes that degrade the extracellular matrix, such as MMP2, MMP9, and MMP12. These MMPs help create a TME that promotes neuroinvasion and further support cancer cell proliferation and invasion through the secretion of CCL‐2 [37] (Figure 2).

MMPs break down the extracellular matrix, creating a tumor microenvironment (TME) conducive to neurotropic invasion, while CCL‐2 promotes cancer cell proliferation and invasion. SCs also enhance the immunosuppressive effect on effector T cells, reducing their ability to kill tumor cells.

In addition to PNI, a “cancer‐associated neurogenesis” pattern can also develop between cancer and nerves. Cancer cells can activate nerve‐dependent pathways to recruit peripheral nerves into the tumor. Reprogrammed SCs utilize their induced neuroregenerative capabilities to promote axonal growth, extending into the tumor and contributing to tumor innervation. As neurogenesis increases, neurotransmitter production also rises, promoting tumor proliferation and dissemination, ultimately furthering tumor progression. Thus, during the early stages of tumorigenesis and progression, SCs exhibit a strong affinity for cancer cells. In this tumor‐neuron interaction, SCs undergo reprogramming to facilitate PNI and tumor innervation, ultimately establishing a positive feedback loop between cancer and nerves [65].

The Role of SCs in Promoting Cancer Progression: Insights From Particular Cancers

Pancreatic Cancer

In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), tumor cell infiltration into nerves is a hallmark feature, involving not only the intrapancreatic nerves but also nerve plexuses outside the pancreas. Studies have shown that the presence of tumor infiltration in both intra‐ and extra‐pancreatic nerves is significantly correlated with decreased patient survival [36]. Compared to other cancers, PDAC exhibits a higher frequency of PNI, with the interaction between SCs and tumor cells playing a crucial role in the poor prognosis associated with PDAC.

Specifically, the mechanisms by which SCs contribute to PDAC progression can be summarized as follows: (1) Paracrine signaling: SCs form feedback loops via paracrine mechanisms, such as TIMP1 and CCL7, which cooperate with PDAC cells to promote the occurrence of PNI [71]. (2) Neurotrophic factor activation: Pancreatic cancer cells secrete neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF) that activate SCs, thereby facilitating tumor progression. (3) Tumor‐stroma interaction: SCs drive the development of cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) toward more malignant subtypes, such as basal‐like and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), exacerbating the aggressiveness of PDAC [72].

Additionally, tumor‐derived extracellular vesicles activate SCs through IL‐8 signaling, inducing the production of a range of cytokines (e.g., IL‐8, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, IL‐6, CCL2, and MMP‐2). This process, which is responsible for stabilizing the protein level of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1), triggers the amplification of NGF expression and thereby facilitates the proliferation and migration of SCs [73].

Lung Cancer

SCs play a critical role in controlling and maintaining the lung cancer microenvironment, supporting metastasis, and their numbers are closely associated with patient survival rates. SCs secrete various factors, including microRNAs, which enhance the lung cancer environment by regulating the tumor‐neuroimmune axis. They can modulate the motility and invasiveness of lung cancer cells in vitro, as well as the development of metastasis in vivo. Studies have demonstrated that SCs support EMT and the migration of lung cancer cells through CXCR2 by releasing the chemokine CXCL5, increasing the expression of Snail and Twist (functional markers of EMT), and activating the PI3K/AKT/GSK‐3β/Snail‐Twist signaling pathway in lung cancer cells [74].

Moreover, SCs also exhibit pro‐tumor activity by directly acting on malignant cells and regulating the infiltration of immune cells in the tumor immune microenvironment. In vitro experiments have shown that SCs can induce macrophages to shift to an M2 phenotype [75]. This transformation serves to underpin the restoration of peripheral nerves while simultaneously spurring the multiplication of lung cancer cells, revealing an additional functional pathway of the tumor‐PNS/glial cells‐immune axis [37].

Prostate Cancer

The role of nerves in cancer progression has also been demonstrated in experimental models of prostate cancer. Approximately 30%–70% of prostate cancer cases show PNI, resulting in a worse prognosis [76, 77]. In contrast, in the denervation prostate cancer model, the growth rate of cancer cells is significantly slower [36]. During metastasis, prostate cancer cells destroy nerves by surrounding or destroying Schwann cells. At the same time, Schwann cells promote integrin‐dependent cancer cell invasion and migration on laminin, increasing the invasion of laminin by 1.3–2.0 times. Integrin function (e.g., ITGA 6p formation) is increased up to 1.5‐fold in prostate (DU 145, PC 3, RWPE‐1) cells and invasion is dependent on ITGA 6p formation and ITGB 1. In the process, the myelin‐forming phenotype of Schwann cells in the tumor microenvironment increases integrin‐independent tumor invasion on laminin [78].

Colorectal Cancer

Enteric glial cells play a crucial role in maintaining the physiological functions of the gastrointestinal tract, including nutrient absorption, barrier integrity, and immune regulation. These cells are also pivotal in the development of colorectal cancer (CRC). Emerging research indicates that enteric glial cells promote CRC progression through interactions with cancer cells, influencing inflammatory responses, and modulating the TME [5]. Notably, exosomes secreted by colorectal cancer cells contain miR‐21‐5p, which inhibits the expression of VHL, stabilizing HIF‐1 protein levels [79]. In colorectal cancer, MAGEA6 has been identified as a key driver of PNI and tumor aggressiveness by inhibiting YY1 ubiquitination to stabilize YY1 expression, which subsequently upregulates CXCL1 transcription and secretion to promote Schwann cell recruitment [80]. This process, in turn, amplifies NGF expression and activates Schwann cells, further exacerbating the malignant progression of CRC [73].

The Differences and Commonalities Across Different Cancers

Based on the mechanisms discussed above, Schwann cells appear to play distinct roles in different cancers. We propose that the fundamental reason for this lies in the dissimilar origins of the respective tissues, which lead to divergent gene expression programs. For instance, pancreatic cancer is characterized by a high prevalence of KRAS mutations [81] and a robust fibrotic response, whereas prostate cancer is driven by androgen signaling [82]. These inherent differences dictate how each cancer type communicates with its microenvironment.

Additional factors contributing to this distinction include variations in both the neural microenvironment and the broader TME. The density, types including sympathetic, parasympathetic, sensory, and distribution of normal innervation differ significantly among organs. The prostate and pancreas, for example, are enveloped by rich autonomic nerve plexuses, while the colon contains a unique enteric nervous system [83]. This provides PNI with organ‐specific “infrastructure.” Similarly, the TME in each tumor constitutes a unique ecosystem, composed of different immune cell infiltrates, fibroblast subtypes, vascular networks, and ECM components [84, 85, 86]. These elements not only interact directly with nerves but also profoundly influence the behavior of both cancer cells and Schwann cells.

However, further comparative analysis indicates that, following its hijacking in different cancer types, this fundamental “repair programme” exhibits significant cancer‐specific differences in its signal amplification mechanisms and downstream effector activation. Based on recent literature, such as the systematic review by He et al. [87] on the functions of SCs in various peripheral cancers and the summary by Zhang et al. [88] on the role of SCs in the tumor immune microenvironment, we have summarized the similarities and differences in the molecular characteristics of SC activation across four representative cancer types. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the IL‐8/HIF‐1α axis, driven by high‐frequency KRAS mutations, strongly amplifies NGF expression, leading to the abnormal activation of SCs as early as the precancerous stage [72, 89, 90]; furthermore, the activation of the L1CAM/STAT3 pathway specifically drives the high expression of MMP2/9 [36]. In colorectal cancer, MAGEA6 enhances CRC invasiveness and PNI by stabilizing YY1, which upregulates CXCL1 secretion and promotes SC recruitment [80]; moreover, miR‐21‐5p from cancer cell‐derived exosomes stabilizes HIF‐1α by inhibiting VHL, constituting a unique paracrine activation pathway, with the positive feedback loop of NGF signaling being particularly prominent [91]. In lung cancer, SCs are more inclined to drive Snail‐ and Twist‐mediated epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) via the CXCL5/CXCR2 axis, and reports of SCs inducing macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype are particularly abundant, suggesting that immunoregulation plays a more central role in the oncogenic function of lung cancer SCs [75, 92]; In prostate cancer, however, SCs are characterized by a particularly pronounced upregulation of the integrin subunits ITGA6 and ITGB1, mediating PNI by promoting the anchoring‐mediated invasion of cancer cells via laminin [77, 78]; furthermore, SC activation is closely intertwined with androgen receptor signaling. These differences may stem from the unique driving mutation profiles of each cancer type (e.g., KRAS mutation rates > 90% in PDAC [93] and AR‐signal‐dependence in prostate cancer [94]) as well as organ‐specific neural microenvironments (e.g., the unique enteric nervous system in colorectal cancer [95]).

In summary, the oncogenic function of SCs is not a simple “all‐or‐nothing” effect, but rather a complex spectrum shaped by both core, universal programmes and cancer‐specific modifications. This molecular landscape, characterized by both commonalities and specificities, suggests that cross‐cancer comparative studies must go beyond descriptions at the organ level to deeply analyze the coupling relationships between upstream driving signals and downstream effector pathways, thereby laying the theoretical foundation for the subsequent development of cancer‐specific targeted intervention strategies [96, 97].

As mentioned above, adenocarcinomas can activate Schwann cells by secreting neurotrophic factors such as NGF and BDNF [98], and mediate extracellular matrix remodeling via matrix metalloproteinases, thereby forming a positive feedback loop that promotes tumor invasion and subsequently drives the infiltration of nerves surrounding the tumor [99]. Despite differences in tissue origin and tumor microenvironment among cancers, SC‐mediated perineural invasion appears to share several conserved molecular programs [87, 88]. Tumor‐derived signals induce SC reprogramming from a myelinating phenotype toward an activated repair‐like state, characterized by downregulation of myelin‐related genes (e.g., EGR2/Krox20, MPZ, MBP, and MAG) and upregulation of regeneration‐associated markers, including GFAP, p75NTR, NCAM, and L1‐CAM [37]. In addition, neurotrophic signaling involving NGF, BDNF, and their receptors promotes bidirectional communication between SCs and tumor cells [36]. Activated SCs further facilitate tumor invasion through adhesion molecules (e.g., integrins and NCAM), extracellular MMPs, and immune regulation (CCL2‐mediated immune modulation) [67]. Thus, although cancer‐specific contexts differ, SC activation during perineural invasion may converge on several molecular switches, including phenotypic reprogramming, neurotrophic signaling, matrix remodeling, and immune regulation, representing a common mechanism exploited by tumors.

Comparison of SC Functions in General Nerve Injury and Tumor Nerve Invasion

The functional differences of SCs in neural and tumor injuries provide novel perspectives into cancer therapy. In normal injury, SCs play a critical role in nerve function recovery through their repair processes. However, in tumor injury, the same functions of SCs may be co‐opted by tumor cells to promote cancer progression. This dual role highlights the importance of precise regulation of SCs in cancer therapy to prevent them from facilitating tumor advancement (Figure 3).

Functional comparison of SCs in general nerve injury and tumor invasion of nerves. When nerves are injured, Schwann cells mediate repair through five sequential processes: Phenotypic Transition of Repair; Clearance of Myelin Debris; Axonal Regeneration and Tumor Metastasis; Role of Neurotrophic Factors and Cytokines; Regulation of Immune Response. In General Nerve Injury: Schwann cells dedifferentiate into a repair phenotype to facilitate nerve regeneration. They recruit immune cells to clear myelin debris, establishing a microenvironment conducive to axonal regrowth. Büngner bands are formed to guide axons. Neurotrophic factors and cytokines are secreted to support repair and modulate immune responses. In Cancer Invasion: Schwann cells are reprogrammed into GFAP+ SCs, which activate cancer cells. Debris clearance promotes tumor cell proliferation and invasion. Büngner bands facilitate the metastatic spread of cancer cells. Neurotrophic factors contribute to TME, while immune regulation is suppressed, enabling tumor immune evasion and accelerating cancer progression.

Phenotypic Transition of Repair

The response of SCs to nerve injury involves demyelination and dedifferentiation, but the outcomes vary depending on the type of injury. In normal injury, SCs transition into a unique repair phenotype resembling neonatal SCs. In contrast, in tumor injury, myelinated SCs dedifferentiate and, upon interacting with cancer cells, are reprogrammed to express high levels of GFAP, becoming activated GFAP+ SCs [100]. These cells, similar to the reprogrammed SCs involved in neural repair, scatter cancer cells and stimulate migration and invasion [101]. However, the specific factors that induce SCs' reprogramming after nerve trauma and cancer infiltration remain unidentified.

Axonal Regeneration and Tumor Metastasis

In normal injury repair, SCs clear myelin debris to create a favorable environment for axonal regeneration. In the context of cancer, SCs retain their neural repair functions, including the ability to promote axonal regeneration and attract immune cells. However, the impact of this cellular recruitment process on tumor cells requires further investigation. In this repair process, SCs also guide axonal regeneration through the formation of the Büngner bands. The mechanisms by which SCs guide cancer progression share strong similarities with those in neural repair. SCs promote axonal regeneration by releasing axon‐guiding molecules, directly interacting with axons via adhesion molecules, and modifying the extracellular matrix to support nerve extension [100]. This guidance function may be hijacked by cancer cells, promoting their metastasis and invasion.

Role of Neurotrophic Factors and Cytokines

In normal neural repair, the neurotrophic factors and cytokines secreted by SCs are crucial for nerve regeneration and repair. In the TME, these same factors—such as NGF, BDNF, and neurotrophin‐3 (NT3) [102], as well as other factors like the chemokine CCL2 and cytokine IL‐6 [100], may contribute to the formation of the tumor microenvironment or directly influence cancer growth, providing survival and proliferative advantages to tumor cells.

Regulation of Immune Response

In the context of neural repair, SCs exhibit immune functions, including antigen recognition, antigen presentation, and the modulation of immune responses. In cancer, SCs also regulate immune responses by releasing certain mediators. For instance, SCs secrete CCL2, which attracts monocytes to the sites of cancer invasion, where they differentiate into pro‐tumor macrophages [103].

Overall, SCs interact with tumor cells in multiple ways during tumor PNI. On one hand, SCs contribute to the formation of the TME and secrete various proteins that promote tumor cell invasion and metastasis. On the other hand, tumor cell invasion leads to nerve damage, which activates SCs to initiate dedifferentiation and reprogram the surrounding environment. The released substances from the tumor microenvironment further promote tumor cell invasion and metastasis. During this process, tumor cells and SCs attract each other, creating a positive feedback loop that worsens the patient's condition. In addition to nerve invasion mechanisms, a “cancer‐associated neurogenesis” pattern also develops between cancer and nerves. Tumor cells recruit peripheral nerves into the tumor, with SCs facilitating this process and contributing to tumor innervation. Furthermore, as neurogenesis increases, the production of neurotransmitters also rises, which further promotes tumor proliferation and dissemination, ultimately advancing tumor progression.

Determinants of Schwann Cell Fate Divergence Between Regeneration and Tumor Progression

Although Schwann cells (SCs) activate similar regenerative programs during peripheral nerve repair and tumor‐associated perineural invasion (PNI), their biological outcomes differ depending on the surrounding microenvironment and signaling context [36, 37].

During acute nerve injury, SCs undergo a transient repair response characterized by c‐Jun activation, demyelination, debris clearance, secretion of neurotrophic factors, and formation of Büngner bands to support axonal regeneration [10, 102]. c‐Jun functions as a key transcriptional regulator that reprograms mature SCs into repair phenotypes by activating regeneration‐associated genes and suppressing the myelination program [10]. During subsequent recovery, restoration of myelination regulators such as EGR2/Krox20 contributes to SC redifferentiation and remyelination [44].

In contrast, tumors generate a persistent injury‐like microenvironment characterized by chronic inflammation, hypoxia, metabolic alterations, and sustained neurotrophic signaling, which may prevent SCs from exiting the repair state [37, 100]. Activated SCs can promote tumor progression through neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF and BDNF), adhesion molecules (e.g., NCAM and integrins), extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation [36, 67, 100].

Inflammatory duration and immune context represent additional determinants of SC functional divergence. During physiological repair, transient inflammation facilitates debris clearance and tissue restoration, whereas chronic tumor‐associated inflammation may convert regenerative responses into tumor‐supportive mechanisms [31, 67]. For example, SC‐derived CCL2 can recruit inflammatory monocytes and contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that facilitates perineural invasion [103].

Importantly, the tumor‐promoting effect of SCs is not an inevitable consequence of their regenerative function but rather a context‐dependent process influenced by cancer type, tumor stage, neural environment, and the balance between regenerative and pathological signals [37, 100]. Under certain conditions, SCs may maintain tissue homeostasis and participate in protective immune regulation rather than promoting tumor progression [67, 102]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms controlling SC activation and termination may provide opportunities to selectively inhibit pathological SC functions while preserving their essential regenerative capacity.

The Context‐Dependent Dual Role of Schwann Cells in Cancer

Although increasing evidence supports a tumor‐promoting role of activated Schwann cells (SCs) during perineural invasion (PNI), the functional effects of SCs are not universally pro‐tumorigenic [36, 37, 100]. Instead, SC functions appear to be highly context‐dependent and influenced by the cellular environment, activation state, and molecular signals received from surrounding tissues [102].

Under physiological or certain pathological conditions, SCs may contribute to tissue protection by maintaining neural homeostasis, regulating immune responses, and limiting excessive inflammation. Previous studies have also suggested that SCs can exert tumor‐suppressive effects in specific contexts through mechanisms including regulation of tumor cell behavior and modulation of the extracellular environment [19, 57].

Therefore, the transition of SCs from regenerative supporters to tumor promoters should not be considered an inevitable consequence of their repair phenotype. Rather, SC fate represents a dynamic spectrum determined by the balance between regenerative and pathological signals. Understanding the molecular determinants that drive SCs toward either tumor‐supportive or tumor‐restrictive states will be essential for developing strategies that selectively inhibit pathological SC activation while preserving their beneficial regenerative functions.

Conclusion

In summary, SCs undergo phenotypic changes in response to injury signals, playing a reparative role in nerves but a tumor‐promoting role in cancer contexts. We posit that this duality represents a fundamental pathological hijacking of neural repair mechanisms. Interactions between SCs and cancers, as exemplified in pancreatic, lung, and colon cancers, facilitate tumorigenesis through PNI, enhanced cell invasiveness, and modulation of the tumor‐immune axis. Looking forward, research must pivot from observation to mechanism and translation. The primary task is to elucidate the specific role of Schwann cells in tumor‐induced nerve damage, which will provide an indispensable theoretical foundation for targeted interventions. Key directions include deciphering the precise molecular signals that switch SCs from a regenerative to a pro‐tumorigenic state and mapping the tumor‐neuro‐immune crosstalk orchestrated by SCs. The paramount translational challenge is to develop therapies that selectively disrupt tumor‐SC interactions—for instance, by targeting adhesion molecules like NCAM‐1 or integrins—while simultaneously validating and ensuring their critical functions in normal neuroregeneration remain unaffected. Addressing this specificity gap will be critical for designing novel interventions and refining prognostic strategies.
