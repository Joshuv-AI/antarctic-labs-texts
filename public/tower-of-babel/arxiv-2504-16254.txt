Abstract
. Modularity is a parameter indicating the presence of community structure in the graph. Nowadays it lies at the core of widely used clustering algorithms. We study the modularity of the most classical random graph, binomial G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) . In 2020 McDiarmid and Skerman proved, taking advantage of the spectral graph theory and a specific subgraph construction by Coja-Oghlan from 2007, that there exists a constant b b such that with high probability the modularity of G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) is at most b / n ​ p b/\sqrt{np} . The obtained constant b b is very big and not easily computable. We improve upon this result showing that a constant under 3 3 may be derived here. Interesting is the fact that it might be obtained by basic probabilistic tools. We also address the lower bound on the modularity of G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) and improve the results of McDiarmid and Skerman from 2020 using estimates of bisections of random graphs derived by Dembo, Montanari, and Sen in 2017.



1.
Introduction

Identifying communities in a graph is a task of a great importance. It has a number of practical applications, varying from identifying the groups of common interests in social networks, through classifying fake news or spam, searching articles on related topic, identifying proteins with the same biological functions, optimizing large technological infrastructures, up to networks visualization
[
13
,
22
]
. Therefore various algorithmic approaches to graph clustering arose over the years. Just to mention a few: some are based on random walks, others on hierarchical clustering, label propagation, or spectral bisection method
[
13
]
. Recently widely used algorithms that offer a good trade-off between the time complexity and the quality of identified communities are Louvain algorithm
[
2
]
and its follow-ups - Leiden algorithm
[
28
]
, and Tel-Aviv algorithm
[
10
]
. They are similar in nature, i.e., they heuristically try to optimize a parameter called
modularity
. Modularity (introduced by Newman and Girvan in 2004
[
23
]
) serves as a measure of the presence of community structure in the graph. As mentioned, it is commonly utilized in practice, however many theoretical questions about it, even for very classical families of graphs, still remain unanswered.

We study the modularity function of the most classical random graph, binomial
G(n,p)
. First such results were given by McDiarmid and Skerman just in 2020
[
20
]
. One can check the appendix therein for a summary of results on modularity for various graph classes (
[
3
,
4
,
17
,
19
,
21
,
24
]
) and further references. More recent discoveries one finds in
[
5
]
by Chellig, Fountoulakis and Skerman (for random graphs on the hyperbolic plane),
[
14
]
by Lasoń and Sulkowska (for minor-free graphs),
[
15
]
by Lichev and Mitsche (for
3
-regular graphs and graphs with a given degree sequence),
[
27
]
by Rybarczyk and Sulkowska and
[
18
]
by McDiarmid, Rybarczyk, Skerman, and Sulkowska (for preferential attachment graphs), and
[
26
]
by Rybarczyk for random intersection graphs.

The authors of
[
20
]
showed that the modularity of
G(n,p)
with high probability decreases at the rate
1/\sqrt{np}
by
n
tending to infinity (note that
np
may be asymptotically interpreted as an average degree). In this paper we study the upper and the lower bound on the modularity of
G(n,p)
. We show how, using basic probabilistic methods, significantly improve the upper bound presented in
[
20
]
. We also improve upon the lower bound from
[
20
]
using the estimates of bisections of random graphs from
[
9
]
.

Notation, previous results, and our findings are presented in Section
2
. In Section
3
we derive a new upper bound on the modularity of
G(n,p)
. Doing that, we use a technical lemma bounding the number of edges of induced subgraphs of
G(n,p)
- its proof may be found in Section
4
. The last section is devoted to the lower bound on
G(n,p)
.

2.
Notation and statement of results

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. If a random variable
X
follows a binomial distribution with parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
we write
X\sim\textrm{Bin}\left(n,p\right)
. For the functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we use the notation
f(n)=o(g(n))
and
f(n)\ll g(n)
interchangeably and understand it as
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)=0
. Unless otherwise stated,
o(\cdot)
relates to
n\to\infty
. By
f(n)\sim g(n)
we mean
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/g(n)=1
.
We say that an event
\mathscr{E}=\mathscr{E}_{n}
occurs with high probability (whp) if the probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathscr{E}_{n}\right)
tend to
1
as
n
tends to infinity. All inequalities in the paper are true for
n
large enough and, for clarity of notation, we omit floors and ceilings when it does not influence a reasoning.

The graphs considered are finite, simple and undirected. Thus a graph is a pair
G=(V(G),E(G))
, where
V(G)
is a finite set of vertices and
E(G)\subseteq V(G)^{(2)}
where, for any set
S
,
S^{(2)}
stands for a set of all
2
-element subsets of
S
. Let
e(G)=|E(G)|
and for
S\subseteq V(G)
set
\bar{S}=V(G)\setminus S
,
e(S)=|\{e\in E(G)\cap S^{(2)}\}|
, and
e(S,\bar{S})=|\{e\in E(G):e\cap S\neq\emptyset\wedge e\cap\bar{S}\neq\emptyset\}|
. The degree of a vertex
v\in V(G)
in
G
, denoted by
\deg(v)
, is the number of edges to which
v
belongs, i.e.,
\deg(v)=|\{e\in E(G):v\in e\}|
. We define the volume of
S\subseteq V
in
G
by
\vol(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\deg(v)
. By the volume of the graph,
\vol(G)
, we understand
\vol(V(G))
.

We also introduce several basic definitions originating from spectral graph theory. We do not make use of its tools in our proofs, however we refer to the results that do that. For a graph
G
on the vertex set
[n]
by
A=\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
denote its adjacency matrix (i.e.
a_{i,j}=1
if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
and
a_{i,j}=0
otherwise) and by
D=\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
its diagonal degree matrix (i.e.
d_{i,i}=\deg(i)
and
d_{i,j}=0
for
i\neq j
). A matrix
{\mathscr{L}}=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}
is called a normalized Laplacian of
G
(here
D^{-1/2}
stands for a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\deg(i)^{-1/2}
if
\deg(i)\neq 0
and
0
otherwise). By
0=\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n-1}\leq 2
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathscr{L}
(see
[
6
]
). Then
\bar{\lambda}(G)=\max_{i\neq 0}|1-\lambda_{i}|=\max\{|1-\lambda_{1}|,|1-\lambda_{n-1}|\}
is called the spectral gap of
G
.

We work with the classical binomial random graph and state its definition as it appears in
[
11
]
by Janson, Łuczak, and Ruciński.

Definition 1

(Binomial random graph
G(n,p)
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
. The binomial random graph is defined by taking the set of all graphs on vertex set
[n]
as a sample space
\Omega
, defining
\mathscr{F}=2^{\Omega}
as an event space, and for
G\in\Omega
setting

\mathbb{P}\left(G\right)=p^{e(G)}(1-p)^{{n\choose 2}-e(G)}.

Obviously,
G(n,p)
may be seen as a result of
{n\choose 2}
independent Bernoulli trials, one for each pair of vertices from
[n]
, with the probability of success (which is including a particular edge in the graph) equal to
p
. Throughout the paper we consider
p
as a function of
n
,
p=p(n)
, and often denote
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
. Moreover, with
S\subseteq V(G(n,p))
we often associate a function
{\bf s}={\bf s}(n)
such that
|S|={\bf s}n
.

We focus on bounding a graph parameter called modularity for
G(n,p)
. Its formal definition is given just below.

Definition 2

(Modularity,
[
23
]
)
.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph with at least one edge. For a partition
{\mathscr{A}}
of
V(G)
define a modularity score of
G
as

(1)

\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G)=\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\left(\frac{e(S)}{e(G)}-\frac{\vol(S)^{2}}{\vol(G)^{2}}\right)=\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\frac{4e(S)e(G)-\vol(S)^{2}}{4e(G)^{2}}.

Modularity of
G
is given by

\modu(G)=\max_{{\mathscr{A}}}\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G),

where maximum runs over all the partitions of the set
V(G)
. For a graph
G
with no edges set
\modu(G)=0
.

A single summand of the modularity score is the difference between the fraction of edges within
S
and the expected fraction of edges within
S
if we considered a certain random multigraph on
V(G)
with the expected degree sequence given by
G
(see, e.g.,
[
12
]
). It is easy to check that
\modu(G)\in[0,1)
. Higher values of
\modu(G)
are meant to indicate the clearer community structure in
G
.

As already mentioned, the first results on the modularity of
G(n,p)
appeared in
2020
and are due to McDiarmid and Skerman
[
20
]
. Among others, they established a general relation between the modularity of
G
and a spectral gap of
G
.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 6.1 of
[
20
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one edge and no isolated vertices. Let
\bar{\lambda}(G)
be a spectral gap of
G
. Then

\modu(G)\leq\bar{\lambda}(G).

The spectral gap of
G(n,p)
was already investigated many years earlier. In
[
7
]
from
2003
Chung, Lu, and Vu studied eigenvalues of random graphs with given expected degrees. Their results applied to
G(n,p)
may be formulated as follows (compare also (1.2) in
[
8
]
by Coja-Oghlan).

Theorem 4

(Theorem 3.6 of
[
7
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in(0,1]
. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
and assume that
{\bf d}\gg(\ln{n})^{2}
. Let also
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph and
\bar{\lambda}(G(n,p))
its spectral gap. Then with high probability

\bar{\lambda}(G(n,p))\leq(1+o(1))\frac{4}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

Obviously, by Lemma
3
and Theorem
4
one gets the whp upper bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
of the order
1/\sqrt{{\bf d}}
for a certain range of
{\bf d}
.

Corollary 5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in(0,1]
and let
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
and assume that
{\bf d}\gg(\ln{n})^{2}
. Then with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\leq(1+o(1))\frac{4}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

The
1/\sqrt{{\bf d}}
growth rate of
\modu(G(n,p))
when
{\bf d}
is above
1
and not too big was first conjectured in 2006 by Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
25
]
. In 2020 McDiarmid and Skerman formally confirmed it proving the following result.

Theorem 6

(Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 4.1 of
[
20
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in(0,1]
, and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
. Then there exists a constant
b
such that with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

Moreover, there exist constants
c_{0}
and
a
such that

(1)

if
{\bf d}
satisfies
c_{0}\leq{\bf d}<n-c_{0}
for sufficiently large
n
, then with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\geq\frac{1}{5}\frac{\sqrt{1-p}}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}},

(2)

if
{\bf d}
satisfies
1\leq{\bf d}<n-c_{0}
for sufficiently large
n
, then with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\geq a\frac{\sqrt{1-p}}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

First, we concentrate on the upper bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
. The authors of
[
20
]
derived their upper bound in Theorem
6
using quite sophisticated tools. Following the construction from
[
8
]
they extracted a particular induced subgraph
H
of
G(n,p)
, not possessing vertices of very high or very low degrees. Next, they took advantage of the result for a spectral gap of
H
from
[
8
]
and applied Lemma
3
to get the whp upper bound on the modularity of
H
. Finally, they proved that modularity is robust under removing not too many edges from the graph to justify that the modularity of
G(n,p)
with high probability will not differ too much from the modularity of
H
. From their proof it follows that the constant
b
in Theorem
6
is huge and, in practice, not easily computable.

In this paper we show that the better upper bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
, with a specific small constant under
3
, may be obtained using basic probabilistic tools. Our proof gives also the straightforward insight into how the
1/\sqrt{{\bf d}}
growth rate of the uppper bound is related to the fluctuations of the number of edges of the induced subgraphs of
G(n,p)
. The first one of two main theorems of this paper is stated below.

Theorem 7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in(0,1)
, and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
and assume that
16.17\leq{\bf d}\ll n
.
Then with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\leq\left(\frac{3+2\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}\approx\frac{2.91}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

Remark 8
.

The bound
{\bf d}\geq 16.17
in the above theorem is the effect of a trade-off between the final value in the upper bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
and the smallest value of
{\bf d}
for which the proof is valid. We could as well set the parameters in the proof (i.e., in Lemma
12
set the lower bound on
C
slightly smaller than
2.1
) to get that for any
{\bf d}\ll n
with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\leq\left(\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})\,2.1}{4}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}\approx\frac{3.06}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

On the other hand, if we consider
{\bf d}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, we can get that for any
C>2\sqrt{\ln{2}}
with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))\leq\left(\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})C}{4}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

Note that
\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})2\sqrt{\ln{2}}}{4}\approx 2.43
. Further comments on this fact one finds in Section
4
in Remark
15
.

Next, we turn to the lower bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
. Lower bounds on modularity are frequently obtained by constructing a partition
{\mathscr{A}}=\{S,\bar{S}\}
that minimizes
e(S,\bar{S})
, and, in many cases, consequently increases the values of
e(S)
and
e(\bar{S})
. This technique has been successfully applied in a number of papers; see, for example,
[
1
,
19
,
20
,
24
]
. Using this approach, and leveraging known results about bisections of random graphs by Dembo, Montanari, and Sen
[
9
]
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\bf d}\geq 1
,
p=p(n)={\bf d}/n
, and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph.
Then with high probability, for large
{\bf d}

\modu(G(n,p))\geq\frac{{P_{*}}+o_{{\bf d}}(1)}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}},

where
P_{*}=0.76321\pm 0.00003
and
o_{\bf d}(\cdot)
relates to
{\bf d}\to\infty
.

For completeness let us also comment on the value of
\modu(G(n,p))
when
{\bf d}\leq 1+o(1)
. Then, if only
{\bf d}n\to\infty
, the modularity score of the partition dictated by connected components with high probability tends to
1
. The proof of this fact may be found in
[
20
]
and is stated formally below.

Theorem 10

(Theorem 1.1 of
[
20
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in(0,1)
, and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
and assume that
{\bf d}n\to\infty
and
{\bf d}\leq 1+o(1)
. Then with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))=1-o(1).

3.
Upper bound on the modularity of
\mathbf{G(n,p)}
– proof of Theorem
7

First, it will be useful to observe that, given a graph
G
and a partition
{\mathscr{A}}
of
V(G)
, the formula for
\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G)
may be rewritten in terms of
e(S)
,
e(\bar{S})
and
e(S,\bar{S})
, where
S\in{\mathscr{A}}
.

Fact 11
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one edge. Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be a partition of
V(G)
. Then

\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G)=\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\frac{4e(S)e(\bar{S})-e(S,\bar{S})^{2}}{4e(G)^{2}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\vol(S)

\displaystyle=2e(S)+e(S,\bar{S})\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle e(G)

\displaystyle=e(S)+e(S,\bar{S})+e(\bar{S}),

therefore

\displaystyle 4e(G)e(S)

\displaystyle=4e(S)^{2}+4e(S)e(S,\bar{S})+4e(S)e(\bar{S})\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\vol(S)^{2}

\displaystyle=4e(S)^{2}+4e(S)e(S,\bar{S})+e(S,\bar{S})^{2}.

Consequently

4e(G)e(S)-\vol(S)^{2}=4e(S)e(\bar{S})-e(S,\bar{S})^{2}

which substituted to formula (
1
) from Definition
2
gives

(2)

\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G)=\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\frac{4e(S)e(\bar{S})-e(S,\bar{S})^{2}}{4e(G)^{2}}.

∎

Next, note that for
S\subseteq V(G(n,p))
with
|S|={\bf s}n
we have
e(S)\sim\textrm{Bin}\left(\binom{{\bf s}n}{2},p\right)
,
e(\bar{S})\sim\textrm{Bin}\left(\binom{(1-{\bf s})n}{2},p\right)
, and
e(S,\bar{S})\sim\textrm{Bin}\left({\bf s}(1-{\bf s})n^{2},p\right)
, thus by
{\bf d}=np
we get
\mathbb{E}[e(S)]=\frac{{\bf s}^{2}{\bf d}n}{2}-\frac{{\bf s}{\bf d}}{2}
,
\mathbb{E}[e(\bar{S})]=\frac{(1-{\bf s})^{2}{\bf d}n}{2}-\frac{(1-{\bf s}){\bf d}}{2}
, and
\mathbb{E}[e(S,\bar{S})]={\bf s}(1-{\bf s})n{\bf d}
. Therefore the following bounds on
e(S)
,
e(\bar{S})
, and
e(S,\bar{S})
may be derived mainly by Chernoff’s inequality.

Lemma 12
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p=p(n)\in[0,1]
and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph. Let also
C\geq 1.999
. Set
{\bf d}={\bf d}(n)=np
, and let
{\bf d}\geq 16.17
. Then we get that with high probability for all
S\subseteq V(G(n,p))
, with
{\bf s}={\bf s}(n)=|S|/n
,

(3)

\displaystyle e(S)

\displaystyle\leq{\bf s}\left({\bf s}+C{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{ and }

(4)

\displaystyle e(\bar{S})

\displaystyle\leq(1-{\bf s})\left((1-{\bf s})+C{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}\quad\quad\quad\text{ and }

(5)

\displaystyle e(S,\bar{S})

\displaystyle\geq\left({\bf s}(1-{\bf s})-C\sqrt{{\bf s}(1-{\bf s})}{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)n{\bf d}.

Now we show how Lemma
12
implies Theorem
7
. The proof of Lemma
12
one finds in Section
4
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Set
C=1.999
. By Lemma
12
we get that with high probability for all
S\subseteq V(G(n,p))
, where
{\bf s}={\bf s}(n)=|S|/n
,

\displaystyle\frac{4e(S)e(\bar{S})-e(S,\bar{S})^{2}}{(n{\bf d})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{\bf s}(1-{\bf s})\left({\bf s}+C{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)\left((1-{\bf s})+C{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left({\bf s}(1-{\bf s})-C\sqrt{{\bf s}(1-{\bf s})}{\bf d}^{-1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={\bf s}^{2}(1-{\bf s})^{2}+C{\bf s}(1-{\bf s}){\bf d}^{-1/2}+C^{2}{\bf s}(1-{\bf s}){\bf d}^{-1}

(6)

\displaystyle\quad-{\bf s}^{2}(1-{\bf s})^{2}+2C({\bf s}(1-{\bf s}))^{3/2}{\bf d}^{-1/2}-C^{2}{\bf s}(1-{\bf s}){\bf d}^{-1}

\displaystyle=C{\bf s}(1-{\bf s})\left(1+2({\bf s}(1-{\bf s}))^{1/2}\right){\bf d}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\leq{\bf s}\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})}{4}C{\bf d}^{-1/2}.

Here the last line follows by finding a supremum of the function
f({\bf s})=(1-{\bf s})\left(1+2({\bf s}(1-{\bf s}))^{1/2}\right)
on the interval
(0,1)
.

Note that
e(G(n,p))\sim\textrm{Bin}\left({n\choose 2},p\right)
,
\mathbb{E}[e(G(n,p))]=\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}-\frac{{\bf d}}{2}
thus, by the concentration of a binomial random variable, we may write that with high probability
e(G(n,p))=\left(1+o(1)\right)\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}
. Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be any vertex partition of
V(G(n,p))
. Recall that
C=1.999
. By (
3
) we get that with high probability

\displaystyle\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}}(G(n,p))

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\frac{4e(S)e(\bar{S})-e(S,\bar{S})^{2}}{4e(G(n,p))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})1.999}{4}{\bf d}^{-1/2}\sum_{S\in{\mathscr{A}}}\frac{|S|}{n}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})1.999}{4}{\bf d}^{-1/2}.

Since the above formula is valid for any vertex partition
{\mathscr{A}}
of
V(G(n,p))
, it is in particular true for a vertex partition
{\mathscr{A}}_{opt}
realizing the modularity of
G(n,p)
. Thus with high probability

\modu(G(n,p))=\modu_{{\mathscr{A}}_{opt}}(G(n,p))\leq\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}.

∎

4.
Bounds on
\mathbf{e(S)}
,
\mathbf{e(\bar{S})}
, and
\mathbf{e(S,\bar{S})}
– proof of Lemma
12

In this section we derive the bounds on
e(S)
,
e(\bar{S})
, and
e(S,\bar{S})
presented in Lemma
12
. The proof is based on quite basic tools, mainly on Chernoff’s inequality. We state it as it appears in Theorem 2.1 of
[
11
]
by Janson, Łuczak, and Ruciński.

Lemma 13

(Chernoff’s inequality)
.

Assume that
X
follows a binomial distribution with the expected value
\mu
. Let also
\varphi(y)=(1+y)\ln{(1+y)-y}
for

5.
Lower bound on the modularity of
\mathbf{G(n,p)}
– proof of Theorem

To get the lower bound on
\modu(G(n,p))
we take advantage of the estimate of the minimum bisection of
G(n,p)
from
[
9
]
. There one finds also an analogous estimate for a random regular graph. The latter was used by McDiarmid and Skerman to get the lower bound on modularity of random regular graphs in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 16

(Theorem 1.2 of
[
9
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a constant, and let
G
be a random graph chosen uniformly at random from all graphs with the vertex set
[n]
and with
\lfloor cn\rfloor
edges. Then with high probability, for large
c

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V(G)\\
|S|-|\bar{S}|\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}e(S,\bar{S})=\left(\frac{c}{2}-P_{*}\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}}+o_{c}(\sqrt{c})\right)n,

where
P_{*}=0.76321\pm 0.00003
and
o_{c}(\cdot)
relates to
c\to\infty
.

It is mentioned in
[
9
]
that the above result implies an analogue for a random graph
G(n,p)
. For completeness, we formulate it as a corollary and briefly sketch the justification.

Corollary 17
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\bf d}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
p=p(n)={\bf d}/n
, and
G(n,p)
be a binomial random graph.
Then with high probability, for large
{\bf d}

(16)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V(G(n,p))\\
|S|-|\bar{S}|\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}e(S,\bar{S})=\left(\frac{{\bf d}}{4}-P_{*}\frac{\sqrt{{\bf d}}}{2}+o_{{\bf d}}(\sqrt{{\bf d}})\right)n,

where
P_{*}=0.76321\pm 0.00003
and
o_{{\bf d}}(\cdot)
relates to
{\bf d}\to\infty
.

Sketch of the proof.

First, recall that
e(G(n,p))\sim\textrm{Bin}\left(\binom{n}{2},p\right)
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[e(G(n,p))]=\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}-\frac{{\bf d}}{2}=(1+o(1))\frac{n{\bf d}}{2}
. By the concentration of the binomial random variable we get that with high probability

(17)

|e(G(n,p))-n{\bf d}/2|\leq\omega\sqrt{n{\bf d}}.

Now, let
c\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a constant and
\omega
a function tending arbitrarily slowly to infinity. Under the condition
e(G(n,p))=\lfloor cn\rfloor
, the graph
G(n,p)
is uniformly distributed over all graphs with vertex set
[n]
and exactly
\lfloor cn\rfloor
edges. Apply Theorem
16
for all values of
\lfloor cn\rfloor
such that
c_{-}\leq c\leq c_{+}
, where
c_{\pm}={\bf d}/2\pm\omega\sqrt{{\bf d}/n}
. Finally, use a union bound over all possible values for the number of edges in
G(n,p)
and apply (
17
) to get the conclusion. For a more detailed analysis, we refer the reader to Section 1.4 in
[
11
]
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\bf d}\geq 1
,

, and

be a binomial random graph. Let also

. Then with high probability for all

Let
