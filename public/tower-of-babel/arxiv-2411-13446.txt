Linearization of quasistatic fracture evolution in brittle materials
Manuel Friedrich, Pascal Steinke, Kerrek Stinson

Abstract
. We prove a linearization result for quasistatic fracture evolution in nonlinear elasticity. As the stiffness of the material tends to infinity, we show that rescaled displacement fields and their associated crack sets converge to a solution of quasistatic crack growth in linear elasticity without any a priori assumptions on the geometry of the crack set. This result corresponds to the evolutionary counterpart of the static linearization result [ Fri20 ] , where a Griffith model for nonsimple brittle materials has been considered featuring an elastic energy which also depends suitably on the second gradient of the deformations. The proof relies on a careful study of unilateral global minimality, as determined by the nonlinear evolutionary problem, and its linearization together with a variant of the jump transfer lemma in GSBD \mathrm{GSBD} [ FS18 ] .

1.
Introduction

A crucial question for nonlinear models in materials science consists in establishing the range of validity of suitably linearized approximations. Indeed, large strain models are often challenging to treat, both analytically and numerically, due to their nonconvex behavior whereas linearized models are significantly easier and, in many situations, still allow to predict accurately observed phenomena. The last decades have witnessed remarkable progress in providing rigorous results relating models with different strain regimes in terms of
\Gamma
-convergence
[
Dal93
]
. After the seminal work on elastostatics
[
DNP02
]
, this question was explored towards various directions, among others, for incompressible materials models
[
JS21
,
MP22
]
, atomistic settings
[
BSV07
,
Sch09
]
, residually stressed materials
[
PT11
]
, problems without Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
MM21
]
, homogenization
[
JS14
,
MN11
]
, brittle fracture
[
Fri17
,
Fri20
,
ADF23
]
, plasticity
[
MSZ14
]
, or multiwell energies allowing for phase transformations
[
Ali+18
,
DF22
,
ARS23
,
Sch08
]
. Beyond the static framework, we refer to evolutionary results on elastodynamics
[
AMM11
]
, viscoelasticity
[
BFK23
,
FK18
,
Oos24
]
, or plasticity
[
MS13
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to prove a linearization result for the quasistatic evolution of brittle fracture.
We consider a variational model for fracture introduced by
Francfort and Marigo

[
FM98
]
based on Griffith’s idea that
crack formation and propagation is the result of the competition between an elastic bulk energy and a surface energy proportional to the size of the crack. Our goal is to show that, in dimension two, crack growth in nonlinear elasticity can be rigorously approximated by crack growth in linear elasticity.

Moving within the frame of variational models for fracture, we start by introducing the underlying energies. For a reference domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we consider nonlinear energies of the form

(1.1)

\energyNonlinTot[y,{\Gamma}]:=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(W\left(\nabla y\right)+\varepsilon^{2\left(1-\beta\right)}|\nabla^{2}y|^{2}\right)\differential{x}+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left({\Gamma}\right),

where
y\in W^{2,2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega\setminus{\Gamma};\mathbb{R}^{2})

is the deformation,
W
is a frame indifferent stored energy density,
\beta\in\left(0,1\right)
,
\Gamma\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
is the crack set (on which
y
is allowed to be discontinuous),
\kappa
denotes the fracture toughness, and
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is the Hausdorff measure penalizing the length of the crack. The prefactor
1/\varepsilon^{2}
represents a high stiffness of the material depending on a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
. For technical reasons discussed below, we consider an energy in the frame of
nonsimple materials
, referring to the fact that the elastic energy depends additionally on the
second gradient of the deformation. However, as the contribution of the Hessian vanishes in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the effective energy is described by a linear Griffith fracture energy without second gradients. Precisely, using
\Gamma
-convergence, in
[
Fri20
]
the first author showed that the above energies converge as
\varepsilon\to 0
to their linearized counterpart

(1.2)

\energyLinTot[u,\Gamma]:=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}e\left(u\right):e\left(u\right)\differential{x}+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma\right),

where
u\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma;\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the displacement approximated by
u\approx(y-{\rm id})/\varepsilon
(
{\rm id}
is the identity mapping) and
\mathbb{C}=\mathrm{D}^{2}W\left({\rm Id}\right)
is a fourth-order elasticity tensor. Whereas the models (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) correspond to the so-called
strong formulation
, the result in
[
Fri20
]
is actually formulated in the weak setting of the functions spaces
\mathrm{GSBV}
(for (
1.1
)) and
\mathrm{GSBD}
(for (
1.2
)).

In this work, our goal is to enhance the understanding of the relation between the models (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) by showing that solutions of quasistatic crack growth associated with the nonlinear energies converge to ones related to the linear energy, see our main result in Theorem
2.2
. This provides a rigorous justification for the fact that crack propagation in stiff materials (described in terms of
1/\varepsilon^{2}
) is well approximated by using a linear theory. We also point out that the energetic rescaling in the energy (
1.1
) may be found by looking at the nonlinear fracture energy (
\varepsilon=1
) on progressively larger domains
\Omega/\varepsilon
, referred to as Bažant’s law
[
NT15
]
.

Linearization for variational fracture evolutions has already received some attention in the literature.
Negri and Zanini

[
NZ14
]
showed that the nonlinear quasistatic fracture evolution converges to the linear counterpart when the path of the crack is restricted to a line segment. Subsequent work by
Negri and Toader

[
NT15
]
showed that this convergence still holds under a weaker assumption on the cracks (effectively, the crack is made of finitely many non-intersecting regular arcs). Our conclusions significantly improve these results: we show convergence in the passage from nonlinear-to-linearized models with
no assumptions
on the geometry of the crack.

The study of quasistatic crack evolutions was initiated in
[
DT02
]
for a
2d
-model with restrictive assumptions on the crack topology. A breakthrough existence result in
\mathrm{SBV}
is due to
Francfort and Larsen

[
FL03
]
in the simplified setting of anti-planar displacements (meaning scalar displacements with control on the full gradient). Subsequently, this result was generalized to nonlinear elasticity
[
DFT05
]
, including the setting of non-interpenetration
[
DL10
]
.
In all these works, the quasistatic fracture evolution is described in terms of
i)
an irreversible crack path,
ii)
unilateral global minimality of the deformation/displacement, and
iii)
an energy-balance equation.
Essential to the verification of
ii)
is the so-called
jump transfer lemma
, allowing the authors to carry over unilateral minimality for approximate free discontinuity problems to their limits, where ‘unilateral’ refers to the irreversible nature of the process.

Surprisingly, despite its paramount relevance for realistic engineering applications, the theory of linear fracture models has been considerably less developed. This is due to technical difficulties inherent to the presence of symmetrized gradients. Indeed, due to the unknown and potentially rough discontinuity sets and the lack of Korn’s inequality, there is no control on the skew symmetric part
(\nabla u)^{\top}-\nabla u
of the strain, leading to an analytically more intricate formulation in the larger space of special functions of bounded deformation (
\mathrm{SBD}
)
[
ACD97
]
. Only recently, departing from
Dal Maso
’s seminal paper
[
Dal13
]
on the generalized space
\mathrm{GSBD}
, an enormous amount of effort has been invested to develop the theory. Technical advances regarding functional inequalities
[
CCS22
,
CCF16
,
Fri18
]
, compactness theorems
[
AT23
,
CC21
]
, and lower semicontinuity
[
FPS21
,
EKM24
]
have allowed a variety of authors to prove existence of Griffith energy minimizers in the strong formulation
[
CFI19
,
CCI19
,
CC19
]
with higher regularity
[
BIL22
,
LL23
,
FLS23
]
, or existence of minimizers in anisotropic and heterogeneous settings
[
CC23a
,
FLS24
]
.

Most important for our work is the existence proof of a solution to the quasistatic evolution for cracks in dimension two
[
FS18
]
. In the linear case, it was essential to develop another jump transfer lemma suited for settings where only control on the symmetric gradient is available. Using a variant of this jump transfer lemma is at the core of our approach dealing with the passage from nonlinear to linearized theory.

We proceed by describing the ingredients of the proof in more detail. To see that solutions of the nonlinear problem associated with the energy
\energyNonlinTot
converge to a solution of linear quasistatic crack growth with energy
\energyLinTot
, we prove
i)
irreversibility,
ii)
minimality, and
iii)
the energy balance for the limit displacement and crack. First, to identify a limiting displacement we need to ensure that deformation gradients are sufficiently close to the identity. Given that the domain can fracture into multiple pieces, we are forced to identify the limit up to modification, which essentially transforms the deformation back to the identity by a piecwise rigid motion related to a Caccioppoli partition of the reference domain. This technique has been employed already in a variety of works, both in a nonlinear
[
Fri17
,
Fri20
]
and linearized setting
[
CC23
,
FS18
,
SW24
]
. The proof of
i)
then follows by design.

The proof of
ii)
is significantly more involved.
Beyond the jump transfer lemma, carrying minimality from nonlinear to linear problems requires a delicate look at the linearization procedure.
Formally, given
(y_{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{\varepsilon})
minimizing
\energyNonlinTot
, we can define a limit displacement
u=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(y_{\varepsilon}-{\rm id})/\varepsilon
and crack
\Gamma=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
. To show that minimality is inherited by the limit, we want to show that
\energyLinTot[u,\Gamma]\leq\energyLinTot[u+\phi,\Gamma\cup\Gamma_{\phi}]
for all test pairs
(\phi,\Gamma_{\phi})
. For this, we use a Taylor expansion to linearize the nonlinear minimality condition
\energyNonlinTot[y_{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{\varepsilon}]\leq\energyNonlinTot[y_{\varepsilon}+\phi_{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}]
for test pairs
(\phi_{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{\phi_{\varepsilon}})
approximating a fixed
(\phi,\Gamma_{\phi})
. The challenge is that, within this expansion, we must carefully control the error on the right-hand side to ensure it disappears in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The key ingredients are a quantitative description of the almost-minimality of the rescaled displacement
(y_{\varepsilon}-{\rm id})/\varepsilon
, see Lemma
5.1
, the jump transfer lemma of
[
FS18
]
, and a density result for
\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}
functions with prescribed boundary values, see
Theorem

3.2
.

The proof of the energy balance
iii)
follows the by now classical idea which relies on global minimality and a Riemann sum argument. Yet, in our setting the expression representing the work of the external loads is more intricate if there are regions determined by the abovementioned Caccioppoli partition where the deformation is not close to the identity but to a different rigid motion. In this case, we will show that this piece is (almost) relaxed to an equilibrium configuration and thus no longer contributes to the change of the total energy. Rigorously, this is done by using our analysis from
ii)
to derive an approximate variational inequality that can pass to the limit in the time-integrated energy-balance equation, see
Corollary

5.3
and
Lemma

6.1
.

As a technical point on our result, we introduce a time step parameter
\tau>0
(later encoded as
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
in equation (
2.1
)) and a ‘stiffness’ parameter
\varepsilon>0
. For
\varepsilon
fixed, we use
\tau
to construct a time-discretized approximate solution to the nonlinear quasistatic crack growth. Then, we pass to the limit jointly in
\tau\to 0
and
\varepsilon\to 0
to derive a solution of linear quasistatic crack growth. This means that at no point we actually use a true solution in the nonlinear setting. (Note that existence of such is not guaranteed by
[
DFT05
]
due to the presence of the second-gradient term in (
1.1
).) Importantly, however, in our analysis, there is no constraint on the relation between
\tau
and
\varepsilon
. Thus, one can expect that we can pass to the limit as
\tau\to 0
first and then
\varepsilon\to 0
. Although we believe that this would in general be possible, the rigorous execution of this procedure is beyond the scope of this paper.

Our result comes with three reasonable caveats: First, to isolate a rotation about which we may linearize, we require the nonlinear energy to be nonsimple and penalize the Hessian of the deformation. Yet, we emphasize that this penalization is small and vanishes in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
(see
Corollary

5.5
). A possible alternative would be to modify the geometric rigidity estimate by
Friesecke et al.

[
FJM02
]
, but this is intricate in this setting as the domain regularity is determined by the a priori irregular crack set. Existing applications using
[
FJM02
]
for fracture require fixed crack geometries
[
NT15
]
, a curvature penalization of the crack
[
FKZ24
]
, or an involved modification procedure of deformations and crack sets on multiple scales
[
Fri17
]
. In particular, the latter modification is incompatible with the irreversibility condition
i)
.

Second, our result is restricted to the plane. This is essentially because the piecewise Korn inequality
[
Fri18
]
has only been proven in the plane. We use this inequality in two places: the jump transfer lemma and a density result with Dirichlet conditions. With more work, we believe that it is possible to extend the density result to higher dimensions as the role of the piecewise Korn inequality therein is to approximate
\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}
by
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-functions. On the other hand, extending the jump transfer lemma to higher dimensions will require fundamentally new ideas.

Third, our model does not account for non-interpenetration and, in particular, for simplicity we require
W
to be locally Lipschitz, preventing the blow-up of
W(M)
as
{\rm det}\,M\to 0
. The necessary techniques to implement non-interpenetration in the framework of crack growth in finite elasticity are already available
[
DL10
]
(based on
[
FM06
]
) and, in the static case, also the linearization has recently been studied
[
ADF23
]
. The extension to the evolutionary setting will require nontrivial adaptations and is a subject of future research.

Section

2
, we introduce the model and formulate the main result.
Section

3
is subject to some preliminaries, namely compactness and density results in
\mathrm{GSBD}
and the jump transfer lemma from
[

]
. In

Section

5
and
Section

are devoted to the stability of minimality and an approximate energy balance as

.
Section

2.
Setting and main results

In this section, we first provide some basic notation. Then, we introduce the nonlinear quasistatic time discretized evolution for crack growth using a time discrete scheme. Next, we define a notion of solution for quasistatic crack growth in linear elasticity. Eventually, we state our main convergence result relating the nonlinear to the linear setting.

2.2.
Nonlinear quasistatic fracture evolution

We begin by constructing a time discretized evolution for a nonlinear model based on the energy (
1.1
), see e.g.
[
DFT05
]
or
[
FL03
]
. Let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i}
be an arbitrary sequence with
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
. For convenience, with an abuse of notation, we will simply use
\varepsilon
in place of
\varepsilon_{i}
and write
\varepsilon\to 0
. Taking our time interval to be
[0,1]
without loss of generality, we partition the time interval using the collection of points

I_{\varepsilon}:=(t_{i}^{\varepsilon})_{i=0}^{n(\varepsilon)}=\left\{0=t_{0}^{\varepsilon}<t_{1}^{\varepsilon}<\cdots<t_{n(\varepsilon)}^{\varepsilon}=1\right\}.

We impose that the partitions created by
I_{\varepsilon}
are nested, i.e.,
I_{\varepsilon}\subseteq I_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
when
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
, and that they refine, in the sense that

(2.1)

\Delta_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq n(\varepsilon)}t_{i}^{\varepsilon}-t_{i-1}^{\varepsilon}

vanishes as
\varepsilon
tends to zero. Note that the union of the partitions
I_{\infty}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\varepsilon}I_{\varepsilon}
is a countable dense subset of
[0,1]
. We also introduce the shorthand notations

I_{\varepsilon}^{t}\coloneqq\left\{\tau\in I_{\varepsilon}\colon\ \tau\leq t\right\}\text{ and }I_{\infty}^{t}\coloneqq\left\{\tau\in I_{\infty}\colon\ \tau\leq t\right\}.

We let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. As is typically done for fracture problems, we impose displacement boundary conditions by considering a larger set
\Omega^{\prime}
containing
\Omega
: we fix a boundary datum

(2.2)

h\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}\big((0,1){;}\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2})\big)\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\big((0,1){;}\mathrm{W}^{2,\infty}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2})\big),

and define

(2.3)

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\big\{y\in\mathrm{GSBV}_{2}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2})\,\colon\,y=\mathrm{id}+\varepsilon h_{n}^{\varepsilon}\text{ on }\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\big\},

where
h_{n}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq h(t_{n}^{\varepsilon})
. We assume that also
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
are Lipschitz sets.

Following
[
Fri20
, Sct. 2.1]
, we define the energy

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{y}),&if $J_{\nabla y}\subseteq J_{y}$,\\
\infty,&else\end{cases}

for
y\in\mathrm{GSBV}_{2}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and some
\beta\in(2/3,1)
,
\kappa>0
. This corresponds to the weak formulation of the energy introduced in equation (
1.1
). Here,
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies

(W1)

W
is locally Lipschitz continuous with

\absolutevalue{ W ( A ) - W ( B ) }\leq C(1+\max\{\absolutevalue{A},\absolutevalue{ B}\}){|A-B|}

for some
C>0
, and there is

such that

is
\mathrm{C}^{3}
in the neighborhood

of

.

(W2)

W(RF)=W(F)
for all

.

(W3)

W(F)\geq c\mathrm{dist}^{2}(F,\mathrm{SO}(2))

,
W(F)=0
if and only if

.

Note that the local Lipschitz continuity in
(W1)
is a consequence of the common assumption
\absolutevalue{ \diff W ( A ) }\lesssim(1+\absolutevalue{ A })
. We emphasize that

excludes

Considering a continuous function

on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that is

{y_{\eta}:=y+\eta\,{\rm id}}\chi_{\{x_{2}>0\}}

2.4

(2.5)

\energyNonlin(y)\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{y}\cup J_{\nabla y}),

see
[
Fri20
, Prop. 2.1]
. Starting from initial conditions
y_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
with bounded energy, i.e.,

(2.6)

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\energyNonlin(y_{0}^{\varepsilon})<\infty,

assuming that
y_{k}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\varepsilon}
for

have already been found, we iteratively define
y_{n}^{\varepsilon}
as a minimizer of the energy

(2.7)

\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{y}\cup J_{\nabla y}\right)\setminus\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(J_{y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)

over

\Omega

\Omega^{\prime}
due to the boundary conditions, but the jump sets are always taken relative to

The existence of a minimizer for the problem (

[

Lastly, we define the

\varepsilon>0
as the piecewise constant interpolation of the minimizers, namely

(2.8)

y_{\varepsilon}(t)=y_{n}^{\varepsilon}\text{ for }t\in[t_{n}^{\varepsilon},t_{n+1}^{\varepsilon})\quad\text{ for each $0\leq n\leq n(\varepsilon)-1$, }\quad y_{\varepsilon}(1)=y_{n(\varepsilon)}^{\varepsilon}.

Note that, if

denotes the piecewise constant interpolation of

with respect to

, which means

then

satisfies the boundary condition

Lastly, we introduce the shorthand notation

(2.9)

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in I_{\varepsilon}^{t}\end{subarray}}J_{y_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}

for the total crack at time

Note then that, as a consequence of the minimization problem (

), for all

,

is a minimizer of the functional

among all

with

on

.

2.3.
Linear quasistatic fracture evolution

We introduce the notation

(2.11)

\mathbb{C}\coloneqq\mathrm{D}^{2}W(\mathrm{Id})\quad\text{ and }\quad Q(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{C}A\colon A,

and note that
\mathbb{C}
is a symmetric positive-definite fourth-order tensor by
(W2)
and
(W3)
. We define the linearized energy as

(2.12)

\energyLin(u)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(u))\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{u}).

As shown in
[
Fri20
]
,
\energyLin
is the
\Gamma
-limit of
\energyNonlin
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for a suitable notion of convergence of the deformations. Based on this energy, we now introduce the notion of a quasistatic fracture evolution in the setting of linear elasticity, see also
[
FS18
, Thm. 3.1]
. As before, we take the time interval to be
[0,1]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
h
be as in (
2.2
) and let
u_{0}\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
with
u_{0}=h(0)
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. We say that displacements
t\mapsto u(t)\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
and cracks
t\mapsto\Gamma(t)\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
are a (linear) quasistatic fracture evolution with the boundary condition
h
and initial condition
u_{0}
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

(Initial condition).
The displacement
u(0)=u_{0}
minimizes (
2.12
) among all
v\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
with
v=h(0)
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and
\Gamma(0)=J_{u(0)}
.

(ii)

(Displacement and boundary conditions).

The displacement satisfies
e(u)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({(0,1)};\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\rm sym}))
, and at each time it holds that
J_{u(t)}\subseteq\Gamma(t)
up to an
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-null set and
u(t)=h(t)
in
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
.

(iii)

(Irreversibility).
It holds that
\Gamma(t)\subseteq\Gamma(t^{\prime})
if
t\leq t^{\prime}
.

(iv)

(Minimality).
The displacement is a global minimizer of the elastic energy given the existing crack, in the sense that

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}e(u(t)):e(u(t))\differential{x}\leq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}e(v):e(v)\differential{x}+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{v}\setminus\Gamma(t))

for all
v\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
with
v=h(t)
in
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

(v)

(Energy balance).
The change in energy is equal to the work done on the system as captured by

\displaystyle\energyLinTot(t)

\displaystyle=\energyLinTot(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}e(u(s)):e(\partial_{t}h(s))\differential{x}\differential{s},

where by

(2.13)

\displaystyle\energyLinTot(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u(t))\big)\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma(t)\right)

we denote the total energy at time
t\in[0,1]
.

We remark that existence of linear quasistatic fracture evolutions for the Griffith energy has been shown in
[
FS18
]
by means of time-discrete approximations (analogous to (
2.7
)).

2.4.
Main result

To describe the convergence of the deformations
y_{\varepsilon}
to a displacement
u
, we need to introduce a proper type of convergence. Typically, in linearization results, one considers the asymptotic behavior of the rescaled displacements
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(y_{\varepsilon}-{\rm id})/\varepsilon.
However, as the material may fracture, it is not possible to linearize around the single rigid motion given by
{\rm id}
as pieces may be broken off. On these pieces, compactness cannot be expected and we essentially have no information except for the fact that the elastic energy should vanish asymptotically due to the minimality of

in the sense of (

).

Let us now describe the convergence towards a limit

). This set represents the ‘broken off pieces,’ and by

Theorem 2.2

(Linearization of quasistatic fracture evolution)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

Then, there exists a linear quasistatic fracture evolution as in Definition

and, up to a subsequence in
\varepsilon
(not relabeled),
y_{\varepsilon}\rightsquigarrow u
.

Moreover, denoting the total energy along the evolution

by

(2.16)

\displaystyle\energyNonlinTot(t):=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t))\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_\eps(t) }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)\right),

(2.17)

\displaystyle\energyNonlinTot(t)\to\energyLinTot(t)\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,1]$}.

Remark 2.3
.

We make the following remarks regarding the main result.

(i)

The existence of well-prepared initial conditions is a consequence of the
\Gamma
-convergence of
\energyNonlin
to
\energyLin
, see
[

, Thm. 2.7]
.

(ii)

More precisely, convergence (

) can be refined to separate convergence of elastic and crack energies, see (

) for details.

(iii)

If we accept that we modify the sequence

to account for the broken off pieces, then we can actually show a much stronger convergence than

. This modification procedure is however rather involved and is done in equations (

), (
4.12
), (

), and (

). The resulting improved convergence is then seen in Item

and
Remark

.

The proof of this result will be our focus for the rest of the paper and it is split into manageable pieces.
In Section
3
, we introduce a variety of tools that will be essential to our analysis.
In Section
4
, we address compactness of the sequence

and its rescaled displacements

to find a candidate displacement

that might be a linear quasistatic fracture evolution in the sense of Definition

. In Section

, we prove minimality for the limit displacement

at times

. Likewise, in Section

We complete the proof of Theorem

in Section

. Precisely, noting that

is an admissible competitor for the minimization problem (

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{n}^{\varepsilon})\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ n }^{ \varepsilon} }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{y_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\setminus\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(J_{y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\nabla(h_{n}^{\varepsilon}-h_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}))\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} + \varepsilon\nabla^{ 2 } ( h_{ n }^{ \varepsilon} - h_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} ) }^{2}\differential{ x }.

Take

in

We want to apply Taylor’s formula to the first summand on the right-hand side of (

\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{n-1}

First we take care of the integral over

By the local Lipschitz estimate for

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)+C\left(1+\absolutevalue{ \nabla y_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} }+\varepsilon\norm{ \nabla\left( h_{n }^{ \varepsilon} - h_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} \right) }_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\right)\varepsilon\absolutevalue{ \nabla\left( h_{n }^{ \varepsilon} - h_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} \right) }\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+C\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}\left(1+\absolutevalue{ \nabla y_{n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} }\right)^{2}\differential{ x }\right)^{1/2}\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)}\differential{ t }

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+C\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }\right)^{1/2}\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)}\differential{ t }

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+C\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)}\differential{ t }.

In the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}
, we apply Taylor’s formula, the differentiability in time of

, and Minkowski’s integral inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\nabla(h_{n}^{\varepsilon}-h_{n-1}^{\varepsilon})\right)\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}{\langle\mathrm{D}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right),\nabla\partial_{t}h\rangle}\differential{ x }\differential{ t }+C\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla\left( h_{ n }^{ \varepsilon} - h_{ n -1 }^{ \varepsilon} \right) }^{2}\differential{ x }

(4.7)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}{\langle\mathrm{D}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right),\nabla\partial_{t}h\rangle}\differential{ x }\differential{ t }+C\left(\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\differential{ t }\right)^{2}.

By expanding the square in the integral involving the second derivatives, we therefore deduce by using the estimates (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), and again the differentiability in time of

, that the right-hand side of inequality (
4.5
) is controlled by

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ n - 1 }^{ \varepsilon} }^{2}\differential{ x }+{}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}{\langle\mathrm{D}W\left(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\right),\nabla\partial_{t}h\rangle}\differential{ x }\differential{ t }

\displaystyle+C\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\right)}\differential{ t }+\dfrac{2}{\varepsilon^{2\beta-1}}\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left\langle\nabla^{2}y_{\varepsilon}\,,\,\nabla^{2}\partial_{t}h\right\rangle\differential{ x }\differential{ t }+{}C\left(\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\norm{ \nabla\partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\differential{ t }\right)^{2}

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta-2}}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\norm{ \nabla^{ 2 } \partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\differential{ t }\right)^{2}

where we also employed the notation in (
2.8
).
Noting that the first two terms in (

) are the same as the first two terms on the left-hand side of (

) with

in place of

, we will iterate the above reasoning to derive the a priori estimate.
Starting from the energy

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{n}^{\varepsilon})\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ n }^{ \varepsilon} }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}\left(J_{y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right),

we iteratively apply inequality (
4.5
), together with the fact that at every time step the right-hand side of inequality (
4.5
) is controlled by the term (
4.8
), to deduce

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{n}^{\varepsilon})\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ n }^{ \varepsilon} }^{2}\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}\left(J_{y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\energyNonlin(y_{0}^{\varepsilon})+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\in B_{r}(\mathrm{SO}(2))\}}{\langle\mathrm{D}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}),\nabla\partial_{t}h\rangle}\differential{ x }\differential{ t }

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{1}\left(\norm{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\notin B_{r}(\mathrm{SO}(2))\}\right)}+\omega(\Delta_{\varepsilon})\left(\norm{ \nabla\partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta-2}}\norm{ \nabla^{ 2 } \partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\right)\right)\differential{ t },

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{2}{\varepsilon^{2\beta-1}}\absolutevalue{ \inner*{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ \varepsilon} }{ \nabla^{ 2 } \partial_t h } }\differential{ x }\differential{ t },

where
\energyNonlin
is defined in (
2.5
) and

vanishes as

By the energy estimate (

) and

, the measure of

vanishes uniformly in

since

\mathcal{L}^{2}\big(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}\big)\leq\dfrac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}\left(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon},\mathrm{SO}(2)\right)\differential{ x }\lesssim\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{n-1}^{\varepsilon})\differential{ x }\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality to the last summand, using the uniform bound on the data (
2.2
) and energy (
4.4
),

, and that the measure of

vanishes uniformly in time by inequality (

), the last two lines of the estimate (

) vanish as

tends to zero, thus yielding

independent of

such that (
4.2
) holds.

To show the uniform bound (
4.1
), we are left with arguing that the last term of inequality (

) stays uniformly bounded.
Denote by
\pi
the (possibly not unique) projection onto
\mathrm{SO}(2)
. Since

is Lipschitz in a neighborhood of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
and
\mathrm{D}W(\pi\nabla y_{\varepsilon})=0
, we deduce that, if

, then

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \langle\diff W ( \nabla y_{ \varepsilon} ), \nabla\partial_t h \rangle}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{ \langle\diff W ( \nabla y_{ \varepsilon} ) - \diff W ( \pi\nabla y_{ \varepsilon} ) , \nabla\partial_t h \rangle}\lesssim\absolutevalue{ \nabla y_{ \varepsilon} - \pi\nabla y_{ \varepsilon} }\absolutevalue{\nabla\partial_t h }

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}\left(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},\mathrm{SO}(2)\right)\absolutevalue{ \nabla\partial_t h }\lesssim\sqrt{W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon})}\absolutevalue{ \nabla\partial_t h },

where the last inequality is due to the lower growth bound

.
Applying the previous inequality and Hölder’s inequality to the last term on the right-hand side of inequality (

{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{{\{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}\in B_{r}(\mathrm{SO}(2))\}}}\left\langle\mathrm{D}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon})\,,\,\nabla\partial_{t}h\right\rangle\differential{ x }\differential{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon})\differential{ x }\right)^{1/2}\norm{ \nabla\partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\differential{ t }.

By the control on the elastic energy (
4.4
) we get that this term stays uniformly bounded. Inserting this information back into inequality (

).
∎

4.2.
From deformations to rescaled displacements

In this subsection, we define rescaled displacements that are sufficiently close to a rotation everywhere in
{\Omega^{\prime}}.

Following
[
Fri20
]
, this requires the introduction of a partition of
{\Omega^{\prime}}
, where on each piece
y_{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently close to a rigid motion. Beyond accounting for rigidity, we will need to carefully zero-out the displacement in a vanishingly small region in
\Omega
. This will later ensure that minimality is stable when passing from deformations to displacements as
\varepsilon\to 0
, see
Section

5
.

Choosing
\gamma\in(2/3,\beta)
, for any time
t\in[0,1]
, we apply the reasoning in
[
Fri20
, Thm. 2.3]
to the sequence
(y_{\varepsilon}(t))_{\varepsilon}
to obtain the modified sequence

(4.11)

y_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{rot}}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}R_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)y_{\varepsilon}(t)\chi_{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)}\in\mathrm{GSBV}_{2}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for a Caccioppoli partition
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}(t):=(P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t))_{j}
of
\Omega^{\prime}
and
(R_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t))_{j}\subseteq\mathrm{SO}(2)
, such that
R_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathrm{Id}
on all elements
P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)
intersecting the boundary in the sense of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)\cap(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}))>0
. We point out that
R_{j}^{\varepsilon}
and the Caccioppoli partition
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
are piecewise constant in time on the intervals determined by
I_{\varepsilon}
(as in (
2.8
)). (We frequently omit
(t)
if no confusion arises.) As many of the properties of this modified sequence are determined solely by the uniform energy bound in (
4.1
), it holds uniformly for all
t\in[0,1]
that

(R1)

y_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{rot}}=\mathrm{id}+\varepsilon h_{\varepsilon}
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
,

(R2)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{rot}}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{rot}}}\right)\setminus\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}\right)\big)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\Big(\Big(\Omega^{\prime}\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\partial^{\ast}P_{j}^{\varepsilon}\Big)\setminus J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}\Big)\lesssim\varepsilon^{\beta-\gamma}
,

(R3)

\norm{ e( y_{ \varepsilon}^{ \mathrm{rot} } ) - \mathrm{Id} }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon
,

(R4)

\norm{ \nabla y_{ \varepsilon}^{ \mathrm{rot} } - \mathrm{Id}}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\gamma}
, and

(R5)

\absolutevalue{ \nabla y_{ \varepsilon}^{ \mathrm{rot} } - \mathrm{Id} }\leq\max\left\{C\varepsilon^{\gamma},2\mathrm{dist}\left(\nabla y_{\varepsilon},\mathrm{SO}(2)\right)\right\}
pointwise in
\Omega^{\prime}
for fixed
C>0
.

The last item is a consequence of
[
Fri20
, Eq. (4.11)]
.
As a result of these properties, the rescaling

(4.12)

u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(y_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{rot}}(t)-\mathrm{id}\right)\in\mathrm{GSBV}_{2}^{2}\left(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)

has boundary value
h_{\varepsilon}
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. However, this will only be an auxiliary object since for the sequence we are actually interested in we will have to multiply the function by a suitable cutoff, see the comments below and
Lemma

4.3
. As a consequence of items
(R2)
–
(R3)
and the a priori estimate (
4.1
), the function
u_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{aux}}
satisfies

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big(\norm{ e( u^{ \mathrm{aux}}_{ \varepsilon}(t)) }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}(t)})\Big)<\infty

and

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\big(J_{u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\right)\setminus\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}\right)\big)\lesssim\varepsilon^{\beta-\gamma}.

For the linearization results later, we will need to apply a Taylor expansion of
W
at the identity with respect to the direction
\varepsilon\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}
.
In order to make sure that the error does not explode, we introduce a cutoff function that cuts out the vanishingly small region where
\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}
is too big. Whereas this procedure is by now standard in the derivation of linearized models, see for example
[
DNP02
]
,
[
FJM02
]
,
[
Sch08
]
,
[
BSV07
]
, and
[
Fri20
]
, in our setting it is important that the removed region is not only small in volume but
also controlled in surface
which requires refined arguments. The cutoff function is precisely introduced in the lemma below and is based on ideas used by the
first author in
[
Fri20
, Thm. 2.7]
. We use the notation
u(x,t)
for
u(t)
evaluated at
x
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists a collection of piecewise constant functions
\eta_{\varepsilon}\colon[0,1]\to(0,\infty)
, indexed by
\varepsilon>0,
associated characteristic functions
\chi_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)}
with

\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\left\{x\in\Omega^{\prime}\,\colon\,|\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)|_{\infty}<\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\},

where
|A|_{\infty}:=\max_{ij}\absolutevalue{ A_{ i j } }
is the infinity norm of a given matrix, with the following properties holding uniformly for
t\in[0,1]
:

(
\omega
1)

\chi_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)}\to 1
in measure (or
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
),

(
\omega
2)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}(t)})\to 0
,

(
\omega
3)

\varepsilon\eta_{\varepsilon}^{3}(t)\to 0
, and

(
\omega
4)

\varepsilon^{1-\gamma}\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let the parameters
2/3<\gamma<\beta<1
be given and define the sequences

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}\coloneqq\varepsilon^{(9\gamma-10)/12}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}\coloneqq\varepsilon^{(\gamma-2)/4}.

We note that
-1/3<(\gamma-2)/4<(9\gamma-10)/12<\gamma-1
. In particular,
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}<\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}
for
\varepsilon<1
.
If
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in[\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-},\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}]
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in[0,1]
, then properties
(

ω

3)
and
(

ω

4)
will hold, along with

(4.15)

\varepsilon^{\beta-1}/\left(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}\right)\to 0.

Indeed, for
(

ω

3)
, we estimate

\varepsilon\eta_{\varepsilon}^{3}\leq\varepsilon^{1+(3\gamma-6)/4}=\varepsilon^{(3\gamma-2)/4},

which vanishes because
\gamma>2/3
. To see convergence
(

ω

4)
, we compute

\varepsilon^{1-\gamma}\eta_{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon^{1-\gamma+(9\gamma-10)/12}=\varepsilon^{(2-3\gamma)/12}

which diverges since
\gamma>2/3
. Lastly, for convergence (
4.15
) we estimate

\dfrac{\varepsilon^{\beta-1}}{\varepsilon^{(\gamma-2)/4}-\varepsilon^{(9\gamma-10)/12}}=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{\beta-1-(\gamma-2)/4}}{1-\varepsilon^{(9\gamma-10)/12-(\gamma-2)/4}}=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{(-2+4\beta-\gamma)/4}}{1-\varepsilon^{(6\gamma-4)/12}},

which vanishes because
4\beta-\gamma>3\gamma>2
and
6\gamma>4
.

From
(

ω

4)
together with inequality
(R4)
, for any
\eta_{\varepsilon}
in this range, we directly deduce via Markov’s inequality that
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\omega_{\varepsilon})\to 0
uniformly in time, which is
(

ω

1)
.

To prove convergence
(

ω

2)
, it suffices to estimate the perimeter for fixed
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in I_{\varepsilon}
by an appropriate sequence
\rho_{\varepsilon}\to 0
. Using the coarea formula for
\mathrm{GSBV}
-functions from
[
AFP00
, Thm. 4.34]
(suppressing dependence on

), and Hölder’s inequality, we deduce that for all
i,j\in\{1,2\}
we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}\int_{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial^{\ast}\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}>s\}\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\right)\differential{ s }

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial^{\ast}\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}>s\}\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\right)\differential{ s }

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}\norm{ \nabla^{ 2 } u^{ \mathrm{aux} }_{ \varepsilon} }_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}=\dfrac{1}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\norm{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ \varepsilon} }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{\varepsilon^{\beta-1}}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}},

where the last inequality uses that the energy of
y_{\varepsilon}
stays uniformly bounded due to Lemma
4.1
. Applying the same reasoning in
[-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+},-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}]
, this implies that we can choose
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in[\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-},\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}]
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial^{\ast}\omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}\sum_{ij}\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\partial^{\ast}\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}\geq\eta_{\varepsilon}\}\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\big)+\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\partial^{\ast}\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}\leq-\eta_{\varepsilon}\}\setminus J_{\nabla u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\dfrac{\varepsilon^{\beta-1}}{\theta_{\varepsilon}^{+}-\theta_{\varepsilon}^{-}}=:\rho_{\varepsilon},

where we used that
\partial^{\ast}(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\omega_{\varepsilon})=\partial^{\ast}\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}(A\cup B))\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}A)+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B)
, and

\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\omega_{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{ij}\left\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}\geq\eta_{\varepsilon}\right\}\cup\left\{\partial_{i}{(u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon})^{j}}\leq-\eta_{\varepsilon}\right\}.

Note that
\rho_{\varepsilon}
vanishes uniformly in time by (
4.15
), which finishes the proof.
∎

Finally, recalling the definition of
u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}
in (
4.12
), we define the modified displacements by

u_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\chi_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)}u^{\mathrm{aux}}_{\varepsilon}(t),

where
\omega_{\varepsilon}(t)
is as in Lemma

.
Note that, since

and

, we have that

for

sufficiently small independently of time. Thus,

still satisfies the boundary conditions

on

. Moreover, as a consequence of convergence
(

ω

2)
and estimate (

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\big(J_{u_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}}\big)\setminus\big(J_{y_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}}\big)\big)\to 0\quad\text{ uniformly in time as $\varepsilon\to 0$}.

4.3.
Compactness of the displacements

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\colon{e(\phi)}\differential{ x }+\theta\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi))\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\theta}\left(H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\theta\phi)\right).

We choose

\theta_{0}=\left(H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi))\differential{ x }\right)^{-1/2}

and note that
\theta_{0}<1
since we ruled out inequality (
5.14
).
Plugging this into the above inequality yields

-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\colon e(\phi)\differential{ x }\leq\left(H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi))\differential{ x }\right)^{1/2}+\dfrac{H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{0}\phi)}{\left(H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\right)^{1/2}}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi))\right)^{1/2}.

By observing inequality (
5.6
), we note that, since
\theta_{0}<1
, we have
\rho_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{0}\phi)\leq\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi)
, and thus we obtain

-\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\colon e(\phi)\differential{ x }\leq 2\left(H_{\varepsilon}+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi))\differential{ x }\right)^{1/2}.

Repeating the argument with
-\phi
then yields the desired inequality.
∎

5.3.
Transfer of minimality

We now want to pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in

to argue that the auxiliary limit

given by

is a minimizer of the Griffith energy accounting for the existing crack, in analogy to

given in

(a refinement of
[

).

Lemma 5.4
.

For each
t\in[0,1]
, the displacement
\hat{u}(t)
from Remark
4.4
minimizes

(5.15)

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(v))\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{v}\setminus\left(\Gamma(t)\cup J_{\hat{u}(t)}\right)\right)

among all

on

We point out that, for

Naturally, this lemma will be critical for verifying the minimality property

Proof.

Let

By the density result in

3.2
(applied for

\hat{u}(t)
with respect to competitors

on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}

We begin by approximating the crack

with finitely many jump sets. Precisely, letting

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}J_{u(t_{k})}\cup J_{\hat{u}(t)}\right)\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma(t)\cup J_{\hat{u}(t)}\right)-\eta.

Choose

as the largest element of

which is smaller than

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\big)\differential{ x }-\rho_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u_{\varepsilon}(t)+\phi)\big)\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{\phi}\cup J_{\nabla\phi}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\right)\cup\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\right)\right)\right),

where

Now, fix

with

on

(defined in equation (

(i)

\phi_{\varepsilon}\to\phi

(ii)

(iii)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(\left(J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\cup J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t)}\right)\right)\right)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{\phi}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}J_{u(t_{k})}\cup J_{\hat{u}(t)}\right)\right)\right)

Since

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\big)\differential{ x }-\rho_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u_{\varepsilon}(t)+\phi_{\varepsilon})\big)\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\right)\cup\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\right)\right)\right),

where the error

By the uniform bound on

By subtracting

\displaystyle-\rho_{\varepsilon}\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}e(u_{\varepsilon}(t))\colon{e(\phi_{\varepsilon})}\differential{ x }+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi_{\varepsilon}))\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\cup J_{\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{p}J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\cup J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t)}\right)\right)

(5.19)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\setminus\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t_{k})}\right)\big)+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(J_{v_{\varepsilon}(t)}\setminus\left(J_{y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\cup J_{\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t)}\right)\big).

∎

5.4.
Elastic energy convergence

As a consequence of
Lemma

5.4
, we have established that
\hat{u}(t)
is a minimizer with respect to its own jump set in the linearized functional, i.e.,
one may replace
\Gamma(t)\cup J_{\hat{u}(t)}
by
J_{\hat{u}(t)}
in the minimization problem (
5.15
). In a similar fashion, the functions
y_{\varepsilon}
are minimizers with respect to their own jump set of the nonlinear functional, cf. (
2.10
). This minimality property now induces convergence of the elastic energies. Whereas for Griffith energies this simply follows from
[
Fri20
]
along with the fundamental theorem of
\Gamma
-convergence, in the present evolutionary setting with irreversibility condition, this is a consequence of the jump transfer lemma (see
Theorem

3.3
).

Corollary 5.5

(Elastic energy convergence)
.

For all
t\in[0,1]
, we have the convergence of elastic energies

(5.20)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t))\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ \varepsilon} ( t ) }^{2}\differential{ x }=\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(\hat{u}(t))\big)\differential{ x },

again along a
t
-dependent subsequence for
t\notin I_{\infty}
. In particular, we have

(5.21)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ \varepsilon} ( t ) }^{2}\differential{ x }=0.

Proof.

First, from lower semicontinuity, see inequality (
5.2
), we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W\left(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\differential{ x }+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\beta}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 } y_{ \varepsilon} ( t ) }^{2}\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}W\left(\nabla y_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)\differential{ x }

(5.22)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(\hat{u}(t))\big)\differential{ x }.

(Note that the proof was only formulated for
t\in I_{\infty}
, but still applies to

Since
y_{\varepsilon}(t)
is a minimizer of the functional (
2.10

To find this sequence, we approximate

and apply the jump transfer lemma (

3.3
) to the approximations. Precisely, by

3.2

Observe that the sequence

defined in equation (

has uniformly bounded jump-set measure, and has uniformly
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-bounded symmetric gradients. For all

3.3
) to the function

6.
Approximate energy balance

We begin the preliminary steps of the proof of the energy-balance equation of
(v)
in
Definition

2.1
. To this end, recall the definition of the total energy in (
2.13
). To derive the energy-balance equation, we make rigorous the idea that once a piece of material is disconnected from the Dirichlet boundary, it will relax to an undeformed equilibrium configuration. Precisely, from the approximate variational inequality in
Corollary

5.3
we will deduce that the partition elements
P_{j}^{\varepsilon}
which do not intersect the Dirichlet boundary do not contribute to the energy-balance equation in the limit, in the sense of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
t\in[0,1]
, let
(P_{j}^{\varepsilon}{(t)})_{j}
be the Caccioppoli partition from equation (
4.11
) and let
(\hat{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon}{(t)})_{j}\subseteq\mathrm{SO}(2)
be any associated collection of matrices that is piecewise constant in time with respect to the same partition of
[0,1]

t\in[0,1]

(6.1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\absolutevalue{
\int_{ 0 }^{ t }
\sum_{ P_j^\eps(s) \in\intpcs_\eps(s) }
\int_{ P_{ j }^{ \varepsilon} ( s ) }
\mathbb{ C } e ( u_{ \varepsilon} (s) ) \colon{ e ( \hat R_{ j }^{ \varepsilon} (s) \partial_t h (s) ) }
\dd{ x }
\dd{ s }
}=0,

where
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\varepsilon}(s):=\{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\cap(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}))=0\}
are the interior partition pieces for all
s\in[0,1]
.

As will be seen in the proof, the only constraint on the test function (
\sum_{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\varepsilon}(s)}\chi_{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\hat{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\partial_{t}h(s)
above) is that it is piecewise regular with respect to the partition and
0
on the boundary.
Formally, this result shows that for the limit Caccioppoli partition
\mathcal{P}
with
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{int}}
, defined as above, and a regular test function
\phi
, one has
\int_{P_{j}}\mathbb{C}{e(u)}:{e(\phi)}\,dx=0
, which implies that
u
is an infinitesimal rigid motion on
P_{j}
, meaning the material has fully relaxed to an equilibrium configuration on
P_{j}
.

Proof.

We know by (
2.2
) that
h\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}((0,1);\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Since
e(u_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-bounded (see estimate (
4.13
) and (
4.16
)), via an approximation it thus suffices to prove the claim under the assumption
h\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}([0,1];\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}))
(note that this is just an assumption on the regularity of
h
explicitly appearing in (
6.1
), not the boundary data).

Let
s\in[0,1]
. We apply the approximate variational inequality in
Corollary

5.3
to the function

\phi_{\varepsilon}(s)=\sum_{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\varepsilon}(s)}\chi_{P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\hat{R}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)\partial_{t}h(s)

to get that the term on the left-hand side of equality (

) can be estimated by the limit of

C\int_{0}^{t}\left(\max\left\{1,\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(\phi_{\varepsilon}))\differential{ x }\right\}\left(\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)+\rho_{\varepsilon}(\phi_{\varepsilon})\right)\right)^{1/2}\differential{ s }.

Since
h\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}([0,1];\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, the term
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}Q(e(\phi_{\varepsilon}))\differential{ x }
stays uniformly bounded in time.
We have
J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}(s)}\subseteq\bigcup_{j}\partial^{\ast}P_{j}^{\varepsilon}(s)
and thus by construction of the Caccioppoli partition for the rotations, see
(R2)
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{\phi_{\varepsilon}(s)}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(s)\right)\lesssim\varepsilon^{\beta-\gamma}

uniformly in time. Thus, by recalling the estimate for
\rho_{\varepsilon}
in (
5.6
), it suffices to show that

(6.2)

\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\delta_{\varepsilon}\left(1+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }^{2}\differential{ x }+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{ \nabla^{ 2 }\partial_t h }^{2}\differential{ x }\right)+\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varepsilon\absolutevalue{ \nabla\partial_{ t } h }^{3}\differential{ x }\Big)^{1/2}\differential{ s }\to 0

as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.
By the Sobolev embedding, we know that
\nabla\partial_{t}h\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}((0,1);\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}))
, where we again used
h\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}([0,1];\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Thus, equation (

) holds true, which finishes the proof.
∎

As the next step for the desired energy balance, we derive inequalities describing the limiting energy evolution.

, Lem. 3.7]
. Recall the definitions in (

) and (

).

Lemma 6.2
.

Assume that the initial conditions are well-prepared in the sense that

(6.3)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\energyNonlin(y_{0}^{\varepsilon})\leq\energyLinTot(0).

Then, for any
t\in I_{\infty}

Appendix A
Bad sets, integral bounds, and measure theory

Lemma A.1

(Bad set)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be bounded Lipschitz domains such that also
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is a Lipschitz set, and suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\cap\overline{\Omega}
is a Borel set with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)<\infty
. Then there exists a unique maximal measurable set
B\subseteq\Omega
(up to
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-equivalence class and with respect to set inclusion) such that
\partial^{\ast}B\subseteq\Gamma
(up to an
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-nullset). If
\Gamma
is additionally closed, we have

(A.1)

B=\left\{x\in\Omega\colon x\text{ is not path-connected to }\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\text{ in }\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma\right\}.

Remark A.2
.

It is convenient for the proof if we denote by
\partial^{\ast}
the
essential boundary
of a set (see
[
AFP00
, Def. 3.60]
), which is defined without assuming the set to be of finite perimeter. Note however that if
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}A)<\infty
, then by
[
Fed69
, 4.5.11]
,
A
is already a set of finite perimeter and the essential and reduced boundary agree up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure zero.

Proof.

We first prove the existence of a maximal set by using the direct method.
Let

M\coloneqq\left\{B\subseteq\Omega\text{ measurable }\colon\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B\setminus\Gamma)=0\right\}.

Note that the empty set is an element of
M
and by boundedness of
\Omega
, the Lebesgue measure of sets in
M
is uniformly bounded. Moreover, the essential boundary of every
B\in M
is bounded with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{1}
.
Thus, for a maximizing sequence
(B_{n})_{n}\subseteq M
with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, we find a subsequence (not relabeled) and some measurable set
B
such that
B_{n}\to B
in measure.
By this convergence it follows that
\mathcal{L}^{2}(B)=\max_{B^{\prime}\in M}\mathcal{L}^{2}(B^{\prime})
. Using lower semicontinuity, we have for every compact set
K\subseteq\Gamma
that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B\setminus K)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B_{n}\setminus K)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\setminus K).

Sending
K\to\Gamma
yields that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B\setminus\Gamma)=0
, and thus
B\in M
. Moreover
B
is maximal: If
B^{\prime}\in M
, then
B\cup B^{\prime}\in M
since
\partial^{\ast}(B\cup B^{\prime})\subseteq\partial^{\ast}B\cup\partial^{\ast}B^{\prime}
. Thus, by
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-maximality of
B
, we must already have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(B^{\prime}\setminus B)=0
. This also proves the uniqueness.

Now let us assume that
\Gamma
is closed. We want to show equation (
A.1
). Denoting by
\hat{B}
the right-hand side of (
A.1
), we aim at checking
B=\hat{B}
with
B
above. First, we show
\hat{B}\in M
. Assume by contradiction that there exists
x\in\partial^{\ast}\hat{B}\setminus\Gamma
. Then, by the closedness of
\Gamma
we find some
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(x)\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma
. Moreover, by the definition of the essential boundary, we find some
y\in B_{\delta}(x)\setminus\hat{B}
.
By definition of
\\
hat{B}
and because
B_{\delta}(x)\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma
, by connecting elements
z\in B_{\delta(x)}
with
y
by a straight segment, this yields that
B_{\delta}(x)
is also path-connected to
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
in
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma
. Thus
B_{\delta}(x)\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\hat{B}
, a contradiction to
x\in\partial^{\ast}\bar{B}
. We thus conclude that
\partial^{\ast}\hat{B}\subseteq\Gamma
, i.e.,
\hat{B}\in M
.

To conclude
B=\hat{B}
, it suffices to show that
\hat{B}
is maximal.
Let
B^{\prime}\in M
and suppose by contradiction that
x\in B^{\prime}\setminus\hat{B}
is a point of Lebesque density
1
of
B^{\prime}\setminus\hat{B}
.
Then, we find a continuous path
\gamma
between
x
and
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
in
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma
.
Since
\gamma
is compact and
\Gamma
closed, we find some
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(\gamma)\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma
. Since we have chosen
x
as a point of density
1
of
B^{\prime}\setminus\hat{B}
, we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{\delta}(\gamma)\cap B^{\prime})>0
. Moreover, because
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is open,
\gamma
ends in
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and
B^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
, we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{\delta}(\gamma)\setminus B^{\prime})>0
. As a consequence, we must have
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{\ast}B^{\prime}\cap B_{\delta}(\gamma))>0
. This is a contradiction to
B^{\prime}\in M
because
\Gamma\cap B_{\delta}(\gamma)=\emptyset
.

Lemma A.3

(
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-bounds)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)<\infty
,
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;[0,\infty))
be a sequence such that
\norm{ f_{ \varepsilon} }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\gamma-1}
for some
\gamma\in(2/3,1)
, and let
g\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;[0,\infty))
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then we find an exponent
q\in(1,\infty)
and some
{\alpha}>0
such that

\varepsilon\int_{\{\varepsilon f_{\varepsilon}\lesssim 1\}}f_{\varepsilon}^{2}g\differential{ x }\lesssim\varepsilon^{{\alpha}}\norm{ g }_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Let
0<{\zeta}<2
and let
q\in(1,\infty)
be such that
1/q+(2-{\zeta})/2=1
. By Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{\{\varepsilon f_{\varepsilon}\lesssim 1\}}f_{\varepsilon}^{2}g\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-{\zeta}}\int_{\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}^{2-{\zeta}}g\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{1-{\zeta}}\norm{ f_{ \varepsilon} }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2-{\zeta}}\norm{ g }_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-{\zeta}+(\gamma-1)(2-{\zeta})}\norm{ g }_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}.

Since

{\alpha}\coloneqq 1-{\zeta}+(\gamma-1)(2-{\zeta})=-1+2\gamma-\gamma{\zeta}

and
-1+2\gamma>0
, we can choose
{\zeta}>0
sufficiently small such that
{\alpha}>0
, which finishes the proof.

Lemma A.4

(Measure theory)
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Borel sets with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}),\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{2})<\infty
. Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, we find open Lipschitz sets
U_{1},U_{2}
such that
U_{1}\cap U_{2}=\emptyset
and

{\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}\cup A_{2})-\epsilon\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}\cap U_{1})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{2}\cap U_{2}).}

Proof.

The measure
\mu\coloneqq\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}
is a Radon measure. Thus, by inner regularity, we find compact sets
K_{1}\subseteq A_{1}
,
K_{2}\subseteq A_{2}\setminus A_{1}
such that
\mu(A_{1}\setminus K_{1}),\mu((A_{2}\setminus A_{1})\setminus K_{2})\leq\epsilon/2
. Since
K_{1}\cap K_{2}=\emptyset
and both are compact, we have
\mathrm{dist}(K_{1},K_{2})=:\delta>0
. We therefore find open, disjoint Lipschitz sets
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
such that
K_{1}\subseteq U_{1}
and
K_{2}\subseteq U_{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}\cup A_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{2}\setminus A_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}\cap K_{1})+\mathcal{H}^{1}((A_{2}\setminus A_{1})\cap K_{2})+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(A_{1}\cap U_{1})+\mathcal{H}^{1}((A_{2}\setminus A_{1})\cap U_{2})+\epsilon,

which finishes the proof.

z_{\delta}=h
on
Q\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Thus, point (iii) and (iv) of (
B.4
) hold. We estimate

(B.7)

\displaystyle\|z_{\delta}-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}\leq\|(\psi_{\delta}h+(1-\psi_{\delta})v)-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}+\|v_{\delta}-w_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}+\|v_{\delta}-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}.

This shows
z_{\delta}\to v
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
\delta\to 0
by (
B.5
)–(
B.6
), the fact that
\mathcal{L}^{2}({\rm supp}\,\psi_{\delta}\cap\Omega)\to 0
, and
v=h
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Thus, it remains to check (
B.4
)(ii). We note that
e(z_{\delta})=\psi_{\delta}e(h)+(1-\psi_{\delta})e(w_{\delta})+(h-w_{\delta})\odot\nabla\psi_{\delta}
.
By an argument similar to (
B.7
) employing (
B.5
)–(
B.6
), we get
\|\psi_{\delta}e(h)+(1-\psi_{\delta})e(w_{\delta})-e(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}\to 0
. On the other hand, setting
\Psi_{\delta}:=\{0<\psi_{\delta}<1\}
, by the first inequality in (
B.6
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
h
, we get

\displaystyle\|(h-w_{\delta})\odot\nabla\psi_{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{-1}\|w_{\delta}-h\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Psi_{\delta})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{-1}\|v_{\delta}-h\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Psi_{\delta})}+\delta

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}\|v(\cdot+\delta e_{2})-h\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Psi_{\delta})}+\delta

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla h\|_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Psi_{\delta})^{1/2}+\delta.

where we used that
v(\cdot+\delta e_{2})=h(\cdot+\delta e_{2})
on
\Psi_{\delta}
. Since this vanishes as
\delta\to 0
, (
B.4
)(ii) follows.

After having found the functions
z^{i}_{\delta}
satisfying (
B.4
), we are now ready to construct the sequence
(w_{\delta})_{\delta}
. To this end, we choose slightly smaller squares
Q^{\prime}_{i}\subset\subset Q_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
such that

(B.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial_{D}\Omega\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(Q_{i}\setminus Q_{i}^{\prime})\right)\leq\eta

and we choose cut-off functions
(\varphi_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\varphi_{i}=1
on
Q^{\prime}_{i}\cap\Omega
and
\varphi_{i}=0
on
\Omega\setminus Q_{i}
. Eventually, we let
\varphi_{0}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi_{i}
. By
[
Iur14
, Thm. 3.1]
(see also
[
Cri19
, Thm. 1.1]
for control on higher Sobolev norms) we find a sequence
(z^{0}_{\delta})_{\delta}\subseteq\mathcal{W}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

(B.9)

\displaystyle\|z^{0}_{\delta}-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|e(z^{0}_{\delta})-e(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathcal{H}^{1}\big((J_{z^{0}_{\delta}}\triangle J_{v})\cap\Omega\big)\to 0\quad\text{as $\delta\to 0$}.

We define
w_{\delta}\in\mathcal{W}(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

w_{\delta}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\varphi_{i}z_{\delta}^{i}&\text{ on }\Omega,\\
h&\text{ on }\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.\end{cases}

By construction and by (
B.4
)(iv) we find
J_{w_{\delta}}\cap Q_{i}^{\prime}=J_{z^{i}_{\delta}}\cap Q_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. Furthermore, by adding a vanishingly small constant to
z^{0}_{\delta}
, we may ensure
J_{w_{\delta}}\supseteq\partial_{D}\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q_{i}
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(J_{w_{\delta}}\triangle J_{v}\big)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\big((J_{z_{\delta}^{0}}\triangle J_{v})\cap\Omega\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\left(\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{z_{\delta}^{i}}\cap Q_{i})+\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{v}\cap Q_{i})\right)}+\mathcal{H}^{1}\left((\partial_{D}\Omega\setminus J_{v})\setminus\bigcup\nolimits_{i}Q_{i}^{\prime}\right).

Thus, combining (
B.2
)(i), (
B.3
), (
B.4
)(iii), (
B.8
), and (
B.9
) we find

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(J_{w_{\delta}}\triangle J_{v}\big)\lesssim\eta+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}\lesssim\eta,

where in the last step we also used (
B.2
)(ii) and the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial_{D}\Omega)<\infty
. This shows (
B.1
). Combining (
B.4
)(i) and (
B.9
), we find
(D1)
. Eventually, since
e(w_{\delta})=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\varphi_{i}e(z_{\delta}^{i})+\nabla\varphi_{i}\odot z_{\delta}^{i}
on
\Omega
and
e(v)
can be written as
e(v)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\varphi_{i}e(v)+\nabla\varphi_{i}\odot v
, by using (
B.4
)(i), (ii) and (
B.9
) we conclude
(D2)
.
∎

Proof of the upper estimate in
Lemma

6.2
.

We prove the upper semicontinuity estimate (
6.5
). As we will show, this is a consequence of
u
being a minimizer with respect to its own jump set, see
Lemma

5.4
. Let
0\leq s\leq t\text{ with }s,t\in I_{\infty}
and, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, suppose that
n=N(\varepsilon)
is such that
t=t_{N(\varepsilon)}^{\varepsilon}
. Then, the function
u(t)+h(s)-h(t)
is an admissible competitor of the minimization problem (
5.15
) at time
s
. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega^{\prime}}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u(s))\big)\differential{ x }

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega^{\prime}}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u(t))\big)+\mathbb{C}e(u(t))\colon{e(h(s)-h(t))}+\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(h(s)-h(t))\big)\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{u(t)}\setminus\Gamma(s)\right),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\energyLinTot(s)\leq

\displaystyle\energyLinTot(t)+\int_{{\Omega^{\prime}}}\mathbb{C}e(u(t))\colon{e(h(s)-h(t))}+\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(h(s)-h(t))\big)\differential{ x }

\displaystyle+\kappa\left(\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma(s)\right)+\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{u(t)}\setminus\Gamma(s)\right)-\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Gamma(t)\right)\right).

Note that the term in the brackets is nonpositive since
\Gamma(s)\subseteq\Gamma(t)
and
J_{u(t)}\subseteq\Gamma(t)
.
Thus, since
h
is continuous in time with respect to
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{W}^{2,2}}
, we find that
\energyLinTot
has no negative jumps as claimed in the lemma. Moreover, letting
s=t_{N(\varepsilon)-1}^{\varepsilon}
and iterating the above estimate (and re-using the variable
s
), we get that for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small

\displaystyle\energyLinTot(t)\geq\energyLinTot(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{N(\varepsilon)-1}\int_{t_{k}^{\varepsilon}}^{t_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}}\int_{{\Omega^{\prime}}}\mathbb{C}e(u(t_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}))\colon{e(\partial_{t}h(s))}\differential{ x }\differential{ s }-\omega(\Delta_{\varepsilon})\int_{0}^{t}\norm{ \nabla\partial_t h }_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\differential{ s }

for a function
\omega
which vanishes as
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
tends to zero as in (
4.9
). Taking the limit superior gives the desired inequality (
6.5
).
∎

Proof of Minimality and Jump-set Inclusion of
Lemma

7.1
.

The bound on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma(t))
is an immediate consequence of the lower semicontinuity (
5.4
). The fact that
u(t)=h(t)
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
for all
t\in[0,1]
follows from Definition
4.5
.

Minimality.
For
t\in I_{\infty}
the minimality follows from Lemma
5.4
. Let us now prove the minimality (
7.1
) for
t\in[0,1]\setminus I_{\infty}
. We know by
Lemma

5.4
that for all
t_{p}\in I_{\infty}
(used to define
u(t)
in Definition
4.5
) and all
v\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
with
v=h(t_{p})
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
it holds that

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u(t_{p}))\big)\differential{ x }\leq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q(e(v))\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{v}\setminus\Gamma(t_{p})\right).

Take
w\in\mathrm{GSBD}^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
with
w=h(t)
on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and test the above inequality with
w+h(t_{p})-h(t)
to obtain

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(u(t_{p}))\big)\differential{ x }\leq\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2}Q\big(e(w+h(t_{p})-h(t))\big)\differential{ x }+\kappa\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(J_{w}\setminus\Gamma(t_{p})\right).

By weak convergence, in the limit
p\to\infty
, the left-hand side can be bounded from below by the integral
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\tfrac{1}{2}Q(e(u(t)))\differential{x}
. The integral on the right-hand side converges to
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\tfrac{1}{2}Q(e(w))\differential{x}
, and since
t\notin I_{\infty}
, we have by continuity from above of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
that the measure of the jump set converges to
\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{w}\setminus\Gamma(t))
. This proves the desired minimality.

Jump-set inclusion.
Next, we argue that the inclusion (
7.2
) holds. If this were not true, then we can find a point
x\in J_{u(t)}\setminus\Gamma(t)
that has
1
-dimensional density
1
with respect to the set
J_{u(t)}
and density
0
with respect to
\Gamma(t)
(see
[
AFP00
, Thm. 2.83 and (2.41)]
). Thus, for some small fixed
r>0
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B_{r}(x)\cap J_{u(t)}\right)\geq r/2\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B_{r}(x)\cap\Gamma(t)\right)<r/2.

Since
J_{u(t_{p})}\subseteq\Gamma(t)
, we know that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B_{r}(x)\cap J_{u(t_{p})}\right)<r/2.

Applying the lower semicontinuity of jump sets as
p\to\infty
, see
Theorem

3.1
(iii)
for
U=B_{r}(x)
, leads to a contradiction.
∎
