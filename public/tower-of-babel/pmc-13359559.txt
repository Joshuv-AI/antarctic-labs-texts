Concomitant cytomegalovirus DNAemia is associated with sustained TNF-α elevation in HIV/AIDS: a multivariable and longitudinal analysis of immune restoration
Authors: Yuansi Lao, Deling Lei, Min Fang, Yongtao Le, Qingmei Huang, Wuning Mo, Gaobin Teng, Zengfu Li, Qituan Jiang, Ming Zhou, Sheng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite successful viral suppression with antiretroviral therapy (ART), incomplete immune reconstitution and chronic inflammation persist in people with HIV, contributing to an increased burden of non-AIDS-defining comorbidities. Emerging evidence points to cytomegalovirus (CMV) co-infection as a critical driver of this residual inflammation. Consequently, this study aims to elucidate the specific role of CMV in sustaining inflammatory pathways in the context of HIV.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 200 participants divided into three groups: 50 with HIV/CMV co‑infection, 100 with HIV mono‑infection, and 50 healthy controls. Baseline immunophenotyping and cytokine profiling were assessed using flow cytometry and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. To account for baseline HIV disease severity and demographic imbalances, cross-sectional comparisons were evaluated using multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, and baseline absolute CD4⁺ T-cell counts, supplemented by CD4-stratified analyses (< 100 and > 200 cells/µL). To evaluate the longitudinal impact of CMV viral load dynamics, co‑infected participants were classified after 6 months follow‑up into “CMV responders” (≥ 68% reduction in CMV DNA load) and “CMV non‑responders” (< 68% reduction or increase).ResultsThe overall cohort was predominantly CMV IgG positive (97.5%). At baseline, the unadjusted expansion of CD8⁺ T-cells observed in HIV/CMV co-infected patients lost statistical significance after multivariable adjustment for baseline CD4⁺ counts (adjusted p = 0.150), indicating this was primarily related to advanced HIV immunosuppression. In contrast, CMV DNAemia remained independently associated with significantly elevated systemic TNF-α levels (adjusted p = 0.043). Stratified analysis revealed that this independent TNF-α elevation was particularly pronounced in patients with relatively preserved immune function (CD4 > 200 cells/µL; adjusted p = 0.042), whereas these differences were obscured in severe immunosuppression (CD4 < 100 cells/µL). Longitudinal analysis revealed that effective reduction of CMV viral load was associated with significant HIV viral suppression in responders (p = 0.0039), whereas no significant reduction was observed in non-responders (p = 0.3894), and seemingly facilitated the resolution of systemic TNF-α levels. In contrast, significant recovery of CD4⁺ T-cell absolute counts occurred in both groups (p < 0.01) and the rate of recovery did not differ significantly between CMV responders and non-responders.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that CMV DNAemia is independently associated with persistent TNF-α elevation in PLWH, particularly in those with preserved CD4 + counts. Rather than directly causing broad T-cell exhaustion, CMV appears to act as a specific driver of the TNF-α inflammatory pathway. Addressing adherence to anti-CMV interventions alongside ART may offer a potential strategy for reducing long-term inflammatory risks.Keywords: HIV/AIDS, Cytomegalovirus co-infection, Chronic inflammation, TNF-α, T-cell exhaustion, Immune restoration, Viral reservoir, Longitudinal analysis

Introduction

The trajectory of HIV management has shifted from an acute crisis of mortality to the complex management of a chronic condition, a transition propelled by the evolution of antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1, 2]. Contemporary first-line regimens, particularly those incorporating integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) such as bictegravir, offer high barriers to resistance and potent viral suppression, fundamentally altering the prognosis for people living with HIV (PLWH) [3–5]. Large-scale randomized trials have definitively established that the immediate initiation of ART significantly reduces the incidence of serious AIDS-related events [2].

Despite these therapeutic successes, the restoration of full immunological health remains incomplete. Even after achieving undetectable HIV viral loads, PLWH continue to experience accelerated immunosenescence and an increased burden of non-AIDS-related comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease and neurocognitive impairment [6, 7]. A principal driver of this residual pathology is chronic immune activation and persistent inflammation [8]. Recent studies have shown that sustained inflammation functions as an independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes, with elevated tumor necrosis factor (TNF) levels being linked to endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerotic plaque formation [9]. In the contemporary ART era, where HIV replication is routinely controlled, chronic inflammation and its associated comorbidities have emerged as primary determinants of long-term patient outcomes [10, 11].

Co-infections represent a pivotal, yet often under-addressed, source of this persistent immune dysregulation. While tuberculosis co-infection has been rigorously studied [12], the immunologic impact of sub-clinical Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection requires greater scrutiny. CMV DNAemia is strongly associated with T cell expansion and persistent immune activation, which may hinder complete immune recovery [13]. However, the relationship is likely bidirectional; advanced HIV induced immunosuppression facilitates CMV reactivation, which in turn acts as a continuous antigenic stimulus. In resource limited settings, where diagnostic delays contribute to complex clinical presentations [14, 15], distinguishing the specific immunologic footprint of CMV from HIV-induced pathology is essential.

This study leverages cross-sectional multivariable analysis and longitudinal data to investigate the specific immunological footprint of CMV DNAemia. Specifically, we aimed to disentangle the inflammatory effects independently associated with CMV from those driven by underlying HIV severity, and to determine whether the clearance of CMV viremia correlates with the resolution of specific inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-2) and the restoration of T-cell subsets.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This prospective observational study was conducted at Hengzhou People’s Hospital. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No. kj2023-001) in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design and participants

A total of 200 participants were enrolled between June 2021 and December 2023 and categorized into three groups: (1) individuals with HIV/CMV co-infection (n = 50; CMV DNA > 500 copies/mL); (2) individuals with HIV mono-infection (n = 100; HIV positive, CMV DNA negative or < 500 copies/mL); and (3) Healthy controls (n = 50; HIV negative). Patients were enrolled at the time of initial HIV diagnosis or upon experiencing virologic failure requiring ART initiation or optimization. The inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical confirmation of HIV/AIDS according to national diagnostic standards; (2) for the co-infection group, a detectable CMV DNA load > 500 copies/mL; and (3) meeting indications for and demonstrating tolerability to antiretroviral and/or antimicrobial therapy. The exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of psychiatric disorders or severe organic diseases (e.g., malignant tumors, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, cognitive impairment); (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) recent use or discontinuation of antiviral medications prior to enrollment; and (4) specifically for the co-infection group, the presence of other concurrent opportunistic infections. None of the participants exhibited overt CMV end-organ disease (e.g., retinitis, colitis, or pneumonitis) at enrollment; CMV detection primarily represented asymptomatic or subclinical DNAemia. Baseline peripheral blood samples were collected at enrollment, prior to the initiation of new antiretroviral therapy (ART) or anti-cytomegalovirus (CMV) therapy.

Stratification

Longitudinal immune dynamics were assessed by calculating the percentage change (Δ%) in immune and inflammatory parameters. Based on CMV viral load response, co-infected patients were stratified into two subgroups: the “CMV Responder Group,” defined as a ≥ 68% decrease in CMV viral load from baseline, and a “Non-Responder Group,” defined as a < 68% decrease or any increase in CMV viral load. This 68% cutoff (approximately 0.5 log10) was selected a priori based on the American Society of Transplantation (AST) Guidelines, which recognize a 0.5 log10 reduction as a clinically meaningful threshold for evaluating viral dynamics and therapeutic response [16].

Treatment regimens and follow up

All HIV-positive participants initiated or optimized standard ART per national guidelines. ART regimens consisted of a backbone of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors combined with a third agent, which was either an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI), a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI), or a boosted protease inhibitor (PI) containing ritonavir or cobicistat. Alternatively, all HIV-positive participants were treated with a single-tablet regimen (STR). Participants in the HIV/CMV group concomitantly received anti-cytomegalovirus therapy (e.g., ganciclovir or valganciclovir) or other standard-of-care interventions as clinically indicated. For participants in the HIV/CMV co-infection group, anti-CMV therapy was prescribed, typically consisting of oral valganciclovir or intravenous ganciclovir at standard dosing for 14 to 21 days, depending on clinical assessment. Follow-up peripheral blood samples were obtained after a median of 6 months following ART and/or anti-CMV treatment initiation. However, retrospective assessment indicated that adherence to anti-CMV therapy was variable among the 50 co-infected patients: approximately 44% (n = 22) demonstrated good adherence, while 56% (n = 28) exhibited poor adherence or prematurely discontinued the medication.

Laboratory measurements

HIV-1 RNA and CMV DNA levels were quantified using standardized real-time PCR assays. While the World Health Organization (WHO) international standard (IU/mL) is currently recommended for CMV quantification, viral loads in this study were reported in copies/mL as per the established historical protocol of the local reference laboratory during the enrollment period. Additionally, serum CMV IgG and IgM antibodies were assessed to determine prior exposure serostatus. T-lymphocyte subsets (CD3+, CD4+, CD8+), B cells (CD19+), and natural killer (NK) cells were analyzed via flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). Serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA; Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), while high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels were determined via dry immunofluorescence assays (Getein Biotech, Inc.).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software (Version 4.4.2). To stringently address potential confounders, particularly the impact of baseline HIV disease severity and age differences, cross-sectional comparisons among the study groups were performed using multivariable linear regression models. Models for immune and inflammatory parameters were adjusted for age, sex, and baseline absolute CD4⁺ T-cell counts. Type III analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess overall group effects. Estimated marginal means were computed using the emmeans package, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted. To quantify the magnitude of the observed associations, effect sizes were reported as Partial Eta Squared for the overall models and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Furthermore, to evaluate whether the immunologic footprint of CMV DNAemia varied by HIV disease stage, stratified analyses were conducted by grouping patients according to baseline CD4⁺ T-cell absolute counts (< 100 cells/µL and > 200 cells/µL). Longitudinal within-group changes were assessed using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and between-subgroup changes (Δ%) were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and serological characteristics

A total of 200 participants were included in the baseline analysis (50 with HIV/CMV co-infection, 100 with HIV mono-infection, and 50 healthy controls). Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Serological testing revealed that the vast majority of the overall cohort (195/200, 97.5%) was CMV IgG positive, indicating highly prevalent prior exposure across all groups. Notably, 99.0% of the “HIV mono-infection” group was CMV IgG positive, confirming that this group serves as a comparator for absent/low-level CMV DNAemia rather than an uninfected cohort. Initial assessment also revealed an age mismatch between the younger healthy controls and the older HIV-infected cohorts, alongside significant differences in baseline absolute CD4⁺ T-cell counts. Multivariable and stratified analysis of baseline immune profiles

Baseline cross-sectional data were subjected to multivariable regression analysis to distinguish the specific immunologic footprint of CMV DNAemia from the confounding effects of advanced HIV disease. Unadjusted initial comparisons indicated broad immune dysregulation in the HIV/CMV co-infected group, including marked CD8⁺ T-cell expansion. However, after strict adjustment for age, sex, and baseline absolute CD4⁺ T-cell counts, the previously observed differences in the CD8⁺ T-cell percentage between the CMV/HIV group and the HIV mono-infection group lost statistical significance (Adjusted p = 0.150; Fig. 1). This indicates that the generalized T-cell exhaustion phenotype is primarily a consequence of severe HIV-induced immunosuppression rather than being independently associated with CMV DNAemia.

In contrast, analysis of systemic inflammatory markers revealed a distinct pattern. Even after rigorous adjustment for demographic factors and CD4⁺ counts, serum TNF-α levels remained significantly and independently elevated in the CMV/HIV group compared to the HIV mono-infection group (Adjusted p = 0.043, Cohen’s d = 0.450; Fig. 1). Other cytokines, such as IL-2, did not show statistically significant differences under this adjusted model (Adjusted p = 0.128).

To further elucidate how underlying HIV disease severity influences CMV-associated inflammation, we performed a stratified analysis based on baseline CD4⁺ T-cell absolute counts. In the cohort with profound immunosuppression (CD4 < 100 cells/µL), no significant differences were observed in TNF-α (p = 0.450), CD8⁺ percentages (p = 0.211), or non-specific inflammatory markers such as hs-CRP (p = 0.262) between the co-infected and mono-infected groups (Fig. 2). This suggests that in the late stages of AIDS, the pervasive immune dysregulation driven by HIV largely masks any additional measurable effects of CMV DNAemia.

Longitudinal response: CMV clearance and HIV suppression

To evaluate the temporal relationship between viral activity and host immune responses, a longitudinal analysis was conducted. This analysis specifically assessed how changes in viral dynamics (e.g., viral load) over time influenced key immune markers, including pro-inflammatory cytokines. In the CMV Responder group, HIV viral load decreased significantly from a median of 146,000 copies/mL to 759 copies/mL (p = 0.0039). In contrast, the Non-Responder group did not achieve a statistically significant reduction (Median: 232,000 to 5230 copies/mL, p = 0.3894; Fig. 4A), suggesting that effective CMV control may be synergistic with HIV suppression. Regarding inflammatory markers, the CMV Responder group maintained stable, low-level TNF-α (Median: 0.00 to 0.00 pg/mL), contrasting with the Non-Responder group which exhibited higher baseline variability and incomplete resolution (Median: 3.29 to 0.01 pg/mL, p = 0.2092; Fig. 4B). Interestingly, CRP levels dropped significantly only in the Non-Responder group (p = 0.002; Fig. 4C), likely reflecting the control of acute phase reactants by ART, distinct from the specific TNF-α pathway modulated by CMV.

CD4+ T-cell recovery is independent of CMV Viral kinetics

To determine whether CMV activity influences immune reconstitution during ART, we compared CD4 + T-cell recovery between CMV virologic responders and non-responders. Despite effective suppression of HIV by antiretroviral therapy (ART), CD4 + T-cell recovery proceeded independently of contemporaneous CMV viral kinetics. Both the CMV Responder and Non-Responder groups exhibited statistically significant increases in absolute CD4 + T-cell counts following ART initiation (Responder: Median 79.9 to 235.9 cells/µL; Non-Responder: Median 156.9 to 256.4 cells/µL; Fig. 4D). Moreover, the magnitude of recovery did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that although CMV co‑infection amplifies certain inflammatory pathways (e.g., TNF‑α), overall CD4 + T‑cell reconstitution during ART is driven principally by HIV suppression rather than by short‑term fluctuations in CMV activity.

Distinct recovery patterns in B-cells and CD8 + T-cells

In contrast to the uniform CD4 + recovery, other immune subsets displayed divergent trajectories. Significant restoration of B-cells (CD19 + absolute count) was observed exclusively in the CMV Responder group (Median: 79.7 to 112.2 cells/µL, p = 0.004; Fig. 4E), whereas the Non-Responder group failed to achieve statistical significance (p = 0.073). Additionally, while CD8 + T-cell percentages remained stable in Responders, the Non-Responder group exhibited a significant partial normalization (Median: 63.1% to 55.2%, p = 0.042; Fig. 4F).

Discussion

In the contemporary ART era, chronic inflammation and its associated comorbidities have emerged as primary determinants of long-term patient outcomes [10, 11]. The contribution of co-infections to this persistent inflammatory state requires rigorous clarification, particularly to separate the effects of active viremia from the consequences of severe immunosuppression. A key strength of this study is the application of multivariable and stratified analyses to disentangle these factors. Our findings indicate that certain classical hallmarks of advanced HIV, such as CD8⁺ T-cell expansion, are primarily driven by the severity of the underlying HIV-induced CD4 + depletion. However, we identified that subclinical CMV DNAemia maintains an independent and robust association with elevated systemic TNF-α levels. This association is particularly pronounced in patients with relatively preserved immune function (CD4 > 200 cells/µL), suggesting that while standard ART is highly effective for CD4 + T-cell reconstitution [3, 4], it may be insufficient to fully normalize specific inflammatory pathways when active CMV replication persists.

The observed association between TNF-α resolution and CMV clearance has significant clinical implications. Previous mechanistic studies have shown that CMV replication promotes inflammation via gut epithelial barrier dysfunction [17, 18] and exacerbates T-cell exhaustion [19]. Extending these findings, our study revealed a specific “inflammatory dissociation”: while generic inflammatory markers like CRP significantly decreased even in CMV Non-Responders (p = 0.002), systemic TNF-α levels failed to resolve in this group. This suggests that while ART effectively dampens generalized acute-phase inflammation (CRP), CMV acts as a specific driver for the TNF-α pathway, which is independently linked to endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular risk [9, 11].

Crucially, our longitudinal analysis unveiled that CMV clearance is tightly linked to the efficacy of HIV viral suppression and broader immune reconstitution. Patients with effective CMV clearance (Responders) achieved a significant reduction in HIV viral load (p = 0.004). In stark contrast, Non-Responders failed to achieve statistically significant HIV suppression (p = 0.389), suggesting that persistent CMV co-infection may contribute to the maintenance of the HIV reservoir or “low-level viremia,” a known predictor of virologic failure [20]. Furthermore, while both groups achieved significant recovery in absolute CD4 + T-cell counts (p < 0.01 for both), indicating that ART effectively restores T-cell quantity, significant recovery of B-cells (CD19 + count) was observed only in the CMV Responder group (p = 0.004). This implies that CMV persistence imposes a broad suppressive effect on the adaptive immune system, hindering not just T-cell quality but also B-cell reconstitution [21].

The persistence of inflammation despite ART highlights the limitations of current therapies. As noted by Franzese et al., while longer duration of HIV suppression is a predictor of CD4 recovery [6], chronic inflammation can persist, accelerating age-associated comorbidities and immunosenescence [22]. The data presented herein suggest that targeted CMV suppression could be a viable strategy to mitigate this specific inflammatory risk, potentially reducing the neurodegeneration [7] and vascular pathologies frequently associated with HIV infection.

The finding that CMV Non-Responders struggled with HIV viral suppression mirrors challenges observed in TB/HIV co-infection [12]. Furthermore, managing chronic co-infections requires strict medication adherence, a challenge highlighted by the high rate of poor adherence to anti-CMV therapy (56%) observed in our cohort. The complex interplay between viral infections, immune vulnerability, and treatment compliance extends beyond the HIV/CMV paradigm and mirrors psychological and structural challenges observed during global viral pandemics. For instance, pregnant women represent another highly vulnerable population where viral infections, such as the Zika virus (implicated in congenital Zika syndrome) [23] and SARS-CoV-2 (across its Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants) [24–26], significantly elevate systemic inflammation and the risk of adverse outcomes, including preterm birth. The fear, depression, and psychosocial distress associated with such high-risk clinical states or pandemic emergencies often negatively influence health behaviors, treatment adherence, and vaccine acceptance [27–29]. Similar to the integrated care needed for high-risk pregnancies [30], addressing the psychological and structural barriers in PLWH through multidisciplinary counseling is essential for optimizing long-term adherence and therapeutic outcomes [5, 31, 32]. In resource-limited settings, the adaptation of point-of-care testing [15] and digital health interventions [33, 34] could facilitate the monitoring of CMV alongside HIV. Furthermore, the integration of advanced diagnostics tools, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) [35], offers potential for characterizing CMV strains and resistance patterns [14, 36].

Future therapeutic strategies could be enhanced by the integration of “shock and kill” strategies targeting latent HIV reservoirs [37, 38], as reducing the cumulative viral burden—encompassing both HIV and CMV—is critical to advancing toward a functional cure. Additionally, given the substantial psychological burden associated with co-morbidities [39], patient-centered care models that address both viral suppression and health-related quality of life [40, 41] are indispensable.

Several limitations to this study must be acknowledged. First, the observational nature of the data precludes definitive determinations of causality. It remains highly plausible that bidirectional relationships exist; while CMV DNAemia may exacerbate inflammation, severe localized inflammation and immune exhaustion inherently facilitate CMV reactivation. Second, CMV viral load was quantified in copies/mL based on historical local protocols rather than the currently standardized IU/mL, which may limit the direct comparability of absolute viral thresholds with newer international studies. Third, as noted, adherence to anti-CMV therapy was variable, and the exact duration and type of antivirals (oral vs. intravenous) were not strictly standardized, introducing treatment heterogeneity. Finally, while multivariable regression was employed, unmeasured confounders inherent to advanced HIV populations, such as concurrent subclinical opportunistic infections, may still exist.

In conclusion, effective management of HIV/AIDS requires a treatment paradigm that extends beyond HIV viral load suppression. Targeting CMV co-infection is critical to mitigating the TNF-α inflammatory axis, facilitating B-cell recovery, and ensuring optimal HIV viral suppression, thereby alleviating the burden of non-AIDS comorbidities in the modern ART era.
