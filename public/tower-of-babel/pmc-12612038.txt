Core-shell silica and fluorogenic hyaluronan nanomaterials in magnesium hydroxyapatite scaffolds for bone regeneration
Authors: Giovanna Calabrese, Damiano Genovese, Dario Morganti, Maria Giovanna Rizzo, Emanuele Luigi Sciuto, Giuseppe Nicotra, Barbara Fazio, Riccardo Bendoni, Stefano Squarzoni, Luca Prodi, Sabrina Conoci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanomaterials represents a class of multifunctional materials that can play a key role in multiple applications of biomedicine. In case of severe bone defects due to trauma, cancer or aging, a huge demand for more effective treatments is one of the important challenges in clinical practice. Biomaterials for bone tissue engineering (BTE) is one of the appealing approach to enhance regenerative capability by combining several biocomponents like biomimetic materials and nanomaterials, cells, growth factors. Hydroxyapatite (HA) represents the most used based biomaterial for bone regeneration. However, there is still a crucial demand for new HA-based biomaterials satisfying clinical needs in terms of improved osteointegration and osteoregeneration. Here we show the synergic application of two nanomaterials, i.e., a fluorogenic hyaluronan nanogel (hyaluronic acid with Rhodamine B, HyRB) and a class of theranostic core–shell silica nanoparticles (PluSNPs) integrated into MgHA-type I collagen-based scaffolds. This novel biomaterial (HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA) showed improved performance in osteoregeneration, both for osteoconductivity and osteoinductivity; furthermore, the HyRB/PluSNPs nanomaterials can act as contrast agent for fluorescence imaging and have a potential for targeted drug delivery and for phototherapy. Results pave the way for the development of new multifunctional biomaterials combining the advantages of improved osteoconductivity/osteoinductivity of HyRB/PluSNPs with their potential drug delivery functionality and phototherapeutic properties.Subject terms: Cell growth, Biomaterials, Health care, Biomaterials, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

Bone-related diseases like trauma, bone tumors, osteomyelitis and other bone disorders cause significant mortality and morbidity1,2. The treatments of these diseases include several approaches to repair the damage and improve the capability of the new bone tissue growth and formation. The use of implantable bio-materials is required in case of large bone defects due to the limited self-repair capability of the bone tissue1. In this context, biomaterials combining several biocomponents like biomimetic materials and nanomaterials, cells, growth factors to promote regenerative capability represent an appealing approach for bone tissue engineering (BTE). The distinctive ability of BTE is the use of three-dimension scaffolds able to mimic the native tissue for the cells attachment, growth and differentiation3–6. Biomaterials for BTE must feature specific multiple functionalities for an efficacious tissue integration including biocompatibility, biodegradability, proper surface properties and porosity to induce cellular proliferation and differentiation through the ability to recruit osteoprogenitors, providing a precise release of differentiation signals7,8.

Among biomaterials, hydroxyapatite (HA) represents one of the most effective bone regenerative materials due to both its osteoconductive and osteoinductive properties9. Composites including hydroxyapatite and collagen (HA/Col) are widely used biomaterials in BTE since their structural and compositional features mimic the natural bone. HA is the main mineral phase providing the bone rigidity 10,11, while the collagen protein constitutes its organic phase reinforcing HA and increasing the tensile strength. Based on that, a plethora of HA/Col composite materials have been developed exhibiting several requirements typical of BTE scaffolds, such as biocompatibility, poor inflammatory activity and good biodegradability12. However, although HA/Col composite materials show these interesting properties, in the last years it has been demonstrated that their applicability in bone graft is limited due to their poor osteoinductivity and its structural fragility13. Therefore, further improvements are necessary to obtain HA/Col based materials able to mainly improve osteo-conductive/inductive properties maintaining their biocompatibility and an adequate bioresorbability.

To address these needs, nanotechnologies can play a crucial role, offering both the opportunity to produce supports with similar structure and size than the native bone and also the possibility to improve scaffold properties by surface functionalization with specific nanoparticles14,15. To date, nanomaterials have acquired a great interest in a wide range of biomedical applications, including antibacterial, anticancer drug/gene delivery, cell imaging and biosensing due to their nanoscale size and prominent biological and physicochemical features16,17.

Here we show the synergic application of two nanomaterials, i.e., a fluorogenic hyaluronan nanogel (hyaluronic acid with Rhodamine B, HyRB) and a class of core–shell silica nanoparticles (NPs) integrated into MgHA-type I collagen-based scaffolds to generate a novel biomaterial with improved performance in osteoregeneration and imaging. The two nanomaterials (HyRB and NPs) have been demonstrated to intimately interact, with a synergic role for bioavailability and cell internalization18 and to be valuable and versatile platforms for drug delivery19 and actively targeted theranostics20,21. The advantages in using these nanomaterials for bone regeneration are multiple. It has been proved that HyRB are biocompatible and biodegradable since HA is a natural extracellular matrix component in bone and supports adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation22. Furthermore, HA-based nanogels enhance mineralization and improve bone density, especially when used as delivery vehicles for osteogenic growth factors23. Additionally, silica nanoparticles have been found to promote osteogenic differentiation trough the release of bioactive ions. In fact the dissolution of silica NPs in physiological environments releases orthosilicic acid (Si(OH)₄), a bioavailable form of silicon. This chemical species plays a direct role in stimulating osteoblast proliferation, Type I collagen production, Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, Calcium deposition and mineralization24. Moreover, functionalized silica–PEG nanoparticles have optimal size (~ 25 nm) to enable efficient interaction with bone cells and good penetration into porous bone structures25 and they show enhanced blood vessel formation and integration in bone defects26. Finally, The fluorescence properties of HyRB and NPs allow for the real-time monitoring of their distribution at the microscale revealing an easy, stable and effective scaffold coating. The prepared bioscaffolds were characterised by SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy), confocal fluorescence microscopy and fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) and tested in terms of both osteoconductivity and osteoinductivity by in vitro studies using hADSC (human Adipose Derived Mesenchymal Stem) cells.

MgHA scaffolds were treated with HyRB and core–shell silica NPs nanomaterials (see Material and Methods section) to obtain four new bioscaffolds consisting of (a) PluSNPs_MgHA, (b), Hy_MgHA (c) HyRB_MgHA and (d) HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA (Fig. 3, Panel A). Bioscaffolds were characterized by means of fluorescence-based techniques to assess the efficacy of the surface coating procedure. The tested nanomaterials, i.e., HyRB, PluSNPs and the adduct of these two materials HyRB/PluSNPs, gave a significant pink coloration to the scaffolds, which was clearly visible by naked eye (Fig. 3, panel A). A similar coloration could be seen under UV lamp, and we proceeded with investigating the in-depth penetration of the coating materials by confocal microscopy. Z-stacks of the scaffolds treated with HyRB, PluSNPs and HyRB/PluSNPs revealed that fluorescence can be found not only at the most superficial layer of the scaffold, but even at several microns of depth (Fig. 3 panel B, Figures S1–S3). Below 15 microns in depth direct fluorescence imaging was not possible due to scattering from the intermediate layers.

The fluorescence lifetime of the rhodamine-based nanomaterials in aqueous solution and after their integration onto the MgHA scaffolds was evaluated (Fig. 4). The FLIM images show that the lifetime is quite homogenous throughout the scaffold, without “islands” with markedly different lifetime throughout the scaffold (Fig. 4a). Rhodamine NPs, as expected, did not show significant variations of the emission decay upon adsorption onto the scaffolds (PluSNPs_MgHA), owing to the fact that the rhodamine dyes are buried inside the silica nanoparticle and their photophysical properties – and, specifically, their lifetime—are thus not affected by the environment surrounding the PluSNPs. The measured lifetime for PluSNPsR is 2.72 ns in PBS solution and 2.91 ns when PluSNPs are adsorbed on the MgHA scaffold (intensity averaged mean lifetime). On the contrary, HyRB (whose rhodamine B moieties are heavily quenched in solution) is very sensitive to the environment, and indeed it shows a very relevant enhancement of fluorescence lifetime from 1.38 to 2.93 ns upon adsorption on the MgHA scaffolds (HyRB_MgHA), which can be explained by the conformational change of the HyRB polymer chains, as previously observed for similar surface adsorption processes18,30. Indeed, owing to the favourable interaction with the MgHA scaffolds, rhodamine dyes are free to disentangle, releasing the π-π stacking interactions responsible for their self-quenching. This switch-on mechanism is well supported by the observed lifetime enhancement, resulting in the strong fluorescence intensity observed when coating the MgHA scaffolds to form HyRB_MgHA (Fig. 4b, Table 3). Interestingly, the adduct of these two materials (PluSNPs and HyRB) adsorbed onto the scaffold (HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA) presents a shorter average lifetime (from 1.63 ns in PBS to 1.35 ns upon adsorption onto the scaffold), indicating that HyRB is more massively packed onto the scaffold in presence of NPs. This can be explained with the onset of multiple layers of HyRB/PluSNPs into the scaffold which pack HyRB close together favouring the self-quenching of RB dyes. (Fig. 4b, Table 3). The higher concentration of the rhodamine dye, on the other hand, counterbalance the observed quenching, so that the intensity observed onto the confocal stacks is equal or even larger compared with the intensity recorded in confocal images of MgHA scaffolds with HyRB alone, despite the shorter lifetime.

The analysis of FLIM images shows therefore that NPs have a relevant role in organizing the HyRB polymer chains onto the MgHA scaffolds, improving their packing and enhancing their local density.

In order to assess the biocompatibility and the osteo-conductivity/inductivity of the HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffolds, hADSCs were cultured on scaffold surfaces and their viability at several timepoints (1, 7, 14, and 24 days) were analysed by MTS assay. Results are reported in Fig. 5 panel A showing that the cell number increases in all new bioscaffolds compared to the control over time from D1 to D14, while at D24 the cell proliferation decreases comparable to the control due to the increasing of the cell differentiation. More in details, MTS values at D14 (maximum peak of proliferation) were 32.5% higher for PluSNPs_MgHA, 25.3% higher for Hy_MgHA respect to the control. An even higher enhancement, compared to the control, were found for both HyRB_MgHA and PluSNPs@HyRB corresponding to 48.4% and 44.8%, respectively. These results suggest that all HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffolds are biocompatible and able to enhance hADSCs proliferation in statistically significant manner compared to the control; notably, both HyRB_MgHA and HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA increase cell viability more than scaffolds coated with only NPs or native Hy, highlighting their relevant osteoconductive properties, while there is no appreciable difference between MgHA scaffolds coated with HyRB respect to HyRB/PluSNPs.

To further evaluate the biocompatibility and ability of seeded cells to adhere, penetrate and propagate into scaffolds, we performed H&E staining at several time points of osteogenic differentiation. Results reported in Fig. 5 panel B and C, after 24 days, showed a very low presence of cells in the MgHA scaffolds (control), whereas in all the MgHA functionalized scaffolds, and particularly in HyRB_MgHA and HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA, hADSCs display very high ability to penetrate, invade the whole scaffold and proliferate. Data collected by both MTS and H&E analyses suggest that both HyRB and NPs@HyRB functionalizations of MgHA scaffolds significantly increase the osteoconductive ability, in terms of cell adhesion and proliferation, compared with the control.

In addition to the biocompatibility and osteconductivity, we also evaluated the new biomaterial ability to induce osteogenic differentiation. The extracellular matrix mineralization is a main feature of the osteogenic differentiation process and the formation of calcium deposits can be evaluated by AR S staining. To this aim, we performed AR S staining after 1, 7, 14 and 24 days of osteogenic induction. Compared to the control MgHA scaffold at day 1 (D1) only HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA scaffolds show a significant increase of mineralization, whereas from D7 to D24 also Hy_MgHA and HyRB_MgHA significantly increased calcium deposit formation. On the contrary, PluSNPs_MgHA scaffold displays the same levels of calcium deposit than the control MgHA scaffold (Fig. 6 panel A–C), therefore proving—in agreement with osteoconductive assays—that NPs alone do not feature remarkable osteoregeneration properties. The synergy between NPs and HyRB appears evident in these osteoinductivity studies: while HyRB displayed its efficient osteoinductive properties with or without the contribution of NPs, here we observe that the osteoinductivity of HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA—already evident compared to the blanks—is enormously improved by the synergistic role of NPs, that are able to increase the local density and packing of HyRB polymer chains.

Discussion

The effect of hyaluronic acid on in vitro osteoconduction is described in many studies reported in the literature in terms of cell viability and proliferation31,32. Actually, it has a ubiquitous distribution in vertebrate tissues and it is involved in many regenerative processes, such as morphogenesis, osteo-conduction/-induction and wound healing33,34. In addition, also some silica NPs have been demonstrated to improve the osteo-regeneration process35,36, while Pluronic-silica NPs were proven to intimately interact with HyRB, also displaying synergistic cell internalization18, and the same NPs were also known to possess good drug delivery abilities19. In the light of that, our strategy is based on the combination of HyRB and Pluronic-silica NPs systems functionalized with RB to develop new MgHA-based bioscaffolds with enhanced properties in bone-repair processes. The idea was to create a synergistic effect of the two biomaterials to obtain improved cell adhesion, viability, proliferation, osteogenic differentiation and osteointegration, compatible also for future use in drug delivery and phototherapeutic treatment systems19,37,38.

In terms of microstructure, our results showed that all functionalizations (HyRB, PluSNPs and HyRB/PluSNPs) give a uniform coating and penetration into the bioscaffold without any morphological and microstructural modification of the native MgHA scaffold (Fig. 8).

Concerning the osteoregenerative properties, both HyRB or HyRB/PluSNPs functionalization give a relevant improvement in the cell viability and proliferation (48.4% for HyRB and 44.8% for HyRB/PluSNPs compared to the control – Fig. 3). Further, when mineralization was monitored, HyRB/PluSNPs functionalization provided by far the best result (59.8% compared to the control – Fig. 4), outperforming not only the control, native Hy and PluSNPs alone, but also the HyRB in absence of PluSNPs, revealing the synergistic effect of PluSNPs and HyRB in cell differentiation onto MgHA scaffolds. This remarkable synergy was confirmed by ALP analysis that show an increase of 32.5% compared to the control (Fig. 5). These results can be explained with the enhanced local density and packing of HyRB due to the presence of NPs, that aid the deposition in multiple layers of HyRB onto the MgHA scaffolds, as witnessed by the photophysical analysis of FLIM images.

Additionally, in analogy with what observed in previous study19 the core–shell silica nanoparticles (NPs) can enhance the effect of HyRB by promoting its cell internalization; furthermore, NPs may interact with the micropores of the native scaffold and act as nucleation sites for mineralization30.

The key novelty of these nanogels lies in their unique single-component design, which eliminates the need for co-polymers or surfactants, thereby simplifying the regulatory translation process. These nanogels are formed through the self-assembly of hyaluronic acid (Hy), resulting in biomimetic structures with intrinsic cell-targeting properties mediated by CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed on various cell types, including cancer and stem cells. One of the most remarkable features is the long intracellular retention of fluorescence, which has been confirmed by microscopy for up to 72 h post-incubation. In addition to their imaging capabilities, HyRB nanogels exhibit a superior biosafety profile, showing no cytotoxic effects or alterations in cell morphology and proliferation, as demonstrated in vitro on human cell lines. They also show outstanding stability in a variety of biological media, maintaining their dispersion without aggregation even in the presence of serum or high ionic strength solutions — an advantage over many polymeric or lipid-based formulations. These features are enabled by a one-step, reproducible synthesis in DMSO, which yields highly water-dispersible nanogels. Furthermore, the HyRB nanogels demonstrate excellent colloidal stability, efficient cellular uptake, and strong, persistent intracellular fluorescence, making them a highly promising platform for bioimaging and targeted delivery applications.

Therefore, we can reasonably attribute the excellent improvement of osteoconductivity and osteo-inductivity of HyRB_MgHA and HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA bioscaffolds to the synergic effect Hy-RB and PluSNPs that promote enhancement of the bone repair processes. Additionally, the presence of RB can be used as tracking molecules for future in vitro and in vivo studies to gain insight on regenerative processes, while the presence of multifunctional NPs can make this novel biomaterial ready for drug delivery and phototherapeutic perspectives.

Methods

Preparation of fluorogenic HyRB nanogels

HyRB nanogels are obtained via reaction of hyaluronic acid (10 mg, 193 ± 2 KDa) with Rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RITC, 1 mg) in 10 mL DMSO, which proceeds through nucleophilic attack by the methyl-hydroxyl group of the polysaccharide to the reactive isothiocyanate groups, as previously reported18. The initially insoluble Hy is progressively dispersed during the reaction in the DMSO solvent, and a homogeneous transparent dispersion is obtained after few hours. The reaction proceeds for 24 h at room temperature, and the resulting dispersion is dialyzed against water, to obtain a non-turbid, concentrated, aqueous dispersion of HyRB nanogels. After work-up, the dye doping degree was evaluated by UV–vis analysis measuring the absorbance of Rhodamine B in ethanol, while the weight of the lyophilized product matched with the starting amount of Hy, indicating that no significant loss of materials occurred during synthesis and work-up, except for the unreacted RB. Effective derivatization degree amounted to 1 fluorophore each 25 Hy repeat units, i.e., about 19 dyes per polymer chain.

In agreement with what previously observed18, HyRB nanogels at 100 nM concentration in PBS feature hydrodynamic diameter dH = 150 ± 35 nm with PdI = 0.255 (from DLS measurements) and a negative zeta-potential of − 17 ± 2 mV. Absorption spectrum of HyRB in water reveals a low value of the peak over shoulder ratio indicating that RB dyes are heavily aggregated at the ground state. The aggregation is also confirmed by the very low emission quantum yield (0.019) compared to the one of RB in water (0.30).

Preparation of fluorescent PluS NPs

Pluronic-Silica (PluS) NPs are very small nanoparticles with a core–shell morphology, featuring a silica core and a polyethylene glycol (PEG) shell providing a total hydrodynamic diameter of 25 nm due to the Pluronic F127 surfactant used during the template synthesis, and a zeta-potential of − 10 ± 4 mV18. The silica core is doped with a silanized Rhodamine B derivative in concentration 0.5% mol/mol of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS). The dye was chosen to avoid overlapping with other cell stains such as DAPI. This type of NPs is already well known to interact with high affinity with HyRB, due to the amphiphilic polymeric PEG shell, yielding a NPs@HyRB adduct18,30.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive x-ray (EDX) spectroscopy

TEM/EDX measurements were performed by depositing HyRB- and HyRB/PluSNPs-based suspensions (2µL) onto a copper grid with a lacey carbon support film. The samples were vacuum dried overnight before the analysis.

A JEOL ARM200F equipped with a Cold Field Emission Electron Gun (CFEG) and a spherical aberration corrector providing a spatial resolution of 0.6 Å was used for the acquisition of the HR-TEM images. Chemical analysis was performed by using a 100 mm2 Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector.

Photoluminescence and Raman spectroscopy characterization

The PL and Raman measurements were performed on solid state by drop casting 10 µL of the HyRB-, PluSNPs-, and HyRB/PluSNPs-based suspensions onto a calcium fluoride (CaF2) slide and vacuum dried for 1 h. As for spectra acquisition, an iHR550 Spectrometer (HORIBA) coupled to an Olympus microscope, equipped with Super-Head for selection of the 473 nm laser line (solid state COBOLT) was used. The measurements were performed by focusing the 1 μW of the laser light with a 100X objective. The instrument is equipped with a CCD Syncerity model (HORIBA).

MgHA scaffolds coating and characterization

To prepare HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffolds we have immersed the porous MgHA scaffolds in PBS solutions of HyRB, PluSNPs, HyRB/PluSNPs, native hyaluronan and pure PBS (as controls). The concentration of HyRB, native hyaluronan, and PluSNPs were 2 micromolar (concentration of polymer chains or NPs, respectively). The scaffolds were soaked for 36 h, after which they were transferred to deionized water, gently stirred for 15 min, and rinsed with water. These cleaning and desorption steps were repeated three times, in order to desorb the weakly adsorbed material from the scaffold surface (some weak pink colouring was only observed at the first rinsing step). After work-up, scaffolds were dried under vacuum and stored for following characterization and experiments. The final prepared bioscaffolds were: (a) PluSNPs_MgHA, (b), Hy_MgHA (c) HyRB_MgHA and (d) HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA.

The presence of HyRB, PluSNPs and HyRB/PluSNPs on the scaffold surface was evident to the naked eye due to the strong pink coloration of these scaffolds (Fig. 3A) and was further characterized via confocal fluorescence microscopy and fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM).

Cell culture and proliferation analysis through MTS assay.

The cell line used in this work, hADSCs was acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (NYSE: TMO), cultured in MesenPRO RS™ Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NYSE: TMO) supplemented with 2.5 mM L-glutamine (Merck Life Science S.r.l., Milan, Italy), 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Merck Life Science S.r.l., Milan, Italy), 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin (PSA, Merck Life Science S.r.l., Milan, Italy) and incubated in a humidified environment at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 95% air atmosphere, as previously reported39. The medium was replaced twice a week, and cells were split at about 80% of confluence.

For MTS assay, 1 × 106 cell were plated on different functionalised MgHA scaffolds (Hy_MgHA, HyRB_MgHA, PluSNPs_MgHA, HyRB/PluSNPs_MgHA) in culture medium and incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 1, 7, 14 and 24 days (D1, D7, D14 and D24). After MTS reagent was added to cell culture media, incubated for 1 h in standard culture conditions, the plate was shacked briefly and absorbance measured at 490 nm.

The proliferation rates differences were assessed using One-way ANOVA test with Holm test as post-hoc for multiple comparisons and for each timepoints three parallel samples were analysed.

Histological analysis and Alizarin Red S staining of hADSCs on functionalized MgHA scaffolds after osteogenic differentiation

For osteogenic differentiation, 1 × 106 hADSCs suspended in 50 ml of MesenPRO RSTM medium were gradually drop seeded onto the functionalised MgHA scaffolds and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Successively, MesenPRO RSTM medium was added and the day after (day 0) replaced with osteogenic differentiation medium (StemPROTM osteogenesis differentiation kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NYSE:TMO). The osteogenic differentiation medium was totally replaced twice a week. Scaffolds have been analysed at different timepoints of osteogenic induction by histological analysis and alizarin red S (AR S) staining.

For both histological analysis and AR S staining functionalized MgHA scaffolds with hADSCs were fixed in 4% PFA (Paraformaldehyde), dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, cut into 5-mm-thick sections and mounted on slides. After, alternate sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and stained with either haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or AR S (Panreac, Castellar del Valles, Barcellona, Spain) as described previously37. H&E stained sections were analysed by using a Leica DMI 4000B microscope (Leica Microsystems Srl, Milano, Italy) and the stained nuclei of at least five sections/scaffold were counted by using Fiji Image J recognition software (ImageJ2 version:2.9.0/1.53t; Open source image processing software, http://imagej.net/Contributors, accessed on 19 February 2023).

For AR S staining, the solution was prepared according to the manufacturer protocol, added to the sections and incubated for 5 min. After the incubation, sections were washed several times to get rid of the excess of the staining solution and lastly, mounted. Stained sections were analysed by using a Leica DMI 4000B microscope. At least five sections/scaffold were analysed, and optical density was quantified using Fiji image J software.

ALP activity evaluation

ALP (Alkaline phosphatase) activity was evaluated by colorimetric assay performed on cell culture supernatants. Specifically, 1 × 106 hADSCs were cultured on each functionalised MgHA scaffold for 1, 7, 14 and 24 days and culture media collected and used to measure ALP activity. This was enzymatically determined using an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (ab83369, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ALP level was measured by reading the absorbance at 405 nm by a Microplate Reader (Bio-tek, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

DLS and Z-potential

The determination of the NP hydrodynamic diameter distributions was carried out through DLS measurements employing a Malvern Nano ZS instrument with a 633 nm laser diode. Samples were housed in disposable polystyrene cuvettes of 1 cm optical path length using water as solvent. The width of DLS hydrodynamic diameter distribution is indicated by PdI (polydispersion index). In the case of a monomodal distribution (Gaussian) calculated by means of cumulant analysis, PdI = (σ/Zavg)2, where σ is the width of the distribution and Zavg is average diameter of the particles population, respectively.

Morphological characterization of functionalized HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffolds after osteogenic differentiation

The morphological and microstructural analysis of functionalized MgHA scaffolds with hADSCs was assessed by SEM by both a Philips XL 30 FEI SEM (Philips, Amsterdam) and a Zeiss EVO MA10 (Zeiss international, Headquartered in Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an LaB6 electron gun, at the operating voltage of 20 kV. Three SEM micrographs were analyzed by image J software.

Confocal images were acquired on a Nikon A1R microscope with 20 × objective, an excitation laser at 561 nm and a GaAsP PMT with emission filter at 595/50 nm. The acquisition parameters were set to 1 A.U., 8 × Signal Average, Galvano Scanning Mode, Laser Power = 0.46%, PMT GaAsP HV = 44. This setting was kept the same for all the samples analysed.

FLIM images were acquired with a time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) system of Picoquant GmbH Berlin, using a 405 nm pulsed excitation laser at 10 MHz repetition rate, a 560 nm long-pass emission filter, a Hybrid PMA detector and a Picoquant PicoHarp 300 correlation board. FLIM images were then analysed with SymPhoTime 64, PicoQuant GmbH.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed either as raw data or as mean ± standard error (SE), as appropriate. Differences between several timepoints of functionalised MgHA scaffolds with hADSCs were evaluated by using multi-way ANOVA with a post hoc Holm test, where appropriate. p < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed new magnesium hydroxyapatite (MgHA) and type I collagen (ColI) scaffolds with the integration of fluorogenic hyaluronan nanogel (HyRB) and a class of core–shell silica nanoparticles (NPs). The silica NPs structure has been assessed by TEM showing a dimension of about 6 nm in diameter. Raman spectroscopy investigation of HyRB well highlights the RB chemical fingerprint confirming the effective functionalization of Hy. The morphological and structural features, evaluated by SEM and fluorescence microscopy shown uniform integration of the fluorescent nanomaterials with lifetime quite homogenous throughout the scaffold. In vitro studies using hADSC cells highlights new bioscaffolds are biocompatible and able to enhance the adhesion, propagation and proliferation of seeded cells. Among all, both HyRB or HyRB/PluSNPs functionalization give a relevant improvement in the cell viability and proliferation (48.4% and 44.8% higher for HyRB and HyRB/PluSNPs compared to the control). The evaluation of matrix mineralization and Alkaline phosphatase activity show an improved osteogenic differentiation ability of functionalized MgHA scaffolds compared to the control. In this case, the HyRB/PluSNPs functionalization outperforms single component functionalization (HyRB or PluSNPs) and give the best result (59.8% compared to the control), revealing a substantial synergy between the two nanomaterials here investigated. In addition, the morphological evaluation of HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffolds confirm that the coating treatment and rinsing steps do not affect the 3D structure and integrity of the scaffold, and that the coating layers do not change the overall microscopic morphology of the MgHA scaffolds.

These HyRB/PluSNPs doped MgHA bioscaffold pave the way for the development of new multifunctional biomaterials combining the advantages of improved osteo-conductivity/-inductivity with drug delivery functionality and photothermic properties, given by the versatile and easy coating with nanomaterials.
