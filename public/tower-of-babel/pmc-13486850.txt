Ultrasensitive serum interferon alpha equals interferon signatures as a marker of lupus disease activity
Authors: Marta Rodríguez-García, Fuensanta Gómez-Bernal, Juan Carlo Quevedo-Abeledo, Beatriz Tejera-Segura, Enrique García-Barrera, Luisa María Villar, Javier Gonzalo Ocejo-Vinyals, Nerea Mota-Pérez, Raquel Largo, Miguel Ángel González-Gay, Iván Ferraz-Amaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryType 1 interferon-alpha gene expression signatures have long served as surrogate markers of pathway activity in systemic lupus erythematosus, but recent ultrasensitive assays now allow direct quantification of circulating interferon-alpha. In this cross-sectional study of 252 patients with systemic lupus erythematosus, serum interferon-alpha was measured by single molecule array, and 51 interferon scores were derived from 72 transcripts analyzed by Nanostring. Both serum interferon-alpha and interferon scores showed only modest associations with disease features, but serum interferon-alpha correlated more strongly with disease activity than most interferon scores and better discriminated remission and low disease activity states. These findings indicate that direct measurement of circulating interferon-alpha may provide clinically useful information and could be at least comparable to, and in some respects more informative than, interferon signatures for assessing disease status.Keywords: Interferon-α, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Interferon signature, Interferon score

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) represents a complex autoimmune condition driven by genetic and environmental interactions, characterized by diverse organ involvement including cutaneous, renal, musculoskeletal, vascular, and neuropsychiatric manifestations. This clinical and serological heterogeneity—predominantly affecting women—complicates timely diagnosis, activity assessment, and personalized treatment. Thus, discovery of reliable immunological biomarkers remains crucial for enhanced disease monitoring and prognostic stratification.1

A hallmark of SLE immunopathogenesis is the dysregulated response to nuclear antigens and associated proteins, triggering activation across innate and adaptive immune compartments. This dysregulated activation drives robust complement consumption alongside persistent production of multiple cytokines, notably interferons (IFNs).2 In this regard, SLE patients consistently demonstrate elevated serum IFN levels,3 particularly IFN-α, with plasma IFN-α concentrations directly correlating with disease activity.4,5 Besides, in SLE, IFN-α—but not IFN-γ—shows significant associations with inflammatory markers, disease activity/remission indices, and autoantibody profiles.6

Quantifying serum IFN levels has historically proven difficult given their sub-picomolar concentrations, typically in the femtogram/mL range.7 Standard ELISA platforms lack sufficient sensitivity for reliable detection. Recently developed ultrasensitive assays like Simoa (single molecule array) now enable IFN-α measurement in serum/plasma at attomolar levels—a 1,000–5,000-fold sensitivity gain over conventional ELISAs.8,9 However, these ultrasensitive IFN-α assays remain costly and have limited accessibility. For this reason, IFN activity is most assessed indirectly through the evaluation of the so-called “IFN signature.” The IFN-signature refers to a characteristic pattern of gene expression induced by type 1 IFNs, particularly IFN-α, reflecting the upregulation of a specific set of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs). The levels of these ISGs are combined into a single summary value called “IFN score,” typically obtained through standardized summation of expression values, scaled medians, or principal components, providing a continuous measure of type 1 IFN pathway activity in each patient. IFN signature scores are routinely utilized in SLE clinical trials and longitudinal studies to monitor therapeutic responses and serve as surrogate measures of type 1 IFN pathway activation.10

Serum IFN-α measurement may offer several advantages over IFN-signature assessment. For example, direct quantification of the cytokine provides actual circulating levels, circumventing indirect ISG-based interpretation potentially confounded by non-IFN factors. Also, ultrasensitive platforms enable precise detection at femtogram/mL concentrations. Besides, protein immunoassays are rapid, highly standardized for clinical labs, and readily implementable—unlike IFN-scores, which lack standardization and vary across studies by ISG panel selection.11,12

In this study, we used ultrasensitive Simoa assays to quantify serum IFN-α in a large and well-characterized SLE cohort. We measured RNA expression of 72 ISGs previously described for IFN signature calculation for the computation of 51 IFN-scores that are frequently used in the literature.11,12 We next compared the strength of correlations between serum IFN-α levels and key disease features, particularly SLE activity indices, with those obtained using IFN-signature scores.

Results

Demographics and disease-related data of SLE patients

An overview of the characteristics of the 252 patients with SLE included in the study is provided in Table 1. The median (interquartile range, IQR) serum levels of IFN-α SLE patients were 194 (37–1,385) femtogram/mL. Most of the participants were women (90%), with a mean age ±SD of 53 ± 12 years. The average body mass index was 28 ± 6 kg/m2. The average disease duration was 19 ± 11 years. Based on SLE disease activity index-2000 (SLEDAI-2K), 43% of the patients had no disease activity, and mild or moderate-high activity was present in 48% and 8% of the patients, respectively. The median SLE Disease Activity Score (SLE-DAS) index value was 1.1 (IQR 0.4–2.1). Regarding the definitions of remission and low disease activity, according to definition of remission in SLE (DORIS) and Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) criteria, 67% and 79% of patients, respectively, met these criteria. The Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics Damage Index (SLICC-DI) was 0 (IQR, 0–1) and an SLICC-DI score of 1 or higher was found in 48% of the patients.

Serum IFN-α levels showed significant, albeit modest, correlations with the three disease activity scores: SLEDAI-2K (ρ = 0.208, false discovery rate [FDR]-adjusted p = 0.001), clinical SLEDAI (ρ = 0.144, FDR p = 0.025), and SLE-DAS (ρ = 0.227, FDR p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Regarding SLEDAI, IFN-scores significantly correlated with this index. Steiger’s test revealed no significant differences between IFN-scores to SLEDAI and serum IFN-α to SLEDAI correlations, indicating comparable associations. For clinical SLEDAI-2K, serum IFN-α levels showed a significant correlation (ρ = 0.144, FDR p = 0.025), whereas none of the IFN signature scores did. Most IFN signatures showed significant associations with SLE-DAS; however, the correlation between serum IFN-α and SLE-DAS was significantly stronger than that observed for most IFN signatures. (Table 2).

Because hydroxychloroquine and prednisone use may confound the relationship between IFN-α and IFN signatures to disease activity, regression analyses were performed to address this potential effect. As shown in Table S1, log IFN-α displayed a stronger standardized association with SLE-DAS than the IFN signature. This difference was statistically significant in the unadjusted and hydroxychloroquine-adjusted models, but was attenuated and no longer significant after adjustment for prednisone.

Additionally, in Figure 1, standardized IFN-α values and IFN scores exhibited comparable trends across SLEDAI-2K, clinical SLEDAI, and SLE-DAS, showing overall modest positive associations (bivariate p values, rather than FDR-adjusted p values, are shown). Figure 2 visually summarizes the relative performance of serum IFN-α and the 51 IFN gene signatures across the four outcomes analyzed. For clinical SLEDAI-2K and SLE-DAS, serum IFN-α was among the markers with the strongest combination of effect size and statistical significance.

Comparison of the discriminative performance of IFN-α serum values and IFN-scores for LLDAS and DORIS

In this analysis, to avoid inverse area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs), LLDAS and DORIS reference category is considered low disease or remission. This mean, an AUC higher than 0.5 must be interpreted as the effect of not being in low activity or remission.

Serum IFN-α exhibited significant AUCs for the presence of non-LLDAS (AUC 0.645 [95% confidence interval, CI 0.565, 0.72]) and non-DORIS (AUC 0.590 95% CI [0.513, 0.669]) (Table 3). All 51 IFN scores showed statistically significant AUCs for the presence of non-LLDAS, but these did not differ from the reference AUC calculated for serum IFN-α. The IFN scores were not significantly associated with non-DORIS status, and their AUCs did not differ from that of serum IFN-α (Table 3).

Relationship of circulating IFN-α and IFN-score with SLE serological markers

To further explore the relationship between the two IFN-related readouts and serological activity, we compared serum IFN-α values and IFN scores across major autoantibody subsets and according to complement levels (Figure 3). For this purpose, the IFN score was computed using the ISG combination (IFI44L, IFIT1, RSAD2, IFI27, ISG15, SIGLEC1) that demonstrated the strongest correlation with serum IFN-α levels. Both markers showed a broadly similar distribution across autoantibody-defined groups. Patients positive for anti-dsDNA, anti-ENA, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, or anti-Sm tended to display higher IFN-related values than seronegative patients, although the magnitude and statistical strength of these differences varied across autoantibody specificities. Overall, the pattern was concordant for circulating IFN-α and IFN-score, indicating that both measures capture a similar biological signal across serological subsets.

Discussion

Our study is among the first to demonstrate comparable, and potentially superior, associations of direct serum IFN-α measurement versus IFN signatures with disease features in patients with SLE. Notably, given the lack of consensus on the optimal ISG combinations for IFN score calculation, we performed a comprehensive comparison across 51 published IFN signatures. Based on our findings and the variability inherent in ISG-based IFN signature determination, we propose that IFN pathway activity in SLE should be assessed using direct serum IFN-α measurements rather than ISG-based scores, which are less standardized and technically more complex.

In our study, the majority of the 51 evaluated IFN scores showed strong correlations with circulating IFN-α levels, with most exceeding Spearman’s ρ > 0.80. This robust association supports the prior literature-based selection of these ISG combinations as reliable surrogate markers of IFN-α pathway activation.10

Serum IFN-α levels significantly correlated with SLEDAI-2K, clinical SLEDAI, SLE-DAS, LLDAS, and DORIS status. These associations were modest but statistically significant, indicating that these indices capture disease aspects not fully reflected by serum IFN-α levels. In contrast, IFN signatures were associated with SLE-DAS and LLDAS but not with SLEDAI-2K or DORIS, indicating that serum IFN-α may show stronger relationships with disease indices than multi-ISG RNA-based signatures. Direct comparisons confirmed that the correlation between IFN-α and SLE-DAS was stronger than that of IFN signatures with SLE-DAS, while correlations with other indices were comparable. In the absence of validated ISG combinations for IFN signatures and given the standardized, low-variability measurement of serum IFN-α, we propose that direct IFN-α quantification provides a superior surrogate marker of disease activity in SLE.

In our study, serum C3 and C4 complement levels showed no association with either circulating IFN-α concentrations or IFN signature expression. Although previous studies have reported inverse correlations between complement components (C1q, C3, C4) and serum IFN-α in serially sampled SLE patients,13 and mechanistic evidence links C1q deficiency to plasmacytoid dendritic cell activation and IFN-α production.14 The complement-IFN relationship in SLE may operate as an amplification cycle in which nucleic acid-containing immune complexes activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells, leading to IFN-α production; IFN-α in turn promotes B cell activation and autoantibody production, which leads to the formation of additional immune complexes, complement consumption, loss of the suppressive effect of C1q on dendritic cells, and further IFN-α production. Therefore, it would not be expected for this complex relationship to be reflected simply as a correlation between IFN and complement. Moreover, the cross-sectional design and the fact that most patients were clinically stable, sampled outside disease flares, and receiving maintenance treatment with prednisone and hydroxychloroquine, which are known to reduce IFN activity and modulate complement levels, may have attenuated the expected relationship between IFN and complement, thus contributing to the null association observed in our study.

By contrast, positivity for multiple antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), including anti double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), was significantly associated with higher serum IFN-α levels and IFN signature expression, suggesting that IFN-α likely contributes to the production of these autoantibodies. Notably, the effects of ANA positivity on both IFN signature expression and serum IFN-α levels did not differ significantly, indicating comparable relationships with both measures. Taken together, these analyses show that serum IFN-α and IFN-score provide highly concordant information in relation to autoantibody status.

In keeping with our results, a previous report by Chasset et al. found that serum IFN-α levels and a type 1 ISG score performed equally in assessing SLE disease activity.15 However, that study evaluated only a single IFN score, unlike our comprehensive analysis of multiple literature-derived scores. In addition, their cohort was smaller (n = 133 vs. our n = 252) and disease activity was assessed solely using SLEDAI-2K, without considering other indices. Our findings both corroborate and extend these results, confirming that serum IFN-α levels and IFN scores show comparable associations with disease characteristics.

In conclusion, although both IFN-α values and IFN-scores showed modest but statistically significant associations with disease features, serum IFN-α demonstrates comparable or superior correlations with disease features relative to IFN signatures. The clinical utility of these findings should be confirmed in future studies. Due to its standardized measurement and lower variability compared with ISG-based signatures, we propose that direct serum IFN-α measurement be adopted as the preferred biomarker for assessing disease activity in patients with SLE.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. We measured serum IFN-α specifically and did not assess other IFNs that may contribute to IFN pathway activation in SLE. Therefore, our findings pertain specifically to circulating IFN-α levels and may not reflect overall IFN pathway activity or other IFN subtypes. Additionally, although we compared serum IFN-α levels with 51 distinct IFN scores, we acknowledge that other literature-derived combinations may exist with potentially different correlations to IFN-α. Nevertheless, the breadth of IFN scores evaluated supports the robustness of our findings. The cross-sectional design is another limitation; therefore, causality cannot be inferred. Besides, it cannot determine whether changes in IFN-α drive disease activity or vice versa, nor whether serum IFN-α is superior to IFN scores in predicting future flares, treatment response, or organ damage. For this reason, prospective longitudinal studies are required to assess whether disease activity changes are more accurately captured by IFN scores or serum IFN-α. Furthermore, most patients in our cohort were in remission or had low disease activity, with a median disease duration of 19 ± 11 years. We believe this reflects appropriate and effective disease control in routine clinical practice, rather than selection bias. However, this limited variability in disease activity is likely to attenuate correlations with IFN-α and ISG-based scores and reduce the robustness of AUC-based discrimination analyses. Therefore, our findings may not be fully generalizable to cohorts with higher disease activity or early SLE. At the same time, this can also be considered a robustness, as the significant associations we observed were detected in patients with low disease activity, a subgroup in which weaker correlations would be expected. Also, the cohort is drawn exclusively from Spanish tertiary care centers, representing a predominantly European ancestry population. SLE disease expression, IFN pathway activation, and autoantibody profiles may differ substantially across ethnic groups. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to more ethnically diverse populations should be interpreted with caution. Besides, the absence of a control group may be considered a limitation. However, our aim was to determine whether ultrasensitive serum IFN measurement is equivalent to IFN signatures in its association with disease features in patients with SLE. Clearly, the relationship between these markers and disease characteristics could not have been assessed in a healthy population. On the other hand, a correlation between serum IFN-α and IFN signatures would be expected to also exist in healthy individuals, although this should indeed be investigated in future studies. Another limitation is that though most key findings are statistically supported, this is not sufficient to establish clinical utility. A robust biomarker should also demonstrate reproducibility, stability, cost-effectiveness, and clearly defined clinical decision thresholds. This study primarily validates the association component, but further studies are needed to address reproducibility, establish clinical decision thresholds, and evaluate cost-effectiveness before serum IFN-α, and not IFN signatures, can be recommended for routine clinical use in SLE. Lastly, although sensitivity analyses adjusted for prednisone and hydroxychloroquine were performed, residual confounding related to treatment exposure and indication cannot be completely excluded.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

This was a cross-sectional study that included 252 patients with SLE. Patient recruitment occurred from September 2023 to February 2024. The study was conducted at four tertiary hospitals in Spain: Hospital Universitario de Canarias (Approval Number CHUC-2023-48), Hospital Universitario Doctor Negrín (Approval Number 2021-023-1), Hospital Insular de Gran Canaria (Approval Number 2021-023-1), and Hospital Fundación Jiménez Díaz (Approval Number PIC135-23). The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Committees of all participating hospitals, and all participants provided written informed consent. Research was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sex and gender: Of the 252 participants, 90% were female and 10% were male. The mean age ±SD was 53 ± 12 years. Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) clinical diagnosis of SLE meeting ≥4 ACR 1997 criteria16; (3) regular rheumatology outpatient follow-up; (4) if receiving glucocorticoids, prednisone equivalent dose ≤10 mg/day. Exclusion criteria: (1) treatment with anifrolumab or other investigational biologics targeting IFN pathways or B cell-directed biologic therapy; (2) inability or refusal to provide written informed consent; (3) insufficient, hemolyzed, or improperly stored serum samples; (4) presence of overlapping systemic autoimmune disease; (5) missing key clinical or laboratory data. Of 300 initially screened patients, 252 (84.0%) were included after sequential exclusion (Figure S2).

Sample size was estimated for the comparison of two dependent correlations sharing one common variable, assuming correlation coefficients of 0.20 and 0.25, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and 80% power. Assuming a correlation of 0.85 between both biomarkers, the required sample size was approximately 250 participants.

Method details

Study design and data collection

This study is reported as per the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline. The analyses reported were pre-specified as part of the study design and detailed in the study protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board. No deviations from the initial study protocol were committed. To address potential sources of bias, several measures were implemented in the study design and analysis. First, patients were consecutively recruited from tertiary hospitals to enhance representativeness and reduce selection bias. Standardized protocols were used for data collection, including clinical assessments and laboratory measurements, to minimize measurement bias. IFN-α Simoa analysis was performed exclusively at the Hospital Ramón y Cajal, and ISGsRNA assessment were executed exclusively at Hospital Marqués de Valdecilla, to ensure consistency and reduce inter-laboratory variability, thereby maintaining biomarker stability and ensuring reliable IFN-α and RNA quantification. There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this study. All participants completed a cardiovascular risk factor and medication questionnaire and underwent a standardized physical examination including weight, height, BMI, abdominal circumference, and blood pressure (measured in the supine position). Medical records were reviewed to ascertain diagnoses and medications.

Disease activity and outcome assessment

SLE disease activity and damage were assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index-2000 (SLEDAI-2K)17 and the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology (SLICC/ACR Damage Index -SDI-),18 respectively. For the present study proposal, the SLEDAI-2k index was divided into none (0 points), mild (1–5 points), moderate (6–10 points), high (11–19) and very high activity (>20) as previously described.19 SLE Disease Activity Score (SLE-DAS) was also calculated20 and categorized as follows: remission (SLE-DAS ≤2.08), mild activity (2.08 < SLE-DAS ≤7.64), and moderate to severe activity (SLE-DAS >7.64), according to established definitions.21 In addition to the SLEDAI-2K, clinical SLEDAI was calculated by excluding laboratory-only variables (anti-dsDNA, complement levels, proteinuria, hematuria, pyuria, and urinary casts), while retaining hematologic abnormalities considered clinical manifestations. Definitions of Remission in SLE (DORIS) was based on the absence of clinical disease activity as measured by the clinical SLEDAI-2K = 0 and physician global assessment (PGA) < 0.5. The patient may be receiving antimalarials, low-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone ≤5 mg/day), and/or maintenance doses of immunosuppressive therapies.22 Similarly, Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) accepts an SLEDAI-2K ≤ 4 with no activity from major organ systems, no new clinical activity compared with the previous assessment, a PGA of ≤1, prednisone dose ≤7.5 mg/day and maintenance doses of antimalarials and immunosuppressive therapies.23Modified LLDAS was also calculated using ≤5 mg/day prednisone criteria instead of ≤7.5 mg.24

Serum IFN-α quantification (Simoa)

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels were measured using a high-sensitivity immunoassay. Serum samples were collected following standard protocols, immediately aliquoted, and frozen at −80°C without delay. Samples were stored continuously at −80°C until assay to preserve the labile nature of IFN. These procedures conform with recommended best practices to maintain the stability and reliability of IFN measurements. IFN-α was measured from serum samples using the Simoa (Single Molecule Array) IFN-α assay (IFN-αMS) Advantage PLUS KitTM (no. 103638, Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA). Sample processing and analysis were done using an HD-X analyzer (software version 4.16.2307.14001; Quanterix). All analyses were performed centrally at the Department of Immunology, Hospital Ramón y Cajal, Madrid.

RNA extraction and NanoString gene expression

Blood samples were collected in PAXgene tubes (PreAnalytix) and kept at room temperature for 2–72 h, frozen at −20 °C, and then stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated from whole blood using the PAXgene RNA isolation kit (PreAnalytix), and concentration was measured using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression was quantified with NanoString nCounter® technology. For each sample, 100 ng of RNA was hybridized to a custom probe panel (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA) at the Department of Immunology, Hospital Universitario Marqués de Valdecilla, Spain.

Expression levels of 72 ISGs, previously reported12 to be used for calculating the IFN signature score, were measured: BDCA1, BST2, CEACAM1, CMPK2, CXCL10, CXCL9, DDX60, DNAJA1, EIFAK2, EPSTI1, GBP1, GBP4, GBP5, HERC5, HERC6, IFI16, IFI27, IFI35, IFI44, IFI44L, IFI6, IFIH1, IFIT1, IFIT2, IFIT3, IFIT5, IFITM1, IFITM3, IL1RN, IL6, IRF1, IRF7, ISG15, ISG20, LAMP3, LGALS3BP, LY6E, MX1, MX2, MYD88, NC0A7, NT5C3B, OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, OASL, PARP12, PARP9, PHF11, PLSCR1, PRKR, PSMB9, RSAD2, RTP4, SAMD9L, SERPING1, SIGLEC1, SOCS1, SP100, SP140, SPATS2L, STAT1, TAP1, TNFAIP6, TNFSF10, TRIM38, TYK2, UB2L6, UNC93B1, USP18, XAF1, ZBP1 (See Table S2 for full gene name, alias, primer sequences accession number and chromosomal localization).

NanoString data normalization

Background correction: Normalization of raw counts first included background correction involved subtracting from the raw countsthe mean + 2 SD of counts obtained with negative controls (alien probes lacking spiked transcript for background calculation). Values < 1 were fixed to 1 to avoid negative values. Positive controls normalization was performed by first calculating the geometric mean of the positive controls for each individual, then computing the arithmetic mean of geometric means across all subjects, dividing this global arithmetic mean by each individual’s geometric mean to generate patients-specific normalization factors, and finally multiplying the raw counts for every gene in each subject by its corresponding normalization factor.

Housekeeping gene normalization: Gene counts were normalized to the geometric mean of 3 (ALAS1, HPRT1 and TBP) housekeeping gene counts selected as the most stable by using the geNorm algorithm25 from an original selection of 5 housekeeping genes (ALAS1, G6PD, HPRT1, POLR2A and TBP). The normalization using the previously mentioned 3 housekeeping genes was performed by first calculating the geometric mean of the selected housekeeping genes for each individual, then calculating the arithmetic mean of these geometric means across all subjects, dividing this global arithmetic mean by each patient’s geometric mean to generate a sample-specific normalization factor, and finally multiplying the raw counts of every gene by its respective subject-specific normalization factor.25,26 Log2 fold-changes were calculated at the gene level as follows. For each gene, the arithmetic mean of the log2-transformed counts in SLE patients was computed and the arithmetic mean of the log2-transformed counts in the control group was computed. The log2 fold-change for each gene was then defined as the difference between these two means, i.e., mean log2(counts) in SLE patients minus mean log2(counts) in 24 controls matched for age and sex. Theses healthy controls were used only to calculate gene-level log2 fold-changes and were not included in the primary comparative analyses. Then IFN-I gene scores (Table S3) were calculated as the arithmetic mean of the relative expression of all genes contributing to the score. None of the patients, had missing data for RNA expression.

IFN score computation

51 distinct IFN scores were computed based on ISG combinations previously reported in the literature for rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases. Each IFN-score value was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the relative expression of the component ISGs for each patient.

Other laboratory measurements

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured using a high-sensitivity immunoassay. ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-ENA, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, anti-Sm, and complement levels (C3, C4) were assessed as part of routine clinical care using standard laboratory methods at each participating center.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses used a 5% two-sided significance level and were performed using Stata software, version 17/SE (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data visualizations were performed using Julius AI (Julius AI Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA; https://julius.ai), utilizing Python 3.x.

Descriptive statistics: Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD; non-normally distributed as median (IQR); categorical as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, Q-Q plots, and histograms.

Missing data: All variables were reviewed for completeness before analysis. Cases with missing key clinical or laboratory data were excluded from specific analyses. The extent of missing data was minimal and is transparently reported.

Correlation analysis: Spearman rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were calculated to assess associations between IFN-α levels, each of the 51 IFN scores, and disease activity indices (SLEDAI-2K, clinical SLEDAI, SLE-DAS). To formally compare whether any IFN score yielded a significantly different correlation with disease activity than serum IFN-α, Steiger’s test for dependent correlations was applied, accounting for the non-independence of coefficients sharing the common biomarker variable.27

Discriminative performance: Discriminative ability for binary outcomes (DORIS remission and LLDAS status) was assessed using the AUC. Pairwise AUC comparisons between each IFN score and serum IFN-α were performed using DeLong’s method for correlated ROC curves. To avoid inverse AUCs, the reference category was set to low disease activity/remission; an AUC >0.5 reflects higher values in non-remission/non-LLDAS patients.

Multiple testing correction: p-values were adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Adjusted q-values are reported; significance threshold was FDR <0.05.

Serological marker analyses: Between-group comparisons of IFN-related variables across binary autoantibody groups used the Mann-Whitney U test. Differential associations between IFN-α and IFN-score with serological markers were evaluated using interaction models (serological variable × IFN measure type); P-interaction values tested for differential associations.

For Figure 1, both IFN-α and IFN-score were Z score standardized before comparison across disease activity strata. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for hydroxychloroquine and prednisone use were performed using regression models comparing log IFN-α and IFN signature as standardized predictors of SLE-DAS (see Table S2). The number of participants contributing to each analysis is specified in the corresponding figure legends and tables.
