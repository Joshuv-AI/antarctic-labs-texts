A qualitative narrative analysis of family carers supporting young adults in the early stages of psychosis
Authors: Kristen E. Zentner, Roxoliana Tsisar, Katherine Shettell, Adam Abba-Aji, Melanie Robles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConsiderable evidence exists to support family carers’ positive impact on the recovery of young adults with psychotic illness. Despite best practice documents that champion family inclusion, many family carers experience obstructions to their involvement in care. The current study aims to trace commonly occurring episodes in the stories of carers of young adults with psychosis to gain a deeper understanding of their narrative and identify points in their stories where intervention and system support were needed.MethodsSemi-structured interviews (n = 8) were conducted with carers who support a young adult with a psychotic illness. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed and coded using narrative analysis. Common episodes in the families’ stories were arranged into a meta-narrative, and common themes were situated under each meta-episode.ResultsMeta-episodes synthesized from the data were (1) Blurry boundary between typical and atypical development, (2) Escalation of psychiatric symptoms, (3) Crisis event, (4) Repeated interactions with the healthcare system, (5) Hospitalization, and (6) Ongoing recovery.ConclusionsFamily carers experience a tumultuous and traumatic journey through the mental health system as they grieve losses within their family, support their loved one’s recovery, and advocate for their loved one’s care. This study raises consciousness about the carers’ journey as they walk alongside a loved one with psychosis, calling on care teams to empathize with families, honour lived experience, prioritize carers as partners in care, and fortify carers with skills and education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44192-026-00548-8.

Introduction

Family carers are an essential source of day-to-day support for young adults affected by psychosis [1, 2]. These carers support their loved ones by coaching activities of daily living (e.g., organizing schedules, paying bills, preparing meals, housekeeping), facilitating treatment adherence (e.g., managing medications, monitoring symptoms), discerning situational safety (e.g., managing crises, arranging safe living conditions), and coordinating care (e.g., getting to appointments, advocating and navigating within the healthcare system) [1, 3–5]. Beyond practical support, carers provide intense and frequent emotional support to their loved ones [5]. Despite these extensive responsibilities, the carers’ critical impact on their loved ones’ lifelong personal and clinical recovery goes largely unrecognized and severely under-supported [4].

Most schizophrenia spectrum disorders have an onset in early adulthood, with symptoms most often first appearing in late teens and early twenties [6]. Young adulthood is characterized by growing autonomy, identity development, and educational and vocational pursuits, all of which impact the wellness trajectory [7, 8]. Legally, young people have full autonomy with respect to their healthcare, including the degree of family involvement, once they reach the age of majority (18 in Alberta [9]). Nevertheless, young adults in Canada remain highly dependent on their families for emotional, practical, and financial support into their mid-twenties, with the majority of 20 to 24-year-olds living with at least one parent [10, 11]. There is an abrupt shift in healthcare practice that reflects only the legal demarcation but not the gradual development of independence of young adults which occurs over time [12]. Mental illness, specifically psychosis, can create contention and distrust in family relationships as symptoms emerge, undermining the supportive function of carers [3, 13].

Impact of anosognosia and paranoia

One of the major contributors to family burden is the devastating impact that the symptoms of anosognosia and paranoia have on family relationships. Anosognosia, the neurological inability to recognize the symptoms of an illness in oneself, is present in 57% of individuals with schizophrenia. These individuals who manifest moderate to severe unawareness of their disease [14] frequently reject their diagnosis and recommended treatment, creating conflict with carers who want their loved one to accept treatment [15]. Paranoia, in addition to other frightening perceptual distortions, can cause affected young adults to assign hostile attributes to their family carers [3, 13, 16]. For example, those with paranoia exhibit social cognition biases such as interpreting carer concern as an attempt to control [16]. Although anosognosia and paranoia may occur in the context of other disorders, these symptoms are most characteristic of psychotic disorders [17, 18]. The fracture of relationships due to distrust emerging from symptoms of anosognosia and paranoia is uniquely distressing for carers of individuals with psychosis which differentiates their experiences from carers supporting loved ones with other disorders where trust and awareness of one’s illness remain relatively intact (e.g., anxiety and depressive disorders [13]). It is common for young adults with psychosis to be resistant to involving their family in their care and for their clinical team to abide by legal standards to uphold the adult’s agency and independence, resulting in limitations to family involvement in care [19–21].

Contextualizing the family role within the Canadian mental health system

The present study is situated within Alberta, Canada, where healthcare is organized provincially within a publicly funded system [22]. Access to services, early psychosis programming, information-sharing processes, and carer support varies across Alberta but families encounter common and unique system gaps as they transition between child and adult mental health systems [12, 23]. This transition gap, in combination with anosognosia and its consequences, can lead to the phenomena known as “falling through the cracks” [21, 24–26]. Programs like ACCESS: Open Minds and Foundry in Canada provide at-risk young adults with support to navigate adult mental health services during the clinically high-risk phase (a time of worsening mental health prior to escalation of psychotic symptoms) and across young adulthood [27, 28, 29]. These programs do patch some of the cracks in the system, but do not provide the fluidity in care continuity that is needed [27].

A triad of care, formed by a young adult affected by psychosis, their family carer(s), and their care team, creates a strong foundation for recovery that bridges gaps in care coordination and continuity [30]. Provincial and national guidelines recognize the importance of family involvement in treatment [31–33]. Yet the triad of care rarely crosses the knowledge-to-practice gap within early psychosis care systems. Families and healthcare providers supporting young adults with psychosis face complex and conflicting priorities within the healthcare system making implementation of best practice guidelines challenging [20, 26, 33]. Healthcare professionals may hesitate to engage with family supporters because of uncertainty about privacy legislation,1 a lack of time, and avoidance of situations where they may be pressured to break confidentiality thereby damaging the patient-clinician relationship [35, 36]. Broader, organizational challenges in implementing family involvement include the lack of resources, protocols, mentorship, and knowledge of best practices [36]. When these barriers prevail, family carers are left lacking critical information necessary to help them prepare and provide the best support for their loved one at hospital discharge and during ongoing recovery. While struggling to more effectively support their loved ones, family carers urgently seek education and personal support through their own reading and by connecting to other families with lived experience [37].

Experience of family burden

The profound effects of psychotic illness penetrate deep into the core of families, demanding collective adaptation and resilience in the face of prolonged crises. Literature has discussed immense financial, emotional, physical, social and time-related burdens which have great impact on the carers’ wellness [38]. The economic burden to carers varies across countries and across studies. One study conducted in the United States found that carers spent 36 h weekly caring for their loved one, with an average of 1810 USD spent monthly on everyday needs, and 3909 USD spent annually on significant life events [39]. In 2006, the work-loss cost to caregivers of persons with mental illness was estimated to be $3.9 billion in Canada [40], but an updated economic evaluation of carer burden within the current economic and healthcare landscape of Canada is needed. Personal cost to carers related to their loved one’s instability is significant, and includes paying for medications, housing, debts, damages, and losses [41]. Family carers mourn losses in both their own and their loved ones’ lives as they take on lifelong caregiver responsibilities with little respite and adapt to their loved one's complex challenges and needs [42].

The social and emotional experience of caring for a loved one with psychosis involves varied and sometimes competing emotional responses. Carers overwhelmingly describe chronic negative emotions including guilt, shame, fear, loss, sadness, anxiety, and worry [43]. There are also positive emotional experiences related to increased family connection, acceptance, self-efficacy with effective coping, renewed appreciation of simple joys, greater tolerance and compassion for others, connection with spirituality or community, and new roles in advocacy and education [43]. Carers experience shifting social circles as the care of their loved one becomes a priority, and while some family and friends offer compassion and allyship, others may distance themselves [44]. Young adults affected by psychosis and their carers remain highly stigmatized through experiences of labelling, stereotyping, separation and isolation [38, 43].

The complex interplay between financial burden, emotional burden, and social factors has significant impacts on a carer's wellness. Family carers often experience a complex grief reaction, with symptoms of post-traumatic stress, depression, and poorer physical health [45]. These mental and physical symptoms contribute to significant burden and lower life satisfaction in the context of caring for a loved one with psychosis [46, 47]. One review identified two trajectories for families in this journey depending on their level of preparedness [48]. Carers that were more prepared were able to be organized and flexible and respond with warmth, decisiveness, confidence and optimism. Carers who were unprepared had poorer self-esteem and were more likely to respond with avoidant coping or be overly critical. To these authors’ knowledge, no previous work has chronologically traced the journey that family carers take across the early stages of psychosis to explore the order of events and when different forms of burden are accrued in the context of illness trajectory and interactions with the health care system (e.g., financial, emotional, etc.). Tracing the carer journey would indicate when family support and engagement with the care team would most effectively alleviate burden and empower family carers in their role within the triad of care.

Current study

A deeper understanding of the family narrative of supporting a young adult in the early stages of psychosis is essential to enhance empathy and compassion among healthcare professionals and provide insight into critical moments of potential intervention that could predictably reduce family burden and increase community stabilization. The guiding research questions of the current study are: (1) what are the commonly occurring episodes in the stories of carers of young adults with psychosis? (2) What experiences characterize these commonly occurring episodes, and (3) at what points in carers’ narrative were interventions and system supports needed?

Methods

Recruitment

Study participants were recruited through a larger study evaluating the effectiveness of a psychoeducation group for family carers of young adults with psychosis.2 The qualitative interviews, under the main study ethics application, were approved by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (Pro00110691). Inclusion criteria for the larger psychoeducation study included that family carers were supporting a young adult (aged 17–27) who (1) had been diagnosed with a psychotic illness, and (2) had an inpatient admission or discharge within the past 12 months related to their mental health concerns. In our study, “family carer” or “carer” refers to a broad range of supporters including parents, siblings, step-family, extended family, close friends, partners, and any other personal supporters involved in regular assistance of the young adult. Family carers were required to have proficiency in the English language as the psychoeducation group is taught in English. Three times throughout the psychoeducation curriculum, author 1 informed the psychoeducational group participants about the opportunity to share their story confidentially. Participants were informed that an aim of the qualitative interview was to raise consciousness about the carer experience amongst healthcare providers and advocate for family involvement in care. Interested participants contacted author 1 via email to initiate a discussion of informed consent and confirm recruitment. There were no cases of multiple carers from the same family unit who volunteered to participate. All data was stored on a secure Recovery Alberta drive, and transcripts were edited to remove any unnecessary identifying information (names, locations, etc.). Data files were labelled numerically to distinguish between them. Participation in the interview was an optional component of the larger psychoeducation study, with a separate consent process, and no additional incentive was offered for the interviews.

Data collection

Six audio-recorded semi-structured interviews were conducted via Zoom videoconferencing software in Spring 2022, and an additional two interviews were conducted in Spring 2023 to reach saturation. The one-year gap was a practical limitation due to infrequency of the psychoeducation groups early in the trial. All interviews were conducted by the first author and began with the prompt, “Please tell me the story of your experience supporting a young adult with addiction and mental health concerns beginning before they started experiencing these concerns and continuing up until the current state of things.” While additional follow-up and clarification questions were asked to gain depth within the participants’ narratives, no interview guide was used as the researchers aimed to allow participants to tell their detailed personal narratives organically without imposing biases from the researchers. Given that interviews were conducted within the context of a supportive psychoeducation group where resources, professional services and personal support systems were routinely discussed, no additional support resources were presented. However, participants were reminded of resources introduced within the group, and a family peer support worker (author 3) was available for further support.

Data analysis

Audio-recorded interviews were manually transcribed by author 1 and 2 verbatim and read three times to gain familiarity with the data. Initially in the iterative data analysis process, Labov and Waletzky’s [50] method was applied to identify individual narrative episodes (Fig. 1). This method provides a systematic and structured framework to narrative analysis, and the method was chosen due to its aptitude in facilitating a comprehensive breakdown of the family supporter’s personal narratives. Our original aim was to explore and represent a narrative that we repeatedly heard from participants in the psychoeducational group, which participants felt unable to communicate to healthcare providers. Representing this narrative from multiple individuals by naming these common episodes was intended to communicate the narrative as well as to explore where in the narrative intervention or family involvement would have been helpful. Naming these common episodes through Labov and Waletzky's approach was insufficient for our goals, providing a thin narrative that underrepresented the meaning attached to these episodes (e.g., what it meant to be in the “crisis event” episode). As the analysis progressed, the researchers chose to add a thematic analysis component to their coding to represent the rich lived experiences that contextualized the narrative episodes [51].Fig. 1Meta-narrative of the family story

Meta-narrative of the family story

To reduce power differentials and increase participant empowerment and safety, authors 1 and 2 (who hold the least power on the research team) conducted all direct participant interactions and data analysis. Participation in this qualitative sub-study was offered to all family carer participants enrolled in the first two cohorts of the psychoeducation curriculum, and individuals self-selected. Before beginning the interview, author 1 provided a detailed informed consent process that clearly outlined steps taken to ensure confidentiality, who on the research team would have access to participants' data, and until what point participants could request that their data be removed from the analysis. The interview’s initial prompt and the decision to forgo a formal interview guide aimed to reduce power and bias imposed by researchers and to allow participants to tell their detailed personal narratives organically and guide the interview process.

Qualitative methodologists rarely prescribe formal sample size guidelines, with sample sizes ranging from a few cases to dozens depending on the specific methodology and study aims [50, 52]. Instead, narrative analysis typically emphasizes detailed engagement with each narrative case, and depth of analysis over breadth, which tends to be time intensive and makes large sample sizes unfeasible [50, 52]. In the current study, the initial six participants offered rich and detailed narrative accounts, and similar key episodes were generated from the preliminary analysis process. Given the time intensive nature of combining Labov and Waletsky’s [50] structural approach with a narrative thematic analysis, and the relative homogeneity of the sample, the researchers determined that eight participants provided sufficient depth and breadth to generate common episodes while keeping the analysis feasible. Positionality

Author 1 is a white, cis-woman with an educational background in psychology, and 4 years of experience working in public and community mental health settings. Author 2 is a white, cisgender woman who is currently a graduate student in psychiatry. Author 3 is a white, cis-woman who is a mother supporting a young adult with severe mental illness and addiction, with twenty-three years of experience in education, family peer support, and social work. Author 4 is a black, cisgender man who has been in clinical psychiatry practice for twenty years. Author 5 is a white, cis-woman who is a mother supporting a young adult with severe mental illness, with an educational background in family and emergency medicine, with thirty-three years of experience in community and hospital medical practice. Notably, the impetus for the current qualitative narrative analysis was a recognition from authors 3 and 5 that their own stories of lived experience were reflected in others within the community of carers, and that this common story goes largely unheard by healthcare providers. As authors 3, 4, and 5 are all healthcare providers, there was a recognition that attitudes and practice towards family involvement do not currently meet the needs of carers. This essentially guided the researchers’ decision to investigate the research questions and choose a narrative approach which would honour the sequential nature of carer journeys.

Trustworthiness and researcher reflexivity

The authors aimed to increase trustworthiness in the analysis in line with Reissman’s [52] view that interpretations should be “plausible, reasonable, and convincing.” (p. 191). Researchers reflected on their educational background and training as potential sources of bias in the analysis. Authors 1 and 2 are the most socially distanced from the topic, and hold relatively less power, as they are neither healthcare providers nor do they have lived experience of caring for an individual with psychosis, and were therefore chosen to conduct all direct work with the participants and data. During analysis, author 1 and 2 engaged in reflexive memoing in the coding sheet describing their thought processes and emotional reactions to the data. Authors 1 and 2 frequently used memos to guide discussions, check one another’s biases, and find consensus in coding. The use of a Labov and Waletzky’s [50] highly structured approach to guide initial phases of analysis and dual coding of one participant also provided a standard for limiting bias in identification and organization of the narratives.

The authors discussed varied interpretations throughout the analysis phase, both through consensual validation between coders and in presenting the analysis and findings to the auditors. Author 3 has a background in education and social work and primarily uses a family systems theoretical lens. Author 5 works in emergency medicine, and her training stems from a biomedical lens. These differing educational backgrounds allowed auditors to recognize and limit biased interpretation for one another while serving as a substitute for member checking. During auditor meetings (attended by authors 1, 2, 3 and 5), emotional reactions and assumptions based on the auditors’ lived experiences and educational background were routinely acknowledged and challenged. Additionally, any recommended changes from auditors were “tested” by authors 1 and 2 against the evidence provided in the data across all 8 participants before changes to interpretations were made. Results

Sample characteristics

Among the 8 participants in this study, 12.5% (n = 1) identified as male and 87.5% (n = 7) as female. Participants had varying relationships with their loved one with psychosis, including parent (n = 6), grandparent (n = 1), and sibling (n = 1). Educational background varied, with 100% having completed high school, 30% holding a college diploma, and 12.5% possessing a university degree or higher. All 8 participants were married and held full-time employment, and 37.5% (n = 3) reported that they had to take time off work to care for their loved one or due to the stress and burnout of caring for their loved one. At the time of data collection, 62.5% (n = 5) reported living in the same household as their loved one with psychosis, 25.0% (n = 2) reported that their loved one with psychosis lives in another home with other family members, and 12.5% (n = 1) reported that their loved one had been hospitalized long term. Of the young adults with psychosis being supported by the participants, 12.5% (n = 1) were female and 87.5% (n = 7) were male. Young adults’ ages ranged from 17–26 (M = 21.38, SD = 3.29).

(i)Breaking a Crisis Threshold. Remarkably consistent across all stories was a crisis event in which the young adult’s symptoms were undeniable and the situation had become dangerous. These events, and the feelings of desperation, overwhelm, and terror were described in acute detail by the carers. During the crisis, most carers assertively pursued emergency services (i.e., call to police, emergency department visit). “One day, [husband] and I were in the living room arguing about a chair or something, and I … and there’s this big thump. And it was a very traumatic moment for me obviously (. . .) And so I was like, you know, ‘No something is really off.’ This is not normal run-of-the-mill depression. ( . . .) I’m taking him to the [Hospital], like now!’”(1001, Mother supporting a 17-year-old male)

Repeated interactions with the healthcare system

(i)Family Possesses Rich but Undervalued Insight. Carers reported that clinical interviewing of their loved one by health care professionals frequently only captured a snapshot of the young adult’s trajectory and failed to consider the carers’ rich knowledge about their young adult’s history and alarming functional decline. At points of contact with the healthcare system where carers sought to communicate their concerns about their loved one, they often felt invalidated and dismissed. “[Nurse] came out with a checklist of, I don’t know, suicide or something [laughing]. It was like [in high pitched falsetto voice] ‘Oh, remove safety risks from his room.’ Or something and it was like, woah, woah, woah (. . . ) I was like, ‘Am I the crazy one? Have I overreacted? Wait a minute, he jumped out the window! That’s crazy! Or am I the crazy one!’ It was really interesting because no one seemed to panic except for me. (. . .) It made me question myself. (. . .) I felt foolish going to the [hospital]. I felt like I was being ridiculous.”(1001, Mother supporting a 17-year-old male)

Discussion

This study captures family carer experiences of supporting a young adult through the early stages of their psychotic illness. Six meta-episodes were consistent across family stories, namely, (1) Blurry boundary between typical and atypical development, (2) Escalation of psychiatric symptoms, (3) Crisis event, (4) Repeated interactions with the healthcare system, (5) Hospitalization, and (6) Ongoing recovery (Fig. 1). These meta-episodes align with the existing literature on family carer lived experiences and reflect how historical cultural narratives have impacted family carer coping and involvement in the care of their young adult with psychosis.

Marginalization of family knowledge and lived experience in psychosis care

Given that family carers are often a primary source of support for young adults affected by psychosis it is unsurprising that the carers are themselves greatly impacted by the mental illness [43]. Some of the most significant negative emotions experienced include guilt and shame, experiences that may be related to internalized stigmatization and the myth that family carers caused psychosis in their loved one [54–56]. This stigmatization may stem from a historical cultural narrative that harkens back to psychoanalytic theories which attribute mental disorders to early parent–child interactions, and consequently places blame on parents [57, 58]. One of the most prominent theories of the etiology of schizophrenia from the late 1940’s to 1969 was that of the “schizophrenogenic mother,” in which cold and overprotective mothers induced schizophrenia in their children [58, 59]. Narratives which situate family carers as disorder-causing have since been criticized as harmful by way of their inaccuracy and stigmatizing effect [57]. Counter to this narrative, and following advancements in antipsychotic medications, a biomedical narrative emerged which described psychosis as a disease resulting from structural and functional brain abnormalities [60], effectively dissociating the family context from both the etiology and treatment. More sophisticated conceptualizations that propose the multidimensional etiology of psychotic disorders emerging through complex interactions between genetic, environmental, social, and psychological factors have become prominent in recent decades [61–63]. In line with this contextualized understanding of psychosis, research has also advanced our understanding of the protective role of family carer involvement as they are well-positioned to support both personal and clinical recovery [1, 3–5, 64, 65]. Lingering biases towards family, however, still permeate health systems, clinical care teams, family systems, and the family carers themselves. This was evident in the current study and aligns with literature that has found indications of carer marginalization when care teams deny carers information, exclude them from treatment and discharge planning, blame or criticize them, and leave them to independently seek out support resources [66].

Evidence for marginalization of family carers is further explored in studies which look at barriers to family engagement at a system and care team level. There may be hesitancy to engage with families because of power dynamics, concern around the need for exclusive patient-professional relationship, lack of time, and avoidance of situations where they may be pressured to break confidentiality [35]. While some mental health professionals face their own disempowerment within service system processes that prevent them from implementing best care practices, communication skills and attitudes of healthcare professionals and understaffing are common barriers [67]. Unfortunately, the mental health system offers little mental health support for families during this frightening and confusing time [68–71]. Rather, support groups consist of communities of families that have experienced similar hardships and have banded together to share their pain, their support, and their resources.

Misalignment between privacy legislation and best practice documents on family involvement in care

Privacy legislation which governs healthcare professionals in Canada (i.e., Health Information Act, Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act, Personal Information Protection Act) and best practice documents in early psychosis care can at times be seen as presenting conflicting messages. Written informed consent for disclosure of health information to third parties including family carers is required to help ensure accountability of mental health professionals. It is perhaps even more important to respect this right in patients who have been formally certified, as many patients are during the crisis event. These clients have experienced significant limitations to their rights, and often feel ashamed, helpless, and at the mercy of the mental health system [72]. To remove their right to decide when and how their personal information is distributed would be to violate one of the last remaining sources of power they have over their life. Best practice documents, however, often highlight the importance of family-centred care while downplaying or ignoring the legislation that binds clinicians [26, 32, 33, 73–75]. Yet in the context of complex care of individuals with severe psychotic symptoms who decline the involvement of their family, these same documents which support family partnership in care as best practice may seem irrelevant. An important nuance is that many of these best practice documents describe how to communicate rather than what to communicate. While a clinician may be very limited in the amount of information they share with family carers, non-stigmatizing and compassionate communication may drastically impact the family carer’s perspective on the mental health system and the carer's role in the triad of care.

The findings from the current study were generated within the context of young adult development, persistent cultural narratives regarding family involvement in care, and the sometimes-conflicting documents guiding healthcare practice. It is apparent that these contextual factors must be acknowledged within healthcare settings to enhance early psychosis care. Family carers often feel helpless and angry, positioned within a system that fails to provide comprehensive support, leaving them isolated and exhausted [19, 20, 26, 43]. Our study captures this meta-narrative and amplifies family voices calling for change to ease the burden at key points of contact within the healthcare system. With a committed response, public health system transformation is possible and can result in a more holistic and collaborative approach to treating young adults affected by psychotic illness and their supporting family carers with better outcomes for all.

Recommendations

Recommendation 1: increase access to services during the clinically high-risk stage

Public health systems should allocate greater resources to psychiatric care for young adults during early stages of psychosis (identified by researchers as Clinically High-Risk [CHR] [27]), alleviating the overwhelming responsibility that currently falls disproportionately on family carers. Episodes one (Blurry Boundary Between Typical and Atypical Development) and two (Escalation of Psychiatric Symptoms) in the current study’s metanarrative indicated that carers are often the first to recognize early or subclinical symptoms in their loved ones and can play a key role in assisting their loved one to access early intervention. Staged intervention models, implemented through integrated youth service hubs or other embedded mental health services, are leading approaches to providing accessible, family-inclusive services before families reach a crisis threshold [27, 76, 77]. Such services must be easily accessible through multiple entry points including through referrals from family carers.

Recommendation 2: fortify integrated crisis management systems

Carers are often left to manage psychiatric emergencies, such as those experienced during episode 3 (Crisis Event) of the current study, in isolation and without adequate safety responses or follow up resources. Mental health crises should be recognized and treated as urgent medical situations rather than domestic issues by first responders and emergency departments. Integrated crisis response teams can play a crucial role in facilitating timely access and coordination to emergency psychiatric care with inpatient admission, or community-based treatment options for young adults and their families.

Recommendation 3: systematize family programming in psychosis healthcare

The current study indicated that carers experience desperation and feel grossly unprepared for their role in supporting recovery, particularly during the cyclical journey of Interactions with the Healthcare System (episode 4), Hospitalization (episode 5) and Ongoing Recovery (episode 6). Systematizing family programming during these stages strategically positions family supporters as partners in the triad of care, bolsters caregivers’ well-being, and acknowledges their long-term supportive role. Standardized psychoeducational programming should be co-created and co-facilitated by the clinical community (a collaborative group of clinicians, other healthcare professionals, and clinical educators) together with advocates from the local community of family lived experience [78]. Clinicians bear the responsibility to proactively direct family carers to integrated psychoeducational programming as well as to family peer support, championing family intervention as an evidence-based strategy which can reduce hospitalization and relapse in their loved one [37].

Recommendation 4: train and model best communication practice for clinical teams

During Interactions with the Healthcare System (episode 4), and Hospitalization (episode 5), carer narratives were characterised by feeling undervalued by healthcare providers as partners in care. A collaborative approach to communication enhances the triad of care by acknowledging both the role of family caregivers and the autonomy of young adults experiencing psychosis [30, 33]. Enacting best practices for engagement with carers requires that these values are embedded in both clinical and organizational frameworks, disseminated through training opportunities, and cemented through modelling by senior clinicians and family peer supporters [36]. Clinicians can concretely facilitate carer engagement by using family inclusive communication styles and habits, providing opportunity for carers to privately share collateral information, and by including carers in discharge planning. Team leaders must intentionally reinforce these trained communication skills to effect a cultural shift that recognizes family caregivers as essential allies in supporting young adults with psychosis [35].

Limitations and future directions

The current study acknowledges several specific limitations. First, our participants were drawn exclusively from a psychoeducation program which limited the study context to the healthcare system in Alberta, Canada. The narratives included represent those from family carers who had computer or device access and ability to attend a 2-h online class in English once a week. The narratives presented here may not represent the experience of families belonging to marginalized populations (e.g., immigrants, family carers with their own mental health struggles, etc.). Importantly, this bias in the sample could reflect that inaccessibility of family-integrated services is exacerbated for families from marginalized populations, and equitable access to family integrated care must be considered in implementing the recommendations above. While the sample is predominantly female identifying, this may reflect population imbalances in caregiving responsibilities between sexes. Six of eight participants had interviews in the Spring of 2022 when COVID-19 restrictions impacted healthcare delivery and visitation.

In addition to limitations related to sample characteristics, the chosen analysis method impacts the findings and conclusions. While a narrative approach allowed the participant's stories to emerge organically, the development of a meta-narrative from these stories meant that similar elements were pulled from these stories to construct meta-episodes and themes while the individual stories and their intricate details were set aside. The social locations of the researchers may have influenced data interpretation, although triangulation with individuals possessing both professional expertise and family carer lived experience was sought to offset this.

This study also highlights several avenues for further exploration. First, there is a distinct need for development and evaluation of co-created, evidence-based psychoeducation for carers of individuals with psychosis. Understanding the short- and long-term impacts of such interventions on outcomes in carers and individuals with psychosis, and particularly their health utilization and economic impacts, may further support family carer wellness. The data in the current study was insufficient to understand how the sharp transition from the pediatric mental health system to the adult mental health system impacts the trajectory of family engagement and wellness, however there were indications that this critical transition is worthy of further study. Finally, while stigmatization of family carers and young adults with psychosis is well documented in the literature, the metanarrative pattern of stigmatization across the carers’ journey has been under studied, leaving questions about how various forms of stigma may interact in complex ways at different points to impact carer burden and wellness.
