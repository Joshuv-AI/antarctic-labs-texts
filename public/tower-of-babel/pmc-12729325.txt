Mitochondrial dysfunction mediates the association between mercury exposure and lipid metabolism in children and adolescents
Authors: Ping Cheng, Chen Liu, Jie Xiang, Peiwei Xu, Min Nian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMercury (Hg) is a widespread environmental pollutant with known neurotoxic and cardiometabolic effects, and its influence on lipid metabolism during childhood remains insufficiently understood. Mitochondrial dysfunction is proposed as a potential mechanism linking Hg exposure to metabolic disruption. Mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNA-CN) is regarded as an indicator of mitochondrial biogenesis and functional capacity, where lower levels generally suggest mitochondrial damage or dysfunction. In contrast, ribosomal DNA (rDNA) and relative telomere length (RTL) reflect genomic stability and cellular aging. This study investigated the associations between blood Hg levels and serum lipid profiles in children and adolescents and assessed the mediating roles of mtDNA-CN, rDNA, and RTL.MethodsA cross-sectional study was performed among 352 children and adolescents aged 6–17 years in eastern China. Blood Hg levels were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and serum lipid markers, namely total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and lipoprotein(a), were assessed along with the genomic indicators such as mtDNA-CN, rDNA, and RTL. Multivariable linear regression and mediation analyses were conducted.ResultsHigher Hg levels were significantly related with increased TC (β = 0.144, P = 0.012), LDL-C (β = 0.266, P < 0.001) and ApoB (β = 3.79, P = 0.005) and with decreased HDL-C (β = − 0.149, P < 0.001). These associations exhibited stronger in girls than in boys. mtDNA-CN was positively associated with Hg exposure and significantly mediated the relationships between Hg and HDL-C (53.8%), ApoA1 (180.6%), and LDL-C (24.7%).ConclusionHg exposure in children and adolescents is linked to an atherogenic lipid profile, potentially through mitochondrial dysfunction. MtDNA-CN appears to be a sensitive molecular mediator of Hg-induced lipid disturbances, which highlights the relevance of mitochondrial health in early-life environmental epidemiology and cardiovascular risk prevention. The findings support early prevention strategies and environmentally focused health policies that reduce toxicant exposure and thus promote long-term cardiometabolic health in young populations.Keywords: Mercury, Lipids, Mitochondria, Adolescent

Introduction

Environmental exposure to heavy metals, particularly mercury (Hg), has long been recognized as a major environmental health concern, especially among vulnerable populations such as children and adolescents [1]. Mercury is a pervasive environmental pollutant released from natural sources and from activities such as coal combustion, mining, and improper disposal of electronic waste [2]. Once introduced into the environment, mercury can bioaccumulate through the food chain, eventually entering the human body through dietary intake, inhalation, or dermal absorption [3]. Numerous epidemiological and toxicological studies have established that mercury exposure is associated with adverse neurological, immunological, and cardiovascular effects [4]. Its impact on metabolic health, particularly lipid metabolism in children and adolescents, has been reported in relatively few researches and is insufficiently understood.

Childhood and adolescence are critical periods of development marked by dynamic physiological changes and increased sensitivity to environmental exposures [5]. During these periods, exposure to environmental toxicants may disrupt endocrine and metabolic pathways, potentially leading to long-term health consequences [6]. Alterations in blood lipid levels in childhood, such as increased total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], or reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) serve as early predictors of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) in adulthood [7]. Despite growing awareness of the developmental origins of metabolic disorders, research examining the association between mercury exposure and lipid profiles in children and adolescents remains limited.

Recent studies suggest that mercury may interfere with lipid homeostasis through several biological mechanisms, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and epigenetic alterations [8, 9]. Mitochondria are central regulators of cellular energy metabolism and lipid regulation, and their impairment has been implicated in metabolic disorders [10]. Mercury readily binds to thiol-containing proteins, damaging mitochondrial membranes, and driving the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [11, 12]. These mitochondrial disturbances can alter lipid synthesis, transport, and clearance, ultimately contributing to dyslipidemia.

To better understand how mercury exposure influences lipid metabolism in children and adolescents, this study examined a set of molecular biomarkers related to mitochondrial function and genomic stability, including mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNA-CN), ribosomal DNA (rDNA), and relative telomere length (RTL). mtDNA-CN reflects mitochondrial biogenesis and activity, while rDNA and RTL reflect genome integrity and cellular aging, all of which are sensitive to oxidative stress and environmental insults [13–15]. However, these biomarkers have not been systematically applied to explore the biological mechanisms connecting mercury exposure to lipid disturbances in younger population.

Accordingly, this study was conducted to evaluate the relationship of blood mercury concentrations with lipid profiles in children and adolescents and to determine whether markers of mitochondrial function can illuminate potential biological pathways. The working hypothesis was that mercury-related mitochondrial disruption, particularly changes in mtDNA-CN, contributes to alterations in lipid metabolism.

Methods

Study population

A total of 352 children and adolescents (ages 6 to 17 years) were enrolled in this cross-sectional investigation, which was carried out at a primary school in Yiwu, China, between August and October 2021. Individuals diagnosed with metabolic disorders affecting lipid metabolism, such as familial hypercholesterolemia or lipid storage disorders, as well as those taking medications known to influence lipid profiles or oxidative stress, including statins, corticosteroids, and antioxidant supplements, were excluded from the study [16]. Before data or sample collection, participants and/or their legal guardians provided consent. The study protocol was granted by the Ethics and Human Subject Committee of Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No. 2020-040-01). To minimize contamination, venous blood was drawn using trace metal-free vacutainer tubes. Serum was separated by centrifuging the blood at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 25℃, then divided into polypropylene tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Blood mercury measurement

Whole blood mercury concentrations were determined with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; iCAP RQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After mixing, 0.1 mL of blood was diluted with 1.9 mL of a prepared solution containing nitric acid, Triton X-100, and gold stabilizer. The diluted samples were introduced into the plasma via nebulization, and mercury ions were quantified based on their mass-to-charge ratios. Rhenium (20 µg/L) was used as an internal standard to correct for signal variability. Calibration curves were prepared using external standards ranging from 0 to 1.0 µg/L. The limit of detection (LOD) was 0.02 µg/L, and mercury levels below the LOD were replaced with LOD divided by the square root of two (LOD/√2).

Quantification of mtDNA-CN, rDNA, and RTL

Genomic DNA was prepared from whole blood with a HiPure Blood DNA Mini Kit (D3111, Magen Biotechnology, China) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative mtDNA-CN was quantified using SYBR Green-based quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) by calculating the ratio of the mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase subunit 1 (ND1) gene to the single-copy nuclear β-globin gene [16]. For rDNA analysis, the copy numbers of 5 S, 5.8 S, 18 S, 28 S, and 45 S rDNA were measured using gene-specific primers and normalized to the TP53 reference gene. RTL was quantified using telomere-specific primers and β-globin as the reference. All reactions were conducted in triplicate in a 20 µL system with 20 ng DNA, 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, and 0.4 µL of each primer. The amplification protocol initiated with a 5-min denaturation at 95 °C, proceeding to 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C (or 56 °C for telomere) for extension. Data were analyzed using the 2^- delta cycle threshold (ΔCT) or 2^- delta–delta cycle threshold (ΔΔCT) method to generate relative expression levels.

Lipid profile assessment

Quantification of TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C was performed with enzymatic colorimetric assays on a fully automated clinical chemistry analyzer. ApoA1 and ApoB levels were measured by immunoturbidimetric methods, whereas Lp(a) was determined via nephelometric immunoassay [16]. Serum lipid concentrations are reported in mmol/L for TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C and in mg/L for ApoA1, ApoB, and Lp(a).

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics and biomarker distributions were summarized with descriptive methods. According to the data distribution, continuous variables were showed using mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). As blood mercury was right-skewed, the model was compared using raw and log-transformed values, and similar Akaike information criterion (AIC) and R² were observed; thus, log-transformed values were used for consistency with prior mercury epidemiology studies [17, 18].

Multivariable linear regression models were adopted to assess the relationship of blood mercury levels with serum lipid concentrations (TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, ApoA1, ApoB, and Lp(a)). To examine potential nonlinear associations between mercury and lipid outcomes, multivariable linear models using restricted cubic splines (RCS) for mercury were fitted. Splines applied four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of mercury [19]. Age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) were included as covariates in the models [20]. Interaction terms between sex and mercury exposure were added to the analytical models. Additionally, stratified analyses were performed by sex to evaluate potential effect modification. A P-value for interaction less than 0.10 was considered suggestive of effect modification. To explore potential mechanistic pathways, mediation analyses were performed with the mediation functions in R [21]. Initial verification focused on the prerequisite associations for each lipid outcome: (i) total effect of ln (Hg) on the outcome; (ii) association of ln (Hg) with each candidate mediator (mtDNA-CN, rDNA, RTL); and (iii) association of the mediator with the outcome adjusting for covariates. Linear models were fit for mediator and outcome. The average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated were evaluated by nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 simulations) with 95% CIs.

All analyses were performed in R software (version 4.4.2), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of < 0.05.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationship of blood mercury concentrations with lipid biomarkers (Fig. 1A). After adjusting for potential confounders, blood mercury exhibited a positive association with TC (β = 0.144, 95% CI: 0.032 to 0.256, P = 0.012), TG (β = 0.059, 95% CI: 0.001 to 0.118, P = 0.047), LDL-C (β = 0.266, 95% CI: 0.161 to 0.37, P < 0.001), and ApoB (β = 3.79, 95% CI: 1.12 to 6.43, P = 0.005). In contrast, a significant inverse association was observed with HDL-C (β = −0.149, 95% CI: −0.232 to − 0.066, P < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for ApoA1 or Lp(a). RCS models were adopted to evaluate potential nonlinear associations between mercury exposure and lipid outcomes. No significant nonlinear relationships were identified (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05) (Fig. 1B).

The relationships of blood mercury levels with mitochondrial biomarkers, including mtDNA-CN, rDNA, and RTL (Fig. 2A), were examined. Mercury exposure showed a significant association with mtDNA-CN (β = 12.84, P < 0.001). mtDNA-CN was significantly related to HDL-C, ApoA1, and LDL-C, suggesting a mediating role in the relationship between mercury and lipid profiles (Fig. 2B). Mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether mtDNA-CN serves as a potential biological pathway linking mercury exposure to lipid dysregulation. As shown in Fig. 2C and E, mtDNA-CN significantly mediated the associations between mercury and HDL-C, ApoA1, and LDL-C. Specifically, the ACME was − 0.080 mmol/L for HDL-C (95% CI: −0.135 to − 0.032, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2C), − 0.802 mg/L for ApoA1 (95% CI: −1.774 to − 0.140, P = 0.004) (Fig. 2D), and 0.065 mmol/L for LDL-C (95% CI: 0.020 to 0.118, P = 0.006) (Fig. 2E), corresponding to mediation proportions of 53.8%, 180.6%, and 24.7%, respectively. The ADEs were not statistically significant except for LDL-C, which suggests that much of the association between mercury exposure and these lipid markers is mediated through changes in mtDNA-CN.

Discussion

This study found that higher blood mercury levels in individuals aged 6 to 17 showed significant relationships with increased TC, TG, LDL-C, and ApoB and with decreased HDL-C levels. These associations appeared stronger in girls than in boys. Mediation analysis indicated that mtDNA-CN was a mediator of the relationship between mercury exposure and lipid levels, while no significant mediation was observed for rDNA or telomere length.

Findings align with previous studies conducted in adolescent and adult populations. Several studies have reported that mercury exposure is linked to altered lipid profiles. For example, a study using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2018 data found that higher total blood mercury was were significantly related with increased TC and decreased HDL-C levels among U.S. adolescents aged 12–19 years [22]. Similarly, a nationally representative study of Korean adolescents aged 10–19 years included in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) determined that those in the highest quartile of blood mercury exhibited 3.72-fold higher risk for hypercholesterolemia than those in the lowest quartile [23]. These findings are in line with earlier studies in adults, where cross-sectional data have shown that mercury exposure, even at background environmental levels, is associated with elevated TC and LDL-C [20]. This study builds on this evidence by focusing on children and adolescents, expanding the lipid profile to include apolipoproteins, and demonstrating a positive association between mercury and ApoB, which is consistent with reports linking higher mercury levels in adults to elevated ApoB, a marker of atherogenic risk [20].

The associations between mercury exposure and lipid levels were stronger in girls than in boys. The difference may be explained by biological or hormonal factors that influence susceptibility to mercury’s metabolic effects. Estrogen affects lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function [24], which could modify the body’s response to mercury exposure. Estrogen also modulates oxidative stress and mitochondrial biogenesis through pathways involving peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α) and antioxidant enzymes, potentially influencing mercury-induced lipid disturbances [25, 26]. Beyond hormonal influences, experimental studies have shown that although females may clear total mercury more efficiently, they can experience higher peak accumulation in lipid-rich tissues and mitochondria, which potentially amplifies metabolic effects [27]. Differences in body composition, fat distribution, and the activity of antioxidant defense systems between girls and boys during childhood and adolescence may also contribute [28]. However, the specific mechanisms through which mercury interacts with these sex-dependent pathways remain to be fully elucidated.

Previous research supports a link between mercury exposure and mtDNA-CN, though the relationship is complex and appears to vary depending on the population and timing of exposure. For example, a European multi-cohort study reported that mercury exposure in the fetal period was related with longer RTL in children, with a nonlinear relationship postnatally, but no significant association was found with mtDNA-CN [29]. Similarly, research from the Seychelles cohort observed associations between prenatal mercury exposure and longer RTL, while the effects on mtDNA-CN were weak or inconsistent, possibly influenced by co-exposures such as to polyunsaturated fatty acids [15, 30]. In contrast, a U.S.-based Project Viva study observed that early pregnancy exposure to mercury had a nonlinear association with maternal mtDNA-CN but no clear association with cord blood mtDNA-CN or RTL [31]. Collectively, these mixed results indicate that the timing of exposure, co-existing nutrient levels, and possibly epigenetic modifications may modulate mitochondrial responses to mercury. While longer mtDNA-CN might initially seem beneficial, some evidence suggests it may also reflect compensatory responses to oxidative damage, warranting cautious interpretation. More longitudinal and mechanistic research is needed to clarify how mercury exposure affects mitochondrial function and cellular aging at different life stages.

rDNA and RTL did not mediate the associations between mercury and lipid levels. This differs from findings in adults, where long-term environmental exposure has been linked to shortened telomeres or altered rDNA stability [32]. This may be due to the relatively short exposure window in children or lower cumulative oxidative burden, supporting the hypothesis that mitochondrial biomarkers may be more responsive indicators of early environmental stress than nuclear genome markers.

The observed associations between mercury exposure and adverse lipid levels may be mechanistically explained by mercury-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, both of which can disrupt lipid metabolism. Mercury exhibits a strong affinity for thiol (sulfhydryl) groups, which enables it to bind covalently to cysteine residues in mitochondrial membrane proteins [27]. This disrupts the respiratory-chain activity, impairs mitochondrial polarization, and induces mitochondrial permeability transition, leading to increased production of ROS [33, 34]. Elevated ROS levels can damage mitochondrial DNA, promote lipid peroxidation, and impair key enzymes that regulate lipid metabolism, and thus ultimately contribute to a pro-atherogenic lipid profile [35, 36]. In response to this oxidative stress, cells may activate the AMP-activated protein kinase–peroxisome–nuclear respiratory factors 1 and 2 (AMPK-NRF1/2) signaling pathway, stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis as a compensatory mechanism to maintain adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and counteract damage [37]. The observed increase in mtDNA-CN in this study may therefore reflect this adaptive mitochondrial response rather than enhanced mitochondrial efficiency [13]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that mercury exposure results in mitochondrial swelling, ATP depletion, and oxidative stress in various biological systems [38]. Furthermore, mercury impairs major antioxidant systems such as glutathione peroxidase and paraoxonase-1 (PON1), both required to maintain lipid homeostasis [39, 40]. Decreased PON1 activity impairs the antioxidative function of HDL and increases LDL oxidation, further promoting atherogenesis [41]. Although this study did not assess inflammatory markers, these mitochondrial and oxidative stress-related processes offer a plausible biological explanation for the link between mercury exposure and lipid disturbances seen in children and adolescents. The mediation proportion for ApoA1 exceeded 100%, which may reflect an inconsistent mediation pattern where the direct and indirect effects of mercury act in opposite directions [42]. This opposite relationship can lead to an inflated mediation estimate and indicates that mitochondrial responses may modulate mercury’s influence on lipid metabolism [13, 43]. Although mtDNA-CN statistically mediated the associations between mercury and lipid levels, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. mtDNA-CN and lipid alterations may both represent downstream responses to mercury-induced oxidative stress rather than a strict exposure-mediator-outcome sequence. Thus, mtDNA-CN should be viewed as a mechanistic indicator of mitochondrial adaptation or injury rather than as a confirmed causal mediator.

Changes in mtDNA-CN reflect mitochondrial biogenesis, an adaptive cellular response to stress and injury [18]. This research revealed a significant positive relationship between mercury exposure and mtDNA-CN, with an estimated increase of 12.84 relative units (95% CI: 5.44 to 20.24) per one-unit increase in ln (Hg). Unlike those of earlier work on the relationship between mtDNA-CN and lipid levels [44, 45], these findings point to its role as a marker of mitochondrial response to environmental stress rather than lipid status. The elevation in mtDNA-CN may reflect a cellular attempt to counteract mercury-induced mitochondrial stress [13]. Since mtDNA-CN measured in peripheral blood serves as a systemic indicator of mitochondrial abundance in metabolically active tissues such as liver tissue, this elevation could indicate an adaptive response aimed at maintaining energy production under stress [13]. Mercury disrupts mitochondrial function, and the liver is particularly susceptible to this damage because of its rich mitochondrial content and central role in lipid metabolism. As mitochondrial efficiency declines, cells may replicate mitochondrial DNA to sustain energy production, though this compensatory process may not fully restore function and could instead indicate ongoing metabolic stress [46, 47]. The present findings suggest that mercury exposure contributes to mitochondrial changes that occur independently of lipid levels that mtDNA-CN has potential as an early marker of biological response to toxicant-induced stress. Although increased mtDNA-CN may indicate adaptive mitochondrial biogenesis, it can also reflect compensatory replication under oxidative stress, signaling dysfunction rather than recovery [13]. In contrast, RTL and rDNA capture longer-term genomic stability and cumulative cellular damage [32]. Their lack of association with mercury exposure in this relatively young population may reflect the earlier and more transient nature of mitochondrial responses compared with nuclear genome alterations.

Strengths and limitations

This research has several strengths. A major strength of this work is that it examines the association between mercury exposure and lipid metabolism in children and adolescents, offering valuable insights into early-life environmental risk factors. The inclusion of a comprehensive lipid profile, including both standard lipids and apolipoproteins, allowed for a nuanced assessment of cardiovascular risk. Furthermore, the integration of molecular biomarkers such as mtDNA-CN, rDNA, and RTL provided mechanistic depth and allowed identification of mitochondrial dysfunction as a key pathway. However, some limitations remain. First, the cross-sectional design constrains causal inference because exposure and outcome were measured at the same point in time, leaving it unable to clarify whether mercury exposure preceded the observed changes in lipid levels or mitochondrial biomarkers. Second, blood mercury does not distinguish between organic and inorganic forms, which may have different biological effects. Additionally, information on dietary factors, particularly fish and omega-3 fatty acid intake, was unavailable. Although these factors may influence both mercury exposure and lipid metabolism, the relatively homogeneous dietary habits of this population may have minimized their confounding effects [48].

Conclusion

Elevated blood mercury levels were associated with adverse lipid profiles in children and adolescents, including increased TC, TG, LDL-C, and ApoB and reduced HDL-C. Mediation analysis showed that mtDNA-CN partially accounted for these associations, indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction may represent an early biological pathway through which mercury influences lipid metabolism. The findings have clinical relevance because lipid abnormalities during childhood are strong predictors of future cardiometabolic disease. Identifying mercury-related lipid disruptions at an early stage may help clinicians strengthen cardiovascular risk assessment in pediatric populations and provide more targeted guidance on environmental and dietary exposures. The sensitivity of mtDNA-CN to toxicant-induced mitochondrial stress also suggests its potential value as an early biomarker to identify children who may be more vulnerable to metabolic disturbances.

Integrating environmental exposure assessment with mitochondrial biomarkers may enhance early detection strategies for cardiometabolic risk in youth. Further work should evaluate whether incorporating such biomarkers into pediatric screening or preventive frameworks can improve long-term cardiovascular outcomes and inform interventions aimed at reducing harmful metal exposure.
