Strategies to improve retention in care among people living with HIV at health facilities in Manaus, Brazil: insight from health care providers
Authors: Zeca Manuel Salimo, Elizangela Farias da Silva, Michael Nosano Yakubu, Paulo Afonso Nogueira, Adele Schwartz Benzaken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetention in HIV care is a key component of the continuum of care and is essential for achieving the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets. In Manaus, Northern Brazil, despite advances in HIV treatment and prevention, loss to follow-up among PLHIV remains a significant barrier to viral suppression and epidemic control. The present research explored health care providers’ perspectives on strategies to improve retention in care among people living with HIV (PLHIV) at referral health facilities in Manaus.MethodsA qualitative cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2023 and October 2025 in six public health facilities in Manaus. Semi-structured, in-depth interviews were performed with 56 health care providers, including physicians, nurses, social workers, psychologists, pharmacists, and HIV/AIDS program state managers. Data were analyzed using thematic framework analysis with MAXQDA software.ResultsSix major themes emerged from the analysis: (1) monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV, (2) challenges related to loss to follow-up, (3) impact of loss to follow-up on UNAIDS 95–95–95 goals, (4) barriers to retention, (5) strategies to optimize retention, and (6) state-level initiatives to improve retention. Providers identified poverty, food insecurity, unemployment, stigma, discrimination, transportation barriers, substance use, and health system constraints such as workforce shortages, long waiting times, and lack of privacy as key drivers of disengagement. Proposed strategies included strengthening multidisciplinary teams, promoting continuous professional training, implementing patient-centered and stigma-free care, enhancing active patient tracing, expanding digital communication tools, decentralizing services, and ensuring adequate financial and human resources.ConclusionsRetention in HIV care in Manaus is shaped by complex social and systemic factors. Integrated, multisectoral strategies addressing socioeconomic vulnerabilities and service-level barriers are essential to improve long-term engagement in care and advance progress toward the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets.Keywords: HIV, Retention in care, Loss to follow-up, Health care providers, Manaus, Brazil, Qualitative study

Background

Retention in follow-up is an essential component of clinical care for people living with HIV (PLHIV). The UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets aim for 95% of all PLHIV to know their HIV status, 95% of those diagnosed to receive continuous antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those on ART to achieve viral suppression by 2030 [1]. Achieving these targets depends not only on access to diagnosis and treatment, but also on continuous engagement in care throughout the HIV care cascade.

In Brazil, HIV care is delivered within the framework of the Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS), which guarantees universal and free access to HIV testing, ART, and follow-up care. National policies for HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) emphasize early diagnosis, immediate ART initiation, decentralized care, and long-term retention through Specialized Care Services (Serviços de Assistência Especializada – SAE) [2, 3]. Despite these advances, Brazil continues to experience regional disparities in HIV indicators, with persistent challenges related to loss to follow-up and unequal access to sustained care.

According to national epidemiological data, the North region of Brazil presents disproportionately high HIV detection and AIDS-related mortality rates when compared to national averages [4]. Over the last decade, the North experienced an increase in AIDS detection rates from 21.4 to 25.7 cases per 100,000 inhabitants, while the South and Southeast regions showed reductions from 35.1 to 20.4 and from 23.4 to 14.5 cases per 100,000 inhabitants, respectively. Amazonas recorded an 8% increase in the AIDS detection rate in 2022, and Manaus, the capital of Amazonas, is among the cities with the highest HIV detection rates in the country, with 54.1 cases per 100,000 inhabitants, ranking as the second highest burden nationwide [2]. In addition, Manaus recorded 322 AIDS-related deaths in 2022, corresponding to a mortality rate of 6.9 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants, higher than the national rate of 4.1 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants [2, 4]. These regional inequalities reflect a complex interplay of social vulnerability, geographic barriers, and health system challenges, highlighting the need to understand how national HIV policies are implemented and experienced at the local level, particularly in geographically vast and socially heterogeneous contexts such as the Brazilian Amazon.

Previous studies [5–7] suggest that achieving the 95–95–95 targets require a comprehensive approach that encompasses prevention, treatment and psychosocial support. From a public health perspective, retention in care is essential for controlling the HIV epidemic because sexual transmission of HIV does not occur among PLHIV who are receiving ART and maintaining an undetectable viral load [8]. Retention is strongly associated with the primary objectives of HIV/AIDS treatment, including the early initiation of ART, the achievement of sustained viral suppression, the reduction of antiretroviral resistance, the monitoring of ART toxicity and opportunistic infections, and the improvement of immune recovery, clinical outcomes, survival and quality of life [6, 9–11].

Health care providers play a pivotal role in ensuring that PLHIV remain engaged in care and adherent to ART. Miranda et al. [12], Nosyk et al. [13], and Barros [14] have reported that providers can offer personalised support, improve access to healthcare services, provide HIV education and prevention services, and tackle stigma among PLHIV. Thus, describing and analyzing strategies to improve retention based on the perspectives of health professionals who experience daily challenges in HIV care enables the design and implementation of evidence-based models tailored to the specific needs of PLHIV in health services. This can enhance the quality of life of PLHIV and reduce AIDS-related morbidity and mortality [11], which often stem from disengagement from care and treatment interruption.

Methods

Aim

The study aimed to explore health care providers’ perspectives on strategies to improve retention in care among PLHIV at referral health facilities in Manaus, Brazil.

Study setting

The research was conducted in Manaus, the capital of the Brazilian state of Amazonas, between November 2023 and October 2025. Manaus consists of 63 neighbourhoods arranged within seven zones: North, West, Central-West, Central-South, East, South and Rural, with an estimated population of 2,063,689 [15]. It has the second highest prevalence of HIV in Brazil, with a detection rate of 54.1 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in 2024 [2]. There are six reference health units monitoring 34,511 PLHIV and a state coordination of the STI/HIV/TB programme administered by the Fundação de Vigilância em Saúde do Amazonas - Dra. Rosemary Costa Pinto (FVS-RCP) (Fig. 1). Two of the units (Fundação de Medicina Tropical Dr. Heitor Vieira Dourado (FMT-HVD) and Fundação Hospitalar Alfredo da Matta (FUHAM)) are tertiary and are administered by the state government. They offer more than two distinct services: the Testing and Counselling Centre (TCC) and the Medication Dispensing Unit (MDU). The other four units [Clínica da Família Dr. Antonio Reis (AREIS), Unidade de Saúde da Família Ajuricaba (USFA), Policlínica Dr. António Comte Telles (COMTE TELLES) and Policlínica Dr. José António da Silva (PMO)] are secondary care units administered by the Manaus Municipal Health Department (SEMSA). In addition to performing HIV testing and monitoring PLHIV, these units are commonly used for the decentralisation of PLHIV care. All of these facilities are public and provide HIV care free of charge. The six facilities have a Serviço de Assistência Especializada (SAE – Specialized Care Service) for PLHIV, with a team of qualified professionals to provide diagnosis, treatment and counselling on adherence to healthcare, as well as data management [including managing antiretroviral therapy (ART) and databases of all PLHIV]. Each SAE receives technical support from the AIDS Healthcare Foundation (AHF), a non-governmental organisation that supports the government’s efforts to respond to HIV/AIDS.

Study population and participant recruitment

The study population consisted of health care providers (nurses, pharmacists, psychologists, social workers, biomedical professionals, and physicians) who provide care to PLHIV in the above-mentioned health facilities, as well as managers from those facilities and the state STI/HIV/TB/HIV/AIDS coordination department (FVS-RCP). Participants were selected through non-probabilistic purposive sampling [16], allowing for the inclusion of individuals based on predefined characteristics and criteria relevant to the research objectives. Only health care providers with at least six months of professional experience in HIV care and follow-up were included, while those with less experience or who declined participation were excluded.

Study design

This was a qualitative cross-sectional study, designed to condense individual statements of experience related to the retention of PLHIV in care services through textual analysis of interview transcripts. By comparing the interpretations of multiple researchers on strategies to improve retention in care among people living with HIV, a shared and consensual understanding was developed [17], from which a semi-structured interview guide was designed. The guide comprised eighteen open-ended questions and additional follow-up questions that allowed the interviewer to probe more deeply into participants’ perceptions and experiences regarding strategies for retaining PLHIV in care [18]. The questions were developed following discussions within an interdisciplinary research team experienced in the HIV care cascade.

Via purposive sampling, 56 health care providers were interviewed. One of the investigators, with prior experience in qualitative research, conducted the in-depth interviews (IDIs). The interviews lasted approximately 60 min and took place in a quiet and comfortable room. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent for later transcription, and each was conducted until data collection reached the point of theoretical saturation. The guide developed for IDIs was previously tested and validated in a smaller sample by the researchers. Methods and results are presented in line with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research [17, 18].

Data analysis

The recording of the interview was transcribed and inserted into the MAXQDA10 program. Qualitative analysis was carried out using thematic framework analysis, and categories were created after prior reading of the interview transcripts. These categories emerged during the analysis process and were discussed among the researchers for consensus. Additionally, two researchers developed a codebook and started line-by-line coding [19].

Results

Social characteristics of health care providers

A total of 56 health care providers from the selected health facilities or institutions participated in the study (Table 1). Among them, 35 (62.5%) were female, most of whom were single (28; 50.0%). The predominant age group was between 18 and 39 years (32; 57.14%). The majority (30; 53.57%) had between six and ten years of experience in providing HIV-related care. Participants included one state HIV/AIDS program manager, six facility directors, six coordinators of SAE, and several physicians, nurses, pharmacists, psychologists, social workers, and nursing technicians directly involved in HIV treatment and care.

Characteristics of the health care providers (PV) interviewed

Content analysis of the interviews revealed six major themes: (1) Monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV; (2) Challenges related to loss to follow-up; (3) The impact of loss to follow-up on UNAIDS 95–95–95 goals; (4) Barriers to retention; (5) Strategies to optimize retention of PLHIV in health services; and (6) State-level initiatives and projects to improve retention of PLHIV.

Theme 1 – monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV

Health care providers described monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV in Manaus as a structured but fragile process, sustained by multidisciplinary teams and standardized clinical routines, yet frequently disrupted by social and systemic constraints. Participants emphasized that follow-up begins immediately after diagnosis and is centered on early linkage to care, prompt initiation of ART, and regular clinical and laboratory monitoring. However, providers highlighted that continuity of this process is not guaranteed and remains vulnerable to interruptions at multiple stages of care (Fig. 2).

Analytical representation of monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV in Manaus, based on health care providers’ narratives

According to participants, routine follow-up generally includes periodic medical consultations, laboratory tests (such as viral load and CD4 count), medication dispensing, and psychosocial support. Clinical monitoring is usually conducted every six months, with shorter intervals during ART initiation or in cases of detectable viral load:

“During follow-up, PLHIV attend regular consultations for laboratory tests, usually every six months, with more frequent visits at treatment initiation or if viral load is elevated.” (Pv_17, male).

Beyond clinical care, providers stressed the importance of psychosocial support as a central component of follow-up. Psychologists and social workers were described as key actors in promoting adherence, addressing emotional distress, and mitigating social vulnerabilities that compromise retention:

“Psychologists and social workers provide counseling that promotes adherence and prevents complications related to infection. Social services also guide patients about social benefits, such as transportation vouchers and subsidies for those in need.” (Pv_22, female).

Despite the existence of these structured routines, providers consistently reported that loss to follow-up is a recurrent phenomenon that can occur at any point along the care trajectory. Participants noted that disengagement is particularly common during the early stages of treatment or after periods of clinical improvement, when patients may perceive less need for continuous care:

“Loss to follow-up among PLHIV is a common phenomenon and can occur at any stage of care, particularly during early visits or long-term follow-up.” (Pv_51, female).

Taken together, these narratives indicate that while monitoring and follow-up of PLHIV in Manaus are formally organized within specialized services, the effectiveness of these processes depends heavily on the capacity of health teams to anticipate vulnerabilities, maintain active engagement, and respond to individual patient needs. This tension between structured service organization and fragile continuity of care underscores the complexity of sustaining long-term retention in HIV services.

Theme 02 – challenges related to loss to follow-up among PLHIV

Health care providers consistently described loss to follow-up among PLHIV as a persistent and systemic challenge in Manaus, rather than an episodic or isolated occurrence. Participants emphasized that disengagement from care often follows a cyclical pattern, in which patients temporarily interrupt treatment and later return to services, frequently after experiencing clinical deterioration.

“Systematic loss to follow-up among PLHIV is multifactorial and complex, resulting from barriers that interfere with continuous care at various stages.” (Pv_57, female).

Providers reported that loss to follow-up tends to occur after periods of perceived clinical stability, when patients experience symptom improvement and may underestimate the importance of long-term engagement in care. This pattern contributes to interruptions in ART, delays in viral suppression, and increased vulnerability to opportunistic infections.

“Discontinuity in care jeopardizes patients’ health, increases the risk of opportunistic infections, and facilitates the spread of the virus in the community.” (Pv_44, male).

These narratives highlight that loss to follow-up is not merely the result of individual non-adherence, but rather reflects broader structural and psychosocial vulnerabilities that undermine sustained engagement in HIV care.

Theme 03 – impact of loss to follow-up among PLHIV on UNAIDS 95–95–95 goals

Providers explicitly linked loss to follow-up to failures across the HIV care cascade and identified it as a major obstacle to achieving the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets in Manaus. According to participants, disengagement from care compromises all stages of the cascade, particularly sustained ART use and viral suppression.

“Loss to follow-up among PLHIV compromises the HIV care cascade, delaying viral suppression and increasing the risk of transmission.” (Pv_49, female).

Participants emphasized that when patients disengage from care, viral suppression, the cornerstone of both individual health and epidemic control is not achieved. This failure not only increases the likelihood of HIV transmission at the community level, but also contributes to the emergence of antiretroviral drug resistance.

“When patients drop out of care, viral suppression, the main goal of treatment is not achieved. This not only increases community transmission but also contributes to the emergence of drug resistance.” (Pv_66, male).

These findings underscore that retention in care is a critical determinant of progress toward national and global HIV targets, reinforcing the need for strategies that prioritize continuous engagement rather than episodic care.

Theme 04 – barriers to retention in care among PLHIV

Economic challenges

Providers identified multiple, interrelated barriers to retention in HIV care, encompassing economic hardship, social vulnerability, behavioral factors, treatment-related challenges, and health system deficiencies. Participants emphasized that these barriers often coexist and compound one another, amplifying the risk of loss to follow-up.

“Most patients are unemployed or have no stable income, which limits access to food and transportation.” (Pv_25, male).

Food insecurity was described as a particularly critical issue, as inadequate nutrition can exacerbate ART side effects and discourage treatment continuation:

“Some patients interrupt ART because they feel weak due to inadequate nutrition.” (Pv_27, female).

Transportation support was often reported as insufficient, especially for patients residing in rural or peripheral areas:

“Transportation vouchers provided by social services are often insufficient, especially for patients from rural areas.” (PV_09, male).

Lack of social and family support

Stigma, discrimination, housing instability, and fear of HIV status disclosure were frequently cited as barriers that undermine continuity of care. Providers noted that social exclusion and lack of family support contribute to isolation and disengagement from health services.

“Many patients report that instead of receiving support, they face stigma and social exclusion from relatives and friends.” (PV_18, female).

“Some patients prefer not to disclose their status for fear of rejection and choose to isolate themselves.” (PV_21, male).

These experiences reinforce internalized stigma and reduce patients’ willingness to maintain regular contact with health services.

Illicit substance use

Participants reported that emotional distress following HIV diagnosis can lead some individuals to engage in illicit substance use, which in turn disrupts treatment adherence and follow-up.

“Some patients admitted using drugs after an HIV-positive diagnosis, believing it would help them cope emotionally.” (PV_35, Female).

Antiretroviral therapy-related challenges

Treatment-related factors, including side effects, pill burden, and regimen complexity, were identified as additional barriers to sustained retention.

“Some patients reported treatment fatigue, while others said they were reluctant to continue due to side effects.” (PV_01, female).

Health system and service-related barriers

Providers also emphasized structural deficiencies within health services, such as long waiting times, shortages of human resources, lack of privacy, poor communication, and negative staff attitudes. These factors were described as directly contributing to patient dissatisfaction and disengagement from care.

“Negative attitudes from health professionals can lead to loss to follow-up among PLHIV. For instance, some patients said they were mistreated or spoken to harshly by staff.” (PV_41, female).

“We have too few professionals and too many patients. Unfortunately, this results in long waiting times, which frustrate patients and lead to dropouts.” (PV_46, female).

“Patients worry about lack of privacy, waiting in open areas where they might be recognized. Some even travel to distant clinics to avoid being seen by acquaintances.” (PV_63, male).

Collectively, these findings illustrate that barriers to retention in care among PLHIV in Manaus are deeply embedded in social, economic, and institutional contexts, reinforcing the need for comprehensive and integrated retention strategies.

Theme 05 – strategies to optimize retention of PLHIV in health services

Health care providers emphasized that optimizing retention in HIV care in Manaus requires a multifaceted and integrated strategy that addresses structural constraints, professional practices, active follow-up mechanisms, and the availability of adequate human and financial resources. Participants highlighted that isolated interventions are insufficient and that sustainable retention depends on coordinated actions across multiple levels of the health system.

Structural and organizational strategies

Providers stressed the importance of strengthening specialized HIV services through clear institutional guidelines aimed at preventing discrimination and fostering a welcoming, inclusive, and patient-centered environment. According to participants, organizational practices that prioritize the biopsychosocial needs of PLHIV are fundamental to sustained engagement in care.

“It is imperative to strengthen specialized care services supported by qualified professionals committed to continuous follow-up and the active recruitment of new patients, in order to facilitate PLHIV retention.” (PV_25, female).

“During follow-up, it is essential to prioritize the biopsychosocial needs of PLHIV to ensure successful retention.” (PV_46, male).

These narratives indicate that retention is closely linked to how services are organized and experienced by patients, reinforcing the need for care models that extend beyond biomedical management.

Professional attitudes and capacity building

Participants consistently identified professional attitudes as a central determinant of retention. Three key elements were highlighted: welcoming practices, the reduction of stigmatizing behaviors, and continuous professional training.

Providers emphasized that respectful communication and empathetic listening are essential for building trust and strengthening the therapeutic bond between patients and health teams.

“Respectful listening is essential to build trust and a strong bond with the patient.” (PV_66, male).

Addressing internalized stigma within health services was also considered critical, as stigmatizing social representations of HIV can negatively influence professional conduct and patient engagement.

“It is imperative to address stigmatizing social representations of HIV that may influence health professionals’ behavior, promoting ethical and prejudice-free conduct.” (PV_66, female).

Continuous education and training were described as fundamental for improving clinical competence, communication skills, and sensitivity to patients’ social vulnerabilities.

“Ongoing training and education of health professionals are crucial to ensure high-quality care that includes clinical management, psychosocial support, and an understanding of each patient’s vulnerabilities.” (PV_52, female).

Commitment to follow-up and active monitoring

Providers underscored that effective retention requires a strong commitment to continuous follow-up, supported by multidisciplinary collaboration and active monitoring systems. Participants highlighted that multidisciplinary teams are essential for addressing the complex and intersecting needs of PLHIV.

“A multidisciplinary team composed of professionals from diverse fields physicians, nurses, social workers, psychologists, among others is essential to address the complex needs of PLHIV.” (PV_38, female).

Active monitoring systems were viewed as critical tools for identifying patients at risk of loss to follow-up. However, providers reported that limited training and staff shortages hinder the effective use of existing monitoring tools.

“Although monitoring tools such as the Clinical Monitoring System (SIMC), implemented by the Ministry of Health, exist to track PLHIV, there is limited training on their use and a shortage of professionals able to operate them.” (PV_47, male).

Participants also emphasized the importance of patient involvement in follow-up processes, highlighting adherence as a dynamic and collaborative process.

“Adherence is a dynamic process that must be built in partnership with the patient. It is essential to value the autonomy of PLHIV and ensure their inclusion in the design of treatment strategies.” (PV_68, female).

Re-engagement strategies for PLHIV

Re-engagement of patients lost to follow-up was described as a critical component of retention strategies. Providers identified three main approaches: active tracing, use of communication technologies, and systematic identification of reasons for disengagement.

Active tracing through phone calls, text messages, or home visits was considered essential to reconnect with patients who miss appointments or interrupt treatment.

“Active tracing of PLHIV can be done by phone calls, text messages, or even home visits, depending on the situation.” (PV_33, female).

Digital communication tools were highlighted as cost-effective and scalable strategies to support appointment reminders, health education, and adherence support.

“Using text messages or phone calls can be an effective tool for health education, appointment scheduling, and medication reminders.” (PV_45, male).

Understanding the underlying reasons for loss to follow-up was considered fundamental to tailoring re-engagement strategies to individual needs.

“It is imperative to investigate and understand the reasons behind loss to follow-up, which may include stigma, medication side effects, lack of social support, or financial hardship.” (PV_72, male).

Human and financial resources

Participants emphasized that the effectiveness of retention strategies is contingent upon the availability of adequate human and financial resources. Funding for retention-specific programs, investment in professional training, and sufficient staffing levels were identified as essential components of sustainable HIV care.

“The availability of specific funding for retention programs is vital, including resources for awareness campaigns, active tracing, and social support.” (PV_58, female).

“Investment in capacity building is essential to ensure service excellence. Teams must have the skills to identify patient vulnerabilities and provide comprehensive care.” (PV_66, male).

“Hiring social workers and psychologists is fundamental to provide social and psychological support, helping to combat isolation and stigma, which remain barriers to adherence.” (PV_42, female).

Theme 06 – state-level initiatives and projects to improve retention of PLHIV

Narratives from state-level HIV/AIDS program coordinators revealed the existence of multiple regional initiatives aimed at strengthening retention in care. These initiatives were described as being aligned with national guidelines of the Brazilian STI/HIV/AIDS Program, with a particular focus on improving ART adherence and reducing loss to follow-up.

“There are multiple regional projects and initiatives aimed at optimizing the retention of PLHIV. However, budgetary constraints limit the effective implementation of these initiatives. These projects align with the guidelines and objectives established by the National STI/AIDS Program. Their main goal is to strengthen adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART), which is essential for effective epidemic control.” (PV_39, female).

Participants described state-level strategies that include strengthening surveillance and case notification systems, training health professionals to deliver more personalized care, decentralizing services to primary care settings, combating stigma through targeted campaigns, and reinforcing partnerships with non-governmental organizations and research institutions.Together, these findings demonstrate that retention in HIV care in Manaus is shaped by both local service-level practices and broader policy and funding contexts, highlighting the importance of multilevel governance and sustained investment in retention-focused interventions.

Discussion

This study explored health care providers’ perspectives on strategies to improve retention in HIV care among PLHIV in Manaus, a city with one of the highest HIV detection and AIDS-related mortality rates in Brazil [2]. The findings demonstrate that retention in HIV care is a dynamic and multifactorial process, shaped by the interaction of social vulnerability, health system organization, professional practices, and broader policy and funding contexts.

Consistent with national [20, 21] and international [22–24] literature, providers identified poverty, food insecurity, unemployment, and transportation difficulties as major barriers to sustained engagement in care. These determinants reflect structural inequalities that disproportionately affect populations living in peripheral urban areas and remote regions of the Amazon. Similar challenges have been documented in other Brazilian settings [20, 21, 25] and low- and middle-income countries [26–28], reinforcing the notion that retention cannot be addressed solely through biomedical interventions, but requires coordinated social protection and intersectoral policies.

Stigma and discrimination, both external and internalized, emerged as persistent barriers undermining retention in care. Providers emphasized that fear of disclosure, social exclusion, and negative experiences within health services frequently lead to disengagement from care. These findings align with previous studies [13, 29–31] showing that stigma erodes trust in health services and compromises long-term adherence. Addressing stigma within health facilities through continuous professional training and humanized care practices is therefore central to improving retention outcomes [22, 32, 33].

At the service-delivery level, participants highlighted long waiting times, shortages of human resources, lack of privacy, and negative provider attitudes as critical obstacles to patient engagement. These barriers are not unique to Manaus and have been reported in other regions of Brazil [3, 22, 34] and internationally [35–38], particularly in overstretched public health systems The findings underscore the importance of patient-centered care models that prioritize respectful communication, confidentiality, and continuity of care, as recommended by global frameworks for HIV service delivery [22, 32, 33].

Providers emphasized that continuous professional training is vital to enhance clinical and communication skills, reduce discriminatory behavior, and improve adherence counseling. These recommendations align with the World Health Organization’s framework for person-centered HIV care, which identifies empathy, confidentiality, and respect as essential components of effective service delivery [5, 38, 39].

Furthermore, the findings reveal that systemic challenges such as insufficient human and financial resources hinder consistent follow-up and patient tracing. The lack of dedicated funding for retention programs and the underuse of monitoring systems, such as Brazil’s Clinical Monitoring System (SIMC), impede timely identification of patients at risk of loss to follow-up. Strengthening these systems, along with improved data management and inter-facility coordination, would enable more effective interventions.

Providers also recognized the potential of digital tools (e.g., mobile messaging, phone calls, telehealth platforms) to enhance treatment adherence and facilitate follow-up, especially in remote or rural areas of Manaus where geographic isolation creates significant barriers to continuous care. These findings highlight the growing importance of integrating digital health strategies into primary care delivery in underserved regions. Similar conclusions have been drawn in other studies [40, 41], which emphasize that telehealth interventions can mitigate access barriers, improve patient engagement, and support continuity of care in geographically dispersed populations.

Overall, the findings emphasize that improving retention in HIV care requires a comprehensive, multisectoral approach. Coordination between health facilities, social services, and Non-governmental Organizations (NGOs) can strengthen support networks and facilitate re-engagement of patients who are lost to care. Similar findings have been reported in other [22, 42], which highlight that interventions combining clinical, psychosocial, and community-based components are more effective in sustaining long-term engagement in HIV care. Therefore, efforts to enhance retention should not be limited to the health system alone but should integrate community mobilization, patient tracking mechanisms, and social support programs that respond to the complex realities faced by PLHIV.

Expanding the decentralization of ART and counseling services to primary care levels, as recommended by the Brazilian Ministry of Health [4], can significantly improve accessibility and continuity of HIV care. By addressing structural inequalities, strengthening professional capacity, and integrating community-based initiatives, Manaus can enhance early diagnosis, treatment adherence, and retention in care. These coordinated actions are essential for advancing equity and achieving the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets by 2030.

From a broader perspective, this local experience offers insights that extend beyond Manaus. The challenges and strategies identified by providers reflect common tensions faced by universal health systems in addressing chronic conditions that require long-term engagement. The findings contribute to national and global debates by illustrating how social vulnerability, service organization, and professional practices intersect to shape retention outcomes. While the contextual specificity of Manaus limits direct generalization, the lessons drawn from this study are relevant for other settings characterized by geographic barriers, social inequities, and constrained health system resources.

Limitations

The research was based on the experiences and subjective viewpoints of the participants; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to broader populations or settings. Nevertheless, the results provide valuable insights for strengthening HIV care programs. As the investigation relied on self-reported data, which could not be independently verified and may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Given the diversity of responses, we believe that the impact of these biases on data accuracy is minimal. Although not all health facilities in Manaus were included, the selected reference units are considered representative of the district. The inclusion of HIV/AIDS program coordinators from the state of Amazonas further strengthens the study’s robustness. Future research should focus on evaluating both clinical and non-clinical outcomes related to retention in care.

Conclusions

Retention in HIV care remains a major public health challenge in Manaus, where social vulnerability, stigma, and structural barriers continue to disrupt the HIV care continuum. This study demonstrates that loss to follow-up is not merely an individual behavior, but the outcome of complex and interrelated socioeconomic, psychosocial, and systemic factors.

Improving retention requires integrated strategies that extend beyond clinical management. Strengthening multidisciplinary collaboration, investing in continuous professional training, and promoting patient-centered, stigma-free, and humanized care are essential components of sustained engagement in HIV services. Expanding the decentralization of HIV services and integrating digital tools such as mobile messaging, telehealth, and electronic monitoring systems can facilitate follow-up and re-engagement, particularly in geographically remote and underserved areas of the Amazon region.

Sustained funding for retention-focused initiatives, adequate human resources, and stronger coordination between governmental and non-governmental organization actors are critical to ensuring continuity of care and long-term treatment success. By addressing both structural and service-level determinants of retention, health authorities in Manaus and Brazilian settings can strengthen the resilience of the HIV care system and accelerate progress toward achieving the UNAIDS 95–95–95 targets.
