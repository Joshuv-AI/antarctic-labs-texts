Engaging communities in public health practice: lived experience or lived expertise?
Authors: Lisa M Lee, Marilyn Metzler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUse of the term ‘expert’ implies specialized knowledge, which is the case for scientists as well as people immersed in communities. Naming this expertise signals to all parties the essential value of understanding the context in which public health decisions are implemented. We outline arguments for and concerns about viewing lived experience as a complementary expertise akin to scientific expertise.Keywords: Community engagement, Public health ethics, Decision making, Lived experience, Lived expertise

Introduction

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, there were renewed calls for meaningful and effective engagement of communities with lived experience in all aspects of public health practice, including research, public health data collection, policy development, outbreak prevention and response, as well as intervention development, implementation, and evaluation [1–3] Many of the recent calls for engaging with affected communities have arisen from the recognition that “community, power, and health are inextricably linked,” [2], p 804 and from public health’s centering of equity as the foundational approach to creating communities that provide members with opportunities to achieve their desired level of health [4].

Recently, some authors have called for community members to be treated as experts to signal that people and communities who experience health outcomes and health inequities are experts in their own experience and community resources [1]. In this paper, we explore the use of the term ‘expertise’ over ‘experience’ in the context of public health policy, research, and practice.

Engaging communities in the practice of public health

In the early 1980s, when a novel virus began infecting young and otherwise healthy men, persons affected by HIV demanded to be part of the decisions about public health prioritization and prevention efforts. HIV/AIDS community planning councils became a requirement for federal funding in 1990 through the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Care Act and one of the first comprehensive guides to community engagement for public health planning was published by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 1993 [5, 6].

Done well, engaging with affected communities to develop and implement public health activities can improve research and public health data collection, help create more effective intervention programs, and build power and collaborative governance that enables communities to work from within to improve health [7–10]. Done poorly, community engagement can be harmful or coercive, and reduce community power and public trust through tokenization and placation [11, 12].

Whether engagement is done well depends on several factors, including a commitment to shared decision making, engagement from the start of a project or change effort, and acknowledgement of power imbalance between scientific experts and persons and communities with lived experience [12, 13]. Contributing to this power imbalance is the perceived value of scientific expertise compared with that of communities with lived experience. Often, more value is assigned to scientific expertise of public health professionals, in part stemming from a belief that scientific facts alone are enough to change behaviors or social and economic conditions contributing to inequitable health outcomes [14]. Individuals and communities, however, rely on a combination of evidence, values, and their current and past experiences to make decisions that affect their health and well-being [15–17].

In addition to individual and community-level barriers to health, historically marginalized communities face long-standing systemic barriers that they alone cannot dismantle. To better balance the power of scientific information with these other inputs into health decision making, it is important to value all the inputs as different kinds of expertise.

Experience and expertise

The question of whether a person living in a community—for example, living with a particular health condition, engaging in a specific health behavior, living in a defined geographic area, or part of a group affected by historical and ongoing marginalization or racism—has lived experience or has gained enough experience to be considered an expert, or a person with lived expertise, requires a critical examination of what differentiates experience and expertise.

In its common meaning, experience connotes involvement in or practical engagement with an event, action, or set of circumstances. Experience can be quantified in several ways, for example by the number of times one engages in an action or set of actions or the duration of engagement, such as number of years doing a particular thing [18]. It implies, even for actions, a passive type of involvement void of reflection or integration [19, 20].

Expertise, on the other hand, connotes a deep understanding or skill gained by reflection and practice, a type of specialized knowledge or skills often cultivated through accumulation of experience. It implies an active or integrative involvement with facts, processes, or events [21].

An early pioneer in describing what made a person an expert, Janet Kolodner emphasized two characteristics of experts: “[E]xperts are more knowledgeable about their domain and an expert knows how to apply and use his (sic) knowledge more effectively than does a novice.” [19], p 497 Another early expertise scholar, Michelene Chi, described experts as people who demonstrate deep knowledge about a particular domain, and who demonstrate understanding of underlying principles and organized knowledge structures [22]. In addition, experts identify and recall patterns and relationships between features and among problems, and they do so faster and with deeper and more integrated cognitive processing compared with novices [22]. Expertise allows one to use deep knowledge, foundational principles, and detailed experiences to develop effective and generalizable problem solving [22].

A more contemporary description of expertise added dimensionality to earlier definitions. In addition to knowing, Harry Collins posited that experts possess tacit knowledge related to a particular topic or subject, which can only be acquired through immersion into a community of others who already have this type of knowledge [20]. An expert, according to Collins, also has “significant social contact” with other experts. He challenged the commonly held idea that by definition expertise is rare. There are domains, such as driving a car or using a computer, in which expertise, defined as tacit knowledge acquired by immersion in a community of others with tacit knowledge, is ubiquitous [20].

It is sensible then to assume that a person living in a particular community has expertise in that life or ‘domain’. The expertise, gained through social contact with others who have deep, tacit, and reflective knowledge of living in or being a part of that community, is a type of expertise analogous to expertise gained by scientists in their scientific domain. As Shaff and Hickson put it, “The communities we [engage with] are experts in their experiences.” [3], p150.

Use of expertise in the context of public health engagement

To make effective decisions, there are a number of inputs for public health practitioners to consider [23]. As an evidence-informed field, public health decisions are in part based on scientific evidence, which tells us what we can do. In addition to evidence, public health laws tell us what we are permitted to do and what we, as public health officials are obligated to do. To ensure that our decisions are desired by and acceptable to the communities who will be affected by them, it is important to also consider the values surrounding the decision, which guide what we should do. These include both public health values as well as the values of affected communities. Finally, we need to consider the social context of the communities that will be affected by the public health decision. We understand the social context by engaging expertise from people who are a part of the affected community [23].

Some individuals, motivated by their lived experience, become scientific experts in areas related to that lived experience. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the US CDC and many state public health departments recruited members of the LGBTQ+ community and people living with HIV to develop, implement, and evaluate HIV prevention programs. With further education and experience, many of these public health practitioners became scientists able to offer both scientific and lived expertise. In these and similar cases, such as sickle cell disease or mental health conditions, this dual expertise can be useful to inform public health decision making.

Bringing together complementary insights from scientific experts and context experts—people with lived expertise—reveals that both parties have essential expertise, without which an effective and equitable public health decision or policy cannot emerge. Use of the term ‘expert’ implies specialized knowledge, which is the case for both scientists as well as people immersed in communities. Each is an expert on different things that together improve the success of public health action. ‘Expert’ signals to all involved that each brings essential knowledge that others do not have. Naming this expertise signals to all parties the essential value of understanding the context in which a public health decision is implemented. It also requires that all parties—community members, public health scientists, and policy makers—recognize that each brings different pieces of the expertise needed to develop an effective public health effort.

The need for lived expertise in HIV prevention, for example, was illustrated early and often. At the start of the pandemic, the simple public health message was “use condoms.” This was scientifically sound advice. Data showing the protective effect of correct condom use were plentiful, yet many people at risk for HIV infection opted out of this life-saving intervention [24]. Reasons were based not on data or a misunderstanding of the message, but rather on the complex context of their experience. Condom use depended on individual circumstances such as stage of relationship, trust, fear of rejection or harm, and desire to protect oneself or one’s partner. It also depended on social norms where some social group norms were negative or suspicious about condom use. An understanding of this kind of lived expertise ultimately helped public health officials tailor messages using a harm reduction approach. It led to effective community campaigns to normalize condom use [25]. Without this lived expertise, the “use condoms” message was ineffective for many people at risk for HIV.

Recognizing that someone who has been living in a context that affects health brings specialized and socialized knowledge—that is, brings expertise—is a step toward equitable public health decision making. As DeWeger and colleagues observed, “Ultimately, [community engagement] initiatives will only be successful if organisations address power imbalances, share control with citizens and professionals and citizens view each other as legitimate and equal partners in the interventions.” [13], p 13 Recognition of lived expertise signals genuine engagement and true power sharing. It moves the engagement from lower-level and potentially harmful impacts such as informing to placate or consulting to tokenize communities toward authentic shared decision making, true collaboration, and co-creation [9, 12, 26]. This genuine community involvement and deference to community expertise can foster community ownership and ultimately foster equitable engagement in public health policy making [9].

Objections to use of the term expertise

There are at least three considerations to appraise when using the term ‘lived expertise’ in place of ‘lived experience’. The first is the lack of criteria for determining who in a community or with a particular experience is an ‘expert’. If a deep understanding and social immersion with others with similar expertise is required, how much time or how many experiences are required to move a person from having experience to a person with expertise? Can people of different generations be experts or is there a particular age associated with expertise? Or, are all people with experience an expert in that experience?

It is noteworthy here that the lack of specific criteria for determining expertise is a challenge within the scientific community as well. Scientists with various levels of experience serve as experts in public health decision making. Chi would describe them as having “relative expertise,” [27], p 23 as they have more expertise about science compared with non-scientist community members. The same could be said for persons living in a community, as they have more expertise about living in or being part of a particular community than anyone without this experience, including public health scientists. Between the scientific and non-scientific communities, use of the term ‘lived expertise’ could signal this relative level of community knowledge.

A second consideration with the use of the term ‘expertise’ is that some individuals within a community might not consider themselves or want to be considered by others as ‘expert’. Community members might be concerned about being viewed elitist. Others might push back on the term out of fear that it implies one knows everything there is to know on the subject and that the term lacks recognition that learning is a continuous, ongoing experience. Still others might be wary of the term due to growing public distrust of scientific experts, [28] worrying that this distrust will spill over to them if they are called ‘experts’. Conversations between public health practitioners and the people and communities they engage can clarify stated preferences. This approach reinforces a commitment to genuine collaboration, power sharing, and shared decision making.

A third consideration with using the term ‘expertise’ is that within affected communities, use of the term ‘expert’ or ‘expertise’ could create a differentiation within communities that could do harm. By deeming some people experts and others not, unintended conflict within a community could arise. Creating hierarchies by elevating some members over others without clear criteria of what makes a person an expert could be one of the ways that the process of engagement could harm communities long after public health practitioners are gone.

While there are no simple answers to these challenges, on balance it seems that there is reason to use the term ‘lived expertise’ over ‘lived experience’ in circumstances when the purpose of community engagement is to genuinely share power and decision making with communities that will be affected by public health policies, research, and practice. Bringing both scientific and lived expertise to the decision-making table signals that while scientific information is necessary for decision making, alone it is insufficient. Also necessary is expertise in the context in which a policy or practice will be developed and implemented, and that unique and important expertise comes from persons who are part of affected communities. By naming both inputs as expertise, we signal to scientists and communities alike that both inputs, while different, are equally necessary and valued in successful public health practice.

Conclusion

Engagement of persons living in a community—for example, living with a particular health condition, engaging in a specific health behavior, living in a defined geographic area, or part of a group affected by historical and ongoing marginalization or racism—in public health decisions that will affect them is fast becoming the standard in public health practice. Done well, this engagement is intentional and thoughtful in order to avoid further harm to historically marginalized communities. This will involve mutual understanding and respect for both scientific and lived expertise, resulting in collaborative decision making and more equitable and productive public health practice.

Using the term ‘lived expertise’ recognizes the critical nature of the contributions from people living in communities. While not without challenges, the use of the term ‘lived expertise’ in favor of ‘lived experience’ has an important role to play in public health deliberation and decision making. As one of several essential inputs, the ‘lived expertise’ terminology places the various decision-making considerations on complementary footing and signals to all parties that each input is of essential value.
