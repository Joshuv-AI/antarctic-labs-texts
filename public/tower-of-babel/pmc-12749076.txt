Mechanical response of randomly disposed and deep layered fill-soft soil composite foundation investigated via field loading tests
Authors: Minglin Zhou, Zhikui Liu, Hanying Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The construction method utilizing only drainage plates combined with direct surcharge preloading remains widely adopted in the construction and improvement of raw material storage yards on soft foundation soils along rivers and waterways. In such projects, the shallow fill layers are often designed as soft clayey strata. Through a large-scale field surcharge model test, this study investigates the influence of layered fill on composite foundation behavior—composed of randomly disposed, deep layered fill and soft clay—under stepwise surcharge loading of bulk mineral materials, with particular focus on the distribution and transfer mechanisms of additional stress. Analysis of in-situ stress and strain monitoring data reveals that the fill layer within 3.0–4.5 m below the surface acts as a stress-diffusing zone similar to an “artificial hard crust”, which develops dynamically and adaptively during loading; this study terms it the Self-Adaptive Hard Crust. In contrast, the fill below 4.5 m shows no evident stress-diffusion effect. An additional stress diffusion angle of 27° is recommended for such foundation conditions. Based on the two-layer foundation theory, the Boussinesq’s and the Flamant’s solutions, and the theoretical distribution in the Schmertmann’s method, an improved correction model for the additional stress coefficient is proposed. This model offers a practical and simplified method for accurately predicting the magnitude, distribution, and transfer of additional stress along the central axis of bulk storage yards during the design phase. The application of the proposed correction model to the design prediction for this project yielded a root mean square error (RMSE) of 27.3 kPa compared to the measured additional stress values. This represents a reduction of 58.1 kPa in RMSE relative to the discrepancy between the Boussinesq solution and measured data, demonstrating a marked improvement in prediction accuracy over the conventional method. Furthermore, a comparative study conducted on the Ma Steel blast furnace raw material stockyard engineering case confirmed the strong practical applicability and engineering value of the proposed model. The findings provide valuable reference for the construction, renovation, and related engineering practices of bulk storage yards along rivers and waterways worldwide.

Introduction

Globally distributed in lakes, rivers, and coastal areas, soft soils can induce a series of engineering problems if improperly treated. Taking the Yangtze River coastal region as an example, which serves as one of the world’s busiest inland freight waterways, the natural crust layer that typically overlies soft soil strata is often absent or significantly thin in this area. This poses considerable challenges for the construction of the bulk cargo storage yards. The surface soft soils in such regions are frequently covered by substantial amounts of miscellaneous fill, consisting of construction debris, excavated soil, and industrial solid waste, often dumped in a disordered manner. Under the dual constraints of limited land resources and environmental regulations, such sites are commonly leveled through simple cut-and-fill operations and subsequently used as bulk storage yards. Consequently, a composite foundation system is formed, characterized by thick miscellaneous fill overlying deep soft clay. For composite foundations comprising thick miscellaneous fill—primarily disordered waste soil—over soft clay layers, the highly uncertain engineering properties of the fill often lead to conservative treatment strategies in engineering practice. The fill is generally regarded as equivalent to, or even weaker than, the underlying soft clay layer in terms of bearing capacity. As a result, ground improvement commonly involves the installation of horizontal, vertical, or bidirectional reinforcement elements to enhance the foundation performance.

In the construction of raw material storage yards within the metallurgical industry, for composite foundations consisting of thick miscellaneous fill (primarily disordered waste soil) overlying soft clay, the method of applying preloading with raw ore materials combined with vertical plastic drainage boards and horizontal sand drainage blankets remains an environmentally and economically favorable approach. In such applications, properly accounting for the role of the thick miscellaneous fill is particularly important. However, in practical design, translating the mechanical contribution of the thick miscellaneous fill into reliable engineering safety indicators is challenging. Accurately predicting the distribution, magnitude, and transfer mechanisms of additional stresses within such composite foundations is crucial for advancing cost-effective design and ensuring long-term safety and stability.

In the historical development of relevant theories, Wolf1 first proposed the theory of elastic layered systems and its analytical method in 1935, laying a crucial foundation for this field. Building upon prior research, scholars further developed analytical methods and approximate solutions for the distribution of additional stresses in transversely isotropic elastic layered foundations under specific boundary conditions2–4. In 1943, the “two-layer system theory” was formally proposed5, followed by the establishment of corresponding stress calculation methods under the assumption of plane strain6. Over the past few decades, extensive research has focused on the consolidation characteristics, settlement development, and prediction of creep behavior in soft soils7–14. On the other hand, regarding the naturally occurring “hard crust layer–soft soil” double-layer foundation system, numerous scholars have investigated its bearing capacity mechanisms, deformation characteristics, and overall mechanical response15–20. Concerning reinforcement measures, some studies have systematically analyzed the mechanical behavior and vertical stress transfer laws of piles under static and cyclic loading through laboratory model tests and three-dimensional numerical simulations. This was achieved by employing rigid piles, semi-rigid piles, semi-rigid-flexible piles, and flexible piles to penetrate the soft soil layer, or by combining with geosynthetics to embed piles into underlying strata with better engineering properties21–23. In recent years, increased attention has been paid to the engineering benefits of natural hard crust layers in both practice and academia, with attempts to apply two-layer theory to guide the treatment of soft soil lacking such a crust. Numerous scholars24–29 have systematically studied the applicability, consolidation process, load-deformation relationships, and load transfer mechanisms of foundations improved using vertical, horizontal, or composite reinforcement elements, employing centrifuge tests, small-scale physical models, and numerical simulations. Furthermore, some research has utilized laboratory model tests and numerical methods to simulate the characteristics of stress distribution and diffusion under treatment methods such as geogrid-reinforced sand blankets (GRSB) on soft ground, floating lightweight cement-soil mixed piles, and lightweight foam concrete replacement layers (LFCRM)30–32. Through laboratory loading simulations, field plate load tests, and numerical analysis, several studies33–35 have further explored the relationship between the stress diffusion angle and factors such as the modulus ratio and the thickness-to-width ratio of the reinforced soil mass for soft soil foundations improved with sand-gravel cushions (or in combination with geogrids).

While extensive research has been conducted on homogeneous soft soil foundations, soft soil foundations with natural hard crust layers, and composite foundations with artificial reinforcements—leading to significant progress in understanding load transfer mechanisms, consolidation behavior, and settlement prediction—a systematic investigation remains notably lacking for composite foundations consisting of thick, disordered miscellaneous fill overlying soft soil layers. Specifically, under unreinforced conditions (only with vertical drains and a horizontal sand drainage blanket), the mechanism by which the miscellaneous fill layer influences the overall foundation response, as well as the patterns governing the diffusion, distribution, and transfer of additional stress, still constitutes a research gap. This knowledge deficit hinders the effective alignment of theoretical predictions with actual engineering performance, posing a persistent challenge for both academia and practice. One essential approach to advancing classical theories (e.g., Boussinesq, Mindlin, and Flamant solutions) lies in their appropriate modification based on field-measured data, thereby improving the consistency between design forecasts and real-world behavior. To date, studies on “disordered miscellaneous fill–soft soil” composite foundations remain relatively scarce. Existing research on fill materials such as construction waste and soil‑rock mixtures has focused primarily on their laboratory‑based compression characteristics and deformation behavior when used as engineering fill36,37. Other scholars have examined foundation settlement mechanisms and predictive formulas considering interactions between backfill and soft clay, with emphasis on the role of particle interlocking during settlement38,39. Additionally, some studies have analyzed the elastoplastic response of backfill layers via stress‑displacement curves, highlighting the critical influence of filler properties and strength on interfacial mechanical behavior, while systematically elucidating macroscopic mechanical performance, deformation modes, and shear mechanisms40–42. Nevertheless, comprehensive research on thick, disordered fill–soft soil composite systems remains insufficient. There is a particular lack of in the depth analysis regarding the functional mechanism of the fill layer within the overall foundation response and the laws governing additional stress diffusion, distribution, and transfer. Current studies have predominantly focused on the intrinsic compression and deformation properties of the fill material itself in laboratory settings, failing to adequately reveal its mechanical contribution within an actual foundation system—especially the dynamic response of thick miscellaneous fill under surcharge loading and its interaction mechanisms with the underlying soft soil. Moreover, due to variations in industry practice, geological settings, and project conditions—coupled with the predominant reliance of current research on laboratory model tests or small‑scale simulations, which lack sufficient validation under real‑field conditions—a significant gap persists between theoretical outcomes and their applicability to specific engineering practices. This gap constrains the development and practical adoption of reliable design methods for such composite foundations.

In light of the aforementioned gaps, this study aims to systematically investigate the mechanical behavior of a composite foundation comprising thick, disordered fill overlying soft soil under ore stockpile loading. A large-scale field loading model test was conducted to monitor the stress-strain response of the foundation under incremental loading. The test revealed that the shallow fill layer may undergo a dynamic evolutionary cycle during loading, involving a series of interrelated mechanical behaviors such as pore collapse and closure, particle rearrangement, generation of new pores, and overall soil compaction. Within a certain thickness range, the upper fill exhibited pronounced compressive hardening characteristics, forming a stress-diffusion effect akin to an artificial hard crust layer. This study further elucidates the formation mechanism of a “Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC)” within the thick disordered fill and clarifies its key role in stress diffusion and self-adaptive development. Building upon these findings and integrating the theoretical frameworks of the two-layer foundation theory, Boussinesq’s solution, Flamant’s solution, and the Schmertmann method43–45, an improved model for modifying the additional stress coefficient along with a corresponding calculation method is proposed. This model introduces three types of modification factors related to the following: the magnitude of additional stress at the base of the SAHC, the ratio of SAHC thickness to effective loading width, and the ratio of foundation depth to effective loading width. To validate the model’s reliability, the proposed method was applied to a major engineering case study. Comparative analysis with field-measured data and theoretical results from classical Boussinesq and Flamant solutions confirmed its superior prediction accuracy and practical applicability. This research provides a scientific and practical design basis for predicting the distribution, magnitude, and transfer patterns of additional stresses along the central axis of large bulk stockpiles on soft soil foundations adjacent to river systems, offering significant theoretical value and engineering application potential.

Full-scale field load test

Engineering background

The study area is located on a floodplain along the Yangtze River in Nanjing, in the lower-middle reaches of the river, serving as a raw material storage yard for an ironmaking plant. The yard is designed with three stockpile lines, each with a length of 430 m and a width of 30 m. The slope angle of the stockpiles is designed between 37 and 40°, with a maximum design load of 280 kPa at the base of the piles. Between the stockpile lines, stacker-reclaimer tracks are installed, with a subgrade bottom width of 10 m. Auxiliary facilities such as belt conveyors, transfer stations, plant roads, and underground pipelines are distributed along the shorter sides of the stockpile lines.

Engineering geological and hydrogeological conditions

The study area is underlain primarily by recent artificial fill (Qml) and Quaternary Holocene alluvial deposits (Q4al). The artificial fill stratum (Layer①) consists of miscellaneous fill and plain fill. The alluvial deposits are subdivided, from top to bottom, into: silty clay with organic matter (Layer②); silt interlayered with silty clay (Layer③); and an interbedded layer of silty clay, silt, and fine sand (Layer④). These are underlain by the approximately 15.0 m thick layer of fine sand. The composition and characteristic properties of each foundation soil layer are summarized in Table 1.

This project is primarily influenced by the phreatic water residing in shallow, unconsolidated strata. Recharged by atmospheric precipitation, the water table exhibits seasonal fluctuations, with a stable depth ranging between 1.50 and 3.00 m.

In-situ surcharge load test model

To guide large-scale stockpiling operations, this study conducted a field surcharge load test with systematic monitoring. The test area featured a stockpile with a base dimension of 60 m × 30 m and side slopes inclined at approximately 39°. The load was applied in two stages: the first stage reached a height of 7.0 m, and the second stage added 3.4 m, resulting in a total height of 10.4 m. Iron ore fines, with a unit weight of approximately 24 kN/m³, were used as the filling material. A horizontal drainage sand layer with a thickness of 0.3 m was placed on the ground surface. Prefabricated vertical drains (PVDs) were installed in a square pattern with a spacing of 2.0 m × 2.0 m to facilitate the dissipation of excess pore water pressure. These drains, with a length of 18.0 m, were embedded within the subsoil. The test conditions were designed to closely represent actual engineering scenarios. The layout of monitoring.

Monitoring instrumentation, measurement accuracy, and measures for data assurance

Surface settlement was monitored using a WILD N3 precision level, with a measurement accuracy of ± 0.1 mm. Subsurface settlement at different depths was measured with a magnetic ring-type CFC-40 settlement gauge, providing an accuracy of ± 0.5 mm. Excess pore water pressure was measured using vibrating wire piezometers, while the corresponding additional stress was quantified with vibrating wire earth pressure cells. Both sensor types were connected to a DKY-51 fully automatic data logger, which recorded readings with an accuracy of ± 0.5 Hz.

To isolate the effect of fluctuating water tables on the excess pore water pressure readings during loading, long-term groundwater level observations were conducted concurrently, achieving a precision of ± 1 mm. For quality assurance, a third-order bedrock benchmark was established 30 m from the periphery of the proposed stockpile site to serve as a stable reference. The monitoring program adhered to the principles of “fixed personnel and fixed instruments” throughout the duration. Furthermore, regular inspections and instrument calibrations were implemented to control systematic errors, thereby ensuring the objectivity and accuracy of the acquired data.

Based on the measured time-dependent variation of vertical induced stress (Fig. 2) and the depth-dependent distribution of its peak values (Fig. 3), it can be observed that the induced stress exhibits significantly nonlinear distribution characteristics within the deep layered fill. By extracting the peak stress differences between adjacent earth pressure cells under various surcharge heights, a distinct nonlinear inflection point is identified between EPC2 and EPC3. The stress difference at this location reached 2.30–2.45 times that between either EPC1–EPC2 or EPC3–EPC4. A remarkable phenomenon is the sharp reduction in measured induced stress within the depth range of 3–5 m (Fig. 2), where the peak induced stress consistently exhibited rapid attenuation and convergence under different surcharge heights (Fig. 3). This stress reduction trend remained consistent as the surcharge height increased. From the stress evolution behavior, it can be inferred that starting from a certain depth between 3.0 and 5.0 m below the surface, the induced stress demonstrates noticeable diffusion effects. The overlying layered fill above this depth acts as an artificial hard crust layer, contributing to stress dispersion, while the distribution of induced stress in the underlying fill layer resembles that of the soft clay stratum beneath.

To elucidate the specific mechanism underlying the stress diffusion characteristics of the crust-like layer formed in the fill above 5.0 m during surcharge loading, the corresponding physical and mechanical evolution process is summarized below in relation to the soil layer structure and loading stages:

The①₁ miscellaneous fill layer has a relatively high coarse-grained content, while the ①₂ layer contains relatively fewer coarse grains. Due to disordered backfilling, a certain number of irregular pores exist within the soil. Before loading, the groundwater level was near the bottom of the ①₁ layer, with most pores in this layer being void; pores in the ①₂ layer were primarily filled with gravitational water. During the initial loading stages (4 m and 5 m), under relatively low load levels, voids in the ①₁ layer were rapidly compressed and closed under basal stress. Pore water in the ①₂ layer was quickly expelled under additional stress, leading to gradual compression of the pores. Coarse particles such as slag, crushed stone, and construction waste in the miscellaneous fill underwent significant rearrangement, accompanied by the generation and re-compaction of local new voids or filling by surrounding fine particles. The pore morphology of the soil changed, and the overall structure tended to become denser, with a marked increase in strength, preliminarily exhibiting stress transfer behavior akin to the arching effect of a crust-like layer. Upon entering the first-stage design height (5 m to 7 m), as the load level increased, the arrangement of coarse particles weakened, the number of voids decreased significantly, while the rearrangement of fine particles and clay particles intensified. Gravitational water continued to be expelled from larger pores, and the soil experienced further compression. During this stage, adjustments in the soil skeleton morphology may have been accompanied by the generation of some new pores, which were gradually compacted again as the drainage consolidation process continued.

Overall, the fill above approximately 5.0 m exhibited strain-hardening characteristics, with continuous strength growth. After a prolonged rest period under the first-stage surcharge, the stress diffusion effect of the crust-like layer was effectively consolidated.

Vertical layered compression deformation by layer within the stratified fill and the overlying soft soil layer.

During the surcharge loading process, the applied stress induced differential layer-wise layered compression within the stratified foundation soils over time. This parameter was used to characterize the actual magnitude of layered compression in each soil layer throughout the duration of the test. Meanwhile, differences in the consolidation behavior of the layered soils also affected the transmission and distribution of the induced stresses. In the field test setup of this study, based on the vertical soil profile identified through drilling, earth pressure cells and magnetic settlement rings were installed at corresponding depths to ensure aligned measurement positions, supplemented by surface settlement markers. An additional magnetic ring was placed at 0.2 m below the ground surface. This configuration enabled accurate back-calculation of the layered compression in both the fill material and the overlying soft soil layer at various depths using the measured layered settlement data. The deformation modulus E₀ is a parameter that characterizes the deformation behavior of soil in its natural state. In definition, the deformation modulus E₀ of soil is equivalent to the elastic modulus (also known as Young’s modulus) E in general elastic theory. Assuming a vertical stress increment Δσ is applied to a soil column, resulting in a vertical strain increment Δε, the deformation modulus E₀ is defined as the ratio of the stress increment to the corresponding strain increment in the same direction45, expressed as E₀ = Δσ / Δε. It is typically determined through in-situ tests such as the field plate load test or laboratory tests including triaxial compression tests.

In this experimental study, the additional stress in the foundation soil corresponds directly to the monitoring points of the magnetic settlement rings. Based on the theoretical definition of E₀, the authors calculated the increment of additional stress and the corresponding layered compression and strain increment for each soil layer—such as layer ①₁ (0–2.1 m) and layer ①₂ (2.1–4.0 m)—using measured stress values and layered settlement data. This approach enabled the estimation of the incremental deformation modulus for each layered soil under various surcharge loading pressures.

The average incremental additional stress for each soil layer can be estimated linearly from measured stress values. With reference to the computational model used in laboratory uniaxial compression tests, the ratio of the calculated layered compression to the layer thickness may be taken as the strain increment under the corresponding stress increment. Under the influence of surcharge loading at various stages, the ratio of the average incremental additional stress to the incremental strain in the in-situ layered soil is interpreted by the authors as an increase relative to the original deformation modulus.

In engineering practice, geotechnical investigation reports typically provide only the compression modulus (Es) obtained from conventional laboratory oedometer tests. When the parameter E₀ is required, it is commonly estimated from Es using empirical relationships by applying an empirical correction factor. Theoretically, Es should be greater than E₀; however, due to factors such as sample disturbance and differences between field load test standards and laboratory compression test protocols, E₀ is usually greater than Es in practice.

As indicated by comparative studies between E₀ from field plate load tests and Es from laboratory compression tests, reference46 reports an empirical E₀/Es ratio of 1.05–2.90 (average: 1.90) for silt and silty clay, with the ratio approaching unity in softer soils. Reference47, incorporating small-strain deformation characteristics, proposed an empirical relationship among Es, E₀, and the small-strain modulus. Reference48 suggests that, when using the laboratory compression modulus for deformation calculations, the E₀/Es ratio may be preliminarily multiplied by an empirical factor of 1.5–2.0, followed by appropriate reduction according to the in-situ confining pressure of the soil. Based on porous media theory, Liang et al.49 derives a computational method for estimating the soil deformation modulus from laboratory compression modulus. According to the established relationship curve between deformation and compression moduli, the E₀/Es ratio ranges approximately from 1.2 to 1.5 when Poisson’s ratio υ = 0.4. In Sect. 4.1, to validate the applicability of the proposed method for estimating incremental deformation modulus in stratified soils, parameters of the ② layer (silty clay) were selected as the analysis subject. Referencing the empirical E₀/Es relationship, coefficients of 1.0, 1.2, and 1.5 were respectively adopted to quantitatively evaluate the SAHC effect in this research.

From the loading stages of 4 m and 5 m up to the first-stage design height of 7.0 m, followed by a 20-day rest period, layers ①₁ and ①₂ (depth 2.1–4.0 m) exhibited continuous rapid compression deformation (Fig. 4a), accounting for 65.9% and 60.8% of their total layered compression, respectively. The estimated deformation modulus for these layers increased by 3.4 MPa and 3.1 MPa, respectively (Table 3). In contrast, although layers ①₃ and ② (depth 7.5–10.0 m) also underwent rapid compression deformation (Fig. 4a), their increments in deformation modulus were only 0.9 MPa and 1.6 MPa, respectively (Table 3). Meanwhile, the intermediate layer ①₂ (depth 4.0–6.3 m) showed relatively small layered compression. This pattern of layered compression deformation essentially persisted until the end of the monitoring period in this study (Fig. 4b, Table 3).

Throughout the surcharge loading process, the fill above approximately 4.0 m depth exhibited rapidly increasing layered compression, demonstrating clear strain-hardening behavior. The estimated increase in deformation modulus in this upper zone was more than twice that of the underlying soil layers. From an engineering perspective, this meets the criteria for a double-layer foundation system, whereby the upper stratum functions similarly to an artificial stiff crust.

During the loading phase from 5 m to 7 m, layer ①₁ experienced a sharp increase in layered compression (Fig. 4a), indicating a potential risk of exceeding its ultimate bearing capacity—similar to the failure of an artificial stiff crust effect. However, this was simultaneously accompanied by a rapid increase in its deformation modulus (Table 3), which ultimately helped it adapt to and consolidate its role as an artificial stiff crust. A comparison of the variations in layered compression between layer ①₂ (depth 2.1–4.0 m) and layers ①₃ and ② (depth 7.5–10.0 m) during loading reveals a dynamic process: their compression magnitudes were initially similar, then that of layer ①₂ lagged behind, later caught up, and eventually exceeded that of the deeper layers (Fig. 4a, b). This illustrates that the fill above approximately 4.0 m depth dynamically developed self-adaptive characteristics during the formation of a crust-like layer, consistent with the concept of the Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC) proposed at the beginning of this paper.

Diffusion effects and distribution patterns of vertical induced stress

Foundation settlement prediction is a critical aspect of geotechnical design in civil engineering. The reliability of its calculation and forecasting depends not only on parameters and model selection but, more importantly, on the accurate determination of the compressible stratum depth and the precise estimation of the magnitude and distribution of induced stress with depth. Stockpile loads, acting directly on the ground surface, are considered perfectly flexible and are generally assumed to fully conform to ground deformation. The resulting contact pressure is thus often equated with the surcharge pressure. In the absence of a natural stiff crust or a specially designed artificial crust, the distribution and magnitude of induced stress in soft ground would theoretically approximate the Boussinesq’s or Flamant’s solutions; however, field observations frequently deviate from these theoretical predictions. Even when a crust-like layer develops during loading—consistent with the two-layer foundation theory—reapplying the Boussinesq’s or Flamant’s solutions based on the diffused interfacial stresses still leads to significant discrepancies from actual field behavior. Although previous studies have extensively investigated stress diffusion due to artificial crust layers, the practical applicability of these approaches remains limited. This is often due to oversimplifying assumptions, computationally intensive procedures, reliance on high-level programming, or lack of project-specific customization. Consequently, these methods have yet to adequately resolve the practical challenges associated with accurate settlement prediction and cost-effective design optimization.

Based on field-measured data from a large-scale surcharge load test, this study conducted a comparative analysis with the Boussinesq’s solution to investigate the distribution and transmission mechanisms of vertical induced stress beneath the center of a stockpile. The research focuses on conditions involving deep layered fill over soft clay, where iron ore fines were directly placed as surcharge material for drainage consolidation, in addition to the installation of PVDs and a horizontal sand drainage layer. The findings offer a scientific, practical, and straightforward methodology for accurate prediction of the magnitude and distribution of foundation-induced stress in similar engineering projects during the design stage.

The stress diffusion angle varies with soil type. When the upper layer consists of stiff soil overlying soft soil, Karl Terzaghi, along with references33,50,51, indicates that the stress diffusion angle (θ) should not exceed 30° in practical engineering. This study proposes a value of θ = 27° for similar projects, which is generally consistent with existing research findings.

Bulk stockyards constructed on similar soft ground typically have base widths ranging from 25 to 60 m. The field test presented in this study utilized a stockpile with a base width of 30 m. To enhance the applicability of the conclusions regarding the distribution and transfer of the induced stress field in analogous engineering contexts, a comparative analysis was conducted with a practical engineering case featuring a larger base width and higher load level. The overview of this case is introduced below.

Case study description

In the design of the raw material yard for the blast furnace system of Maanshan Iron and Steel Company, the stockpile has a base length of 600 m and a width of 50 m. The ground improvement scheme employed included the installation of prefabricated vertical drains (PVDs), dynamic compaction in shallow layers, and preloading with surcharge of iron ore fines for drainage consolidation. The loading process was carried out in three stages, with designed surcharge heights of 5 m, 9 m, and 13 m, resulting in a maximum design base pressure of 350 kPa. The monitoring section of the actual project is located on a Grade I terrace of the Yangtze River floodplain, exhibiting a typical binary structure in morphology. The original strata, from top to bottom, are distributed as follows: a 2.5 m thick layer of miscellaneous fill (disordered backfill) in a loose state; underlain by a 7.5 m thick layer of mucky silty clay in a fluid-plastic state; followed by a 12.0 m thick interbedded layer of mucky silty clay, silt, and fine sand, ranging from fluid-plastic to loose or slightly dense states; the lower strata mainly consist of fine sand, locally interlayered with fluid-plastic mucky silty clay and soft to stiff silty clay.

To guide the safe sequential loading in subsequent stockpile strips of the raw material yard, a comprehensive stress and strain monitoring system was installed on a previously completed strip. Along the central longitudinal axis of the stockpile, vertical induced stress monitoring points were established at depths of 0.6 m, 4.4 m, 6.7 m, 9.5 m, 12.7 m, 15.1 m, and 22.0 m below the surface. Prior to stockpiling, conventional dynamic compaction was applied to improve the shallow disordered miscellaneous fill. During the tamping process, waste steel slag, excavated gravel and block stones from surrounding earthworks, and residual or slope-wash cohesive soils were simultaneously backfilled, forming a shallow artificial hard crust layer. This crust layer is purely flexible and exhibits properties similar to the Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC) described in this study. After dynamic compaction, a 2.95–4.05 m thick “slag-improved layer” (averaging about 3.2 m within the monitoring area) was formed, primarily composed of the treated miscellaneous fill. Due to the dynamic compaction effect, the properties of the approximately 2.5 m thick mucky silty clay layer immediately beneath the improved layer were enhanced, transitioning from a fluid-plastic to a fluid-soft plastic state, with localized areas reaching a stiff plastic state.

From the initial stockpiling until reaching the first-stage height of 5.0 m, the material was left to rest for 105 days, after which frequent stacking and reclaiming operations began—a phase that was monitored for 307 days. After the second-stage loading reached approximately 9.0 m, it was allowed to settle for only 12 days before production activities resumed. Due to this short consolidation period, the induced stresses had not fully stabilized, and variations in material density resulted in a back-calculated surcharge height of about 8.5 m for the second stage. Owing to project scheduling constraints, field monitoring was terminated after a total of 380 days covering both the first- and second-stage loading. Based on these observations, it can be inferred that the thickness of the Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC) in this case was approximately 3.0–4.0 m, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Given that the length-to-width ratio of the stockpile strip exceeds 10, the scenario can be appropriately analyzed under plane strain conditions.

For similar engineering problems, the industry standard involves determining the stress diffusion angle and then using the additional stress diffused to the bottom of the stiff crust layer as the interfacial stress between the upper and lower layers of the two-layer foundation system. Beneath the fill, Layer ② is muddy silty clay interbedded with thin layers of silt and silty sand. Layer ③ is silt with silty clay inclusions, and Layer ④ is silty clay interlayered with silt and silty sand. Both Layers ③ and ④ belong to the category of interbedded cohesive soil, silt, and silty sand. The underlying layer consists of medium-dense to dense silty fine sand. Overall, the site exhibits a stratigraphic structure with strong horizontal permeability, conducive to drainage consolidation. The sand cushion at the stockpile base and the approximately 2.1 m thick coarse-grained fill layer near the surface form a favorable shallow horizontal drainage path. The installed PVDs (Prefabricated Vertical Drains) extend to or into Layer ③, creating an effective vertical drainage channel. The primary target consolidation layer contains thin silt and silty sand interbeds, and the secondary target consolidation layer has an interbedded structure of cohesive soil, silt, and silty sand. These conditions support the adoption of Schmertmann’s IZ distribution model for developing a stress coefficient Id correction model, as the drainage paths are generally consistent with the geological basis of Schmertmann’s research model.

This study focuses on a large-scale ore stockyard project located in a first terrace floodplain geomorphic unit of the middle and lower Yangtze River region. The area is characterized by typical binary structured stratigraphy: upper soft cohesive soils (possibly including a natural stiff crust layer 1–4 m thick), middle-upper interbedded soils of silt and silty sand, and lower sand layers. Moderately to low-compressibility sand layers are generally encountered within about 15–30 m below the surface, with bedrock depth typically within 50 m. The width of the stockyard strips is usually no less than 30 m, and the equivalent loading width B₀ at the ground surface exceeds 17 m. Assuming a SAHC thickness of 3.0 m in this study, the equivalent loading width at the base of the SAHC after stress diffusion is not less than 20 m.

With reference to Schmertmann’s IZ distribution model for sandy soil, when Z/B₁ = 2.0, the depth Z at which foundation stress approaches zero reaches 40 m. Combined with actual monitoring results from existing similar projects, this depth provides a considerable safety margin for calculating the compression layer deformation. Therefore, it is reasonable to adopt Z/B₁ = 2.0 as the depth for zero additional stress when applying the triangular Id model for stress correction. This assigned value is also consistent with the influence factor (IZ) distribution model proposed by Schmertmann for sandy foundations.

For the zone above the base of the Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC), additional stresses are calculated using Boussinesq’s or Flamant’s theory. Below the SAHC base, the additional stresses are still initially estimated using Boussinesq’s or Flamant’s solutions at corresponding depths, but are then modified by referencing the strain influence factor distribution model from Schmertmann’s elastic theory solution. This approach is used to investigate the distribution and magnitude of the vertical additional stress correction factor, Id, in the diffused stress zone. For similar engineering projects, this method holds promise for resolving the challenge of accurately predicting the distribution, magnitude, and transfer law of additional stresses along the centerline of a stockpile after stress diffusion.

Based on Schmertmann’s model, the distribution of Id is approximately assumed to be triangular. Beyond a normalized depth of Z/B₁ ≥ 2, Id is considered negligible. The large-scale field model test in this study, as well as the full-scale engineering case validated, were both located in river floodplain settings along the middle-lower Yangtze River. These sites exhibit a typical binary structure: upper soft cohesive soils underlain by sandy layers.

Beneath the fill, the mucky silty clay layer contains intermittent thin layers of silt and silty sand. Towards the bottom of this mucky layer and in the underlying soils, the frequency of these thin silt and silty sand layers increases significantly, resulting in an interbedded sequence of cohesive soil, silt, and silty sand—hereafter referred to as “interlayered soil.” This interlayered stratum is underlain by a sand layer. The presence of thin silt and silty sand layers within the interlayered soil enhances drainage consolidation. However, the composition of this interlayered soil is uncertain—whether it is dominated by cohesive soil or by silt/sand—and its lateral distribution is highly heterogeneous, even within a single project site. In the test project, the mucky silty clay is underlain by approximately 10 m of interlayered soil. The PVDs extend to the top of this zone. The combination of the underlying interlayered soil, the sand layer, the PVDs, and the sand cushion at the base of the stockpile creates a drainage path analogous to two-way drainage. Consequently, the interlayered soil in the test project behaves similarly to sandy soil in terms of drainage consolidation. In the full-scale engineering case, the PVDs penetrate into the underlying sand layer, resulting in true two-way drainage. This particular geological configuration—where the soil profile permits the establishment of an effective two-way drainage mechanism—is likely the key reason why it was feasible to adapt Schmertmann’s theoretical distribution of the vertical strain influence factor for sandy soils, forming the basis for the modified model proposed in this study. Should the interlayered soil be significantly thicker (creating a condition intermediate between one-way and two-way drainage), or if the target soft soil layer is underlain by clay (resulting in one-way drainage only), the applicability of the proposed stress correction model under such alternative engineering conditions would require further investigation.

The formation of a Self-developed Artificial Hard Crust (SAHC) within thick fill during loading is likely attributed to the high proportion of coarse particles and the layered nature of the shallow fill, as well as the fact that the fine-grained soils are not highly compressible (such as peat or muck). Under the initial surcharge heights of 4 m and 5 m, the shallow miscellaneous fill was directly loaded, leading to rapid particle rearrangement and void compression. This resulted in significant, short-term compression and rapid hardening, substantially increasing strength in a short period and enabling arching effects similar to an artificial hard crust even under relatively low loads. At this stage, the primary contribution to SAHC likely came from the approximately 2.1 m thick layer of miscellaneous fill (Layer ①1) near the surface, where the skeletal support of the high proportion of coarse particles played a dominant role.

As the surcharge height increased from 5 m to 7 m, the additional stress became considerable. It is probable that the arching effect of the 2.1 m thick surface miscellaneous fill (Layer ①1) had reached its limit and partially failed, after which the underlying miscellaneous fill (Layer ①2, depth 2.1–4.0 m) began to serve as the main load-bearing layer. Meanwhile, the miscellaneous fill within the shallow SAHC zone continued to undergo further particle rearrangement and densification through increased deformation. By this stage, coarse particles, fine particles, and clay particles had likely been compacted and hardened into an integrated whole, fully establishing the stress diffusion effect characteristic of SAHC. Overall, the development of the layered SAHC involved simultaneous and interrelated processes: compression and closure of voids, particle rearrangement, pore compaction, and load transfer between successive bearing layers in the stratified fill. When the surcharge height reached 9 m and 10.4 m, the behavior of SAHC likely became consistent with that of normally consolidated soils undergoing drainage consolidation. Further development of engineering properties at this stage was likely governed by the characteristics of the fine-grained soil components. The micromechanical and microstructural mechanisms underlying SAHC formation—influenced by the composition, content, properties of discarded fill materials, and the thickness of layered fills under surcharge loading—warrant further in-depth investigation.

Conclusion

Based on the two-layer foundation theory, the concept of a Self-Adaptive Hard Crust (SAHC) is proposed. In a combined foundation system consisting of thick, discarded layered fill over soft soil, where only prefabricated vertical drains (PVDs) are installed, a dynamic and self-developing stress diffusion effect is observed within a depth range of 3.0 m to 4.5 m below the surface as the surcharge loading progresses.An optimal SAHC thickness of approximately 4.0 m is recommended. This thickness is not proportional to the total depth of the thick fill layer. The additional stress characteristics within the fill soil beneath the SAHC do not exhibit its stress-diffusion effect and remain largely consistent with those in the underlying soft clay. For similar projects, a stress diffusion angle of 27° is proposed for the SAHC.Based on empirical observations of the self-adaptive stress diffusion zone within thick, discarded layered fill, a modified model for the additional stress coefficient is proposed. This model provides a practical and validated calculation method for large-scale bulk material stockpiles—a specific scenario not adequately addressed by classical Boussinesq’s and Flamant’s theoretical solutions.
