Periventricular radionecrosis after conventionally fractionated radiation for low grade meningiomas
Authors: Cecilia Jiang, Tara McWilliams, Melanie Berger, Emily S Lebow, Harper Hubbeling, Elizabeth Azar, James D Kolker, Suneel N Nagda, Goldie Kurtz, Jennifer Wei Zou, Ryan Scheuermann, Ajay Kumar, Michelle Alonso-Basanta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadionecrosis (RN) is a serious late complication of radiotherapy (RT). While its risk is well characterized after stereotactic radiosurgery, RT dose thresholds for development of RN following conventionally fractionated RT (CFRT) are poorly defined; in the periventricular region, the risk of RN has not been systematically investigated. Our study objectives are therefore to estimate the incidence of periventricular white matter radionecrosis (PVRN) following CFRT for low grade meningiomas and identify associated clinical and dosimetric predictors. We chose a cohort of low-grade meningiomas (World Health Organization grade I–II) for this purpose because of their low recurrence risk, thus minimizing diagnostic uncertainty between RN and progression.MethodsThis is a single-institution retrospective analysis of 135 adult patients who received CFRT between June 2011 and March 2023. The periventricular white matter was retrospectively contoured and dosimetric details to this region were extracted. PVRN was radiographically diagnosed and graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5.0). Univariate analysis was performed to assess associations between PVRN and clinical or dosimetric variables. Logistic regression using restricted cubic splines and segmented models was used to identify dosimetric constraints predictive of PVRN.ResultsMedian RT dose was 59.4 Gy (Gy) in 33 fractions; median follow-up was 66.5 months (range 1-151 months). 15% (n = 20) developed RN, with 11% (n = 15) PVRN and 6% (n = 8) grade 2 + PVRN. Overlap between the planning target volume and periventricular structures, as well as multiple high-dose constraints, were significantly associated with PVRN. V55 Gy to the periventricular substructure was the strongest predictor for both any-grade and grade 2 + PVRN; between 0 and 8 cc, V55 Gy values of 2.7, 3.6, and 4.8 cc were associated with < 5%, < 10%, and < 20% risk of grade 2 + PVRN, respectively. After excluding patients with prior cranial RT, V55 Gy remained the most predictive constraint with cutoff values of 2.7 and 3.1 cc associated with < 5% and < 10% risk of grade 2 + PVRN.ConclusionsOverlap with the periventricular region is associated with risk of symptomatic RN among patients receiving conventionally fractionated radiotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-025-02745-1.Keywords: Periventricular, Radionecrosis, Meningioma, Dosimetric predictors, Conventional fractionation

Background

Radionecrosis (RN) is a well-documented late complication of cranial radiotherapy (RT) associated with significant and long-standing morbidity [1]. It is primarily thought to be the result of radiation-induced vascular injury leading to dysregulated endothelial permeability, edema, and ischemia in white matter [2]. Most of the existing literature on RN focuses on stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), where high-dose constraints, such as V12 Gy (volume receiving ≥ 12 Gy) in single fraction, have consistently been predictive of RN [3].

However, the risk factors and dose thresholds for RN following conventionally fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) remain less well defined. A historical study estimated a 5% risk of RN after 50 Gy of CFRT was delivered to two-thirds of the brain, though this was based on outdated RT techniques; limited imaging capabilities also presented a significant barrier to distinguishing RN from tumor progression especially in high grade entities [4–6].

Additionally, certain brain substructures may exhibit heightened vulnerability to RN [7, 8]. The periventricular white matter, known for its relative hypovascularity, is particularly susceptible to vascular injury [9]. In elderly patients, cerebral small vessel disease commonly occurs in the periventricular region and is associated with faster functional decline than small vessel disease in other brain regions [10]. These observations led to our hypothesis that RN, a radiation-induced vascular injury, may disproportionately affect the periventricular region and contribute to morbidity.

To our knowledge, no previous study has assessed the incidence or predictors of periventricular RN (PVRN). Therefore, we evaluated the incidence of PVRN compared to non-periventricular white matter (deep and subcortical) RN (WMRN) in patients with low-grade meningiomas treated with CFRT. This is the first study to characterize differential rates of PVRN and identify any clinical and dosimetric factors that may predict for its development.

Methods

Study cohort and clinicopathological details

This retrospective single-institution study was approved by the institutional review board. Inclusion criteria consisted of adult patients over the age of 18 with a diagnosis of low grade meningioma who received either definitive or adjuvant CFRT between June 2011 and March 2023. CFRT was defined as < 3 Gy per fraction for a minimum of 15 fractions. Prior cranial RT and proton therapy were included. Patients lacking post-RT imaging were excluded. Extracted clinical data included age, gender, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, meningioma grade and location, Ki-67, and surgical status (determined radiographically and categorized into Simpson grade I-III, or complete resection; Simpson grade IV, or subtotal resection; and Simpson grade V, or biopsy alone).

Radiotherapy details

All patients underwent CT simulation with a thermoplastic mask for immobilization. Baseline and post-operative MRIs were fused for treatment planning, which was in accordance with established national recommendations and guidelines [11]. Patients received either intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) on the Varian linear accelerator (Varian, Palo Alto, CA) or proton beam therapy (inclusive of pencil beam scanning or passive scatter) on the IBA proton therapy unit (IBA, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium) with daily image-guided radiotherapy. Extracted RT parameters included total dose, number of fractions, and modality (proton, photon, or mixed).

Periventricular delineation and dosimetric extraction

Within the treatment planning system (TPS), two periventricular structures were retroactively contoured on each RT plan’s simulation CT: a 5-mm radial expansion (“PV5mm”) and a 1-cm expansion (“PV1cm”) around the lateral ventricles. This method was chosen as an easily reproducible way to approximate the location of the periventricular white matter, which can project up to 1.3 cm from the ventricular surface; beyond this threshold, non-periventricular white matter is expected to predominate [12]. Notably, 1 cm expansions were similarly used in another study to approximate this region [13]. In our study, PV5mm and PV1cm were created by first manually contouring the body and the anterior, posterior, and inferior horns of the lateral ventricles on the simulation CT; afterwards, the structures were radially expanded by 5 mm–1 cm for PV5mm and PV1cm, respectively, which were cropped from anatomical barriers such as the ventricles, brainstem, cerebellum, and bone.

Two additional structures, hereafter referred to as “PTV-PV5mm” and “PTV-PV1cm”, included the portion of planning target volume (PTV) that overlapped with PV5mm and PV1cm, respectively; if multiple dose levels were present, the highest dose PTV was used. The volume of these structures was used as an approximation of the amount of high-dose RT overlap with the periventricular region.

Dose-volume histograms (DVH) were generated for PV5mm and PV1cm and the following dosimetric parameters were extrapolated from each: Dmax (maximum dose to either PV5mm or PV1cm), mean (mean dose to PV5mm or PV1cm), D0.03cc (dose to 0.03 cm3 of PV5mm or PV1cm), D1cc (dose to 1 cm3 of PV5mm or PV1cm), D2cc (dose to 2cm3 of PV5mm or PV1cm), and V40 Gy to V60 Gy in 5 Gy increments. These parameters were chosen in order to characterize the moderate and high-dose overlap with the periventricular region.

Follow-up evaluation

After RT, patients were followed with serial brain MRIs and clinical evaluations. RN was defined radiographically by new area of enhancement with corresponding T2/FLAIR hyperintensity within the irradiated field. When available, advanced perfusion MRI had to demonstrate low rCBV in the area of interest. RN was confirmed by a consensus of a neuroradiologist and a radiation oncologist and classified as periventricular (PVRN) or non-periventricular white matter RN (WMRN). Each case was clinically graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. The brain MRIs of patients identified with RN were retroactively fused with their original RT plans, and the area of RN was contoured to assess dose to this area.

Statistical analysis and endpoints

The primary endpoint was the development of any-grade PVRN. Secondary endpoints included symptomatic (grade 2+) PVRN, WMRN, and overall RN. Summary statistics for patient characteristics and dosimetric variables are presented as number and percent for categorical variables and median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to identify clinicopathologic and RT variables that significantly varied between a variety of RN cohorts. Univariable logistic regression was used to identify dosimetric variables associated with increased incidence of PVRN. Logistic regression analyses were summarized using odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan-Meier curves were used for survival analysis. The main survival endpoints were time to RN and time to PVRN. These were defined from the time between RT completion.

Logistic regression models were used to estimate the univariable relationships between any-grade PVRN and each dose constraint. Each dose constraint was fit using a restricted cubic spline with 3 knots at the 0.10, 0.5, and 0.90 quantiles to allow for nonlinearity in the logarithmic odds of PVRN. Marginal effect plots were created to visualize the predicted probability of PVRN. Five measures of predictive performance were then examined for each constraint: (1) Nagelkerke R-squared, (2) Brier score, (3) Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), (4) Calibration slope, and (5) Calibration intercept. Each dose constraint was then assigned five separate rankings for each of these five statistical measures, whereby lower rank corresponded to better performance. Each of these rankings were summed together for a global rank. The dose constraint with the lowest global rank score was then identified. Given the scarcity of higher dose constraint values, we limited our subsequent analysis to the range of dose constraint values that had the densest, and thus most reliable, data. Odds ratios (ORs) for the likelihood of PVRN were calculated within this data-dense range, as were the minimum dose values corresponding to clinically interpretable risk levels (5%, 10%, 20%, and/or 30%), which were identified based on an inverse logit transformation of the spline-fitted logistic regression model. Bootstrapped confidence intervals were generated around these predicted probabilities. This process was then repeated on the same dose constraints with grade 2 + PVRN as the endpoint of interest. Finally, in order to assess the potential modifying effect of prior cranial RT on our findings, an interaction term between prior RT status and the top-performing dose constraint was tested in a logistic regression model utilizing restricted cubic splines.

Results

Patient and treatment characteristics

135 patients were included in the final analysis. Patient characteristics are provided in Table 1. Median age was 57 years. Most patients had WHO grade II meningiomas (63%), and the median Ki-67 index was 5%. Nearly one-fifth (18%) received overlapping prior cranial RT. The median prescribed dose was 59.4 Gy delivered in 33 fractions (range 50.4–63 Gy). Proton therapy was used in 65% of patients, photon therapy in 22%, and mixed-modality RT in 13%.

Median follow-up was 62.8 months (range 1-151 months). At the date of last follow-up, 18% of patients had died and therefore median survival time was not calculable.

Incidence and patterns of radionecrosis

Twenty patients (14.8%) developed RNat a median of 12 months after RT (range 5-101 months). Of these 20 cases, 50% were CTCAE grade 1, 15% were grade 2, and 35% were grade 3. On univariate analysis, higher WHO grade and baseline ECOG performance status were the only clinicopathological features significantly associated with development of RN (Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, 90% of patients with RN had WHO grade II disease compared to 57.3% of patients without RN (p = 0.006), and 25% of patients with RN had ECOG 2 prior to CFRT compared to 6.1% without RN (p = 0.024). Although ECOG performance status was significantly associated with development of RN when treated as either an ordinal or categorical variable, this association appeared to be primarily driven by the ECOG 2 group; no patients with ECOG 3 developed RN, and there was no statistically significant association between ECOG and RN when ECOG was collapsed into 0 vs. 1 + or < 2 vs. 2+ (p > 0.05).

When grouped by RT modality, the proton therapy cohort had numerically higher rates of RN than the photon cohort but this was not statistically significant (17% of proton cohort vs. 10% of photon cohort, p = 0.555). This was consistent when assessing any-grade PVRN (13% proton vs. 7% photon, p = 0.514) and grade 2 + PVRN (8% proton vs. 0% photon, p = 0.191).

Among the 20 cases of RN, 15 were PVRN and 5 were WMRN. Representative cases of PVRN and WMRN are shown in Fig. 1. Of those who developed RN, median time to RN was 15.9 months for the PVRN cohort vs. 12 months for the WMRN cohort (p = 0.39). Figure 2A shows the Kaplan-Meier curve for time to PVRN. PVRN cases were CTCAE grade 1 in 7 patients, grade 2 in 3 patients, and grade 3 in 5 patients. For the 8 patients with grade 2 + PVRN, median time to PVRN was 9.6 months (IQR 23.7) and median time to symptom onset was 12.6 months (IQR 19.0). Half of the 8 patients had received a prior course of overlapping RT. Main symptoms included headache (5/8 patients), and 2 patients each experienced neurocognitive changes, seizure, visual deficits, and instability/dizziness. Treatment included corticosteroids (6 patients), pentoxifylline with vitamin E [4], bevacizumab [2], hyperbaric oxygen [1], and surgical resection [1]. Symptom improvement or resolution occurred in 50% of symptomatic cases.

Discussion

This study represents the first detailed investigation into the incidence and dosimetric predictors of PVRN following conventionally fractionated radiotherapy for low-grade meningiomas. Our patient cohort was ideal for studying RN because of the low recurrence rates typical of WHO grade I–II meningiomas, which helped to minimize diagnostic uncertainty between RN and tumor progression. This is a critical advantage over high-grade gliomas or brain metastases, where pseudoprogression complicates radiographic interpretation [5, 6]. Further, meningiomas can occur anywhere along the meninges, thus allowing for heterogeneity in periventricular region dosing.

In our study, 14.8% developed any-grade RN after conventionally fractionated RT, with 5.2% of patients developing grade 3 + RN. Excluding the cases of PVRN, there was a 3.7% rate of non-periventricular any-grade RN. In the meningioma literature, reports on RN mostly exist in the ion beam therapy, SRS, and reirradiation setting. For example, a retrospective study on 96 patients undergoing conventionally fractionated proton therapy for WHO grade I-III meningiomas reported a 2.1% rate of CTCAE grade 3 + RN [14]. Another study on linear accelerator-based SRS found a 5% rate of any-grade radionecrosis among 80 patients with cerebellopontine angle meningiomas [15]. Finally, institutional studies on reirradiation for recurrent meningiomas have cited rates of RN ranging from 13–28% following a variety of RT modalities [16–20].

Although direct comparisons are challenging to make due to variations in clinical and treatment factors, the incidence of RN in our population appears to be consistent and possibly higher due to our comparatively longer follow-up duration. Additionally, our diagnostic criteria for RN was generous, as neither pathologic confirmation nor advanced MRI were required for diagnosis; this was in an effort to minimize the likelihood of a false negative in a disease where progression is uncommon. For example, two patients had only punctate but nodular areas of enhancement with corresponding FLAIR abnormality which did not progress further on subsequent scans; these cases were identified as RN per our criteria even though no pathologic confirmation or advanced MRI was available.

A limitation of the current literature is the lack of information about precise geographic distribution of RN. 75% of RN cases were periventricular in our cohort, and the overlapping volume between PTV and the periventricular region was significantly higher in PVRN cases. Taken together, this suggests that RT to the periventricular region may confer elevated risk of RN. No studies to date have demonstrated a particular susceptibility of the periventricular region to RN. For example, a large study on 426 patients receiving varying RT doses for gliomas found no significant association between intracerebral location and RN; however, the evaluated brain regions were not explicitly described and RT techniques were older and less conformal [21]. There are potential mechanistic explanations for this finding. Small vessel ischemic white matter disease, arising from reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF) to white matter, arises most notably in the periventricular region and shares similar imaging phenotypes with PVRN, such as reduced relative cerebral blood flow (rCBV) on advanced MRI [22–24]. We hypothesize that these similarities may reflect overlapping pathophysiologies as well, although further investigations focused on imaging and clinicopathology are warranted.

Our analysis highlights V55 Gy to PV5mm as the strongest predictor of both any-grade and symptomatic (grade 2+) PVRN. It is more predictive than V55 Gy to PV1cm. This may be because a radial 1 cm expansion around the lateral ventricles is more likely to inadvertently capture deep white matter, which is relatively better perfused from anastomoses and collateral networks from surrounding cortical arteries compared to true periventricular white matter, perfused by long penetrating medullary arteries without much branching [9]. Another observed trend was that volumetric constraints utilizing a “cc” unit of measurement were more significantly predictive of PVRN than those using “%” units; this indicates that the absolute, rather than relative, volume of PV5mm receiving 55 Gy is the more important consideration during treatment planning. A possible explanation for this is the heterogeneity in cerebral perfusion even within the periventricalar region; a study utilizing single-photon emission computer tomography to map relative CBF found that not only was perfusion globally deficient in the periventricular region, noticeable heterogeneity in CBF existed within the periventricular region [25]. Although the relationship of this to PVRN is unknown, smaller volume hot spots of ≥ 55 Gy located in the most poorly perfused areas of the periventricular region may have more of an impact on development of PVRN than higher volume hot spots of ≥ 55 Gy located in a relatively better perfused area of the periventricular region [25].

Numerous other dosimetric constraints were also significantly associated with PVRN. The majority of these constraints (e.g. Dmax, D1cc, D2cc) pertain to the characterization of high doses to the periventricular region. Other studies have similarly implicated high dose constraints in the development of RN. For example, multiple retrospective studies on conventionally fractionated RT for nasopharyngeal cancer have implicated high dose constraints such as Dmax and D2cc in the development of temporal lobe or brainstem RN [26–29].

V55 Gy has been implicated in other studies on RN. A retrospective study of 313 pediatric patients receiving conventionally fractionated proton RT for brain tumors found that V55 Gy was one of two dosimetric parameters significantly associated with brainstem RN; 1.3% of patients with V55 Gy < 17.7% developed brainstem RN compared to 10.5% with V55 Gy ≥ 17.7% (p < 0.01) [30]. Another pediatric study on 216 patients found that V55 Gy ≤ 6% decreased the rate of brainstem RN to < 2% when the Dmax constraint was also met [31]. Notably, both studies reported V55 Gy as a relative volume, and it is unclear if they assessed the absolute volume of the brainstem as well. Although this precludes direct comparison, V55 Gy could be an important consideration for multiple substructures including the periventricular region.

In our analysis, higher WHO grade was significantly associated with development of RN. When compared to WHO grade I, WHO grade II meningiomas have more aggressive features such as higher concentration of mitoses, necrosis, and brain invasion [32]. These features may independently injure the brain parenchyma and increase its susceptibility to an additional insult in the form of RT. Importantly, higher WHO grade meningiomas are also typically treated with wider margins, thus increasing the volume of the brain that gets exposed to high dose RT [33].

We did not find an association between proton therapy and increased RN or PVRN risk. Although a constant RBE is typically used when comparing proton and photon RT doses, variations in LET along the trajectory of a proton beam have led to numerous studies on the impact of proton LET on RN [34]. Some studies have shown a correlation between an area of RN and comparatively higher LET, which may explain higher rates of observed RN after proton therapy in certain single-institution studies [35–37]. However, another study of 50 RN cases did not show a correlation between area of RN and proton LET, instead finding inter-patient radiosensitivity to be the more predominant factor [38]. In our study, analysis of proton LET in the PVRN cohort was not performed due to limited analyzable sample size; however there was no empiric evidence that any LET variations that may have been present led to meaningfully higher rates of PVRN. Regardless, larger detailed investigations into proton LET and RN would be informative, as the potential benefits of protons such as decreased integral dose must be weighed against modality-specific risks.

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and single-institutional with a limited number of RN events. As mentioned, pathological confirmation and advanced brain MRI techniques were not required for a diagnosis of RN, which could have impacted diagnostic accuracy. Also, the small amount of cases with V55 Gy exceeding 8 cc precluded our analysis of this dosimetric constraint among the entire continuous range of values. Future prospective studies with diverse histopathologies and treatment paradigms are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

The periventricular region appears particularly prone to radionecrosis following CFRT. PV5mm V55 Gy is a strong and practical predictor of PVRN risk and should be considered during treatment planning.

In both the overall cohort and the cohort excluding prior cranial RT, PV5mm V55 (cc) was identified as the top ranked constraint in predicting both PVRN and grade 2 + PVRN after ranking the dosimetric constraints by five measures of predictive performance and generating a global ranking. The range of 0 to 8 cc was identified for further analysis as it captured the majority (87%) of patients; with values beyond 8 cc, there were fewer datapoints and CI grew prohibitively wide, denoting less reliable data. Thus, between this range of 0-8 cc, PV5mm V55 cutoff values of 1.3 cc (95% CI 0-2.8 cc), 2.2 cc (95% CI 1.0-3.6 cc), 3.4 cc (95% CI 2.1–5.5 cc), and 4.4 cc (95% CI 2.8-7.0 cc) were associated with a < 5%, < 10%, < 20%, and < 30% risk of any-grade PVRN. For grade 2 + PVRN, cutoff values of 2.7 cc (95% CI 1.7–5.2 cc), 3.6 cc (95% CI 2.4–6.2 cc), and 4.8 cc (95% CI 3.0-7.9 cc) were associated with < 5%, < 10%, and < 20% risk between the same range.

When considering patients with prior cranial RT, both the linear and nonlinear interaction terms were statistically non-significant (p > 0.7), indicating no evidence that prior RT modifies the dose-response relationship. After excluding these patients, PV5mm V55 cutoff values of 1.3 cc (95% CI 0-3.1 cc), 2.2 cc (95% CI 1.0-3.8 cc), 3.4 cc (95% CI 2.1–5.5 cc), and 4.2 cc (2.8–6.2 cc) were associated with < 5%, < 10%, < 20%, and < 30% risk of any-grade PVRN. For grade 2 + PVRN, cutoff values of 2.7 cc (95% CI 1.7–3.8 cc) and 3.1 cc (95% CI 2.4–4.5) were associated with < 5% and < 10% risk.

For the overall cohort, median V55 Gy to PV5mm was 0.6 cc (IQR 4.7) and the 75th percentile of V55 Gy was 4.7 cc. The OR of developing any-grade PVRN with a V55 Gy of 4.7 cc vs. 0 cc was 17.83 (95% CI 3.9–81.2), corresponding to an average increase of 1.85 (95% CI 1.34–2.55) for each unit increase within the range of 0 to 4.7 cc. OR increased to 29.44 (95% CI 5.2, 168.0) when comparing risk of any-grade PVRN with V55 Gy of 8 cc vs. 0 cc, corresponding to an average risk increase of 1.53 (95% CI 1.23–1.90) for each 1 unit increase within 0 to 8 cc. For the overall cohort, odds ratios for developing grade 2 + PVRN were significantly higher with V55 Gy of 4.7 cc vs. 0 cc (OR 23.3; 95% CI 2.6-212.6), and 8.0 cc vs. 0 cc (OR 45.7; 95% CI 3.4-618.8).
