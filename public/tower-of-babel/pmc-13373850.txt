Conserved symbiosis‐associated genes in the cycad Encephalartos natalensis suggest co‐option for cyanobacterial symbiosis
Authors: Cassandra Schoeman, Danielle Roodt, Amy Mc Menamin, Oliver Bezuidt, Choaro Dithugoe, Desré Pinard, Eshchar Mizrachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Plant‐cyanobacterial symbioses have evolved independently at least four times across land plants, yet their underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely elusive. Here, we elucidate the pathways involved in this specialised symbiosis and nutrient exchange within coralloid roots (CRs) of Encephalartos natalensis.Using anatomical analysis and RNA sequencing, we characterise the structural and transcriptional features of CRs harbouring active, heterocyst‐rich, nitrogen‐fixing Nostoc cyanobacteria. Notably, no fungal hyphae or arbuscular structures were observed under the sampled conditions.CR‐associated upregulation of core common symbiosis signalling pathway (CSSP) genes was evident, genes shared across multiple nodulating symbioses. Transcriptome‐wide analysis further revealed elevated expression of citrulline and ornithine biosynthesis genes, indicating host assimilation of Nostoc‐fixed ammonia.Together, these findings demonstrate that cycads retain and transcriptionally upregulate conserved symbiosis signalling genes during cyanobacterial associations. Building on the evolutionary link between CSSP genes and the ancient arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) signalling toolkit, our results support differential retention of these genes across plant lineages. Specifically, in E. natalensis, CSSP gene expression in CRs suggests transcriptional co‐option for cyanobacterial symbiosis in CRs. This study provides a framework for understanding the role of ancient molecular pathways in driving plant–microbe symbiosis evolution and diversification. Keywords: convergent evolution, cyanobacterial symbiosis, Encephalartos natalensis (cycad), nitrogen‐fixing bacteria, Nostoc, RNA‐seq

Introduction

Plants have been foundational to terrestrial ecosystems since their colonisation of land over 450 million years ago (Ma; Gensel, 2008). The transition was facilitated in part by symbiotic associations that supported early plant adaptation from aquatic to terrestrial environments (Pirozynski & Malloch, 1975; Heckman et al., 2001). Over time, symbioses have evolved into a diverse spectrum of interactions, from loosely associated extracellular partnerships to highly specialised intracellular mutualisms – fundamentally shaping plant evolution and diversity, as well as nutrient acquisition strategies and adaptation (Vessey et al., 2005; Santi et al., 2013; MacLean et al., 2017; Roy et al., 2020).

Nitrogen (N), an essential nutrient for plant growth, is a major limiting factor for plant productivity in most terrestrial ecosystems (Vitousek & Howarth, 1991; Ågren et al., 2012). To overcome this limitation, many plants engage in mutualistic associations with mycorrhizal fungi and diazotrophic (i.e. N‐fixing) bacteria (Soltis et al., 1995; Swensen, 1996; Brundrett, 2002). The establishment of these symbioses is mediated by a conserved molecular framework termed the common symbiosis signalling pathway (CSSP), which supports both AM and rhizobial interactions (Oldroyd, 2013; Delaux & Schornack, 2021). The CSSP functions as an early signalling cascade, activating downstream developmental and physiological processes that govern symbiotic development and nutrient exchange at host‐microbe interfaces (MacLean et al., 2017; Roy et al., 2020).

In arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbioses, lipids constitute the primary form of carbon transferred from the plant to the fungal symbiont. In this system, the host synthesises β‐monoacylglycerol (β‐MAG) de novo through the activity of acyl‐ACP thioesterase (FatM) and glycerol‐3‐phosphate acyltransferase (RAM2), after which these lipids are exported across the periarbuscular membrane into the periarbuscular space by the arbuscule‐associated STR and STR2 ABC transporters (Zhang et al., 2010; Gutjahr et al., 2012; Bravo et al., 2017). The disruption of RAM2 impairs β‐MAG synthesis and arbuscule development, highlighting the importance of lipid supply for AM symbioses (Liu et al., 2022). In addition, carbohydrates like glucose and fructose – derived from sucrose – may also be transported across the periarbuscular membrane by Sucrose Transporters (SUTs), Sugars Will Eventually be Exported Transporters (SWEETs), and Monosaccharide Transporters (MSTs) to supply the fungal symbiont (Doidy et al., 2012). By contrast, in rhizobial bacteroid symbioses typical of legumes, dicarboxylates such as malate, succinate, and fumarate serve as the principal carbon source facilitating bacterial N fixation within nodules (Terpolill et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2018). Actinorhizal symbioses are thought to utilise similar carbon sources (Jeong et al., 2004). In both AM and nodulating symbioses, N, in the form of ammonia (NH3 or NH4 +) – captured from the soil or fixed from atmospheric N2, respectively – is assimilated into amino acids via the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase pathway before being distributed throughout the plant (Govindarajulu et al., 2005; Jin et al., 2005; Kobae et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2018). Together, these systems demonstrate the central role of mutualism in enhancing plant nutrient acquisition and promoting ecological success.

Beyond these well‐characterised symbioses, a third type of mutualistic symbiosis involves N‐fixing cyanobacteria. Although relatively uncommon, cyanobacterial symbioses occur across diverse plant lineages, including bryophytes (Meeks, 1990), ferns (e.g. Azolla; Braun‐Howland & Nierzwicki‐Bauer, 1990), gymnosperms (cycads; Lindblad et al., 1990), and angiosperms (Gunnera; Bonnett, 1990). These associations typically involve filamentous heterocystous cyanobacteria, primarily Nostoc spp., in which N fixation is restricted to specialised cells known as heterocysts, which maintain a microoxic environment conducive to nitrogenase activity (Gallon, 1981; Fay, 1992; Wolk et al., 1994; Wolk, 1998). Fixed N, produced as ammonia (NH3 or NH4 +), may be transferred either directly to the host or assimilated into amino acids, such as glutamine, citrulline, and arginine (Wolk et al., 1976; Thomas et al., 1977; Martín‐Figueroa et al., 2000). As in other symbioses, heterocyst metabolism is thought to be supported by host‐derived carbon, most commonly proposed to be in the form of sucrose, glucose, and fructose (Rai et al., 2000, 2002, 2018).

Among cyanobiont‐hosting plants, cycads are unique, as they develop specialised coralloid roots (CRs) that host Nostoc spp. within a mucilage‐filled cortical region referred to as the cyanobacterial zone (recently reviewed in Salzman et al., 2025). Cyanobacterial colonisation is facilitated by host‐derived hormogonium‐inducing factors that attract motile cyanobacterial filaments (Hashidoko et al., 2019), while developing CRs (i.e. precoralloid roots) undergo anatomical and biochemical changes to support their symbionts (Ahern & Staff, 1994; Bustos‐Diaz et al., 2024). Recent research suggests that these roots harbour diverse microbial communities beyond cyanobacteria, suggesting a broader role for these roots in microbial interactions and nutrient cycling (Bustos‐Diaz et al., 2024).

In this study, we investigate the Encephalartos natalensis‐Nostoc symbiosis to characterise the anatomical, cellular, and molecular features associated with this interaction through a comprehensive analysis that integrates microscopy, shotgun metagenomics, and a de novo transcriptome assembly across multiple E. natalensis tissues and organs. We characterise the structural and transcriptional specialisation of CRs and investigate host gene expression patterns linked to N assimilation of cyanobiont‐derived ammonia through glutamine synthetase and glutamate dehydrogenase pathways, as well as carbon metabolism and transfer from starch degradation into simple sugars or dicarboxylates (e.g. malate and succinate) facilitated by SWEET transporters. Comparative transcriptomic analyses were performed across representative cyanobacterial symbioses in a bryophyte (Anthoceros punctatus), fern (Azolla filiculoides), gymnosperm (E. natalensis), and angiosperm (Gunnera perpensa), alongside rhizobial (Medicago truncatula) and actinorhizal (Datisca glomerata) nodulating symbioses, which serve as reference systems for experimentally validated upregulation of the CSSP in N‐fixing root associations. Furthermore, we investigate the expression and evolutionary positioning of conserved symbiosis‐associated genes, including components of the CSSP, to determine their transcriptional use across diverse symbiotic contexts.

Whole‐plant transcriptome sampling was used to identify tissue‐specific expression patterns and differentiate coralloid‐root‐associated transcriptional signatures from constitutive or developmentally regulated gene expression throughout the plant. Although cyanobacterial colonisation is restricted to CRs, comparisons with other root types and non‐root organs are essential to prevent misattributing broadly expressed genes as specific to symbiosis. Together, this study provides a comparative framework for understanding conserved and lineage‐specific features of plant‐cyanobacterial interactions and offers insight into the molecular landscape associated with cycad CR symbiosis.

Materials and Methods

Sample collection and preparation

Tissues from Gunnera perpensa L. were collected from naturally growing plants at the Manie van der Schijff Botanical Garden, University of Pretoria, South Africa. From three G. perpensa plants, we sampled cyanobacteria‐containing stem gland tissue, along with surrounding stem, leaf, and root tissues. Tissues from Encephalartos natalensis R.A.Dyer and I.Verd were collected from three plants maintained in individual pots at the Cycad and Indigenous Plant Nursery at the University of Pretoria, including mature and immature leaflets, mature and immature rachis, leaf base, stem, upper primary root (UPR), lower primary root, and CR tissues to supplement previously generated data (Table S1). Due to the distinct anatomy of the sampled tissues/organs from these species, tissue types were easily distinguished and separated based on clear morphological boundaries, avoiding cross‐contamination. Samples were flash‐frozen in liquid N immediately after dissection to preserve RNA integrity. One biological replicate corresponds to one individual plant. Importantly, because the sampled E. natalensis plants were nursery‐maintained and not experimentally inoculated for AM colonisation, our conclusions regarding AM‐related activity are restricted to the sampled material and conditions.

RNA was extracted from E. natalensis and G. perpensa tissue samples using protocols tailored to each species and tissue types. For E. natalensis, all tissues (including leaflets, rachis, leaf base, stem, upper and lower primary root, and CR) were processed using a CTAB‐based RNA extraction protocol, as described by Chang et al. (1993), optimised for high‐phenolic content and tough plant material. Stem gland, stem, leaf, and root tissues of G. perpensa were extracted using the Plant/Fungi RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp., St Catharines, ON, Canada). All RNA samples were subsequently treated with DNase using the DNase Max and RNase‐Free DNase Kits (Qiagen) to remove genomic DNA contamination. High‐quality RNA was submitted for sequencing to two service providers: E. natalensis samples were sent to BGI (China) for total RNA sequencing, while G. perpensa samples were sent to Macrogen (the Netherlands) for poly(A)‐selected RNA sequencing. Libraries were constructed following the Illumina TruSeq protocols and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using 2 × 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Total RNA sequencing was utilised for E. natalensis to create a comprehensive transcriptomic resource for the species, while poly(A) selection was applied for G. perpensa, as these data were specifically generated for within‐species comparative analyses of host gene expression in cyanobacteria‐containing vs control tissues. All downstream differential expression analyses were performed exclusively within species.

For microbiome analysis, a shotgun metagenomic sequencing approach was used to characterise the microbial community associated with the CRs of E. natalensis. This method was chosen to facilitate simultaneous taxonomic and functional profiling of symbionts without primer bias. Consequently, total genomic DNA was extracted from E. natalensis CR samples using the DNeasy® PowerSoil® Kit (Qiagen) after multiple rounds of surface sterilisation according to the protocol established by Gutiérrez‐García et al. (2018). Briefly, CRs were submerged in 15 ml of each solution and continuously stirred for 3 min in hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), 7 min in 70% ethanol, 30 s in Sabax water, 4 min in 6% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), and three 1 min washes in Sabax water. As a control, the last Sabax wash was plated onto agar bacteriological media (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated overnight at 37°C to ensure absence of exogenous bacteria. The total genomic DNA was RNase treated (Omega Bio‐Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) and ethanol precipitated. The quality and quantity of the resultant DNA was determined by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel and spectrophotometry using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) instrument, respectively. DNA was sent for sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq platform at the DNA Sequencing Center, Brigham Young University (Provo, UT, USA), using the 2 × 250‐bp chemistry.

Microscopy sample preparation and imagining for morphological comparison

Light microscopy

To determine the presence of symbiosis, cyanobacteria‐containing tissue (precoralloid and CRs), as well as control tissue (upper primary and lateral roots) were sampled from E. natalensis plants. These samples were then placed in formalin for fixation (Sigma‐Aldrich) for at least 24 h at room temperature. The roots were dehydrated with 2‐methoxyethanol, 100% ethanol, 100% propan‐1‐ol and 100% 1‐butanol for 4 to 6 h each. Each dehydration solution was performed twice. Glycol methacrylate (GMA) infiltration was performed in a stepwise manner. Samples underwent an initial infiltration for 24 h at room temperature with previously used GMA solution, followed by a subsequent 24 h infiltration with freshly prepared GMA. A final infiltration step in fresh GMA was performed for 5 d at room temperature. The material was transferred to gelatine embedding capsules, and left to polymerise in a 52°C oven for 24 h (Feder & O'Brien, 1968; Bruhl & Ashford, 1986).

Sections of 5 μm were made with a Leica‐Jung 2045 Multicut microtome (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) and stained with 0.05% toluidine blue for 3 min (Feder & O'Brien, 1968). Toluidine blue O was selected as the stain because of its effectiveness in differentiating cellular components in GMA embedded sections. The sections were imaged with a Nikon Eclipse E2000 light microscope and Olympus EP50 camera.

Transmission electron microscopy

To confirm the presence of cyanobacteria heterocysts, CRs were sampled and placed in a fixative solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde/formaldehyde in 1.5 M sodium phosphate buffer), for at least 24 h at room temperature. The CRs were washed three times with 1.5 M sodium phosphate buffer for 15 min and dehydrated with a graded series of ethanol (30, 50, 70, 90% and three times with 100%) for 15 min each except for the last 100% dehydration which was left for 30 min. Resin infiltration was done through the addition of increasing concentrations (20, 40, 60 and 80%) of LR White resin (SPI® Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA) in 100% ethanol for 1 h each at room temperature, followed by the addition of 100% LR White resin left overnight at 4°C. Additional 100% LR White resin exchanges were performed before transferring the material to gelatine embedding capsules, after which they were left to polymerise in a 60°C oven for 96 h.

Ultrathin (0.05–0.09 μm) and semi‐thin (0.5 μm) sections were made with a Reichert Ultracut E microtome. Ultrathin sections were stained in 4% aqueous uranyl acetate and Reynold's lead citrate (Reynolds, 1963) for 3 min each, while semi‐thin sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue O in 1% sodium borate buffer for 2 min. Ultrathin sections were imaged with a JEOL JEM‐2100F transmission electron microscope and semi‐thin sections with a Zeiss Axio Imager.M2 light microscope. Cyanobacteria heterocyst and vegetative cell ratios were determined by counting these cell types on grinds. Three biological repeats for CRs were used to determine the ratio with at least five grid blocks counted for each and considered as representative of the sample.

Metagenomic analysis of E. natalensis cyanobacteria‐containing tissue

Taxonomic classification and quality filtering

Novelty‐inclusive microbial community profiling using single‐copy marker genes from the raw metagenomic reads were taxonomically classified using singlem (v.0.18.3; Dataset S1; Woodcroft et al., 2024). Followed by quality assessment using fastqc (v.0.11.7; Andrews, 2010) with default parameters and low‐quality reads were trimmed and removed using trimmomatic (v.0.36) with a phred score threshold of 33 (Bolger et al., 2014). The trimmed metagenomic reads were assessed with fastqc (v.0.11.7) for comparison to the raw metagenomic reads.

Assembly, binning, genome annotation, and phylogenetic analysis

Metagenomic reads were assembled using default parameters in megahit (Li et al., 2015). The quality of the unbinned contigs was evaluated using quast (Gurevich et al., 2013). Unbinned contigs were indexed using bwa (v.0.7.17; Li & Durbin, 2009) and were subjected to binning with metabat (Kang et al., 2015) and maxbin (Wu et al., 2014). The quality of the resulting bins was assessed using checkm (Parks et al., 2015). Medium‐ and high‐quality bins were selected according to the minimum information about metagenome‐assembled genome (MAG) standards (Bowers et al., 2017). Taxonomic assignment of the bins was achieved using gtdb‐tk (Chaumeil et al., 2019). Genome annotation was performed using prokka (Seemann, 2014) and the resultant data were visualised using proksee (https://proksee.ca/). A circular genome map was curated for each draft genome (bin). The metabolic potential of the draft genomes was assessed using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Orthologs (KO) database. kofamkoala was used to assign KO numbers, using protein sequences (Aramaki et al., 2019). The resulting KO numbers were then processed with KEGG Decoder (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000). gtotree (Lee, 2019) was used to assign the draft genomes, within the remaining de‐replicated bins, to a phylogenetic tree, with ‐T parameter set to iq‐tree. A total of 96 Nostoc genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database and used for phylogenetic placement of the 4 Nostoc genomes from this study. itol was used to visualise these results and construct a tree diagram (Letunic & Bork, 2021).

RNA‐seq‐based screening for fungal and arbuscular mycorrhizal taxa and sequencing depth assessment

To determine the presence of low‐abundance fungal taxa, including AM fungi, which may have been undetected by shotgun metagenomics, we performed an independent taxonomic screening of RNA‐seq data generated from all sampled E. natalensis tissues. fastqc (v.0.11.7; Andrews, 2010) was used to assess raw mRNA‐Seq data before and after the removal of low‐quality reads and sequencing adaptors using trimmomatic (v.0.36; Bolger et al., 2014). The adapter‐trimmed RNA‐seq reads were aligned to the de novo assembled E. natalensis transcriptome (see assembly details in the following section) using bowtie 2 (v.2.4.1; Langmead & Salzberg, 2011). Unmapped paired‐end reads per tissue were retrieved (Table S2) and taxonomically classified using kraken2 (v.2.1.3; Wood et al., 2019) against a fungal‐specific reference database. Taxonomic abundances were subsequently estimated using bracken (v.2.9; Lu et al., 2017) at the phylum and order levels. This approach allowed the detection of transcriptionally active fungal lineages while reducing host‐derived signals.

To further evaluate the adequacy of sequencing depth for detecting low‐abundance microbial taxa, richness accumulation analyses were performed on the shotgun metagenomic datasets using singlem (v.0.18.3; Woodcroft et al., 2024). Taxonomic richness at the genus and species levels was quantified across incrementally subsampled read depths, with three replicates per depth. Because the three CR metagenomes differed in retained quality‐filtered read‐pair counts, subsampling was extended individually to the maximum informative depth available for each sample: beginning at 100 000 read pairs and extending to 4 million (CR003), 30 million (CR002), and 45 million (CR005) read pairs, respectively. Sample‐specific richness curves were then evaluated to determine whether sequencing efforts were sufficient to recover detectable low‐abundance taxa, whether richness approached saturation with increasing sequencing effort, and whether additional sequencing was likely to yield substantial further taxon discovery. For the processed taxonomic richness values across subsampled depths for each CR sample, see tables 4–6 in Dataset S1.

Transcriptome assemblies, annotations, differential, and preferential gene expression

Transcriptome assemblies and annotations

Adapter‐trimmed RNA‐seq reads were subsequently used for de novo transcriptome assemblies. The transcriptomes for E. natalensis and G. perpensa were constructed using a combinatorial approach of soapdenovo‐trans (v.1.03; Xie et al., 2014) and trinity (v.2.6.5; Haas et al., 2013) using default parameters and k‐mer lengths of 21 to 111 and 25 and 31, respectively. After constructing individual k‐mer assemblies, these were merged into multi k‐mer assemblies, and redundancy was removed using the EvidentialGene pipeline following author guidelines (Gilbert, 2013). busco (v.3.0.2) was used to assess assembly completeness. A custom script was used to compute the number of contigs, total number of bases, N50, N75, maximum and minimum contig length, first and third quartile contig length, and median and mean contig length. Finally, the trinity Ex90N50 Statistic and Ex90 Gene count scripts were used to further filter out potentially fragmented contigs (Haas et al., 2013).

Assemblies for E. natalensis and G. perpensa generated here, and for An. punctatus (Li et al., 2020), Az. filiculoides (F. W. Li et al., 2018), M. truncatula (Pecrix et al., 2018), and D. glomerata (Battenberg et al., 2018), were annotated with the following: blastx against the TAIR10 database (Berardini et al., 2015) and BLASTx against each above‐mentioned transcriptome retaining only significant BLASTx hits, MapMan bin ID and descriptions (Thimm et al., 2004), KEGG ID and descriptions (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000), InterProScan (Blum et al., 2020), Pfam (Mistry et al., 2020), and Gene Ontology (Ashburner et al., 2000), and orthology assignment using orthofinder (Emms & Kelly, 2015, 2019).

Differential and preferential gene expression

To ensure consistency and comparability across all tissue types within each species, mRNA‐seq libraries were mapped to the corresponding assembled transcriptomes using salmon (Patro et al., 2017). Salmon outputs were used to generate two expression measures for subsequent analyses: (1) transcripts per million (TPM) values utilised for visualisation and within‐species expression profiling, and (2) estimated transcript counts (NumReads) employed for statistical inference. TPM values were uniformly recalculated across all tissues for each species and added to the respective annotation files to enable comparable heatmaps, clustering, and percentile‐based expression summaries. This approach reduces inter‐sample variation and limits false positives that may result from genes exhibiting differential expression due to low or inconsistent expression levels in individual experiments. This approach strengthens the reliability of subsequent differential and preferential expression analyses. TPM values were utilised descriptively and were not employed for hypothesis testing.

Differential expression was determined using deseq2 (Love et al., 2014) based on raw estimated count data (NumReads) derived from Salmon. Transcript‐level counts were compiled from samples into a count matrix using a custom script and subsequently imported into deseq2 via DESeqDataSetFromMatrix() with the design formula ~ tissue. Counts were provided in their raw, untransformed form; deseq2's default settings for normalisation and dispersion estimation were utilised. Differential expression was analysed between cyanobacteria‐containing (symbiotic) and anatomically related, non‐symbiotic reference (control) tissues within each species, with significance determined using Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P‐values (adjusted P ≤ 0.05). No specific log2 fold‐change threshold was implemented; deseq2 log2 fold changes were regarded as estimates of effect size and were not utilised as a criterion for filtering. Accordingly, transcripts with modest but statistically significant expression would still be considered for our interpretation of transcriptome‐wide change.

We analysed deseq2‐significant transcripts to identify those exhibiting preferential expression in cyanobacteria‐containing tissues for An. punctatus, Az. filiculoides, E. natalensis and G. perpensa, using a custom R Shiny app (accessible at https://github.com/CassandraUP/R‐Shiny‐app). For the transcriptome‐wide analysis, TPM values for each transcript were converted into within‐species percentile ranks for all sampled tissues for each species. Transcripts were identified as preferentially expressed in symbiotic tissue based on two criteria: (1) significant differential expression as determined by deseq2 (adjusted P ≤ 0.05) and (2) a TPM percentile rank of ≥ 50 in the cyanobacteria‐containing tissue compared with that transcript's TPM distribution across all tissues within that species, together with lower percentile ranks in non‐symbiotic tissues. The ≥ 50 percentile threshold was used as a permissive filter to reduce background in the transcriptome‐wide analysis and was not intended as a stringent criterion for strong tissue specificity. Percentile ranks were computed separately for each species and were utilised solely for comparisons of tissues within the same species, excluding cross‐species quantitative comparisons. By contrast, this percentile‐based filter was not applied to the curated candidate symbiosis gene analysis, for which all retained significant blastp hits to the candidate gene list were examined directly (see the Materials and Methods section for details).

Orthology relationships among species were inferred using OrthoFinder (Emms & Kelly, 2015, 2019) and supported by reciprocal BLASTp analyses, enabling the alignment of gene identities across datasets and the comparison of preferentially expressed orthogroups. Gene sets of interest were extracted for subsequent analyses, including KEGG pathway enrichment and presence/overlap summaries (e.g. Venn diagrams), utilising the preferentially expressed transcript lists supported by deseq2 as input. Transcriptome‐wide expression summaries of all assembled transcripts in E. natalensis and G. perpensa are provided in Dataset S2.

Identifying genes of interest

Candidate gene list analysis

Known genes involved in the establishment and maintenance of AM and rhizobial symbiosis were identified from the literature. These data were then used to construct a candidate gene list for further analysis (MacLean et al., 2017; Roy et al., 2020). Additional genes, such as those involved in flavonoid and coumarin biosynthesis, were also included in this candidate gene list since little is known about their involvement in cyanobioses (Winkel‐Shirley, 2001; Winkel‐Shirley, 2002; Falcone‐Ferreyra et al., 2012; Stassen et al., 2021). The respective transcriptomes were annotated with these candidate genes using BLASTp, and hits with P ≤ 0.05 were retained. In addition to our transcriptome‐scale approach, we examined the preferential expression of these candidate genes across An. punctatus, Az. filiculoides, E. natalensis, G. perpensa, M. truncatula and D. glomerata using the same R Shiny app described above. The resulting sets of preferentially expressed genes were used to determine enrichment and Venn diagrams drawn to compare presence. For the comprehensive candidate gene list and relative expression levels see table 9 and tables 12–17 in Datasets S3 and S4, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis

Genes associated with carbon exchange, from the candidate gene list as well as the literature, were investigated in the E. natalensis, G. perpensa, Az. filiculoides, An. punctatus, M. truncatula, and D. glomerata gene catalogues, and their phylogenetic relationships and expression explored. To this end, protein sequences of the A. thaliana SWEET family were obtained (Berardini et al., 2015) and used as queries in BLASTx analyses to retrieve putative SWEET homologues (significant BLASTx hits) across all six species. Because the SWEET family comprises multiple related paralogous clades, these BLAST‐based matches were treated as an initial homology‐retrieval step rather than as definitive orthology assignments. Multiple alignments of the differential candidate sequences were completed using mafft and alignments curated using trimAI (both on https://ngphylogeny.fr/; Dereeper et al., 2008; Lemoine et al., 2019), and maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using 1000 bootstrap iterations to evaluate their relationships relative to the established A. thaliana SWEET clades. For the relative expression levels of candidate SWEET genes in the corresponding symbiotic tissues, see table 18 in Dataset S4.

Results

Cycad coralloid roots are dominated by a heterocyst‐rich Nostoc symbiosis

CRs of naturally growing E. natalensis were associated with cyanobacterial symbionts consisting almost exclusively of Nostoc spp., with a high proportion differentiated into N‐fixing heterocysts. Previous studies suggest that CRs develop irreversibly from precoralloid roots (Caiola, 1974; Ahern & Staff, 1994; Lindblad, 2009), although the anatomical similarities between these root types have not been investigated. To address this, the anatomy and cellular composition of CRs (Fig. 1b) were compared with those of presymbiotic precoralloid roots (Fig. 1a) and non‐symbiotic root samples (Fig. S1a,b), and cell types were quantified.

Light microscopy revealed a clearly defined cyanobacterial zone in CR sections, characterised by dense cyanobacterial colonies localised within a distinct cortical layer composed of mucilage‐filled intercellular spaces separated by radially elongated, columnar host cells (Fig. 1d,f). A comparable anatomical zone was also present in precoralloid roots (Fig. 1c,e), however, with a distinct absence of cyanobacterial cells. The presence of this specialised cortical region in both precoralloid and CRs indicates that host root tissues undergo anatomical differentiation before observable cyanobacterial colonisation, consistent with previous reports of mucilage deposition preceding symbiont recruitment (Nathanielsz & Staff, 1975). However, as prior exposure to cyanobacteria or cyanobacterial signalling molecules could not be confirmed, it remains unclear whether these traits are constitutively expressed or induced by environmental or nutritional cues. In this context, N deprivation alone has been shown to stimulate the development of columnar cells and mucilage‐producing glands in Gunnera in the absence of cyanobacterial inoculation (Chiu et al., 2005), suggesting that such anatomical features can be host‐regulated and anticipatory independently of direct symbiont contact.

Comparative anatomical analyses further showed that coralloid and precoralloid roots share greater similarity with primary roots than with lateral roots (Figs 1, S1). Although full cross‐sections of the primary root could not be obtained due to its large diameter, representative segments (2–5 cm) were examined. These segments contained large, starch‐rich parenchymatous cortical cells, a feature also observed in coralloid and pre‐CRs (Figs 1c–f, S1a). By contrast, lateral roots were characterised by oblong cortical cells lacking visible starch granules and containing numerous tannin‐containing structures (Fig. S1b). Differences in vascular organisation, including tracheid structure and arrangement, further distinguished coralloid and precoralloid roots from lateral roots. Together, these observations are consistent with precoralloid roots arising as specialised secondary roots derived from the primary root rather than from ‘transformed lateral root apices’, as previously proposed (Nathanielsz & Staff, 1975; Norstog & Nicholls, 1997). Similar conclusions were reported by Ahern & Staff (1994), who observed that pre‐CRs of Macrozamia communis predominantly emerge along the primary root as secondary laterals and display distinct vascular organisation relative to lateral roots.

Within CRs, cyanobionts were consistently localised to the mucilage‐filled intercellular spaces within the cyanobacterial zone (Fig. 1d,f). These populations comprised both vegetative cells and heterocysts (Figs 1g, S1e). Heterocysts were identifiable as large, thick‐walled cells with cyanophycin granules and narrow septal junctions connecting them to adjacent vegetative cells (Kumar et al., 2010; Maldener et al., 2014). In some cases, adjacent heterocysts were observed (Fig. 1g) – although uncommon in free‐living cyanobacterial filaments, this cell arrangement was repeatedly observed in the symbiotic cyanobacterial populations within CRs. Quantification of cyanobacterial cell types indicated that heterocysts accounted for an average of 57.6% of total cyanobacterial cells within the cyanobacterial zone (Fig. 1h), substantially exceeding the heterocyst frequencies typically reported for free‐living cyanobacteria (5–10%) and comparable to values observed in other plant‐cyanobacteria symbioses (Rai et al., 2000, 2002, 2018).

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing from total genomic DNA of three independent CR samples revealed that c. 49% of classified sequences were assigned to Viridiplantae, reflecting host‐derived DNA. Following removal of host sequences, bacterial taxa accounted for over 96% of the remaining classified signal based on SingleM single‐copy marker gene profiles (Woodcroft et al., 2024). Cyanobacteria belonging to the order Nostocales overwhelmingly dominated the bacterial community, comprising c. 99.6% of the classified bacterial signal (Fig. 2a). Other bacterial phyla, including Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, and Pseudomonadota, were detected only at trace levels (< 0.1% relative abundance). Although SingleM marker gene profiles (Dataset S1) indicated that some of these minor taxa include lineages known to contain diazotrophs, their low relative abundance suggests that they are unlikely to disproportionately contribute to N fixation within CRs as compared to cyanobionts, although functional contributions cannot be determined from abundance data alone.

Non‐plant eukaryotic sequences accounted for c. 4.2% of the classified signal and were assigned to low‐abundance, taxonomically unresolved lineages. Fungal sequences were detected at extremely low levels (< 0.01%; Fig. 2a; Dataset S1). Consistent with this, no AM fungal structures – such as arbuscules, vesicles, or intracellular hyphae – were observed in toluidine blue‐stained sections (Fig. 1c–f). While these data indicate a strong dominance of cyanobacteria in E. natalensis CRs, the presence of fungi at abundances below the detection limits of shotgun metagenomics and anatomical analyses cannot be excluded.

To further assess whether low‐abundance fungal taxa, including AM fungi, were present but missed due to bacterial dominance, we performed an independent taxonomic screening of RNA‐seq data generated from all E. natalensis tissues (Fig. S2a,b). Across tissues, fungal taxonomic classification was supported by consistent sequencing depth, with a median of c. 9.9–15.5 million unmapped paired‐end RNA‐seq reads classified using a fungal‐focused Kraken‐Bracken pipeline (Table S2). Despite this depth, classified unmapped reads failed to detect Glomeromycota or other AM‐associated fungal orders in any tissue (Fig. S2b). Detected fungal transcripts were instead restricted to low‐abundance Ascomycota and Basidiomycota lineages across all tissues (Fig. S2a), consistent with background environmental or endophytic fungal signals rather than an active AM symbiosis.

To evaluate whether sequencing depth was sufficient to detect rare microbial taxa, richness accumulation analyses were performed on the shotgun metagenomic datasets using SingleM (Woodcroft et al., 2024; Fig. S2c,d). Genus‐level richness remained constant at one detected genus across all sequencing depths in all three E. natalensis CR samples. By contrast, species‐level richness increased with sequencing depth and approached saturation in two of the three CR samples, CR002 and CR005, whereas the other (CR003) showed weaker evidence of saturation over its lower maximum sequencing depth. Overall, these results suggest that sequencing depth was sufficient to recover most detectable species‐level diversity in at least two of the three samples and that the absence of AM fungi in the analysed CRs is unlikely to be explained solely by insufficient sequencing depth or masking by cyanobacterial DNA.

Our findings are consistent with recent culture‐independent metagenomic analyses identifying Nostocales cyanobacteria as the primary symbionts of cycad CRs (Bustos‐Diaz et al., 2024). Bustos‐Diaz et al. (2024) further suggested that the higher diversity observed in in situ co‐cultures reflects culture‐induced shifts in relative abundance because co‐culture conditions fail to recapitulate the internal CR microenvironment. This contrasts with reports from other cycad species in which anatomical evidence of fungal colonisation (Muthukumar & Udaiyan, 2002; Fisher & Vovides, 2004) or amplicon‐based ITS‐ and 16S rRNA profiling revealed more diverse microbial communities (Zheng et al., 2018; Suárez‐Moo et al., 2019). In contrast to amplicon‐based or co‐culturing approaches that may recover a broader range of microbial taxa, our shotgun metagenomic, RNA‐seq‐based taxonomic screening, and SingleM marker gene analyses collectively indicate that the sampled E. natalensis CRs harboured a highly specific microbial community dominated by Nostocales cyanobacteria.

De novo assembly and binning yielded four MAGs of medium (n = 2) to high‐quality (n = 2) quality according to the standards of Bowers et al. (2017), all classified within the phylum Cyanobacteriota. Phylogenomic analysis revealed that all four MAGs clustered within the SYMB‐3 clade (Fig. 2b) previously described by Bustos‐Diaz et al. (2024), a symbiotic lineage of Nostoc associated with diverse hosts, including cycads. Three of the four MAGs formed a closely related subclade present across all samples (Fig. 2b), which includes Nostoc punctiforme PCC 73102 isolated from Macrozamia (Moraes et al., 2017) and Nostoc sp. KVJ20 from Blasia pusilla (Warshan et al., 2018). Representative circular maps of two assembled MAGs are shown in Fig. 2c,d to illustrate successful recovery of cyanobacterial genomes from the metagenomic dataset.

Despite variation in sequencing depth across samples, taxonomic and functional profiles were highly consistent, indicating reproducibility across independent CR samples. Collectively, the anatomical, cellular, and genomic data demonstrate that E. natalensis CRs are dominated by cyanobacteria belonging to a symbiotic lineage of Nostoc and that these organisms are spatially localised within a distinct cortical zone. While these observations indicate a strong and structured association between CRs and cyanobacteria, they do not exclude the presence of additional microbial taxa at abundances below the detection limits of the current study.

Nitrogen‐ and carbon‐associated transcriptional responses in coralloid roots during Nostoc symbiosis

To characterise transcriptional differences between CR and UPR of E. natalensis, mRNA‐sequencing was performed and differential expression analysis conducted using deseq2, with CR specified as the reference condition as per default settings. Sequencing generated c. 140 million and 200 million clean reads for CR and UPR, respectively, with comparable GC content (44.3 and 44.5%; Table S1). De novo transcriptome assembly yielded 34 713 contigs, of which 24 996 were functionally annotated, with a mean contig length of 1371 bp (N50 = 2222 bp and N75 = 3237 bp) and an estimated completeness of 85.9% based on BUSCO analysis (Table S3; Simão et al., 2015). In total, 5246 transcripts were identified as differentially expressed between CR and UPR tissues (adjusted P ≤ 0.05). Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering incorporating transcriptome data from all sampled tissues revealed that CR samples formed a distinct expression cluster, separated from all other tissue types, and crucially, from all other root types (Fig. S3).

UPR were selected as the comparative root tissue because of their developmental derivation from primary roots (Nathanielsz & Staff, 1975; Norstog & Nicholls, 1997) and their anatomical similarity to coralloid and precoralloid roots, including large, starch‐rich cortical cells and comparable vascular organisation (Figs 1, S1). Lateral roots instead show notable differences in cortical cell morphology and were therefore excluded as the principal control tissue. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering show that CRs form a transcriptionally distinct cluster, especially relative to the other root tissues (Fig. S3). As CRs are specialised symbiotic organs, we interpret this distinctive expression profile as reflecting their symbiosis‐associated functional specialisation rather than root identity alone.

Pathway enrichment analysis of genes expressed above the median revealed that CRs were enriched for transcripts annotated to N metabolism, particularly enzymes associated with ammonia assimilation and citrulline and ornithine biosynthesis (intermediates of arginine biosynthesis) pathways (Fig. 3). Transcripts encoding glutamine synthetase, glutamate dehydrogenase, and several enzymes involved in ornithine and citrulline biosynthesis showed significantly higher expression in CR relative to UPR. Among the annotated enzymatic steps for which corresponding transcripts were recovered in these pathways, c. 69.2% were represented by transcripts that were significantly differentially expressed (adjusted P ≤ 0.05) with higher expression in CRs than in UPRs (Fig. 3). The remaining annotated steps were significantly more highly expressed in UPRs. By contrast, transcripts corresponding to nitrate (NO3 −) and nitrite (NO2 −) transporters and reductases were detected in the assembled transcriptome but did not meet the adjusted significance threshold for differential expression and were therefore excluded from downstream analyses. In addition, no CR‐specific expression of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was observed (Fig. 3). Together, these results support significant enrichment of host ammonia assimilation and related biosynthetic functions in CRs, while also indicating that not all N metabolism‐associated components were CR‐enriched under the sampled conditions. A transcript annotated as a molybdate transporter (MOT1) exhibited significantly higher expression in CRs compared with other tissues (Fig. 4a).

Genes associated with the synthesis and transport of fatty acids and β‐MAG that underpin the canonical AM lipid pathways showed either low expression or were not detected across E. natalensis root tissues (Fig. S4a). The only acyl‐ACP thioesterase transcript displaying increased expression in CRs (Enat_0023671) did not group with the AM‐associated FatM clade. Instead, phylogenetic analyses placed this transcript within a lineage containing FatB‐type thioesterases, including Arabidopsis AT1G08510, firmly indicating that it represents a related family member rather than a canonical AM‐associated orthologue (Notes S1; Fig. S7). A transcript annotated as a putative RAM2 glycerol‐3‐phosphate acyltransferase (Enat_0006582) was identified; however, maximum‐likelihood phylogeny consistently placed Enat_0006582 outside the clade containing canonical symbiotic RAM2 orthologues, including experimentally validated references from M. truncatula and orthologues from other AM‐competent taxa. Instead, it grouped with RAM2‐like homologues from non‐AM species, including Az. filiculoides and Salvinia cucullata (Notes S1; Fig. S8). We therefore interpret Enat_0006582 as a RAM2‐like homologue rather than a canonical AM‐associated RAM2 orthologue. Although structurally related to canonical RAM2 orthologues, this transcript is unlikely to represent the established lipid biosynthesis component required for canonical AM symbiosis in host plants. Transcripts corresponding to the ATP‐binding cassette transporter STR, which is essential for lipid transfer to arbuscules during symbiosis, were not recovered from the E. natalensis transcriptome (Notes S1; Fig. S9). Notably, canonical FatM, RAM2, and STR orthologues were identified in another cycad species, Cycas panzhihuaensis, consistent with the view that orthologues of the canonical AM symbiosis toolkit were ancestrally retained across cycads (Figs 4, S7–S9; Notes S1). Collectively, these findings provide no evidence for the loss of AM‐associated lipid pathway genes in E. natalensis, but instead indicate an absence of detectable transcriptional activation of the canonical AM lipid synthesis and transfer pathway in roots under the sampled conditions.

By contrast, genes involved in starch breakdown, sugar metabolism, glycolysis, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were expressed in both CR and UPR, with several exhibiting tissue‐specific differential expression patterns (Fig. S4b). Transcripts encoding isoamylase (ISA), α‐amylase, and D‐enzyme were differentially expressed between CR and UPR, consistent with starch turnover. Invertase (INV) and xylose isomerase (IX) transcripts were also significantly expressed in these tissues (Fig. S4b).

Genes associated with glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and the pyruvate‐to‐succinate branch of the TCA cycle displayed variable expression across root tissues (Fig. S4b). Enzymes involved in fumarate, malate, and oxaloacetate interconversion were generally highly expressed, although without consistent CR‐specific enrichment (Fig. S4b). A transcript annotated as an aluminium‐activated malate transporter (ALMT) exhibited elevated expression in CRs (Fig. S4b).

Comparative transcriptomic analyses across cyanobacterial symbioses in a bryophyte (An. punctatus), fern (Az. filiculoides), gymnosperm (E. natalensis), and angiosperm (G. perpensa) revealed that SWEET family homologues expressed in symbiotic tissues clustered within the four canonical Arabidopsis SWEET clades (I–IV; X. Li et al., 2018). In each cyanobiosis lineage except Az. filiculoides, a single SWEET homologue exhibited elevated expression in symbiotic tissues. In E. natalensis, Enat_0023088 ranked in the 99th percentile of expression in CRs, while homologues in An. punctatus (Apun_evm.model.utg000039l.841.1) and G. perpensa (Gper_0003345) also exhibited high relative expression (Fig. S5).

Retention and transcriptional regulation of common symbiosis signalling pathway genes in cycad cyanobiosis

To investigate the molecular basis of plant‐cyanobacteria symbiosis in cycads and its evolutionary context across land plants, we performed comparative transcriptomic analyses across all major plant lineages known to host cyanobacterial symbionts. In addition to mRNA‐seq datasets generated here for E. natalensis, we analysed publicly available transcriptomes from the hornwort An. punctatus (Li et al., 2020) and the water fern Az. filiculoides (F. W. Li et al., 2018). We further generated a de novo reference transcriptome for cyanobacteria‐colonised stem glands of G. perpensa – a southern African angiosperm for which no prior symbiosis‐focused transcriptomic resources existed. mRNA‐seq of colonised stem glands and non‐symbiotic control tissues of G. perpensa yielded c. 140 million and 150 million clean reads, respectively, with an average GC content of 45.6% (Table S1). De novo assembly produced a transcript catalogue of 33 346 contigs, of which 23 101 were functionally annotated (N50 = 1714 bp and N75 = 2572 bp). BUSCO analysis indicated 90.4% completeness, consistent with a high‐quality assembly (Table S3; Simão et al., 2015). Differential expression analyses identified 11 526, 5493, and 1059 transcripts (adjusted P ≤ 0.05) as differentially expressed in An. punctatus, Az. filiculoides, and G. perpensa, respectively. In all species, cyanobacteria‐containing tissues formed transcriptionally distinct clusters relative to non‐symbiotic tissues (Fig. S6b–f), consistent with patterns observed in E. natalensis CRs (Fig. S6a).

To contextualise cyanobacterial symbioses within a broader evolutionary context, transcriptome data from the rhizobial host Medicago truncatula (Pecrix et al., 2018) and the actinorhizal host Datisca glomerata (Battenberg et al., 2018) were used for comparative analysis. Given that AM, rhizobial, and actinorhizal symbioses all share a conserved CSSP (Delaux et al., 2015), we investigated whether homologous CSSP genes are retained and expressed during cycad‐cyanobacterial symbiosis. Utilising a literature‐curated gene set (Dataset S3), we combined phylogenetic inference with expression profiling for six key CSSP‐associated genes: SYMRK, CCaMK, CYCLOPS, POLLUX, NUP133, and VPY.

Maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic analyses identified E. natalensis transcripts that clustered within well‐supported CSSP orthologue clades for the core signalling genes SYMRK (Enat_0018308), CCaMK (Enat_0024269), and CYCLOPS (Enat_0010760), alongside orthologues from AM and rhizobial model species, such as M. truncatula and O. sativa (Notes S1; Figs S10–S12). Critically, these transcripts were phylogenetically distinct from homologues in non‐symbiotic species like A. thaliana and P. cynaroides. Moreover, E. natalensis orthologues for POLLUX (Enat_0013225) and NUP133 (Enat_0021078) nested firmly within canonical land plant CSSP clades, forming strong sister relationships with gymnosperm homologues in the broader POLLUX/DMI1 and NUP133 gene families (Notes S1; Figs S13–S14). Although the E. natalensis Vapyrin‐like transcript (Enat_0001797) is associated with divergent VPY family members rather than canonical orthologue clades, the overall phylogenetic retention of core CSSP components underscores their evolutionary conservation in E. natalensis (Notes S1; Fig. S15).

Targeted expression analyses further corroborate this retention, revealing pronounced upregulation of key CSSP orthologues – including CCaMK, CYCLOPS, POLLUX, and the Vapyrin‐like – specifically in CRs compared with other E. natalensis tissues (Fig. 4b). By contrast, SYMRK and NUP133 orthologues displayed broader expression with less tissue specificity (Fig. 4b). These differential patterns provide evidence of quantitative co‐option of CSSP‐related transcripts for cyanobacterial symbiosis in CRs, rather than uniform or root‐exclusive activation.

By contrast, comparative genomics revealed the absence of several canonical CSSP genes in Az. filiculoides and Az. cf. caroliniana genomes, eliminating their potential role in cyanobacterial colonisation (Fig. 4a; F. W. Li et al., 2018) – a pattern aligning with documented secondary loss of mycorrhizal symbiosis in Azollaceae and implying reliance on alternative signalling pathways within this lineage (Harley & Harley, 1987a,b; Gemma et al., 1992). Similarly, hornworts (An. punctatus and An. agrestis) retain CSSP genes but exhibit minimal or inconsistent transcriptional responses to cyanobacteria (Chatterjee et al., 2022; Neubauer, 2024). By comparison, E. natalensis not only preserves orthologous CSSP genes but demonstrates symbiosis‐specific transcriptional activation in CRs (Fig. 4). Taken together with the conservation of these orthologues in C. panzhihuaensis (Fig. 4a), these results provide multi‐species evidence that cycads possess conserved core CSSP signalling components, with further evidence of their CR‐associated expression in E. natalensis suggesting (in the absence of arbuscular mycorrhiza) that these orthologues may reflect co‐option for cyanobacterial symbiosis.

Discussion

Throughout the c. 450 million years of land plant evolution, plants have repeatedly formed mutualistic associations with diverse microbial partners – including AM fungi, rhizobia, actinorhizal bacteria, and cyanobacteria – to overcome nutrient limitations and expand ecological niches. These symbioses have often evolved through the reuse and modification of conserved molecular frameworks rather than the emergence of entirely novel pathways (Werner et al., 2018). The identification of the CSSP in legumes was pivotal in revealing a deeply conserved signalling architecture that underpins multiple intracellular symbioses, including AM and rhizobial associations (Oldroyd, 2013). By contrast, the molecular mechanisms governing extracellular symbioses, particularly those involving ectomycorrhizae and N‐fixing cyanobacteria, remain comparatively poorly understood (Martin et al., 2017).

Cyanobacterial symbiosis represents one of the most ancient N‐fixing mutualisms, likely predating the diversification of terrestrial plants and originating in early cyanolichen associations over 500 Ma (Raven, 2002; Yuan et al., 2005). Research has predominantly concentrated on free‐living cyanobacteria, while evolutionary inference has been constrained by limited genomic and transcriptomic resources for non‐angiosperm hosts. By integrating comparative transcriptomic analyses across extant cyanobiont‐hosting lineages – Anthoceros (Li et al., 2020), Azolla (F. W. Li et al., 2018), Encephalartos, and Gunnera – our study provides a broad molecular framework for understanding the independent evolution of cyanobacterial symbioses across land plants.

Our analyses demonstrate that E. natalensis has retained a conserved complement of core CSSP genes, including SYMRK (Enat_0018308), CCaMK (Enat_0024269), CYCLOPS (Enat_0010760), POLLUX (Enat_0013225), and NUP133 (Enat_0021078), all of which fall within robustly supported orthologous clades alongside AM and rhizobial model species (Notes S1; Figs S10–S14). These genes therefore represent bona fide members of the CSSP rather than lineage‐specific paralogues. By contrast, the E. natalensis Vapyrin‐like transcript (Enat_0001797) clusters with divergent family members outside the canonical VPY orthologue clade (Notes S1; Fig. S15), suggesting functional divergence despite sequence similarity. Expression analyses further reveal heterogeneous but structured patterns of CSSP transcript expression across E. natalensis tissues (Fig. 4b). Several transcripts, including CCaMK, CYCLOPS, POLLUX, and the VPY‐like transcript, exhibit significantly higher expression in CRs than in other tissues, whereas SYMRK and NUP133 show broader expression with minimal tissue bias. Although these patterns do not indicate uniform or exclusive CSSP activity in CRs, they do support retention of essential CSSP components and quantitative regulation of selected pathway genes in the symbiotic CR context.

This pattern contrasts with other extant cyanobacterial symbioses. In Azolla, most CSSP genes have been secondarily lost (F. W. Li et al., 2018), consistent with the obligate, vertically transmitted, and developmentally canalised nature of its cyanobacterial association, which is largely insulated from soil‐derived microbial interactions (Braun‐Howland & Nierzwicki‐Bauer, 1990). In hornworts, CSSP genes are retained but show limited transcriptional responsiveness to cyanobacterial colonisation (Li et al., 2020; Chatterjee et al., 2022; Neubauer, 2024). Similarly, although Gunnera hosts intracellular cyanobacteria (Bonnett, 1990), transcriptomic data indicate that CSSP components are not consistently induced during symbiosis (Fig. 4a). By contrast, the inclusion of C. panzhihuaensis in the phylogenetic analyses reveals retention of canonical symbiosis‐related genes in cycads (Fig. 4a; Notes S1). Together with expression data from E. natalensis, this supports the interpretation that cycads conserve core CSSP orthologues and that, in E. natalensis, these genes are transcriptionally associated with CRs in the context of cyanobacterial symbiosis.

Although these genes were first characterised in the context of AM symbiosis, the CSSP is not mycorrhiza‐specific and is also required for rhizobial and actinorhizal N‐fixing associations. We therefore interpret CSSP genes as components of a conserved intracellular symbiosis signalling pathway rather than as markers of AM genes per se. The retention of orthologous CSSP genes in E. natalensis, together with context‐dependent increases in transcript expression for selected genes in CRs, is consistent with lineage‐specific co‐option of ancestral symbiosis‐related signalling machinery to accommodate or regulate cyanobacterial interactions. Supporting this interpretation, experimental studies in rice have revealed partial recruitment of CSSP genes during association with Nostoc, indicating that CSSP function can extend beyond canonical nodulation or mycorrhizal contexts (Álvarez et al., 2022).

Despite retaining core CSSP components and their characteristic CR‐associated expression, E. natalensis exhibits no evidence of active canonical AM symbiosis across the tissues and organs sampled. Anatomical examination revealed neither arbuscules nor intracellular fungal hyphae in coralloid or primary roots (Figs 1, S1); shotgun metagenomics detected fungal sequences only at trace levels, none of which were AM fungi (Fig. 2a); and RNA‐seq‐based taxonomic screening similarly uncovered only trace non‐AM fungal sequences (Fig. S2a,b), alongside no detectable transcripts of E. natalensis orthologues encoding essential AM lipid‐transfer components – including FatM, canonical RAM2, and STR – in root tissues (Figs S4a, S7–S9; Notes S1). Although targeted amplicon‐based metabarcoding could improve sensitivity for rare fungal taxa, this convergence of independent multi‐omic datasets clearly demonstrates that AM symbiosis is neither active nor functionally significant in E. natalensis CRs under the sampled conditions. Conversely, the recovery of canonical FatM, RAM2, and STR orthologues in C. panzhihuaensis confirms ancestral retention of these AM toolkit genes in cycads (Fig. 4a; Notes S1). Thus, findings in E. natalensis reflect the targeted absence of detectable AM‐specific taxa (Fig. S2a,b) and canonical lipid exchange machinery required for arbuscule development (Liu et al., 2022) in these tissues and organs under sampled conditions, rather than any inherent incapacity or cycad‐wide AM absence. E. natalensis thereby exemplifies modular evolution of symbiosis pathways, wherein ancestral signalling components persist while downstream AM‐specific pathways remain inactivated during CR‐cyanobacterial symbiosis.

Transcriptomic evidence further indicates that CRs of E. natalensis are transcriptionally specialised for carbon mobilisation through sugar‐based pathways rather than lipid‐mediated or exclusively dicarboxylate‐based exchange. Elevated expression of starch‐degradation enzymes, glycolytic components, and sugar‐processing pathways (Fig. S4b) – together with lineage‐specific upregulation of SWEET transporters (Fig. S5) – supports a model in which host‐derived simple sugars (i.e. sucrose, glucose, and fructose) constitute the primary carbon source supplied to cyanobionts. While dicarboxylates (malate, succinate, and fumarate) may represent supplementary substrates, their transcriptional signatures do not show CR‐specific enrichment comparable to sugar metabolism pathways in contrast to rhizobial and actinorhizal symbioses (Jeong et al., 2004; Terpolill et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2018).

The specialised anatomy of CRs provides a structural framework consistent with this carbon exchange model. Precoralloid roots, an early developmental stage of CRs (Milindasuta, 1975; Ahern & Staff, 1994), establish a cyanobacteria‐receptive zone before colonisation, and mature CRs compartmentalise cyanobionts within a mucilage‐filled cortical zone enriched in polysaccharides (Fig. 1; Rai et al., 2000, 2002, 2018). Starch‐rich cortical cells surrounding this zone (Fig. 1) likely act as local carbon reservoirs, facilitating controlled sugar release to sustain heterocyst differentiation and N fixation while maintaining host control over carbon allocation.

Complementing this carbon exchange model, N assimilation in E. natalensis appears to occur predominantly through host metabolic pathways. Although heterocystous cyanobacteria can synthesise amino acids, such as glutamine, aspartate, and arginine (Wolk et al., 1976; Flores et al., 2019), the absence of CR‐specific upregulation of host AST, together with increased expression of host enzymes associated with ammonia assimilation and ornithine/citrulline biosynthesis, suggests that fixed N is likely made available to the host as reduced N, plausibly ammonium, and subsequently assimilated by host metabolic pathways (Fig. 3). Preferential expression of host enzymes involved in ammonium assimilation and amino acid biosynthesis – including citrulline, glutamine, and glutamate synthesis – aligns with classical physiological studies demonstrating that these N‐rich amino acids dominate N transport in coralloid xylem sap of Encephalartos species among others (Pate et al., 1988).

Together, our findings indicate that the cycad E. natalensis has potentially transcriptionally co‐opted an ancestral symbiotic signalling framework to support its extracellular mutualism with N‐fixing cyanobacteria. By retaining core CSSP components while lacking detectable activation of canonical mycorrhizal lipid exchange and instead adopting sugar‐based carbon provisioning and host‐controlled N assimilation, E. natalensis illustrates the modularity of plant–microbe symbiosis pathways. More broadly, our study provides a comparative framework for investigating how ancient conserved molecular components may be retained, co‐opted, or repurposed in symbioses with diverse microbial symbionts and cellular architectures. Further functional and metabolic studies will be essential to validate the mechanistic roles of CSSP components and transporters in cycad cyanobiosis and to determine whether similar patterns occur in other plant–microbe mutualisms.
