The Skolem Problem in rings of positive characteristic
Ruiwen Dong, Doron Shafrir

Abstract
We show that the Skolem Problem is decidable in finitely generated commutative rings of positive characteristic. More precisely, we show that there exists an algorithm which, given a finite presentation of a (unitary) commutative ring ℛ = ℤ / T ​ [ X 1 , … , X n ] / I \mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/I of characteristic T > 0 T>0 , and a linear recurrence sequence ( γ n ) n ∈ ℕ ∈ ℛ ℕ (\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{N}} , determines whether ( γ n ) n ∈ ℕ (\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} contains a zero term. Our proof is based on two recent results: Dong and Shafrir (2025) on the solution set of S-unit equations over p e p^{e} -torsion modules, and Karimov, Luca, Nieuwveld, Ouaknine, and Worrell (2025) on solving linear equations over powers of two multiplicatively independent numbers. 1
Introduction and main results

The Skolem Problem in a commutative ring
\mathcal{R}
is the problem of determining whether a given linear recurrence sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
over
\mathcal{R}
contains a zero term.
The Skolem Problem is deeply related to central questions in program verification
[
OW15
,
AKK+21
]
, control theory
[
BT00
,
FOP+19
]
, dynamical systems
[
KL86
,
AAGT15
]
, and number theory
[
Bel06
,
LMN+23
]
.
Despite its simple formulation, decidability of the Skolem Problem over
\mathbb{Z}
(and equivalently,
\mathbb{Q}
) is a major open problem in mathematics and computer science: see
[
Mah35
,
Lec53
,
TMS84
,
BP02
,
LLN+22
,
LOW25
]
for a selected list of partial results and recent progress.
Indeed, the celebrated Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem
[
Sko34
]
shows that the zero set of a linear recurrence sequence over
\mathbb{Z}
is a union of a finite set and finitely many arithmetic progressions.
However, the Skolem-Mahler-Lech theorem is not
effective
, meaning there is currently no known computable bound on this finite set, and hence the
decidability
of the Skolem Problem over the ring
\mathbb{Z}
remains open.

In contrast, decidability of the Skolem Problem in fields of prime characteristic (such as
\mathbb{F}_{p}(X)
) was shown by Derksen
[
Der07
]
, who gave an
effective
description of the zero set as a
p
-normal set
.
In this paper we continue Derksen’s work in this direction by showing decidability of the Skolem Problem in
rings
of arbitrary positive characteristics.
In contrast to fields, the characteristic of such a ring is not necessarily prime (e.g., the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}_{/6}[X]
has characteristic
6
).
Thus a difficulty is to describe the interaction of different prime divisors of the characteristic.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a commutative ring with unity
1
.
The
characteristic
of the ring
\mathcal{R}
is defined as the smallest positive integer
T
such that
T\cdot 1=0
in
\mathcal{R}
.
If no such positive integer exists, the ring is said to have characteristic zero.
A sequence
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots)\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called an
linear recurrence sequence
if there exist
d\geq 1
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathcal{R}
with
a_{d}\neq 0
, such that

\gamma_{n}=a_{1}\gamma_{n-1}+\cdots+a_{d}\gamma_{n-d}

(1.1)

for all
n\geq d
.
Note that the sequence
\gamma
is uniquely determined by the recurrence relation (
1.1
) as well as its
d
initial terms
\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{d-1}
.
Since every term in
\gamma
is an element in the subring generated by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d},\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{d-1}
, by restricting to this subring we can suppose
\mathcal{R}
to be finitely generated.
The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finitely generated commutative (unitary) ring of characteristic
T>0
.
Given a linear recurrence sequence
\gamma\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, it is decidable whether
\gamma
contains a zero.

We say a few words about how the ring
\mathcal{R}
is represented.
Denote by
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}
the quotient ring
\mathbb{Z}/T\mathbb{Z}=\{0,1,\ldots,T-1\}
and let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}
be the generators of
\mathcal{R}
.
Since
T\cdot 1=0
in
\mathcal{R}
, we have a surjective homomorphism
\varphi
from the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
to
\mathcal{R}
, defined by
X_{i}\mapsto r_{i},i=1,\ldots,N
.
Therefore
\mathcal{R}
can be written as a quotient
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]/I
, with
I=\ker(\varphi)
.
This ideal
I
is finitely generated since
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is Noetherian.
Therefore, throughout this paper, the ring
\mathcal{R}
is always represented as a quotient
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]/I
, where the generators of
I
are explicitly given.

Theorem
1.1
for rings of
prime
characteristics can be deduced from the case of fields following Derksen’s result.
By
[
Der07
, Section 9]
, given any linear recurrence sequence
\gamma
over a finitely generated ring of prime characteristic
p
, its zero set
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)\coloneqq\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\gamma_{n}=0\}
is effectively
p
-normal
(see Section
2
for a formal definition).
For example, the linear recurrence sequence
\gamma
over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
, defined by
\gamma_{n}=(X+1)^{n}-X^{n}-1
, admits the zero set
\{n=p^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}

[
Der07
, Example 1.3]
.

Overview of main contributions and proof structure.

More generally, when considering a linear recurrence sequence
\gamma
over a ring of
non-prime
characteristic
T=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, we can use the Chinese Remainder Theorem to decompose the zero set
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
as an intersection
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{k})
of zero sets, where each
\alpha_{i}
is a linear recurrence sequence over a ring of characteristic
p_{i}^{e_{i}}
.
Then, in order to decide whether
\gamma
contains a zero (i.e., whether
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
is non-empty), one is immediately confronted with two major obstacles.

The first obstacle is that Derksen’s result does not apply to rings of prime-power characteristics
p^{e},e\geq 2
, due to its reliance on the
Frobenius homomorphism
.
To overcome this, in this paper we will extend Derksen’s result from prime characteristic to prime-power characteristics
p^{e}
.
Namely, we will show the following.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number,
e
be a positive integer, and
\mathcal{A}
be a finitely generated commutative unitary ring of characteristic
p^{e}
.
For any given linear recurrence sequence
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, its zero set
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha)\coloneqq\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\alpha_{n}=0\}
is effectively
p
-normal.

The formal definition of
p
-normal sets
will be given in Section
2
(Definition
2.1
).
An example of
p
-normal sets is
\{1+5\cdot p^{2a}+p^{2b}\mid a,b\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
In particular, these are special forms of
p
-automatic sets

[
WB00
]
, and is a generalization of linear combinations of powers of
p
.
Our key argument to proving Proposition
1.2
is a deep result of Dong and Shafrir
[
DS25
, Theorem 1.3]
, who showed that the solution set to an
S-unit equation
over a
p^{e}
-torsion module is effectively
p
-normal.

The second obstacle is to compute the intersection
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{k})
of the zero sets.
Due to Proposition
1.2
, this boils down to computing the intersection of
p_{i}
-normal sets for
different
primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
.
In general, it is unknown whether one can effectively compute the intersection of
p_{i}
-automatic
sets for different
p_{i}
’s
[
HS22
,
AB23
]
.
However, we will show that this is possible in the special case of
p_{i}
-normal sets.
In particular, we will prove that every
intersection
of
p_{i}
-normal sets can actually be written as a
union
of
p_{i}
-normal sets:

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be multiplicatively independent positive integers.
For
i=1,\ldots,k
, let
S_{i}
be a
p_{i}
-normal subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
Then the intersection
S_{1}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}
is effectively equal to a union
T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{k}
, where each
T_{i}
is a
p_{i}
-normal subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, it is decidable whether the intersection
S_{1}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}
is empty.

Our main tool for proving Proposition
1.3
comes from a recent paper by Karimov, Luca, Nieuwveld, Ouaknine, and Worrell
[
KLN+25
]
, which gave an effective procedure to decide the existential fragment of Presburger arithmetic extended with
two
power predicates.
For example, this allows an effective resolution of equations of the form
1+5\cdot p_{1}^{2a}+p_{1}^{2b}=3+p_{2}^{c}
, over the variables
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}
.
However, the effective procedure in
[
KLN+25
]
does not extend to
more than two
power predicates.
Therefore, additional insights are needed to compute the intersection of
p_{i}
-normal sets for
k\geq 3
distinct primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
.

Combining Proposition
1.2
and
1.3
, one also obtains an effective characterization of the zero set of a linear recurrence sequence over a ring
\mathcal{R}
of characteristic
T=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
: namely it is effectively a finite union of
p_{i}
-normal subset of
\mathbb{N}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
p
-normal sets

We define
p
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathbb{N}
, following the convention established in
[
Der07
]
and
[
DM15
]
.

Definition 2.1

(reformulation of
[
DM15
]
)
.

A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called
elementary
p
-nested
if it is of the form

\{\boldsymbol{a}_{0}+p^{\ell k_{1}}\boldsymbol{a}_{1}+\cdots+p^{\ell k_{r}}\boldsymbol{a}_{r}\mid k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\},

(2.1)

where
\ell\geq 1
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{0},\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
A singleton is by definition elementary
p
-nested with
r=0
.
Note that the entries of
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
’s do not have to be integers: for example, the set
\left\{\frac{1}{2}+3^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\;\middle|\;k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is elementary
p
-nested.

A subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called
p
-succinct
, if it is of the form
H+D\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{d}\mid\boldsymbol{h}\in H,\boldsymbol{d}\in D\}
, where
H
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
D
is elementary
p
-nested.
For example, the set
\{(x,x+p^{k})\mid x\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is
p
-succinct since it is the sum of the subgroup
H=\{(x,x)\mid x\in\mathbb{Z}\}\leq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and the elementary
p
-nested set
D=\{(0,1)\cdot p^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

A subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called
p
-normal
if it is a finite union of
p
-succinct sets.
The empty set is by definition
p
-normal.
A set is called
effectively

p
-normal if all its defining coefficients can be effectively computed.

Specializing Definition
2.1
at
n=1
, we obtain a simpler characterization of
p
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
.
Indeed, a
p
-succinct subset
S=H+D\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is either elementary
p
-nested (if the subgroup
H
is trivial), or it is a finite union of cosets
(a\mathbb{Z}+b_{1})\cup\cdots\cup(a\mathbb{Z}+b_{k})
with
a\geq 1
(if the subgroup
H
is of the form
a\mathbb{Z}
).
Therefore,
p
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
can be equivalently defined as follows.

Observation 2.2
.

A subset
S
of
\mathbb{Z}
is
p
-normal, if and only if it is a finite union of elementary
p
-nested sets and sets of the form
a\mathbb{Z}+b
,
a\geq 1,b\in\mathbb{Z}
.

For subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, the definition is slightly more technical:

Definition 2.3
.

A subset
S
of
\mathbb{N}
is called
p
-normal
, if there exists
n\geq 0
, a finite set
F\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and a
p
-normal subset
S^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{Z}
, such that

S=\left(S^{\prime}\cap[n,\infty)\right)\cup F.

In other words, a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
p
-normal if it
ultimately
coincides with a
p
-normal subset of
\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore various closure properties for
p
-normal sets:

Lemma 2.4

(
[
Der07
, Lemma 9.5]
)
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}
be
p
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{N}
(or
\mathbb{Z}
). Then the intersection
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
is effectively
p
-normal.

Lemma 2.5

(Special case of
[
DM15
, Proposition 2.1]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
B
be a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
U\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be an elementary
p
-nested set.
Then, the set of integers
\{z\in\mathbb{Z}\mid z\cdot\boldsymbol{b}\in B+U\}
is effectively
p
-normal.

From Lemma
2.5
, we immediately obtain:

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be a
p
-normal set and let
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Then, the set of integers
\{z\in\mathbb{Z}\mid z\cdot\boldsymbol{b}\in S\}
is effectively
p
-normal.

Proof.

Since
p
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
are closed under finite union, it suffices to prove the case where
S
is
p
-succinct, which corresponds exactly to Lemma
2.5
.
∎

2.2 commutative algebra

All rings considered in this paper are commutative and unitary.
We recall some standard definitions from commutative algebra
[
Eis13
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
R
be an effective commutative Noetherian ring.

(i)

An element
r\in R
is called a
zero-divisor
if there exists a non-zero
x
in
R
such that
rx=0
. For example,
3
is a zero-divisor of the ring
\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
.

(ii)

An element
r\in R
is called
nilpotent
if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r^{n}=0
. For example,
6
is nilpotent in
\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
.

(iii)

A proper ideal
I\subsetneq R
is called
prime
if for every
a,b\in R
such that
ab\in I
, either
a\in I
or
b\in I
.
Equivalently,
I
is prime if the ring
R/I
has no zero-divisor other than zero.

(iv)

A proper ideal
I\subsetneq R
is called
primary
if for every
a,b\in R
such that
ab\in I
, either
a\in I
or some power of
b
is in
I
.
Equivalently,
I
is primary if every zero-divisor of
R/I
is nilpotent.

(v)

Let
I
be an ideal in
R
. Then
I
can always be written as a finite union of primary ideals:
I=P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{n}
. Such a finite union is called a
primary decomposition
of
I
.
Given a finite set of generators of
I
, a primary decomposition of
I
can be effectively computed
[
Rut92
]
.

(vi)

Let
S\subset R
be a set of non zero-divisors of
R
and denote by
\widetilde{S}\coloneqq\{s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N},s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in S\}
its multiplicative closure.
The
localization

S^{-1}R
is defined as the ring
\left\{\frac{r}{s}\;\middle|\;r\in R,s\in\widetilde{S}\right\}
, endowed with the operations
\frac{r_{1}}{s_{1}}+\frac{r_{2}}{s_{2}}=\frac{r_{1}s_{2}+r_{2}s_{1}}{s_{1}s_{2}}
and
\frac{r_{1}}{s_{1}}\cdot\frac{r_{2}}{s_{2}}=\frac{r_{1}r_{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}
.
In particular,
S^{-1}R
contains

as a subring
\left\{\frac{r}{1}\;\middle|\;r\in R\right\}
.
For example, the localization
\{2\}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
is the ring of rational numbers whose denominators are powers of
2
.
Note that for the canonical map
R\rightarrow S^{-1}R
to be injective, the set

must not contain zero-divisors.

(vii)

An
R
-module
is defined as an abelian group
(\mathcal{M},+)
along with an operation
\cdot\;\colon R\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, satisfying
r\cdot(m+m^{\prime})=r\cdot m+r\cdot m^{\prime}
,

,

and

.
For example, an ideal of

is an
R
-submodule of

.
For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
R^{d}
is an
R
-module by

.

(viii)

Given two

such that
\mathcal{M}\supseteq\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, define the quotient
\mathcal{M}/\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\overline{m}\mid m\in\mathcal{M}\}

\overline{m_{1}}=\overline{m_{2}}
if and only if
m_{1}-m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
This quotient is also an
R
-module.
An
R
-module is called

if it can be written as a quotient

for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
and some

.
Here,
\langle{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}}\rangle
denotes the
R

.

For
T>0
, denote by
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{N}^{\pm}]
the Laurent polynomial ring over
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}
with
n

X_{1},X_{1}^{-1},\ldots,X_{N},X_{N}^{-1}
, with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{/T}
, such that

.

Finally, we say a few words about the relation between linear recurrence sequences and formal power series.
Let
R
be a commutative ring.
A (univariate)
formal power series
over
R
is a formal sum
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}Y^{n}=a_{0}+a_{1}Y+a_{2}Y^{2}+\cdots
, where each
a_{n}
is an element in
R
.
The set of all formal power series over
R
forms a ring and is denoted by
R[[Y]]
.

Given a linear recurrence sequence
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots)\in R^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying the recursion

\forall n\geq d,\quad\gamma_{n}=a_{1}\gamma_{n-1}+\cdots+a_{d}\gamma_{n-d},

the
characteristic polynomial
of
\gamma
is defined as

f(Y)\coloneqq Y^{d}-a_{1}Y^{d-1}-\cdots-a_{d-1}Y-a_{d}\in R[Y].

Consider the polynomial

\phi(Y)\coloneqq Y^{d}\cdot f\left(\frac{1}{Y}\right)=1-a_{1}Y-\cdots-a_{d-1}Y^{d-1}-a_{d}Y^{d},

and the formal power series

g(Y)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}Y^{i}\in R[[Y]].

The product

\phi(Y)g(Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}Y^{i}-a_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{i-1}Y^{i}-\cdots-a_{d}\sum_{i=d}^{\infty}\gamma_{i-d}Y^{i}

is equal to a polynomial
h(Y)\in R[Y]
, since all its coefficients of degree
\geq d
is zero.
Therefore
g(Y)=\frac{h(Y)}{\phi(Y)}
.

3
Skolem Problem in rings of positive characteristic

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.
For a linear recurrence sequence
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots)\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, denote by
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
its zero set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\gamma_{n}=0\}
.
We now fix a ring
\mathcal{R}
of characteristic
T>0
and a linear recurrence sequence
\gamma
, and Theorem
1.1
boils down to deciding whether
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
is empty.

Let
T=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
be the prime factorization of
T
.
Since
p_{1}^{e_{1}}\mathcal{R},\ldots,p_{k}^{e_{k}}\mathcal{R}
are pairwise coprime ideals of
\mathcal{R}
(i.e.,
1\in p_{i}^{e_{i}}\mathcal{R}+p_{j}^{e_{j}}\mathcal{R}
for
i\neq j
), we have

p_{1}^{e_{1}}\mathcal{R}\cap\cdots\cap p_{k}^{e_{k}}\mathcal{R}=\{0\}

(3.1)

(c.f.
[
Mat89
, Theorem 1.3]
).
For each
i=1,\ldots,k
, consider the quotient
\mathcal{A}_{i}\coloneqq\mathcal{R}/p_{i}^{e_{i}}\mathcal{R}
and the projection map
\rho_{i}\colon\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}/p_{i}^{e_{i}}\mathcal{R}
.
Then
\alpha_{i}\coloneqq\rho_{i}(\gamma)
is a linear recurrence sequence over the ring
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.
By Equation (
3.1
), we have
r=0
if and only if
\rho_{i}(r)=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Therefore

\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{k}).

We will first show that each
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{i})
is effectively
p_{i}
-normal for
i=1,\ldots,k
, and then show that their intersection is effectively computable.

3.1 Effective
p_{i}
-normality of
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{i})

Fix an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. For brevity, we now omit the subscript
i
, so
\alpha
is a linear recurrence sequence over
\mathcal{A}
, where
p^{e}\mathcal{A}=0
for a prime number
p
and positive integer
e
.
In this subsection we will prove:
See
1.2

Let

f(X)\coloneqq X^{d}-a_{1}X^{d-1}-\cdots-a_{d-1}X-a_{d}\in\mathcal{A}[X]

be the characteristic polynomial of
\alpha
.
Observe that by extending the ring
\mathcal{A}
, one can without loss of generality suppose
f
splits in
\mathcal{A}
:

Lemma 3.1

(folklore)
.

One can effectively compute a ring
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\supseteq\mathcal{A}
, finitely generated as an
\mathcal{A}
-module, such that
f(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(X-r_{i})^{d_{i}}
with
t\in\mathbb{N},r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a new variable and consider the quotient ring
\mathcal{A}[T]/\langle{f(T)}\rangle
, where
\langle{f(T)}\rangle
denotes the ideal generated by
f(T)
.
The ring
\mathcal{A}[T]/\langle{f(T)}\rangle
is finitely generated as an
\mathcal{A}
-module by
\{1,T,\ldots,T^{d-1}\}
.
Since
f
is monic, the map
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}[T]/\langle{f(T)}\rangle
is injective.
Over the base ring
\mathcal{A}[T]/\langle{f(T)}\rangle
, the polynomial
f(X)
has a linear factor, namely
X-T
. We can then replace
f(X)
by
\frac{f(X)}{X-T}
and obtain the lemma by induction on the degree of
f
.
∎

From now on we suppose that
f
splits as a product
\prod_{i=1}^{t}(X-r_{i})^{d_{i}}
over
\mathcal{A}
.
When
\mathcal{A}
is a
field
or an
integral domain
, it is a standard result
[
OW15
]
that the
n
-th term of the sequence
\alpha
can be written as an exponential polynomial
\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}r_{i}^{n}g_{i}(n)
with
g_{i}(X)\in\mathcal{A}[X]
, (for example by considering the Jordan normal form of the transition matrix of
\alpha
).
However this no longer holds in the presence of zero-divisors in the ring
\mathcal{A}
, since these zero-divisors can neither be inverted nor localized.
To overcome this, we will use primary decomposition to reduce to the case where all zero-divisors are nilpotent.

Let
\langle{0}\rangle=P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{s}
be the primary decomposition of the zero ideal
\langle{0}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.
Consider the projection maps
\tau_{j}\colon\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}/P_{j}
.
Since
\langle{0}\rangle=P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{s}
, an element
a\in\mathcal{A}
is zero if and only if
\tau_{j}(a)=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,s
.
Therefore

\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{Z}(\tau_{1}(\alpha))\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\tau_{s}(\alpha)).

Since the intersection of
p
-normal subsets is effectively
p
-normal (Lemma
2.4
), to show
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha)
is
p
-normal it suffices to show that each
\mathfrak{Z}(\tau_{j}(\alpha)),j=1,\ldots,s
is
p
-normal.
Thus, we are reduced to considering the case where
\mathcal{A}
is of the form
A/P
, where
A
is a ring with
p^{e}A=0
, and
P
is a primary ideal of
A
.
In particular, every zero-divisor of
\mathcal{A}=A/P
is nilpotent (Definition
2.7
(iv)).

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose every zero-divisor of
\mathcal{A}
is nilpotent, and the characteristic polynomial
f
of
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
splits as a product
\prod_{i=1}^{t}(X-r_{i})^{d_{i}}
with
t\in\mathbb{N},r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then there is a finite, effectively computable set of non zero-divisors
S\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, numbers
u,N\in\mathbb{N}
, a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t\}
, and
c_{ij}\in S^{-1}\mathcal{A},i\in\mathcal{I};j=1,\ldots,u
, such that

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}r_{i}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{u}c_{ij}n^{j}

(3.2)

for all
n\geq N
. Furthermore, every
r_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}
is invertible in
S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the set
\{i\mid r_{i}\text{ is not a zero-divisor}\}
, and let

S=\{r_{i}-r_{j}\mid r_{i}-r_{j}\text{ is not a zero-divisor}\}\cup\{r_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}\}.

Then every
r_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}
is invertible in
S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
.
Denote
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
.
Consider the formal power series ring
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}[[Y]]
and let
g(Y)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}Y^{i}
.
Note that elements of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}[[Y]]
whose constant term is one are invertible.
Denote

\phi(Y)\coloneqq Y^{d}\cdot f\left(\frac{1}{Y}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}=1-a_{1}Y-\cdots-a_{d-1}Y^{d-1}-a_{d}Y^{d}.

Then
g(Y)=\frac{h(Y)}{\phi(Y)}
for some polynomial
h(Y)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}[Y]
(see Section
2
).

We claim that
\frac{1}{\phi(Y)}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}
is effectively equal to a sum

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{u}a_{ij}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}

(3.3)

for some
u\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{ij}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}[Y],1\leq i\leq t,0\leq j\leq u
.
The proof is done in two steps.

Step 1:
we reduce to the case where all
r_{i}-r_{j},1\leq i<j\leq t
are zero-divisors.
Suppose there are
r_{i},r_{j}
such that
r_{i}-r_{j}
is not a zero-divisor.
Without loss of generality suppose these are
r_{1},r_{2}
.
Then
r_{1}-r_{2}\in S
, so
r_{1}-r_{2}
is invertible in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
.
We have

\frac{1}{(1-r_{1}Y)(1-r_{2}Y)}=\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}-r_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-r_{1}Y}+\frac{-r_{2}}{r_{1}-r_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-r_{2}Y}.

Divide both sides by
(1-r_{1}Y)^{d_{1}-1}(1-r_{2}Y)^{d_{2}-1}\prod_{i=3}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}
, we obtain that
\frac{1}{\phi(Y)}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}
is equal to a sum of two fractions

\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}-r_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{d_{2}-1}\prod_{i\neq 2}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}+\frac{-r_{2}}{r_{1}-r_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{1}Y)^{d_{1}-1}\prod_{i\neq 1}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}.

(3.4)

We can repeat this argument for each of the two fractions in (
3.4
).
Note that the two denominators in (
3.4
) have smaller degree than
\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}
.
Therefore, we can apply this argument iteratively until each denominator is of the form
\prod_{i\in J}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d^{\prime}_{i}}
for some
J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t\}
, where all
r_{i}-r_{j},i,j\in J
are zero-divisors.
This reduces to the next case.

Step 2:
we now consider the case where all
r_{i}-r_{j},1\leq i<j\leq t
are zero-divisors.
We will show that
\frac{1}{\phi(Y)}
can be written in the form (
3.3
), using induction on
t
.
The base case
t=1
is trivial.
For the induction step, suppose
t\geq 2
.
Since
r_{1}-r_{2}
is a zero-divisor and hence nilpotent, there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(r_{1}-r_{2})^{\ell}=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-r_{1}Y)(1-r_{2}Y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1-r_{1}Y}{1-r_{2}Y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})Y}{1-r_{2}Y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{2}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})Y}{1-r_{2}Y}+\cdots+\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})^{\ell-1}Y^{\ell-1}}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{\ell-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{2}}+\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})Y}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{3}}+\cdots+\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})^{\ell-1}Y^{\ell-1}}{(1-r_{2}Y)^{\ell+1}}.

Divide both sides by
(1-r_{1}Y)^{d_{1}-1}(1-r_{2}Y)^{d_{2}-1}\prod_{i=3}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}
, we obtain that
\frac{1}{\phi(Y)}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}
is equal to a sum of
\ell
fractions

\frac{1}{(1-r_{1}Y)^{d_{1}-1}(1-r_{2}Y)^{d_{2}+1}\prod_{i=3}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}+\cdots+\frac{(r_{1}-r_{2})^{\ell-1}Y^{\ell-1}}{(1-r_{1}Y)^{d_{1}-1}(1-r_{2}Y)^{d_{2}+\ell}\prod_{i=3}^{t}(1-r_{i}Y)^{d_{i}}}.

(3.5)

We can repeat this argument for each of the
\ell
fractions in (
3.5
).
Note that the multiplicity of the factor
1-r_{1}Y
in the denominator of each fraction is smaller than
d_{1}
.
Therefore, we can apply this argument iteratively until this multiplicity becomes zero.
This eliminates the factor
1-r_{1}Y
, thereby decreasing
t
by one.
We thereby conclude the induction step.

Combining the two steps, we have obtained

\frac{1}{\phi(Y)}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{u}a_{ij}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}.

Consider a term
a_{ij}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}
, we have

\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{j+n-1}{j-1}r_{i}^{n}Y^{n}.

Write
a_{ij}=\sum_{s=0}^{D_{ij}}b_{s}Y^{s}
.
If
r_{i}
is not a zero-divisor, it is invertible in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
, so

a_{ij}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{s=0}^{D_{ij}}\binom{j+n-s-1}{j-1}(b_{s}r_{i}^{-s})r_{i}^{n}Y^{n}

and its
n
-th coefficient is some exponential-polynomial sum
r_{i}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{u}c_{ij}n^{j}
.
If
r_{i}
is a zero-divisor and hence nilpotent, then
r_{i}^{\ell_{i}}=0
for some effectively computable
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, so
a_{ij}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-r_{i}Y)^{j}}
is a polynomial of degree at most
\ell_{i}+D_{ij}
.
This allows us to conclude the proof of the lemma by taking
N\coloneqq 1+\max_{i\not\in\mathcal{I},0\leq j\leq u}\{\ell_{i}+D_{ij}\}
.
∎

From now on, we can without loss of generality replace
\mathcal{A}
with its extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=S^{-1}\mathcal{A}
from in Lemma
3.2
, and write

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}r_{i}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{u}c_{ij}n^{j}

for all
n\geq N
, with
c_{ij},r_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i,j
.
In particular, each
r_{i}
is invertible.

A linear recurrence
\alpha
is called

if
u=0
in the above expression.
Since
p^{e}=0
in
\mathcal{A}
, we have
n^{j}=(n+p^{e})^{j}
in
\mathcal{A}
.
Therefore the linear recurrence sequence
\alpha
can be written as a union of
p^{e}

simple

\left(\alpha_{p^{e}n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\left(\alpha_{p^{e}n+1}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\ldots,\left(\alpha_{p^{e}n+(p^{e}-1)}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
In particular, we have

\alpha_{p^{e}n+q}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}r_{i}^{p^{e}n+q}\sum_{j=0}^{u}c_{ij}(p^{e}n+q)^{j}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(r_{i}^{p^{e}}\right)^{n}d_{i,q},

with

.
Therefore we can without loss of generality replace each
r_{i}
with
r_{i}^{p^{e}}
and consider each simple recurrence.

Let

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}c_{i}r_{i}^{n}

(3.6)

be a simple recurrence sequence.
Since each
r_{i}
is invertible, we can extend the definition of the sequence (
3.6
) from

to all

.
We now show that the solution set of

is effectively

-normal in

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a ring where
p^{e}=0
and
\mathcal{I}
be a finite set of indices.
Let
c_{i},r_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
, such that each
r_{i}
is invertible.
Then, the set of integers

such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}c_{i}r_{i}^{z}=0
, is an effectively

-normal subset of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{I}
as
\{1,\ldots,t\}
for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
We can give
\mathcal{A}
a
\mathbb{Z}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{t}^{\pm}]
-module structure by letting
X_{i}
act as

.
Denote by
\mathcal{M}
the submodule generated by
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}

.
Using the standard algorithm in commutative algebra (see
[

[

) we can compute a finite presentation of

as a
\mathbb{Z}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{t}^{\pm}]

Then, the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}r_{i}^{z}=0
in
\mathcal{A}
is equivalent to the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i}^{z}\cdot c_{i}=0
in the
\mathbb{Z}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{t}^{\pm}]
-module

.

Consider the set of solutions
(z_{11},z_{12},\ldots,z_{tt})\in\mathbb{Z}^{t^{2}}
to the S-unit equation

X_{1}^{z_{11}}X_{2}^{z_{12}}\cdots X_{t}^{z_{1t}}\cdot c_{1}+\cdots+X_{1}^{z_{t1}}X_{2}^{z_{t2}}\cdots X_{t}^{z_{tt}}\cdot c_{t}=0,

in
\mathcal{M}
.
By Theorem
2.8
, the solution set
S
is effectively
p
-normal in
\mathbb{Z}^{t^{2}}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{11},b_{12},\ldots,b_{tt})\in\mathbb{Z}^{t^{2}}
be the vector defined by
b_{ij}=1
for

, and
b_{ij}=0
for

.
Applying Corollary
2.6
to the solution set
S
and the vector

, we obtain that the set of integers

satisfying

X_{1}^{z}\cdot c_{1}+\cdots+X_{t}^{z}\cdot c_{t}=0,

is effectively

-normal in
\mathbb{Z}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

This allows us to conclude the proof of Proposition

:

Lemma 3.5
.

U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, where
U_{i},i=1,2
, is elementary
p_{i}
-nested or
a\mathbb{Z}+b
.
We show that
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is either effectively
p_{1}
-normal or effectively
p_{2}
-normal.
There are four cases.

Case 1:

U_{1}
is elementary
p_{1}
-nested and
U_{2}
is elementary
p_{2}
-nested.
Write

U_{1}=\{a_{0}+p_{1}^{\ell_{1}k_{1}}a_{1}+\cdots+p_{1}^{\ell_{1}k_{r}}a_{r}\mid k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\},

and

U_{2}=\{b_{0}+p_{2}^{\ell_{2}k^{\prime}_{1}}b_{1}+\cdots+p_{2}^{\ell_{2}k^{\prime}_{s}}b_{s}\mid k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,k^{\prime}_{s}\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Take
p\coloneqq p_{1}^{\ell_{1}}
and
q\coloneqq p_{2}^{\ell_{2}}
, they are multiplicatively independent.
Any
z\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}
satisfies

z=a_{0}+p^{k_{1}}a_{1}+\cdots+p^{k_{r}}a_{r}=b_{0}+q^{k^{\prime}_{1}}b_{1}+\cdots+q^{k^{\prime}_{s}}b_{s}.

Therefore by Lemma
3.5
, we can effectively compute a bound
C\in\mathbb{N}
such that every
z\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}
satisfies
|z|\leq C
.
Therefore,
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is effectively bounded (so it is a finite union of singletons), and is therefore both effectively
p_{1}
-normal and
p_{2}
-normal.

Case 2:

U_{1}
is elementary
p_{1}
-nested and
U_{2}
is
a\mathbb{Z}+b
.
In this case, both
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are
p_{1}
-normal, therefore their intersection is effectively
p_{1}
-normal (Lemma
2.4
).

Case 3:

U_{1}
is
a\mathbb{Z}+b
and
U_{2}
elementary
p_{2}
-nested.
Similar to the previous case,
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is effectively
p_{2}
-normal.

Case 4:

U_{1}=a\mathbb{Z}+b
and
U_{2}=a^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}+b^{\prime}
.
In this case
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is either empty or of the form
A\mathbb{Z}+B
, where
A
is the least common multiplier of
a
and
a^{\prime}
.
Therefore
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is both
p_{1}
-normal and
p_{2}
-normal.

In all four cases, the intersection
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is either effectively
p_{1}
-normal or effectively
p_{2}
-normal.
Note that a finite union of
p
-normal sets is
p
-normal.
Since
S^{\prime}_{1}\cap S^{\prime}_{2}
is a finite union of sets of the form
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, it is effectively equal to a union
T_{1}^{\prime}\cup T_{2}^{\prime}
, where
T_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is effectively
p_{1}
-normal and
T_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is effectively
p_{2}
-normal.
Since

S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\left(S^{\prime}_{1}\cap S^{\prime}_{2}\cap[n,\infty)\right)\cup(F_{1}\cap F_{2})=\left(T_{1}^{\prime}\cap[n,\infty)\right)\cup\left(T_{2}^{\prime}\cap[n,\infty)\right)\cup(F_{1}\cap F_{2}),

we conclude that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
is effectively a union of the
p_{1}
-normal subset
T_{1}\coloneqq T_{1}^{\prime}\cap[n,\infty)\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and the
p_{2}
-normal subset
T_{2}\coloneqq\left(T_{2}^{\prime}\cap[n,\infty)\right)\cup(F_{1}\cap F_{2})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Lemma
3.6
allows us to prove Proposition
1.3
in full generality:

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

We use induction on
k
.
The base case
k=2
follows directly from Lemma
3.6
.
For the induction step, for each
i=1,\ldots,k
, let
S_{i}
be a
p_{i}
-normal subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
By Lemma
3.6
, we can effectively write
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=T_{1}\cup T_{2}
, where
T_{1},T_{2}
are respectively
p_{1}
-normal and
p_{2}
-normal sets.
Then

S_{1}\cap S_{2}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}=(T_{1}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k})\cup(T_{2}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}).

By the induction hypothesis on
k
, both
T_{1}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}
and
T_{2}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}
can be effectively written as a union of
p_{i}
-normal sets with
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Therefore
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\cap S_{3}\cap\cdots\cap S_{k}
is also effectively a union
T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{k}
of
p_{i}
-normal sets with
i=1,\ldots,k
.
In particular, it is empty if and only if each
T_{i}
is empty.
∎

Combining Proposition
1.2
and
1.3
, we immediately obtain Theorem
1.1
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
T=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
be the prime factorization of
T
, then

\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)=\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{k}),

where
\alpha_{i}
is the linear recurrence sequence
(\gamma\mod p_{i}^{e_{i}})
over the quotient ring
\mathcal{R}/p_{i}^{e_{i}}\mathcal{R}
.
By Proposition
1.2
, the zero set
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{i})
is effectively
p_{i}
-normal.
Then by Proposition
1.3
, the intersection
\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{Z}(\alpha_{k})
is effectively equal to a union of
p_{i}
-normal subsets of
\mathbb{N}
with
i=1,\ldots,k
, whose emptiness can be tested.
This yields a procedure to test whether
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
is empty.
∎

It is worth noting that the proof of Theorem
1.1
also shows that the zero set
\mathfrak{Z}(\gamma)
is effectively a union of
p_{i}
-normal sets with
i=1,\ldots,k
.
