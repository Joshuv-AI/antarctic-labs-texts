Single-cell whole-genome sequencing reveals convergent evolution in Burkitt lymphoma
Authors: Alexander S. Steemers, Markus J. van Roosmalen, Rico Hagelaar, Laurianne Trabut, Mark Verheul, Jurrian K. de Kanter, Siem Jongsma, Lucca L. M. Derks, Alexander R. Blackwell, Friederike Meyer-Wentrup, Ruben van Boxtel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Burkitt lymphoma carries multiple oncogenic drivers yet arises predominantly in children, whose normal cells harbour few age-related mutations. To investigate this paradox, we seek to define the sequence and timing of mutational drivers underlying Burkitt lymphoma development. Here, we analyse single-cell whole-genome sequencing data of 250 paired normal and malignant B-cells from Burkitt lymphoma patients and integrate this data with 21 bulk WGS samples and an existing dataset of 157 clonally expanded B-cells from healthy individuals. Phylogenetic reconstruction reveals an accelerated accumulation of mutations following the emergence of the most recent common ancestor, leading to the early establishment of extensive genetic intratumoural heterogeneity. We further provide evidence that convergent evolution shapes this diversity at both the point mutation– and copy number variation–levels. This in-depth characterisation further establishes Burkitt lymphoma as a paradigmatic model of tumorigenesis with implications for therapeutic intervention.

Introduction

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) has been termed the “Rosetta Stone” of cancer, reflecting its vital role in advancing our understanding of oncogenesis. It was the first cancer for which a viral association has been described (with the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV))1, the first tumour with a characterised MYC chromosomal translocation2, and the first malignancy successfully treated with chemotherapy3. Despite these landmark discoveries, the mechanisms underlying BL initiation and clonal evolution, both within and across anatomical sites, remain poorly understood.

The genetic hallmark of BL is a reciprocal translocation that places the MYC oncogene under the control of immunoglobulin enhancers, resulting in constitutive MYC overexpression4. However, MYC dysregulation alone is insufficient for BL oncogenesis, and additional genetic alterations are required to fully drive malignant transformation5–7. These cooperating mutations typically involve genes important in B-cell receptor (BCR) signalling (e.g., ID3, FOXO1, TCF3), cell survival and proliferation (e.g., MYC, CCND3, TP53), and in SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling (e.g., SMARCA4, ARID1A), highlighting recurrent disruption of signalling, growth regulation, and epigenetic networks in BL8.

Paradoxically, BL arises predominantly in children, posing the intriguing question of how multiple driver mutations accumulate within such a short developmental timeframe. This apparent contradiction highlights a critical gap in our understanding of BL evolution. Consequently, despite advances in defining the molecular drivers7–11, the timing and clonal architecture of BL mutations remain largely unresolved, in part because conventional bulk sequencing of biopsies blurs signals from genetically heterogeneous subclones. Obtaining longitudinal tumour samples prior to diagnosis would provide invaluable insight into the clonal dynamics and the evolutionary steps preceding overt disease. In practice, however, such sampling is not possible. Consequently, most studies infer evolutionary history from single time-point samples, leveraging intratumoural heterogeneity (ITH) as a molecular record of past mutational events.

In this study, we perform single-cell whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on both normal and malignant B-cells from primary sporadic BL samples, enabling direct tracing of clonal lineages and detailed characterisation of ITH. By sampling tumour cells from either a single anatomical site or multiple spatially distinct regions within the same patient, we infer both the temporal and spatial evolution of BL.

Results

Single-cell WGS cohort represents broader BL mutational landscape

To reconstruct the clonal architecture and the sequence of genetic driver events, we performed single-cell WGS on six Burkitt lymphoma patients. These patients ranged in age from 4 to 17 years (median 13 years) and included five males and one female. Notably, two of these patients had multi-site involvement, allowing us to study dissemination dynamics. Specifically, two tumour site samples were collected at diagnosis for one patient, and paired diagnosis-relapse samples were obtained from another patient. Performing single-cell WGS on patient-matched normal and malignant B-cells requires the isolation of sufficient genomic DNA from individual cells, which is typically achieved via single-cell clonal expansion12,13. However, primary malignant B-cells are challenging to expand ex vivo, often requiring advanced culturing systems to support their survival and proliferation14. To ensure that our single-cell WGS cohort is truly representative of BL, we additionally analysed bulk WGS data from a total of 18 BL patients (14 males, 4 females; median age 12 years, range 3–17). These comprised the 6 patients included in the single-cell WGS analysis and 12 additional cases (Supplementary Data 1). Evidence of EBV involvement was assessed using two complementary approaches, namely histologically using EBER in situ hybridisation and computationally using VIRUSBreakend16 (see “Methods”). Neither method revealed EBV involvement in any of the Burkitt samples. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that the majority of sporadic BL cases in children are EBV negative9,17. Furthermore, this increases the biological uniformity of our cohort since it reduces any variability that may be introduced by an EBV infection. We next assessed the driver landscape of somatic mutations, including single-base substitutions (SBS) and insertions and deletions (INDELs) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 2).

The number of driver genes mutated per sample ranged from 3 to 10 (mean = 6.3), reflecting the multi-step nature of BL tumorigenesis8,18. MYC, TP53 and ID3 were the most frequently mutated genes, consistent with previous findings7,19. However, with bulk WGS data, determining whether these mutations occur in the same or in distinct subclones remains inferential and cannot be confirmed with certainty20. To elucidate the selection pressure, we applied dN/dS analysis to all somatic SBS and INDELs identified in our cohort (Fig. 1c). This method detects genes with an excess of nonsynonymous over synonymous substitutions, indicative of positive selection21. Indeed, all genes under significant positive selection (dN/dS >1; FDR < 10%) were also among the most frequently mutated in our cohort, including TP53, ID3, CCND3, MYC, SMARCA4, ARID1A, FBXO11 and GNA13. In terms of copy number variations (CNVs), the most frequent gain was in chr1q (14/21 samples), in line with previous studies (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 3)22,23. Other recurrent events included gains in chr7q (9/21), chr7p (4/21), and chr1p (3/21). Taken together, our single-cell WGS cohort closely represents the genetic landscape of BL.

Phylogenetic reconstruction

Following single-cell WGS, 82 out of 332 single cells were excluded due to low sequencing coverage or allelic imbalance (see “Methods”), resulting in a final dataset of 250 high-quality single-cell genomes (Supplementary Data 4). A mean of 42 whole genomes were taken forward per patient (range 16–58 whole genomes), with a mean sequencing depth of 11.7× (range 7.6–24.6×) and an average of 83% genome coverage at ≥5× depth. Following germline and artefact filtering, we identified a total of 283,400 autosomal SBSs and 51,164 autosomal INDELs24. To explore the distribution of these mutations between the different cells of the same patient, we reconstructed phylogenies based on shared genome-wide SBSs, enabling us to resolve the clonal relationships at single-cell resolution (Fig. 2). In these trees, internal branches represent mutations propagated across multiple descendants from a shared ancestral cell, terminal branches capture more recent mutations unique to individual cells, and nodes mark inferred ancestral cells from which downstream lineages arose, offering a unique window into the past. All trees displayed a dense cluster of coalescences (branching nodes) near the root, reflecting the rapid expansion of B-cell precursors and the resulting genomic divergence during early lymphopoiesis, consistent with previous reports during healthy haematopoiesis25,26. This indicates that for any two normal B-cells, their most recent common ancestor (MRCA) lies early in development. Beyond this early expansion, the phylogenies of normal B-cells displayed a ‘comb-like’ branching pattern. However, in one patient (PIA9), the normal B-cell phylogeny deviated from this expected structure and instead showed evidence of clonal expansion within the normal B-cell compartment (Fig. 2). BL cells exhibit convergent evolution

A distinctive feature of the tumour phylogenies is the clustering of malignant cells into a single clade, marked by a long trunk. The basal node of this single clade corresponds to the MRCA, defined here as the single cell from which the sampled tumour population expanded. B-cell receptor (BCR) recombination analysis showed that all tumour cells had the same immunoglobulin heavy-light chain rearrangement, confirming that the clone started expanding following this process (i.e. monoclonal). In contrast, normal B-cells remained polyclonal (Supplementary Fig. 2). This indicates that in the case of BL cells, the identity of the MRCA was a B-cell, whereas in the case of normal B-cells it is a more primitive cell. In line with previous reports describing a high frequency of BCR rearrangements involving the IGHV3 genes10,27, this V gene family was observed in 5/6 of the tumours. When comparing each patient’s bulk tumour sample to the corresponding phylogenetic tree, 98.4% (5447/5538) of all truncal mutations, defined as mutations arising prior to the MRCA, were detected in bulk sequencing. In contrast, 74.4% (1916/2576) of all intermediate mutations, acquired during early post-MRCA clonal expansion but before the emergence of private tumour branches, were also detected in the bulk tumour samples. Finally, 0.5% (460/86714) of all private branch mutations, arising later during tumour evolution in individual tumour cells, were detected (Supplementary Fig. 3). This not only demonstrates the high recall rate of our single-cell WGS approach but also underscores the limitations of bulk sequencing in capturing subclonal and private variants.

Driver analysis revealed that the hallmark MYC::IGH translocation was always located in the shared trunk of the tumour phylogenies and was consistently accompanied by additional truncal mutations (Supplementary Fig. 4). Unexpectedly, we were able to place mutations in known driver genes in the intermediate branches of the trees. These intermediate nodes appear to have given rise to subclones that subsequently expanded, as inferred from the bursts of coalescences emerging directly beneath them. Although we could not place any mutations in known driver genes in the intermediate branches of patient P3G6, subclonal expansions were still observed. This observation indicates that BL tumours exhibit substantial ITH, where multiple clonal lineages arise from a common ancestor. Within these subclonal events, we found strong evidence of convergent evolution. This occurs when separate tumour lineages independently acquire similar genetic alterations, including mutations in the same driver genes or CNVs affecting the same chromosomal regions. Specifically, in PJBU, PTEN and P2RY8 were independently mutated in the trunk and in an intermediate branch that led to the largest subclone; in PVA9, PHF6 was recurrently altered across two separate intermediate branches; and in PIA9, three distinct ID3 mutations arose in intermediate lineages, each driving parallel subclonal expansions. We next evaluated whether these convergent mutations occurred in trans using read-based phasing. In PJBU, variant pairs PTEN p.V275E and p.R233* were too distant to phase, while the truncal P2RY8 p.V178A mutation was phased in trans with the subclonal p.D177N mutation. In PVA9, variant pairs PHF6 p.H104fs* and p.A288D were too distant to phase. In PIA9, the truncal ID3 p.D43G mutation was phased in trans with both p.P56S and p.L64F, whereas p.D43G versus p.Q100* was too far apart for read-based phasing (Supplementary Fig. 5). The finding that subclonal mutations occur on a different allele than the clonal mutation supports strong selective pressure for biallelic inactivation of these genes. In addition to SBSs and INDELs, we found evidence of convergent evolution at the CNV level, particularly affecting chromosome 1 (Supplementary Fig. 6). In PIA9, two independent gains were detected (chr1p11.2–q44 and chr1q12–q32.2), while PRN4 exhibited a similar pattern, with a chr1q21.1–q41 gain in the bone marrow and a chr1q21.1–q31.3 gain in the lymph node. These findings demonstrate that tumour evolution is not purely stochastic but instead shaped by convergent selective pressures that repeatedly target the same genomic regions and pathways, driving parallel subclonal trajectories.

Our single-cell WGS cohort also included two patients with dissemination, allowing us to dissect the spatial evolution of tumours. In PRN4, an ancestral clone appeared to bifurcate. One branch migrated to the bone marrow, while the other branch presumably remained in the lymph node. Driver analysis indicated that the bone-marrow-associated branch carried a TFAP4 subclonal mutation, whereas the lymph-node-associated branch harboured a PHF6 mutation. This pattern demonstrates that ITH not only exist across, but also within anatomical sites. In P856, bulk WGS data revealed that 28 of the 305 mutations (9.2%) shared specifically by all bone marrow tumour cells were detectable at low VAF in the diagnostic pleural sample (median VAF = 0.08), providing evidence that the relapse-associated BM lineage was already present as a minor subclone at diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, a subset of pleural-specific mutations were detectable in the bone marrow (7/102, median VAF = 0.07), indicating that the relapse bone marrow contains a small subpopulation carrying pleural-lineage variants. This could be explained by low-frequency migration or dissemination between the two sites. Moreover, no known subclonal driver events were found in either the diagnostic or relapse samples, indicating that the tumour expansion in both diagnosis and relapse was not fuelled by the acquisition of additional genetic drivers. Instead, the phylogeny was characterised by a single dominant truncal lineage. A previous study has indicated that relapse BL patients can be characterised by having complex karyotypes and being TP53-deficient due to two mutations, namely an SBS variant and a low-frequency CNV28. Indeed, patient P856 acquired multiple CNVs across the genome and a homozygous R273H mutation in TP53 due to a missense mutation combined with a chr17p13.3-13.1 focal loss. All other patients in our cohort who did not relapse displayed a heterozygous clonal TP53 mutation, with the exception of PVA9, where a subclonal homozygous G245S TP53 mutation arose due to a local chr17p13.3-11.2 LOH. However, this alteration was present only in a minor subclone within the tumour mass, suggesting that the timing of a driver event may be more critical than the mutation itself in determining tumour aggressiveness.

Copy-number variations represent late events in tumour evolution

Our single-cell approach enabled us to infer the sequential order of CNV events. Strikingly, many CNVs presented as subclonal events, including chromosome 1 gains in PJBU, PIA9 and PRN4, a chromosome 17p LOH in PVA9, and a chromosome 4q loss in only a subset of tumour cells in the bone marrow of PRN4. To validate the late timing of CNVs in a larger cohort, we performed a relative timing analysis on our bulk WGS data (see “Methods”). This approach estimates timing in regions with copy number gains or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH) by considering the co-occurrence of SBSs and INDELs. Specifically, the relative timing of a CNV is calculated as the ratio of amplified to non-amplified mutations within the affected region. In line with our single-cell data, we found that CNV timings were not evenly distributed, instead, they tended to arise late (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Notably, the recurrent copy number gains mentioned earlier in our BL cohort (chr1p, chr1q, chr7p and chr7q) consistently showed a late relative timing (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Taken together, our results demonstrate that CNVs represent one of the late genomic events preceding the establishment of the MRCA, and in many cases are acquired as subclonal events.

Accelerated mutation rate in BL cells

We next sought to compare the SBS mutational burden between normal and BL cells. It was previously reported that bulk tumour samples harbour the same mutational burden as age-matched normal memory B-cells13. To validate our observations within a larger dataset, we incorporated 157 previously clonally expanded single-cell genomes of naive and memory B-cells from 7 healthy donors13. Since SBS9 is associated with somatic hypermutation and therefore distinguishes naive B-cells from differentiated memory B-cells13, we subdivided our patient-derived normal B-cells into SBS9⁺ and SBS9−. We then applied a linear mixed-effects model trained on naive B-cells from healthy donors to estimate the expected mutation load at each age, thereby providing an age-adjusted observed-vs-expected (O/E) ratio baseline (Fig. 3a). Patient-derived SBS9- normal B-cells (median O/E ratio 1.49) displayed slightly higher mutation load compared to naive B-cells (median O/E ratio 0.93) from healthy donors. Further analysis showed that this was skewed due to the SBS9- normal B-cells from patient PIA9 which previously showed evidence of clonal expansion within the normal B-cell compartment (Supplementary Fig. 8). Indeed, the median O/E ratio decreased from 1.49 (IQR 1.18–2.37) when all cells were included to 1.31 (IQR 1.05–1.49) when PIA9 cells were excluded, although both groups remained significantly elevated compared with naive B-cells from Machado et al. (P = 7.5 × 10−12 with PIA9; P = 3.1 × 10−7 without PIA9). By contrast, memory B-cells from healthy donors and SBS9⁺ normal B-cells from patients both exhibited elevated mutation burden, with no significant difference between them (median O/E ratio 1.97 vs 2.36; P = 0.59). The median O/E ratio of clonal mutations identified in bulk WGS tumour samples was slightly higher but remained comparable to that observed in SBS9⁺ normal B-cells (2.94 vs 2.36), consistent with previous reports13. Having established that mutation rates increase during tumour development, we next wondered what the consequence of this is on selection. For this, we performed dN/dS analysis across normal B-cells and distinct phases of Burkitt lymphoma evolution (Fig. 3b). Normal B-cells exhibited dN/dS values close to neutrality (dN/dS = 0.85, 95% CI 0.65–1.10). In contrast, both the pre-MRCA expansion phase of BL, i.e. trunk branches (dN/dS = 1.40, 95% CI 0.82–2.39), and the early post-MRCA expansion phase, i.e. intermediate branches (dN/dS = 1.95, 95% CI 0.72–5.27), showed mean dN/dS estimates >1, consistent with positive selection. However, the confidence intervals for these dN/dS estimates overlapped with 1, reflecting the limited number of coding mutations recovered per group in the single-cell data. To increase statistical power, we also looked at the dN/dS ratio of the 21 bulk WGS samples which, as we have shown earlier, largely consist of truncal and intermediate mutations (Supplementary Fig. 3). In line with our single-cell data, these mutations yielded a dN/dS ratio of 1.36 (95% CI 1.12–1.64), consistent with positive selection. Surprisingly, the private branches displayed evidence of purifying selection (dN/dS = 0.62, CI 0.52–0.72), indicating that once major drivers had been acquired, further nonsynonymous mutations might harm the fitness of the clones and are thus selectively constrained. To determine whether purifying selection in BL-private mutations preferentially targets essential genes, we derived a Burkitt lymphoma-specific essential gene set (2099 genes) from DepMap CRISPR dependency data across five BL cell lines and compared it to a matched control set. In BL-private mutations, both essential and control genes showed evidence of purifying selection (dN/dS <1). However, essential genes were not more strongly constrained than controls (dN/dS = 0.63 vs 0.42), and the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.86). These findings indicate that although late-stage BL evolution exhibits global negative selection, this constraint is not specifically enriched within CRISPR-defined essential genes and instead appears to act broadly across the coding genome. Collectively, these findings support a model in which Burkitt lymphoma tumours arise from pre-expanded cells with baseline mutation rates under early positive selection, undergo a subsequent phase of accelerated mutational activity marked by continued positive selection and acquisition of additional driver mutations, and are later characterised by clonal expansions constrained by purifying selection.

Spatial and temporal dynamics of mutational processes

We next wondered which mutational processes lead to the excess mutational load associated with malignant outgrowth. For this, we investigated the mutational processes active in both normal and malignant B-cells by quantifying mutational signature contributions across individual branches of the phylogenetic trees (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). Mutational signature analysis extracted the following single-base substitution signatures: SBS1, SBSblood, SBS7a, SBS17b, SBS9, and SBS18 (see “Methods”). SBS1, caused by spontaneous deamination of methylated CpGs and linked to replication29,30, and SBSblood, thought to be a hematopoietic form of the SBS5 process13, are both clock-like endogenous signatures. SBS7a is frequently found in cancers of the skin and is thus likely due to exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light13. As mentioned earlier, SBS9 is attributed to somatic hypermutation, while SBS17b and SBS18 are linked to damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS)31–33. In patient PRN4, from which multiple sites were sampled at diagnosis, both lymph node and bone marrow tumours shared a common ancestral branch dominated by endogenous processes, namely SBS1, SBSblood, and SBS9 (Fig. 4b). However, site-specific private branches revealed distinct mutational exposures. Private mutations in lymph node–derived tumour subclones showed an elevated contribution of SBS7a (relative contribution = 14.1%), whereas this signature was largely absent in the private mutations of the bone marrow-derived tumour subclones (relative contribution = 1.6%). The affected lymph node was located in the head and neck region, an area frequently exposed to UV light. However, given the limited penetration depth of UV radiation in human skin, direct irradiation of lymph node tissue is unlikely34. Similar UV-like mutational signatures have been reported in tumours lacking UV exposure, such as paediatric B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, where the underlying mechanism remains unresolved and may reflect a mutational process that mimics canonical UV-associated patterns35,36. Furthermore, a similar pattern of site-restricted mutational processes was observed for the ROS-associated SBS18 signature. In P856, SBS18 was detected specifically in the pleural cavity–derived branches but was absent from the relapsed bone marrow branches. Conversely, in PRN4, SBS18 was found specifically in the bone marrow-derived branches and was not detected in the lymph node private branches (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). These findings suggest that local microenvironmental or tissue-specific exposures may shape the mutational landscape of spatially separated Burkitt lymphoma cells.

We next sought to assess the temporal dynamics of mutational processes. SBS9 remained active in truncal and intermediate branches but declined sharply in late private branches, while SBSblood showed the opposite pattern, increasing in late private branches, indicating a shift in mutational process dominance during malignant evolution (Fig. 4c). This pattern suggests that SBS9 may contribute to positive selection and driver mutation acquisition during the early stages of malignant transformation. To study the likelihood that driver mutations were directly caused by polymerase η-associated mutagenesis, we used a previously established method to calculate the probability that a certain mutation can be attributed to a signature37. This analysis showed that only six of the 92 identified driving SBSs had high probability (>70%) to be attributed to the SBS9 signature, whereas the majority of drivers (41/92) had a high probability to be attributed to the clock-like signatures SBS1 and SBSblood (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Timing BL expansion

We further sought to time the driving events and clonal expansions in years. To convert our phylogenetic trees from somatic SBS mutations to time, we first determined which processes accumulate at a constant mutation rate, as not all processes are expected to be constantly active with age. Hence, mutational signature contributions were examined in normal B-cells from our cohort combined with the Machado et al. dataset and correlated with donor age at the timepoint of sampling (Fig. 5a)13. SBS1 (slope = 1.3 SBSs/year) and SBSblood (slope = 8.9 SBSs/year) both correlated linearly with time, as previously shown13. In contrast, SBS18 showed no age-dependent accumulation and was undetectable in 13% of normal B-cells. Although SBS17b, SBS9, and SBS7a showed positive slopes with age, these signatures were absent from a substantial fraction of cells (28%, 16%, and 8%, respectively). Altogether, these observations suggest that SBS1 and SBSblood indeed represent constant clock-like endogenous mutational processes, while SBS7a, SBS9, SBS17b and SBS18 likely arise through more stochastic or episodic processes13. After restricting our analysis to SBS1 and SBSblood, we found that both normal B-cell subtypes (SBS9− and SBS9⁺) exhibited similar O/E ratios, implying that these processes indeed accumulate mutations at a comparable rate despite cell type differences (Fig. 5b). Nevertheless, malignant cells displayed a higher O/E ratio compared to normal B-cells, with a notable exception in the samples of PRN4 (bone marrow), P856 (diagnosis) and PIA9. This indicates an acceleration of these otherwise stable, clock-like mutational processes after malignant transformation, which could be due to either an increased cellular turnover or a decreased DNA repair efficiency. To test the latter, we analysed transcriptional strand bias and observed no change in the late private mutations, suggesting that the transcription-coupled repair pathway is not impaired during late tumour evolution (Supplementary Fig. 12). Based on these findings we inferred the timing of the coalescences using the rtreefit package38 while accounting for a higher rate of mutation accumulation at the end of the trunk branch of the tumour clade, just before the emergence of the MRCA.

From our ‘time-based’ trees, we could now time several key events, including the emergence of the MRCA, the acquisition of subclonal drivers, and the latency period, which is the time between the MRCA of the tumour and sampling (Fig. 5c). Despite the clinically aggressive nature of Burkitt lymphoma, our data indicate latency periods of several years (mean 5.1 years; range 2.1–8.8 years). Interestingly, we were able to time the origin of the relapse in patient P856 ~6 years before the initial diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 13). Although our cohort was small, there seemed to be a trend where younger patients correlated with a shorter latency period (Fig. 5d). This has previously also been described in BCR::ABL1-driven chronic myeloid leukaemia, where younger patients showed more explosive growth together with a shorter duration between the beginning of BCR::ABL1 clonal expansion and diagnosis39. Moreover, subclonal drivers were acquired soon after the establishment of the MRCA (1–1.5 years post-MRCA), suggesting that ITH arises during early tumour development, followed by stable expansion of a limited number of dominant clones (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged the high resolution of single-cell WGS to resolve the ITH and reconstruct the temporal and spatial sequence of genetic events in BL. We found that BL cells harbour a substantially higher mutational burden than age-matched normal B-cells. This contrasts with previous bulk sequencing studies that reported comparable mutation loads between BL and normal B-lymphocytes13, underscoring the greater sensitivity of single-cell WGS for capturing genetic diversity.

Reconstruction of phylogenetic trees revealed that clonal expansions were driven by subclonal, fitness-enhancing mutations arising after the MRCA. This suggests that the hallmark MYC::IGH translocation and other early genetic drivers are important in enabling tumour initiation and survival, but do not directly account for the explosive growth of BL. We found compelling evidence of convergent evolution, where distinct subclones independently acquired mutations in the same genes or CNVs affecting the same chromosome. dN/dS analysis across phylogenetic branches revealed strong positive selection during the pre-MRCA expansion and early tumour evolution, consistent with the acquisition of key driver mutations. Following major clonal expansions, selection shifted toward a purifying regime, indicating that deleterious mutations were increasingly removed as dominant clones became well adapted and further nonsynonymous changes were more likely to compromise fitness.

Our cohort also included a patient sampled at diagnosis and relapse. In this case, phylogenetic reconstruction revealed a linear evolutionary trajectory in which all driver mutations were present in the ancestral clone, with no evidence of subclonal diversification. This implies that tumour expansions in each timepoint were likely driven by non-genetic or microenvironmental factors, rather than by the acquisition of detectable genetic alterations. Nonetheless, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of additional subclonal drivers that remain undetected or as-yet uncharacterised. Previous studies have reported that BL relapses are frequently associated with biallelic TP53 disruption through damaging point mutations and CNVs, whereas non-relapsing patients either lack TP53 mutations or carry only heterozygous variants11,28. In our cohort, four of six patients (P3G6, PRN4, PVA9 and P856) harboured truncal TP53 mutations. In all cases except P856, these were heterozygous. Patient P856, who was the only individual in our cohort to relapse, exhibited truncal biallelic TP53 loss. Intriguingly, a biallelic TP53 alteration was also seen in patient PVA9, but this arose later as a subclonal event (due to a subclonal focal copy number loss), which remained restricted to a minor clone. This contrast suggests that the timing, rather than the mere presence, of TP53 mutations may determine whether TP53-deficient clones achieve clonal dominance and thereby increase the risk of relapse. Acknowledging the limited size of our cohort, further work is required to delineate the role of TP53 mutation timing in shaping tumour evolution and relapse risk.

Our temporal inference relies on two key assumptions: (i) that the observed shift in mutation rate occurs around the time of emergence of the MRCA of the tumour clone, and (ii) that SBS1 and SBSblood continue to accumulate in a clock-like manner after this shift, albeit at an increased rate. Under these assumptions, temporal calibration of phylogenetic trees showed that the MRCA emerged several years before diagnosis (mean 5.1 years), with the latency period positively correlating with patient age. However, we acknowledge that the observed phylogenetic structure could also be consistent with alternative evolutionary scenarios, such as rapid early monoclonal expansion followed by later diversification, or strong early selective sweeps that eliminate intermediate diversity, without requiring a prolonged latent phase. This finding raises important questions about which cell-intrinsic and extrinsic factors sustain prolonged latency and, conversely, what triggers the rapid clinical progression characteristic of BL. Moreover, our temporal analysis showed that subclonal driver mutations were acquired within 1–1.5 years of MRCA emergence, indicating that ITH arises early rather than accumulating gradually during tumour growth.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that BL tumours are characterised by pronounced intratumoural heterogeneity, which is shaped by convergent evolution both on the small-scale (SBSs and INDELs) and large-scale (CNVs) genomic levels. These observations imply that therapeutic strategies targeting subclonal pathways sustaining tumour proliferation may be more effective than interventions solely directed at early events such as MYC activation, which have yet to show clinical efficacy. However, because such pathways are active only in a subset of tumour cells, targeting them alone is unlikely to yield a durable clinical benefit. Instead, these pathways may be more effectively exploited within combination strategies, either alongside standard-of-care treatment or in conjunction with other targeted therapies.

Methods

Patient samples

Samples were collected from patients with Burkitt lymphoma via the biobank of the Princess Máxima Center for Pediatric Oncology in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients or their parents/legally authorised representatives (for patients under 16 years of age) provided informed, written consent for the use of their samples for research. For each sample, viably frozen single-cell suspensions were obtained, which is part of our routine diagnostic workup40. In addition, bulk WGS data (tumour and matched normal samples) from BL patients were obtained from our in-house diagnostics database, where samples are routinely sequenced as part of clinical evaluation. The study was covered under the proposal PMCLAB2022-303 approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Máxima. Clinical information of the patients, including the sex, age and sampling sites, is summarised in Supplementary Data 1. Informed consent was obtained for the inclusion and publication of this clinical data.

Sample work-up

Samples were worked up for single-cell WGS according to our previously published STAR Protocol guidelines41. Briefly, samples were stained for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) after thawing. Burkitt lymphoma (BL) cells and normal B-cells were purified on a SH800S Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology SH800S Cell Sorter, RRID:SCR_018066) using a 100 μm microfluidic sorting chip. First, manual gates were used to remove debris (FSC-A vs. SSC-A), doublets (FSC-A vs. FSC-H) and dead cells (DAPI + ). BL cells were identified using the following surface markers: CD20+CD10high and either IgK+ or IgL+ (depending on their monoclonal immunoglobulin light chain rearrangement). Single BL cells and normal B-cells (CD20+CD10low) were index sorted in a 96-well plate or 384-well plate prepared with PTA-buffer. Bulk BL cells were then sorted for DNA isolation. Representative gating strategies are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All antibodies were obtained from BioLegend. Live/dead staining and antibodies used for BL and normal B-cell populations, including the dilutions: DAPI (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat#D3571, RRID:AB_2307445) CD20-FITC (clone 2H7, 1:25, BioLegend Cat#302303, RRID:AB_314251), CD10-APC (clone HI10a, 1:50, BioLegend Cat#312209, RRID:AB_314920), IgK-PE/Cy7 (clone MHK-49, 1:50, BioLegend Cat#316520, RRID:AB_2561645), IgL-AF700 (clone MHL-38, 1:25, BioLegend Cat#316631, RRID:AB_2819949).

Germline controls

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) were cultured from the bone marrow fraction of 500,000 cells/well in 12-well culture dishes with 2 mL Advanced DMEM-F12 medium (Cat#12634010, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Cat#16000044, Gibco), 1% GlutaMax (Cat#35050061, Gibco) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Cat#15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Medium was refreshed every other day to remove non-adherent cells, and MSCs were harvested when confluent (after approximately 3 weeks). For more details, see Supplementary Data 5.

DNA isolation and WGS

DNA was isolated from cell pellets of bulk BL cells and MSCs using the DNeasy DNA Micro Kit (Cat#56304, Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The standard protocol was slightly adjusted by adding 2 µL RNase A (Cat#19101, Qiagen) during the lysis step and eluting DNA in 50 µL low EDTA TE buffer (10 mM Tris, 0.1 mM EDTA, G Biosciences, Cat#786150).

DNA from single cells was whole-genome amplified (WGA) using the ResolveDNA® WholeGenome Amplification Kit (Cat#100136, BioSkryb) or the ResolveDNA® Whole Genome Amplification Kit v.2.0 (Cat#100545, BioSkryb) using a D100 Single Cell Dispenser (HP), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Details on which kit was used per sample are summarised in Supplementary Data 4.

For each sample, DNA libraries for Illumina sequencing were generated from at least 45 ng genomic DNA using standard protocols. For PTA-amplified DNA at least 200 ng genomic DNA was used. The libraries were sequenced at a depth of 15× (single cells) or 30× (bulk tumour and MSC samples).

Read mapping

Sequencing reads were first mapped to genome GRCh38 using the BWA-MEM2 (Burrows–Wheeler Aligner) algorithm (v.2.2.1, RRID:SCR_022192) using the settings “–M –c 100 -R”. Duplicates were then marked using GATK4spark (v.4.4.0.0), and base recalibration was performed using GATK4 (v.4.4.0.0, RRID:SCR_001876).

Mutation calling and annotation

ASAP (v1.0.4) was used to call both germline and somatic variants in both bulk and single-cell samples. Specifically, mutation calling was performed using GATK’s HaplotypeCaller on combined samples per patient.

Variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) was applied separately for SNPs and INDELs using the VariantRecalibrator tool with the following annotations: -an QD -an MQ -an MQRankSum -an ReadPosRankSum -an FS -an SOR -mode SNP for SNPs, and -an QD -an MQ -an MQRankSum -an ReadPosRankSum -an FS -an SOR -mode INDEL for INDELs. ApplyVQSR was then run with --truthsensitivity-filter-level 99.9 -mode SNP and --truth-sensitivity-filter-level 99.9 -mode INDEL.

Next, GATK’s VariantFiltration was run with the following options: “--filter-expression ‘QD < 2.0’ --filterexpression ‘MQ < 40.0’ --filter-expression ‘FS > 60.0’ --filter-expression ‘HaplotypeScore > 13.0’ --filterexpression ‘MQRankSum < −12.5’ --filter-expression ‘ReadPosRankSum < −8.0’ --filter-expression ‘MQ0 > = 4 & & ((MQ0 / (1.0 * DP)) > 0.1)’ --filter-expression ‘DP < 5’ --filter-expression ‘QUAL < 30’ --filter-expression ‘QUAL > = 30.0 & & QUAL < 50.0’ --filter-expression ‘SOR > 4.0’ --filter-name ‘SNP_LowQualityDepth’ --filtername ‘SNP_MappingQuality’ --filter-name ‘SNP_StrandBias’ --filter-name ‘SNP_HaplotypeScoreHigh’ --filter-name ‘SNP_MQRankSumLow’ --filter-name ‘SNP_ReadPosRankSumLow’ --filter-name ‘SNP_HardToValidate’ --filter-name ‘SNP_LowCoverage’ --filter-name ‘SNP_VeryLowQual’ --filter-name ‘SNP_LowQual’ --filter-name ‘SNP_SOR’ -cluster 3 -window 10”. Annotation of variants was done using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)42 (v.112.0, RRID:SCR_007931) with settings = “--vcf --plugin AlphaMissense, file --plugin NMD --plugin dbNSFP” (RRID:SCR_005178). The full pipeline is available at https://github.com/ToolsVanBox/ASAP.

Somatic mutation filtering

SMuRF v3.0.0 was used for obtaining high-confidence somatic mutation calls from both bulk and single-cell samples (available at https://github.com/ToolsVanBox/SMuRF). Variants were retained if they met the following criteria: (A) assigned a GATK Phred-scaled quality score of at least 100, (B) had a mapping quality of ≥60 for 30× coverage data or ≥55 for 15× coverage, (C) had minimum base coverage of 10 (30×) or 5 (15×), (D) exhibited a GATK genotype quality of 99 (for heterozygous calls) or 10 (for homozygous calls) in both the tumour sample and matched normal, and (E) showed no supporting reads in the matched control. Finally, mutations with low variant allele frequencies (VAF) were removed to obtain a set of clonal mutations. The VAF cut-off for single-cell was 0.15, while for bulk samples this was 0.05, as mutations below this cut-off could be technical artefacts.

Removing PTA-associated artefacts

For single cells subjected to PTA-based whole-genome amplification, we further processed the SMuRF filtered VCF files using our in-house pipeline PTATO (v1.3.3, RRID:SCR_025353), which is specifically designed to identify and remove PTA-associated artefacts. Briefly, the trained PTATO random forest model calculates the probability that each variant is a PTA artefact. Subsequently, it uses a linked read analysis (Walker, v2.2.0) and cosine similarity calculations to determine the sample-specific probability cut-off. For a full description of this pipeline, see our previous publication24.

Quality control of single-cell WGS data

To ensure high-confidence single-cell genomic profiles, we first excluded samples with a callable loci fraction below 0.45 (n = 13). The cut-off was determined based on the distribution of the callable loci fraction among all samples (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Callable loci were identified using GATK CallableLoci (v3.8.1) with the following parameters: --minBaseQuality 10, --minMappingQuality 10, --minDepth 8, --minDepthForLowMAPQ 10, and --maxDepth 100. For the remaining samples, we fitted a logistic regression model to describe the relationship between mean coverage and callable genome fraction and removed samples with residuals below −0.05 as having low mapping quality (n = 37; Supplementary Fig. 14b). Again, the cut-off here was selected based on the distribution (Supplementary Fig. 14c). B-allele frequency (BAF) plots were then manually reviewed, and samples with widespread apparent chromosomal losses across the genome, a pattern characteristic of allelic dropout in PTA rather than true biological CNVs, were removed (n = 25; see Supplementary Fig. 14d for representative examples). Finally, to evaluate variant-level quality, we computed the total variation distance (TVD) between each sample’s VAF histogram and the cohort-wide median distribution. Samples with strongly deviant VAF profiles, which could be due to technical artefacts such as allelic imbalance due to amplification bias or potential doublets, were flagged as outliers using a threshold of median + 3.5 × MAD (n = 7; Supplementary Fig. 14e).

Per sample MYC::IG mutation status

For bulk WGS samples, the location of the MYC::IGH translocation was manually examined using IGV (v.2.8.2, RRID:SCR_011793). Patient-specific primers were designed that flank the translocation breakpoints to assess the presence or absence of the MYC::IGH rearrangement in single-cell samples. In addition, the hallmark translocation was manually reviewed and validated using IGV (v.2.8.2)43. Samples were classified as translocation-positive if at least one pair of reads spanned the breakpoint region (Supplementary Fig. 15). Single-cell samples were classified as normal B-cells or malignant cells according to the absence or presence of a MYC::IGH translocation, respectively.

EBV status

EBER in situ hybridisation was performed using the BOND Ready-to-Use ISH EBER Probe (Leica Biosystems Cat#PB0589) on the Leica BOND-III Stainer (RRID:SCR_026521). Of the 18 patients included in this study, EBER staining was available for 13, all of whom were EBER-negative (Supplementary Fig. 16). In the remaining five patients, no tissue sample was available for analysis. To assess Epstein–Barr virus presence and potential genomic integration of those patients as well, we used VIRUSBreakend16, a computational tool for detecting viral sequences and integration breakpoints from WGS data. As a positive control, we analysed a previously sequenced bulk WGS sample from the AHH-1 cell line, known to be EBV-transformed (NCBI Taxonomy ID: 10376)24. As expected, EBV reads were detected in the AHH-1 sample. In contrast, no evidence of EBV DNA or viral integration was found in any of the patient-derived bulk WGS samples analysed in this study.

B-cell receptor repertoire

BAM files for each single-cell or bulk sample were first subset to the TCR and BCR loci using a custom BED file. The locus-specific BAMs were then converted to paired-end FASTQ files. Receptor reconstruction was performed with MiXCR (v.4.3.2, RRID:SCR_018725)44. For each clone, the top-scoring V, D and J genes, as well as the amino acid CDR3 sequence, were extracted. Clones supported by only one read and with CDR3 sequences containing incomplete (“_”) and/or stop (“*”) codons were filtered out from downstream analysis. BCR repertoires were visualised using the R package ComplexHeatmap v.2.22.045.

Phylogenetic tree construction

Phylogenetic reconstruction of normal and malignant single cells from the same patient was performed using CellPhy (v.0.9.254)46, which relies on RAxML-NG for maximum likelihood inference. CellPhy considers allelic dropout and amplification errors on a per-sample basis to estimate the most likely tree structure. Analyses were performed using phred-scaled genotype likelihoods (PL) under the default “GT16 + FO + E” model. For each tree, 100 bootstrap iterations were generated, and the proportion of replicates supporting each split is shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, providing a measure of node confidence. Since CellPhy assigns mutations based on where it will provide the most information on which split occurs and not based on where the mutations actually are within the tree, we addressed this by using our own CellPhyWrapper function available at https://github.com/ToolsVanBox/CellPhyWrapper.

Trees were rooted using the “root” function from the treeio R package, with MSCs used as the outgroup. To validate tree topology, variant allele frequencies (VAFs) from bulk tumour samples were assessed. Clonal (trunk) branch mutations displayed variant allele frequencies (VAFs) of ~0.5, consistent with their presence in all tumour cells. By contrast, mutations assigned to intermediate branches—positioned between trunk and private branches—were either undetectable or detected at low VAFs in bulk sequencing, reflecting the resolution limits of this method. Private branch mutations were almost uniformly undetectable in bulk, indicating their restriction to individual malignant cells (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To account for differences in sequencing sensitivity between cells, we corrected branch lengths using callable loci information. Specifically, we calculated a node-specific detection power, p, as p = 1 − Π(1 – Si), where Si is the callable-loci fraction of sample i, and scaled the original CellPhy branch lengths by this value to yield callable-normalised branch lengths.

SBS and INDEL driver annotation

For single-base substitution (SBS) and insertion–deletion (INDEL) variants, somatic variants were identified by filtering against matched germline controls, and only variants classified as somatic were retained. To ensure high-confidence calls, we included only variants labelled as “PASS”, indicating that they had passed all standard quality filters implemented in GATK. Variants were further filtered based on predicted functional impact using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP), retaining only variants classified as having HIGH or MODERATE impact. Missense variants were subsequently prioritised using AlphaMissense predictions, and only variants classified as “likely pathogenic” or “ambiguous” were retained, while variants predicted to be “likely benign” or lacking prediction scores were excluded. Analyses were restricted to protein-coding variants with predicted functional consequences, including stop gained, frameshift variant, missense variant, inframe insertion, inframe deletion, splice donor variant, splice acceptor variant, protein-altering variant, start lost, and stop lost. Finally, to focus on biologically relevant alterations, mutations were restricted to genes previously implicated in Burkitt lymphoma based on published literature7,11. The full gene set used (n = 71) is provided in Supplementary Data 12. Oncoplots were generated using the maftools package47(v2.22.0). For single-cell WGS data, variants were additionally required to be present in at least two tumour cells. If a driver mutation was not detected in a given tumour cell despite being present in other cells within the same clade, we attributed this absence to technical limitations, following the principle of parsimony (Occam’s razor) based on the phylogenetic reconstruction (see Supplementary Fig. 4). All SBS and INDEL driver mutations identified in the bulk and single-cell samples are provided in Supplementary Data 2 and 6–11.

CNV annotation

Structural variants and copy number alterations were called using the HMF tools GRIDSS-PURPLE-LINX pipeline48 from Hartwig Medical Foundation, consisting of the following tools: GRIDSS (v1.3.2)49 for SV breakpoint calling, GRIPSS (v1.9) for SV filtering, AMBER (v3.5) for BAF calling, COBALT (v1.11) for read depth ratio calling, PURPLE (v2.51) for copy number, purity and ploidy estimation and LINX (v1.12) for SV annotation.

Copy number inference was performed at 100 kb genomic bin resolution across all single cells. For visualisation and robustness, 100 kb–binned read depth (i.e., normalised coverage) values were smoothed using a sliding 5 Mb window. In addition, pericentromeric regions and the acrocentric chromosome arms of chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22 were excluded from the analysis to avoid artefactual copy number calls due to a high frequency of mapping artefacts in these regions. Furthermore, to improve interpretability and better illustrate CNA evolution, we sorted single-cell copy number profiles according to their position in the phylogenetic trees (see Supplementary Fig. 6). As for SBS and INDEL drivers, we followed the principle of parsimony (Occam’s razor) based on the phylogenetic reconstruction to assign CNVs in the branches.

Selective pressure analysis

To assess selective pressures on coding mutations in our bulk WGS data, we applied the dNdScv R package (v0.1.0, RRID:SCR_023123)21 using the hg38 reference genome. Here, only autosomal SBS variants were included for standard dN/dS analysis. The analysis was performed against the hg38 reference genome using default trinucleotide context corrections. For each gene, the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates (dN/dS) was estimated separately for missense and truncating classes. Gene-level selection coefficients were extracted, and genes with a global q value < 0.1 were considered under positive selection. Effect sizes (w) and q values were log-transformed for visualisation, and the most significant class per gene (missense or truncating) was used to construct a volcano plot highlighting genes under positive selection.

To assess selective pressures across tumour evolution, we estimated dN/dS ratios within single-cell WGS-derived phylogenetic branches. Mutations were assigned to phylogenetic branches and grouped into four categories per patient: Normal B-cells, BL-Trunk (pre-MRCA expansion), BL-Intermediate (early post-MRCA expansion), and BL-Private (late post-MRCA expansion). Immunoglobulin loci (IGH, IGK, IGL) were excluded to avoid bias from somatic hypermutation. dNdScv was run separately for each group to compute overall dN/dS values and 95% confidence intervals, which were visualised as dot and forest plots.

To determine whether purifying selection in BL-private mutations preferentially affects essential genes, we defined a Burkitt lymphoma-specific essential gene set using DepMap CRISPR-Cas9 dependency data from five BL cell lines: TL-1 (ACH-002055), BL-70 (ACH-000402), BLUE-1 (ACH-001450), ST486 (ACH000567), and NAMALWA (ACH-000944). For each cell line, the 500 genes with the lowest Chronos gene effect scores were selected and combined into a nonredundant set. After restricting to genes present in the dndscv hg38 reference database, this yielded 2099 genes. A matched control gene set of equal size was constructed by sampling from the remaining coding genes while balancing for expected mutational opportunity (synonymous and nonsynonymous rates) as estimated by dndscv, thereby controlling for gene length and sequence-context effects. Within BL-private mutations, gene set-specific dN/dS ratios were calculated, and confidence intervals were estimated by bootstrapping genes within each set (5000 iterations).

SBS burden analysis

We estimated per-cell mutational load from PTATO-filtered VCFs by retaining autosomal variants and correcting for each cell’s callable-genome fraction, thus yielding SBS per callable genome. To place tumour loads in context, we combined our normal and malignant B-cells with healthy naive/memory B-cell colonies from Machado et al.13 and with bulk BL tumours. For bulk tumour samples, clonal mutations were identified using cancer cell fraction (CCF) estimates to account for the effects of tumour purity and copy number alterations on variant allele frequencies (VAFs). CCFs were estimated using the R package ccube (v.1.0, RRID:SCR_027721), which infers the fraction of tumour cells harbouring each mutation from variant and reference read counts together with sample purity and allele-specific copy number information derived from PURPLE. Mutations with an estimated CCF = 1 were classified as clonal and used for downstream analyses. Age effects were modelled using linear mixed-effects regression, with the naive B-cells from healthy donors fit provided the age-specific baseline. Then, per sample, we computed the Observed/Expected (O/E) ratio (i.e. mutation load/baseline at that age). Group differences were assessed with two-sided Wilcoxon tests. No data were excluded from this analysis.

Relative timing of CNVs in bulk samples

The relative timing of CNVs found in bulk WGS samples was inferred using the MutationTimeR package (v1.0.2)50. Timing was estimated from the proportion of mutations in different copy-number states within each CNV, which reflects the chronological order of mutational acquisition under the assumption of a constant mutation rate. Copy-number profiles generated by PURPLE were smoothed by merging adjacent segments with copy-number ratios between 0.9 and 1.1, rounding values to integers, and combining neighbouring events with equal CN. Small segments (<1 Mb) were subsequently merged with the nearest larger event to further reduce noise. The resulting list of CNVs was used as input to generate a circos plot depicting the frequency of CNVs in the bulk BL samples. MutationTimeR was then applied to all samples using the processed CNV events and previously identified somatic mutations from SMuRF. The method estimates the relative timing of each mutation and CNV on a 0-1 scale, with 0 representing the earliest events and 1 representing the latest events. Low-confidence CNV events were excluded by removing those with wide timing uncertainty—defined as a confidence interval width >0.8 (i.e. time.up – time.lo > 0.8)—and with genomic widths <7 Mb.

Mutational signature extraction and refitting

Mutational signature analysis was carried out using the MutationalPatterns (v.3.16.0, RRID:SCR_024247) R package51. 96-trinucleotide profiles were extracted from the SBS mutations in the single-cell WGS samples to identify the landscape of mutational contexts. To increase our confidence in extracting mutational signatures, we included additional mutational profiles: bulk WGS of Burkitt lymphoma samples from the Princess Máxima Center for Pediatric Oncology cohort (n = 21), a collection of previously published healthy T-cell, B-cell and HSC reference samples (n = 734)13 and genomes from 7 blood cancer types, including Burkitt lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (mutated), chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (unmutated), and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) and multiple myeloma (MM) (n = 233)52,53.

De novo mutational signature extraction using non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF) was performed to identify the signatures that are present in the samples. Extraction was done by applying the “extract_signatures” function with options “rank = 8, nrun = 100. Cosine similarities between the extracted signatures and COSMIC SBS signatures (version 3) were calculated to identify previously described signatures among the extracted signatures. Signature SBSblood was also included13. From this, the following signatures were identified: SBS1-like (cosine 0.9), SBS9-like (cosine 0.86), SBS18-like (cosine 0.9), SBSblood (cosine 0.92), SBS17b-like (cosine 0.91), SBSA, SBSB, and SBSC. The SBSC profile showed high similarity with SBS2 (cosine 0.85) and SBS7a (cosine 0.83) and was therefore substituted for these two signatures (Supplementary Fig. 18). SBSA and SBSB had low cosine similarity to any known signature. Together, the following set of signatures was used for signature refitting: SBSA, SBSB, SBS1, SBS2, SBS7a, SBS9, SBS17b, SBS18 and SBSblood.

To determine the contribution of these signatures to the single-cell and bulk WGS Burkitt samples, strict bootstrapped refitting was performed using 100 bootstraps per sample with “max_delta = 0.002”. SBSA, SBSB and SBS2 all had less that 5% in each genome and were excluded from the analysis, and the signature proportions were re-estimated using the remaining 6 signatures: SBS1, SBS7a, SBS9, SBS17b, SBS18 and SBSblood. The cosine similarity between the original and reconstructed matrices was >0.8 in all samples (mean: 0.94; range: 0.82–0.98). To test signature robustness, we added a non-extracted signature (including the artefact signatures due to PTA-based whole genome amplification) one-at-a-time and recalculated the cosine similarity, as well as removing one of the six extracted signatures individually. For each sample, we quantified the Δ cosine similarity relative to the original refitting using the 6 extracted signatures and visualised the per-sample changes. Indeed, adding the artefact signatures did not improve the refitting, while removing any of the 6 signatures did worsen the cosine similarity (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Mutational signature contribution within phylogenetic trees

Fitting mutational signatures to the mutations of single branches of the tree was done with the MutationalPatterns51 function “fit_to_signatures_strict_tree” with option “max_delta = 0.002”. For this analysis, branches were assigned to one of four categories: “Normal B-cell”, “BL-Trunk”, “BL-Intermediate”, or “BL-Private”. Normal B-cell branches comprised all private branches derived from normal B-cells. The BL-Trunk category corresponded to the ancestral branch preceding the MRCA of the tumour sampled. BL-Intermediate branches were defined as those arising after the MRCA expansion but prior to the emergence of private branches. BL-Private branches represented private branches restricted to individual Burkitt lymphoma cells. To contrast normal subgroups with BL compartments, we built per-patient signature matrices for BL trunk, intermediate, and private branches, converted them to within-group proportions, and merged them with normal (SBS9+/−). For each signature, we then fitted a beta-GLMM with Group as a fixed effect and Patient as a random intercept and derived estimated marginal means. To test if changes in relative contribution between normal/malignant groups were significant, a two-sided Wald z-test was used. No data were excluded from this analysis.

Potential impact of mutational signatures on driver mutations

The probability that a driver mutation was caused by the mutational signatures present in a sample was calculated using an approach similar to that described by Morganella et al.37. Briefly, the contribution of each signature to the sample was multiplied by the probability that the signature generates a mutation with the same mutation type and trinucleotide context as the driver mutation. Finally, these values were then summed across all signatures and the proportion contributed by each signature to the total sum was multiplied by 100 to obtain the probability expressed as a percentage.

Timing branches

To estimate the timing of mutational events, we reconstructed ‘time-based’ phylogenetic trees using the rtreefit R package (https://github.com/nangalialab/rtreefit). This approach converts phylogenetic trees with branch lengths in molecular time (number of SBS mutations) into trees scaled in chronological time (years). In brief, the method simultaneously estimates wild-type and mutant mutation rates (the number of SBSs accumulated per year) and absolute branch durations within a Bayesian, per-individual tree model. It assumes that the number of mutations assigned to each branch follows a Poisson distribution while constraining the total root-to-tip length to match the patient’s age at sampling. This was calculated as the patient's age plus 9 months, to account for the fertilisation age.

We defined the branch absolute mutation lengths as the sum of SBS1 and SBSblood contributions, representing linear, clock-like mutational processes, and the age at sampling as being post-conception. Furthermore, we accounted for an elevated mutation rate during embryogenesis by assuming an excess mutation rate through development. Finally, the mutant clade was defined as not including the tumour trunk, so the method assumed that the mutation rate switch from normal to mutant occurred at the end of the trunk. For donors with two anatomically distinct tumour sites (PRN4 and P856), rate switches were introduced at the end of the shared branches corresponding to each site. No switch was placed for the samples PRN4 bone marrow, P856 pleural, and PIA9 ascites, as tumour cells had a similar mutation burden compared to healthy B-cells.

We noted that for the larger trees of patients PJBU, PVA9 and PIA9, the algorithm took a long time when all cells were included, but that time dropped drastically (from ~2 months to ~2 min) when one random normal B-cell was removed. Therefore, we removed normal B-cells PIA9GTDBBC56, PJBUGTDBBC73 and PVA9GTDBBC74 for PIA9, PJBU and PVA9, respectively. To test whether removing a normal cell from a tree influences timing estimates, we tested this on P3G6 and found no difference in MRCA timing (Supplementary Fig. 20). The rtreefit algorithm was run with 12 chains and 20,000 iterations per chain.
