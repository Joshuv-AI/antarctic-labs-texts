PRC1-mediated H2A.Zub promotes gene expression by preventing H3.1K27me1 incorporation in Arabidopsis
Authors: Fernando Baile, Javier Antúnez-Sánchez, José Gutiérrez-Marcos, Myriam Calonje
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPcG complexes are pivotal in orchestrating the transition from embryonic to vegetative development in plants. However, the mechanisms underlying the gene expression reprogramming that takes place during this developmental transition are still not fully understood. Several studies suggest that incorporating PcG modifications into distinct histone variants may play a key role in this process. However, while PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 is essential for gene repression, the timing of PRC2 action on canonical H3.1 or variant H3.3 remains unclear. Furthermore, the exact role of PRC1 in transcriptional regulation is still unresolved, partly owing to limited knowledge of the conditions under which this complex monoubiquitinates canonical H2A or H2A.Z variant.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that H2A.Z undergoes monoubiquitination during the seed-to-seedling transition. H2A.Zub facilitates the recruitment of PRC2 to mediate H3.3 trimethylation, repressing seed-specific genes; however, H2A.Zub also promotes the activation of vegetative-specific genes by preventing H3K27me1 incorporation into H3.1. Notably, the histone demethylase REF6 initiates this process by removing two methyl groups from stably repressed H3.1K27me3-marked genes, enabling the subsequent H2A.Zub incorporation. This result suggests that REF6 activity is a critical early step in PRC1-mediated transcriptional activation.ConclusionsOur findings reveal the long-sought mechanism by which PRC1 participates in transcriptional activation. We demonstrate that PRC1-mediated H2A.Zub, acting as a “switcher”, plays a pivotal role in reprogramming active and repressed genes during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development. Moreover, our results provide new insights into the intricate relationship between histone modifications and histone variants in reprogramming and maintaining gene expression patterns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03856-4.

Background

Polycomb group (PcG) histone-modifying activities are conserved between animals and plants. There are two main complexes, Polycomb Repressive Complex 1 (PRC1) and PRC2. While PRC1 is responsible for the monoubiquitination of histone H2A (H2Aub) at lysine 118 (K118) in Drosophila, K119 in vertebrates, or K121 in plants, PRC2 mediates the trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) in different organisms [1]. These two modifications are pivotal epigenetic marks that mediate gene repression.

In Arabidopsis, the PRC1 monoubiquitin ligase module consists of RING1A or RING1B and one of three Polycomb RING Finger proteins, BMI1A, B, or C [2–6]. However, they may also act independently, as they both display H2A E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, and the phenotypes of their respective mutants differ to a certain extent [3, 4, 6]. The monoubiquitin ligase module can associate with other proteins that provide accessory activities or are involved in target recognition [1]. Concerning PRC2, three distinct complexes can be distinguished on the basis of their core subunits [7, 8]. While FERTILISATION INDEPENDENT ENDOSPERM (FIE) and MULTIPLE SUPPRESSOR OF IRA 1 (MSI1) are common subunits for all three complexes [9, 10], the catalytic subunit can be either CURLY LEAF (CLF), SWINGER (SWN) or MEDEA (MEA) [7, 11, 12], and the scaffold subunit SEED INDEPENDENT FERTILISATION 2 (FIS2), EMBRYONIC FLOWER 2 (EMF2) or VERNALISATION 2 (VRN2) [13–15].

PRC1 and PRC2 regulate phase transitions during plant development [8, 16, 17]. Accordingly, the loss of PRC1 or PRC2 function, as in the strong PRC1 mutant bmi1abc or PRC2 mutant clfswn [6, 18, 19], prevents the proper transition from embryonic to vegetative development. These two complexes modulate gene expression in a precise and coordinated manner, ensuring that the necessary genes are expressed at the right time and place; however, the exact mechanism by which they exert this regulation is still not entirely clear.

Genome-wide localization studies of H2Aub and H3K27me3 in Arabidopsis revealed three distinct types of PcG targets based on the presence/absence of these modifications: genes marked only with H2Aub (only-H2Aub), double marked genes (H2Aub/H3K27me3), and genes marked only with H3K27me3 (only-H3K27me3) [20]. The analysis of these marks in the PRC1 and PRC2 mutants revealed that the incorporation of H2Aub is independent of PRC2 activity, but PRC1 activity is required for H3K27me3 marking of double-marked genes. In contrast, PRC1 seems to be dispensable for the incorporation of H3K27me3 at only H3K27me3-marked genes. This, together with expression analyses in wild-type (WT) and mutant plants, led to the proposal that there are two distinct types of repressive mechanisms [1, 20–23]. While the first mechanism, involving the incorporation of H2Aub for PRC2 recruitment, is required to switch off transcriptionally active genes, the second mechanism involves only H3K27me3 and is directed to maintain gene repression. Nevertheless, it is still a matter of debate whether only H3K27me3-marked genes result from removing H2Aub marks after PRC2 recruitment or if PRC2 is recruited to these genes independently of PRC1.

PRC1 appears to have functions that are independent of PRC2, as evidenced by several observations. Unlike clfswn, bmi1abc shows a greater number of downregulated than upregulated genes [17]. Additionally, the average expression levels of genes marked only with H2Aub is higher than H2Aub/H3K27me3 marked genes [22, 23]. Furthermore, bmi1abc exhibits long-range chromatin interactions associated with gene repression that are absent in clfswn [24]. Accordingly, it has been proposed that H2Aub is not a repressive mark by itself but rather causes repression via PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 deposition [22]. Moreover, on the basis of the colocalization of only H2Aub peaks with the DNA binding motifs of the H3K27-demethylase RELATIVE OF EARLY FLOWERING 6 (REF6) [25, 26], it has been proposed that REF6 removes H3K27me3 from double-marked genes, resulting in genes marked only by H2Aub, to activate gene expression [22]. Biochemical studies have shown that overexpression of REF6 reduces H3K27me3 levels, and that many genes gain H3K27me3 in the ref6 loss-of-function mutant [25, 26], supporting its H3K27-demethylase activity. Nevertheless, REF6 activity leaves H3K27me1 behind, which is associated with low expression levels [27], suggesting that this may not be enough to activate gene expression. In addition, whether there is a direct connection between H2Aub and REF6 activity remains to be investigated.

PRC1 also mediates monoubiquitination of the histone variant H2A.Z [28]. This histone variant can display different localizations within the gene region. H2A.Z occupancy of the entire gene body (GB) is highly correlated with gene repression, whereas the localization of H2A.Z at the transcriptional start site (TSS) is associated with gene expression [28–33]. In any case, it is still unknown in which context H2A.Z or H2A is monoubiquitinated. This is in part because the human H2Aub antibody used for genome-wide studies of H2Aub in Arabidopsis also cross-reacts with H2A.Zub; therefore, the distribution may be a mix of H2Aub and H2A.Zub marked regions (hereafter referred to as H2A/Zub). In addition, available anti-H2A.Z antibodies recognize both H2A.Z and H2A.Zub [28], which hampers the precise localization of H2A.Zub.

Consequently, several unresolved questions remain regarding the role of these complexes, particularly PRC1, in regulating gene expression. In this work, we show that H2A.Zub is required to switch OFF and ON gene expression during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development. While the repressive role of H2A.Zub is linked to PRC2 activity, its activating role is connected to preventing H3K27me1 deposition. Furthermore, our data uncover a hitherto unrecognized crosstalk between H2A.Zub and the replacement of histone variants to shape chromatin states in Arabidopsis.

Results

The PRC1 mutant bmi1abc displays a seed-like transcriptome

To gain insight into the function of PRC1, we focused on the transition from embryonic to vegetative growth, as this developmental transition is strongly affected by the loss of PcG function [6, 19]. Accordingly, the PRC1 mutant bmi1abc is unable to switch to generative development after germination, and the PRC2 mutant clf28swn7 (clfswn) initiates phase transition but subsequently experiences a progressive loss of cell differentiation [6, 19] (Fig. 1A). Comparative analyses of bmi1abc and clfswn mutants with WT seedlings at ~ 10 days after germination (DAG) revealed strong upregulation of embryonic genes in both mutants [17, 19, 34, 35], supporting the role of PRC1 and PRC2 in repressing the seed and embryonic developmental programs. However, how bmi1abc and clfswn seedlings compare to WT seeds during imbibition or before germination is not known. Therefore, we conducted a comparative whole genome transcriptome analysis with WT seeds at 0 h and 48 h after imbibition (HAI) and WT, bmi1abc and clfswn seedlings at 7 DAG [20, 36]. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a relationship between the transcriptomes of bmi1abc and WT seeds at 48 HAI, whereas the transcriptomes of clfswn, WT seeds at 0 HAI and WT seedlings were distinct (Fig. 1B). We then determined the number of genes with comparable expression levels in bmi1abc at 7 DAG and WT seeds at 48 HAI (fold change < 2 and a p-value < 0.05), and intersected with the genes up- and downregulated in bmi1abc compared with WT seedlings at 7 DAG (fold change ≥ 2 and a p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 1C, and Additional File 1: Table S1A). A notable proportion of the genes that were either up- or downregulated in bmi1abc presented comparable expression levels in bmi1abc and WT seeds at 48 HAI (72% and 85.4%, respectively). We then performed a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the bmi1abc up- and downregulated genes, and selected a set of representative GO terms comprising 1,246 genes to compared their expression across WT seeds (0 and 48 HAI), WT seedlings, and bmi1abc (Fig. 1D). The upregulated genes in bmi1abc were enriched in GO terms associated with lipid metabolism, seed development, and germination, and their expression levels were more similar to those of WT seeds at 48 HAI. In contrast, the downregulated genes were enriched in GO terms related to photosynthesis and vegetative growth. The expression of these genes in bmi1abc showed an intermediate profile, with some genes resembling the expression levels in WT seeds at 0 HAI, and others aligning more with seeds at 48 HAI. H2A.Z is monoubiquitinated during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development

PRC1 has been shown to monoubiquitinate one of the four canonical H2A histones (HTA10) in Arabidopsis, with a preference for K121 (HTA10ub) [4]. Similarly, one of the three H2A.Z variants (HTA9) is also predominantly monoubiquitinated at K129 (HTA9ub) [28] (Fig. 2A). However, the precise timing of the monoubiquitination of these histones remains unknown. H2A.Z can also be acetylated at its N-terminal region by the nucleosomal acetyltransferase of the H4 (NuA4) complex [37, 38], but it has not yet been established whether this modification is required for the deposition of this variant and/or if it plays a role in transcriptional regulation [37, 38]. To evaluate the possible impact of H2A.Z monoubiquitination and acetylation in plant development, we generated different versions of HTA9 by replacing with arginine (R) the putative lysine residues targeted by acetylation at the N-terminal region (K3, K6, and K12) [39] and those that are known to be monoubiquitinated at the C-terminal region (K129 and K132) [28] (Fig. 2B). Wild-type (NN) and mutant HTA9 versions (NR, RN, and RR) were introduced into the H2A.Z mutant hta9/11 [40] under the control of a constitutive promoter (see methods). H2A.Z in Arabidopsis is encoded by three genes, HTA8, HTA9, and HTA11 [41], whose functions are largely redundant, as mutations in the two most highly expressed genes, HTA9 and HTA11, cause a weak phenotype characterized by early flowering [40]. Therefore, after verifying that all the transformed HTA9 versions were expressed and that those with a mutated C-terminus were unable to undergo monoubiquitination (Fig. 2C), we determined the number of rosette leaves at flowering in multiple transgenic lines (Fig. 2D and E). We found that lines harbouring wild-type HTA9 (NN) and HTA9 with mutations at its N-terminus (RN) partially rescued the early-flowering phenotype characteristic of hta9/11 plants (Fig. 2D and E). However, transgenic lines carrying mutations in the C-terminal region (NR) or in both the N- and C-terminal regions (RR) of HTA9 failed to complement the early flowering phenotype of hta9/11. In fact, although not significantly, they seemed to flower earlier than hta9/11 (Fig. 2E). One possible explanation for this observation is that hta9/11 is a knockdown mutant in which some residual HTA9 is incorporated into chromatin ([28] and Fig. 2C). Overexpression of the HTA9 NR or RR mutant variant could result in the incorporation of these modified histones at genomic sites occupied by this residual HTA9, and thereby further altering the phenotype. We also examined the levels of H2A.Z acetylation in the different plants by WB using a commercial anti-H2A.Zac antibody. However, the antibody cross reacted with different acetylated histones, thus limiting the accurate quantification of H2A.Zac levels (Additional File 2: Fig. S1). Consequently, we cannot rule out the possibility that mutation of the N-terminal lysine residues led to the loss of a histone modification other than acetylation. In any case, these results show that mutation of these residues play a minor developmental role.

Next, we investigated which H2A was monoubiquitinated during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development. Consistent with previous findings [28], immunoblotting analysis using anti-HsH2Aub showed that monoubiquitinated forms of both H2A (HTA10ub) and H2A.Z (HTA9ub) were detected in WT 10 DAG seedlings, whereas only HTA10ub was detected in hta9/11 and none of them in bmi1abc (Fig. 2F). When looking at seedlings at 2, 7, and 10 DAG we found that while HTA9ub is present at all-time points (being stronger at 2 DAG), HTA10ub is only detected at 7 and 10 DAG (Fig. 2G top). We validated this observations using an Arabidopsis HTA10K121ub-specific antibody [6] (Fig. 2G middle). Notably, in the clfswn mutant, which remains in an embryo-like stage, HTA9ub but not HTA10ub was detected (Fig. 2H). These results support the view that H2A.Z is monoubiquitinated during the embryo-to-seedling developmental transition, whereas the histone H2A is monoubiquitinated later on, during vegetative growth.

H2A.Zub is required for the repression of seed-specific genes

Since PRC1 activity is required to recruit PRC2 to a considerable proportion of genes [20, 23], we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) to analyse the distribution of H3K27me3 in WT seeds at 48 HAI (Additional File 2: Fig. S2). We compared the genes marked with H3K27me3 at this stage to those marked with H3K27me3 in WT seedlings at 7 DAG [20] (Fig. 3A; Additional File 1: Table S1B). We found that 67.15% of the genes marked with H3K27me3 at 7 DAG were already marked in imbibed seeds. To distinguish between PRC1-dependent and PRC1-independent H3K27me3 marked genes, we included those genes that presented a loss of H3K27me3 (over 20%) in bmi1abc seedlings relative to WT seedlings (2,523 genes, Additional File 1: Table S1B). Approximately 40% of the genes that lost H3K27me3 in bmi1abc were exclusively marked with H3K27me3 in seedlings (1,019 genes), whereas the remaining 60% were marked both in WT imbibed seeds and seedlings (1,504 genes) (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we investigated in more detail the levels of H3K27me3 at these two gene groups in WT seeds at 48 HAI, WT seedlings, clfswn and bmi1abc (Fig. 3B). All these genes were marked with H3K27me3 in WT seedlings and deprived of this modification in clfswn and bmi1abc. Nevertheless, a subset of them displayed some levels of H3K27me3 in WT imbibed seeds (e.g., DELAY OF GERMINATION 1 (DOG1)), while other genes did not (e.g., CRUCIFERIN 3 (CRU3)). Accordingly, the overall H3K27me3 levels at these 2,523 genes were reduced in seeds at 48 HAI compared to those in WT seedlings. These data suggest that the timing of H3K27me3 acquisition differs in these two subsets of genes. We also analysed the levels of H2Aub and/or H2A.Zub (H2A/Zub) and H3K4me3 in WT, bmi1abc and clfswn 7 DAG (Fig. 3B). We observed high levels of H2A/Zub in both WT and clfswn, which were reduced in bmi1abc. In contrast, H3K4me3 levels were elevated in bmi1abc and clfswn compared to WT. Accordingly, the average expression levels of PRC1-dependent H3K27me3 marked genes were higher in bmi1abc and clfswn than in imbibed seeds or WT seedlings (Fig. 3B). We then examined the epigenetic landscape of the genes marked with H3K27me3 in WT imbibed seeds and seedlings whose levels were not altered in bmi1abc (3,091, Fig. 3A and C). These genes were marked with H3K27me3 in all the genotypes except for clfswn (Fig. 3C). In contrast, they presented negligible levels of H2A/Zub and H3K4me3 in bmi1abc and WT, and somewhat increased in clfswn, which results in a strong upregulation in this mutant, suggesting that they are PRC1-independent H3K27me3 marked genes (Fig. 3C). GO term analysis of these genes revealed enrichment in terms associated with plant organ development, particularly floral development (Additional File 2: Fig. S3). These findings suggest that these genes are stably repressed during development until their activation is needed.

Interestingly, when we analysed the ChIP-seq localization of different histones [canonical H3.1, H3.3 variant [42], canonical HTA13 [43], and H2A.Z variant (HTA9) (this study) (Additional File 2: Fig. S4)] at these genes in WT seedlings, we found that both PRC1-dependent and PRC1-independent H3K27me3 marked genes were enriched in H3.1 and H2A.Z at their gene body (GB) (Fig. 3B and C). This contrasted with the distribution of histone variants in active genes, which are enriched in H3.3, particularly at the 3´ region, H2A.Z in the TSS region and HTA13 in the GB (Additional File 2: Fig. S5). This histone distribution suggests that the trimethylation of H3.1, H2A.Z distribution, and PcG-mediated stable repression are interconnected. Moreover, this finding also indicates that PRC2 acts on H3.3 when initially recruited by PRC1, as target genes are active and thus enriched in H3.3.

REF6 recruitment is independent of PRC1 activity

Although the repressive role of PRC1 in combination with H3K27me3 is well supported by these and previously published data [20, 23], the fact that only-H2Aub marked genes in WT seedlings are expressed led to the hypothesis that H2Aub is not a repressive mark by itself [22]. Furthermore, the colocalization of only-H2Aub peaks with the DNA binding motif (CTCTGYTY) of the H3K27me3 demethylase REF6 [26, 44, 45] led to the proposal that H2Aub may facilitate the recruitment of REF6 to these genes to remove H3K27me3 and allow gene expression [22]. In line with this, it has been shown that REF6 could be recruited to target genes by other factors [46]. We thus reasoned that if REF6 recruitment is dependent of H2A/Zub, the genes that gain H3K27me3 in ref6 loss-of-function mutants should also do it in bmi1abc. Hence, we compared the genes marked with H3K27me3 in bmi1abc [20] with those marked in ref6-5 and WT seedlings at 14 DAG [27] (Additional File 1: Table S1C). However, we did not find overlap between the genes marked with H3K27me3 in bmi1abc and those gaining H3K27me3 in ref6-5 (2,313 genes, Fig. 4A). In addition, we intersected the genes marked with H2A/Zub in WT (14,087) and the genes losing H2A/Zub in bmi1abc (11,071) [20] with those gaining H3K27me3 in ref6-5 (2,313) (Fig. 4B). It is noteworthy that not all H2A/Zub-marked genes in WT seedlings lose this mark in the bmi1abc mutant. This likely reflects residual ubiquitin ligase activity of RING1 proteins in the absence of BMI1s. In any case, the majority of genes that gained H3K27me3 in ref6-5 belonged to the subset of genes that either lost H2A/Zub in bmi1abc, or were not marked with this modification. Next, we investigated whether the loss of REF6 activity affects H2A/Zub levels. For this, we determined the levels of H2A/Zub at all genes in 10-day-old ref6-5 seedlings by ChIP-seq, and compared them to those in WT and bmi1abc at 7 DAG (Additional File 2: Fig. S6A). However, we did not find major changes in ref6-5 compared to WT. Given that REF6 binding sites colocalize with only-H2A/Zub peaks, we examined the levels of this modification at all only-H2Aub/Zub marked genes in WT across different genotypes, and found that ref6-5 exhibited WT-like average levels (Fig. 4C). Finally, we focused the analysis on the genes marked only with H2A/Zub in WT that gained H3K27me3 in ref6-5 (1,488; Fig. 4C). Interestingly, these genes showed reduced levels of H2A/Zub in both ref6-5 and bmi1abc. A similar result was obtained when analyzing the levels of this modification at REF6 bound peaks [26] (Additional File 2: Fig. S6B), suggesting that REF6 activity is required for proper incorporation of H2A/Zub at this subset of genes. We then compared the expression levels of these 1,488 genes in WT, bmi1abc, and ref6-5 [27], finding low levels in WT, slightly lower (though not significantly) in bmi1abc, and significantly lower in ref6-5 (Fig. 4D). In concordance with their low expression levels in WT, these genes were enriched in canonical H3.1 and H2A.Z at their GB (Fig. 4E).

Subsequently, we generated the bmi1abc ref6-5 mutant by crossing bmi1abc with ref6-5, and determined the genome-wide distribution of H3K27me3 in this genotype at 10 DAG (Additional File 2: Fig. S7, Additional File 1: Table S1C). Phenotypic analysis revealed that bmi1abc ref6-5 were indistinguishable from bmi1abc mutants (Fig. 4F). When we compared the genes marked with H3K27me3 in bmi1abc ref6-5, ref6-5 and WT seedlings, we found that the majority of the genes marked with H3K27me3 in WT were also marked in ref6-5 and bmi1abc ref6-5 (5,425 genes, Fig. 4G). These genes largely corresponded to the subset of only-H3K27me3 marked genes that were stably repressed in WT imbibed seeds, WT seedlings and bmi1abc (Additional File 2: Fig. S8A). We also identified 2,765 genes marked with H3K27me3 in WT and ref6-5 but not in bmi1abc ref6-5. These genes correspond to PRC1-dependent H3K27me3 genes, as they displayed a similar loss of H3K27me3 in both bmi1abc and bmi1abc ref6-5 (Additional File 2: Fig. S8B). Additionally, we observed that most of the genes gaining H3K27me3 in ref6-5 also acquired this modification in bmi1abc ref6-5 (1,640 out of 2,313 genes). However, we also identified a distinct subset of genes that gained H3K27me3 exclusively in bmi1abc ref6-5 (1,658 genes, Fig. 4G and H), which was a priori unexpected. We thus analyzed the levels of H2A/Zub and H3K4me3 in WT and bmi1abc at this subset of genes (Fig. 4H). The levels of these two modifications were higher in WT compared to bmi1abc (Fig. 4H). In addition, they displayed increased expression levels in WT compared to bmi1abc (Fig. 4I), suggesting that H2A/Zub incorporation may be required to promote the expression of these genes. Consistent with their expression levels in WT, these genes were enriched in H3.3 and H2A.Z localized at the TSS region (Fig. 4J). Nevertheless, the reason why these genes gained H3K27me3 exclusively in the bmi1abc ref6-5 mutant remained unclear.

H2A.Zub prevents the incorporation of H3.1K27me1 to activate gene expression

REF6 activity results in the accumulation of H3K27me1 at target genes [25, 27, 47, 48]. Since we found that H3K27me3 marked genes were enriched in canonical H3.1, we investigated if REF6 targets also displayed an enrichment in this histone variant. Indeed, we found an enrichment of H3.1 at REF6 targets when compared to all genes (Additional File 2: Fig. S6C), supporting that REF6 can act on H3.1K27me3 marked genes. Thus, we wondered whether the downregulated expression in bmi1abc of the genes gaining H3K27me3 in bmi1abc ref6-5 was related to the presence of H3.1K27me1. Hence, we determined the levels of H3K27me1 in WT and bmi1abc seedlings (Additional File 2: Fig. S9). We found that the genome-wide distribution of H3K27me1 was notably altered in bmi1abc, with decreased levels of this histone modification at pericentromeric regions and increased levels at chromosome arms (Additional File 2: Fig. S10). Importantly, although H3K27me1 is predominantly associated with constitutive heterochromatin, it can be also found in euchromatin. This mark is deposited by ARABIDOPSIS TRITHORAX-RELATED PROTEIN5 (ATXR5) and ATRX6, specifically on canonical H3.1 during the process of DNA replication. Moreover, it has been proposed that ATRX5/6 mediated H3.1K27me1 and tethering of PRC2 to the replication fork restores H3K27me3 to maintain stable repression [49–51].

We focused our analysis on coding genes, as they are the primary targets of PcG regulation, finding that the number of genes marked with H3K27me1 in bmi1abc was significantly higher than that in WT seedlings (Fig. 5A, Additional File 2: Fig. S11 and Additional File 1: Table S1D). We determined the average expression levels of the genes marked with H3K27me1 in WT and bmi1abc (2,214) or marked only in bmi1abc (5,897, hereafter referred to as hyper-H3K27me1 in bmi1abc) in WT, bmi1abc and clfswn seedlings (Fig. 5B). While the first subset of genes presented low expression levels in all the genotypes, the average expression of the hyper-H3K27me1 genes in bmi1abc was lower than that in WT or clfswn, supporting that H3K27me1 is a repressive mark. Intriguingly, most of the hyper-H3K27me1 genes in bmi1abc showed similar expression levels in bmi1abc and seeds at 48 HAI (Additional File 2: Fig. S12A). Moreover, we found that downregulated genes in bmi1abc were enriched in H3K27me1 compared to WT (Additional File 2: Fig. S12B). Next, we determined the levels of H3K27me1 in WT and bmi1abc seedlings at the genes gaining H3K27me3 only in bmi1abc ref6-5, revealing an enrichment of this modification in bmi1abc (Fig. 5E, Additional File 2: Fig. S14A). In contrast, the genes gaining H3K27me3 in both ref6-5 and bmi1abc ref6-5 displayed high levels of H3K27me1 in WT and bmi1abc (Additional File 2: Fig. S14B). These results support the view that REF6 leaves a signature of H3K27me1 after acting on H3K27me3 and explain why some genes gain H3K27me3 only in bmi1abc ref6-5.

Given that REF6 target genes are enriched in H3.1, it is reasonable to assume that REF6 removes two methyl groups from H3.1K27me3 marked genes, leaving H3.1K27me1. Therefore, since the hyper-H3.1K27me1 genes in bmi1abc are transcribed and enriched for H3.3 in WT seedlings, we propose that H2A.Zub incorporation may be necessary to prevent H3.1K27me1 deposition and promote histone exchange to support transcriptional activation.

To test this hypothesis, we performed a chromatin analysis of genes based on histone variants (H3.1, H3.3, H2A.Z and HTA13) and histone modifications (H2A/Zub, H3K27me3, H3K27me1, H3K4me3) in WT seedlings, selecting six differentiated gene clusters (Fig. 6A, Additional File 3: Table S2). Discussion

Plants undergo several developmental transitions throughout their life cycle, where the precise temporal and spatial regulation of gene expression is essential. This is particularly critical during the transition from seed to seedling, a phase characterized by extensive transcriptional reprogramming. PcG complexes play a key role in orchestrating gene expression during this transition, as evidenced by the inability of PcG mutants to switch from embryonic to vegetative development [6, 19]. However, the mechanisms by which repression is established or removed from specific genes during developmental transitions and maintained in subsequent stages remain largely unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that the interplay between different epigenetic modifications and histone variants plays a critical role in preserving gene expression patterns [43]. For example, H3.1 may facilitate the deposition of H3K27me3 and H3K27me1 during cell division [50, 52]. Additionally, the activation of gene expression in response to environmental cues involves the deposition of H3.3 and the eviction of H2A.Z [53, 54], indicating the contribution of different epigenetic mechanisms in reprogramming gene expression.

Interestingly, our data reveal that PRC1 specifically monoubiquitinates H2A.Z during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development, as HTA10ub is absent in seeds after imbibition or in clfswn, which remains in an embryonic-like stage after germination [6]. Furthermore, our findings indicate that H2A.Zub plays a pivotal role in plant development. This is supported by the observation that the mutation of the lysine residues of H2A.Z targeted for monoubiquitination results in more pronounced developmental defects than mutating those targeted for acetylation. This is in line with recent findings indicating that the N-terminal tail of Arabidopsis H2A.Z is not necessary for normal plant development [55]. However, these results open the question of when, where, and for what purpose canonical HTA10 undergoes monoubiquitination.

In any case, our findings indicate that H2A.Zub is required to switch OFF and ON gene expression during the transition from seed to seedling (Fig. 7). This aligns with the high number of upregulated and downregulated genes observed in the bmi1abc mutant [17, 23]. Consistent with the repressive role of H2A.Zub, a recent study has shown that a mild reduction of ambient temperature (16 °C) causes an increase of H2A.Zub at key embryonic regulators (ABI3, LEC1, CUC1, etc.), which partially restores clfswn developmental defects. Genetic analyses revealed that the rescue is abolished in hta9-1 (H2A.Z) but not in hta10 (canonical H2A) mutants, indicating that PRC1 targets the H2A.Z variant in this context [56]. Together with our data, these findings reinforce the view that PRC1-mediated H2A.Zub is a context-dependent mark whose regulatory outcome, permissive or repressive, depends on the surrounding chromatin landscape and environmental cues.

We found that the incorporation of H2A.Zub at actively expressed seed genes facilitates the recruitment of PRC2, which subsequently trimethylates H3.3, a histone variant preferentially associated with transcription [57–59], leading to gene repression (Fig. 7). Once repression is established, evidence suggests that monoubiquitination may be removed for stable repression, as was proposed for FLC [60]. Moreover, the activity of the UBIQUITIN-SPECIFIC PROTEASES 12 and 13 (UBP12/13) has been shown to be required for stable PRC2-mediated repression [22]. In addition, we found that stably repressed genes are marked with H3.1K27me3 and H2A.Z in their GB, which is consistent with previous observations [31, 33, 43, 57], indicating that at some point H3.3 is exchanged by H3.1. In any case, the combination of trimethylated H3.1 and H2A.Z on nucleosomes is a hallmark of stable repression.

We also identified numerous genes whose H3K27me3 levels remained unaffected in the bmi1abc mutant. These PRC1-independent genes were stably repressed in seeds and seedlings, suggesting the existence of an alternative mechanism for PRC2 recruitment, as previously proposed [1, 20, 61, 62]. GO term enrichment analysis of these genes revealed associations with plant organ development, indicating that their expression must be tightly regulated in time and space. Notably, these genes were also enriched in H3.1 and H2A.Z. Since H3.1 is incorporated into chromatin during DNA replication [63, 64] and ATRX5/6 monomethylates H3.1 at the replication fork [49, 50], H3.1K27me1 may act as a substrate for PRC2, facilitating the maintenance of mitotically stable repression, as previously proposed [64]. Remarkably, H2A.Z appears to be incorporated into chromatin simultaneously with H3.1, which aligns with observations that HTA9 and HTA11 are expressed in a replication-dependent manner [65]. Furthermore, components of the SWR1 complex, which is responsible for H2A.Z incorporation into chromatin [66, 67], are strongly expressed in tissues with a high proliferation rate [68–71]. This suggests that the co-incorporation of H3.1 and H2A.Z during replication may play a key role in establishing and maintaining stable gene repression in proliferative tissues.

On the other hand, the genes whose expression is required for vegetative growth may exist in an inactive but responsive state in imbibed seeds (Fig. 7). Our data indicate that these genes are associated with H2A.Z and marked by H3.1K27me1 due to the activity of REF6, which removes two methyl groups from H3.1K27me3. This activity may enable the epigenetic reprogramming of genes stably repressed by H3.1K27me3. In support of this, recent studies have shown that REF6-mediated H3K27 demethylation is necessary to establish an H3K27me3-depleted state, facilitating gene activation during germination [36]. During the transition to vegetative development, the incorporation of H2A.Zub may counteract the deposition of H3.1K27me1. This is consistent with the observation that in WT and clfswn, in which H2A.Zub incorporation is not compromised, the subset of genes hyper-H3K27me1 in bmi1abc are depleted of this modification. Interestingly, most of the hyper-H3K27me1 genes in bmi1abc show similar expression levels in imbibed seeds and bmi1abc, opening the possibility that the H3K27me1 distribution in this mutant resembles that in imbibed seeds.

Our working hypothesis is that the incorporation of H2A.Zub may impede or inhibit H3.1 monomethylation and/or promote the replacement of H3.1 with H3.3, thereby facilitating the incorporation of activation marks such as H3K4me3 to switch ON transcription. This hypothesis is supported by the observed negative correlation between H3K27me1 and H2A/Zub levels in WT seedlings, which seems to be accompanied by the exchange of H3.1 with H3.3. Our data also suggest that the removal of H2A/Zub is necessary for stable gene activation, likely to prevent PRC2 recruitment and subsequent gene re-silencing. In support of this, recent findings have demonstrated that the activity of UBIQUITIN SPECIFIC PROTEASE 5 (UBP5) is essential for preventing the deposition of H3K27me3 by PRC2 [72] and for activating gene expression [73]. After the initial switch, maintaining a stable activation of gene expression may require the participation of histone remodelling complexes to establish the histone combination on nucleosomes characteristic of transcriptionally active genes. Accordingly, various studies suggest that transcriptional activation in response to different cues is facilitated by the coordinated eviction of H2A.Z, which is mediated by the chromatin remodelling INOSITOL REQUIRING 80 (INO80) complex, and the deposition of H3.3 by the chaperone HISTONE REGULATORY HOMOLOG A (HIRA) complex [53, 54, 74, 75].

Conclusions

In summary, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized role of PRC1 in activating gene expression. We demonstrate that PRC1-mediated H2A.Zub, acting as a “switcher”, plays a pivotal role in reprogramming active and repressed genes during the transition from embryonic to vegetative development, with potential implications for other developmental processes. Notably, the removal of H3.1K27me3 marks is a prerequisite for H2A.Zub to orchestrate gene activation, highlighting the functional relationship between REF6 and PRC1 activities. Furthermore, our results provide new insights into the relationship between histone modifications and histone variants in gene expression regulation and emphasise the importance of histone exchange to preserve gene expression patterns.

Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 wild type (WT), hta9/11 [40], bmi1abc [6], clfswn (clf28swn7) [18], ref6-5 [27] mutants and transgenic plants generated in this study were surface sterilized in 10% (v/v) commercial bleach for 20 min and washed four times with sterile double-distilled water. Sterilized seeds were cold-stratified in the dark at 4 °C for 2–4 days and then grown under long-day conditions (16 h light and 8 h dark) at 21 °C on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates containing 1.5% sucrose and 0.8% agar for different days, depending on the experiment. MS agar plates were supplemented with hygromycin (20 µg ml−1) for transgenic plant selection.

Transgenic plants

Arabidopsis wild-type (NN) or mutated HTA9 cDNAs, in which C-terminal K129 and K132 (NR), the N-terminal K3, K6, and K12 (RN), or both N- and C-terminal K residues (RR) were replaced by arginine (R), were introduced into a modified pCAMBIA 1302 vector (pCAMBIA 130 N) [76] under the control of the CaMV35s promoter. For the incorporation of N- and C-terminal substitutions, we designed specific primers, which are listed in Additional File 2: Fig. S15. The constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101, and subsequently, transformed into hta9/11 mutant plants by the floral dip method [77].

Nuclei and Histone extraction

Nuclei were extracted from 1 g at 2, 7, or 10 DAG using two consecutive extraction buffers. EB1 (0.4 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris HCl pH 8, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM Beta-mercaptoethanol, 0.1 mM PMSF, and 1 × complete protease Inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) was added to ground material, then filtered with Miracloth (Millipore 475,855-1R) and pelleted by centrifugation. EB2 (0.25 M sucrose 10 mM Tris HCl pH 8, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM Beta-mercaptoethanol, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM PMSF, and 1 × complete protease Inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) was used to wash the nuclei pellet 3 times. Samples were resuspended in Laemmli buffer and boiled for 10 min to be analyzed by Western blot (WB).

For histone-enriched extract, nuclei from 1 g of 7-day-old seedlings were obtained as described and treated overnight with 0.4 N H2SO4. Proteins were precipitated with 33% trichloroacetic acid and then washed 3 times with acetone, air-dried, and re-suspended in urea 3 M. Laemmli buffer was added to the samples and boiled for 10 min to be analyzed by western blot.

Western blot

Protein extractions were separated on 13.75% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Immobilon-P Transfer membrane, Millipore) by semi-dry blotting in 25 mM Tris HCl, 192 mM glycine, and 15% methanol. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-HTA9 (1:2000, generated for this study against the HTA9 peptide SLINKSAKE), anti-HTA10K121ub (1:2000, generated against a branched C-terminal peptide [6]), anti-HsH2Aub (1:1000, CST 8240S) anti-H3 (1:3000, Abcam ab1791) and anti-H2A.Zac (1:2000, Diagenode C15410202), and as secondary antibody, the horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:10,000, Sigma-Aldrich, A0545) was used. Chemiluminescence detection was performed with ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

ChIP and ChIP-sequencing

ChIP experiments were performed on 1 g of 10-day-old seedling tissue and 5 g of 48-h imbibed seeds fixed in 1% formaldehyde. Chromatin was extracted from the fixed tissue and fragmented using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode). The sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight using the following antibodies and dilutions: anti-H3K27me3 (1:300, Millipore 07–449), anti-HsH2Aub (1:100, CST 8240S), anti-H3K27me1 (1:150, Millipore 07–448) or anti-HTA9 (1:300) antibodies. Immunocomplexes were captured using Protein-A Sepharose beads CL-4B (Merck). After washing the Protein-A beads, chromatin was eluted, and the cross-linking was reversed overnight at 65 °C. The DNA from the immunoprecipitated chromatin was treated with RNase and proteinase K and purified using the MicroChIP DiaPure columns kit (Diagenode C03040001). Libraries for ChIP-seq samples were prepared with TruSeq ChIP Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) and sequenced using Illumina NextSeq with single-end 75 bp reads by Cabimer Genomic Unit (https://www.cabimer.es/).

Quality control and read mapping

Reads were quality-controlled using FASTQC software [78] (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Reads were mapped onto the TAIR10 reference genome using Bowtie [79] with mismatch permission of 2 bp. Bowtie output SAM format was converted to BAM, sorted, and indexed with the software package SAMtools [80]. BAM files were used to generate bigWig coverage track files of 10 bp size bins with the deepTools utility bamCoverage [81] and normalized by Counts Per Million mapped reads (CPM) adding the -normalizeUsing CPM parameter.

Peak calling and annotation

MACS2 software [82] was used to identify significantly read-enriched regions, using one input library for each genotype and condition. The chosen parameters were: fold change between 5 and 50, adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05, genome size 1.1e8 (Arabidopsis genome size), and –nomodel. The identification of genes associated with peaks was performed with Bioconductor package ChIPseeker [83] according to the Nearest Downstream Gene (NDG) criterion. Specifically, a peak was associated with a gene when it overlapped any of the gene regions or when it was located at most 500 bp upstream of its TSS. A gene was assumed to be marked when at least one peak was found to be associated with it. However, we observed that both H3K27me3 and H3K27me1 peaks were sometimes wide enough to cover several genes, resulting in only one gene annotated for these long peaks. To solve this, we selected peaks longer than 2 kb and annotated all the genes inside. Pie charts showing the distribution of annotated peaks for H3K27me1 marks in WT and bmi1abc were also generated with the ChIPseeker package. Each replicate was analyzed separately, and the final set of marked genes was determined as those detected in both replicates. A transposon gene list obtained from TAIR was subtracted from each set of common genes between replicates. ChIP-seq datasets used from other studies were re-analyzed following the same strategy indicated above.

Peak visualization

BigWig files generated by bamCoverage were used for peak profile visualization in the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV) [84]. Also, bigWig files were compared and summarized with multiBigwigSummary utility from deepTools [85] to fraction the genome in 1 kb regions and represent the replicates using scatter plots. These quality graphs showed high similarity and reproducibility between replicates. Metagene plots representing the coverage of marks over genes were generated with a custom R script based on the R Bioconductor package ChIPeakAnno [97] and Arabidopsis thaliana genome database R package, TxDb.Athaliana.Biomart.plantsmart28 [86]. Genome plots for comparing peak enrichment between genotypes were performed using LIMMA [87] package for statistical enrichment between samples, creating a count matrix from multiBigwigSummary as input, and a custom R script for drawing the plot. Heterochromatin regions represented in the genome plot were previously defined [27, 88].

Clustering approaches

A matrix containing each histone variant or modification quantification was created and normalized by the read counts across the gene body and flanking regions for all annotated genes. The gene body was normalized to the median gene length, and upstream and downstream regions were extended by 3 kb from the TSS and TES, respectively. For the generation of clusters of genes based on their histone variants and modifications, we removed the distal upstream and downstream regions (leaving 300 bp from the TSS and TES) to focus on the gene body and nearby regulatory regions. We then explored different clustering approaches. Initially, k-means clustering was performed directly on the combined matrix without any scaling. However, this led to biased clusters due to the vastly different numerical ranges between datasets (e.g., ChIP-seq read counts for histone variants vs. ChIP-seq read counts for histone modifications). To address this issue, we applied Z-score normalization to each matrix individually and then combined them. For each dataset, we subtracted the mean and divided it by the standard deviation, ensuring that the data were centered and scaled to comparable ranges. This allowed us to perform clustering on a dataset where no single histone mark dominated due to numerical scale differences. In addition to direct k-means clustering, we used PCA to further refine the clustering by reducing the dimensionality of the data. Each scaled matrix was combined, and PCA was performed to identify the principal components (PCs) that captured most of the variance across datasets. The top 10 principal components were selected, as they explained a substantial proportion of the variance. K-means clustering was then performed on these PCs, allowing for a more refined partitioning of genes into clusters based on the most informative patterns across all marks, while reducing the impact of noise or irrelevant variance. Heatmaps to visualize the clustering results, including the 3 kb upstream and downstream from each gene, were generated using the Bioconductor R package EnrichedHeatmap [89]. Rows in the heatmaps were split according to the k-means clustering results.

Transcriptomic analysis

Data previously generated from WT and bmi1abc at 7 DAG (GEO number GSE89358 [20]), clfswn 7 DAG (GEO number GSE155378 [23]), WT at 0HAI and WT at 48HAI (GEO number GSE167502 [36]) were re-analyzed together for this work. Raw data in fastq files from GEO were re-mapped using the TAIR10 reference genome and transcript assembly was performed with the software tools HISAT2 and StringTie [90]. DEGs were selected using the Bioconductor R packages Ballgown [90] and LIMMA [87]. Since datasets come from different sequencing backgrounds, upper quantile normalization was performed. Gene expression was measured in FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon and million mapped reads) and summarized in a matrix containing all Arabidopsis nuclear genes in rows and FPKM values for each sample by columns. DEGs were selected according to a log2-fold change cut-off >|1| in the different comparisons and a p-value < 0.05 according to a moderated t-test, obtaining activated, repressed, and unaltered genes. GO enrichment analyses were done using the bioconductor R package clusterProfiler [91], setting a q-value and p-value cutoff > 0.05. DEGs from significant GO terms were selected to generate a heatmap with Bioconductor R package ComplexHeatmap [92] using as input a mean matrix with average log2 expression in FPKM values for each genotype. Treemapplots representing GO terms enrichment were generated with bioconductor R package rrvgo [93], in which the parameter score (-log10 q-value) was added to construct the graph.
