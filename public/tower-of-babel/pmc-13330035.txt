Hybrid fixation with a double-row suture and low-profile plate for comminuted or avulsed greater tuberosity fractures
Authors: Zhennan Chen, Yabiao He, Qiaomin Qiu, Songtu Zhang, Qiang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated greater tuberosity (GT) fractures account for approximately 20% of proximal humeral fractures and are frequently associated with glenohumeral dislocation and rotator cuff injuries. Displacement exceeding 5 mm is recommended for surgery to prevent subacromial impingement and functional impairment. Current fixation methods, such as cannulated screws, suture anchors, and locking plates, each have limitations, including inadequate stability for comminuted fractures, risk of impingement, and suboptimal anatomical adaptation. This underscores the necessity of a hybrid technique that integrates the benefits of both soft tissue and rigid fixation.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 12 patients with comminuted split-type or avulsion-type greater tuberosity fractures treated with a hybrid surgical technique from January 2023 to June 2025. A deltopectoral approach was utilized to achieve fracture reduction, which was temporarily stabilized via K-wires. Two anchors were placed at the greater tuberosity, and a double-row suture technique was employed, with the sutures secured through an external row anchor arranged in a parachute configuration. A low-profile anatomic locking plate was subsequently applied to provide buttress support, resulting in a composite construct referred to as a “suture plate.” The outcomes measured included operative time, blood loss, Constant-Murley and ASES scores, VAS pain scores, and radiographic healing status.ResultsAll patients were followed up for a mean duration of 11.3 ± 5.0 months. The mean operative time was 82.5 ± 15.8 min, and the mean blood loss volume was 170.8 ± 52.6 ml. All fractures achieved clinical union at a mean of 11.0 ± 1.5 weeks, with no instances of anchor pullout, suture cutout, or implant failure. The Constant-Murley scores improved from 30.5 ± 4.8 preoperatively to 90.3 ± 3.7, the ASES scores improved from 32.1 ± 5.8 to 89.4 ± 3.6, and the VAS scores decreased from 6.8 ± 2.1 to 1.0 ± 0.6. Excellent or good outcomes were observed in 91.7% (11/12) of the patients.ConclusionThe combination of the double-row parachute suture technique and a low-profile anatomic locking plate provides a hybrid fixation system that integrates flexible and rigid stabilization. This construct can provide reliable fixation, supporting early rehabilitation, and yielding satisfactory short-term functional outcomes for comminuted split-type or avulsion-type greater tuberosity fractures, representing a potentially feasible alternative to conventional methods, although further high-level studies are required to confirm its long-term efficacy and safety.

Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures account for approximately 5% of all fractures, with approximately 20% involving the greater tuberosity (GT) [1]. These fractures are frequently associated with rotator cuff injuries because of the critical role of the GT as the primary attachment site for the rotator cuff tendons [2, 3]. Displacement of the GT fragment, often resulting from muscular traction, can significantly impair shoulder function. Surgical intervention is generally recommended for fractures with displacement exceeding 5 mm to restore anatomical alignment and prevent complications such as subacromial impingement and functional impairment [4–6].

Various surgical techniques are currently employed in orthopedic procedures, including the use of cannulated screws, suture anchors, and locking plates [7]. Although cannulated screws are straightforward to apply, they have limited holding power and may worsen fragment comminution [8]. Suture anchors effectively address concomitant rotator cuff tears; however, they often lack sufficient rigidity to stabilize comminuted fractures, which can delay early rehabilitation [9]. Conventional locking plates provide stable fixation but are frequently bulky, increasing the risk of soft tissue irritation and subacromial impingement [10]. Although arthroscopic double-row suture bridge techniques have demonstrated promising outcomes, they require a steep learning curve and remain technically challenging in the context of severely comminuted fractures [11, 12].

To address the limitations of traditional fixation methods, we developed a composite fixation system that integrates a double-row “parachute” suture bridge configuration with a low-profile anatomic locking plate. This technique, performed via a mini-open approach, facilitates anatomic reduction and provides rigid support for fracture fragments while enhancing overall stability through a tension band effect created by the sutures. Furthermore, this approach allows for the simultaneous repair of associated rotator cuff injury.

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of a novel composite fixation technique for treating comminuted split-type and avulsion-type GT fractures. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 12 patients, focusing on early fracture union, functional recovery, and complication rates to assess the technique’s feasibility and effectiveness.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient cohort

This retrospective case series enrolled 12 consecutive patients with isolated, displaced, and comminuted greater tuberosity (GT) fractures. All patients underwent surgical management between January 2023 and June 2025. The intervention for each patient involved open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) via a combined construct of double-row suture anchor fixation, which was reinforced with an anatomic locking plate. All operative procedures were performed by a single, senior orthopedic surgeon (H.-Y.B.).

Preoperative radiographic evaluation included standard anteroposterior and lateral shoulder radiographs supplemented by computed tomography (CT) scans with three-dimensional reconstructions. Fracture characteristics, such as the pattern of comminution and the degree of superior or posterior displacement, were systematically assessed via coronal and axial CT imaging planes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were aged ≥ 50 years and exhibited one of two specific fracture patterns: (1) split-type greater tuberosity (GT) fractures involving two or more major fragments or (2) avulsed GT fragments with documented displacement of ≥ 5 mm in either the superior or posterior direction, regardless of concomitant anterior shoulder dislocation. The key exclusion criteria included a history of any pathology or prior surgical intervention that could compromise shoulder function (e.g., rotator cuff tear, previous arthroscopy), the presence of an associated bony Bankart lesion, GT fractures occurring in the context of posterior shoulder dislocation, and any active systemic or local infection.

All 12 patients (three men and nine women) completed the study. The mechanisms of injury included sports injuries in two patients, fall-related injuries in two patients, and road traffic accidents in eight patients. Five patients (41.7%) had greater tuberosity (GT) fractures associated with anterior glenohumeral dislocation. None of these patients with dislocation presented with preoperative neurological deficits. Concomitant injuries at other sites were observed in three patients, including ipsilateral rib fractures (4th to 7th ribs) in two patients and ipsilateral meniscal injury in one patient.

Implant

The anatomical locking plate (Double Medical Technology Inc., Xiamen, China) features a low-profile design with a thickness of approximately 1 mm and consists of contoured proximal and distal components (Fig. 1A). It is available in specific right and left configurations to accommodate the anatomical laterality. The proximal section included two 2.7-mm locking holes and one binding hole, which could accept either 2.7-mm locking screws or 3.5-mm cancellous screws. This section is intentionally inclined to match the anatomical contour of the rotator cuff tendon insertion (Fig. 1A). The distal part of the plate contained one 3.5-mm locking hole and one binding hole. Additionally, the implant features a dual-plane arc design oriented in both the transverse and coronal planes, allowing it to conform to the physiological morphology of the greater tuberosity and provide a stabilizing buttress.

Surgical technique

Under brachial plexus anesthesia, the patient was placed in the beach chair position. A standard deltopectoral approach was used. The greater tuberosity fragment was exposed, and the fracture site was meticulously cleared of the periosteum and hematoma. The core “suture plate” construct was subsequently established in the following sequence.

Suture-bridge fixation: The displaced greater tuberosity fragment was reflected to expose the undersurface. Two suture anchors were positioned at the humeral head–neck junction. The sutures were passed through the rotator cuff tendons in a mattress configuration. Following anatomic reduction, provisional fixation was secured using 1.5-mm Kirschner wires. The two suture anchors were subsequently tied (Fig. 1B). Employing a “parachute” technique, an additional knotless anchor was placed laterally to the greater tuberosity as an external row, thereby creating a double-row suture-bridge construct (Fig. 1C).

Plate augmentation fixation

After satisfactory fluoroscopic confirmation of correct placement, a mini locking plate was positioned laterally over the greater tuberosity, approximately 3–5 mm anterior to its superior aspect. One or two 3.5-mm cancellous bone screws and three or four locking screws were inserted to provide a rigid bony support. This fixation method resulted in a composite “suture-plate system” (Fig. 1D). Finally, multiangle fluoroscopic imaging was performed, followed by a range-of-motion assessment of the shoulder to rule out subacromial impingement and confirm the stability of the greater tuberosity fracture fixation, as well as the adequacy of the screw lengths (Fig. 2).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

A standardized phase-based rehabilitation protocol was implemented. During the initial four postoperative weeks, the affected arm was immobilized via an abduction pillow. During this period, the patients performed active range-of-motion (ROM) exercises for the elbow, wrist, and fingers. Gentle passive pendulum exercises for the shoulder were initiated immediately, followed by formal passive shoulder mobilization within a pain-free range, starting in the first postoperative week.

Active-assisted and active shoulder ROM exercises were commenced at six weeks post-surgery. Progression to stretching and resistance strengthening exercises was permitted only after the 12-week postoperative mark.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Patients were evaluated clinically and radiographically at scheduled intervals: 4, 8, 10, and 12 weeks, and 6 months postoperatively, with follow-up assessments occurring every six months thereafter. Fracture union was assessed using standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the shoulder.

At each follow-up visit, an independent blinded observer recorded the following outcome measures: shoulder function assessed using the Constant–Murley and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores, active shoulder range of motion (ROM), pain intensity measured on a visual analog scale (VAS), and any complications. Standardized radiographic measurements were also performed. In this study, loss of reduction was defined as fragment displacement greater than 3 mm compared with the initial postoperative radiographs, whereas malunion was defined as displacement greater than 3 mm on any radiographic view at the time of healing compared with the initial postoperative reduction.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Postoperative clinical outcomes, including the Constant–Murley score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, visual analog scale (VAS) score, and active range of motion (ROM), as well as demographic and operative data, are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For proportional data, we applied the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the sample size and distribution. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 for all comparisons.

Results

All patients were followed up for a mean duration of 11.3 ± 5.0 months (range, 6–22 months), with a mean age of 62.3 ± 6.6 years (range, 52–74 years). The mean operative time was 82.5 ± 15.8 min (range: 60–110 min), and the average intraoperative blood loss was 170.8 ± 52.6 ml (range, 100–250 ml). The mean interval from the initial trauma to surgery was 3.3 ± 0.7 days (range: 2–4 days). The fracture fragments presented mean superior and posterior displacements of 6.7 ± 1.4 mm (range, 5–9 mm) and 6.8 ± 1.3 mm (range, 5–9 mm), respectively. Concomitant rotator cuff tears were identified in 10 patients (83.3%) (Table 1). At the final follow-up, all primary incisions healed without postoperative infection. Radiographic union was achieved in all patients at a mean of 11.0 ± 1.5 weeks (Table 2). No instances of implant failure, including anchor pullout, suture cut-through, screw/plate loosening, or breakage, were observed.

Discussion

In this retrospective case series of 12 patients, our results suggest that hybrid fixation—combining a double-row suture bridge with a low-profile anatomic plate—is a feasible and effective technique for managing comminuted or displaced greater tuberosity fractures. This construct provides both flexible and rigid stabilization, which supported early postoperative rehabilitation and yielded satisfactory functional recovery in our cohort. At a mean follow-up of 11.3 ± 5.0 months, all fractures achieved clinical union without implant failure or loss of reduction. Significant functional improvements were observed, as reflected by the marked increases in both Constant-Murley and ASES scores. Overall, 11 of 12 patients (91.7%) achieved an excellent-to-good clinical outcome. Only one patient (8.3%) had a ‘fair’ result, characterized by occasional activity-related pain during heavy lifting despite regaining a functional range of motion (ROM). This suboptimal outcome, observed in a split-type fracture case, may be attributed to the initial severity of comminution. Such complex fracture morphology can lead to subtle alterations in subacromial space volume or persistent tendon-to-bone irritability under high-demand loading conditions.

While these preliminary results are encouraging, they should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and the absence of a control group.

Surgical management of displaced greater tuberosity fractures is indicated to prevent the dual sequelae of bony impingement and rotator cuff dysfunction. While minimal displacement may be treated non-operatively, displacement > 5 mm is a well-established indication for fixation [13], with some advocating for a threshold as low as 3 mm in high-demand individuals [14].

These fractures often indicate rotator cuff avulsion injuries. In our series, the incidence of concomitant cuff pathology was higher (83.3%) than that reported in the literature [15], likely reflecting an older cohort with predisposing tendon degeneration.

Malunion presents a significant biomechanical challenge [16]. Superior displacement compromises the subacromial space, leading to impingement and limiting the range of motion. More critically, it places the rotator cuff under non-physiological tension, weakens abduction and external rotation strength, and increases the risk of irreversible muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration. Additionally, the displaced fragment can abrade the underlying supraspinatus tendon, potentially causing secondary tears of the tendon. Therefore, surgical goals must include both anatomical bony reconstruction and concurrent rotator cuff repair to restore normal glenohumeral mechanics and optimize functional outcomes.

Currently, there is no consensus regarding the optimal fixation method for isolated greater tuberosity (GT) fractures. Current techniques include percutaneous cannulated screws, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using locking plates or suture/anchor constructs, and all-arthroscopic anchor-based repairs [6]. The primary surgical challenges arise from the often thin, fragile, and comminuted nature of the fracture fragments, which are frequently compounded by osteoporosis in older adult patients. Isolated screw fixation provides limited purchase in comminuted fractures, carrying the risk of secondary displacement and subacromial impingement [16]. Although arthroscopic techniques are minimally invasive, they entail a steep learning curve and can complicate the precise anatomical reduction of comminuted fragments [18].

Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) allows for direct anatomical reduction and has yielded satisfactory clinical outcomes [4]. However, traditional proximal humeral locking plates are bulky, posing a significant risk of postoperative impingement and requiring extensive surgical exposure [19]. In recent years, the application of various low-profile mini plates has shown promising results. Chen et al. reported 19 patients with displaced greater tuberosity (GT) fractures treated with an AO midfoot locking plate via ORIF and reported uneventful union in all patients, with an average Constant–Murley score of 90.6 and a 94.7% excellent-to-good rate [20]. Similarly, Popp et al. reviewed 10 patients treated with a small Bamberg plate and reported a Constant–Murley score of 94.2 at the 6-month follow-up, with no complications or secondary loss of reduction [21]. Bogdan et al. achieved union in all 10 patients treated with a mesh plate, with final average DASH scores of 28.2 (overall) and 13.6 (work module) [22]. These mini-plates are shorter, thinner, and less invasive than traditional plates, with the potential to mitigate hardware-related impingement, leading to generally satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes.

Although these plates are less invasive and potentially lower-profile [20–22], they are not specifically designed for GT. They often necessitate intraoperative contouring and typically lack integrated suture holes, which are essential for the reliable repair of concomitant rotator cuff avulsions. This represents a significant design flaw, particularly given the high reported incidence of articular-sided partial rotator cuff tears associated with these fractures [23]. Consequently, this limitation compromises the overall reliability of the fixation systems. In contrast, the use of a low-profile, anatomically contoured locking plate provides distinct advantages in managing comminuted and displaced isolated GT fractures [24–25].

The low-profile plate used in this study features a precontoured anatomical design that conforms to the greater tuberosity, eliminating the need for intraoperative bending and minimizing soft tissue dissection to preserve fragment vascularity. Its beveled edge creates a smooth contour for the rotator cuff footprint, thereby reducing the risk of secondary impingements. Although suture holes in plates are sometimes employed to augment rotator cuff repair, we have observed that this method alone may be inadequate for achieving reliable tendon fixation in comminuted fractures, particularly in osteoporotic bones, and can result in clinical failure. In our series of 12 patients, classified according to Mutch et al. [26], four had avulsion-type fractures, and eight had split-type fractures, with the latter group comprising at least two fragments.

The double-row suture bridge technique is a well-established method for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair [27]. Compared with traditional methods, this technique provides significant biomechanical advantages for tendon-to-bone healing [28]. Building on this foundation, our approach employs initial medial-row anchor fixation to secure the rotator cuff tissue close to the humeral head, thereby reducing the tension. A single lateral-row anchor utilizes a “parachute” configuration to distribute suture coverage over the fracture fragment, effectively dispersing shear and torsional forces to achieve optimal fixation. Lin et al. [29] reported that suture bridge fixation significantly outperforms cannulated screw fixation in resisting traction forces exerted by the supraspinatus on the greater tuberosity (GT) fragment in a biomechanical model, achieving 100% footprint coverage.

Although double-row anchor fixation is effective, patients with osteoporosis are particularly susceptible to suture anchor pullout. In our study, the mean age of the participants was 62.3 ± 6.6 years, and osteoporosis was highly prevalent. Notably, no cases of suture anchor pullout were observed, which can be attributed to the precise placement of the anchors during surgery combined with fixation via a locking plate. Consequently, the adjunctive low-profile locking plate enhances stability by providing a dual “suture-and-plate” effect, ensuring secure fixation of the fracture fragments. Follow-up assessments demonstrated well-maintained reductions and rigid fixations.

Although anatomic plate suture holes effectively repair the rotator cuff in displaced comminuted greater tuberosity fractures, the reported cohorts were young [15], and failure risk persists in osteoporotic patients. The present study proposes a composite strategy that integrates a dedicated low‑profile anatomic plate with double‑row anchor fixation. This design is not a simple combination of techniques but rather leverages the complementary advantages of the two components: the plate provides stable, contoured support for comminuted fragments, while its low‑profile design minimizes the risk of impingement. Independent medial-row anchor fixation serves as a reliable point for repairing concomitant cuff-avulsions. Compared with sutures passed solely through plate holes, this configuration distributes suture tension more evenly, theoretically reducing the risk of stress concentration, which can lead to suture cut-through and fragment redisplacement. Furthermore, in the context of China’s volume-based procurement policy, the costs of the relevant anchors and plates have decreased significantly (with a single suture anchor and a low-profile plate each costing approximately USD 250), thereby increasing the cost-effectiveness of this technique.

The incorporation of double-row anchor fixation did not prolong the operative time. The mean duration of the procedure was 82.5 ± 15.8 min, which is consistent with previously published data for similar interventions [30]. As anticipated with a deltopectoral approach, intraoperative blood loss (170.8 ± 52.6 ml) was modestly greater than that typically reported for arthroscopic techniques [31]. Notably, the patients experienced no postoperative dizziness and did not require blood transfusions.

Study limitations

This investigation has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design from a single center introduced inherent selection and information biases. Second, the sample size was small (n = 12), which limits the statistical power to detect rare complications or generalize the findings to a broader population. Third, the study lacked a randomized comparative control group utilizing alternative fixation techniques, such as isolated plate fixation or total arthroscopic methods, making it difficult to definitively establish the superiority of this hybrid approach. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively short; long-term assessments are needed to monitor potential late-stage complications such as hardware irritation or avascular necrosis. Therefore, large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the clinical efficacy and complication profile of this surgical technique.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the combination of double-row suture anchor fixation and a low-profile anatomic locking plate can provide reliable stability and satisfactory short-term radiographic and functional outcomes for comminuted isolated greater tuberosity fractures. This hybrid technique addresses the challenges of comminuted fragments by providing secure fixation, presenting a feasible and alternative option for the clinical management of these injuries.
