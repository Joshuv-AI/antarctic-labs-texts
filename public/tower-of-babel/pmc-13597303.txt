m6A mRNA methylation regulates pancreatic α-cell plasticity
Authors: Dario F. De Jesus, Natalie K. Brown, Garrett Fogarty, Guilherme Gabriel, Shirong Wang, Shen Wang, Lenee Shrestha, Kristin Kendall, Lily Young, Jiang Hu, Jermaine Austin, Joan Sabadell-Basallote, Sevim Kahraman, Ling Xiao, Rohit N. Kulkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic α-cells are central regulators of glucose and amino acid homeostasis, yet the mechanisms that preserve α-cell identity and function remain incompletely understood. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a widespread mRNA modification that is essential for β-cell biology and pancreatic endocrine differentiation. Here we show that m6A is a key regulator of α-cell function and plasticity. In α-cells, metabolic cues that stimulate glucagon secretion such as L-arginine increase METTL3, METTL14 and m6A levels. Loss of m6A impairs amino acid-stimulated glucagon secretion, disrupts α-cell identity programmes and induces metabolic rewiring. In mice, α-cell-specific Mettl14 deletion reduces α-cell mass, increases β-cell mass and promotes α-to-β-cell conversion, accompanied by the emergence of late β-like states with features of incomplete maturation. Mechanistically, m6A-eCLIP identifies Yy1 as a direct m6A-sensitive target, and elevated YY1 links m6A loss to signalling rewiring and erosion of α-cell identity. These findings identify m6A as a central regulator of α-cell state and reveal an epitranscriptomic mechanism controlling endocrine cell plasticity.

Main

Pancreatic endocrine cells secrete hormones that are essential for systemic glucose homeostasis. This regulation depends on coordinated communication among distinct endocrine cell types. Although islet biology has traditionally focused on the loss or dysfunction of insulin-producing β-cells, glucagon-producing α-cells are also critical for metabolic control1,2. Beyond their classical role in counter-regulating hypoglycemia, α-cells are central components of the liver–α-cell axis, which couples glucagon secretion to systemic amino acid metabolism3,4. Disruption of this axis alters circulating amino acid levels, α-cell growth, and glucagon output, with important consequences for whole-body metabolic homeostasis. In parallel, diabetes has been shown to alter α-cell function and identity5.

Pancreatic α-cell identity is established and maintained by a complex transcriptional network that includes factors such as PAX66, MAFB7,8, FOXA29 and RFX610, which together sustain α-cell identity and its respective glucagon production. Nevertheless, α-cells retain remarkable plasticity. Under conditions of severe β-cell loss or targeted manipulation of transcription factors involved in lineage determination, α-cells can convert into insulin-expressing β-cells, highlighting their potential as an endogenous source for β-cell replacement11–13. Studies in mouse models have provided compelling proof of principle for α-to-β cell conversion, and epigenomic analyses suggest that α-cells maintain a chromatin landscape permissive for reprogramming5,14. However, the molecular mechanisms that preserve α-cell identity under physiological conditions and whose disruption may unlock this plasticity, remain poorly defined.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most abundant internal modification in mammalian mRNA and regulates transcript fate through coordinated control of splicing, nuclear export, stability and translation15. m6A deposition is mediated by a multicomponent writer complex centred on METTL3 and METTL14, together with adaptor proteins such as WTAP, VIRMA, RBM15/15B and ZC3H13, which help determine mRNA substrate selection and subcellular localization15,16. In turn, m6A is interpreted by reader proteins, including members of the YTH domain family and IGF2BP proteins, which influence mRNA decay, translation and localization17,18, while the erasers FTO and ALKBH5 can remove this mark19,20. Through this dynamic network of writers, readers and erasers, m6A provides a post-transcriptional layer of gene regulation that fine-tunes virtually all mammalian cellular processes.

Emerging work has identified m6A as an important regulator of diverse metabolic tissues and especially pancreatic biology21–24. In β-cells, m6A is required for functional integrity, and its levels are decreased in islets from donors with type 2 diabetes21. We reported previously that loss of m6A writers METTL3 and METTL14 in β-cells leads to m6A hypomethylation, impaired insulin secretion, decreased β-cell identity genes and a marked decrease in β-cell proliferation, supporting a central role for this pathway in maintaining β-cell functional mass21. In addition, we have shown that METTL14-mediated m6A regulates early human pancreatic lineage specification. Silencing METTL14 between stages S3 and S4 of human islet differentiation reduces the expression of β-cell identity genes such as INS and PDX1, as well as α-cell identity genes including MAFB and GCG at the end of differentiation23. These findings indicate that m6A contributes not only to mature endocrine cell function but also to endocrine cell formation.

Here, we identify METTL14-dependent m6A methylation as a key regulator of pancreatic α-cell function and identity. We show that metabolic cues that stimulate glucagon secretion such as L-arginine increase METTL3 and METTL14 abundance and m6A levels, whereas inhibitory cues such as insulin suppress them. Using αTC1-6 cells, α-cell-specific Mettl14 knockout (KO), lineage-tracing mouse models, single-nucleus transcriptomic approaches, m6A-eCLIP, mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq), phosphoproteomics and correlation analysis leveraging human α-cell single-cell RNA-seq data, we find that loss of m6A impairs amino acid-stimulated glucagon secretion, disrupts α-cell identity programmes and promotes α-to-β-cell transdifferentiation.

Mechanistically, m6A-eCLIP and functional analyses identified YY1 (Yin Yang 1), a multifunctional zinc-finger transcription factor that coordinates chromatin remodelling and transcriptional regulation25,26, as a direct m6A-sensitive target in α-cells. Recent work has demonstrated that YY1 regulates β-cell function and survival27,28; however, its role in α-cells remains unexplored. Here, we show that m6A deposition on Yy1 transcripts limits mRNA stability, whereas Mettl14 silencing increases YY1 abundance, consistent with enhanced transcript stability upon Ythdf2 loss. Elevated YY1 disrupts α-cell identity, as evidenced by reduced PAX6 and MAFB, and activates signalling pathways involving IRS2, mTOR and MAPK/ERK. Together, these findings identify YY1 as a key downstream effector of m6A loss that drives signalling rewiring and promotes α-cell plasticity.

Results

METTL14 is critical for amino acid-induced glucagon secretion in α-cells

To determine the expression levels of various m6A modulators in human pancreatic cells, we reanalysed the PANC-DB scRNA-seq dataset29,30, which contains data from over 2 × 105 human pancreatic cells (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Focusing on non-diabetic samples, we plotted expression levels of m6A modulators across different pancreatic cell types (Extended Data Fig. 1b). Interestingly, among the endocrine cells, human α-cells exhibited among the highest expression levels of m6A editors and reader proteins, particularly when compared with insulin-producing β-cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c).

To investigate whether m6A modulates transcriptional variability, we analysed transcriptional noise (transcriptional variability) in human α- and β-cells using the PANC-DB scRNA-seq data and categorized genes as m6A-tagged or non-tagged (we defined m6A-tagged genes as having a significant peak that was enriched in IP over input at twofold or more with a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05) based on our m6A-seq data from non-diabetic human islets21,22. We found that m6A-tagged genes exhibited lower transcriptional noise in both human α- and β-cells compared with non-m6A-tagged genes (Extended Data Fig. 1d). These findings suggest that m6A plays a role in fine-tuning transcription in α-cells and may regulate α-cell biology, a feature shared by β-cells and reported by us previously21.

Next, we investigated the impact of metabolic regulators of α-cell physiology on the two primary m6A writers: methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) in the mouse α-cell line, αTC1-6 (Fig. 1a). Low glucose and amino acids are known to positively influence glucagon secretion31. We observed that treatment with low glucose (2.8 mM) for 24 h upregulated both METTL14 and METTL3 proteins (Fig. 1b,c). Among other metabolic stimuli, we considered recent findings that emphasize the critical role of amino acids in α-cell function3,4 and treated αTC1-6 cells with L-arginine or L-glutamine for 24 h. L-arginine induced a dose-dependent increase in METTL14 levels without significantly affecting METTL3 (Fig. 1d,e). Similarly, L-glutamine increased METTL14 protein levels, while METTL3 remained virtually unchanged (Fig. 1f,g). The changes in METTL14, but not METTL3, were notable since both m6A writers are known to stabilize each other32. We investigated whether METTL3 responds to amino acids at earlier timepoints, as its basal expression is lower in α-cells compared with METTL14 (Extended Data Fig. 1c). A time-course treatment with L-arginine supported this hypothesis, with METTL3 increasing as early as 3 h after amino acid treatment (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Together, these findings support the idea that METTL3 and METTL14 are regulated by the same metabolic cue but display distinct temporal dynamics. Glucagon secretion is negatively regulated by paracrine signals, particularly by insulin secreted by β-cells33. Insulin treatment resulted in the downregulation of both METTL14 and METTL3 (Fig. 1h,i). Moreover, fatty acids (for example, palmitate) can have stimulatory or inhibitory effects on glucagon secretion34. Palmitate treatment led to the downregulation of METTL14 and METTL3 in αTC1-6 cells (Fig. 1j,k). The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) plays a crucial role in regulating calcium homeostasis and glucagon secretion in α-cells35. However, we observed minimal impact of ER stress on METTL3 and METTL14 levels following treatment with the stressor thapsigargin (Fig. 1l,m).

To determine whether glucose, amino acids or insulin also influence RNA methylation, we measured m6A levels in total RNA under the conditions used for western blot analyses (Fig. 1n). We observed that total m6A levels increased in response to low glucose, L-arginine or L-glutamine. We next confirmed the increase in m6A levels induced by amino acids. For this, we repeated treatments for 24 h with L-arginine, isolated mRNA and then confirmed the increase in m6A levels by a dot plot (Extended Data Fig. 1g). Collectively, these data suggest that metabolites that stimulate glucagon secretion also upregulate m6A writers, whereas those that abrogate glucagon secretion also downregulate m6A writer levels in αTC1-6 cells.

We further evaluated the role of m6A using a loss of function model by knocking down Mettl14 in αTC1-6 cells (Fig. 1o,p) followed by stimulating with L-arginine (Fig. 1q,r). As anticipated, under low-glucose conditions L-arginine stimulation increased glucagon secretion in scramble control cells, but this response was absent in Mettl14-knockdown (KD) cells (Fig. 1q), despite the cells having no significant changes in glucagon content (Fig. 1r). Glucagon secretion is modulated by various signalling pathways. For instance, insulin receptor KO in α-cells augments L-arginine-stimulated glucagon secretion in mice33, while reduced AMPKα phosphorylation decreases glucagon release36. To identify the signalling events mediating Mettl14’s effect on glucagon secretion, we compared basal state signalling analyses in αTC1-6 cells deficient in Mettl14 versus scramble controls (Fig. 1s). These analyses revealed upregulation of insulin receptor, ERK and mTOR phosphorylation and downregulation of AMPKα phosphorylation in Mettl14-KD cells compared with scramble (Fig. 1t). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the m6A writer METTL14 is responsive to changes in metabolic stimuli that modulate glucagon secretion and that METTL14 is critical for the α-cell’s glucagon response to amino acids (Fig. 1u).

α-cell Mettl14-KO mice show impaired glucagon response to amino acids and elevated insulin secretion

To validate our in vitro findings within an in vivo mammalian system, we generated an α-cell-specific Mettl14-KO mouse line paired with a tomato lineage-tracing system (M14αKO) (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). As previously demonstrated37, virtually all tomato-positive cells co-localized with glucagon, confirming the precision of our targeting strategy (Fig. 2b,c). Successful silencing of Mettl14 in α-cells was confirmed by sorting α- and β-cells from the KO mice (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 2b,c). Interestingly, M14αKO mice presented decreased glucagon mRNA levels compared with control mice (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The M14αKO mice were born at the expected Mendelian ratios and displayed smaller body weights (Fig. 2e). Blood glucose levels in the fasting state were lower in M14αKO mice compared with controls (Fig. 2f). Consistently, the KO mice exhibited reduced fed glucose levels (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Notably, M14αKO mice also exhibited decreased fasting glucagon levels (Fig. 2g) while the fed levels remained unchanged compared with controls (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Moreover, M14αKO mice presented elevated fasting insulin levels, a trend that persisted in the fed state as well (Fig. 2h and Extended Data Fig. 2f). Despite these changes, no significant differences were observed in glucose tolerance (Extended Data Fig. 2g) or insulin sensitivity (Extended Data Fig. 2h) between groups. To further explore this phenomenon, we conducted in vivo L-arginine tolerance tests in fasted animals. Control mice exhibited higher blood glucose levels (Fig. 2i) and demonstrated a robust glucagon response to amino acid stimulation, which was notably diminished in M14αKO mice (Fig. 2j). Conversely, the KO mice exhibited an amplified insulin response to L-arginine compared with controls (Fig. 2k). Importantly, these trends were also observed in female mice. Female M14αKO mice displayed decreased blood glucose levels 30 min after L-arginine challenge (Fig. 2l), correspondingly reduced glucagon response relative to controls (Fig. 2m) and a trend towards higher insulin response to amino acids compared with controls (Fig. 2n).

To further investigate the α-cell dysfunction associated with Mettl14 KO, we performed in vitro L-arginine stimulation on isolated, size-matched mouse islets. We chose to stimulate the islets with L-arginine at low glucose levels given that mouse β-cells do not respond robustly to L-arginine under these conditions38. This approach allowed us to eliminate potential paracrine confounding effects from the increased insulin response observed in M14αKO mice in vivo. As anticipated, control mice exhibited elevated glucagon secretion in response to L-arginine compared with high glucose conditions alone (Fig. 2o). However, this response was absent in the KO islets (Fig. 2o). M14αKO islets secreted more insulin at a high glucose condition compared with controls, and as expected, there was no increase in insulin levels in response to L-arginine in low-glucose conditions in either group (Fig. 2p). In addition, M14αKO islets displayed a numerical decrease in glucagon content (Extended Data Fig. 2i) and no changes in insulin content (Extended Data Fig. 2j). In summary, these results reinforce our initial findings in αTC1-6 cells, demonstrating that α-cell-specific Mettl14 ablation leads to a diminished glucagon response to amino acids, underscoring the critical role of METTL14 in α-cell function.

Loss of Mettl14 in α-cells drives α-to-β cell transdifferentiation and expands β-cell mass

We next investigated whether the observed changes in glucagon secretion and insulin levels could be explained by alterations in islet cell composition. To this end, we performed immunofluorescence staining of pancreatic sections from control and M14αKO mice. Assessment of α-cell mass by quantifying glucagon-positive cells (Fig. 3a) revealed significantly reduced levels in M14αKO mice compared with controls (Fig. 3b). We then quantified β-cell mass by co-staining for insulin and tomato (Fig. 3c). In contrast to the reduction in α-cells, M14αKO mice exhibited an increase in β-cell mass relative to controls (Fig. 3d). To determine whether this increase arose from α-to-β cell transdifferentiation, we took advantage of the α-cell lineage-tracing system and quantified cells co-expressing tomato (marking cells of α-cell origin) and insulin (Fig. 3e). This analysis revealed a higher proportion of tomato+insulin+ double-positive cells in M14αKO mice compared with controls, consistent with enhanced α-to-β cell conversion even under physiological conditions. Notably, these double-positive cells were frequently localized towards the islet core rather than the peripheral mantle, suggesting acquisition of a β-cell-like spatial organization. Our previous work demonstrated that m6A hypomethylation induced by Mettl14 ablation in β-cells leads to reduced β-cell mass, accompanied by a marked decrease in β-cell proliferation21. We therefore asked whether the reduction in α-cell mass observed in M14αKO mice might be driven by a similar mechanism. To address this, we performed immunofluorescence staining for glucagon and the proliferation marker KI67 (Fig. 3f). Surprisingly, we did not observe a decrease in α-cell proliferation in M14αKO mice compared with controls (Fig. 3g). We next assessed whether the increase in β-cell mass was associated with altered proliferation. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 3h) revealed a significant increase in β-cell proliferation rates in M14αKO mice relative to controls (Fig. 3i). Together, these findings indicate that Mettl14 ablation in α-cells leads to reduced α-cell mass and increased β-cell mass through transdifferentiation into β-cells, as well as via β-cell proliferation.

There is a notable gap in the literature concerning the potential metabolic mechanisms underlying the regulation of α-cell identity. However, metabolic inflexibility and altered mitochondrial respiration are known to precede β-cell de-differentiation39. Furthermore, we have recently shown that early ablation of METTL14 during the differentiation of human islets from embryonic stem cells leads to the downregulation of key islet cell identity genes, including PDX1, MAFA, MAFB and FOXA223.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that Mettl14 and Mettl3 ablation would decrease α-cell identity genes. Consistent with our hypothesis, silencing of m6A writers in α-cells resulted in the downregulation of several critical transcription factors involved in maintaining α-cell identity and function, including Pax640,41, Mafb5,8, Rfx610 and Foxa242 (Fig. 4d).

Next, we sought to investigate whether METTL14 regulates human α-cell identity. We performed regression analyses on human α-cells (high GCG expression) from the PANC-DB scRNA-seq database, identifying genes that were positively or negatively correlated with METTL14 expression (FDR <0.01) (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Positively correlated genes were enriched for pathways linked to oxidative phosphorylation, mRNA splicing, mTOR and ERK signalling (Extended Data Fig. 3e). Conversely, negatively correlated genes were associated with pancreatic secretion, metalloproteases, metallothioneins, AKT signalling and SASP (Extended Data Fig. 3f).

Among the top positively correlated genes with METTL14 in human α-cells were SLC7A2 and IRX2 (Extended Data Fig. 3g,h). SLC7A2 was recently implicated in regulating the α-cell glucagon response to arginine43, while IRX2 is a transcription factor enriched in α-cells often used to confirm α-cell identity44,45. On the other hand, negatively correlated genes included insulin (INS) and somatostatin (SST) (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 3i). These results suggest that elevated levels of METTL14 in human α-cells potentially suppress the expression of genes typically associated with β- and δ-cells, such as insulin and somatostatin. This observation is further supported by the relatively lower number of genes negatively correlated with METTL14 compared with those positively correlated (Extended Data Fig. 3d), suggesting that α-cells with reduced METTL14 levels lose their identity and their GCG expression and are no longer recognized as α-cells (that is high GCG expression) in scRNA-seq analysis. Together, these findings support a role for METTL14/m6A in α-cell and β-cell identity and suggest that this regulatory axis is relevant in human endocrine cells.

To investigate this further, we analysed the protein levels of key α-cell identity modulators in αTC1-6 cells. As expected, Mettl14 silencing resulted in the downregulation of PAX6 and MAFB (Fig. 4f,g). Moreover, Mettl14 ablation led to the upregulation of NKX2.2, a transcription factor whose repressive activity is essential for α-cell specification46 (Fig. 4f,g). Consistently, α-cells from M14αKO mice exhibited a qualitative decrease in nuclear MAFB levels compared with controls (Fig. 4h). These findings suggest that METTL14 plays a crucial role in maintaining α-cell identity by regulating key transcription factors, including PAX6, MAFB and NKX2.2.

To define cell state heterogeneity within α-cells, we performed unsupervised clustering using Seurat (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 5b). Within the α-cell compartment, we identified two distinct clusters, #7 and #15. The latter cluster was markedly enriched in M14αKO islets, whereas cluster #7 was predominantly derived from controls. We therefore used these clusters as a proxy to assess the impact of Mettl14 loss on α-cell state.

Differential expression analysis between cluster #15 (KO-enriched) and cluster #7 (control-enriched) identified 829 upregulated and 226 downregulated genes in cluster #15 (FDR <0.05) (Fig. 5c,d). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated genes were associated with chromatin organization and cell cycle processes (Fig. 5e), while downregulated genes were enriched for oxidative phosphorylation, pancreatic secretion and glucose metabolism (Fig. 5f).

Consistent with these pathways, cluster #15 showed increased expression of chromatin regulators, including Kmt2b and Kmt2d (Fig. 5g) and upregulation of cell cycle genes such as Ccnd1 and Ccnd3 (Fig. 5h). In parallel, these cells exhibited a marked reduction in glucagon (Gcg) transcript levels, together with increased expression of genes involved β-cell in differentiation (Insm1 and Sox4) and ductal/progenitor markers (Krt18 and Epcam) (Fig. 5i). In addition, genes involved in α-cell secretory function, including Atp1a1, Atp2b2 and Slc4a4, were downregulated.

Together, these data identify a distinct α-cell state enriched in M14αKO islets characterized by loss of canonical α-cell identity, activation of chromatin remodelling and proliferative programmes and acquisition of features associated with β-cell differentiation and progenitor-like states. This transcriptional profile is consistent with a permissive and plastic cell state induced by loss of Mettl14 in α-cells.

To identify a potential master regulator of α-cell function and identity regulated by m6A, we first conducted a transcription factor enrichment analysis on the DEGs (FDR <0.05) shared between Mettl3 and Mettl14 KD compared with scramble in αTC1-6 cells (Fig. 7g). We then applied the same analysis to the genes correlated with METTL14 (FDR <0.01) in human α-cells from the PANC-DB scRNA-seq database (Fig. 7h). Finally, we intersected these enriched transcription factors with the differentially m6A-methylated genes (P < 0.05) in αTC1-6 cells following Mettl14 silencing (Fig. 7i). Remarkably, this integrative approach led to the identification of Yin Yang 1 (YY1) as a singular candidate regulator, with an m6A-susceptible site located in its 3’UTR (Fig. 7j and Extended Data Fig. 6e).

To explore the regulation of YY1 by m6A we used three approaches: first, we silenced METTL14 and analysed YY1 protein levels via Western blotting (Fig. 7k). Consistent with our hypothesis, METTL14 downregulation resulted in YY1 upregulation (Fig. 7l). Second, we treated αTC1-6 cells with STM2457, a potent and selective METTL3 catalytic inhibitor (that inhibits the m6A writer complex enzymatic activity), for 16 h, which similarly led to increased YY1 protein levels (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g). Third, we performed immunofluorescence analysis on pancreatic sections from control and M14αKO mice. In control animals, YY1 was detected in acinar, ductal and endocrine cells, with particularly strong immunostaining in the islet core, indicating higher expression in β-cells, as previously reported27,28, while α-cells showed less intense staining (Fig. 7m). By contrast, M14αKO mice exhibited markedly increased YY1 staining specifically in α-cells compared with controls (Fig. 7m).

Next, we explored the mechanism by which m6A regulates YY1 expression. To do this, we independently silenced the three primary cytosolic m6A readers (Ythdf1, Ythdf2 and Ythdf3) in αTC1-6 cells and conducted an actinomycin D (transcription inhibitor) chase experiment (Extended Data Fig. 6h). While Yy1 mRNA levels decreased rapidly upon actinomycin D treatment in scramble cells, the KD of Ythdf2 and, to a lesser extent, Ythdf3, led to increased stability of Yy1 mRNA (Fig. 7n). Overall, these data suggest that m6A regulates YY1 stability through the m6A reader YTHDF2 in α-cells.

Finally, we generated two luciferase 3′UTR reporter constructs (Fig. 7o and Extended Data Fig. 6i) containing either the wild-type (WT) Yy1 3′UTR or a point mutation disrupting a specific m6A site (A1829T) identified by m6A-eCLIP. First, we confirmed selective overexpression of luciferase (Luc1) fused to the Yy1 3′UTR region and a region of the Yy1 3’UTR fused to luciferase (Fig. 7p). Next, mRNA decay assays using luciferase mRNA levels as a readout showed that, whereas the WT Yy1 3′UTR reporter displayed the expected decay after transcriptional arrest, mutation of the candidate m6A adenosine disrupted this pattern and resulted in marked stabilization of the reporter transcript, with an apparent transient but significant increase at the early 3 h timepoint (Fig. 7q). Altogether, these data strongly support the observation that the adenosine site at position 1829 contributes to Yy1 mRNA stability in an m6A-dependent manner.

m6A–YY1 axis rewires α-cell signalling

YY1 is a ubiquitous transcription factor essential for various biological processes such as embryogenesis, differentiation and cell proliferation26. Depending on context, YY1 can either activate or repress gene transcription26. Recently, two independent studies highlighted the critical role of YY1 in preserving pancreatic β-cell function and identity27,28. Notably, YY1 is highly expressed in pancreatic β-cells, and YY1 ChIP-seq analyses in whole islets revealed that YY1 binds to several key islet cell identity markers, including Rfx628.

First, we examined whether YY1 upregulation affects α-cell identity. YY1 overexpression was robust and led to reduced PAX6 and MAFB protein levels compared with empty vector controls (Fig. 8a,b). We then silenced YY1 in αTC1-6 cells and assessed MAFB, a key regulator of α-cell identity and function. Notably, Yy1 KD also decreased MAFB protein levels (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). This bidirectional effect is consistent with the known context-dependent activity of YY1, which can function as either a transcriptional activator or repressor depending on interacting cofactors. Accordingly, both increased and decreased YY1 levels can disrupt normal transcriptional programmes49. This interpretation is further supported by evidence in other systems where YY1 exhibits dosage sensitivity. In B cells (immune cells), YY1 loss impairs lineage progression50, whereas YY1 overexpression disrupts differentiation and promotes apoptosis51. Together, these findings indicate that α-cell identity is sensitive to YY1 dosage and suggest that tight regulation of YY1 abundance is required to maintain a stable α-cell transcriptional Next, because YY1 is a well-established chromatin-associated regulator25, we examined the transcriptomic changes induced by YY1 overexpression in αTC1-6 cells by mRNA-seq. YY1 overexpression was robust and resulted in clear segregation between control and YY1-overexpressing samples (Fig. 8c). However, we did not observe widespread changes in protein-coding gene expression (Fig. 8d). Instead, we noted alterations in several long noncoding RNAs (Fig. 8e and Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). Nevertheless, pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed protein-coding genes revealed enrichment for pathways related to ATP metabolism and mitochondrial function (Fig. 8f). Notably, YY1 overexpression increased expression of Prkn, a regulator of mitochondrial quality control52, and P2rx7, a purinergic receptor previously shown to be enriched in β-cells and to influence insulin secretion53 (Fig. 8g).

Subsequently, given prior evidence linking YY1 to the mTOR machinery and to physically interact with metabolic co-regulators such as PGC-1α54, we next investigated whether YY1 impacts signalling pathways. We therefore performed phosphoproteomic profiling in αTC1-6 cells overexpressing YY1 or an empty control vector (Fig. 8h). Phosphoproteomic profiles segregated robustly by condition and resulted in 257 increased phosphosites and 68 decreased phosphosites (Fig. 8i and Extended Data Fig. 7e). Pathway enrichment analysis identified several growth factor-associated signalling pathways including insulin, FoxO and mTOR (Fig. 8j and Extended Data Fig. 7f). In particular, YY1 overexpression increased phosphorylation of IRS2, AKT and components of MAPK signalling (Fig. 8k,l).

Together, these data support a model in which YY1 links m6A-dependent regulation to metabolic and growth factor signalling in α-cells. YY1 overexpression engages insulin, mTOR and MAPK/ERK pathways at the phosphoproteomic level, closely mirroring the activation of these same signalling programmes observed upon Mettl14 depletion in α-cells. This alignment suggests that YY1 acts downstream of m6A loss to mediate signalling rewiring in α-cells. While the precise mechanism(s) by which YY1 coordinates these transcriptional and signalling outputs remain to be defined, our findings position the transcription factor as a central effector of m6A-mediated control of α-cell identity.

Discussion

In this study, we identify m6A RNA methylation as a central regulator of pancreatic α-cell function, identity and plasticity. We show that amino acids and low glucose increase METTL3, METTL14 and enhance m6A levels, whereas insulin suppresses them. Using complementary in vitro and in vivo models, we further demonstrate that loss of Mettl14 impairs amino acid-stimulated glucagon secretion, disrupts α-cell identity programmes and promotes α-to-β cell conversion. Mechanistically, we identify YY1 as a direct m6A-sensitive target and provide evidence that dysregulated YY1 links loss of m6A to signalling rewiring and erosion of α-cell identity. Together, these findings place m6A-dependent post-transcriptional regulation at the centre of α-cell state control.

This work expands the emerging view that m6A is an important regulator of metabolism and endocrine pancreas biology24. We have previously established that m6A is required for β-cell function, β-cell proliferation, innate immune control and endocrine differentiation21–23. In β-cells, loss of m6A writers impairs insulin secretion, decreases β-cell identity genes and reduces proliferative capacity21. In human stem cell-derived islet differentiation, METTL14 loss compromises both β-cell and α-cell lineage programmes23. These findings support a broader model in which m6A regulates multiple cells in the endocrine lineage and helps stabilize their cell states in a context-dependent manner.

An important aspect of our study is the link between m6A and nutrient-responsive α-cell biology. Amino acids have emerged as major physiological regulators of glucagon secretion and α-cell growth3,4,14,34. In this context, our findings suggest that the m6A machinery is responsive to nutrient availability in α-cells. The distinct temporal behaviour of METTL3 versus METTL14 after amino acid stimulation is notable. Although these proteins function together within the writer complex, they did not respond identically. A likely explanation is that their basal abundance differs in α-cells, with METTL3 present at lower levels and therefore responding with different kinetics. Additional layers of regulation, including post-translational modifications that influence METTL3 activity, may also contribute. Thus, nutrient regulation of the writer complex is probably determined by both protein abundance and enzymatic control.

Our functional data indicate that METTL14 is required for a normal glucagon secretory response to amino acids. This conclusion is supported by studies in both αTC1-6 cells and α-cell-specific Mettl14-KO mice. In isolated islets, we did not observe clear differences in glucagon or insulin content, although the former trended lower in KO islets. These results should be interpreted carefully, since islets were size matched prior to secretion assays and this probably minimized potential differences in total hormone content.

A central question raised by our in vivo model is whether Mettl14 loss first impairs α-cell function and only later affects its identity or whether these processes occur simultaneously. We collected pancreases and began phenotyping the in vivo model at 1 month of age. We were not able to identify a stage at which a clear functional defect was present while α-cell mass and identity remained unaffected. Instead, the detectable changes in glucagon secretion and α-cell mass emerged at 4 months of age. Therefore, our data support a model in which loss of function and loss of identity are tightly linked. This is biologically plausible, as mature α-cell function depends on preservation of a transcriptional state that supports glucagon production, nutrient sensing, and secretory competence5,10.

Our data further indicate that Mettl14 loss in α-cells leads to profound remodelling of islet cell composition. In particular, we observed increased β-cell mass at the expense of α-cell mass, and an increase in lineage-traced tomato+insulin+ cells, consistent with α-to-β cell conversion. The localization of these cells towards the islet core further supports acquisition of β-cell-like spatial organization, in agreement with previous reports of α-to-β transdifferentiation11. Concomitantly, our results suggest that the phenotype cannot be explained by lineage conversion alone. Although we did not detect changes in α-cell viability, we observed an increase in β-cell proliferation. Therefore, the increase in β-cell mass probably reflects both α-derived conversion and expansion of the β-cell compartment. One possibility is that some α-derived β-like cells remain plastic or immature and therefore retain proliferative capacity. Another possibility is that altered signals (for example exosomes, growth factors) released from Mettl14-deficient α-cells indirectly promote β-cell proliferation.

The single-nucleus RNA-seq analyses identified a KO-enriched α-cell state characterized by a drastic reduction in glucagon expression, downregulation of α-cell secretory genes, impaired oxidative phosphorylation and activation of chromatin remodelling and cell cycle programmes. This profile supports the notion that Mettl14 loss induces a permissive, unstable α-cell state that is compatible with lineage plasticity. Second, pseudotime analyses identified β-like populations enriched in Mettl14-KO islets that occupy late positions along the α-to-β trajectory. These populations showed upregulation of β-cell-related pathways, but also retained features of incomplete maturation, including neuronal gene programmes and reduced oxidative metabolism. Thus, the cells generated after Mettl14 loss do not appear to be fully transcriptional mature β-cells. In this setting, m6A loss may facilitate exit from the α-cell state while impairing consolidation of a stable mature β-cell programme.

These findings also help explain the apparent discrepancy between the histological and transcriptomic readouts of β-cells. Histology showed increased β-cell mass, whereas snRNA-seq did not show a corresponding increase in nuclei assigned to canonical β-cell clusters in M14αKO. These observations are not necessarily contradictory. Insulin immunofluorescence reflects protein abundance and area, not simply cell number, and may be influenced by larger cell size, greater insulin content per cell and/or accumulation of insulin protein in lineage-converted cells that do not fully match canonical β-cell transcriptomes. By contrast, snRNA-seq reflects the distribution of recovered nuclei after dissociation, nuclei isolation and quality-control filtering. Transitional α-derived β-like cells may therefore contribute substantially to insulin staining while mapping to specific β-like states, mixed endocrine states or clusters not called as canonical β-cells.

An important conclusion of this study is that the phenotypes we observe are largely m6A dependent. We cannot fully exclude m6A-independent functions of METTL14, including chromatin-associated effects described in other systems55,56. However, several lines of evidence support a m6A-dependent mechanism. First, independent KDs of Mettl3 and Mettl14 led to shared transcriptional alterations in the same direction. Second, pharmacologic inhibition of METTL3 catalytic activity increased YY1 abundance. Third, m6A-eCLIP identified specific hypomethylated sites enriched at 3’UTR after Mettl14 depletion, including a functional site in the Yy1 3′UTR. Fourth, disruption of YTHDF2- and YTHDF3-dependent mRNA decay increased Yy1 stability. Fifth, mutation of the identified Yy1 3′UTR m6A site stabilized the reporter luciferase transcript. Together, these orthogonal results strongly support a model wherein the regulation of YY1 and α-cell identity is mediated through m6A-dependent RNA control.

YY1 emerged from our integrative analyses as a plausible downstream effector linking m6A loss to α-cell state remodelling. This finding is interesting because YY1 is a multifunctional transcription factor with established roles in chromatin architecture, enhancer-promoter communication, proliferation and metabolic signalling25,26,50,51,54. It is also highly relevant to islet biology, as recent studies demonstrated important roles for YY1 in β-cell function and survival27,28. In α-cells, however, its role had not been explored. Our findings indicate that YY1 overexpression disrupts α-cell identity, with reduced PAX6 and MAFB, while activating signalling pathways involving IRS2, mTOR and MAPK/ERK. These signalling changes closely mirror those observed after Mettl14 depletion, supporting the idea that YY1 functions downstream of m6A loss. A possible explanation for the reduced oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial respiration observed in Mettl14-deficient α-cells is the known interaction between YY1 and PGC-1α54. In this context YY1 can cooperate with PGC-1α downstream of mTOR to regulate mitochondrial oxidative function. Whether YY1 also regulates the tRNA57 and/or interacts with pathways associated with chromatin-associated RNAs58 to regulate a-cells requires further investigation.

A notable and initially paradoxical observation was that YY1 overexpression or silencing both reduced MAFB abundance. We interpret this to suggest that α-cell identity is sensitive to YY1 dosage rather than to YY1 directionality alone. In this model, YY1 does not behave as a simple binary activator or repressor but rather as a dosage-sensitive regulator whose proper level must be maintained within a permissive range. This interpretation is consistent with the context-dependent functions of YY1 reported in other systems, where gain or loss of YY1 can both disrupt lineage stability50,51.

In summary, our study identifies m6A RNA methylation as a key mechanism that couples nutrient sensing to maintenance of α-cell function and identity. By integrating metabolic, transcriptional, epitranscriptomic and lineage-tracing approaches, we show that loss of METTL14 destabilizes the α-cell state, impairs glucagon secretion, and promotes α-cell plasticity towards β-like fates. We further identify YY1 as a downstream dosage-sensitive effector of this pathway. These findings enhance our understanding of α-cell biology and suggest that epitranscriptomic regulation is an important determinant of endocrine cell state. More broadly, these data raise the possibility that controlled manipulation of m6A-dependent pathways is one approach to modulate islet cell plasticity for regenerative purposes in diabetes or optimization of islet differentiation protocols.

Methods

Study approval

All animal experiments were conducted following the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Joslin Diabetes Center following NIH guidelines.

Mouse studies

α-cell-specific Mettl14 KO mice were generated by breeding a Mettl14 floxed mouse21 with B6;129S-Gcgtm1.1(icre)Gkg/J (Jackson Labs)37. To create an α-cell lineage tracer system we used the B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J mice (Jackson Labs)59. Animals were maintained on a chow diet (PicoLab mouse diet 20–5058) and housed in cages (four or five per cage) at a relative humidity of 30–70% and room temperature of 22.2 ± 1.1 °C on a 12-h light–12-h dark cycle with ad lib access to food and water at the Animal Facility of the Joslin Diabetes Center. Mice were anesthetized, and blood was collected by heart puncture from animals used in our experiments unless otherwise specified. Sample sizes for animal experiments were chosen based on experience in previous in-house studies of metabolic phenotypes and to balance the ability to detect significant differences with minimization of the number of animals used that is consistent with NIH guidelines.

In vivo arginine-stimulated glucagon secretion, intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test and insulin-tolerance test

For the in vivo arginine-stimulated glucagon secretion test, mice were fasted for 16 h, and L-arginine solution in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was injected via the intraperitoneal (i.p.) route (3 g kg−1body weight). Serum was collected by tail vein at minutes 0, 5 and 30 after injection in tubes containing aprotinin. Glucagon was assayed immediately with a mouse glucagon enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (#81518; Crystal Chem) according to manufacturer instructions. Insulin was assayed with a mouse insulin ELISA kit (#90080; Crystal Chem). Blood glucose levels were measured using an automated glucose monitor (Glucometer Elite, Bayer) at the moment of blood collection. For the glucose tolerance tests, animals were fasted for 16 h, and 20% (w/v) dextrose (Hospira) was injected via the i.p. route (2 g kg−1body weight). Blood glucose levels were measured using an automated glucose monitor (Glucometer Elite, Bayer) by tail puncture immediately before and at 15, 30, 60 and 120 min after injection. The insulin-tolerance test was performed after fasting animals for 5 h and 0.75 U kg−1insulin (Humalog) was administered. i.p. Blood glucose levels were measured using an automated glucose monitor (Glucometer Elite, Bayer) by tail punch at timepoints 0, 15, 30 and 60 min after injection and were plotted to the initial glucose levels.

Mouse islet isolations

Islets were isolated as previously described60,61. In brief, mice were anesthetized and their pancreas was infused with Liberase TL Research Grade (#05401020001; Roche). Following incubation at 37 °C for 17 min, the digested pancreases were washed and filtered through a 400-μm filter and run on a Histopaque (10771-500 ML; Sigma) gradient. The purified islets were handpicked, counted and cultured overnight in 11 mM glucose RPMI media (#11875093; Gibco) containing 10% FBS (#A5670402, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (#15140122; Gibco). Islets were handpicked, washed two times with ice-cold DPBS by self-sedimentation and used for experiments.

β- and α-cell sorting by FACS

Overnight cultured mouse islets from control or M14KO islets were dispersed using TrypLE Express Enzyme (#12604013, Gibco) followed by incubation for 15 min at 37 °C. During the digestion, the islets were vortexed every 5 min for 5 s. Media containing 30 µg ml−1DNase (#04536282001; Roche) was added to stop digestion and prevent cell clumping, and the cells were washed 2× in DPBS containing 1% fatty-acid-free bovine serum albumin (#3117057001; Roche). Before sorting, islet cells were filtered through a 35-µm filter and sorted using MoFlo Cytometer (Dako); cells were gated according to forward scatter and sorted based on endogenous fluorescence (FITC)62 and tomato (PE).

Pancreas immunostaining and analyses

Mouse pancreas was collected and fixed in 4% formaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, followed by paraffin embedding. The 5-µm-thick slides were cut and subjected to immunostaining. Slides were heated in 10 mM sodium citrate, followed by blocking with donkey serum and incubated with various primary antibodies: tomato (#200301379; Rockland Immunochemicals, DF1:200), insulin (#5330-0104G; Bio-Rad, DF1:400), glucagon (#G2654; Sigma, DF1:10,000), MAFB (#NBP1-81342; Novus, DF1:500) or YY1 (#ab245365; Abcam, DF1:200). Specific signal was detected by using fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson Immunoresearch, Alexa 488, Alexa 594 or AMCA). Images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Imager A2 upright fluorescence microscope. Quantification of α- and β-cell mass was performed as previously described60,63,64.

Cell culture

αTC1-6 cells (#CRL-2934; ATCC) were cultured and passaged in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (#10-014-CV; Gibco) supplemented with D-(+)-glucose (#G8769-100ML; Sigma) to achieve 11 mM. Complete media contained fetal bovine serum (FBS) to a final concentration of 10% FBS (#A5670402; Gibco), non-essential amino acids to a final concentration of 0.1 mM (#11140050; Gibco), fatty-acid free bovine serum albumin fraction V to a final concentration of 0.02% (#3117057001; Roche), 2-mercaptoethanol (50 μM) (#21985023; Gibco) and penicillin–streptomycin (1%) (#15140122; Gibco).

Transient knockdowns

Reverse transfections were performed by mixing cells in treated six-well plates (8 × 105cells per well) overnight with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (#13778075; Thermo) and small interfering RNA complexes at a final concentration of 15 nmol l−1siRNA in complete media modified to contain 2% FBS (#A5670402; Gibco) and no penicillin–streptomycin (#15140122; Gibco). After 16 h, the medium was changed, and cells were maintained in complete media for 72 h until experiments. siRNA consisted of siGENOME Non-Targeting siRNA Pool #1 D-001206-13-05, siGENOME Mouse Mettl3 siRNA M-049446-01-0005; and siGENOME Mouse Mettl14 siRNA M-063715-00-0005 (Dharmacon).

Transient overexpression

Forward transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000008; Thermo) according to the standard protocol. Plasmids were generated by VectorBuilder expressing Yy1 (NM_009537.4) or a control ‘stuffer’ under the control of a human cytomegalovirus immediate early enhancer/promoter fused with the splicing signal from the human β-globin intron 2. Cells were seeded in six-well treated plates (1 × 106cells per well) in complete media and after 24 h were forward transfected with 1.5 μg of cDNA, 3 μl of P3000 and 3.5 μl of lipofectamine 3000 in complete media modified to contain 2% FBS (#A5670402; Gibco) and no penicillin-streptomycin (#15140122; Gibco) for 6 h. The medium was exchanged to complete medium, and cells were collected 72 h post-transfection.

Glucagon secretion assay

αTC1-6

Cells were cultured as described above and placed in complete media containing 25 mM glucose for 1 h. After that cells were cultured in Krebs Ringer Buffer (KRB) containing NaCl (115 mM), NaHCO3 (24 mM), KCl (5 mM), MgCl2 (1 mM), CaCl2 (1 mM), HEPES (10 mM), bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0.2% wt/vol) and 25 mM glucose for 1 h. Glucagon secretion assays were then initiated by adding KRB containing 25 mM glucose or 2.8 mM glucose plus 10 mM L-arginine for 1 h. Aliquots of supernatants were collected in tubes containing aprotinin, centrifuged to discard dead cells and assayed immediately for glucagon using a mouse glucagon ELISA kit (#81518; Crystal Chem) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mouse islets

After 24 h of culture size-matched islets were incubated in RPMI containing 25 mM glucose for 1 h. After that islets were cultured in KRB containing NaCl (115 mM), NaHCO3 (24 mM), KCl (5 mM), MgCl2 (1 mM), CaCl2 (1 mM), HEPES (10 mM), BSA (0.2% wt/vol) and 25 mM glucose for 1 h. Glucagon secretion assays were then initiated by adding KRB containing 25 mM glucose (#G8769-100ML; Sigma) or 2.8 mM glucose (#G8769-100ML; Sigma) plus 10 mM L-arginine (11009-25G-F; Sigma) for 1 h. Aliquots of supernatants were collected in tubes containing aprotinin, centrifuged to discard dead cells and assayed immediately for glucagon using a mouse glucagon ELISA kit (#81518; Crystal Chem) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Seahorse mitochondrial stress tests and glycolytic capacity

In brief, 25,000 αTC1-6 cells were seeded per well in a Seahorse XF24 cell culture microplate and transfected as described above. At 72 h post-transfection, the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (#103015-100; Agilent) or Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test (#103020-100; Agilent) was performed using the XFe24 Analyzer according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA isolation and RT-PCRs

Total RNA isolation and RT-PCR were performed as previously described65. High-quality total RNA (>200 nt) was extracted using standard Trizol reagent (#15596026; Invitrogen) according to manufacturer instructions and the resultant aqueous phase was mixed (1:1) with 70% RNA-free ethanol and added to Qiagen RNeasy mini kit columns (#74104; Qiagen) and the kit protocol was followed. RNA quality and quantity were analysed using Nanodrop 1000 and used for reverse transcription using the high-capacity cDNA synthesis kit (#4368814; Applied Biosciences). cDNA was analysed using the ABI 7900HT system (Applied Biosciences) and gene expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Data were normalized to β-actin.

Quantification of m6A

Colorimetric quantification

m6A was measured as previously described22. In brief, EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (#P-9005-96; EpigenTek) was used to measure the percentage of m6A methylation level in total RNA. αTC1-6 cells were used according to the protocols of the manufacturer using the kit provided with negative and positive controls; our samples consisted of 200ng of total RNA from αTC1-6 cells.

Single-nuclei RNA-sequencing and analysis

Single-nuclei RNA-sequencing

Single nuclei were isolated from frozen pellet islet samples using Nuclei Extraction Buffer (#130-128-024, Miltenyi Biotec) and resuspended in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.4 U μl−1RNase inhibitor (#M0314S, NEB). Nuclei quality and concentration were assessed by acridine orange and propidium iodide staining using a Cellometer Auto 2000 (Nexcelom). A total of 30,000 nuclei per sample were loaded onto a Chromium Controller using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Gene Expression v3.1 platform (10x Genomics). After partitioning and generation of gel bead-in-emulsions, reverse transcription was performed and cDNA was isolated using Dynabeads MyOne SILANE beads (10x Genomics). cDNA was amplified for 12 cycles and purified using SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter). cDNA quality was assessed using a High Sensitivity D5000 TapeStation assay (Agilent Technologies,) and quantified with the Qubit 2.0 DNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 3′ gene expression libraries were generated from 10 μl of cDNA by enzymatic fragmentation. Libraries were size selected using SPRI beads, and P5/P7 adaptors containing i7 and i5 sample indexes were ligated to the fragmented cDNA. Final libraries were purified by SPRI bead size selection. Library quality was assessed using a High Sensitivity D1000 TapeStation assay (Agilent Technologies) and quantified with the Qubit 2.0 DNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were pooled equimolarly based on QC metrics and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus instrument (Illumina) using a 150-bp paired-end configuration, targeting 800 million total reads per sample.

Single-nuclei RNA-sequencing analysis

Raw single-nucleus RNA-seq data were processed with Cell Ranger66. Ambient RNA contamination was reduced with CellBender67. Putative doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder68 before downstream analysis. The filtered count matrices were imported into R and converted into Seurat objects, which were then merged across samples for integrated analysis. Quality control filtering excluded low-quality nuclei based on total counts, number of detected genes and mitochondrial transcript content. Data were normalized and scaled, followed by dimensionality reduction with principal component analysis. Batch correction and data integration were performed with Harmony69. Shared nearest-neighbour graph-based clustering was then used to identify transcriptionally distinct cell populations, which were visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Cell types were assigned on the basis of canonical marker genes together with reference-based annotation approaches implemented with rcellmarker70,71, scCATCH72 and ScType73. For trajectory analysis, a subset of nuclei representing the transitional populations of interest was analysed with Monocle 370,74. Cells spanning clusters 7 and 15 through the trajectory towards β-cells were analysed. Pseudotime values were then extracted for each cell, and gene expression changes across pseudotime were assessed in Monocle 3 by fitting gene-wise models as a function of pseudotime. Expression patterns of selected α-cell and β-cell marker genes across pseudotime were visualized by a heat map.

m6A-eCLIP

m6A-eCLIP sequencing

m6A-eCLIP in αTC1-6 cells was performed at Eclipse BioInnovations, San Diego, CA, USA, as described below. The procedure started by isolating mRNA from 20 µg of total RNA through poly(A) selection using magnetic oligo (dT) beads (#61002; ThermoFisher). The mRNA was treated with DNase at 37 °C for 10 min then immediately sheared into 100–200-nt fragments by heating at 95 °C for 16 min in 1× Turbo DNase buffer (#AM2238; ThermoFisher). An anti-m6A antibody (#ABE572-I-1MG; Millipore Sigma) was added, and samples were UV-C crosslinked using a UVP CL-1000 crosslinker at two rounds of 150 mJ cm−2using a 254-nm wavelength. The antibody-RNA complexes were coupled overnight to protein G beads (#70024; Cell Signaling). Following overnight coupling, library preparation (including adaptor ligations, SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, nitrocellulose membrane transfer, reverse transcription and PCR amplification) was performed as previously described for standard eCLIP48, with the 30–150-kDa region size selected by cutting from the membrane (corresponding to RNA fragments crosslinked to antibody heavy and light chains). A total of 20 ng of fragmented mRNA was run as an RNA-seq input control, starting with FastAP treatment, followed by adaptor ligation, reverse transcription and PCR amplification, as described48. The final library’s shape and yield were assessed by Agilent TapeStation.

m6A-eCLIP analysis

To investigate the enrichment of m6A-modified RNA sites and regions in Mettl14-KD cells compared with scramble siRNA (SCR) control, eCLIP was performed on both conditions. The 5′-UMI of the forward RNA-seq reads was trimmed using UMI-tools75, while the 3′-adaptor sequences were removed using Cutadapt76. Trimmed reads were aligned to the UCSC mouse genome (mm10) using STAR77, and PCR duplicates were removed with UMI tools.

Crosslinked sites were identified with PureCLIP78, and adjacent sites (within 8-bp windows) were merged to define enriched regions. The analysis was performed at both single-nucleotide resolution and as merged regions. Reproducibility of eCLIP peaks across biological duplicates was assessed using CHIP-R79, which generates optimal peak sets. Nonstandard chromosome peaks were removed. Known and novel motifs (4–8 bp) per condition were searched using HOMER80, with the DRACH m6A consensus motif identified in multiple sites.

The PureCLIP optimal peak sets for each condition were merged using bedtools81, and overlapping peaks between SCR and Mettl14-KD conditions were retained. Aligned read counts for IP and input RNA samples in merged peak sets were obtained with multiBamSummary82, followed by differential peak enrichment analysis using DESeq283. Metagene plots for eCLIP peaks were generated using Guitar84 over mRNA and ncRNA genomic features, and the optimal peak sets were annotated for genomic features using ChIPseeker85. Pie charts of genomic feature distributions were created with the plotAnnoPie function.

Bulk mRNA-seq analysis

Mettl3 or Mettl14 KD in αTC1-6

Mouse α-cell line αTC1-6 was subjected to KD of Mettl3 or Mettl14 or scramble control using siRNA. Total RNA was extracted, and RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a standard protocol for paired-end sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. The RNA-seq reads were trimmed for adaptor sequences and poly(X) tails, and low-quality bases (Phred score <Q15) were removed using fastp86. The trimmed reads were pseudo-aligned to the mouse transcriptome (Ensembl v111) using kallisto87, and transcript counts were converted to gene-level counts using the tximport package88. After filtering out genes with low expression (CPM of 0.46 in fewer than four samples), a total of 13,816 genes were retained for analysis. The data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method89, and further transformed to log2 counts per million (logCPM) using voom90 for linear modelling and differential expression analysis.

To identify DEGs between groups, we applied the linear regression modelling R package limma91, whose tests are moderated via empirical Bayes. A linear regression model was fit for each gene with log2 total counts as a covariate to account for variability in library sizes, and moderated t-tests were conducted to assess the significance of each KD versus control.

To discover genes that were differentially expressed in both M3KD and M14KD conditions compared with the SCR control and in the same direction, we tested replication with ScreenDMT92. Genes identified as significant in both KD conditions, with consistent directionality of change, were considered replicated hits. We performed GSEA using the Reactome database93,94 to identify enriched pathways among the DEGs.

Yy1 overexpression in αTC1-6

Mouse α-cell line αTC1-6 was subjected to YY1 overexpression or empty vector control. Total RNA was extracted, and RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a standard protocol for paired-end sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC and trimmed for adaptor sequences and poly(X) tails using fastp86. Trimmed reads were pseudo-aligned to the mouse transcriptome (Ensembl v113, GRCm39) using kallisto87 with strand-specific quantification, and transcript counts were converted to gene-level counts using the tximport package88. The data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method89 and transformed to log2 counts per million (logCPM) using voom90 for linear modelling and differential expression analysis. DEGs between YY1-overexpressing and empty vector control cells were identified using R package limma91.

Reanalyses of PANC-DB scRNA-seq

We downloaded the Seurat object of the scRNA-seq data at PANC-DB for human whole islets29,30 and kept only non-diabetic samples. To test the association of each gene’s expression to METTL14 expression, we log-normalized the data using Seurat, selected the subset to α- and β-cells, only kept genes that had average expression above 0.01, and tested the association performing regression analyses using limma91.

To test the difference in transcriptional variability at the single-cell level between genes that carry m6A modification versus those that do not, we defined m6A-tagged genes as having a significant peak that was enriched in IP over input at twofold or more with FDR <0.05 based on our m6A-seq data in human non-diabetic islets22 and other genes as non-m6A-tagged genes. We quantified genes’ transcriptional variability using their coefficient of variation and tested the null hypothesis of no difference using two-sided t-tests.

YY1 overexpression phosphoproteomics

αTC1-6 cells harbouring YY1 overexpression or empty vector control were analysed by label-free phosphoproteomics with four biological replicates per group by Metware Biotechnology. Proteins were extracted, reduced, alkylated, trypsin-digested and phosphopeptides were enriched by IMAC before liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Phosphopeptides were separated by nanoLC and analysed on a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer operated in data-dependent PASEF mode95. Raw data were processed using FragPipe (v21.1) with IonQuant and MaxLFQ96. Searches were performed against a combined FASTA database with decoys and contaminants, using strict trypsin specificity, up to two missed cleavages, carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification and oxidation (M), protein N-terminal acetylation and phosphorylation (STY) as variable modifications. Peptide- and protein-level FDR thresholds were set to 1%, and phosphosite localization probability was filtered at ≥0.75. Phosphosite intensities were median-centred within each sample. Principal component analysis was performed on normalized phosphosite abundance values. Differential phosphosites were defined using fold change ≥1.5 or ≤0.6667 with P ≤ 0.05. P values for differential phosphorylation were calculated using a two-sided t-test on normalized phosphosite intensities. Pathway and protein–protein interaction analyses were performed on proteins harbouring differential phosphosites.

Pathway enrichment, protein–protein analysis and transcription factor enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analyses were performed using ConsensusPathDB97 using default settings. Protein–protein functional networks were constructed using STRING98. Transcription factor enrichment analyses were performed using eXpression2Kinases (X2K)99 using default settings.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen on the basis of prior experiments, while minimizing animal use. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates unless otherwise indicated; exact sample sizes and statistical tests are reported in Figs. 1–8 and Extended Data Figs. 1–7. For analyses using parametric tests, data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. Equal variances were not formally tested unless otherwise indicated. Parametric tests were used as specified in Figs. 1–8 and Extended Data Figs. 1–7, including two-sided t-tests for two-group comparisons and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the indicated multiple-comparison corrections for comparisons involving more than two groups. For repeated-measure time-course assays, mixed-effects models were used when individual timepoint measurements were missing. Individual data points are shown when applicable. Outliers were assessed using Grubbs’ test (α=0.05) and excluded when statistically significant. In dynamic in vivo assays, including hormone-secretion time-course assays, occasional individual timepoint measurements were not obtained because of insufficient blood volume or technical sample-collection failure. These missing values were not imputed, and all available measurements were included in the analyses and reported in the data source. Animals were randomly grouped according to genotype and matched for age and sex when applicable. Cell culture treatments and genetic-manipulation experiments were processed in parallel under matched culture conditions. β-cell mass and α-cell mass analyses were performed blinded to genotype or treatment group. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments for cell culture experiments involving KD or overexpression, because group identity was required during sample preparation and processing. Representative images, immunoblots and gels are from experiments independently repeated with similar results, as indicated in Figs. 1–8 and Extended Data Figs. 1–7.
