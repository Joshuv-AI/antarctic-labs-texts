Owen-Shapley Policy Optimization (OSPO): A Principled RL Algorithm for Generative Search LLMs
Abhijnan Nath, Alireza Bagheri Garakani, Tianchen Zhou, Fan Yang, Nikhil Krishnaswamy

Abstract
Large language models are increasingly trained via reinforcement learning for personalized recommendation tasks, but standard methods like GRPO rely on sparse, sequence-level rewards that create a credit assignment gap —obscuring which tokens drive success. This gap is especially problematic when models must infer latent user intent from under-specified language without ground truth labels, a reasoning pattern rarely seen during pretraining. We introduce Owen-Shapley Policy Optimization ( OSPO ), a framework that redistributes sequence-level advantages based on tokens’ marginal contributions to outcomes. Unlike value-model-based methods requiring additional computation, OSPO employs potential-based reward shaping via Shapley-Owen attributions to assign segment-level credit while preserving the optimal policy—directly from task feedback without parametric value models. By forming coalitions of semantically coherent units (phrases describing product attributes or sentences capturing preferences), OSPO identifies which response parts drive performance. Experiments on Amazon ESCI and H&M Fashion datasets show consistent gains over baselines, with notable test-time robustness to out-of-distribution retrievers unseen during training.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly central to modern recommendation pipelines. They can synthesize nuanced user preference summaries, reformulate complex queries, and generate natural-language artifacts that condition downstream retrieval and ranking processes
(
Peng et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
. A major factor driving this adoption is their ability to learn directly from explicit task feedback through Reinforcement Learning (RL), bypassing costly human annotations. This makes them especially effective in dynamic, personalized settings where fine-grained supervision is infeasible
(

;

;

)
. Efficient algorithms such as Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(

)
enable LLMs to optimize against verifiable sequence-level signals from black-box components—retrievers, rankers, or quality assessors—without requiring an external value model.

To address these limitations, we introduce

For example, in product search, customers often make queries that contains attribute-specific preference signals expressed as phrases like “looking for [product] in
my
usual neutral tones
” where the text in bold shows such a segment.
OSPO
treats these segments as high-level abstractions in the action space as valid segments for Owen–Shapley value computation, akin to options
(

)

(
Chai et al. 2025
)
in hierarchical RL. To assign such values, each segment’s marginal contribution to the total reward is measured by how much it increases the reward when added to different coalitions—segments that consistently improve outcomes receive credit, while those that dilute or distract are down-weighted. These attributions are obtained by querying any feedback model that returns scalar scores (e.g., a retriever or reward model), requiring no external value networks or process reward models
(
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Lightman et al. 2023
)
.

Our key contribution is proposing a policy learning approach that can integrate such fine-grained value attributions from Owen values in learning good policies, while additionally leveraging from Monte-Carlo-based sampled rollouts that allow
OSPO
to achieve a reasonable trade off between exploration and exploitation
(
Sutton & Barto 2018
)
.
Our core insight in achieving this balance is one of
gradient budget reallocation via advantage redistribution
: rather than spreading policy gradients uniformly across all tokens in a sequence
(
Shao et al. 2024a
)
,
OSPO
concentrates updates on high-attribution segments while reducing signal on low-value content.

Problem Formulation and Background

We model text generation for recommendation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\rho_{0},\gamma)
, where: the
state space

\mathcal{S}
consists of all partial sequences
(x,y_{<t})
combining the input context
x
(e.g., user purchase history or raw search query) and tokens generated so far
y_{<t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})
; the
action space

\mathcal{A}
is the vocabulary from which the policy selects the next token
y_{t}
; the
transition function

P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})
is deterministic—appending token
a_{t}=y_{t}
to the history to form state
s_{t+1}=(x,y_{\leq t})
; the
reward function

r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
assigns scalar feedback; the
initial state distribution

\rho_{0}
samples input
x\sim\mathcal{D}
from the data distribution; and the
discount factor

\gamma=1
for the undiscounted episodic setting as is standard for LLMs
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
. A trajectory
\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},\ldots,s_{T},a_{T})
corresponds to autoregressively generating a complete sequence
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})
via the policy
\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|s_{t})=\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
, where
\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
.

The learning objective

2

We set the KL coefficient to
\beta=0

(

)
.
is to find a policy that maximizes expected downstream performance:

\max_{\theta}\;\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},~y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,],

(1)

where
r(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
is a terminal reward from the downstream system—for instance, NDCG in product search or a reward-model score in user profile generation
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
.

Because
r(x,y)
is
sequence-level and terminal
, with
r_{t}=0
for
t<T
and
r_{T}=r(x,y)
, the policy receives no signal about which tokens—or, more meaningfully, which
semantic segments
of the response—contributed to success, creating the credit assignment problem. To address sparse feedback, methods like
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

(
Shao et al. 2024a
;
Lin et al. 2025
)
replace the learned value function with a sampling-based baseline. GRPO samples
G
responses per prompt and computes group-relative advantages by normalizing rewards within the batch:

\scriptsize\hat{A}^{(g)}=\frac{r^{(g)}-\bar{r}}{\sigma_{r}},\hskip 8.19447pt\text{where}\hskip 8.19447pt\bar{r}=\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{g^{\prime}=1}^{G}r^{(g^{\prime})},\hskip 8.19447pt\sigma_{r}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{g^{\prime}=1}^{G}(r^{(g^{\prime})}-\bar{r})^{2}}.

(2)

The GRPO objective is then:

\scriptsize\mathcal{J}^{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\!\Big(\rho_{t}^{(g)}\hat{A}^{(g)},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t}^{(g)},\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}^{(g)}\Big),

(3)

where
\rho_{t}^{(g)}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(g)}\mid x,y_{<t}^{(g)})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}^{(g)}\mid x,y_{<t}^{(g)})}
is the importance sampling ratio.
While GRPO reduces variance via group normalization, it applies a
uniform advantage

\hat{A}^{(g)}
to all tokens in a sequence
(
Samineni et al. 2025
)
, offering no mechanism to distinguish which tokens—or meaningful semantic segments—actually drove the final reward
r^{(g)}
.

3.1
Owen-Shapley Policy Optimization: Segment-Level Credit Assignment

The mismatch between GRPO’s token-level uniform advantages and the compositional structure of recommendation tasks motivates a shift in granularity. To resolve this, our approach,
OSPO
, leverages the structure of Owen “coalitions”
(
Owen 1977
)
—groups of segments or players in game theory—that compute what value
should
be assigned to each segment based on its
expected marginal contribution
to the total reward. Intuitively, this allows the policy
\pi_{\theta}
to learn which among the segments it generated actually led to the observed feedback. In other words, the policy learns counterfactually—reasoning over which parts of its response were relatively “more valuable” than others.

Specifically, let a generated response
y^{(g)}
from the policy
\pi_{\theta}
during group-based

sampling be decomposed into
N
semantically meaningful segments
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
, where each
a_{j}
denotes a textual span corresponding to a linguistically coherent unit—such spans may represent noun phrases, adjective-noun pairs, or verb-led expressions or even full sentences depending on the task (see
Section

A.7
for extraction details). For any coalition
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we define the
characteristic function

v(S)
as the reward obtained when only the segments indexed by
S
are present. To implement this, we construct a partial query
y_{S}
by concatenating the segments in coalition
S
while keeping the input context
x
fixed:
y_{S}=a_{i}\oplus a_{i+1}\oplus\cdots
for
i\in S
, where
\oplus
denotes string concatenation.

Classical Shapley values
(
Shapley 1953
)
assign to each segment
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
its average marginal contribution across all possible subsets:

\small\phi_{j}~=~\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!\,(N-|S|-1)!}{N!}\,\Big(v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\Big).

(4)

This formulation ensures key fairness properties—
efficiency
(
\sum_{j}\phi_{j}=v(\{1,\ldots,N\})
),
symmetry
, and
linearity
—but quickly becomes intractable as
N
grows, requiring evaluation of
2^{N}
coalitions. The Owen value
(
Owen 1977
)
extends Shapley’s framework by restricting the coalitions to a structured subset while preserving the core fairness guarantees
(
Owen 2014
)
. In our case, we impose a
contiguity constraint
: we only evaluate coalitions
S=\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+w-1\}
consisting of consecutive segments, where
w\leq w_{\max}
is a maximum coalition width hyperparameter. This restriction serves two purposes: (1) it reduces computational complexity from
O(2^{N})
to
O(N\cdot w_{\max})
, making online evaluation tractable during RL training, and (2) it preserves semantic coherence, as non-contiguous coalitions (e.g., skipping middle segments) would produce fragmented queries that may confound reward evaluation. We denote the resulting segment attributions as
\phi_{j}^{\text{Owen}}
, computed by averaging marginal contributions
v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)
only over contiguous coalitions
S
where
j\notin S
but
S\cup\{j\}
remains contiguous:

\small\phi_{j}^{\text{Owen}}~=~\frac{1}{|C_{j}|}\sum_{S\in C_{j}}\Big(v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\Big),

(5)

where
C_{j}=\{S:S\text{ contiguous},j\notin S,S\cup\{j\}\text{ contiguous}\}
is the set of applicable coalitions for segment
j
. The full coalition generation and sampling strategy, including how we manage computational budgets via sampling when
N
is large, is detailed in
Section

A.6
.

This coalitional view naturally aligns with the linguistic structure of LLM outputs in recommendation tasks, where contiguous spans—such as attribute–intent phrases (“blue midi dress”) or preference clauses (“prefers seasonal styles”)—form coherent compositional units. By assigning credit to these coalitions,
OSPO
captures how phrase combinations jointly influence downstream reward without enumerating all
2^{n}
subsets. Conceptually, this mirrors
options
or
temporal abstractions
in reinforcement learning
(
Sutton & Barto 2018
;
Konidaris & Barto 2009
;
Chai et al. 2025
)
, allowing more efficient exploration and faster credit propagation across a response.

From Segment Attributions to Token Advantages.

While Owen values
\phi_{j}^{\text{Owen}}
quantify segment-level contributions, we must bridge two granularity gaps to effectively use such fine-grained values in policy optimization. First, standard baseline-based advantage computation requires exploring multiple completions per prompt via Monte Carlo sampling
(
Williams 1992b
;
Kool et al. 2019
)
to estimate which responses are better than average—yielding per-sequence advantages
\hat{A}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}-\bar{R}
that indicate whether completion
g
should be reinforced or suppressed. Second, clipped surrogate objectives
(
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Yue et al. 2025
;
Shao et al. 2024a
)
operate at the ”token” level, requiring decomposition of sequence-level feedback into token-level signals. Owen values address
within-sequence
attribution—identifying which segments contributed to quality—but the policy must also learn from these
between-sequence
group-level advantages to effectively explore and distinguish better completions from worse ones.

To achieve both objectives, our core insight is
advantage redistribution
: instead of uniform assignment, we redistribute the per-sequence advantages
\hat{A}^{(g)}
across tokens, weighted by their Owen values as computed above. This strategy allows us to leverage group-based advantages for variance reduction
(
Williams 1992b
;
Kool et al. 2019
)
during exploration while learning fine-grained token attributions—overcoming the credit assignment problem without relying on an external value model. We construct a mapping
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T}
where
W_{it}
encodes
fraction of token span covered by the segment
a_{i}
and token
y_{t}
, projecting segment Owen values to token space:
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{tok}}=W^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\text{seg}}
. After normalizing to unit sum
\tilde{\phi}_{t}=\phi_{t}/\sum_{t^{\prime}}\phi_{t^{\prime}}
, a naive proportional redistribution
\tilde{A}_{t}=\hat{A}^{(g)}\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}
would create severe length bias
Liu et al. 2025
;
Yue et al. 2025
where shorter responses are rewarded more while longer incorrect responses go under-penalized. Since Owen values must sum to the total reward (due to the efficiency property), longer sequences experience systematic dilution where each token receives exponentially weaker gradient signals compared to shorter responses. To eliminate this bias, we multiply by sequence length:

A_{t}^{(g)}=T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}.

(6)

This ensures the average token advantage
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}
equals the original sequence-level advantage regardless of length (proof in Lemma
A.1
,
Section

A.1
of Appendix), eliminating the length-dependent training bias identified by
Liu et al. 2025
.

Substituting these
token-specific
advantages
A_{t}^{(g)}
into the GRPO objective (Eq.
3

\hat{A}^{(g)}
—yields the
OSPO objective

\mathcal{D}

\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
.

\scriptsize\mathcal{J}^{\text{OSPO}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\!\Big(\rho_{t}^{(g)}A_{t}^{(g)},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t}^{(g)},\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)A_{t}^{(g)}\Big),

(7)

where
A_{t}^{(g)}=T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}
are Owen-weighted token advantages (cf. Eq.
6

The key distinction from GRPO
: while GRPO assigns the same advantage
\hat{A}^{(g)}
to all tokens in sequence
g

\phi_{t}^{(g)}

Proportional vs. Rank-Based Redistribution.

Equation
6
uses Owen values directly as importance weights—we call this
OSPO-Prop
(proportional). We also explore
OSPO-Rank
, which converts Owen values to ordinal ranks before redistribution, providing robustness when estimates are noisy in long sequences. This spreads credit more uniformly according to relative importance rather than absolute magnitudes. In our experimental setup and results section, we focus on
OSPO-Prop
as our primary approach, using
OSPO-Rank
as an ablation study.

Rank-Based Owen Redistribution.

Instead of redistributing the group advantage
\hat{A}^{(g)}
proportionally to the normalized Owen values
\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}
, we map each token’s Owen value
\phi_{t}^{(g)}
to its ordinal rank position among all tokens in the sequence. Let
\text{rank}_{t}^{(g)}
denote the rank (1 = lowest,
T
= highest) of token
t
by its
\phi_{t}^{(g)}
value. The rank-based redistributed advantage is then defined as:

A_{t}^{(g)}=T\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}\cdot\frac{\text{rank}_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\text{rank}_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}.

(8)

This formulation preserves the same length-invariant property as Eq.
6
, ensuring that

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}
,
while redistributing credit more uniformly across tokens according to their relative importance rather than absolute Owen magnitudes. Intuitively,
OSPO-Rank
smooths noisy or highly skewed Owen attributions—useful when a few tokens dominate the proportional weights or when the estimated
\phi_{t}
values are unstable in long sequences.

Experimental Setup

In this section we outline our experimental settings including tasks/datasets, prompts, metrics, and implementation details.

We evaluate
OSPO
on two e-commerce tasks testing complementary aspects of advantage redistribution.
Product search query refinement
tests whether models can identify which tokens in expanded queries drive retrieval success—customers provide under-specified queries that LLMs expand with attributes, but standard RL methods assign uniform credit across all tokens despite unequal contributions to retrieval performance.
User profile summarization
tests whether advantage redistribution improves intermediate reasoning quality: models generate purchase history summaries before predicting next items, and since true preferences are latent, summary quality directly determines downstream accuracy. A key design choice: Owen values target the refined query for search tasks but the reasoning trace for summarization, assessing OSPO’s impact across different output types. We use two e-commerce datasets: ESCI shopping queries
(
Reddy et al. 2022
)
for conventional search, H&M Fashion Recommendations
(
Ling et al. 2022
)
for contextualized search and user summarization. Since H&M lacks explicit queries, we generate them using Claude Sonnet 3.0 from transaction data. Similar to prior work
(

Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced
Owen–Shapley Policy Optimization (OSPO)
, a reinforcement learning framework that directly tackles the credit assignment problem in language-driven recommendation systems. By decomposing sequence-level rewards into segment-level attributions through cooperative game theory,
OSPO
enables precise gradient updates without relying on auxiliary value networks. Across product search and user summarization tasks,
OSPO
consistently outperforms both online and offline baselines, achieving ranking performance comparable to models an order of magnitude larger. This efficiency arises from its attribution-driven optimization, which focuses updates on the sequence segments that meaningfully influence task outcomes. Even with unseen retrievers,
OSPO
preserves robust performance, showing that its alignment signal transfers across feedback models and representation spaces. Ablation studies further highlight the importance of its coalition structure: moderate-width, contiguous, and sufficiently sampled coalitions act as an intrinsic regularizer that stabilizes learning and improves generalization. In summary,
OSPO
learns not by memorizing feedback patterns but by leveraging
principled coalition-based attributions that promote stable, transferable, and interpretable behaviors
. Future work can extend these ideas to multi-turn, agentic environments and broader reasoning domains such as code generation, dialogue planning, and interactive decision-making.

Limitations.

OSPO
incurs additional computational overhead compared to standard policy-gradient methods, since estimating Owen values for advantage redistribution adds extra computation. While FAISS-based retrieval feedback keeps this cost manageable for product search, it becomes more noticeable when using slower reward models, as in user profile summarization. Still, the trade-off is favorable for precision-critical applications where
OSPO
achieves higher performance and efficiency than much larger models. Several practical challenges remain. Performance is sensitive to coalition width and sampling depth, making hyperparameter tuning crucial but challenging to the practitioner. Additionally, Monte Carlo approximations of Owen values during coalition sampling may underrepresent long-range dependencies between non-adjacent segments, and model stability can vary with feedback quality across domains. Finally, our evaluation spans only two recommendation settings and single-turn RL; extending
OSPO
to multi-turn, conversational, and multimodal environments remains promising future work.

More Details on OSPO Objectives

Lemma A.1

(Length-Invariant Owen Redistribution)
.

Let
y^{(g)}
be a generated sequence of length
T
from a Monte Carlo rollout with reward
R^{(g)}
. Following the REINFORCE algorithm
(
Williams 1992b
;
Kool et al. 2019
;
Shao et al. 2024a
)
of sampling rollouts without replacement with baseline subtraction, define the sequence-level advantage as:

\hat{A}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}-\bar{R},\quad\text{where }\bar{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g^{\prime}=1}^{G}R^{(g^{\prime})},

(9)

is the mean reward across
G
sampled rollouts. Let
\{\phi_{t}^{(g)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
denote Owen values satisfying the efficiency axiom
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}

(
Cheng-Guo et al. 2014
;
Owen 2014
)
. Define the normalized Owen values as
\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}=\phi_{t}^{(g)}/\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}
, and the redistributed token advantages as:

A_{t}^{(g)}=T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}.

(10)

Then the average token advantage equals the original sequence-level advantage, independent of sequence length:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}.

(11)

Proof.

To prove this, starting from the definition of the redistributed token advantages, we compute the average:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(T\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{T}\cdot\hat{A}^{(g)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}

(13)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{(g)}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}.

(14)

Now we evaluate the sum of normalized Owen values. By the definition of
\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\phi_{t^{\prime}}^{(g)}}

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}^{(g)}}

(18)

\displaystyle=1.

(19)

Substituting this result back into equation (3), we obtain:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}^{(g)}=\hat{A}^{(g)}\cdot 1=\hat{A}^{(g)}.

(20)

This equality holds for any sequence length
T>0
, proving that the redistribution scheme is length-invariant.
∎

Remark A.2
.

Lemma
A.1
guarantees three critical properties for OSPO’s credit assignment:

1.

Scale Preservation:
The average token advantage matches the original sequence-level advantage
\hat{A}^{(g)}=R^{(g)}-\bar{R}
, ensuring gradient magnitudes in
Equation

7
remain comparable across sequences of different lengths.

2.

Length Invariance:
A sequence’s total gradient contribution depends on its advantage
\hat{A}^{(g)}
and Owen-based credit distribution
\{\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{(g)}\}
, not its verbosity. This eliminates the length bias where longer sequences (e.g., detailed user summaries with
T>100
) would otherwise receive disproportionately small gradient signals when Owen values are naively redistributed.

3.

Credit Prioritization:
High-value tokens (e.g., product-specific attributes) receive proportionally more advantage than low-value tokens (e.g., filler words or inconsistent reasoning), while maintaining consistency with the per-token policy gradient formulation. Specifically, if
\tilde{\phi}_{i}>\tilde{\phi}_{j}
, then
A_{i}^{(g)}>A_{j}^{(g)}
, preserving the ordinal ranking of token contributions.

Remark A.3

(Connection to the Efficiency Axiom)
.

The normalization step
\tilde{\phi}_{t}=\phi_{t}/\sum_{t^{\prime}}\phi_{t^{\prime}}
is essential because Owen values satisfy the efficiency axiom:
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\phi_{t}=R^{(g)}
(the terminal reward). Without the length multiplication factor
T
, a naive proportional redistribution
\tilde{A}_{t}=\hat{A}^{(g)}\cdot\tilde{\phi}_{t}
would yield an average advantage of:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{A}_{t}=\frac{\hat{A}^{(g)}}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\phi}_{t}=\frac{\hat{A}^{(g)}}{T},

(21)

introducing a
1/T
dilution effect where longer sequences receive significantly weaker gradient signals. The multiplication by
T
precisely counteracts this dilution, ensuring that gradient magnitude depends on the quality of the generation (via
\hat{A}^{(g)}
) rather than its length.

A.2
Length-Invariant Credit Redistribution for GRPO

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024a
)
computes sequence-level advantages by normalizing rewards within a sampled group, but assigns this advantage uniformly to all tokens in the sequence. This uniform assignment creates a fundamental tension: when combined with the standard per-token gradient normalization
\frac{1}{|y|}
, GRPO exhibits length-dependent bias where positive advantages produce stronger gradients for shorter sequences, while negative advantages under-penalize verbose failures
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
. This bias is particularly problematic in recommendation tasks where response length varies significantly—from terse product queries to detailed user preference summaries.

Our approach leverages Owen-Shapley values to redistribute the sequence-level GRPO advantage to individual tokens while explicitly eliminating length bias. Given a sequence
y
with GRPO advantage
\hat{A}
and Owen values
\{\Phi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{|y|}
(obtained via the efficient approximation in
Section

3.1
), we face a redistribution challenge. A naive proportional approach would compute token advantages as
\tilde{A}_{t}=\hat{A}\cdot\frac{\Phi_{t}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\Phi_{t^{\prime}}}
. However, since Owen values satisfy the efficiency axiom (
\sum_{t}\Phi_{t}=R
, where
R
is the terminal reward), this denominator equals the reward
R
, causing per-token advantages to scale as
\hat{A}/R
. For sequences with correlated advantages and rewards, this creates the same length bias as vanilla GRPO, where the average token advantage
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\tilde{A}_{t}\propto\frac{1}{|y|}
.

To eliminate this dependency, we propose
two
redistribution strategies that multiply the proportional allocation by sequence length
|y|
, ensuring the average token advantage equals the original GRPO advantage regardless of sequence length. This length normalization is critical: it decouples gradient magnitude from verbosity while preserving Owen-based prioritization of important tokens.

Proportional Owen Redistribution.

Our first approach uses Owen values directly as importance weights:

\tilde{A}_{t}^{\text{prop}}=\hat{A}\cdot\frac{\Phi_{t}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\Phi_{t^{\prime}}}\cdot|y|

(22)

This strategy assigns credit proportional to each token’s marginal contribution, as quantified by its Owen value. Tokens in high-value
6
6

6

We assume that we have access to a reasonably efficient and realistic retrieval system that is more likely pull up relevant articles for product-specific attributes, while lowering those of filler words.
phrases (e.g., “noise cancelling” in a headphone query) receive proportionally more advantage than tokens in low-value phrases (e.g., filler words). The length multiplication ensures that
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\tilde{A}_{t}^{\text{prop}}=\hat{A}
, making the average token advantage independent of sequence length. Our experiments in product search tasks suggests that this redistribution is particularly effective when Owen values accurately reflect token importance—typically in short sequences with clear, attributable contributions.

Rank-Based Owen Redistribution.

Our second approach converts Owen values to ordinal ranks before redistribution:

\text{rank}_{t}=\text{argsort}(\{\Phi_{t^{\prime}}\}),\quad\tilde{A}_{t}^{\text{rank}}=\hat{A}\cdot\frac{\text{rank}_{t}}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\text{rank}_{t^{\prime}}}\cdot|y|

(23)

This rank transformation acts as a form of regularization on the Owen-based credit assignment. While proportional redistribution can concentrate credit heavily on a few high-value tokens (e.g., one token receiving 80% of the advantage), rank-based redistribution spreads credit more uniformly according to relative importance order. For a sequence of length
N
, the top-ranked token receives weight
\frac{N-1}{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}k}=\frac{2}{N}
instead of a potentially much larger proportion. This uniform spreading is advantageous in two scenarios: (1) when Owen value estimates are noisy due to complex token interactions in long sequences, the ordinal transformation filters magnitude errors while preserving relative importance; (2) when task success requires multiple complementary components (e.g., multiple attributes in a product query), rank-based redistribution prevents over-concentration on a single dominant feature and encourages the model to improve all relevant tokens.

Both strategies maintain the theoretical guarantees of potential-based reward shaping
(
Ng et al. 1999
)
. The choice between proportional and rank-based redistribution depends on the task structure: proportional excels when token contributions are clearly separable and accurately measurable, while rank-based provides robustness when contributions are compositional and interdependent.

A.3
Expert Data Generation

Unlike ESCI
(
Reddy et al. 2022
)
, the H&M Fashion Recommendation dataset
(
Ling et al. 2022
)
lacks explicit customer queries—crucial for evaluating search-oriented LLMs. To ensure a fair comparison of
OSPO
with SFT, preference-tuning, and RL baselines (e.g., GRPO), we conduct an
expert data generation phase
using
Claude Sonnet 3.0
. The expert model synthesizes missing annotations from sampled customer profiles drawn from rich transactional histories. Specifically, it produces
(i)
next-purchase predictions and preference-grounded summaries for the
user summarization
task, and
(ii)
naturalistic, history-conditioned queries for the
contextualized product search
task (see
Figure

6
). The full pipeline proceeds as follows.

Customer Data Preparation.

We construct a sequential recommendation dataset by segmenting each customer’s purchase history into temporally ordered windows. A customer is retained if they have at least five prior and four subsequent purchases, providing adequate temporal context while including light users. To maintain computational efficiency, we subsample a fixed number of eligible customers, yielding a balanced and diverse training cohort.

Expert Data Generation.

Following prior work on RL-based sequential recommendation
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
, we generate
expert user summaries
that compress behavioral histories into concise representations. Because no ground truth exists for such summaries or reasoning trails, the expert LLM produces Chain-of-Thought (CoT) explanations grounded in observed behavior—capturing both short- and long-term preferences. Unlike
Wu et al. 2025
, which predicts only the next item, our expert forecasts entire future sequences, pruning implausible candidates while reasoning over temporal dependencies. These CoT traces form structured summaries encoding customer intent and preference dynamics.

SFT and DPO Trajectories for User Profile Summarization.

From the generated expert data, we construct SFT and DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023a
)
training sets based on the expert’s performance in sequential purchase prediction, where future items are ranked in temporal order. To ensure data quality, we retain only samples where the expert’s NDCG exceeds a threshold
T=0.5
, tuned empirically. These high-performing traces constitute the SFT data. For DPO, we obtain contrastive pairs by sampling two reasoning traces per example—keeping the higher-NDCG trace as the “winning” response and the lower one as the “losing” response. This consistent expert-driven generation enables fair comparative analysis across SFT, DPO, and RL-based methods trained under identical supervision signals.

Contextualized Query Generation

For the contextualized product search task, we prompt the same expert model to generate realistic, user-grounded fashion queries conditioned on each customer’s purchase history and item context. Using the role-based prompt in
Figure

6
, the expert produces diverse, natural-language queries that mimic how real users express shopping intent—balancing explicit style and brand references with implicit preferences, seasonal cues, and underspecified needs. Each generated query is paired with a detailed item rationale, enabling the resulting dataset to capture nuanced intent representations crucial for training and evaluating OSPO on fashion-specific retrieval tasks.

Baselines.

All trainable baselines—

Supervised Finetuning (SFT)

To stabilize RL training during product search tasks, we sample query-specific candidate pools that densify reward signals and are shared across GRPO and

A.6
Coalition Formation and Sampling Strategy

Our approach to computing Owen-Shapley
(
Owen 1977
;
Owen 2014
)
attributions requires evaluating the marginal contribution of each segment across multiple coalitions. We now detail how we construct these coalitions and manage the computational trade-offs inherent in this process.

Contiguous Coalition Generation.

Given a response
y^{(g)}
sampled from the policy
\pi_{\theta}
during
OSPO
training, we decompose it into
N
segments
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
via linguistic phrase extraction for product search tasks (where segments are clauses) or regex-based sentence splitting for user summary generation tasks. We then generate contiguous coalitions—subsets of consecutive segments that preserve the sequential structure of the response. Formally, a coalition
S
is
contiguous
if
S=\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+w-1\}
for some starting position
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and width
w\in\{1,\ldots,\min(w_{\max},N)\}
. This contiguity constraint ensures that coalition-based partial segments
y_{S}
remain semantically coherent—a critical requirement for meaningful reward evaluation in our tasks.

The complete set of contiguous coalitions
\mathcal{C}
includes:

•

The
empty coalition

\emptyset
, representing no query (baseline reward
8
8

8

In practice, for the user summary generation task—where the reward function
r(x,y_{S_{m}})
depends on the context
x
—we allow for negative baseline rewards. Note that rewards for search tasks that are assigned by a retriever acting as a reward model only depend on the partial query
y_{S_{m}}
and not on the context.
of 0)

•

All
contiguous windows
of width
w=1,\ldots,\min(w_{\max},N)

•

The
full coalition

\{1,\ldots,N\}
, representing the complete response

The total number of possible contiguous coalitions is:

|\mathcal{C}|=1+\sum_{w=1}^{\min(w_{\max},N)}(N-w+1)

(24)

where the summation counts all starting positions for each width
w
, and the leading
1
accounts for the empty coalition. For example, with
N=5
segments and
w_{\max}=3
:

•

Width 1:
\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\},\{5\}
(5 coalitions)

•

Width 2:
\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,4\},\{4,5\}
(4 coalitions)

•

Width 3:
\{1,2,3\},\{2,3,4\},\{3,4,5\}
(3 coalitions)

yielding
|\mathcal{C}|=1+5+4+3=13
total coalitions including the empty and full coalitions.

Computational Budget and Sampling.

Computing rewards for all coalitions becomes expensive and computationally intractable as
N
grows, since
|\mathcal{C}|=O(N\cdot\min(w_{\max},N))
. To maintain tractability, we impose a sampling budget
M
(see
Algorithm

1
) that caps the number of coalitions evaluated per response. The actual number of sampled coalitions is:

k=\min(M,|\mathcal{C}|)

(25)

When
|\mathcal{C}|\leq M
, we evaluate all possible coalitions, providing exact Owen-Shapley values. When
|\mathcal{C}|>M
, we sample
M
coalitions using the following strategy:

1.

Anchor coalitions
: Always retain
\emptyset
and
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, as these provide essential boundary conditions for marginal contribution estimates

2.

Random sampling
: Uniformly sample
M-2
coalitions from the remaining
|\mathcal{C}|-2
candidates

This sampling strategy ensures that our Owen value estimates remain well-calibrated: the empty coalition anchors the baseline, while the full coalition captures the terminal reward. The intermediate coalitions provide estimates of each segment’s contribution across diverse contexts.

Coalition Evaluation.

For each sampled coalition
S_{m}\in\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
, we construct the partial query
y_{S_{m}}
by concatenating the corresponding segments in order:

y_{S_{m}}=a_{i}\oplus a_{i+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{i+w-1}\quad\text{where }S_{m}=\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+w-1\}

(26)

and evaluate its coalition value
v(S_{m})=r(x,y_{S_{m}})
using our reward function. In our search task, this involves issuing
y_{S_{m}}
as a query to the retrieval system and computing the NDCG@1000 score against the target item(s). The empty coalition receives
v(\emptyset)=0
by convention, reflecting the absence of information.

Contiguity Rationale.

Our choice to restrict coalitions to contiguous subsets—rather than exploring all
2^{N}
possible subsets—is motivated by both computational efficiency and semantic coherence. Non-contiguous coalitions (e.g.,
\{1,3,5\}
skipping segments 2 and 4) would produce fragmented queries that (a) require
O(2^{N})
evaluations, and (b) may yield semantically incoherent text, confounding reward evaluation and possibly lead to performance degradation due to the loss of local structure and high-variance Owen value estimates. Contiguity preserves the narrative flow of the response while reducing complexity to
O(N\cdot w_{\max})
, making Owen value estimation tractable for online RL training. In fact, our ablation study on coalition sampling in
Section

A.7
suggests that non-contiguity in sampling can lead to a significant performance drop in conventional product search task on ESCI dataset.

As summarized in Table

and

w1
,
w2

w12
,
w16
) underutilize the sampling budget given typical query lengths.

)

A.8
Coalition Width and Learning Dynamics

Our ablation reveals a non-monotonic relationship between coalition width and generalization performance, with distinct learning dynamics emerging across the spectrum.

A.8.1
Small Coalitions: Fast Learning, Early Collapse

Narrow coalition widths (w2_p64) exhibit rapid initial learning, achieving NDCG 0.75 by step 200, but subsequently degrade catastrophically to 0.44 by step 1000. This pattern reflects a fundamental trade-off: smaller coalitions converge quickly by identifying surface-level patterns (e.g., “black jeans” or “high waist” as isolated features), but lack the compositional structure needed to capture how multiple preference attributes interact.

The degradation after step 600 suggests that width-2 coalitions introduce noise into the credit assignment signal. When evaluating coalitions containing only 1–2 consecutive phrases, marginal contribution estimates become unstable—a single phrase’s Owen value fluctuates depending on which adjacent phrase appears in the coalition, without sufficient context to disambiguate genuine causal effects from spurious correlations. This instability causes the model to oscillate between competing hypotheses about which tokens drive performance, ultimately converging to a suboptimal solution that fails to generalize.

A.8.2
Very Wide Coalitions: Underutilization Due to Query Length

Configurations with maximum width (w16_p256) fail to outperform more moderate settings despite their theoretical expressiveness. Analysis of generated queries during training reveals the constraint:
most queries contain only 6–10 segmented phrases
, making width-16 coalitions impossible to construct. Even when the maximum permutation limit is set to 256, we observe that only approximately 41–48 unique coalitions are actually sampled per query, as the contiguous constraint combined with limited phrase count restricts the coalition space.

This underutilization has two consequences. First, the model cannot leverage the fine-grained credit assignment that wider coalitions theoretically enable—there simply aren’t enough phrases to form them. Second, the high permutation budget (256) is wasted on resampling similar small coalitions, rather than exploring diverse coalition structures. As a result, w16_p256 performs no better than more efficient configurations with moderate widths, demonstrating that coalition strategy must account for the
actual
query structure distribution, not just the maximum possible width.

A.8.3
Moderate-Wide Coalitions: Delayed but Stable Convergence

The w12_p128 configuration demonstrates a distinct learning profile: slower initial learning compared to small coalitions, but convergence to a stable solution with sustained performance. This configuration peaks around step 700 (NDCG 0.71) and maintains reasonable performance through step 1000, suggesting that width-12 coalitions provide sufficient compositional range for the task without exceeding typical query lengths.

However, w12_p128 exhibits late-training instability (declining from 0.71 to 0.55 between steps 700–1000), indicating potential overfitting to the coalition structure itself. With 128 sampled coalitions per query, the model may learn spurious correlations specific to the training coalition distribution rather than robust compositional patterns.

A.8.4
Optimal Range: w4_p48 as the Goldilocks Configuration

The configuration with width
W=4
and
P=48
permutations achieves the best final performance (NDCG 0.71) through a qualitatively different learning trajectory. Unlike narrow coalitions that peak early or wide coalitions that show volatility, w4_p48 exhibits
gradual, monotonic improvement
throughout training, with its steepest gains occurring in the final 400 steps (0.63 at step 600
\rightarrow
0.71 at step 1000).

This delayed convergence reflects a more principled learning process. Width-4 coalitions can capture meaningful multi-attribute patterns (e.g., “black skinny jeans + stiff fabric”) without requiring query lengths that rarely occur. The moderate permutation count (48) provides sufficient diversity for robust Owen value estimation without oversampling, avoiding both the noise of w2_p64 and the overfitting risk of w12_p128.

Critically, w4_p48’s continued improvement in late training—when other configurations plateau or degrade—suggests it has not converged to a local optimum exploiting training-specific patterns. Instead, the gradual ascent indicates the model is progressively refining its understanding of compositional query semantics, leading to solutions that generalize to novel test cases.

A.8.5
The Role of Coalition Noise

The dramatic failure of w2_p64 (collapsing from 0.75 to 0.44) and instability of w1_p16 validate a key intuition:
coalition width acts as a regularization parameter
. Excessively narrow coalitions introduce noise by providing insufficient context for stable marginal contribution estimates. When Owen values are computed from coalitions containing only 1–2 tokens, the credit signal becomes dominated by local interactions rather than compositional semantics.

This noise has a delayed effect—early training benefits from rapid exploitation of surface patterns, but as training progresses, the accumulation of noisy credit assignments leads the model to overweight spurious features. By contrast, width-4 coalitions filter this noise by requiring evaluation contexts large enough to disambiguate genuine compositional effects from random co-occurrences, leading to more stable and generalizable learning.

A.9
Design Implications

These findings establish coalition width as a
critical hyperparameter
for OSPO, with optimal values determined by task-specific query structure:

•

Task constraint
: Coalition width must match typical query complexity. For product search with 6–10 segmented phrases,
W\in\{4,6,8\}
provides the sweet spot.

•

Noise-stability trade-off
: Widths
<3
introduce excessive noise; widths
>12
waste capacity on unattainable coalitions.

•

Convergence profile
: Moderate widths (w4) show delayed but stable learning, while extremes (w2, w16) either collapse or plateau prematurely.

•

Permutation budget
: Should scale with achievable coalition diversity, not just theoretical maximum. For width-4 with 8 phrases,
P=48
suffices;
P=256
provides no additional benefit.

The non-monotonic relationship between coalition width and performance—where both too-narrow and too-wide configurations underperform—demonstrates that fine-grained credit assignment in OSPO requires careful calibration to the task’s semantic structure.

Coalition Reward Patterns.

To understand how coalition composition affects retrieval performance, we examine individual coalition-level rewards during training.
Successful coalitions (non-zero rewards) consistently combine three key elements: concrete product identifiers (e.g., jeans”, coat”, dress”), specific attributes (e.g., mid-blue wash”, high-waist”, ankle length”), and customer intent or use-case context (e.g., weekend outing”, beach vacation”).
For example, the coalition
your current wardrobe various situations cooler spring days a rich blue hue shorter length neutral colors a coat”
achieves perfect reward (1.0) by balancing contextual framing with discriminative product features. In contrast, coalitions receiving zero reward fall into predictable failure modes: generic phrases without product anchors (a pair”, modern fit”), pure adjective sequences lacking concrete nouns (sleek polished clean”), junk phrase contamination from parsing artifacts (- wash”, a step”), or excessive repetition and incomplete fragments (few lightweight… lightweight… a touch”). Notably, even coalitions containing strong individual phrases fail when dominated by vague descriptors; for instance,
prefer a vibrant color to add my usual neutral tones my beach vacation some personality”
receives zero reward despite capturing user preferences, because it lacks a specific product type or actionable attributes.
This analysis suggests that moderate coalition widths ((w=4
–
6)) succeed by maintaining sufficient phrase diversity to construct discriminative queries while avoiding the accumulation of noisy fragments that degrade semantic coherence in wider configurations.

Together, these findings suggest that
OSPO-Prop
achieves optimal balance at moderate coalition configurations
(
w{=}4\text{--}6,~p{=}24\text{--}64
),
where the phrase context is sufficiently rich to capture meaningful dependencies but constrained enough to avoid semantic drift.
The corresponding low zero-reward rates and stable token lengths further confirm that controlled coalition sampling preserves dense and informative gradients, leading to robust policy learning and higher downstream ranking quality.
