Simple cubic variance functions in ℝ n , \mathbb{R}^{n}, Part one
Abdelhamid Hassairi, Gérard Letac

Abstract
The classification of natural exponential families started with the paper [ 15 ] where Carl Morris unifies six very familiar families by the fact that their variance functions are polynomials of degree less or equal to two. Extension of this classification to ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} and to degree three is the subject of this paper. Keywords : Actions of the group G ​ L ​ ( n + 1 , ℝ ) GL(n+1,\mathbb{R}) , classification of natural exponential families, multivariate Lagrange formula. variance functions.

Introduction

1.1
Description

Typically, a natural exponential family
F
is characterized by a convex function
k_{F}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

which is the logarithm of the Laplace transform of a generating measure of
F
. This function
k_{F}
is called a cumulant of
F
. All the paper is based on the following idea: if we move the representative surface of
k_{F}
we get locally the representative surface of a new convex function:. For instance, if
n=1
and if we move the parabola
y=x^{2}
by the
90
degrees rotation, we get the new convex function

y=-\sqrt{-x}.
If this new convex function is a cumulant function
k_{F_{1}}
we have created a new exponential family
F_{1}
with deep links with
F
, obtaining a powerful tool for the classification of the natural exponential families.

This paper considers the set of natural exponential families on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that one can identify to the set of their covariance matrix functions. The group
G=GL(\mathbb{R},n+1)
is operating on this set, in a natural way described in Section 3.

The group
G
has a subgroup
G_{0}
, defined in (
4
), containing the group of affinities on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
plus an extra parameter that one can interpret as the Jorgensen one. For instance, in one dimension, the set
\mathcal{P}_{2}
of quadratic variance functions has been splitted by Morris
[
15
]
into six
G_{0}
-orbits. However this set
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is not preserved by
G
while it is the case of the set
\mathcal{P}_{3}
of cubic variance functions which is splitted into four
G
-orbits.

If
F
is a natural exponential family in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the covariance matrix
V_{F}(m)
of the element of
F
of mean
m
defines the variance function
V_{F}
of
F.
The set
\mathcal{P}_{2}(n)
is the set of variance functions for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that all entries are polynomials in
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
of total degree
\leq 2.
In other terms
V_{F}(m)=A(m)+B(m)+C
where
C
is a constant matrix,
B
is linear and
A
is quadratic. The description of the elements of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(n)
is an open problem, but if we consider the subset
\mathcal{SP}_{2}(n)
of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(n)
such that
A=amm^{T}
has rank one or zero, Muriel Casalis
[
4
]
has shown that it can be splitted into
2n+4

G_{0}
-orbits and has described them. Here again, this set
\mathcal{SP}_{2}(n)
is not preserved by
G.

We therefore denote by
\mathcal{SP}_{3}(n)
its image by
G
. It is a set of matrices
V(m)
whose entries are polynomials of degree
\leq 3.
The elements of
\mathcal{SP}_{2}(n)
and
\mathcal{SP}_{3}(n)
are respectively called the simple quadratic and simple cubic variance functions.

The article is the first of a two parts work for the following program: to describe the
n+3

G
-orbits of
\mathcal{SP}_{3}(n)
, meeting many more or less known distributions of the literature.
This present Part one constructs only the tools of the realization of this program: essentially the properties of the
T_{g}
transformation with
g
in
G
in Section 3, the splitting in
n+3
orbits in Sections 4 and 5, the particular role of the exponential families on
\mathbb{N}^{n}
(with
\mathbb{N}
being the set on nonegative integers) in the classification with the help of Lagrange formula for several dimensions in Section 6, and a powerful extension of a result of Philippe Rouquès for computing explicitely the densities of the transformations by a certain subgroup
H
of
G
in Section 7.

1.3
Basics for natural exponential families.

The linear space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is considered as a Euclidean space with its canonical scalar product. Its elements are column matrices of height
n
and the scalar product between
c
and
x
can be denoted in two ways:
c^{T}x
or
\langle c,x\rangle
, depending on the readability of formulas.

Given a positive measure
\mu
on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
its Laplace transform is defined by

L_{\mu}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\langle\theta,x\rangle}\mu(dx)\leq\infty.

The Hölder inequality implies that the set
D(\mu)=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n};L_{\mu}(\theta)<\infty\}
is convex and that

k_{\mu}=\log L_{\mu}
defines a convex function on
D(\mu).
In the sequel we consider the set
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of measures such that the interior
\Theta(\mu)
of
D(\mu)
is not empty and such that
\mu
is not concentrated on an affine hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
it is standard to prove that
k_{\mu}
is strictly convex and real-analytic on
\Theta(\mu).
In general, a real function
k
defined on an open convex set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a cumulant if there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\Theta\subset\Theta(\mu)
and
k=k_{\mu}
on
\Theta.
Let us insist on the fact that in this definition,
\mu
is not necessarily a probability and can be unbounded.

A
\mu
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
generates for
\theta\in\Theta(\mu)
the following probability on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

P(\theta,\mu)(dx)=e^{\langle\theta,x\rangle-k_{\mu}(\theta)}\mu(dx)

while the set
F(\mu)=\{P(\theta,\mu)\ ;\theta\in\Theta(\mu)\}
is called the natural exponential family (NEF) generated by
\mu
. Along the paper when we will speak of exponential families, we mean NEF. The general exponential families of the form
e^{\langle a(\theta),t(w)\rangle-c(\theta)}\nu(dw)
will not concern us here. Note that
F(\mu)=F(\mu_{1})
if and only if there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a real number
b
such that
\mu_{1}(dx)=e^{\langle a,x\rangle+b}\mu(dx).
The set of such
\mu_{1}
is the set of generators of
F=F(\mu)
, and in particular any element of
F
is a generator.
From the strict convexity and real-analyticity of
k_{\mu},
the map
\theta\mapsto m=k^{\prime}_{\mu}(\theta)
from
\Theta(\mu)
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a diffeomorphism of
\Theta(\mu)
onto its image
M_{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Because
m=k^{\prime}_{\mu}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xP(\theta,\mu)(dx),
the set
M_{F}
is called the domain of the means of
F
. If
C_{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the closed convex support of
\mu
then
M_{F}
is a connected open set contained in the interior
C^{o}_{F}
of
C_{F}
. In many cases
M_{F}=C^{o}_{F}
, and we say that
F
is steep. A sufficient condition of steepness is regularity, that means that
D(\mu)
is open (see Barndorff- Nielsen
[
2
]
for a proof). In general, the problems raised by the extension of
P(\theta,\mu)
to
\theta\in D(\mu)\setminus{\Theta(\mu)},
although they have an important place is literature (see Chentsov
[
5
]
or Csiszár and Matus
[
6
]
), will not concern us either. The monograph
[
12
]
is representative of our point of view on NEF.

The covariance matrix of
P(\theta,\mu)
is the Hessian matrix
k^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(\theta).
The inverse
m\mapsto\theta=\psi_{\mu}(m)
of the diffeomorphism
k_{\mu}^{\prime}(\theta)
maps
M_{F}
onto
\Theta(\mu).
Therefore

V_{F}(m)=k^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(\psi_{\mu}(m))

is the covariance matrix of the unique element of
F
of mean
m
. The map
m\mapsto V_{F}(m)
is called the variance function of
F
. Since

k^{\prime}_{\mu}(\psi_{\mu}(m))=m,
by derivation we get

\psi^{\prime}_{\mu}(m)k^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(\psi_{\mu}(m))=I_{n},
which implies

V_{F}(m)=(\psi^{\prime}_{\mu}(m))^{-1}.

This formula shows that the knowledge of
(V_{F},M_{F})
gives the knowledge of
F:
indeed
\psi^{\prime}_{\mu}(m)=\psi^{\prime}_{\mu_{1}}(m)
imply the existence of
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
\mu_{1}(dx)=e^{\langle a,x\rangle+b}\mu(dx).

A last important object about a
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is its Jorgensen set
\Lambda(\mu).
It is defined as the set of
\lambda>0
such that there exists
\mu_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the following property: for all
\theta\in\Theta(\mu)
we have

L_{\mu_{\lambda}}(\theta)=(L_{\mu}(\theta))^{\lambda}.

A simple example is given by the Bernoulli distribution
\Lambda(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1})=\{1,2,\ldots\}.

In general
\Lambda(\mu)
is an additive semigroup of
(0,\infty)
whose structure can be quite complex (see for instance Letac, Malouche and Maurer
[
13
]
). The particular case
\Lambda(\mu)=(0,\infty)
is important: we say in this case that
\mu
is infinitely divisible, and hence
P(\theta,\mu)
is an infinitely divisible probability in the ordinary sense. For instance
\mu(dx)=1_{(0,\infty)}(x)dx
is infinitely divisible since
\mu_{\lambda}(dx)=1_{(0,\infty)}(x)\frac{x^{\lambda-1}}{\Gamma(\lambda)}dx
is in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
for any
\lambda>0.

If
F=F(\mu)
we denote
F_{\lambda}=F(\mu_{\lambda})
since
\mu_{1}(dx)=e^{\langle a,x\rangle+b}\mu(dx)
implies
(L_{\mu_{1}}(\theta))^{\lambda}=e^{\lambda b}L_{\mu_{\lambda}}(\theta+a).
One easily sees that
M_{F_{\lambda}}=\lambda M_{F}
. We have now the important formula for
\lambda\in\Lambda(\mu)
and
m\in M_{F_{\lambda}}

V_{F_{\lambda}}(m)=\lambda V_{F}(\frac{m}{\lambda})

(1)

A similar formula is obtained when considering an affine isomorphism
T
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
x\mapsto A(x)+b
where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A
is a non singular
d\times d
matrix. Denoting by
TF
the set of the images of the elements of
F
by
T
,
clearly
TF
is a NEF with
M_{TF}=TM_{F}
and we have for all
m\in M_{TF}
the equality

V_{TF}(m)=A^{T}V_{F}(A^{-1}(m-b))A.

(2)

2.2
Moving the graph of a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

The reader could follow the transformation of a convex fonction below with the help of the two examples given for
n=1
in Section 2.3.

Consider a strictly convex and real-analytic function
k
defined on an open convex set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a linear isomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
defined by the non singular square matrix
g
of order
n+1
written by blocks

g=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&b\\
c^{T}&d\end{array}\right].

where
A
is a
n\times n
matrix,
b,c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
d\in\mathbb{R}.

In other terms we have for
x,x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
y_{1}\end{array}\right)=g\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&b\\
c^{T}&d\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)=g^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
y_{1}\end{array}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{1}&b_{1}\\
c_{1}^{T}&d_{1}\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
y_{1}\end{array}\right).

The graph of
k
is the set

E=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ ;x\in\Theta,\ y=k(x)\}.

After transformation by
g
it becomes the set

E_{1}=\{(x_{1},y_{1})\ ;c_{1}^{T}x_{1}+d_{1}y_{1}=k(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}y_{1})\}

Denote by
\Theta_{1}
the interior of the image of
E_{1}
by the projection
(x_{1},y_{1})\mapsto x_{1}.

Suppose now that
y_{1}=f(x_{1})
is a real-analytic function defined on an open subset
U
of
\Theta_{1}
such that its graph is contained in
E_{1}
. This last condition is saying that for
x_{1}\in U
we have

c_{1}^{T}x_{1}+d_{1}f(x_{1})=k(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))

Taking the second differential of both sides of the above equation implies the equality of the two quadratic forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are
h\mapsto d_{1}h^{T}f^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})h
and

h^{T}(A_{1}^{T}+f^{\prime}(x_{1})b_{1}^{T})k^{\prime\prime}(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))(A_{1}+b_{1}f^{\prime}(x_{1})^{T})h+b_{1}^{T}k^{\prime\prime}(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))b_{1}\times h^{T}f^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})h

From the implicit function theorem,
f^{\prime}
is real-analytic and is zero on a connected open set
V
if and only if
f^{\prime}\equiv 0.
This would imply that
f
is linear on
V
which contradicts the fact that
k
is strictly convex. Therefore
A_{1}+b_{1}f^{\prime}(x_{1})^{T}
is non zero on some open set
V\subset U.

Because
k
is strictly convex
k^{\prime\prime}(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))
is positive definite as well as
(A_{1}+b_{1}\otimes f^{\prime}(x_{1}))^{T}k^{\prime\prime}(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))(A_{1}+b_{1}f^{\prime}(x_{1})^{T}).

As a consequence if

s(x_{1})=d_{1}-k^{\prime\prime}(A_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}f(x_{1}))b_{1}^{T}

then the matrix
s(x_{1})f^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})
is positive definite on
V
.
Therefore if
U_{1}\subset V
is a convex open set such that

s(x_{1})>0

for
x_{1}\in U_{1}
, we have
f
strictly convex on
U_{1}.

This condition
s(x_{1})>0
for all
x_{1}\in U_{1}
is quite important and is in fact equivalent to assume that
f
is strictly convex on
U
. Actually if
g
is the simple symmetry

g=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{n}&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right]

f=-k
is concave and of no interest for our purposes. Indeed we intend to use this construction by
g
of a new convex function
f(x_{1})
from an old one
k(x)
when
k(\theta)=k_{\mu}(\theta)
is a cumulant function in order to obtain sometimes a new cumulant function
f(\theta_{1})=k_{\mu_{1}}(\theta_{1}).
We say ’sometimes’ since this is not always the case: for instance, consider for
n=1
the cumulant function of the ordinary Bernoulli distribution
k(\theta)=\log{\frac{1+e^{\theta}}{2}}
and the symmetry

g=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]

leading to

Clearly

while convex is not a cumulant function since

is the Laplace transform of the non positive measure

3
The actions of
G
and
G_{0}
on the exponential families

In this section we explain how an element of
G_{0}
and
G
may transform
an exponential family
F
into another one. This action can be seen in two ways: either by acting on the cumulant function of a generating measure of
F
, or by acting directly on the variance function of
F.
The word ’action’ should not been quite taken in the sense of group theory, since it is an action on functions (cumulant or variance), but
as we have seen in Section 2, this transformation does not always yield the cumulant of a positive measure, or similarly a function which is a variance function. The action on the variance is simpler to visualize, but ignores the hard work of the interpretation of the new variance by its Laplace transform and by a generating measure. The action on variance functions is described in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, the action on the cumulant functions is described in Section 3.4.

3.1
The homography associated to an element of
G

Given
g=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A&b\\
c^{T}&d\end{array}\right]
in
G
we consider the map
x\mapsto h_{g}(x)
from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into itself (called homography) defined by

h_{g}(x)=\frac{Ax+b}{c^{T}x+d}

(9)

Since this does not make sense for
x
in the hyperplane
H_{g}=\{x;\ c^{T}x+d=0\}
we mention that a proper way to deal with this transformation would be to inbed
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in its natural projective space. However it will not be really useful for our purposes: we will simply avoid to use
h_{g}
on the hyperplane
H_{g}.
A simple calculation shows that

h_{g_{1}}\circ h_{g}=h_{g_{1}g}.

(10)

Proposition 3.1.
For
x\not\in H_{g}
, the differential
h^{\prime}_{g}(x)
is the endomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equal to

\Delta\mapsto h^{\prime}_{g}(x)(\Delta)=\frac{1}{(c^{T}x+d)^{2}}[(c^{T}x+d)A\Delta-(Ax+b)c^{T}\Delta]

and it is non singular. Written as a square matrix of order
n
we have

h^{\prime}_{g}(x)=\frac{1}{(c^{T}x+d)^{2}}[(c^{T}x+d)A-(Ax+b)c^{T}]

In particular

h^{\prime}_{g_{1}g}(x)=h^{\prime}_{g_{1}}(h_{g}(m))\times h^{\prime}_{g}(m).

(11)

Proof.
The calculation of
h^{\prime}_{g}(x)
is done by considering
h_{g}(x+\Delta)-h_{g}(x)
and by using the definition of the differential. For seeing that
h^{\prime}_{g}(x)
is non singular, assume that there exists
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
h^{\prime}_{g}(x)(\Delta)=0.
One easily sees that

A\Delta=h_{g}(x)c^{T}\Delta.

(12)

Introduce for such a
\Delta
the vector
u
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
defined by

u=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(c^{T}x+d)\Delta-xc^{T}\Delta\\
-c^{T}\Delta\end{array}\right)

Observe that
u=0
would imply
\Delta=0
since the number
c^{T}x+d
is not zero. However, using (
12
)

g(u)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(c^{T}x+d)A\Delta-Axc^{T}\Delta-bc^{T}\Delta\\
c^{T}(c^{T}x+c)\Delta-c^{T}xc^{T}\Delta\-dc^{T}\Delta\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(c^{T}x+d)h_{g}(x)c^{T}\Delta-Axc^{T}\Delta-bc^{T}\Delta\\
0\end{array}\right)=0

Since
u\neq 0
and
g(u)=0
this contradicts the fact that
g
is not singular and proves the proposition. Formula (
11
) is clear.

Proposition 7.3.
Let
(\mu_{\lambda}(dx))_{\lambda>0}

a convolution semigroup on

Proof.
It is useful to reformulate (
110

p_{k}(\lambda,c)1_{(0,\infty)}(\langle c,k\rangle)=\frac{1}{\langle c,k\rangle}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\langle c,k^{\prime}\rangle f(\lambda,k^{\prime})f(\langle c,k\rangle,k-k^{\prime})\right)1_{(0,\infty)}(\langle c,k\rangle))

DL_{\lambda}=D(L_{1}^{\lambda})=\lambda L_{1}^{\lambda-1}D(L_{1})\Rightarrow L_{A\lambda}DL_{\lambda}=\lambda L_{1}^{(A+1)\lambda-1}D(L_{1})=\frac{\lambda}{(A+1)\lambda}D(L^{(A+1)\lambda})

The right hand side of (

\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(A\lambda,k^{\prime})\delta_{k^{\prime}},\ \ \ \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\langle c,k\rangle f(\lambda,k)\delta_{k}.

Conclusion

In this Part one, we have gathered tools and results for the classification of the simple cubic exponential families on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Their set is denoted by
\mathcal{SP}_{3}(n)
. This description will be achieved in Part two. The main points are

•

The introduction of the group

The definition of the set of simple cubic exponential families and its splitting into

•

The Lagrange formula in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Theorem 6.2, which gives a necessary and sufficient condition for having an exponential family concentrated on

Part two will perform the considerable work of describing the elements of each of the

G
