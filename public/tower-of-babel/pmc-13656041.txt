Single pass specimen adequacy in endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration
Authors: Yongkang Huang, Zengli Zhang
Published: 2026

We appreciate the valuable comments by Mao et al. (1)regarding our recent study on determinants of single‑pass specimen adequacy in endobronchial ultrasound‑guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) (2). Their methodological scrutiny is insightful, and we welcome this opportunity to clarify several aspects of our work.

Regarding the suggestion to incorporate real‑time endosonographic features into the analytical framework, we fully agree that such characterisation is appealing, and published studies have shown that endosonographic features can aid in differential diagnosis (3-5). However, as stated in our original manuscript, advanced features such as elastography were not systematically captured owing to equipment constraints. More importantly, the operators in our study, as is the case in most Chinese centers, were predominantly clinical pulmonologists, and per‑pass annotation of subtle sonographic patterns varied considerably. Additionally, the target lymph nodes varied widely in size and anatomical location. Unlike pulmonary lesions, the accessible ultrasound window was often narrow in many cases, making fine‑grained intranodal mapping difficult. Under these real‑world constraints, our finding that the central versus peripheral geometric pathway did not independently affect adequacy remains clinically meaningful—it suggests that, as long as the operator targets a sonographically solid‑appearing region, the exact trajectory is less critical than traditionally assumed.

Concerning the association with final malignant pathology, we acknowledge that this predictor cannot guide the very first puncture because it is not available intra‑procedurally. Nevertheless, we believe this observation still carries clinical utility at a different level: malignant lymph nodes are more likely to yield adequate specimens, likely reflecting underlying biological differences such as looser tumor cell clusters and reduced fibrosis, which facilitate tissue acquisition.

The commentators also raised the issue of sequential adaptive bias, arguing that later passes within the same node are influenced by preceding MOSE results. We fully recognise this phenomenon. However, we wish to emphasize that our study was designed to explore determinants of specimen adequacy at the individual‑puncture level across all passes, not solely the first pass. The adaptive process is inherent to clinical practice, and our mixed‑effects model with random intercepts for lymph nodes and patients was intended to account for clustering. We agree that a first‑pass‑only analysis addresses a different question. For the present study, we maintain that analysing all punctures with appropriate statistical handling provides complementary real‑world evidence directly applicable to intra‑procedural decision‑making across serial passes.

We sincerely thank Mao et al. for their constructive critique. We hope our clarifications contribute to a balanced interpretation of our findings.
