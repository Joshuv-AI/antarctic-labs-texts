Cerebrospinal fluid orexin testing in Australians with narcolepsy type 1
Authors: Mario Fernando, Gianluca Cotta, Samantha Kung, Grace Garden, Jian Eu Tai, Elie Matar, Brendon Yee, Claire Ellender, Sheila Sivam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA clinical diagnosis of narcolepsy type 1 can be challenging, particularly in relation to cataplexy. Despite its specificity, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) orexin testing is under‐utilised in Australasia. We describe the use of CSF orexin testing in 15 clinically indicated patients from two sleep centres, highlighting its utility in the Australasian context.

Narcolepsy is a central nervous system disorder of hypersomnolence with game‐changing, state‐of‐the‐art therapeutics likely becoming available in the near future. 1 Narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) is associated with cataplexy and low cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) orexin levels, differentiating it from other central disorders of hypersomnolence including narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH). Although the presence of cataplexy is a key differentiating characteristic in NT1, it can be challenging to identify with certainty in many instances due to atypical features. 2 Cataplexy may also not be present early in the disease course. 3 Successfully differentiating these groups is essential to provide patients with optimal therapy, which includes adequately treating cataplexy and being able to help patients better understand the natural history of the disease. NT1 is a lifelong condition, whereas patients with NT2 may experience a self‐limiting clinical course with remission seen in 45% of cases within 5 years of diagnosis. 4

General physicians frequently encounter excessive daytime sleepiness and fatigue. These symptoms carry broad differentials including sleep, psychiatric and systemic disorders, and medication effects. 5 Narcolepsy, though uncommon and often under‐recognised, may present outside specialist settings. Cataplexy may be misinterpreted as syncope, arrhythmia or epilepsy, contributing to diagnostic delay. 6 Patients often have cardiometabolic and psychiatric comorbidities, leading to presentations in general medical care. 7 Awareness of an objective diagnostic pathway, including CSF orexin testing, may assist in confirming or excluding NT1 in patients with non‐specific symptoms.

Current diagnostic tools have limitations in differentiating NT1 from other disorders of hypersomnia. Polysomnography followed by multiple sleep latency testing (MSLT) to assess for reduced sleep latency and sleep‐onset rapid eye movement periods (SOREMPs) has poor test–retest reliability, especially in non‐NT1 disorders. 3 , 8 Reasons include the high background prevalence of reduced sleep latency in the general population (~20%), insensitivity of MSLT in older patients, the frequency of SOREMPs in any state of high REM‐sleep drive and confounding by psychiatric medications. 3 , 8 , 9 HLA testing for the DQB1*0602 haplotype is observed in a high proportion of the general population and is not specific for narcolepsy. 10

Quantitation of CSF orexin levels offers an objective diagnostic tool to discriminate between NT1 and other disorders of hypersomnolence. CSF orexin levels <110 pg/mL are considered diagnostic of NT1 with a specificity of 99% and sensitivity of 87% in patients with typical cataplexy. 11 Patients with intermediate levels of CSF orexin between 110 and 200 pg/mL are also more likely to have typical cataplexy or MSLT features of NT1 than those with normal levels. 12 Despite this, the use of CSF orexin testing remains rare in Australia and New Zealand. Testing is not readily available locally. Discrepancies between best‐practice international guidelines and the management of narcolepsy in both Australia and New Zealand have been previously highlighted. 13 , 14 Understanding the clinical utility of CSF orexin testing in the Australasian context may increase its use, justify local testing and assist in equalising the quality of care of patients with narcolepsy in our region, with the international standard.

A retrospectively collected cross‐sectional study was performed among 15 patients referred for CSF orexin testing via sleep clinics at the Woolcock Institute of Medical Research (Sydney, New South Wales) and Princess Alexandra Hospital (Brisbane, Queensland) between January 2018 and August 2025. CSF orexin levels were requested if clinically indicated by a sleep physician. Lumbar punctures were performed at local, private radiology centres to optimise procedure success and safety. CSF samples were sent to Oxford University Hospital, United Kingdom, immunology laboratory for analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sydney Local Health District (X21‐0127).

The rationale for CSF testing in most cases was due to atypical features of the initial clinical diagnosis. In those with an initial clinical diagnosis of NT1, reasons for CSF testing included discordant MSLT result, negative HLA testing, lack of or infrequent cataplexy, poor response to stimulants or shift work at the time of the MSLT. In those with initial diagnoses other than NT1, CSF testing was performed due to the emergence of cataplexy, good clinical response to empiric stimulants or diagnostic uncertainty. Baseline demographics are compared between patients with a final diagnosis of NT1 and patients with a final diagnosis other than NT1 (Table 1). CSF orexin sampling changed the diagnosis of four (27%) of 15 patients (Fig. 1). Two (33%) of six patients with an initial diagnosis of NT1 were diagnosed with a non‐narcolepsy cause for their symptoms. One (33%) of three patients with an initial diagnosis of NT2 was diagnosed with NT1, and another one (33%) of the three NT2 patients was diagnosed with a non‐narcolepsy cause for their symptoms. The majority of non‐narcolepsy diagnoses were IH and the remainder were not conditions of hypersomnolence.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first published study of CSF orexin testing in the Australasian region. Our study demonstrates that CSF orexin testing changed the diagnosis of NT1 and non‐NT1 disorders in a substantial proportion of patients with hypersomnolence and atypical features after an initial clinical diagnosis. Whilst expected differences in mean sleep latency, cataplexy and SOREMP frequency were observed, these factors would not have been able to definitively distinguish NT1 from other diagnoses in our cohort. The high prevalence of cataplexy among the non‐NT1 group highlights the challenging nature of identifying and interpreting reported cataplexy. Despite a small sample size, CSF orexin testing altered the initial clinical diagnosis in a significant proportion of patients in this cohort and provides an important diagnostic tool in the assessment of patients with hypersomnolence. It may also delineate patients more likely to benefit from novel orexin agonist therapies, which have been shown to improve outcomes in NT1. 1 However, the significant out‐of‐pocket expense of the non‐Medicare rebated CSF orexin test and additional cost of image‐guided lumbar punctures may be prohibitive to many patients. Rendering this test more accessible and affordable is one key step in optimising the care of patients with narcolepsy in Australia and New Zealand and matching international best‐practice guidelines.
