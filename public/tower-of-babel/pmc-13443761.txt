Synthesis of nanocrystalline cellulose via microwave-assisted acetic acid pretreatment and endoglucanase-xylanase-mediated enzymatic hydrolysis
Authors: K. Y. Lim, K. Y. Foo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tangerine (Citrus unshiu) peel-derived cellulose nanofibrils (TP-CNFs) were synthesized via an integrated microwave-assisted acetic acid pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis using a cocktail of endoglucanase and xylanase. The resulting TP-CNFs were characterized in terms of morphology, dimensions, chemical composition, yield, crystallinity, functional properties, thermal stability and surface charge. The biosynthesized nanofibrils formed an interwoven, elongated network, with diameters of 2.89–17.12 nm (mean 9.23 nm) and lengths of 340.10–898.93 nm (mean 603.48 nm). Benefiting from the selective and protective actions of the enzyme mixture, which limited excessive hydrolysis, the CNF yield reached 70.80%, while crystalline domains and amphiphilic properties were well-preserved. The process efficacy was further evidenced by a clear cellulose signature and removal of amorphous components, notably hemicellulose, lignin and pectin, accompanied by enhanced accessibility of hydroxyl groups. These structural modifications resulted in a crystallinity index of 64.35%, thermal stability of 338.90 °C and stable colloidal behaviour, with a zeta potential of -30.2 mV. This proposed research framework demonstrates a green and effective strategy for valorizing citrus waste into high-quality nanocellulose, providing a sustainable pathway compatible with future biorefinery applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53753-4.

Introduction

Today, lignocellulosic biomass has gained prominence as a leading alternative feedstock to alleviate the excessive reliance on conventional polymeric materials, petrochemical resources and mineral-based products. Among its constituents, cellulose is a fundamental structural biopolymer widely found in plants, marine organisms, invertebrates and certain microorganisms (algae and fungi), and has attracted significant attention due to its abundant availability, biodegradability and biocompatibility1. Specifically, nanocellulose represents a crystalline and dimensionally confined form of cellulose, in which at least one structural dimension (typically diameter or length) falls within the nanometric scale. The remarkable physicochemical properties of nanocellulose, notably low density, high aspect ratio, excellent thermal stability and crystallinity, superior mechanical strength and stiffness, good hydrophilicity, tunable surface chemistry and low toxicity, render it a promising building block for a wide spectrum of advanced nanomaterial applications2.

Concentrated mineral acid hydrolysis, particularly using sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 64–65%), remains the predominant technique for nanocellulose preparation, mainly due to the high crystallinity and good colloidal stability imparted to the resulting materials. However, the grafting of negatively-charged sulfate ester groups onto the nanocellulose surface can compromise thermal stability, diminishing the nanocellulose yield. Additionally, the accumulation of toxic hydrolysis intermediates, the tendency towards uncontrolled cellulose degradation, and the requirement for extensive post-treatment of acidic effluents further constrain its large-scale industrial application3.

In recent years, enzymatic extraction has been proposed as a greener and more versatile top-down strategy for nanocellulose production. This method enables selective depolymerization of carbohydrate components and hydrolysis of amorphous regions within the cellulose matrix, and the subsequent fibrillation into nanoscale fibers. The advantages of enzymatic processing stem from the high specificity of enzymatic catalysis, mild and controllable processing conditions, reduced waste generation and lower energy consumption, making it a more environmentally benign alternative to conventional chemical methods4. Moreover, under controlled conditions, this approach allows precise tuning of the morphological features and surface chemistry of the resulting nanocellulose.

In the enzymolysis of nanocellulose, cellulase enzymes play a central role by selectively cleaving accessible glycosidic linkages in the amorphous regions of cellulose fibers. This process yields shorter-chain cellulose molecules while preserving the crystalline domains. Among these enzymes, endoglucanase is particularly important for initiating chain scission within amorphous cellulose regions5. However, the use of a single mono-component enzyme is generally insufficient for efficient nanocellulose isolation from lignocellulosic biomass. This limitation arises from the highly ordered structure of cellulose fibrils, and the complex interactions between cellulose and hemicellulosic components, especially xylan.

Within the lignocellulosic matrix, xylan may form irreversible hydrogen-bonding with cellulose. This interaction creates a protective barrier that restricts enzyme accessibility to the internal cellulose chains. Meanwhile, the presence of cellobiohydrolase and β-glucosidase in conventional cellulase systems may promote excessive hydrolysis of cellulose into soluble domains (glucose and secondary sugar-degradation compounds), lowering cellulose crystallinity and diminishing the overall production yield. Under such circumstances, the development of controlled and targeted enzymatic strategies is essential to balance the fibrillation and depolymerization reactions. Within this framework, the supplementation of endoglucanase with a xylanase accessory enzyme is expected to enhance the hydrolysis process by selectively cleaving the β-1,4-glycosidic linkages of xylan and disrupting lignin-carbohydrate complex interactions within the lignocellulosic matrix. The removal of hemicellulosic components, primarily xylan and glucomannan surrounding the cellulose microfibrils, promotes cellulose amorphogenesis and increases fiber porosity, swelling and accessibility for subsequent nanofibrillation. In addition, the synergistic interaction between endoglucanase and xylanase facilitates the degradation of re-deposited xylan and xylo-oligomers on the fiber surface. This process fragments the robust xylan coatings associated with amorphous cellulose, and removes portions of loosely bound xylan from crystalline domains6. These coordinated enzymatic reactions enhance the efficiency of cellulose fibrillation, while preserving desirable structural properties, specifically crystallinity, thermostability and mechanical integrity of the resulting nanocellulose.

However, enzymatic treatment alone is often insufficient to overcome the inherent recalcitrance of lignocellulosic biomass, particularly due to its compact and heterogeneous structure, which limits enzyme accessibility. Therefore, an effective pretreatment step is essential to improve the efficiency of nanocellulose production. In this context, microwave (MW)-assisted acetic acid (AA) pretreatment provides a promising strategy. It enables rapid heat transfer, enhances solvent penetration, and partially removes lignin and hemicellulose, which improves substrate accessibility for the subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis step7.

In this study, a hybrid extraction strategy integrating MW-assisted AA pretreatment with a dual-enzyme (endoglucanase-xylanase) hydrolysis system is proposed for the efficient production of cellulose nanofibrils. Tangerine (Citrus unshiu) peel (TP), an abundant agro-industrial residue accounting for approximately 40–50% of the total fruit mass, was selected as a renewable lignocellulosic feedstock due to its favourable compositional and structural characteristics, as well as its widespread availability8. TP comprises of predominantly cellulose (25 to 40 wt%), hemicellulose and pectin as major amorphous components9. While its cellulose content is comparable to that of conventional lignocellulosic feedstocks such as corn stover and sugarcane bagasse10,11, the substantial amorphous polysaccharide content and the unique primary cell wall-dominated architecture of citrus peel reduce biomass recalcitrance and enhance cellulose accessibility, which in turn facilitates chemical and enzymatic fractionation under milder pretreatment conditions. We examine how physicochemical pretreatment and dual-enzyme hydrolysis could act synergistically to enhance nanocellulose formation. The structural, physicochemical and functional properties of the resulting TP-derived cellulose nanofibrils were comprehensively characterized in terms of morphology, dimensions, chemical composition, yield, crystallinity, functional properties, thermal stability and surface charge. The underlying mechanism governing the transformation of TP biomass into nanocellulose was elucidated. Collectively, these findings provide a valuable reference for advancing sustainable bio-material production strategies, particularly nanocellulose synthesis protocols, and support a broader development of high-value nanocellulose products within lignocellulosic biorefinery systems.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Tangerine (Citrus unshiu) peel (TP) waste sourced from a fruit canning facility was selected as the cellulosic raw precursor. The collected TP was washed extensively with double distilled (DI) water to remove adhering dirt particles and water-soluble impurities. TP was oven-dried, cut, milled and sieved using mechanical grinding and screening equipment to obtain a uniform particle size of 1–2 mm. The processed TP powder was stored in air-tight containers for further processing. Endoglucanase FiberCare® (Novozymes A/S, Batch No. CGK20074, specific activity of 812.9 U/g) and recombinant xylanase from Aspergillus oryzae (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. X2753, specific activity of 2,500 U/g) were used as hydrolytic enzymes, while citrate buffer (0.5 M, pH 4.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No.: J61249) was applied as the buffer solution, in the enzymatic hydrolysis.

Microwave system

A domestic microwave heating oven [inner dimension: L 36 cm x W 33 cm x H 24 cm], with an operating power of 1,000 W, and a heating frequency of 2.45 GHz, was adopted as the heating source for CNF preparation. The microwave unit was modified by integrating a digital timer-temperature control system, and a type-K thermocouple to enable precise monitoring of irradiation time and temperature throughout the experiments. Hydrolysis reactions were conducted in a Teflon pressure reactor, which was placed on the rotary plate located within the microwave cavity for uniform heating.

Preparation of tangerine peel-derived CNFs

The preparation of TP-CNFs was achieved through an integrated process of microwave (MW)-assisted acetic acid (AA) pretreatment and endoglucanase-xylanase-mediated enzymatic hydrolysis (Supplemental Fig. S1). Prior to pretreatment, the raw TP was dispersed in DI water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), and mechanically stirred at 300 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was decanted via filtration and centrifugation (6,000 rpm, 15 min), and the wet TP solid was collected for MW-assisted AA organosolv pretreatment.

In accordance with the “bio-refinery concept,” AA organosolv pretreatment serves as a crucial step to deconstruct the biomass cell wall, and remove non-cellulosic components, mainly lignin, hemicellulose and pectin that encapsulate cellulose within the lignocellulosic matrix. The pretreatment process was conducted in a custom-modified microwave system, where 1 g of water-swollen TP was homogenized in 20 mL of 50% AA solution (CH3COOH, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. 64-19-7), at the solid-to-liquid ratio of 1: 20 (g/mL), in a closed 50 mL Teflon reactor12. The reaction mixture was irradiated at 100 °C for 10 min under microwave heating13. The resultant slurry was washed repeatedly with DI water to remove residual chemicals until the filtrate reached a neutral pH (6.0–7.0). After pretreatment, the resulting precipitate was separated by centrifugation (6,000 rpm, 15 min), and stored at 4 °C for the subsequent enzymatic treatment.

Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed in a 100 mL screw-capped Erlenmeyer flask containing 2% (w/w, dry substrate basis) pre-treated TP-CFs in citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.8), with a total reaction volume of 50 mL, and 0.01% (w/w) sodium azide14. Enzyme loadings of 25 U/g substrate for endoglucanase and 50 U/g substrate for xylanase were introduced into the reaction system15. Prior to the enzymatic hydrolysis, the solution mixture was homogenized, and subjected to ultrasonic dispersion for 30 min (FB15057 Fisherbrand® Ultrasonicator). The reaction mixture was then incubated in a shaking incubator (model NB-205, N-biotek, Korea) at 50 °C and 150 rpm for 5 h.

At the completion of the hydrolysis step, the reaction was terminated by boiling the mixture at 100 °C for 5 min (inactivation of enzyme), subsequent by centrifugation (6,000 rpm, 15 min) for the separation of supernatants and precipitates. Aliquots of the hydrolysate were withdrawn for the quantification of total reducing sugars via 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method as outlined described by Miller16. The final off-white precipitate was re-suspended and washed with excessive DI water until neutrality (pH 6–7), before it was oven-dried to constant weight for further characterization.

Untreated tangerine peel (TP) served as the baseline control to establish a reference state for the evaluation of the integrated treatment process. Comparisons were made between untreated TP and processed samples obtained after MW-assisted AA pretreatment followed by endoglucanase-xylanase enzymatic hydrolysis. These comparisons enabled a systematic assessment of structural, compositional and physicochemical changes induced by the treatment sequence. Key process parameters, including microwave irradiation conditions (temperature, time and solvent composition) and enzyme loadings, were selected based on preliminary optimization trials and supported by established literature. This approach ensured effective disruption of lignocellulosic recalcitrance while preserving cellulose integrity and crystallinity. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3) under identical conditions to ensure reproducibility. Quantitative results are reported as mean values, and independent repetitions were carried out for all analytical measurements to confirm data consistency and reliability.

Morphological microscopic observations

The morphologies of TP and TP-CNFs were examined using a field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, ZEISS Supra 35VP, Germany), at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a beam current of 200 pA. The dried TP sample was mounted on stubs using double-sided carbon conductive tape, and a diluted TP-CNFs suspension (~ 0.001%, w/v) was drop-cast onto a silver stub and air-dried under ambient conditions, which were then sputter-coated with gold (Au) to prevent charging during imaging.

Transmission electron microscopy (EF-TEM, Libra 120, Carl Zeiss, Germany), operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV, was applied for the illustration of surface morphology and dimensional profiles of CNFs. For accomplishment of the analysis, a droplet of diluted CNF suspension (0.001%, w/v) was deposited onto the carbon-coated copper grids, negatively stained with saturated (2%) ethanolic uranyl acetate solution, and air-dried at room temperature. Representative TEM micrograph was adopted for the quantification of geometrical dimensions of CNFs (n = 100 randomly selected CNFs), including diameter (D), length (L) and aspect ratio (L/D), using ImageJ software.

The X-ray diffractogram patterns, crystalline structures and crystallinities of TP and TP-CNFs were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker-AXS D8, Germany), equipped with a monochromatic Cu Kα X-ray radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 30 kV and 10 mA. XRD data were collected over a 2θ range of 10–80°, with a step size of 0.03°. Surface functionality

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopic analysis was performed for the verification of surface functional groups within the cellulosic samples using a PerkinElmer’s FTIR-2000 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were prepared as pressed potassium bromide (KBr) pellets at a sample-to-KBr mass ratio of 1:100 (w/w). Spectra were recorded in transmittance mode over the range of 4000 –400 cm⁻¹, with a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

Thermal stability

The thermal stabilities of the cellulosic samples were examined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using a PerkinElmer TGA 7 system (USA). Measurements were conducted under a nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere at a constant heating rate of 5 °C/min, with a gas flow rate of 10 mL/min. Sample weight loss was recorded as a function of temperature over the range of 50–600 °C.

Zeta (ζ) potential measurement

The zeta potential characteristics were recorded using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS system (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) for assessment of electrostatic interactions (attractive or repulsive) among the colloidal particles. Sample suspensions were prepared at 0.01% (w/v), and ultrasonically dispersed for 30 s prior to analysis. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the mean values were reported.

Result and discussion

Physicochemical characterizations

Morphology and size

The SEM micrographs of (a) raw TP and (b) the corresponding cellulose nanofibrils (TP-CNFs) are presented in Fig. 1. The untreated TP [Fig. 1(a)] exhibits a relatively compact, smooth and uniform flake-like morphology. A waxy layered architecture is evident on the TP surface, overlain by viscous constituents, including pectin and lignocellulosic components (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, soluble organic species and residual impurities), which collectively confer high impermeability, structural rigidity and protective functionality to the plant cell wall. In contrast, TP-CNFs [Fig. 1(b)] illustrate pronounced structural changes after the MW-assisted AA pretreatment and dual-enzymatic reactions (endoglucanase and xylanase), which have specifically disintegrated the amorphous regions (pectin, lignin and hemicellulose) of raw TP, and reveal the formation of a three-dimensional (3D) multilayer structure with an interwoven long-fibril network.

Fig. 1SEM images of (a) raw TP and (b) TP-CNFs.

SEM images of (a) raw TP and (b) TP-CNFs.

The distinctive nano-fibrillated structure of TP-CNFs [Fig. 1(b)] highlights the effectiveness of MW-assisted AA pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis in disrupting the recalcitrant supramolecular assembly of TP and loosening the packing of cellulose fibril network through a swelling–relaxation mechanism. It has been suggested that the initial MW-assisted AA pretreatment alters the pectin-lignin-hemicellulose matrix, presumably by introducing additional reactive end groups on the cellulosic substrates, which enhances enzyme accessibility and adsorption within both external and internal cellulose fiber regions. Fragmentation and subsequent nano-fibrillation of cellulose aggregates into individual cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) were clearly observed after the enzymatic hydrolysis stage. The acquired findings signify the synergistic actions of xylanase and endoglucanase in developing interfibrillar voids within the cellulose-hemicellulose network, resulting in significant morphological deformation and increased network porosity21.

The geometric dimensions of TP-CNFs were further visualized using TEM technique, as depicted in Fig. 2. The fibrillar TP-CNFs detected an average diameter of 9.23 nm and length of 603.48 nm, with ranges of 2.89–17.12 nm and 340.10–898.93 nm, respectively, corresponding to an aspect ratio of 65.36. The analytical measurement falls within the typical dimensional range reported for cellulose nanofibers (length 0.5–1.5 μm, diameter 5–30 nm and aspect ratio ≥ 50)22. The TEM image reveals a long, entangled filament network, without observable fragmentation. This behaviour may be attributed to the random scission of cellulose chains during enzymatic treatment. The higher aspect ratio of TP-CNFs, which lies within the long-fiber regime (aspect ratio ≥ 50), implies improved mechanical performance, enhanced thermal stability and increased reinforcing capability in cellulose-based polymer systems. Furthermore, the relatively consistent size distribution and morphology of TP-CNFs, as observed from the TEM images (Fig. 2), indicate strong interfibrillar bonding, which is critical for maximizing the nano-reinforcement effect.

Xylanase is widely recognized as an enzyme-assisted bleaching or bleach-boosting agent that enhances fiber flexibility and swelling behaviours by selectively attacking amorphous regions or non-cellulosic polysaccharides within the cellulose fibers, while preserving the crystalline cellulose structure23. The positive role of xylanase, as an accessory enzyme in the enzymolysis process, is generally attributed to the selective conversion of re-located/ re-precipitated xylan, xylan-lignin complexes and embedded xylan within the cellulosic matrix into smaller and less recalcitrant fragments. This structural modification results in a more porous and permeable fiber network, benefitting the efficiency of the subsequent depolymerization and hydrolysis steps. According to the reported literature, xylanase-assisted hydrolysis produces a series of distinct cellulosic fibers, consisting of both crystalline and amorphous domains, with characteristic micrometer-scale lengths and nanometer-scale diameters24.

Endoglucanase-mediated enzymatic hydrolysis is another crucial lignocellulose conversion process that selectively catalyzes accessible glycosidic bonds along cellulose molecule chains. Within the reaction system, nanofibrillation process is promoted by random scissoring of intra-molecular β-1,4-glycosidic linkages, particularly within amorphous portions into less-ordered cellulose chains, resulting in structurally less-ordered cellulose chains and newly formed reducing and non-reducing end groups. The application of mono-component cellulase enzymes in the enzymatic reactions demonstrated a higher tendency to generate an entangled, irregular filament network, characterized by a normal length distribution ranging between 300 and 900 nm25.

The reported findings were mainly ascribed to the presence of non-cellulosic compounds, mainly lignin or hemicellulose fractions, which form a physical barrier that restricts the enzyme accessibility, or limits the cutting and splitting actions of endoglucanase on the cellulosic substrate. Specifically, endoglucanase may bind irreversibly to lignin and hemicellulose via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, which suppresses its overall activity during the enzymolysis reaction. In this sense, the introduction of additional pretreatment technique or accessory enzymes, may intensify the enzymatic accessibility effect for the synthesis of cellulose nanostructures, with higher aspect ratios and better size uniformity. In the present work, TP-CNFs produced from the synergistic application of xylanase and endoglucanase exhibited diameters between 2.89 and 17.12 nm, and lengths of 340.10–898.93 nm. This dimensional profile underscores the efficacy of mixed enzymatic systems in accelerating hydrolytic reactions while operating at reduced enzyme concentrations and under comparatively mild conditions. Nevertheless, the presence of larger aggregates (diameter ≥ 20 nm) was observed in TP-CNFs, likely arising from the lateral association between adjacent CNFs driven by extensive surface hydroxyl (-OH) interactions.

Chemical composition

Compositional analysis represents a chemical constituent determining step to quantify the variations of the structural components of plant cell walls. From Table 1, raw TP contains 30.82% of cellulose, 13.61% of hemicellulose, 11.20% of lignin, 23.56% of pectin and 20.81% of other extractives. The relatively high cellulose content of TP, accompanied by lower fractions of lignin and hemicellulose, supports its suitability as a viable source for the subsequent nano-fibrillation reaction. However, the presence of recalcitrant components, mainly pectin, lignin and hemicellulose, forms a rigid and structurally complex matrix, that is highly resistant to enzymatic hydrolysis process, impeding the accessibility of binding enzymes to the cell wall. The cleavage of tight entanglement, hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions among chemical constituents necessitates additional pretreatment procedures or complex enzymatic digestion to achieve effective purification requirement.

In contrast, the synergistic actions of MW-assisted AA pretreatment and enzymolysis have enriched the cellulose content (92.15%), and subsequently reduced the pectin, lignin and hemicellulose contents to 0.01 to 0.31%. The outcome implied the rising cellulose hydrolytic reactivity, in response to the dipole rotation and ionic conduction of microwave dielectric heating26,27. AA molecules align themselves with the oscillating electric field, which ensures the efficient dissolution and better contact with TP. The subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis step offers better control over the selective hydrolysis of less crystalline or amorphous components through substrate-specific enzymatic action for higher purity and greater product yield. Xylanase has been shown to both selectively reduce the residual hemicellulose content on the surface of microfibers by breaking down β-1,4-linkages in the xylan backbone, and to react synergistically with endoglucanase to catalyze endo-hydrolysis of β-1,4-glycosidic linkages in cellulose chains, with an extraction yield of 70.80%.

According to 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method, the corresponding reducing sugar released from the enzymolysis reaction was identified to be 1.12 mg/mL. The concentration of the released reducing sugar falls within the reported range of commercial enzymes, specifically Endoglucanase 7, Cellic HTec2 and Celluclast, as documented by Chorozian et al.28., which signifies the suitability of the cocktail mixture of xylanase and endoglucanase in the enzymatic hydrolysis reaction. Meanwhile, it has been inferred that excessive cellobiose and reducing sugar release during enzymatic cellulose hydrolysis may induce a negative end-product inhibitory effect, that would significantly affect enzymatic efficiency and reduce the crystallinity of the synthesized nanocellulose.

Crystallinity

The diffraction patterns and crystallinity indexes (CI) of TP and TP-CNFs were examined using X-ray diffractometry (XRD), as presented in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3(a), the presence of a broad amorphous halo at 2θ = 15–25° indicates the amorphous nature of TP biomass. This behaviour is characteristic of the complex mixtures of polymeric components, including pectin, lignin, hemicellulose and amorphous cellulose29. In contrast, the XRD diffractogram of TP-CNFs [Fig. 3(b)] displayed sharp, high-intensity diffraction peaks, with well-defined reflections observed at 2θ = 14.8°, 16.8°, 22.6° and 34.3°. These peaks are indexed to the (1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{1}$$\end{document}0), (110), (200) and (004) planes, consistent with the cellulose Iβ polymorph (ICDD 00-060-1502). Comparison of the XRD findings revealed an intensification of the characteristic diffraction peaks, which suggests an increase in the relative crystalline fraction due to the removal of amorphous components rather than indicating any structural transformation of cellulose crystalline allomorphs.

XRD diffraction patterns of TP and TP-CNFs.

The reduction of the amorphous halo intensity in TP-CNFs suggested the effective elimination of amorphous components and inorganic substances after the MW-assisted AA pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis steps. This observation is primarily attributed to the selective hydrolysis of hemicellulose, pectin and less-ordered cellulose regions, rather than the complete elimination of the amorphous phase. A recent study reported a similar observation, which was primarily attributed to the hydrolytic actions of endoglucanase and xylanase in degrading labile amorphous components and less-ordered cellulose fragments30. By adopting the crystallinity equation20, the relative crystallinity index of TP-CNFs was determined to be 64.35%. The enhanced crystallinity of TP-CNFs, corroborated by the chemical compositional findings (Table 1), indicates enrichment of crystalline cellulose domains following the removal of non-cellulosic and amorphous constituents. This result demonstrates that the synergistic MW-assisted AA pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis approach enables the effective synthesis of CNFs, while preserving the native crystalline structure and essential material attributes of the resulting product.

Surface functionality

The alterations of the chemical functionalities of TP and TP-CNFs are described in Fig. 4. From Fig. 4(a), the intense and broad absorption peak at 3400 cm− 1 is attributed to O-H stretching vibrations arising from extensive intra- and inter-molecular hydrogen bonding within the TP structure. The absorption band within the region from 2978 cm− 1 to 2805 cm− 1, is related to the asymmetric and symmetrical stretching vibrations of aliphatic -CH, -CH2 and -CH3 over the cellulose surface. The absorption features at 1050 cm− 1 and 627 cm− 1 can be ascribed to C-O-C/ C-O and C-H stretching vibrations associated with β-(1,4)-glycosidic bonds, respectively. The characteristic peak appearing near 1650 cm− 1 is due to the H-O-H bending vibration of adsorbed water molecules. In contrast, the spectral signals detected at 1731 cm− 1, 1519 cm− 1 and 1378 cm− 1, can be ascribed to the ester C = O groups, aromatic C = C bonds and asymmetric C-H deformations, respectively, originating from the aromatic skeletal structures of lignin, hemicellulose and pectin.

FTIR spectra of TP and TP-CNFs.

The FTIR profile of TP-CNFs [Fig. 4(b)] displays typical cellulose I features, with -OH stretching (3400 cm− 1), C-H stretching (2918 cm− 1), in-plane CH2 and OCH3 bending (1413 cm− 1), asymmetric and symmetric deformation modes of -CH and C-O groups (1360 cm− 1) and asymmetric C-O-C stretching vibrations of the pyranose ring/ β-glycosidic linkages (1050 cm− 1). The elevated absorption intensities at 3400 cm− 1, 1650 cm− 1 and 1050 cm− 1 indicate strengthened hydrogen bonding interactions and improved accessibility of hydroxyl groups. Another pronounced structural variation is evidenced by the reduction or disappearance of absorption bands at 1731 cm− 1, 1519 cm− 1 and 1378 cm− 1, which indicates the successive removal of hemicellulose-, lignin- and pectin-derived components during the hybrid MW-assisted AA pretreatment and enzymolysis process. During the enzymatic hydrolysis reaction, it can be implicated that xylanase preferentially cleaves the β-1,4-glycosidic linkages within the xylan backbone, and liberates short and soluble xylan fragments, that promote pores opening and unlocking of hydroxyl groups over the microfiber surface. Further, degradation of residual xylan matrix within the inner structures may enhance the accessibility and penetration of exogenous enzymes, as reported by Campioni et al.31 and Immerzeel and Fiskari32. Meanwhile, the role of endoglucanase can be described as a three-step mechanism: (a) adsorption, in which the enzyme transfers from bulk aqueous phase to the cellulose surface through the carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) or catalytic domain (CD), (b) complexation, characterized by partial decrystallization of surface cellulose chains and insertion of a single chain into the catalytic domain to form a productive enzyme-substrate complex, subsequent by (c) catalysis reaction, whereby glycosidic bonds are hydrolyzed at the active sites of CBM and CD, resulting in the release of insoluble cellulose fragments (microfibrils or fibril aggregates) and soluble enzymatic products. The appearance of new absorption bands at 1413 cm− 1 and 1360 cm− 1, which are indicative of crystalline cellulose regions, supports the preservation of native cellulose structure after the hydrolysis reactions.

Thermostability

Figure 5 depicts the thermal stability profiles of raw TP and TP-CNFs. The corresponding thermograms can be divided into three distinctive thermal degradation events: dehydration, depolymerization and decomposition of polysaccharide-based units. TP and TP-CNFs recorded initial weight losses of 19.3% and 5.5%, respectively, during the first degradation stage (50–100 °C), which are primarily attributed to the desorption of physically and chemically-bound water, along with the volatilization of low-molecular-weight organic constituents from the sample materials. At the temperature regime between 100 and 180 °C for TP and 100–200 °C for TP-CNFs, the thermogravimetric mass-loss curves exhibit a relatively stable plateau region, corresponding to the gradual removal of residual moisture. This stage is characterized by a relatively constant evaporation rate with negligible structural degradation.

TGA thermograms of TP and TP-CNFs.

Significant weight losses of 38.5% (TP) and 58.9% (TP-CNFs) were registered within the temperature windows of 180 °C to 323 °C and 200 °C to 362 °C, respectively. This decomposition phase is associated with the pyrolysis of lignocellulosic components (pectin and hemicellulose), accompanied by dehydration reactions, and the scission of glycosidic linkages and covalent bonds (C-H, C-O and C-C) within the cellulose macromolecular chains. According to the Broido-Shafizadeh cellulose pyrolysis model33, cellulose undergoes thermal decomposition within the temperature range of 200–280 °C, with concurrent dehydration and condensation reactions. The final degradation stage between 300 °C and 600 °C, shows weight losses of 13.5% and 9.1% for TP and TP-CNFs, respectively, which are mainly assigned to the decarboxylation reactions that leave behind low-molecular-weight carbonaceous residues. Beyond 325 °C and 362 °C, respectively, the curves level off, indicating a slower decomposition rate dominated by the aromatic ring configuration of the cellulose structure.

Comparatively, the higher thermal stability of TP-CNFs (Tmax = 338.9 °C) can be attributed to nanofibril entanglement, thermal cross-linking and the removal of amorphous fragments from the cellulose surface. The improved thermal tolerance of TP-CNFs relative to TP correlates with their higher crystallinity and greater cellulose content, as evidenced by the XRD and compositional findings. Additionally, the enhanced thermal stability of TP-CNFs may be related to the presence of aggregated particles, which limit the effective heat-exposed surface area. The experimental observations support the strong potential of TP-CNFs as reinforcing fillers in bio-materials under high temperature conditions (≤ 300 °C).

Apparent zeta potential

The zeta potentials of TP and TP-CNFs suspensions are presented in Fig. 6. TP detected a zeta potential reading of -15.3 mV [Fig. 6(a)]. The negatively charged surface likely arises from the presence of anionic galacturonic acids (C6H10O7), with their ionizable carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on the surface. However, TP-CNFs recorded a substantially higher zeta potential value of -30.2 mV [Fig. 6(b)], which falls within the range generally associated with good colloidal stability (≤ -30 mV). The results signify the strong electrostatic repulsion force among the negatively-charged fibrils of TP-CNFs, which suppresses particle agglomeration.

It is important to emphasize that, despite the absence of grafted surface charges, xylanase plays a key role in microfibril depolymerization and fragmentation, which facilitates the removal of hemicellulose and xylan from both external and internal regions of the cellulose structure. The loosened microfibrillar architecture enhances the accessibility of endoglucanase for the random scission of β-1, 4-glycosidic linkages within the cellulose chains, resulting in the formation of additional hydroxyl groups, as corroborated by the FTIR analysis (Fig. 4). This structural modification increases surface polarity and enhances intra/ inter-molecular repulsion forces, which promotes nanofiber separation, and improves dispersion and colloidal stability34. These findings are consistent with earlier reports35,36, which highlight the favorable colloidal properties of enzymatically hydrolyzed nanocelluloses, and their suitability as reinforcing agents in composite or polymer matrices for environmental remediation applications.

Preparation mechanism of nanocellulose

A schematic illustration of the TP-CNF preparation mechanism is presented in Fig. 7. Pretreatment constitutes a fundamental step in biomass fractionation, as it (a) disrupts and removes the interpenetrating cross-linked network of lignin, hemicellulose and pectin within cellulose fibers, (b) weakens the hydrogen-bonding interactions in the cellulose domains, (c) induces physical, chemical and structural modifications of the biomass, and (d) improves cellulose accessibility for biorefinery processes37. The organosolv process serves as an effective alternative pretreatment for lignin, hemicellulose and pectin solubilization, through the action of organic solvents or their aqueous solutions, which support environmentally sustainable lignocellulosic biorefining.

Fig. 7Schematic illustration of the TP-CNF preparation mechanism.

Schematic illustration of the TP-CNF preparation mechanism.

Among the organic solvents, acetic acid (AA) offers additional advantages over mineral acids, as (a) AA exhibits favourable lignin solvency, with a Hildebrand solubility parameter (δ value) of 25.8 (J/cm3)−1/2, which is close to that of lignin (δ value = 22.5 (J/cm3)−1/2) and D-polygalacturonic acid (δ value = 31.3 (J/cm3)−1/2), which enables hydrogen-bonding interactions with lignin and pectin fragments38,39; (b) AA delignification can be performed effectively at higher temperature (> 100 °C) without the need for catalysts40; and (c) AA can be readily recovered by simple distillation, making this process more sustainable than conventional techniques. During AA pretreatment, (a) AA partially dissociates into hydronium ions (H3O+), which penetrate into the macro-molecular network and amorphous region of the fibre, and interact with ether (-O-) and carbonyl (-C = O) groups at C2, C3 and C6 positions of TP, subsequent by (b) solvolysis or homolytic cleavage of lignin and lignin-carbohydrate linkages, specifically the α-aryl ether and β-aryl ether bonds, and (c) solvation and dissolution of lignin and pectin fragments in the acid medium, to produce a solid phase that consists of primarily cellulose and hemicellulose.

The delignification and depectination mechanisms of organic AA can be satisfactorily described by a pseudo-homogeneous first-order kinetic model41, which accounts for the solubilization of biomass components and subsequent secondary reactions (condensation, precipitation and fragmentation) that influence the lignocellulosic structure. In most cases, AA-assisted pretreatment alone is not sufficient to achieve complete fractionation of biomass components, due to the presence of residual recalcitrant structures42. Integration of microwave (MW) irradiation provides a rapid and energy-efficient heating technique compared with conventional methods, which enhances heat transfer and reaction kinetics within the lignocellulosic matrix43,44. This facilitates AA penetration and induces swelling or softening of the outer and inner layers of TP, reduces mass transfer resistance, improves substrate accessibility, and modifies the hydrophilic nature of its cellulosic fibres.

Enzymatic hydrolysis is a top-down reaction for the bio-conversion of cellulose into nanocellulose and simple sugar, via microbial enzymes. The enzyme-mediated isolation of TP-CNFs has been conducted with the synergistic actions of xylanase and endoglucanase, and assisted by ultrasonic radiation, for the preservation of crystalline domains and physico-chemical properties of nanocellulose. The integration of a sono-mechanical effect induced by ultrasound at the initial stage of enzymatic reaction enhances the interaction between high-mobility enzyme molecules and low-mobility polymer substrates, and promotes the formation, growth and expansion of microbubble streams on the solid surface. This ultrasound-assisted process enhances heat and mass transfers, and improves the transport of reactants into the internal porous matrix, which supports enzyme activation and facilitates the disruption of the hierarchical structure of cellulose fibres45. However, ultrasound should be applied for a limited duration, as excessive localized pressure and temperature generated under high-intensity sonication may induce conformational damage to amino acid residues and polypeptide chains of enzymes, particularly within substrate-binding and catalytic domains, resulting in reduced enzymatic activity and stability46.

Xylanase, a glycoside hydrolase enzyme, exhibits catalytic activity comparable to cellulase in the hydrolysis of glycosidic and ester linkages associated with hemicellulosic polysaccharides, including xylose-, arabinose-, galactose- and mannose-containing structures, as well as acetate and ferulic acid ester bonds within plant cell walls. In parallel, endoglucanase (endo-β-1,4-glucanase) catalyses random cleavage of internal β-1,4-glycosidic linkages within amorphous regions and internal domains of cellulose, and generates new chain ends while augmenting the accessibility of cellulose microfibrils for the subsequent enzymatic degradation.

The proposed synthesis strategy integrating MW-assisted AA pretreatment with xylanase-aided endoglucanase enzymatic hydrolysis in this work (Table 2) achieves a markedly higher production yield (70.80%) and improved physicochemical properties of TP-CNFs within a substantially reduced processing time (~ 5 h), as compared with reported literature findings. The enhanced performance arises from the synergistic effects of MW-assisted AA pretreatment and endoglucanase-xylanase enzymatic hydrolysis. Specifically, (a) the high thermal efficiency of microwave heating enables rapid energy transfer within the pretreatment system, which facilitates effective removal of recalcitrant structures and components by organic acid within a short reaction time and improves subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency54, and (b) ultrasonic-assisted dual-enzyme hydrolysis increases enzyme-substrate contact area, reduces diffusion limitations and accelerates enzyme-substrate reactions, leading to efficient hydrolysis of amorphous cellulose and xylan fractions55. These effects support a sustainable and high-yield nanocellulose production process.

Conclusion

The versatility of MW-assisted AA pretreatment and xylanase-endoglucanase-mediated enzymatic preparation of tangerine peel derived nanocellulose (TP-CNFs) has been successfully validated. The green-synthesized TP-CNFs revealed a nano-fibrillated architecture, with an average diameter and length of 9.23 nm and 603.48 nm, respectively. The synergy of endoglucanase and xylanase, applied after microwave-assisted acetic acid (AA) pretreatment, facilitated the enzymatic extraction treatment, without compromising the crystalline structure or physicochemical properties of cellulose Iβ, resulting in a rising crystallinity (64.35%) and production yield (70.80%). The associated glycosidic linkages and individual cellulose chains were cleaved, unlocking of free hydroxyl groups over the TP-CNF surface, as evidenced by the negative zeta potential reading of -30.2 mV, and a thermal decomposition temperature at 338.90 °C. These acquired research findings open a new green avenue for the upscaling of TP into high quality nanocelluloses, aligned with the sustainable and green chemical design principles.
