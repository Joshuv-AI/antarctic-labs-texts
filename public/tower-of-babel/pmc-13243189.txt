Modelling Knowledge, Attitudes and Contraceptive Use Among Adolescents in the Ho Municipality, Ghana
Authors: David Adedia, Mavis Pearl Kwabla, Williams Agyemang‐Duah, Esther Banahene, Vincent Uwumboriyhie Gmayinaam, Judith Akworkor Anaman‐Torgbor, Kennedy Diema Konlan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundResearch evidence indicates that good knowledge, positive attitudes, and regular contraceptive use are linked to a reduction in unintended pregnancies among adolescents worldwide. Despite the high rate of unintended pregnancies in Ghana, particularly in the Volta Region, there is limited information about adolescents' knowledge, attitudes, and contraceptive practices. This study examined the knowledge, attitudes, and contraceptive use among adolescents in the Ho Municipality of Ghana.MethodsThis study used a quantitative cross‐sectional design, with a convenience sampling method to recruit 841 adolescents in the Ho Municipality. The primary outcome variable was contraceptive use, and the secondary outcome variables—knowledge of and attitude towards contraceptive use—were measured with standardised scales with Cronbach's α reported above the cutoff of 0.7 (contraceptive use: α = 86; Knowledge: α = 0.93; Attitude: α = 0.95). All tests were done using a 5% level of significance in the R statistical package and the SPSS software version 27.ResultsThis study found that the prevalence of contraceptive use was comparatively low (48.2%). The multiple logistic regression model indicated that employed participants (aOR = 3.98, 95% CI = 1.51, 11.62, p = 0.007) and older adolescents (aOR=1.44, 95% CI = 1.23, 1.69, p < 0.001) were more likely to use contraceptives compared to their counterparts. Additionally, the analysis showed that adolescents with no formal education (aOR = 4.95, 95% CI = 1.65, 18.56, p = 0.008) were more likely to have positive attitudes than those with tertiary education. The results also revealed that adolescents aged 18 and above (aOR = 4.09, 95% CI = 2.21, 7.66, p < 0.001), those who were employed (aOR = 3.26, 95% CI = 1.32, 8.65, p = 0.013), and those with one child (aOR = 6.29, 95% CI = 1.03, 43.73, p = 0.049) were more likely to have good knowledge compared to their counterparts.ConclusionThe rate of contraceptive use among the participants was low. Socio‐demographic factors such as education, employment status, and age contribute to unequal outcomes in attitude, knowledge, and contraceptive use among adolescents. Health interventions aimed at addressing inequalities in attitudes, knowledge, and contraceptive use among adolescents in the study setting should target socio‐demographic factors.

Background

Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) has the world's largest proportion of adolescents, constituting about 20% of global youth and nearly a quarter of SSA's population [1]. Yet contraception coverage in SSA remains far below other regions, with only about 56% of family planning needs met with modern methods. Further, two‐thirds of SSA countries report modern contraceptive use below 20%, often < 5% in West Africa [1]. In fact, barely one in five married women in SSA uses any contraception [2]. Over 90% of the world's adolescent births and unsafe abortions occur in low‐income regions like SSA, and SSA accounts for about 94% of the global 600,000 annual pregnancy‐related deaths [1]. High unmet need in SSA translates into unsafe abortion, HIV/STI exposure, and poverty [1].

Findings from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey indicate that approximately 15% of females aged 15–19 years have experienced pregnancy, with 11% having already given birth [3]. Adolescents in the Volta regions recorded low policy awareness regarding reproductive health services after years of implementation of national policies [4]. In 2016, the region reported 10,296 adolescent pregnancies, accounting for 15% of all regional births [5, 6]. Contraceptive use and adoption rates among adolescents remain suboptimal, with only 18%–22% reporting consistent use. Usage patterns reveal that older adolescents aged 18–19 and those with higher educational attainment are more likely to utilize contraception, indicating disparities in access, awareness, and socio‐cultural influences [7].

Adolescent contraceptive use is influenced by several factors, including cultural, religious, systemic, interpersonal, and individual factors [8, 9, 10]. In some Ghanaian communities, contraceptive use among unmarried girls faces social disapproval, with limited parental communication, partner support, and healthcare systems, such as fears of judgment from providers, logistical issues like long waiting times and stock shortages, and the requirement of parental consent, further hindering access [3, 7, 11, 12]. Adolescents also harbour misconceptions and fears, such as concerns about side effects and infertility, which deter contraceptive use despite adequate knowledge. These barriers, including myths, stigma, and misinformation, can prevent even well‐informed adolescents from utilizing contraception effectively [13, 14, 15]. Despite extensive research on adolescent fertility in Ghana, significant gaps persist, particularly regarding how knowledge, attitudes, and access barriers influence contraceptive behaviour. The Volta Region, particularly the Ho Municipality, remains an understudied area, despite having some of the highest adolescent pregnancy rates in the country [16]. This study seeks to fit models that predict knowledge, attitudes, and contraceptive use among Adolescents in the Ho Municipality. Addressing this gap, this current study aims to elucidate the possible predictors that impact contraceptive behaviour, to inform interventions that support Ghana's progress toward good health, well‐being, and gender equality among the youth.

Materials and Methods

This study adopted the ‘Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology’ (STROBE) statement in all stages [17].

Study Design, Site, and Population

A cross‐sectional design, with a convenience sampling method, was employed in studying knowledge, attitude, and contraceptive use among adolescents in the Ho Municipality. The capital of the Volta Region is Ho, and it is situated in the southeast between Mount Adaklu and Mount Galenukui of the Togo Atakora Range (coordinates 6°36′43″ N 0°28′13″ E). It serves as the government, commerce, and education. The Municipality has a diverse population in terms of ethnicity, age, and socio‐economic background, totalling 177,281 people‐8.4% of the region's population, with females making 52.7% [18]. The Municipal has institutions and facilities, which influence access to reproductive health information and contraceptive care. Examining these dynamics is essential for understanding the factors that affect adolescent contraceptive behaviour and identifying ways to strengthen the support systems. Adolescents aged 10 to 19 years who lived in the municipality for not less than 6 months, were not sick, and were willing to participate were included in the study.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Procedure

A sample of 841 adolescents was determined using the Soper [19] sample size calculator, ensuring a larger sample of adolescents is included for better model fit [20]. Adolescents who satisfied the inclusion criteria were selected using convenience sampling. Potential non‐response error was reduced by designing the questionnaire to focus on the objectives, encouraging the adolescents to participate, giving friendly reminders, and assisting those who could not read in completing the questionnaire.

Measures

A structured questionnaire was used, including validated instruments [2, 21, 22]. For accuracy in completing the self‐administered questionnaire, adolescents were given clear instructions.

Outcome Variables

Primary Outcome: Contraceptive use was the main outcome variable in this study. An adapted 7‐item scale, coded as 1 (highly disagree) to 5 (highly agree), was used. The scale reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.86.

Secondary Outcome: Knowledge and Attitude were considered secondary outcomes in the study. A scale with ten items was used to measure knowledge, where each was coded on a scale of 1 (highly disagree) to 5 (highly agree). The minimum and maximum total scores were 10 and 50, respectively. The scale was reliable and reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.93.

Attitude towards contraceptive use was measured with a fifteen‐item scale coded as 1=highly disagree to 5=highly agree, having a minimum and maximum scores of 15 and 75, respectively. This scale was reliable and reported a Cronbach's α of 0.95.

Socio‐Demographics

This study examined various socio‐demographic factors, including age, gender, employment status, educational attainment, religious affiliation, marital status, residential area, number of children, and ethnicity. Before conducting regression analysis, some of these categorical variables were reclassified to better suit the modelling process. The demographic data provided a comprehensive overview of the population's characteristics, facilitating a detailed analysis of how these factors may influence the study outcomes.

Data Transformation

The knowledge and attitude raw scores were dichotomized as good when the score was above the overall mean composite score and poor when the score was less than or equal to the mean composite score. Similarly, contraceptive use scores above the mean composite score signify usage, while composite scores less than or equal to the mean composite score were coded as no usage. Also, Primary and Junior High levels of education were reclassified as Basic education [23].

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive results were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical data, while mean and standard deviation were reported for continuous data. The Chi‐square test was a bivariate analysis used to assess associations between categorical variables. Three logistic regression models were used to fit the models for socio‐demographic determinants of contraceptive use, knowledge, and attitude towards contraceptive use among adolescents. The possible determinants from the bivariate analyses with p‐values of at most 0.20 were included in the logistic regression models. Chi‐square and logistic regression tests were done using a 5% level of significance in the R statistical package and the statistical package for social scientists, SPSS software version 27.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the University of Health and Allied Science Research Ethics Committee (UHAS‐REC A.5 [12] 23–24). To obtain approval for data collection, a cover letter was requested from the School of Nursing and Midwifery and sent to the Ho Municipal Health Directorate. Written consent was obtained from adolescents aged 18 and above before participating in the study. In addition, parents or guardians of adolescents under the age of 18 received forms for parental consent and child assent. The data collection was done in a secluded, preferred place of the participants to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Only the researcher collecting the data was present during the data collection, and records were kept confidential as required by the institutional ethics review committee. The data emanating from this study were not shared with any third party, and in terms of publication of results, no identifiers of the participants were included. Also, prior arrangements were made with a clinical psychologist to support those who will require the services during the data collection. None of the participants required the services of the psychologist during the data collection period.

Determinants of Knowledge on Contraceptive Use

The overall knowledge of adolescents is 50.9% (Figure 3). From the bivariate analysis (Table 4), the knowledge level of adolescents concerning contraceptive use (p < 0.050) was associated with their sex, age, employment status, education, marital status, ethnicity, and number of children. A logistic regression model was accurately fitted using a likelihood ratio test (Chi‐square statistic=230.24, p < 0.001) with Nagelkerke and Cox & Snell pseudo‐R‐square values of 0.32 and 0.24, respectively. The model also showed no multicollinearity. The logistic regression model for determining factors of good knowledge showed that adolescents who are 14–17 years (aOR=1.81, 95% CI = 1.09, 3.07, p = 0.024), 18 and above years (aOR=4.09, 95% CI = 2.21, 7.66, p < 0.001) are more likely to have good knowledge compared with those who are aged 10 to 13 years. Also, those employed (aOR=3.26, 95% CI = 1.32, 8.65, p = 0.013), had one child (aOR=6.29, 95% CI = 1.03, 43.73, p = 0.049), are more likely to have good knowledge compared with those who are unemployed, had two children, respectively. Those with Basic school education (aOR=0.10, 95%CI = 0.04, 0.22, p < 0.001) or those who have no formal education (aOR=0.07, 95% CI = 0.02, 0.21, p < 0.001) are less likely to have good knowledge of contraceptive use compared with those with a tertiary education. Also, Akans (0.58, 95% CI = 0.35, 0.97, p = 0.040) and adolescents from other ethnic groups (aOR=0.2, 95% CI = 0.08, 0.44, p < 0.001) are less likely to have good knowledge of contraceptive use compared with Ewes, respectively.

Discussion

This study is particularly important because it identified the factors accounting for the relatively low (48.2%) use of contraception among adolescents. Findings revealed that older adolescents were significantly more likely to use contraceptives than younger adolescents. A cross‐sectional study by Nyarko and Amoah et al. found that contraceptive use among Ghanaians increased with age [2, 7]. Also, age correlates with knowledge and decision‐making capacity, which influences contraceptive behavior [24]. Older adolescents were more likely to access and use contraceptives, possibly due to their engagement with health services and peer networks [3, 10] likely due to greater exposure to sexual activity and reproductive health education or increased autonomy and awareness. In Koforidua, despite having some knowledge, adolescents showed low uptake due to fear, stigma, and misinformation [3]. This contradiction highlights that knowledge alone may not translate into use, especially among younger adolescents who face social and psychological barriers. Discrepancy may be a result of the development gap that exists within the age, as older adolescents are likely to have higher knowledge compared to younger ones. Interventions must be age‐specific, with tailored messaging and support systems for younger adolescents to bridge the gap in contraceptive use.

We also found that males were less likely to use contraceptives compared to females. Cultural norms and limited male‐targeted education and maternal health programmes (like antenatal) contribute to low contraceptive uptake [2, 7, 25]. Consequently, male adolescents often lack access to accurate information and feel excluded from reproductive health services, which contradicts the assumption that awareness alone drives use [8]. This is possible because females in the Ghanaian society are both the primary targets and bearers of contraceptive responsibility. The implication is that male‐inclusive education and services are essential to balance responsibility and improve overall contraceptive uptake. Education enhances awareness and access to reproductive health services, leading to higher contraceptive use. There is an association between educational attainment and contraceptive knowledge and use across sub‐Saharan Africa [23, 24, 26]. However, among adolescents with knowledge of contraceptives, uptake was low due to fear and stigma, suggesting that education alone may not overcome social barriers [3]. This demonstrates that education is a facilitator of informed decision‐making, not a direct cause of behavior. University‐aged adolescents in Uganda had higher contraceptive knowledge, largely due to institutional support and targeted programs [22]. Yet misconceptions related to contraceptive use may be directly related to family or community belief systems that fuel complete health knowledge [10]. This points out the importance of content quality and delivery methods in providing health education to adolescents because of the cumulative nature of learning and social interaction over time. Consequently, reproductive health education should begin early and be progressively tailored to developmental stages to ensure an accurate and comprehensive understanding.

The finding that adolescents with one child are more likely to have good knowledge of contraceptive use compared to unemployed adolescents and those with two children suggests that life experience and socioeconomic engagement may enhance awareness. Adolescent mothers often gain knowledge post‐childbirth, especially when accessing maternal health services [6, 8]. This is because of the experiential learning that comes with employment and early parenthood. Reproductive health programs should target unemployed adolescents and those with multiple children with tailored outreach and support. The finding that adolescents with basic education or no formal education, as well as Akans and other ethnic groups, are less likely to have good knowledge of contraceptive use compared to those with tertiary education and Ewes, underscores the intersection of educational and cultural disparities in reproductive health literacy. Cultural beliefs among Akans and other ethnic groups often limit open discussions about contraception, contributing to lower knowledge levels [6]. Klu et al. found that adolescents in Ewe‐majority districts had better access to reproductive health services and education [27]. This reveals the need for culturally responsive and comprehensive sexual education. Reproductive health programs must address both educational access and cultural relevance to effectively improve knowledge among diverse adolescent populations.

Limitations of the Study

This study acknowledged some limitations. Firstly, it draws data from a cross‐sectional survey, which means no causal relationships could be established between the dependent and independent variables. Secondly, this study sampled participants using a convenience sampling method, which may limit the generalisation of the study findings. Convenient sampling had inherent gaps that include the fact that only health‐aware persons are likely to be recruited and may also be influenced by research desirability. Consequently, generalization of the findings to larger adolescent populations ought to be done with caution. Lastly, this study relied on self‐reported data from participants, which could potentially introduce recall bias. By using a large sample and following appropriate standardised methods to measure the dependent variables, the researchers were able to minimise the impact of these limitations on the quality of the study results or findings.

Conclusion

The findings from this study reveal a low rate of contraceptive use among adolescents. Socio‐demographic factors such as education, employment status, age, and ethnicity contributed to disparities in attitudes, knowledge, and contraceptive use. These findings could guide the development of health policies and interventions that promote positive attitudes, knowledge, and contraceptive use. The results could also help social workers and health professionals—such as doctors, nurses, and midwives—better understand the factors affecting adolescents' attitudes, knowledge, and contraceptive use, aiding the development of tailored interventions to promote uptake. Future studies should utilise longitudinal data with both quantitative and qualitative methods to examine specific adolescents' attitudes, knowledge, and contraceptive use. Also, tailoring developed interventions and testing them within poor resource settings, taking into consideration the factors influencing uptake among adolescents.
