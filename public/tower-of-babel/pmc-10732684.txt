Integrated services for neglected tropical diseases and mental health: pilot study assessing acceptability, feasibility and attitudes in Benue State, Nigeria
Authors: Taiwo Obindo, Julian Eaton, Paul Tsaku, Emeka Nwefoh, Philip Ode, Theresa Baird, Pius Sunday, Tolulope Afolaranmi
Published: 2023

Introduction

Mental distress, mediated largely by stigma and social exclusion, has a major impact on the lives of people affected by neglected tropical diseases (NTDs). There is high comorbidity with depression and anxiety and elevated rates of suicide.1 In a linked article,2 we describe these factors in the context of Benue State, in the North Central Region of Nigeria. Despite high rates of depression and anxiety, we found very little access to support for mental health problems and substantial concerns expressed by the people affected about social and economic stresses they experience, which are recognised determinants of mental ill health.

The World Health Organization (WHO) road map for NTDs (2021–30)3 lays out a progressive agenda, promoting a paradigm shift towards greater national leadership in elimination efforts, with impacts being based on local priorities, and integrated approaches that promote a more person-centred approach to care and support. These changes provide an excellent policy framework for better addressing mental health problems associated with NTDs and demand concrete intervention models for meeting the broader range of needs of people with NTDs.4

Lessons from the field of global mental health suggest that provision of care for people with mental comorbidities is integrated care, usually located at the primary care or district level.5

This project was implemented by two non-governmental organisations (NGOs) working in mental health and NTDs (CBM Global and The Leprosy Mission [TLM] Nigeria) in collaboration with academic partners at the University of Jos. The two local government areas of Gboko and Konshisha were identified, as they had significant populations of people with leprosy and lymphatic filariasis (LF) and mental health services are available in primary care centres provided by a Comprehensive Community Mental Health Programme run by Methodist Church Nigeria and the Benue State government Ministry of Health. Benue State is unusual in having such decentralised mental health services, so while it is a useful context in which to conduct this study, it is worth noting that the availability of mental health services for people with comorbid NTDs is the exception rather than the rule in many parts of the world where NTDs are endemic. For example, in Nigeria, the treatment gap for even severe mental conditions is around 80%,6 and this would be higher in more rural ‘end of the road’ communities where NTDs are particularly prevalent.

The service and evaluation ran from February 2020 to September 2021 and involved not only integration of evidence-based mental health care into primary care centres, but engagement with local service user associations and communities to raise awareness of mental well-being and address stigma.

We developed an integrated intervention to address mental health among people affected by LF and leprosy2, through a structured process of Theory of Change. The intervention includes many of the key characteristics of integrated services, such as task shifting, collaborative stepped care for assurance of quality, and engagement with communities to address stigma and facilitate better pathways to care and service uptake. It also draws on recently published guidance for improving mental health and well-being and reducing stigma by the WHO7 and a consortium of NGOs led by the International Federation of Anti-Leprosy Associations (ILEP).8

In this article we describe the formative evaluation of the project, consisting of two linked components: a qualitative study to understand the feasibility, acceptability and potential sustainability of the service from the perspective of service users, caregivers, healthcare professionals and healthcare leaders and an assessment of the attitudes of healthcare staff towards people with NTDs affected by mental health problems (a frequently cited barrier to good quality care that we sought to address in the intervention).

Methods

The sample was made up of adults with obvious physical signs of leprosy or LF living within Benue State who were screened for depression and anxiety disorder using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire9 and the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder questionnaire,10 respectively. These questionnaires have been widely used for screening in general health settings, including in Nigeria. Those who screened positive were referred into the intervention. Qualitative data were collected after the intervention period on acceptability, feasibility and sustainability from people affected by LF and leprosy, their immediate caregivers and healthcare workers. A total of 85 persons affected by LF and leprosy who screened positive for depression and anxiety and had accessed mental health services following their referral as well as their immediate caregivers participated in the study. Of those who screened positive for depression and anxiety and had accessed mental health services, 36 participated in the five focus group discussions (FGDs) for persons affected by leprosy and LF and 25 consenting caregivers of the persons who had accessed mental health services participated in the three FGDs for the caregivers. All participants were >18 y of age and lived in the study area. We also interviewed nine healthcare service providers from all the facilities providing mental health services for NTDs or who worked in NTD coordination. These were community health extension workers (CHEWs) and community health officers (CHOs), who are the main providers of services in primary care in Nigeria and have been trained in the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) in some clinics in Benue State.

To assess any change in the attitudes of healthcare service providers, we collected data before and after mhGAP training11 using the Community Attitudes toward Mental Illness (CAMI) tool.12 mhGAP is designed for use by non-specialist health workers as a guide to identify, treat and refer those with mental, neurological and substance use disorders in primary care settings.11 In Nigeria, the mhGAP Intervention Guide was adapted for local use in 201513 and is specifically recommended as normative guidance in the Nigeria National Policy for Mental Health Service Delivery.14

Data collection

For the qualitative component, interview topic guides were developed for the study in line with the study objectives and following a literature review to understand potential areas of concern for people using NTD and mental health services (Supplementary Annex 1). The FGD guides were translated into the local Tiv language and back-translated into English prior to commencement of data collection. Six research assistants were trained in data collection and then data collection tools were pretested on a small number of people. Prior to the administration of the questionnaires, written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all the respondents, with the assurance of confidentiality and anonymity.

Five FGDs were conducted with men and women with leprosy and LF and three FGDs with their caregivers. The FGDs were conducted in the Tiv language in a quiet location chosen by the self-help groups, using voice recorders and keeping field notes. Additionally, nine key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with healthcare workers providing mental health services to persons with NTDs and the officers in charge of coordinating structures for NTDs and mental health in the study area. The KIIs were conducted in English in the respective offices of the respondents using voice recorders and keeping field notes.

The CAMI scale used for the quantitative aspect of the study was designed to measure the degree of social stigma toward people affected by mental illness8 and has been used in research in a variety of different populations.15 It has four key subscales: authoritarianism (negative attitudes), benevolence (compassion and empathy), social restrictiveness (perceptions of threat and dangerousness) and community mental health ideology (belief in social integration). Each of these items has pro- and anti- components assessed with five questions each on a Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (5). The scores for each CAMI key item were obtained as well as an overall score from the cumulative total.

Data analysis

For the qualitative component of the study, direct transcription of the recordings of each of the FGDs and KIIs was done by two different people skilled in transcription, supplemented by the field notes and cross-checked for synergy and completeness by a third person, following which each of the paired transcripts was harmonised for analysis. NVivo version 12 (QSR International, Burlington, MA, USA) was used for the content analysis where a priori (items identified in advance) and emergent codes were brought out, recurrent themes identified and a summary of findings made in line with the objective of the study.

Data analysis for the quantitative aspect of the study was carried out using SPSS version 23 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The χ2 test was used as a test comparison of the components of the key items of the CAMI tool before and after the intervention, while unpaired Student's t-test was used as a test of difference in mean scores before and after the intervention, except for the antisocial responsiveness scores, where the Mann–Whitney U test was employed as the test of the difference in median.

Results

Findings from the FGDs with service users and caregivers show that the services were deemed acceptable and useful, as they offer affordable treatment, and were judged to improve physical and mental health. Overall, service users felt that they were treated with respect and dignity by the staff and accorded the right privacy, but described examples of both positive and negative treatment from personnel. The service users indicated that the services are easy to use, but mentioned barriers that hindered service accessibility, including distance, convenience, transportation cost and payment for medications. They suggested ways of making treatment easier to access.

Acceptability and perceived usefulness of service

Improvement in physical health

Service users felt that the service improved their physical health:

I used to have headache, body weakness and leg pain but these drugs have taken care of those things remaining the leg pain. (34-year-old man with leprosy)

I feel better because I used to have frequent palpitations with chest pain but this has reduced significantly. (48-year-old woman with leprosy)

Most complaints were of body pains, which were felt to have decreased significantly after making use of the services. Common responses of caregivers support this:

First, Baba had some wound on his hands and the wounds are healing ever since he was given medication and treated. So, for me that is a big difference. (27-year-old female caregiver)

Improvement in mental health and experience of stigma

Service users reported that their mental health improved after using the service. This included improvements in self-esteem, getting over feelings of inferiority, stigma and general mental wellness. For example,

I don't feel so inferior anymore and I feel like that I am a human being and deserve to be among others. (45-year-old man with LF)

I don't feel inferior any longer and I feel free now to express myself. (60-year-old man with leprosy)

The link between the provision of mental health service and stigma reduction was further substantiated by the service users and caregivers. For instance,

Before people used to run away from my house because they said they will get infected but since this treatment, people are coming closer to us so for me that is a big difference. (50-year-old woman with leprosy)

Additionally,

My wife used to be unable to meet with people in a gathering because she always felt they were looking at her legs, so it affected her psychologically but now she is better physically and psychologically especially her state of mind. I believe that she will begin to open up to meeting other people soon. (68-year-old male caregiver)

Ways of making treatment easier

In terms of facilitating easier access to treatment, service users said they would prefer treatment days to be known beforehand, to have money for transportation and to get free drugs:

They should tell us of treatment days ahead of time so we can prepare for it. (50-year-old woman with leprosy)

They also expressed concern about the lack of available drugs:

The doctors are doing well but the drugs are insufficient so if the drugs are increased then it will go a long way. (60-year-old man with leprosy)

Treatment by personnel

With regard to treatment by service personnel, service users said that they were treated well by the staff, with respect and dignity, and accorded the right privacy. However, some complained of long waiting times.

Respect and dignity

Service users reported being treated in an acceptable way compared with the previous pattern of being kept away from others and spoken to rudely, as indicated by one person, implying they had a feeling of control over their own life and decisions:

They have treated me well because if they had spoken to me in a disrespectful way, I would not have come back here at all. (65-year-old man with leprosy)

Caregivers also acknowledged that the staff treated the patients with respect and dignity as attested to by one caregiver:

The programme has helped me. My wife has also spoken very well about this project because I know my wife is never satisfied with anything but she seems satisfied with this one, hence, I believe there was no bad treatment. (58-year-old male caregiver)

In addition, another caregiver expressed this about respect and dignity:

The way one talks to a sick person also is a form of treatment as sick people believe that this sickness can take my life but when the doctors talk to them it comforts them. When they are advising them they tell them to do things that will help improve their health so that is good treatment from the doctors. (53-year-old caregiver)

And referring to how doctors treated people:

They treated [relative] with dignity. When he falls sick and we bring him here they care for him to ensure the sickness stops. (41-year-old female caregiver)

Privacy

Findings showed that the personnel accorded the patients reasonable privacy during treatment. Caregivers reported:

The doctors are seeing the patients one on one which means there is privacy. (38-year-old female caregiver)

There is usually privacy because they advise them generally outside and then call them in one after the other. My mother has never said anything negative about them. (26-year-old female caregiver)

Positive treatment

Service users appreciated the positive attitude from the staff towards them in terms of interaction and encouragement:

Words of encouragement and the way they have attended to me has made me happy. (70-year-old man with leprosy)

Some caregivers described the way that the personnel provide care in terms of attention and follow-up, for example:

One of the positive things I have noticed is that even when my father forgets to come for his drugs, one of the doctors will call him to come for his drugs and they also give him transport money to go back. (32-year-female caregiver)

These doctors pay so much attention to our relatives, when they come in the morning, they are given something to eat before they commence and after treatment they give them transport money to go home. (52-year-old female caregiver)

Negative treatment

Responses also revealed that users had some criticisms, mainly about waiting times:

When we got there [there] were only 2 doctors and for that reason we stayed very long there. If they were more, it will be faster. (75-year-old man with LF)

The bad thing is that sometimes when we come, we sit here for long and we get tired. (44-year-old man with leprosy)

There was a general consensus that increasing the number of staff would reduce the waiting time experienced:

I would say they should increase the number of doctors seeing our people because some people come from home and sit here until 1 pm without eating but if they are like 3 or 4 people will be seen faster so they can go back home instead of waiting for long hours. (58-year-old woman with LF)

Accessibility of the service

Patients indicated that the service is easy to use, but stated that there are barriers that hindered the service accessibility, such as distance, convenience, transportation cost and payment for medication.

Distance and transport costs

Despite the service being made available at a primary level, distance from the clinic was frequently cited as a barrier to accessibility and use of the service:

There are some people that live very far so getting here can be a problem but I believe you can look into that issue to assist them to continue to come. (35-year-old woman with LF)

In addition to time, cost is the main barrier, especially as a caregiver usually accompanies the patient:

The things that will hinder them are transport money sometimes as they look for money to borrow but don't get, so if there are provisions for transport money then it will help. (53-year-old female caregiver)

Furthermore, more participants had this to say regarding transportation-related problems:

It was inconveniencing because I had to look for money to come. Yes, if they can be giving us these drugs in the health facilities that are close to us it will help us with transportation problems. (54-year-old man with leprosy)

The things that will hinder them are transport money sometimes as they look for money to borrow but don't get, so if there are provisions for transport money then it will help. (38-year-old female caregiver)

Convenience

Convenience is another barrier to access and use of the service, especially when service users had other engagements that coincide with treatment days. This is attested to by the following statement:

The only things that will stop me will be…the elders meeting because I am very involved in the affairs of the elders in the community. (77-year-old man with leprosy)

This is made worse by only giving short notice to patients of clinic days:

If it is abrupt then a lot of people will not be available to come, but if the people are informed ahead of time, they will be able to come. (44-year-old man with leprosy)

Service use costs and payment for medication

Service users appreciated the financially accessible treatment, but affordability of drugs for the treatment of NTDs was a common problem:

I believe what will make it difficult will be if they ask them to bring money for treatment then it will be a barrier. (38-year-old woman with leprosy)

I am worried about the fact that I was told I will have to bring money the next time I am coming because that has always been my issue. (60-year-old woman with leprosy)

Additionally, another expressed this:

So if there are more drugs her legs will heal faster. I believe that if she had been taking the drug she is taking now and is always available, she will get help. (60-year-old female caregiver)

If they get more drugs, it will help his condition better. (68-year-old female caregiver)

It is worth noting that many of the services for people affected by NTDs had previously been offered for free, but this had now changed due to cuts in services.

Acceptability for personnel and users

Healthcare staff reported an appreciation of the program and a willingness to provide the service, which was the first time mental health personnel had specifically provided support to people with NTDs:

We are doing well because the first time some clients were referred to us, we attended to them and scheduled appointments for them which is today and they coming with good results. So, I can say it's helpful to them.

As we are treating, counselling and advising them, they are appreciating so we know that we are progressing.

The use of indigenous languages as the means of communication was also seen as important for acceptability:

We use our local language to communicate with them so they understand what we are saying.

Also, the trained personnel are able to use local clinical settings in different local government areas to provide services, which improved access and convenience for service users:

For the first time in many years, the little interaction with people in the villages that are very sick and after the scheme training, they have reasons to live and be happy and they feel people care about them in the villages too.

Effectiveness and impact

Staff felt that the program had shown effectiveness in both mental and physical well-being:

I think the program has worked so far because those with mental problems come to me, I psychoeducate them and they feel better in their mind.

They also pointed to the value of the social and financial support provided to service users, which helped them in taking care of other needs that supported their well-being. One staff member said:

It's good and it's helping people. Some of the patients with NTDs are also getting financial support from you apart from the counselling we are giving. So that financial support has taken them to another level, some have other disease conditions but with the money you gave them they were able to settle some things in their houses and also got other medication.

Capacity of personnel and supervision

In general, personnel appreciated investment in their competencies and the healthcare leaders felt that the training received by personnel equipped them for reaching and working with people with NTDs in rural areas. One participant expressed it this way:

This study again has a capacity building component which is very good and I haven't seen a model like these but I think it's going to really succeed because you are highlighting issues, affected persons and also caregivers are also brought to the same forum to see how we can come up with ways to address their mental health…which is a capacity that we never had before now.

However, some staff requested training, provision of essential equipment and supervision.

Challenges described included the limitations of the training in only covering anxiety and depression, as various other psychological problems existed. Also, there is the challenge of monitoring, evaluation and supervision in the field for quality assurance:

Another thing is service provider is very low capacity and they couldn't remember their trainings and they must need a supervisor to see them through and if we need them to carry out this training effectively, they need more training and manpower to make the program go far.

Feasibility of the project in the local setting

Health service leaders felt that the service was feasible in local settings owing to the perceived success of the project despite the relatively short duration:

I won't go further to make a pronouncement on the impact but like I said it is promising and auspicious and I know that it has been established that this mental health issues are associated with NTDs.

Logistics

Findings showed that there are gaps in capacity and logistics with the program and these are essential components to be addressed before scaling. The lack of proper data on people affected by mental health issues poses an initial challenge:

But when it comes to mental health one needs to identify that there is a problem, do we even have a data of people affected by mental health issues complications arising from NTDs.

Other logistic issues, like transportation and need for more health personnel, were also major problems.

Scalability

There was a desire to see the project replicated to more local government areas in Benue State. The staff had talked to their colleagues about the project and people with NTDs had shared their experiences with peers: One service provider said:

It will work well because I was communicating with my colleague from Vandeikya Local Government Area and she said those patients are there and asking for this service. The last time you came I called her to bring them down here but she said there was no mobility to bring them, so if this program can be expanded there, it will help a lot of people.

What I want is to see how we can scale up this programme because Benue State has a very large burden.

Sustainability

Healthcare providers felt that the project could be sustained through collaboration between government and NGOs participating in the project. Staff tended to identify government as being responsible for sustaining the project, which was modelled around integration in the formal health system, although in this project this had been funded externally:

We implore them to ensure that other implementing partners and Federal Ministry of Health get to see the findings of this research so that going forward we can integrate it with our protocols and strategies when implementing.

However, staff also felt that NGOs could support the program for sustainability, in part because of gaps in government provision and a lack of political will:

Some NGOs that support programs can support because I can't trust the government now as they are unable to pay even salaries. So, if they are not able to pay personnel how can they fund other things.

Personnel and service providers’ motivation

Health personnel stated that training and financial motivation determined their work level and interest in expanding the program:

Well, this all depends on the behaviour of the health workers also because if the workers are well motivated then they will be able to expand the program but it all depends on the way they have been handled. It's not only financial motivation there are motivation in different ways such as calling them for refresher trainings and other ways then they will be willing to take the program to any level.

Attitudes of healthcare personnel

A total of 18 healthcare service providers participated in the study, with a mean age of 45.2±9.6 y with men making up 11 (61%) of the 18 participants. Eleven (61%) had been in practice for >11 y. CHEWs were the most represented and constituted just over half of the participants (Table 1), with doctors, CHOs and community directed distributors making up the other cadres.

Overall, the results show high levels of authoritarianism and desire for control of perceived dangerous behaviour, but also of benevolence and a sense of responsibility of the community to support people with mental health problems. With regards to the individual items, there was little change pre- and post-mhGAP training, save on three items. There was a statistically significant reduction in agreement with the statement that ‘There are sufficient existing services for the mentally ill’ (p=0.003) in the benevolence section and a similar change towards agreement with the statement that ‘The best therapy for many mental patients is to be part of a normal community’ (p=0.003) and away from the statement ‘The mentally ill should be isolated from the rest of the community’ (p=0.031). On the other hand, there was an increase in agreement with the statement ‘Local residents have good reason to resist the location of mental health services in their neighbourhood’ (p=0.014).

Discussion

This project sought to examine the feasibility and acceptability of integration of mental health care and NTDs (leprosy and LF) in a rural and peri-urban setting in North Central Nigeria. The intervention was developed through a participatory process, with local service users, clinicians and health service leaders utilising Theory of Change to apply international guidance to local contextual needs. The result was an intervention that incorporated community activities and health service interventions, including engagement with service user self-help groups, capacity building of service providers and strengthening of relevant system components.

Service users and caregivers reported positively on their perception of the usefulness and positive impact of the service and of its acceptability in terms of user experience, language and affordability, although distance and waiting times remained an issue. Staff reported similar satisfaction with the opportunity to provide mental health care. In terms of feasibility and sustainability, the gaps in basic health systems were apparent, e.g. in terms of health information systems, basic training and supervision and other logistics. Even this short-term project was able to have a positive impact on knowledge and service delivery, but fundamental issues of the number and motivation of staff, as well as supervision systems and quality assurance, remained a concern. We were also unable to demonstrate significant changes in attitudes towards mental health from trained staff, but this is notoriously difficult to achieve in short-term training,16 and this result is perhaps unsurprising as attitude change is not the primary purpose of mhGAP training. Both for professionals and general populations, anti-stigma interventions require more comprehensive approaches, targeting attitudes specifically, e.g. contact interventions.17 However, overall these results imply that even the short-term project was able to demonstrate valuable improvements in access to mental health care within an established primary care system, even in a very-low-resource setting. The major issues of system components (like information systems and drug availability), as well as community and professional stigma, would require a longer-term intervention and study to evaluate, as would the greatest challenge raised, of financial sustainability in light of a lack of government investment.

A recent systematic mapping review of mental health, stigma and NTDs found only 14 health intervention studies of mental health and NTDs.18 There were few universal findings, due to the small number of studies and heterogeneity of interventions, methods and outcomes used. However, all but 2 of the 12 studies reported positive outcomes, including, for leprosy, improvements in depression, anxiety, general mental health and quality of life,19 and decreased cognitive and other disabilities for LF.20 Interventions included cognitive behavioural therapy,21 therapeutic workshops, reminiscence group therapy, empowerment projects and peer counselling, as well as physical interventions like surgical correction.

In order to see applications of these models of change, evidence is only one part of the puzzle. The welcome inclusion of mental well-being and stigma in the new WHO road map is likely to increase national attention and potentially strategic planning and investment in more person-centred approaches. Following recognition of need and a requirement for action, the next step is usually provision of clear guidance, and we hope that this work will contribute to this through essential care packages in mental health and well-being, which in turn should strengthen the hand of local actors with the knowledge and experience to advocate for investment.

Limitations and strengths of the study

The study sought to make use of and evaluate an intervention model that fit into the local health system and community resources. However, limitations in resources and the time frame for the project meant that only specific proposed components of the model could be applied. For example, there was no time for a large-scale anti-stigma intervention, although we recognise the importance of stigma as a driver for mental distress and depression. This was a proof-of-concept study, focused on qualitative assessment of perspectives on acceptability, feasibility and sustainability of the model, and we did not seek to measure quantitative outcome as a measure of impact. While in general we were able to carry out interventions and collect all the data intended, this was made substantially more difficult by the coronavirus disease pandemic and intercommunal violence in the areas in which we were working.

The most significant risk of bias is social desirability, where service users, clinicians and health leaders may have felt the need to report positively, both to please the researchers and also potentially due to a desire to see the project continue.
