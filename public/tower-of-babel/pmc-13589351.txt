Optimized anterior anatomical fusion plate for ankle arthrodesis: biomechanical and clinical outcomes
Authors: Yi Liu, Yaokuan Ruan, Zhende Jiang, Shichen Hu, Aoran Zhang, Nan Mei, Fei Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnkle osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disorder that often necessitates ankle arthrodesis. Conventional fusion methods may offer limited fixation stability and require extensive soft-tissue exposure. The goal of this study was to evaluate the biomechanical properties of a geometrically optimized anterior anatomical fusion plate (AAFP) for ankle arthrodesis, which has been designed to enhance mechanical performance and minimize surgical trauma.MethodsThis study developed a finite element model of the ankle based on CT scans of a healthy male volunteer. Various fusion models were created, incorporating the optimized AAFP with or without transarticular screw, anterolateral fusion plate (ALFP) with or without transarticular screw, and configurations using three or two screws. The biomechanical performance of these systems was assessed by analyzing peak displacement of the fusion surface and peak von Mises stress of implants and bone-implant models under seven common ankle postures. Additionally, the preliminary feasibility of the optimized AAFP was assessed by analyzing the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores and X-ray images.ResultsThe optimized AAFP with transarticular screw fixation exhibited great stability, with peak displacement of 0.14 mm in neutral position, 0.19 mm in dorsiflexion, 0.07 mm in plantar flexion, 0.09 mm in internal rotation, 0.07 mm in external rotation, 0.10 mm in inversion, and 0.12 mm in eversion. The peak stress of the fusion method was relatively low, with 132.91 MPa in neutral position, 145.83 MPa in dorsiflexion, 97.15 MPa in plantar flexion, 84.27 MPa in internal rotation, 74.70 MPa in external rotation, 98.07 MPa in inversion, and 88.00 MPa in eversion. The transarticular screw enhanced biomechanical stability of the optimized AAFP in ankle fusion by reducing stress and displacement. Clinically, the optimized AAFP showed satisfactory short-term outcomes, with improvements in the AOFAS scores (from 53 to 83, 57 to 82, 51 to 84 and 57 to 83) and imaging results.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the geometrically optimized AAFP combines a smaller incision with favorable biomechanical performance and warrants further clinical evaluation as a potential option for ankle fusion.Keywords: Biomechanics, Finite Element Analysis, Fusion and Arthrodesis, Foot and Ankle

Background

Ankle osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative disorder, with trauma being the predominant contributing factor in most cases [1]. Currently, approximately 1% of the global population is affected by ankle OA [2]. For patients with end-stage ankle OA, ankle arthrodesis (AA) is commonly regarded as the preferred treatment due to its broad applicability and relatively high success rates [3]. However, several limitations are associated with conventional plates for AA. This method carries the risk of long operative durations and large surgical incisions, leading to increased trauma for patients [4]. Furthermore, the mismatch between conventional implants and bone anatomy can lead to damage to both the bone and surrounding soft tissues [5]. Such complications may result in severe adverse outcomes, including nonunion [6].

In recent years, implant optimization has attracted significant attention, with various studies investigating innovative design strategies [7–9]. Optimizing implant designs aims to minimize surgical incisions and to enhance mechanical performance, addressing critical challenges in the treatment of OA [10]. Moreover, implants with greater stiffness have been reported to reduce micromotion at articular surface, thereby decreasing the risk of nonunion [11]. These advancements offer valuable insights into improving clinical practice [12]. Among these advancements, the primary advantage of anatomical implant design is its ability to restore anatomical structures and yield favorable clinical outcomes [13]. However, the limited evaluation methods for assessing the performance improvements of optimized implants hinder the design efficiency of joint fusion plates.

Finite element analysis (FEA) is widely employed across various disciplines and has numerous applications in orthopedics [14–16]. In previous studies, multiple mid/hindfoot arthrodesis simulations were conducted using FEA, where internal loadings were compared [17]. Additionally, the biomechanical performance of the optimized internal fixation device has been evaluated using FEA [18]. For example, FEA has been employed to identify the optimal personalized implant size for mandibular fractures [19]. In another study, a research group designed a new anatomical plate based on the scapular anatomy, achieving good biomechanical stability [20]. However, to date there is not any plate available for ankle fusion that has achieved a balance between small incisions and stability.

In this study, we developed an optimized anterior anatomical fusion plate (AAFP) specifically designed to match the anatomical characteristics of the ankle and surrounding soft tissues. The primary goal of this study was to compare the biomechanical properties of fusion methods using optimized AAFP with those of conventional methods. Additionally, the optimized AAFP was applied in clinical practice, and postoperative evaluations were conducted during follow-up to assess clinical outcomes. The hypothesis of this study is that the optimized AAFP demonstrates robust biomechanical performance while preserving a small incision area.

Methods

Modeling

This study was granted approval by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (No. 2025023), and informed consent was obtained from the participant. A healthy 31-year-old male (176 cm in height, 70 kg in weight), with no prior ankle trauma history, was recruited for the study. A 256-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens, Omaha, NE, USA) was used to perform ankle imaging with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. The tibia and talus were reconstructed from CT images using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) and further smoothed with Geomagic 2019 (Geomagic, Cary, NC, USA) (Fig. 1A, B). Standard surgical techniques were simulated to place the implants into the ankle fusion models using SolidWorks 2021 (SolidWorks Co., Concord, MA, USA). To establish the bone fusion surface, the talus was elevated by 2 mm, and the articular cartilage along with the subchondral bone was removed to achieve adequate contact. Screws and plates used in this study were designed and modeled using SolidWorks. To simplify the finite element analysis, the threads of the screws were excluded to reduce computational complexity. Additionally, the anterolateral fusion plate (ALFP) was reconstructed based on designs described in previous literature [21]. Building on this foundation, the optimized AAFP was produced by widening and shortening the plate, and reshaping the profile to match ankle anatomy, thereby reducing stress concentration and enhancing fixation stability (Fig. 1C). These modifications included shortening the plate length, thickening areas prone to stress concentration, and incorporating additional screws to enhance fusion stability. Furthermore, the plate was redesigned based on the removal of articular surface anatomy, aiming to improve its versatility and adaptability in clinical applications. Therefore, optimization in this study specifically referred to the design-level geometric improvements of the plate. The screw fixation methods were determined based on previous literature [22]. Finally, six fusion models were developed to evaluate the mechanical performance of different systems (Fig. 1D): (a) optimized AAFP with 3.5 mm screws combined with a 4.5 mm transarticular screw; (b) ALFP with 3.5 mm screws combined with a 4.5 mm transarticular screw; (c) optimized AAFP with 3.5 mm screws; (d) ALFP with 3.5 mm screws; (e) fusion using three 6.5 mm lag screws; and (f) fusion using two 6.5 mm lag screws.

Finite element analysis

This study employed a previously validated finite element model to ensure the reliability and consistency of the analysis. In our earlier research, the stress distribution in the joint under a neutral position was simulated and validated against published data [23, 24].

The Hypermesh 2019 (Altair Engineering, Troy, MI, USA) was employed for meshing the 3D models with four‑node linear tetrahedral elements (C3D4). A sensitivity analysis was performed in Abaqus 2016 (Dassault Simulia, Paris, France) to identify the optimal element size that balances model accuracy and computational efficiency. Element sizes ranging from 0.25 mm to 2.00 mm were analyzed systematically using the convergence of peak von Mises stress, with results indicating that a mesh size of 1.75 mm provided the best compromise between accuracy and computational cost. Additionally, the implant mesh was further refined by reducing the element size to 0.5 mm, thereby enhancing the evaluation of its mechanical behavior (Fig. 1E).

The material properties used for modeling the different components are listed in Table 1 [25, 26]. Contact between the components was set according to the actual condition. The tibia-talus contact was modeled as face-to-face with finite sliding and a friction coefficient of 0.4 [27]. The bone-screw and plate-screw interfaces were defined with tie constraints to simulate the initial fixation state. To simulate the neutral position, a vertical load of 350 N, corresponding to half of the subject’s body weight, was applied to the proximal surface of the tibia. Subsequently, a 10 N·m torsional moment was applied to simulate functional ankle movements (dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, internal rotation, external rotation, inversion, and eversion) [28]. These loads including force and moment were applied at the ankle joint center of rotation and were kinematically coupled to the proximal surface of the tibia. Additionally, to simulate the effect of lag screws, a uniform pressure of 50 MPa was applied perpendicular to the top surface of each lag screw [29]. The inferior surface of the talus was fully fixed in six degrees of freedom (Fig. 1F). The biomechanical performance of each fusion method was assessed by recording the peak displacement at the fusion surface and the peak von Mises stress of implants and bone-implant models.

Preliminary observational feasibility of the optimized AAFP

A preliminary observational feasibility series was conducted to describe the short-term clinical application of the optimized AAFP. The surgical procedure is outlined below (Fig. 1G). Following general anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine, and the ankle was cleaned and draped. A 6 cm anterior incision was made to expose the joint, and the optimized AAFP was implanted and secured with screws. In cases of poor bone quality, a transarticular screw was inserted through the medial malleolus. After thorough irrigation, drainage tubes were placed, and the incisions were closed. Additionally, postoperative evaluations included American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) score assessment, with values recorded preoperatively and at three months postoperatively. Furthermore, patients underwent regular ankle X-rays to assess ankle fusion and bone healing.

Results

Peak displacement of the fusion surface

The peak displacement of the fusion surface under different fusion methods and loading conditions is presented in Fig. 2. Under all loading conditions, optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation exhibited the lowest peak displacement (Table 2). Specifically, under plantarflexion and external rotation loads, optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation showed the lowest peak displacement (0.07 mm). In contrast, under plantarflexion load, double-screw fixation demonstrated the highest peak displacement (0.49 mm). The application of transarticular screws to both optimized AAFP and ALFP fixation substantially reduced the peak displacement at the fusion surface. For instance, under dorsiflexion load, the peak displacement for optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation was 0.19 mm, compared to 0.39 mm for optimized AAFP fixation without transarticular screw. Similarly, for ALFP combined with transarticular screw fixation, the peak displacement was 0.22 mm under neutral loading condition, whereas ALFP fixation without transarticular screw exhibited a peak displacement of 0.42 mm under external rotation load.

Peak stress of the implant and the bone-implant model

The von Mises stress of the implant and the bone-implant model under different fusion methods and loading conditions are shown in (Figs. 3 and 4). Excluding the double-screw fixation under neutral load, the peak stresses in all cases were concentrated within the implant region. Under all loading conditions, optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation exhibited the lowest peak stresses (Tables 3 and 4). Specifically, under external rotation load, optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation demonstrated the lowest peak stress (74.70 MPa). In contrast, under plantarflexion load, double-screw fixation showed the highest peak stress (402.08 MPa). The addition of transarticular screws substantially reduced implant and bone-implant model stress. For optimized AAFP combined with transarticular screw fixation, the peak stress under dorsiflexion load was 145.83 MPa, compared to 279.50 MPa for optimized AAFP fixation without transarticular screw. Furthermore, under neutral load, the peak stress for ALFP combined with transarticular screw fixation was 200.46 MPa, while ALFP fixation without transarticular screw exhibited a peak stress of 273.53 MPa.

Results of the preliminary observational feasibility series

Four patients (1 male, 3 female) with ankle osteoarthritis were treated using the optimized AAFP (mean age 51.8 ± 20.1 years, mean body weight 64.3 ± 18.8 kg). None of the patients experienced complications such as vascular or nerve injuries, wound infections, or implant fractures. The AOFAS score of patients improved from 53 to 83, 57 to 82, 51 to 84 and 57 to 83 for Patient A, Patient B, Patient C and Patient D. Follow-up radiographs obtained at 3 months postoperatively demonstrated satisfactory fusion outcomes (Fig. 5). These short-term changes in clinical scores and radiographic findings were accompanied by observable improvements in gait.

Discussion

The most important contributions of this study were the design of the optimized AAFP and the evaluation of its mechanical properties. The results indicated that the peak displacement and stress of the optimized AAFP with transarticular screw fixation were lower than those observed in the other five fusion methods. These findings indicate improved construct mechanics under the simulated loading conditions. In plate fixation, the insertion of a transarticular screw significantly reduced both peak displacement and von Mises stress, suggesting that this method enhances fixation effectiveness. In terms of stress distribution, a more uniform stress pattern suggests that the design better aligns with the anatomical characteristics of ankle fusion. Additionally, in most cases, peak stress was observed in the implant, indicating that the implant carried a larger share of the load. This load transfer may also reduce mechanical stimulation to the bone. Overall, the optimized AAFP incorporated optimized geometric and morphological parameters of the fusion plate. Compared with existing anterior or anterolateral plating systems, the optimized AAFP has a shorter, wider, and locally thickened anatomical profile. These modifications may help reduce implant prominence and soft-tissue exposure while maintaining sufficient construct rigidity for ankle fusion. This enhancement may help reduce the likelihood of incision-related complications and may provide a more favorable biomechanical environment that could potentially lower the risk of nonunion.

Most of the existing literature has compared the effectiveness of various AA methods. Common methods for AA include internal fixation with plates, screws, and retrograde intramedullary nails [30–32]. Since Albert first proposed AA in 1879, over 40 distinct methods have been developed [31]. In the case of normal bone quality, Abidi et al. [33] recommend the use of double-crossed screws to achieve rigid fixation. Recent studies utilizing arthroscopic AA have shown no significant difference in efficacy between double-screw and three-screw fixation [34]. However, biomechanical experiments conducted by Alonso-Vázquez et al. [35] have demonstrated that three-screw fixation offers superior stability. Our study similarly found differences in stability between double-screw and three-screw fixation. However, arthroscopic AA supports only screw fixation and may not provide adequate stability, potentially leading to complications [36]. Furthermore, consistent with previous reports that plate fixation can increase construct stiffness, our finite element results showed that constructs using plate fixation generally produced lower displacement at the fusion surface than fixation using screws alone [22, 37]. Additionally, we observed that the peak displacement of the optimized AAFP is lower compared to ALFP. Across the seven loading conditions, mean fusion surface displacement and implant stress were lower for the optimized AAFP than for the ALFP, decreasing from 0.25 to 0.20 mm and from 206.99 to 163.66 MPa, respectively. This could be attributed to improved bending and torsional rigidity resulting from increased plate width and enhanced anatomical conformity. Building upon this, we also found that the addition of a transarticular screw further limited ankle displacement in all directions, thereby enhancing overall construct stability. The plate geometry may primarily improve bending and torsional rigidity, whereas the transarticular screw likely provides additional compression and multidirectional constraint across the fusion surface.

One of the major complications of AA is nonunion and reducing its incidence has consistently been a central objective of the procedure [37]. Previous research has demonstrated variations in nonunion rates associated with different fusion methods [37, 38]. Although screw fixation yields favorable outcomes in most patients, studies have reported that the incidence of nonunion may be as high as 35% [39]. Another study found a nonunion rate of 15.4% among patients treated with compression screws, in contrast to a nonunion rate of 7.7% observed in patients who underwent anterior plate fixation [36]. It is widely recognized that bone nonunion is strongly correlated with excessive micromotion at the articular surface [40]. Prissel et al. [41] and Nicot et al. [42] demonstrated that plate fixation was associated with a lower nonunion rate due to its enhanced stability, which is consistent with the observed trend of lower displacement in our study. In this study, the displacement at the fusion surface in the optimized AAFP was lower than that in the screw fixation group, suggesting a more favorable biomechanical environment for fusion. Additionally, the compression provided by the transarticular screw may improve contact between the fusion surfaces and potentially support bone healing.

Beyond construct stability, incision-related complications are another important consideration in AA, which provides the rationale for developing a shorter and more anatomically contoured anterior plate. Numerous studies have conducted retrospective analyses of incision-related complications [43–46]. Van et al. [43] reported that 21.4% of 41 patients with ankle OA developed complications, six of which were attributable to surgical incisions. In contrast, Miller et al. [44] found that the incidence of incision-related complications was as low as 4.2%. Although the incidence of complications varies across studies, this issue remains a critical focus. Our research explores the potential to mitigate incision-related burden by reducing surgical incision size. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that smaller incisions offer greater clinical benefits in reducing complications [46]. In ankle fusion, the use of longer plates increases the stimulation to the poor soft tissue envelope of the distal tibia [47]. Furthermore, excessive implant volume can lead to increased tension at the incision site, potentially impairing the healing of surgical incisions [48]. Compared with the ALFP, the shorter profile of the optimized AAFP may reduce implant prominence and the extent of soft-tissue exposure. The optimized AAFP fusion method was implemented in a preliminary clinical case series and enabled a smaller incision. In this preliminary case series, no postoperative complications were observed, while the incision size was reduced from 10 to 15 cm to 6 cm [30, 49]. The AOFAS scores and X-ray findings provided preliminary clinical observations suggesting good bone-implant conformity and generally acceptable short-term outcomes of the optimized AAFP (Fig. 5). However, given the preliminary study design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in larger clinical studies.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the model used in this study was necessarily simplified in terms of anatomical structures, thread geometry, and material properties. The omission of the fibula and ligamentous structures may alter load sharing across the fusion construct, while the homogeneous isotropic bone assumption may affect stress estimation at the implant-bone interface. In addition, the omission of screw threads and the use of tie constraints may underestimate bone-screw interface micromotion. These simplifications may influence the absolute stress and displacement values and limit the generalizability of the findings for patients with ankle OA or poor bone quality. Further biomechanical experiments are needed to validate the findings in our study. Secondly, the seven loading conditions simulated in this study were based on idealized scenarios, which may not fully capture the complexities of actual motion. Thirdly, although we have compared several of the most common clinical procedures, this may not provide a comprehensive representation of the various joint fusion methods. Finally, the sample size and follow-up data in this study remain limited. Moreover, because the fibula was omitted from the finite element model but preserved in all clinical cases, the biomechanical simulation results may not be directly comparable with the clinical observations. Future work will expand the sample size and incorporate a broader range of fusion methods for comparative evaluation and validation.

In conclusion, the fusion method utilizing the geometrically optimized AAFP with a transarticular screw demonstrated favorable biomechanical stability. Preliminary observational feasibility data also suggest acceptable short-term clinical feasibility. Due to its smaller surgical incision and anatomical design, the optimized AAFP may offer potential clinical advantages, which require validation in prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up. Additionally, this study provides data that may serve as a reference for clinicians in evaluating the biomechanical effects of various fusion methods.
