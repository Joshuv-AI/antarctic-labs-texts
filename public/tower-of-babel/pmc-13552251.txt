Effectiveness of Resistance Training in People With Down Syndrome: Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: María Jesús Benzo Iglesias, Lucía Torres Álamo, Alejandro Salazar, Patricia Rocamora Pérez, David Manuel Lucena Antón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of resistance training (RT) in people with Down syndrome (DS) as a way of improving strength, physical and metabolic function, and possible adverse effects.MethodsSystematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs in PubMed, PEDro, Scopus, and WoS databases in September‐2023, registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022312810). Methodological quality and risk of bias were analysed using the PEDro scale, the Downs & Black Checklist, and the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias Tool.ResultsSixteen articles were included. RT increased the strength (SMD = 1.88; 95% CI = (1.26; 2.51); I 2 = 72%) and improved physical function (SMD = −0.58; 95% CI = (−1.06; −0.11); I 2 = 13%), but there were no clear results on whether it improved anthropometric and cardiorespiratory measures. There were no serious adverse effects.ConclusionAn increase in overall and maximal muscle strength and improved physical function was produced by RT in DS people. RT is a safe technique as serious adverse effects were not produced.

Background

Down syndrome (DS), caused by trisomy 21, is the most common congenital anomaly, affecting 1 in 700 live newborns in the 1980s (Stoll et al. 2015). It is characterised by a complex phenotype, including short stature, atlantoaxial instability, intellectual disability, congenital heart defects, and metabolic disorders, along with significant musculoskeletal impairments, such as hypotonia, ligamentous laxity, and early loss of muscle mass and strength (Antonarakis et al. 2020; Bull 2020; Capone et al. 2018; Coelho‐Junior et al. 2019; Foley and Killeen 2019; Lejeune et al. 1959; Sugimoto et al. 2016; Whooten et al. 2018).

This muscle weakness, often intensified by sedentary behaviour and pain, compromises gait coordination, increases energy expenditure, and hinders activities of daily living (ADL) (Agiovlasitis et al. 2009; De Miguel Rubio et al. 2018; Kubo and Ulrich 2006; Lin and Wuang 2012). However, physical activity in this population counteracts these deficits, improving cardiorespiratory function, musculoskeletal strength and metabolic health (Sugimoto et al. 2016; Lambourne and Donnelly 2011; Legerlotz 2018; Strong et al. 2005; Wilson and Clayton 2010).

Resistance training (RT), defined as exercises using resistive loads to improve health (Lloyd et al. 2014), offers a wide range of benefits, including improved body composition, injury and metabolic chronic disease prevention and psychological well‐being (ten Hoor et al. 2015). In clinical settings, RT has proven effective for managing conditions such as joint hypermobility (Kemp et al. 2010), pain derived from juvenile idiopathic arthritis (Catania et al. 2017; Kuntze et al. 2018) or muscle weakness in children with infantile cerebral palsy without adverse effects on spasticity or range of motion (Legerlotz 2018; Kemp et al. 2010; Catania et al. 2017; Kuntze et al. 2018; Scholtes et al. 2010, 2012; Ruiz‐González et al. 2019). In the specific context of DS, RT is proposed to attenuate strength loss, promote functional capacity, and assist in obesity management by increasing muscle mass (Legerlotz 2018; Cowley et al. 2010; Colella et al. 2009; Westerterp et al. 1995; ten Hoor et al. 2014). This is particularly relevant as strength‐based interventions may be better tolerated than aerobic exercise in obese individuals with lower endurance capacity (Legerlotz 2018; ten Hoor et al. 2014).

RT interventions can be implemented through various modalities, ranging from progressive resistance exercise (PRE), which emphasises progressive overload as muscle strength increases, providing enough resistance to keep the number of repetitions (reps) relatively low (8–12 reps) before muscle fatigue appears (Scholtes et al. 2012); to plyometric training, which has shown benefits in improving the strength and power of the lower extremities, and maximum oxygen volume (VO2max) (Johnson et al. 2011). Despite these potential benefits, there is a lack of specific reviews analysing RT outcomes in this population. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to evaluate the effectiveness of RT in people with DS as a way to improve strength and other variables related to physical and metabolic function, as well as to assess the potential adverse effects derived from RT.

Methods

Design

A systematic review was reported considering the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA) (Moher et al. 2009) recommendations. It has been registered in the PROSPERO International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (CRD42022312810).

Data Sources and Searches

Articles were searched electronically in August–September 2023, in PubMed, PEDro, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, without language or publication date restrictions. The following descriptors were used: ‘Down syndrome’, ‘training’, ‘exercise’, ‘resistance training’, ‘strength training’, ‘physical fitness’, ‘strengthening’, ‘isokinetic training’ and ‘isokinetic’. MESH (Medical Subject Headings) terms were used in the PubMed database whenever possible, as they provide greater precision in searches. Exclusion criteria: Quasi‐randomised clinical trials, pilot studies, preliminary studies, or study protocols were not considered. Studies that performed any exercise intervention in addition to strength exercise in the experimental group (e.g., aerobic exercise and RT) were excluded.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two independent authors screened the titles and abstracts, eliminating duplicates and identifying potentially relevant articles according to the previously established criteria. The full texts of those studies were acquired. In case of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted.

We extracted the following characteristics from each study, whenever possible: number of participants, participants' characteristics, interventions, duration of treatment, outcome measures and results. All extracted results were presented using tables and summarised in the text.

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

To assess the methodological quality of the RCTs, the PEDro scale was used (Foley et al. 2003; Maher et al. 2003) and the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias Tool was used to assess the risk of bias (Hartling et al. 2009).

In addition, all studies were analysed with the Downs & Black Quality Assessment Checklist (Downs and Black 1998), which is a recommended tool for systematic reviews that include different study designs.

These assessments were performed by two authors independently. In case of discrepancies, the final decision was discussed with a third author.

Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analyses were conducted to compare the standardised mean differences (post‐pre) between the intervention and control groups. The following five main outcomes were considered, along with their subgroup analyses:

First: strength, with subgroups for handgrip strength, bench press and leg press. Second: anthropometric variables, including subgroups for weight, body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference. Third: physical function, including grocery shelf task and timed stairs test. Fourth: cardiorespiratory fitness, with subgroups for VO2max, HRmax and minute ventilation. Fifth: work task performance, with one subgroup: weighted cube transport test.

The presence of heterogeneity in each subgroup was determined using the chi‐square test and the I 2 statistic. Random‐effects models were fitted accordingly. The results are shown with the 95% confidence interval and are represented in forest plots. The significance level was set to p < 0.05.

Analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3.

Characteristics of the Participants

Age ranged from 7 to 50 years, with eight studies focusing on adulthood (over 20 years old) (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011), two other studies with children (under 12 years of age) (Eid et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2011), two studies ranging from adolescence to young adults (from 13 to 19 years old) (Shields and Taylor 2010; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), two studies included children and adolescents (González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Shields et al. 2013) involved adolescents and adults, and (Dilek et al. 2018) included participants of all ages.

Regarding sex, in most studies, there was a greater number of male participants (Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; Eid et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Shields et al. 2013), and in five studies, only men participated (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021). In the study by (Dilek et al. 2018) there was equality in the number of participants of each sex, and only in the study by (Shields et al. 2008) was there a greater number of females.

Type of Intervention

Therapy Used by the Comparison Group

All studies had a single comparison group. Most studies compared RT with a control group (CG) without an intervention program (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; Gupta et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018), except for three studies in which the CG performed some intervention: the study by (Shields et al. 2013), in which the CG carried out a social program, the study by (Eid et al. 2017), in which the CG carried out a conventional physical therapy program, and the study by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), in which the CG carried out an aerobic exercise program. It consisted of a physical activity programme focused on aerobic capacity and motor development with a total session duration of 60 min divided into three phases: a 10‐min warm‐up, followed by a 45‐min main aerobic block (comprising treadmill walking, adapted or modified group games, sports, and coordination/balance exercises), and concluding with a 5‐min cool‐down period.

Types of Resistance Training

Various types of RT were implemented across the 16 trials included. Seven trials performed an RT circuit with 6 stations (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010), except (Shields et al. 2013), which had 7 stations due to adding a trunk exercise. Several studies conducted resistance and balance training (Shin et al. 2021; Gupta et al. 2011), with (Gupta et al. 2011) focusing especially on the lower extremities. Eid et al. (2017) conducted an isokinetic training program after a conventional physical therapy program. Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. (2019) performed a PRE in circuit and Tabata. González‐Agüero et al. (2014) and Suarez‐Villadat et al. (2024) implemented RT programs focused on the development of physical fitness. Two studies conducted a non‐circuit PRE (Cowley et al. 2011; Dilek et al. 2018).

Material Resources

Regarding material resources, nine of the studies used weight machines (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Cowley et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010). Eid et al. (2017) used an isokinetic dynamometer. The study by (Gupta et al. 2011) used sandbags, while (Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019) used medicine balls, elastic bands, weighted discs, dumbbells and hand exercisers. In (González‐Agüero et al. 2014), jumping, push‐ups, and exercises with elastic bands and medicine balls were used. Shin et al. (2021) used elastic bands for upper extremities exercises, and in the study by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), dumbbells, TRX, steps, elastic and resistance bands, and balls were used. Only (Dilek et al. 2018) did not describe the material used in the intervention.

Resistance Training Dosage

Six studies performed 2 sets of exercises (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Gupta et al. 2011), five carried out 3 sets of exercises (Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; Eid et al. 2017; Shields and Taylor 2010; Shields et al. 2013), the study by (Dilek et al. 2018) conducted only 1 set, the study by (Shields et al. 2008) performed 2–3 sets depending on the progression of the participants, and the study by (González‐Agüero et al. 2014) carried out 1–2 sets. Two studies did not describe the number of sets performed (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019).

The number of repetitions generally ranged between 8 and 12 (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2021; Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Cowley et al. 2011; Eid et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010; Dilek et al. 2018), except for four studies that performed between 6 and 10 repetitions (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014), the study by (González‐Agüero et al. 2014), that conducted between 10 and 20 repetitions, and the study by (Shin et al. 2021), that performed 15 repetitions. Two studies did not report the number of repetitions (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019).

Five trials included rest times between exercises and/or sets of 90 s (sec) (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021). Three performed 2 min (min) rest (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010). Eid et al. (2017) performed 3 min rest, González‐Agüero et al. (2014) performed a 30 s rest. Six trials did not report rest time between exercises and/or sets (Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; Gupta et al. 2011; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018).

Session Frequency

The frequency of the intervention varied across studies: Ten studies conducted 3 sessions per week (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shin et al. 2021; Eid et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2011; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Dilek et al. 2018), five studies performed 2 sessions per week (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Cowley et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010; González‐Agüero et al. 2014), and the study by (Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019) conducted 5 sessions per week.

Total Duration of the Intervention Program

Regarding the total duration of the intervention program, six studies conducted the study for 12 weeks (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Eid et al. 2017), four studies conducted it for 10 weeks (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Cowley et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010), two studies lasted 16 weeks (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019), the study by (Gupta et al. 2011) lasted 6 weeks, the study by (González‐Agüero et al. 2014) for 21 weeks, the study by (Shin et al. 2021) for 8 weeks, and the study by (Dilek et al. 2018) conducted the intervention for 24 and 48 weeks.

Session Duration

Nine of the 16 included studies did not specify the duration of the sessions (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Cowley et al. 2011; Gupta et al. 2011; Shields and Taylor 2010). Excluding warm‐up and cool‐down time, two studies performed 10–20 min of RT (Eid et al. 2017; González‐Agüero et al. 2014), although (Eid et al. 2017) previously performed 45 min of conventional physical therapy. Five other studies performed 30–45 min of RT (Shin et al. 2021; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Shields et al. 2013; Dilek et al. 2018).

Sub‐Analysis of Male Participants

Given the greater representation of men in the analysed studies (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021), a sub‐analysis was conducted in this population, alongside the sex‐disaggregated data from (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), comprising a total of 187 male participants with DS. Unfortunately, insufficient data prevented a similar analysis in female participants.

Physical fitness results regarding strength were heterogeneous across the studies. Two studies (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2014, 2021) reported non‐significant improvements in upper limb strength following RT. In contrast, Suarez‐Villadat et al. (2024) found significant gains in both upper (p = 0.009) and lower (p = 0.041) limb strength in the male RT group compared to the aerobic exercise group.

Regarding functional tasks, Fornieles et al. (2014) observed a significant improvement in the weighted box stacking test (p = 0.0141). In terms of cardiorespiratory fitness, Suarez‐Villadat et al. (2024) reported significant benefits in the RT group compared to aerobic exercise (p = 0.014) as well as in 6MWT (p = 0.025), although the aerobic group showed superior improvements in motor fitness (p = 0.038). Note on sex differences: Suarez‐Villadat et al. (2024) reported that female participants generally showed similar positive trends in physical fitness, except for motor fitness, where women in the RT group outperformed the aerobic group, unlike their male counterparts [upper (p = 0.029) and lower (p = 0.032) limb strength, cardiorespiratory fitness (p = 0.018), 6MWT (p = 0.012) and motor fitness (p = 0.041)].

Evidence on body composition was conflicting. Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. (2013) found a significant improvement in waist circumference (p = 0.0416) and fat‐free mass (p = 0.011). Conversely, Suarez‐Villadat et al. (2024) found no differences in waist circumference between the RT and aerobic groups in male participants, although they did observe a significant improvement in hip circumference (p = 0.001). Note on sex differences: a significant difference in hip circumference was also found in females, but due to an increase in the aerobic exercise group (p = 0.024).

The most consistent findings in the male cohort were related to biochemical markers and hormonal profile (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021). RT significantly improved inflammatory and oxidative status compared to controls, including reductions in leptin, TNF‐α, IL‐6, malondialdehyde, and 80HdG (all p < 0.05) (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014), alongside increases in antioxidant capacity (TAS, GSH, plasma ferric reducing capacity) (all p < 0.05) (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2014, 2021). Hormonally, Fornieles et al. (2014) observed a significant increase in IgA levels (p = 0.012) and testosterone levels (p = 0.008), with no changes in cortisol levels.

Finally, no adverse effects related to RT interventions were reported in these five studies (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024).

The study by Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. (2019) also differentiated the results between males and females, but we did not include it in this sub‐analysis since the age group (8–16 years) was very different from the rest of the articles included (17–31 years) (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024).

Sub‐Analysis of Adult Participants

As in the previous section, a sub‐analysis was performed on the eight studies that included exclusively adult participants (aged 20–50 years) (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021) to evaluate the effectiveness of RT in adults with DS, comprising a total sample of 262 participants.

Within physical fitness, five studies evaluated strength outcomes with generally positive but variable results (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011). Most reported significant improvement in strength parameters; however, exceptions were noted. Shin et al. (Shin et al. 2021) found no significant changes in handgrip strength. Similarly, Shields et al. (2008) showed no significant changes in lower limb strength and muscle endurance, although they observed significant improvements in upper limb muscle endurance (p = 0.002) and a trend towards improvement in upper limb strength (p = 0.08). In terms of balance, Shin et al. (2021) found a significant improvement in the centre of pressure (COP) in the RT group (p = 0.004). Additionally, regarding aerobic capacity, Cowley et al. (2011) observed a significant improvement in peak relative VO2max (p = 0.02) and respiratory exchange ratio (p = 0.01) in favour of the RT group.

Results regarding functional tasks were heterogeneous across the five studies assessing these measures (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011). While Shields et al. (2008) found no significant changes in any functional test, Diaz et al. (2021) found a significant improvement in the weighted pail carry test (p = 0.004), and Fornieles et al. (2014) observed significant gains in the weighted box stacking test (p = 0.0141). Shin et al. (2021) reported a significant improvement in the weight load of the EG compared to the CG (p = 0.029). Moreover, Shin et al. (2021) reported significant within‐group improvements in the EG for the stand‐up test (p = 0.021) and the incremental shuttle walking test (p = 0.005), although these did not reach statistical significance when compared to the CG. In addition, Cowley et al. (2011) reported significant reductions in the 10‐step ascend (p = 0.01) and descend (p = 0.04) tests.

There was controversy in the findings on body composition. Regarding weight, BMI, and waist circumference, Shin et al. (2021) found significant improvement (p = 0.013, p = 0.01 and p = 0.006, respectively). In contrast, Diaz et al. (2021) found no significant changes in these variables, although Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. (2013) supported the positive findings regarding waist circumference (p = 0.0416). There were discrepancies in tissue analysis: Shin et al. (2021) found a significant improvement in fat percentage (p = 0.047), but not in fat‐free mass. Conversely, Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. (2013) found a significant improvement (p = 0.011). In addition, Diaz et al. (2021) found significant increases in muscle mass (p = 0.018) and Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI) (p = 0030).

Since the studies evaluating biochemical markers and hormonal profile in adults correspond to the male cohort analysed above (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021), the detailed results are presented in Section 3.4. In summary, the results of these studies showed that RT significantly improves the inflammatory and oxidative profile in adults with DS. Furthermore, Diaz et al. (2021) added that the intervention did not induce significant alterations in the markers of muscle damage creatine kinase or lactate dehydrogenase, suggesting that RT is well tolerated by the tissues of this age range of the studied population.

Finally, only the study by Shields et al. (2008) reported that 4 out of 9 participants experienced mild muscle soreness as an adverse effect of RT in the first weeks of the study, which spontaneously recovered. No serious adverse effects related to RT interventions were reported in the other seven included studies (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011).

Meta‐Analysis

Five groups of meta‐analysis were conducted according to the outcome measure to which they belonged. These are: Muscle Strength, Anthropometric Measures, Physical Function, Cardiorespiratory Measures and Work Task Performance.

Muscle Strength

A meta‐analysis integrating the results of three sub‐meta‐analyses on muscle strength in people with DS was conducted.

In the first submeta‐analysis, general muscle strength was assessed using Handgrip Strength in two studies (Shin et al. 2021; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019). In Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. (2019), separate analyses were performed for females (F) and males (M). Considering the heterogeneity between the studies (I 2 = 64%), the results were statistically significant in favour of EG (p = 0.006). This indicates that RT was superior for improving Handgrip Strength compared to CG in people with DS (SMD = 2.24; 95% CI = (0.65; 3.84)).

In the second submeta‐analysis, the maximal muscle strength of the upper extremities was assessed using 1RM with the Bench Press in three studies (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010). There was heterogeneity between studies (I 2 = 59%), and the results were statistically significant (p < 0.001) in favour of EG compared to CG (SMD = 1.68; 95% CI = (0.94; 2.43)).

Regarding the third submeta‐analysis, the maximal muscle strength of the lower extremities was measured using 1RM with the Leg Press in three studies (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010). As in the previous two, heterogeneity between the studies was found (I 2 = 88%), and the results were statistically significant (p = 0.010) in favour of RT compared to CG in people with DS (SMD = 1.97; 95% CI = (0.45; 3.50)).

Overall, muscle strength was assessed in five clinical trials (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shin et al. 2021; Shields and Taylor 2010; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019). The studies were heterogeneous (I 2 = 72%), and the results of the meta‐analysis were statistically significant (p < 0.001) in favour of EG compared to CG in people with DS (SMD = 1.88; 95% CI = (1.26; 2.51)). All results are shown in Figure 3.

Meta‐analysis of muscle strength based on the results of handgrip strength, bench press and leg press comparing RT with CG. CI, confidence interval; P, statistical significance; SD, standard deviation.

Anthropometric Measures

A comparative meta‐analysis of anthropometric measures between RT and CG in people with DS was also conducted, compiling the results of three sub‐meta‐analyses. In the first sub‐meta‐analysis, anthropometric measures were assessed using the Weight in six studies (Diaz et al. 2021; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018). The included studies did not show heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%), but the results were inconclusive (p = 0.590) SMD = 0.08; 95% CI = (−0.21; 0.37). In the study by Dilek et al. (2018), results obtained at 6 months (6M‐DS) and 12 months (12M‐DS) were analysed separately, and in the study by (Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019) separate analyses were performed for females (F) and males (M).

The second sub‐meta‐analysis assessed BMI as an anthropometric measure in five studies (Diaz et al. 2021; Shin et al. 2021; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018). The included studies presented heterogeneity (I 2 = 79%), and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.720) (SMD = −0.13; 95% CI = (−0.85; 0.59)).

The third sub‐meta‐analysis assessed Waist Circumference from two studies (Diaz et al. 2021; Shin et al. 2021). The studies showed heterogeneity (I 2 = 67%), and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.190) (SMD = −0.68; 95% CI = (−1.69; 0.34)).

Overall, six clinical trials (Diaz et al. 2021; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018) were included in the meta‐analysis group and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.470) (Figure 4).

Meta‐analysis of anthropometric measures based on weight, BMI and waist circumference outcomes comparing RT with CG. BMI, body mass index; CI, confidence interval; P, statistical significance; SD, standard deviation.

Physical Function

The third group of meta‐analyses assessed the effects on physical function, comprising two sub‐meta‐analyses. In the first sub‐meta‐analysis, results were analysed using the Grocery Shelf Task for upper extremities from two clinical trials (Shields et al. 2008; Shields and Taylor 2010). There was homogeneity between the studies (I 2 = 0%), and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.270) (SMD = −0.34; 95% CI = (−0.95; 0.26)).

In the second sub‐meta‐analysis, results were analysed using the Timed Stairs Test for lower extremities from two clinical trials (Shields et al. 2008; Shields and Taylor 2010). Heterogeneity might not be discarded (I 2 = 54%), and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.080) (SMD = −0.85; 95% CI = (−1.80; 0.09)).

Even though the sub‐meta‐analyses were inconclusive, the overall meta‐analysis for physical function was statistically significant (p = 0.020) in favour of RT. This could be interpreted to mean that, overall, RT produces improvements in physical function. All results are shown in Figure 5.

Meta‐analysis of physical function based on the outcomes of the grocery shelf task for upper extremities and timed stairs test for lower extremities comparing RT with CG. CI, confidence interval; P, statistical significance; SD, standard deviation.

Cardiorespiratory Measures

Similarly, another meta‐analysis was performed compiling the results of three sub‐meta‐analyses on cardiorespiratory measures in people with DS.

The first sub‐meta‐analysis assessed cardiorespiratory measures using VO2max in two clinical trials (Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014), comparing RT with CG. The studies presented heterogeneity (I 2 = 91%), and the results were inconclusive (p = 0.440) (SMD = −0.76; 95% CI = (−2.69; 1.16)).

The second sub‐meta‐analysis analysed HRmax as a cardiorespiratory measure, comparing RT with CG in two clinical trials (Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014). This time, homogeneity could be assumed (I 2 = 19%, and p = 0.270), and the results were statistically significant in favour of RT (p < 0.001) (SMD = 1.85; 95% CI = (1.12–2.58)).

The third sub‐meta‐analysis was performed on two clinical trials (Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014) comparing RT with CG in terms of Minute Ventilation as a cardiorespiratory measure. The studies were heterogeneous (I 2 = 91%) and inconclusive (p = 0.880) (SMD = −0.14; 95% CI = (−1.98; 1.70)).

Overall, two clinical trials (Cowley et al. 2011; González‐Agüero et al. 2014) analysed cardiorespiratory measures comparing RT with CG, and no conclusive results were obtained (p = 0.590) (Figure 6).

Work Task Performance

A final meta‐analysis was performed to assess the effects on work task performance. Two clinical trials (Diaz et al. 2021; Shields et al. 2013) focused on the analysis of physical function using this measure, comparing RT with CG (Figure 7). A high heterogeneity was observed between the two studies (I 2 = 99%), and although a trend towards improvement of RT compared to CG was observed, the results were inconclusive to demonstrate this (p = 0.560).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis included 16 studies involving 576 participants and was aimed to analyse the effectiveness of RT in people with DS for improving strength and other outcomes related to physical and metabolic function. The importance of this study lies in the need to address the physical difficulties faced by people with DS, such as muscle weakness and lack of motor skills, through interventions such as RT. This analysis will shed light on the effectiveness of the intervention and will contribute to the overall understanding of how RT can improve the quality of life of people with DS.

Although we are not aware of published reviews that analyse the effects of RT in people with DS, we conduct the discussion based on similar works. In the systematic review and meta‐analysis by Sugimoto et al. (2016), the effect of neuromuscular training in children and young adults with DS was analysed, including studies that conducted an RT program, and showed an increase in general and maximal strength. However, no significant improvements were found in terms of physical function. In contrast, our meta‐analysis (Figure 5) showed improvements in physical function following RT. This discrepancy may be due to methodological differences between reviews. Sugimoto et al. (2016) included broader neuromuscular training rather than RT‐specific protocols, which may have diluted the specific effects of RT. Furthermore, while Sugimoto et al. (2016) focus on children and young adults, the studies included in our meta‐analysis cover a wider age range: from 7 to 50 years. Differences in age groups and the type of intervention may have contributed to this divergence in results, as it is possible that the larger number of specific RT trials included in our review provided greater statistical power to detect functional changes.

Additionally, Sugimoto et al. (2016) suggested that the lack of improvement in physical function could be attributed to executive function impairments characteristic of DS, which may limit the transfer of muscle strength gains to task performance. However, our findings indicate that muscle strength is associated with improved physical function, suggesting that increased muscle strength may contribute to better work task performance despite these difficulties in neurological development.

In other reviews, such as the one conducted by (Legerlotz 2018), the authors evaluated the effects of RT on the physical and mental health of children and adolescents with various disabilities, neurodegenerative pathologies, such as Charcot–Marie‐Tooth, hypermobility, or DS itself. Their findings supported the idea that RT offers multiple benefits and is practically free of adverse effects. However, these results should be interpreted in the context of the substantial clinical heterogeneity of the populations included, which limits the direct extrapolation of their findings to individuals with DS specifically. In this regard, our review provides more condition‐specific evidence, supporting that RT may improve muscle strength and physical function in individuals with DS in a safe way. Importantly, while our findings broadly support those of Legerlotz (2018), the magnitude and transferability of these benefits may depend on the characteristics of the training program, participant characteristics and the outcomes assessed. Our review corroborates and expands the potential clinical value of RT in DS to improve muscle strength, physical function and other measures. Despite this, the findings should be interpreted with caution until more standardised high‐quality trials become available.

In 2019, a systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted by (Ruiz‐González et al. 2019) on the different modalities of physiotherapy in DS showed once again that RT increases muscle strength in DS. Our findings are broadly consistent with those of Ruiz‐González et al. (2019). However, their conclusions were based on only three RT trials, limiting the robustness of their evidence. Our review prolongs these findings by incorporating a larger number of studies and performing quantitative synthesis. Nevertheless, despite the larger evidence base, heterogeneity in sample size, characteristics of participants, training protocols, and outcome measurement remains a challenge, suggesting that strength improvements may not be equally transferable across all clinical contexts.

To improve clarity, the discussion below first addresses the findings derived from the pooled meta‐analyses and then discusses additional narrative evidence from individual studies that were not included in the quantitative synthesis.

Meta‐Analysis Findings

Overall, the pooled meta‐analyses support the effectiveness of RT for improving muscle strength and physical function, whereas evidence for anthropometric, cardiorespiratory, and work task performance outcomes remains limited or inconclusive.

In relation to the different meta‐analyses carried out in this article, we found that the meta‐analysis conducted on muscle strength shows a significant improvement in strength after RT in people with DS. Physical function, and especially muscle strength, is related to increased self‐concept, achieving an improvement in motor competence, which allows for greater ease in walking or performing ADLs (Legerlotz 2018; Wilson and Clayton 2010; Andrade et al. 1991; Schranz et al. 2014; Velez et al. 2010).

Our specific meta‐analysis on anthropometric measures did not reveal significant differences between groups, neither in the subgroup meta‐analysis nor in any group considered individually. This indicates that RT, as a standalone intervention, may not be sufficient to achieve substantial changes in the body composition of people with DS. Even though some individual studies reported isolated benefits in terms of fat mass control (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013), weight loss (Shin et al. 2021; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019) or improving body image (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013; Shin et al. 2021; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019), these effects did not reach statistical significance when pooled. The lack of conclusive results in anthropometric measures could be due to the inherent limitations of these metrics: RT‐induced increases in lean mass may occur without substantial changes in body weight or BMI, limiting the sensitivity of these outcomes to detect clinically relevant body composition changes (Diaz et al. 2021; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013). Moreover, the heterogeneity among included trials in terms of training dosage and duration may have hindered the collection of measurable changes. Finally, the absence of dietary control introduces an important confounding factor, particularly when assessing obesity‐related outcomes. Therefore, the lack of significant pooled effects does not necessarily indicate that RT has no metabolic benefits, but currently available study designs and measures may be insufficient to determine whether RT is effective for weight or obesity management in this population.

The pooled meta‐analysis of physical function was statistically significant, even though the results of the individual sub‐meta‐analyses for the upper and lower extremities did not reach statistical significance on their own. This discrepancy may be because only two studies were included in the meta‐analysis and both had small sample sizes; therefore, by pooling the data using a random‐effects model, the effect of the individual studies became evident. Overall heterogeneity was low, likely due to the high similarity between the RT protocols of both studies. However, the sub‐meta‐analysis for the Timed Stairs Test (lower extremities) showed moderate heterogeneity, which may be caused by the age difference among participants, as the study by Shields et al. (Shields et al. 2008) included adults (20–49 years) and the study by (Shields and Taylor 2010) included adolescents (13–18 years). Given that the developmental stage of the participants is notably different, levels of muscular maturation also differ, influencing the results. Although the overall findings suggest a benefit, the combination of a small sample size and differences in developmental stage means that we must interpret the results with caution.

A meta‐analysis of work task performance was conducted incorporating the studies by (Diaz et al. 2021) and (Shields et al. 2013). The study by (Diaz et al. 2021) used the weighted pail‐carry test, whereas the study by (Shields et al. 2013) used the weighted box stacking test. The study by (Fornieles et al. 2014) could not be included due to lack of data. Although the results of the meta‐analysis were inconclusive, a trend towards improvement after RT was identified, which is broadly consistent with the positive findings obtained in the meta‐analysis on physical function. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the meta‐analysis was based on only two studies, which reduces statistical significance. Furthermore, the two studies used different outcome measures to assess work task performance, assessing different functional demands and contributing to clinical heterogeneity. It should also be considered that work task performance is a multidimensional outcome that depends not only on muscle strength, but also on other functional requirements such as motor coordination and executive function (Sugimoto et al. 2016). These factors may influence how increased strength translates into functional improvements in people with DS.

In the meta‐analysis of cardiorespiratory measures, where no conclusive findings were found in the subgroup analysis, it is worth highlighting that, when examining the meta‐analysis of HRmax individually, a clear improvement in this measure was observed. The inconsistent findings across studies on VO2max and minute ventilation measures may reflect the differences in RT program modality, age of participants (adolescents vs. adults) and training dosage. In addition, the small number of studies and the small sample size reduce confidence in these findings. As an interesting fact, it seems that RT programs focusing on lower extremity function may indirectly improve aerobic capacity without necessarily inducing classic cardiorespiratory adaptations comparable to aerobic training (Cowley et al. 2011). This finding coincides with the results of the study by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), where the RT group improved significantly in the cardiorespiratory fitness test, while the aerobic exercise group did not show significant differences.

An important consideration when interpreting meta‐analyses is the heterogeneity observed across different outcomes. This heterogeneity probably reflects differences in participant characteristics (age, sex distribution), intervention protocols (type of RT, material resources, training dosage), and methods of measuring outcomes. For example, RT interventions ranged from PRE programs using machines to plyometric and mixed training approaches, while participant ages ranged from childhood to adulthood (7–50 years). Consequently, the magnitude of the observed benefits should be interpreted with caution.

Narrative Findings

Beyond the quantitative synthesis, several studies provided ‘additional evidence’ regarding strength adaptations, balance, biochemical markers, motor fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness, and exercise capacity and safety.

Muscle Strength

Regarding the systematic review carried out, the narrative findings showed that, in general, the included studies that assessed muscle strength have shown a significant improvement in it in the medium term, with the exception of the study by (Shin et al. 2021), which did not show a significant improvement. This may be due to the training that was conducted in this study, as the exercises and materials used were different from the rest of the trials. The study by (Cowley et al. 2011) did not obtain a significant improvement in the isometric peak torque of knee flexors (general strength) but did obtain a significant improvement in the extensors and isokinetic knee flexors and extensors (power strength). Studies that assessed maximal muscle strength using 1RM (Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010) did not find significant improvements in upper extremities strength, but they found significant improvements in lower extremities strength, even though all three studies used similar RT protocols. The interventions consisted of comparable PRE programs performed twice weekly for 12 weeks and included exercises targeting both upper and lower extremities (e.g., seated chest press, seated row, leg press, knee extension and calf raise). Consequently, the discrepancy in maximal strength gains between the upper and lower extremities is unlikely to be fully explained by differences in training dosage or type of intervention. A possible explanation is that the lower extremities, due to their persistent mechanical stimulation through their continuous involvement in gait, postural control, and daily functional activities, may demonstrate greater responsiveness and neuromuscular adaptation to RT (Sousa et al. 2011). In addition, regional differences in muscle architecture may also contribute to these distinct adaptation patterns (Suchomel et al. 2018). However, these interpretations are theories that should be examined in future studies specifically designed to compare regional adaptations to RT in people with DS. Furthermore, in the study by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024), assessing lower extremities power strength using the long jump test, they found that the RT group improved significantly, while the aerobic exercise group worsened. This observation reinforces the principle of training specificity and suggests that intervention selection should be guided by the particular physical capacities intended to be improved.

Balance

The three studies that assessed balance in the medium term showed a significant improvement in balance (Shin et al. 2021; Eid et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2011). This may be because greater muscle strength leads to greater recruitment of muscle fibres and allows for greater self‐awareness, resulting in improved balance (Wilson and Clayton 2010; Andrade et al. 1991; Schranz et al. 2014; Velez et al. 2010).

Biochemical Markers

Among the studies that assessed biochemical measures, two looked at changes in muscle damage markers, and both studies showed that strength exercise does not produce muscle damage (Diaz et al. 2021), or even significantly reduces muscle damage markers (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2021). This finding is of great interest as it shows that, in people with muscle hypotonia, exercise is safe and even beneficial, if it is supervised and controlled by professionals. Several studies reported favourable biochemical adaptations following RT, including reductions in inflammatory cytokines (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013) and oxidative stress markers (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2021), as well as improvements in antioxidant capacity (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2014) and immune‐related biomarkers (Fornieles et al. 2014). All these findings indicate that RT becomes a good ally to prevent injuries, diseases, or pathological situations in this population.

Functional Performance

The study by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024) assessed motor fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness, and exercise capacity, finding that RT had a significant improvement in these measures, whereas the group that performed aerobic exercise only had a worsening of their cardiorespiratory fitness. These results suggest that muscle weakness may be a key limiting factor for physical performance in people with DS. Therefore, improvements in muscle strength could lead to broader benefits in motor and cardiorespiratory fitness by reducing the relative effort required to perform functional tasks (Cowley et al. 2010). However, the decline observed in the aerobic exercise group should be interpreted with caution, as these findings come from a single study with a limited sample size.

Exercise Safety

The adverse effects found in the trials in this review were not serious; they were generally exercise‐induced muscle discomfort that resolved spontaneously (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014; Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Shields and Taylor 2010; Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024; González‐Agüero et al. 2014).

Age‐ and Sex‐Specific Findings

Subgroup analyses suggest that RT may benefit different demographic subgroups. However, the available evidence remains insufficient to establish definitive age‐ or sex‐specific recommendations.

Following the specific sub‐analysis of male participants, our findings suggest that RT induces robust physiological adaptations in men with DS. Notably, the male‐only cohort showed consistent and significant improvements in inflammatory and oxidative stress markers (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021). This is of particular clinical relevance, as individuals with DS exhibit a prevalence of chronic oxidative stress and early aging (Bull 2020; Coelho‐Junior et al. 2019; Whooten et al. 2018). The observed increase in salivary testosterone and IgA (Fornieles et al. 2014) further supports the potential of RT to enhance the anabolic and immune status of male adults with DS, mechanisms that are crucial for muscle maintenance and overall health. Regarding physical fitness, while strength results were heterogeneous across the male‐only studies, the direct comparison by (Suarez‐Villadat et al. 2024) suggested that RT was more effective than aerobic exercise for improving strength in men. Interestingly, when comparing these results with the female data provided by the same study, adaptations appear to be very similar between sexes. However, some minor differences were observed. For instance, females in the RT group showed significant improvements in motor fitness compared to those in the aerobic group, a benefit not seen in males.

When analysing data from studies that only included adult populations (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021; Shields et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2021; Cowley et al. 2011), it can be observed that RT is a safe intervention that significantly improves physical fitness and biochemical markers. These findings suggest that adults with DS are capable of adhering to standardised RT protocols. Notably, this feasibility is accompanied by a high safety profile, evidenced by the absence of adverse effects in most studies and the lack of significant alterations in muscle damage markers (creatine kinase, lactate dehydrogenase). The main concern in adults with DS is premature aging and sarcopenia, unlike the paediatric population, where neural development is still ongoing (Coelho‐Junior et al. 2019). The findings from this sub‐analysis are encouraging regarding these mechanisms. RT induced a potent anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effect (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014, 2021), which is clinically critical given the chronic oxidative stress in DS. Regarding body composition, although the results on general adiposity were conflicting, Diaz et al. (2021) specifically assessed muscle quality (muscle mass and SMI) and reported positive adaptations (Diaz et al. 2021; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013), suggesting that RT counteracts the sarcopenic process. In terms of physical performance, while strength generally increased, the transfer to functional tasks was task‐dependent. Improvements were observed in tasks requiring force application or load carriage (e.g., weighted pail carry, stair climbing) (Diaz et al. 2021; Fornieles et al. 2014; Shin et al. 2021), whereas generalised mobility tasks (e.g., TGUG, walking speed) showed less consistent gains (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013; Shields et al. 2008). This indicates that increases in muscular capacity do not necessarily translate into generalised mobility improvements. Functional transfer may depend on multiple factors such as task specificity, coordination requirements, cognitive demands, and initial motor competence, all of which are relevant in DS.

Methodological Considerations and Clinical Implications

The methodological quality of the evidence was low‐moderate, possibly due to not being able to blind the participants or therapists, although the risk of bias decreases and the quality increases as some studies did blind the assessors (Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013; Shields et al. 2008, 2013; Eid et al. 2017; Shields and Taylor 2010). By not clearly reporting hidden allocation in some trials, the risk of bias in this review also increased (Fornieles et al. 2014; Rosety‐Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014; González‐Agüero et al. 2014; Ortiz‐Ortiz et al. 2019; Dilek et al. 2018). The lack of information provided by some studies on outcome measures means that the quality varies from very low to moderate, and the risk of bias increases.

From a clinical perspective, the observed improvements in muscle strength and physical function suggest that RT may represent a valuable component of physiotherapy programs for individuals with DS, improving many characteristics of this population, such as muscle hyperlaxity or muscle weakness. Given the central role of strength in mobility and ADL performance (Legerlotz 2018; Wilson and Clayton 2010; Kemp et al. 2010; Scholtes et al. 2010, 2012), incorporating RT may significantly impact independence and quality of life.

Limitations and Future Lines of Research

Among the limitations of this review, we can find that few studies had high methodological quality and low risk of bias. Furthermore, the small sample size and differences between the number of participants, heterogeneity in characteristics of participants, and intervention protocols (e.g., duration, exercises performed and training dosage) increase the risk of bias, limit the generalizability of the results, and make it difficult to draw conclusions about the outcomes. A significant limitation identified in the current literature is the lack of standardised reporting of RT dosage. As shown in Table 2, key training variables, such as the number of sets, repetitions, rest intervals, and session duration, were frequently omitted, limiting the transparency regarding exercise volume and density. This incomplete information about RT reduces the reproducibility of interventions and hinders the establishment of clear dose–response relationships for individuals with DS.

For a more accurate assessment of anthropometric measures in people with DS after RT, further studies are needed that include measures of fat mass and fat‐free mass, and even advanced body composition analyses, with a longer follow‐up period to clarify the role of RT in body composition.

Although the overall results suggest that RT could be beneficial for people with DS, the available evidence does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding whether the magnitude of these effects varies by age or sex. The developmental stage may influence the response to RT, as children and adolescents are still in the process of neuromuscular maturation, whereas adults may benefit from a reduction in age‐related muscle function decline. Similarly, although the available data suggest generally comparable responses between males and females, the lack of specific studies conducted exclusively on females prevents definitive conclusions from being reached. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as supporting the general use of RT in individuals with DS, recognising that optimal training prescriptions may vary across different demographic subgroups.

Nevertheless, considering these methodological limitations, the current literature emphasises the urgent need for future high‐quality RCTs with standardised and comprehensive reporting of training protocols. Future studies should protocolise the RT programs to minimise bias due to heterogeneity and allow for more robust conclusions. It will also be necessary to standardise assessment tools for quality of life, physical function, and work task performance to facilitate data pooling in future reviews.

Likewise, it would be desirable that future lines of research perform more trials comparing RT with aerobic exercise to observe both: participant preference and overall benefits in terms of fat loss and physical and mental function in people with DS. Finally, future research should prioritise sex‐ and age‐disaggregated results.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis summarised the existing scientific evidence on RT intervention for people with DS (see Appendix 1 in Supporting Information for a graphical abstract). In response to the initially stated objectives, our findings indicate that: The current evidence suggests that RT, compared to no intervention, may increase general and maximal muscle strength in individuals with DS, and may improve their physical function. Moreover, while the systematic review suggests a possible positive effect on balance and certain biochemical measures, such as a reduction in markers of muscle damage, there is insufficient evidence to confirm that RT improves anthropometric measures, cardiorespiratory measures, or work task performance.RT appears to be a generally safe and feasible intervention for people with DS, with no serious adverse effects reported; however, mild and transitory adverse effects, such as muscle soreness, may occur.

Nevertheless, given the substantial heterogeneity reported in the meta‐analyses regarding intervention protocols and participant characteristics, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, we encourage authors to conduct RCTs with unified RT protocols, where long‐term data are collected to observe whether this type of work produces sustained benefits over time. We also encourage authors, due to the heterogeneity of the available evidence, to compare it with aerobic exercise, to prioritise sex‐ and age‐disaggregated results, and to include the outcome measures mentioned in the discussion section so that they are comparable and quality conclusions can be drawn.

The findings of this manuscript could support the inclusion of tailored and supervised RT as part of physiotherapy programmes for individuals with DS in clinical practice.
