From Heritage to Innovation: A Review of the Safety and Production of Aminopeptide Seasonings
Authors: José María Martín‐Miguélez, José de la Rosa Morón, Nabila Rodríguez Valerón, Alberto Alía, Josué Delgado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAminopeptide seasonings, produced through proteolytic fermentation or enzymatic hydrolysis, represent a global family of ingredients with a high concentration of umami‐active free amino acids and umami‐ or kokumi‐active peptides derived from protein‐rich substrates. The present review introduces the complexity of aminopeptide seasonings with the intention of preserving gastronomic heritage and classifying the variety of aminopeptide seasonings. This review examines the biochemical mechanisms, microbial processes, sensory attributes, and safety considerations underlying their development. Proteolysis, driven by endogenous and microbial proteases, generates free amino acids and short‐chain peptides responsible for taste and aromatic depth. Historical and cross‐cultural analyses reveal the widespread evolution of these seasonings, from Mediterranean fish sauces and African legume ferments to Asian soy pastes and Northern autolytic preparations, each reflecting distinct environmental and technological contexts. Modern adaptations integrate controlled fermentation, isolated enzymes, and multi‐hurdle food safety strategies—including temperature, pH, water activity, vacuum processing, and protective cultures—to ensure microbial safety while preserving traditional flavor complexity. Moreover, aminopeptide seasonings can modulate the sensory properties of plant‐based food matrices, facilitating sodium and fat reduction while supporting the development of more acceptable plant‐based products and, consequently, sustainable dietary shifts. This work proposes a comprehensive classification of traditional aminopeptide seasonings and outlines contemporary biotechnological and regulatory frameworks for their safe production within the European Union. By bridging heritage and innovation, aminopeptide seasonings exemplify the intersection of gastronomy, microbiology, and food safety science.

Introduction

Aminopeptide seasonings are developed from the fermentation or enzymatic hydrolysis of protein‐rich substrates and are characterized by containing a high concentration of umami‐active free amino acids and umami‐ or kokumi‐active peptides. They have historically served as essential culinary elements across Mediterranean, African, and Asian cultures, providing complex and layered flavor profiles rooted in a combination of free amino acids, small peptides, and nucleotides like glutamic and aspartic acids, as well as synergists such as 5’‐inosinate and 5’‐guanylate. Early works focused on products like Roman garum, Japanese miso, and African dawadawa, noting their profound umami impact and the dual role of endogenous and microbial proteases in determining taste and aroma characteristics (Curtis 1991; Kuroda 2024; Mouritsen et al. 2017; Ninomiya 2015; Xie et al. 2024). Subsequent research has deepened the understanding of how fermentation variables—such as starter culture selection, raw material composition, fermentation length, and biotechnological innovations—shape the generation of key sensory compounds (Kuroda et al. 2012; Hakimi et al. 2022; Kan et al. 2025). Contemporary developments combine traditional practices with process controls, including temperature and pH management, water activity (a w) regulation, and the use of protective cultures and isolated enzymes to boost microbial safety and reproducibility (Samson et al. 2025).

These seasonings now stand at the intersection of tradition, health, and technological innovation, supported by evidence that they foster natural flavor enhancement in plant‐based and reduced‐sodium foods while aligning with clean‐label and minimal‐processing trends (Abbaspour 2024; Sharma et al. 2020; Mouritsen 2024). In modern culinary and food industry contexts, aminopeptide seasonings not only reinforce cultural identity and culinary creativity but also act as vectors for nutritional improvement and sustainable dietary shifts, leveraging the depth of taste perceptions for greater acceptance of alternative proteins and dietary diversification. As the production of aminopeptide seasonings has evolved from predominantly artisanal practices to industrial manufacturing and global commercialization, the challenges associated with ensuring consistent quality and safety have become increasingly important. Larger production volumes, extended distribution chains, and the need for batch‐to‐batch standardization can amplify risks that were historically managed at smaller production scales, highlighting the importance of robust manufacturing practices and regulatory oversight.

The growing application and global commercialization of these products require vigilant attention to safety hazards, including the presence of pathogenic bacteria, biogenic amines (BAs), mycotoxins, and products of lipid and protein oxidation, which are addressed through regulatory controls and scientific guidance released by authorities such as the European Union and FDA (Capozzi et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2022; Skowron et al. 2022). Preservation of gastronomic heritage while adapting to current biotechnological capacities relies on developing robust classification systems, thorough documentation of regional product types, and risk management frameworks that ensure both consumer protection and sensory authenticity (Curtis 1991; European Parliament and Council 2002, 2008, 2015).

Consequently, this review aims to systematically classify both heritage and innovative aminopeptide seasonings, compare their underlying biochemical and microbial processes, and critically synthesize recent advances in their production, sensory attributes, safety, and applications in food systems. Particular emphasis is placed on the regulatory framework of the European Union, while relevant international regulations are discussed where appropriate to provide broader context. By integrating traditional knowledge with current scientific and technological developments, this review provides a comprehensive foundation for the safe and innovative use of aminopeptide seasonings, supporting the development of healthy and sustainable foods while preserving the cultural heritage and unique biochemical transformations that underpin their characteristic flavor profiles.

Importance of Flavor in Aminopeptide Seasoning

The sensory attributes of food shape consumer acceptance and cultural relevance, with flavor playing the most critical role. Aminopeptide seasonings, crafted through the enzymatic and microbial breakdown of proteinaceous substrates, intrinsically embody complex flavor profiles that have been honed over centuries of culinary tradition. These seasonings deliver nuanced balances of taste and aroma derived from diverse biochemical transformations. Understanding the molecular and microbial underpinnings that generate these sensory qualities is essential for innovation in food science and the development of healthier, sustainable, and culturally resonant food products.

Flavor is the key element that defines the value and relevance of aminopeptide seasonings in both traditional and modern food systems (Curtis 1991). These products—developed through fermentation or enzymatic hydrolysis of protein‐rich substrates—contain high concentrations of free amino acids and small peptides responsible for umami and koku sensation, as well as different volatile compounds (Kuroda 2024; Mouritsen et al. 2017).

While historically developed to preserve food, modify raw materials, and improve digestibility, the use of these seasonings today is primarily motivated by their sensory appeal (Mannaa et al. 2021). Their flavor richness supports cultural expression and culinary identity, while also offering functional benefits such as increased protein availability and natural preservation (Katz, 2012; Samson et al. 2025). The recent resurgence of interest in fermentation reflects a broader shift toward health, sustainability, and flavor‐driven innovation. Aminopeptide seasonings exemplify how ancient preservation methods have evolved into modern flavor technologies—tools that connect nutrition, tradition, and palatability (C. Li et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2018; Mouritsen 2024; Xie et al. 2024). As such, they are increasingly valued not only for their heritage but for their ability to meet contemporary culinary and nutritional demands through flavor. To understand the unique sensory impact of these seasonings, it is essential to examine the biochemical basis of their flavor formation, which results from the controlled breakdown of proteins and the subsequent generation of a wide range of taste‐ and aroma‐active compounds.

Flavor in aminopeptide seasonings develops through a sequence of enzymatic reactions and chemical conversions that occur during protein hydrolysis and prolonged fermentation. Proteolysis—catalyzed by a range of endo‐ and exo‐proteases—breaks down proteins into short‐chain peptides and free amino acids. Glutamic acid (Glu) and aspartic acid (Asp) are primary contributors to umami taste, while glutamine (Gln) and asparagine (Asn) may serve as precursors to these umami‐active compounds during deamidation (C. Li et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2024). Glycine, alanine, and serine contribute to sweetness, whereas hydrophobic amino acids such as leucine and valine are associated with bitterness (Hakimi et al. 2022). This bitterness can be modulated during fermentation: through the action of γ‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), via bitter amino acids are converted into γ‐glutamyl peptides—such as tri‐peptides γ‐Glu‐Val‐Gly, γ‐Glu‐Cys‐Gly, or di‐peptides γ‐Glu‐Val, γ‐Glu‐Glu, γ‐Glu‐Leu, and γ‐Glu‐Phe, among others—which reduce perceived bitterness and enhance koku sensation (Kuroda et al. 2012; Rodríguez Valerón et al. 2023). Together with the inherent savoriness of umami, these compounds contribute to complex, layered flavor profiles with depth, aftertaste, and a mouthfeel quality that can transform a dish with minimal quantities. This intensity allows for precise culinary application, enhancing overall taste while reducing the need for added salt, fat, or artificial flavorings (Ninomiya 2015; Xie et al. 2024; Kuroda 2024).

Beyond γ‐glutamyl peptides, fermentation generates a structurally diverse repertoire of kokumi‐active compounds, predominantly through CaSR activation (Kitajima et al. 2026). These include non‐γ‐glutamyl α‐dipeptides such as Leu‐Lys, Leu‐Gln, and Ala‐Leu (Kitajima et al. 2026; J. Liu et al. 2015); pyroglutamyl and β‐aspartyl peptides formed during proteolysis (Q. Li et al. 2022); N‐acetylated amino acids (N‐acetyl‐Val, N‐acetyl‐Leu, N‐acetyl‐Met, among others); and N‐succinyl derivatives such as N‐succinyl‐L‐Phe and N‐succinyl‐L‐Tyr (Kitajima et al. 2026; J. Lin et al. 2023). In addition, free amino acids such as phenylalanine, histidine, methionine, and tyrosine are capable of CaSR activation (Kitajima et al. 2026), while ornithine enhances kokumi through the distinct GPRC6A receptor (Yamamoto et al. 2025). In addition, N‐lactoyl derivatives such as N‐lactoyl‐L‐Trp and N‐lactoyl‐L‐Met, produced during fermentation, further contribute to bitterness reduction through dual interaction with CaSR and bitter taste receptors, simultaneously enhancing kokumi perception (J. Wu et al. 2023).

Fermented products such as miso and soybean pastes contain various organic acids, including lactic, acetic, oxalic, succinic, malic, and citric acids, produced by microorganisms during fermentation. Together, these compounds lower the pH, enhance preservative stability, and modulate flavor balance (Coblentz et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2023). Alongside non‐volatile taste compounds, volatile organic compounds (VOCs)—including alcohols, aldehydes, short‐chain acids and esters, heterocyclic compounds, and phenolic compounds—play a crucial role in defining the aroma and flavor complexity of aminopeptide seasonings (Table 1). Umami is the predominant taste, perceived as savory and long‐lasting. It is largely driven by free Glu and Asp, along with synergistic nucleotides such as 5′‐inosinate (IMP) and 5′‐guanylate (GMP), which enhance umami perception even at low concentrations (Gabriel et al. 2024). Gln and Asn may also contribute indirectly through deamidation pathways (Gabriel et al. 2024).

These sensory properties not only define the identity of aminopeptide seasonings but also explain their growing relevance in contemporary food practices and product development. Aminopeptide seasonings are increasingly valued for delivering natural flavor without additives (Siddiqui et al. 2023). In modern and plant‐based cooking, they offer depth sensations that fill sensory gaps (Mouritsen 2024; Mouritsen and Styrbæk 2020). As fermentation gains renewed cultural and culinary relevance, these seasonings serve as accessible, functional tools for flavor innovation across diverse food applications.

The processes offer a targeted approach to improving the nutritional profile of foods without compromising flavor (Sharma et al. 2020). The production of umami and kokumi compounds enhances flavor perception in reduced‐sodium, low‐fat formulations and mitigates bitterness or off‐flavors (Rodríguez Valerón et al. 2023). This opens opportunities for clean‐label product development without relying on synthetic flavor enhancers. In this context, aminopeptide seasonings can contribute to healthier reformulations while maintaining sensory appeal (Siddiqui et al. 2023).

Furthermore, aminopeptide seasonings may play a strategic role in encouraging greater vegetable consumption by addressing common sensory limitations in plant‐based dishes (Abbaspour 2024). By enhancing flavor, these seasonings improve the appeal of vegetables and legumes, making them more enjoyable and satisfying. In this context, flavor acts as a lever for both culinary creativity and public health, supporting broader efforts to promote nutrient‐dense, plant‐forward diets in line with sustainability goals (Abbaspour 2024; Sharma et al. 2020).

Classification by Type: Aminopeptide Seasonings

Proteolytic seasonings exhibit remarkable diversity in raw materials, processing microbiology, enzymology, physical form, and cultural innovation. To make sense of this variation, we have devised a four‐axis typology as can be observed in Figure 1: by raw material (animal, plant, and mixed), fermentation strategy (spontaneous and inoculated), proteolytic mechanism (endogenous, microbial, and isolated enzymes), and final format (liquid, paste, powder, and blocks). Although no single prior work has encompassed all these dimensions, similar multi‐factor approaches have been applied to global fermented foods (Steinkraus 1996) and to culinary innovation frameworks (Samson et al. 2025). Our classification integrates biochemical drivers (e.g., enzyme source), technological choices (e.g., starter vs. wild fermentation), and product attributes (e.g., consistency, novelty) into a coherent scheme that facilitates direct comparison across geographic regions.

By Raw Material

The choice of protein source fundamentally shapes both the biochemical pathways of proteolysis and the sensory outcome. Animal‐based proteins contain endogenous proteases in fish, shellfish, meat, blood, and viscera that initiate hydrolysis immediately upon salting or cooking. Examples include Roman garum, Korean jeotgal, and Inuit kiviak, all of which release high levels of glutamate and nucleotides (Yamaguchi and Ninomiya 2000). Plant‐based proteins, such as legumes and cereals, require microbial or added enzymes to achieve proteolysis. Classic cases are Japanese miso, West African dawadawa, and Thai thua nao, in which Aspergillus, Bacillus, or lactic acid bacteria (LAB) generate umami precursors. Also, the combination of animal‐based and plant‐based proteins can develop hybrid aminopeptide sauces, where mixed substrates such as fish‐soy miso, insect–legume garum, or blood–soy pastes combine complementary amino acid and nucleotide profiles to amplify taste intensity through synergistic interactions (Samson et al. 2025).

By Fermentation Type

Control over microbial succession determines consistency, safety, and flavor complexity. Indigenous microbial population drives spontaneous proteolysis without deliberate inoculation. Examples include Amazonian fish sauces and African locust‐bean ferments, which reflect local ecological niches but can yield batch‐to‐batch variability. The inoculation of selected starters (e.g., Aspergillus oryzae) ensures homogeneous, controlled products, reproducible enzyme activity, and predictable flavor development (Redzepi and Zilber 2018).

By Proteolytic Mechanism

Different enzyme sources release umami‐active compounds at distinct rates and profiles. Native tissue enzymes act on collagen, muscle, or gonadal proteins. This governs autolytic ferments like bottarga and stinkhead, where no defined starter cultures or exogenous enzymes are added. However, microorganisms such as molds (Aspergillus spp., Rhizopus spp., …), bacteria (Bacillus spp., LAB, …), or yeasts can also produce proteases. These enzymes dominate in solid‐state pastes (e.g., miso, murri) and in brine fermentations (e.g., garum, nam pla). Nowadays, commercial proteases (e.g., alcalase, papain) enable a fast transformation of by‐products into flavor concentrates. Such processes underpin modern vegan garums and industrial extracts.

Final State

The physical presentation of each seasoning reflects both cultural convention and processing technique. Filtered or decanted liquid sauces (e.g., garum, nuoc mam, and vegan analogs) are prized for ease of dosing and rapid flavor pickup in cooking. Pastes are high‐viscosity preparations (e.g., miso, gochujang, and dawadawa) that retain solids and oils, imparting texture as well as taste. Dehydrated powdered ferments (e.g., thua nao cakes and miso powders) offer extended shelf life and precise portioning for industrial applications. Dried, cured protein blocks (e.g., bottarga and katsuobushi) are produced via salting, smoking, and microbial or enzymatic aging, then thinly shaved or grated to release concentrated umami compounds (Yamaguchi and Ninomiya 2000).

The preceding functional classification provides a systematic lens through which to compare these products across cultures. Building on this “type classification,” the categories are mapped onto the world's major regional traditions, revealing the remarkable diversity of ingredients and techniques, as well as their proteolytic mechanisms and fermentation characteristics (Table 2). Given the vast scope of proteolytic fermentations and the uneven documentation of many indigenous practices, it is inevitable that some examples may fall outside the purview of this review; nevertheless, this geographic taxonomy aims to capture a representative picture of global tradition and innovation in aminopeptide seasonings (Figure 2).

Europe and the Classical Mediterranean

The classical Mediterranean basin offers some of the earliest documented examples of proteolyzed seasonings derived from proteolytic transformation. These products, born of both necessity and ingenuity, played crucial roles in food preservation, flavor enhancement, and nutritional enrichment in Greco‐Roman, Byzantine, and Islamic societies (Curtis 1991; Wilkins and Hill 2006).

Garum

Garum was the main and most appreciated seasoning of ancient Rome, produced on an industrial scale along the Mediterranean coast. Fish, most often anchovies or mackerel, were layered with up to 25% salt by weight in large vats and left under the sun for several months. The combination of endogenous fish proteases and halophilic microbial communities caused robust proteolysis, liberating glutamate and peptides. Its distinctive savory aroma and syrupy consistency made garum both a cooking sauce and tabletop seasoning, traded across the empire for its ability to impart umami to otherwise bland staples (Expósito 2004; Mediterranean et al. 2004).

Colatura Di Alici

A modern heir to garum, Colatura di Alici is still being produced on the Amalfi Coast using fresh anchovies packed with coarse salt in chestnut barrels. After a controlled autolysis period (minimum 6 months), the exuded liquid is pressed, decanted, and often aged further in glass carboys. This method yields a remarkably clean, translucent fish sauce whose moderate saltiness and pronounced umami come from both free glutamate and short‐chain peptides. Chefs prize it for its finesse and minimal fishy off‐notes compared with more rustic fish sauces (Corona et al. 2023; Russo et al. 2020).

Bottarga

Bottarga is the salted, air‐dried roe sac of grey mullet (Mugil cephalus) or tuna, traditionally produced in Sardinia, Sicily, and parts of North Africa. After lightly salting, the sacs are pressed and sun‐dried for weeks, during which endogenous proteases continue to hydrolyze the high‐protein roe into free amino acids and small peptides. The process concentrates umami compounds alongside distinctive marine notes and a firm, sliceable texture. In contemporary cuisine, finely grated bottarga adds depth to pasta, salads, and antipasti (Rosa et al. 2020).

Western and Central Africa

Sub‐Saharan Africa has developed an extensive repertoire of proteolytic seasonings, leveraging both legume fermentations and fish autolysis under tropical conditions. These products serve as flavor enhancers, nutritional supplements, and preservation systems in local cuisines (Obafemi et al. 2022; Odunfa 1988; Olasupo et al. 2019).

Dawadawa

Dawadawa is produced by fermenting African locust bean seeds (Parkia biglobosa) following dehulling and boiling. Packs of boiled cotyledons are heaped and maintained at high temperature for a few days, during which Bacillus subtilis predominates, secreting proteases that hydrolyze storage proteins into glutamate, short peptides, and ammonia (Azokpota et al. 2006; Owusu‐Kwarteng et al. 2022). The resulting paste or dried balls exhibit an intense aroma and are used to season soups and stews across Nigeria, Ghana, and Benin.

Momone, Furundé, Lanhouin

In coastal and riverine areas of Benin, Ghana, and Cameroon, small freshwater or marine fish (e.g., anchovies, tilapia) are gutted, salted (5–10% w/w), and sun‐dried for 1–2 days, then buried in pits or sealed in clay jars for 5–14 days. Endogenous proteases and halophilic bacteria drive partial autolysis, liberating glutamate, amino acids, and volatile fatty acids. Texture ranges from thick paste to liquid sauce; local names include momone in Ghana, furundé in Burkina Faso, and lanhouin in Benin (Ndudi et al. 2024; Odunfa and Oyeyiola 1985).

Eastern and Southeastern Asia

Eastern and Southeastern Asia represent some of the most diverse and technically sophisticated systems of proteolytic seasonings in the world. Within this region, China occupies a particularly important position due to its long‐standing traditions of soybean‐ and fish‐based aminopeptide seasonings, including jiangyou, yulu, douchi, sufu, and broad‐bean pastes such as doubanjiang. These products employ mold‐inoculated solid‐state fermentations, halophilic bacterial autolysis, and mixed‐culture starters to convert proteins into free amino acids, peptides, and nucleotides—key drivers of umami taste (Tamang et al. 2016; F. Wang et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2016).

Jiangyou/Chinese Soy Sauce

Jiangyou, or Chinese soy sauce, represents one of the most historically and technologically important soybean‐based aminopeptide seasonings in East Asia. Traditional Chinese soy sauce is generally produced through a two‐stage fermentation process involving a solid‐state koji‐like phase, often using cooked soybeans and wheat inoculated or colonized by filamentous fungi such as A. oryzae or related species, followed by a long brine fermentation in which the mash matures under high‐salt conditions. During this process, fungal proteases and peptidases hydrolyze soybean proteins into free amino acids and short peptides, while amylolytic activity releases fermentable sugars from cereal starches. The subsequent brine fermentation is shaped by halotolerant LAB and yeasts, including Tetragenococcus halophilus and Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, which contribute organic acids, alcohols, esters, aldehydes, phenols, and Maillard‐derived aroma compounds. As a result, Chinese soy sauce combines intense salinity, umami, sweetness, acidity, and roasted aromatic notes, making it a central liquid aminopeptide seasoning in Chinese cuisine and a technological counterpart to other East Asian soy sauces such as Japanese shoyu and Korean ganjang (Devanthi and Gkatzionis 2019; Diez‐Simon et al. 2020; Y. Feng et al. 2014; L. Zhang et al. 2024).

Yulu/Chinese Fish Sauce

Yulu is a traditional Chinese fish sauce obtained by fermenting fish with salt for prolonged periods, particularly in coastal regions where small marine fish and fish‐processing by‐products have historically been preserved and transformed into liquid seasonings. As in other Southeast and East Asian fish sauces, yulu fermentation relies primarily on endogenous fish proteases from muscle and viscera, together with salt‐tolerant and halophilic microorganisms capable of surviving in high‐salt environments. These enzymatic and microbial activities solubilize fish proteins into peptides, free amino acids, and nitrogenous taste compounds, generating a clear to amber liquid rich in umami and marine aromas. Recent studies have also explored controlled or low‐salt yulu fermentation using protease‐producing starter cultures such as B. subtilis to accelerate protein hydrolysis, improve flavor development, and reduce spoilage risks. Within the geographical classification of aminopeptide seasonings, yulu should therefore be considered a representative Chinese animal‐based, liquid, proteolytic seasoning, closely related to but culturally distinct from Vietnamese nuoc mam, Thai nam pla, Korean jeotgal‐derived liquids, and Mediterranean garum (Du et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2007; C. Li et al. 2023; Y. Wang et al. 2018, Y. Wang et al. 2020).

Shoyu or Tamari (Soy Sauce)

Shoyu is brewed by inoculating a cooked soy and roasted wheat mash with koji (A. oryzae), then aging the moromi (a mixture of rice or soybeans, koji, salt, and water) in 15%–20% brine for 6–12 months. Koji proteases and amylases liberate glutamate, peptides, and sugars, while halotolerant LAB and yeasts (e.g., Tetragenococcus spp., Z. rouxii) build aroma. Tamari follows the same technique but is soy‐dominant with little or no wheat (historically the runoff from miso), yielding a darker, fuller‐bodied seasoning with higher amino‐nitrogen and fewer sweet/ester top notes than shoyu. Both are inoculated (koji), then liquid‐state brine fermentations; the key distinctions are substrate and resulting flavor balance (Steinkraus 1996; Tamang et al. 2016; Yamaguchi and Ninomiya 2000).

Katsuobushi

Katsuobushi is made from skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis) that is filleted, simmered, repeatedly smoked, sun‐dried, and then microbially matured via surface colonization by xerophilic filamentous fungi, such as Aspergillus glaucus. After the smoking phase, the blocks are inoculated with A. glaucus—sometimes with Aspergillus pseudoglaucus—whose extracellular proteases hydrolyze muscle proteins into glutamate‐ and peptide‐rich fractions. During several alternating cycles of mold growth and sun‐drying, up to 80% of the water is removed, inosinate becomes concentrated, and the characteristic floral–woody aroma develops (Yamaguchi and Ninomiya 2000). In the late stages of drying, limited colonization by aerobic spore‐forming bacteria, mainly Bacillus spp., contributes additional proteolysis and a mild increase in surface alkalinity, enhancing taste and shelf stability (Albala 2019; An et al. 2023; Wada et al. 1992; Yu et al. 2023). The hardening of katsuobushi results primarily from extensive dehydration, smoke‐induced cross‐linking, and tyrosine crystallization rather than mineral deposition. The finished blocks, now dense and wood‐like, are shaved into translucent flakes that dissolve easily in dashi, rice, and vegetable dishes.

Doenjang

Doenjang is Korea's principal soybean paste, produced from meju bricks—cooked soybeans that are pounded, shaped, and left to dry/ferment spontaneously for approximately 30–60 days under a mixed environmental microbiota dominated by Bacillus spp., together with filamentous molds such as Aspergillus (and often Rhizopus/Mucor)—after which the bricks are soaked in high‐salt brine (≈14%–20%) for 30–90 days. During brining and subsequent maturation, Bacillus and Aspergillus proteases continue to hydrolyze soy proteins into free amino acids and short peptides, while halotolerant LAB and yeasts generate organic acids and aroma volatiles that drive preservation and flavor development. The solids are then mashed and typically aged further to reach the characteristic coarse, savory paste (W. Y. Jung et al. 2016).

Jeotgal

Jeotgal encompasses a variety of salted seafood fermentations (shrimp, anchovies, oysters, pollock guts) in Korea. Raw seafood is combined with 15%–25% salt and aged for 1–6 months, during which endogenous proteases and halophilic bacteria (e.g., T. halophilus, Halanaerobium spp.) drive proteolysis. The result is a pungent paste or liquid seasoning used as a seasoning or kimchi starter, rich in free amino acids and volatile compounds (M. Y. Jung et al. 2018; Lim et al. 2024; Song et al. 2018).

Nam Pla or Nuoc Mam

Nam pla (Thailand) and nuoc mam (Vietnam) are clear fish sauces produced by layering small fish (anchovies) with 15%–30% salt in wooden or ceramic vats for 6–18 months. Endogenous enzymes and halophilic microbes catalyze proteolysis, releasing glutamate and peptides into the liquid phase. These sauces typically are potent natural taste enhancers (Khair et al. 2020).

Prahok

Prahok is a Cambodian fermented fish paste made from small freshwater fish, crushed with salt (10–15%), and sun‐fermented in jars for up to one year. The process involves endogenous proteases, LAB, and occasional fungal growth, producing a pungent, flavorful paste central to Khmer cuisine (Tamang et al. 2016).

Thua Nao

Thua Nao is a Northern Thai fermented soybean product, prepared by soaking soybeans, steaming them, and then fermenting them under banana leaves or in bamboo baskets for 2–3 days at 30°C–40°C. B. subtilis dominates, producing proteases that hydrolyze soy proteins into flavorful peptides (Tamang et al. 2016).

Traditional Oyster Sauce

Traditional oyster sauce was a late‐19th‐century Cantonese seasoning prepared by thermal extraction and concentration of fresh oysters. Shucked oysters are gently simmered in water; as moisture evaporates, endogenous proteases (active early in heating) and heat‐assisted autolysis release free amino acids and 5′‐nucleotides (notably inosinate), yielding a glossy, flavorful reduction. Some modern artisanal makers introduce a brief, low‐salt LAB step before bottling to deepen aroma, whereas commercial products often blend oyster extract with salt/sugar/starch and sometimes MSG/caramel to standardize color and flavor (Fu et al. 2024; Hajeb and Jinap 2015; Nguyen and Wang 2012; Yamaguchi and Ninomiya 2000).

Western Asia and the Islamicate World

In West Asia and the broader Islamic world, proteolytic seasonings evolved in contexts where marine proteins were scarce, leading to innovative grain‐ and legume‐based ferments that replicate the taste impact of fish sauces. These products demonstrate convergent enzymatic strategies and reflect sophisticated medieval culinary science (Bertelsen 2009; Ibn Sayyār al‐Warrāq et al. 2007).

Murri

West Asia fostered two foundational cereal‐based aminopeptide seasonings—murri nīkīh and murri bi‑samāka—whose technological roots trace to 9th‑century Abbasid Iraq. Murri nīkīh employs roasted barley or wheat dough fermented under Aspergillus‐rich conditions, while murri bi‑samāka co‑ferments this grain mash with salted fish brine, coupling cereal glutamate with marine inosinate (Ibn Sayyār al‐Warrāq et al. 2007). Between the 10th and 13th centuries, both variants diffused westward via Islamic trade and scholarship into Al‑Andalus and Sicily, where they appear in Hispano‑Arabic and Latin cookbooks, yet retained their West‑Asian nomenclature and core process (Bertelsen 2009). For this reason, Murri is classified under Western Asia rather than Europe in this review.

Chickpea‐Based Paste

Chickpea paste—precursors to modern hummus—was traditionally from the Islamicate World, elaborated with soaked and sprouted chickpeas. Under humid storage, fermentation by LAB can occur, producing mild proteolysis that releases glutamate and small peptides, yielding an umami‐rich spread (Bertelsen 2009).

Arctic, Russia, and the Caucasus

In cold‐climate regions—from the Inuit communities of Greenland to the mountain villages of the Caucasus—fermentation and enzymatic aging have been critical for preserving protein‐rich foods. These practices leverage psychrophilic and anaerobic strategies to generate umami compounds under low‐temperature conditions (Campbell et al. 2022; Stimmelmayr and Sheffield 2022; Tamang et al. 2016).

Kiviak

Kiviak is a traditional Greenlandic preparation in which dozens of whole auk birds (including bones, feathers, and viscera) are tightly packed into a seal carcass, sealed, and buried beneath stones or permafrost for 3–6 months. The stable, cold, anaerobic environment facilitates endogenous proteolysis and anaerobic fermentation, producing a soft, pungent meat rich in free amino acids, particularly glutamate, and volatile fatty acids (Campbell et al. 2022; Dissanayake et al. 2024; Stimmelmayr and Sheffield 2022).

Stinkhead

Stinkhead is an Alaskan Inuit and Yup'ik specialty made by fermenting salmon heads (and sometimes guts) in sealed containers or buried pits for several weeks in cool conditions. Endogenous salmon proteases and halotolerant microbes (e.g., Psychrobacter spp.) drive proteolysis. The resulting paste is intensely aromatic and is used sparingly as a flavoring agent for carbohydrates or stews (Krotova et al. 2024; Myachikova and Shamtsyan 2022).

Rybnaya Pasta

Rybnaya pasta is produced in rural Russian regions along the Volga and Siberian rivers. Minced freshwater fish (roach, perch) are salted (∼10%), packed in barrels, and fermented at 5°C–15°C for 1–3 weeks. Indigenous psychrotolerant bacteria (e.g., Psychrobacter, Clostridium spp.) secrete proteases that yield a savory paste with high glutamate content and mild lactic notes (Tamang et al. 2016).

Oceania and Polynesia

Oceania and Polynesia encompass a range of island cultures that historically relied on marine protein fermentations for preservation and flavor intensification. Their techniques—often marginally documented—demonstrate convergent proteolytic strategies adapted to tropical and subtropical environments (Barrau 1961; Steinkraus 1996).

Traditional Fish Pastes

Ethnographic accounts from Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, and Papua New Guinea describe pastes made by burying small reef fish or shellfish in pits lined with banana leaves or coconut husks. After 3–7 days, endogenous fish enzymes and ambient bacteria produce a salty, sour, umami‐rich paste used sparingly to season root crops and stews (Pollock 1992). These preparations are locally called lairo, or mashed reef fish.

Latin America

Latin America's proteolytic seasonings span ancestral Amazonian fish sauces that are traditionally meat‐based. These products reflect localized microbial ecologies and cultural imperatives for preservation and flavor (Fogaça et al. 2023; Tamang et al. 2016).

Amazonian Fermented Fish Sauces

In the Amazon basin, riverine communities ferment freshwater fish (e.g., tambaqui, pacu) by layering gutted fish with 5–10% salt in clay vessels and leaving them under the sun for 3–7 days. Indigenous LAB (Lactobacillus spp., Pediococcus spp.) and Clostridium spp. contribute proteases that hydrolyze fish proteins into glutamate and peptides. The resulting Amazonian fermented fish preparations exhibit a balance of umami, acidity, and subtle bitterness (Fogaça et al. 2023; Shivanne Gowda et al. 2016).

Hazards of Traditional Aminopeptide Seasonings

The development of traditional aminopeptide seasonings through spontaneous fermentation, endogenous proteolysis, and mixed‐culture systems has historically delivered profound complexity and cultural value. However, these time‐honored processes, when conducted under uncontrolled or suboptimal conditions, can introduce biological, chemical, and physical hazards that compromise both consumer safety and product quality. Addressing these hazards becomes essential as contemporary production scales up from artisanal batches to industrial volumes, and as regulatory frameworks increasingly demand quantifiable risk mitigation.

Biological Hazards

Traditional aminopeptide seasonings rely on spontaneous fermentation, which poses a hazard because the indigenous microbiota can include opportunistic pathogens alongside desirable fermenting organisms (Capozzi et al. 2017). In fermented soybean products such as doenjang, thua nao, and sieng, Bacillus cereus has been isolated at high frequencies, with several strains harboring the cereulide‐encoding crs gene responsible for emetic toxicity (X. H. Chin et al. 2024). Although final pH values below 4.6 generally inhibit most vegetative pathogenic bacteria, many soybean‐based ferments pH values finish around 5.0–5.5, creating a window in which pathogens may persist or even proliferate (Zhou et al. 2022).

Clostridium botulinum poses a unique concern in low‐acid, anaerobic fermented products (Rawson et al. 2023). Traditional fish fermentations conducted at temperatures between 25°C–37°C and pH > 4.6 provide ideal conditions for spore germination and neurotoxin production (Novakova et al. 2023). However, non‐proteolytic strains of C. botulinum (Group II) are of particular concern in refrigerated aminopeptide seasonings, as they can grow at refrigeration temperatures (Derman et al. 2011; Pernu et al. 2020).

BAs are nitrogenous compounds formed primarily through microbial decarboxylation of free amino acids during fermentation and storage (Wójcik et al. 2021). Histamine, tyramine, putrescine, and cadaverine are the most prevalent BAs in fermented fish and soy sauces, with histamine being the principal toxicological concern (Ma et al. 2022). Regulatory agencies, including the FDA and Codex Alimentarius, have established maximum histamine limits of 50–400 mg/kg in fermented fish products, yet field surveys reveal that some traditional Asian fish sauces frequently exceed these thresholds (Huang et al. 2025). Microbiologically uncontrolled, long‐fermented seasonings lead to high concentration of BAs (Q. Zhang et al. 2024).

Filamentous fungi—particularly Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Rhizopus spp.—are integral to the solid‐state fermentation stage of many aminopeptide seasonings (Owolabi et al. 2022). Selected strains such as A. oryzae are Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) and prized for their proteolytic and amylolytic enzyme production (Sewalt et al. 2016). However, contamination with toxigenic molds can introduce mycotoxins, such as certain aflatoxins, classified as Group 1 human carcinogens (Skowron et al. 2022).

Chemical Hazards

Aminopeptide seasonings rich in unsaturated fatty acids and free amino acids are inherently susceptible to oxidative degradation during fermentation, aging, and storage. Lipid oxidation generates primary products such as hydroperoxides (measured as peroxide value [POV]), as well as volatile aldehydes (hexanal, heptanal, and nonanal) that impart rancid, fishy, and cardboard‐like off‐flavors (L. Feng et al. 2021; Y. Wang et al. 2022). Protein oxidation yields carbonyl derivatives and advanced oxidation end‐products that reduce digestibility, compromise texture, and may pose toxicological risks through the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Domínguez et al. 2021; D. Wang et al. 2022).

Fermentation conditions that favor oxidation include elevated temperatures, prolonged aging, high oxygen exposure, and low antioxidant capacity in raw materials. Fish‐based ferments, which contain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), are particularly vulnerable (Y. Wang et al. 2023). Studies on fermented sausages demonstrate that lipid oxidation accelerates protein carbonylation, creating a vicious cycle in which oxidative stress propagates across multiple macromolecular systems (Fuentes et al. 2014). Moreover, it has been evaluated how lipid oxidation is also proportional to the BAs content (Y. Liu et al. 2022). High salt concentrations, commonly employed to control microbial hazards, can paradoxically enhance oxidation by disrupting cellular antioxidant enzyme systems and promoting pro‐oxidant metal release from protein matrices (Kabil et al. 2025).

Raw materials for aminopeptide seasonings—particularly fish, shellfish, and soybeans—may accumulate heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Hg, and As) from contaminated environments, including polluted waters, agricultural soils, and industrial emissions (Nuanphuen et al. 2023; Sarsembayeva et al. 2020; Scutarașu and Trincă 2023). Although fermentation does not generate heavy metals, concentration effects during drying, smoking, or evaporation can elevate contaminant levels in finished products.

Physical Hazards

Physical hazards in traditional aminopeptide seasonings arise from inadequate raw material preparation, substandard processing equipment, and insufficient packaging controls. Common physical contaminants include bone fragments, fish scales, shell particles, sand, stones, glass shards, and metal fragments from worn processing machinery (Raj et al. 2024). Mitigation strategies include standardized raw material cleaning protocols, sieving and filtration during processing, metal detection and x‐ray inspection of finished products, and robust supplier quality assurance programs.

Technological Transformations: Contemporary Processing and Process Control

The art and science of proteolytic fermentation have witnessed significant evolution driven by advances in enzymology, microbial ecology, and process engineering. Modern approaches to aminopeptide seasoning production leverage precise control over enzymatic pathways and microbial communities to optimize flavor development, safety, and scalability. Contemporary reworkings of ancient proteolytic seasonings draw on three enzymatic pathways while solving the traditional issues of high sodium concentration and BAs: endogenous autolysis of animal or plant tissues, microbial proteolysis mediated by molds, bacteria, and yeasts, and isolated proteases’ hydrolysis.

In parallel, naming conventions are evolving. Currently, innovative aminopeptide seasonings are being classified as garum or shoyu, whether they are animal‐based or plant‐based ferments, respectively. Other lineages (e.g., doenjang/jeotgal/thua nao/dawadawa) are also being adapted to modern times with targeted controls and new substrates, although their terminological references are being lost to garum and shoyu. The present review introduces the complexity of aminopeptide seasonings and broadens the vision of professionals dedicated to the development of aminopeptide seasonings, with the intention of preserving gastronomic heritage.

Accelerated Proteolysis Regimes

Traditional aminopeptide seasonings typically employ high sodium concentrations, even though sodium has been found to be one of the most dangerous ingredients of the modern diet, and a high‐rate consumption might lead to different heart pathologies (World Health Organization 2012). Moreover, the fermentation over long periods of time without microbial control leads to high amine content that could be avoided with the utilization of starters and by shortening of the ripening time (Zhang et al. 2024).

Advances in understanding the kinetics of enzymatic hydrolysis and peptide formation have revealed opportunities to improve the taste profile of aminopeptide seasonings by deliberately tuning salt (≈2%–20%), temperature (≈4°C–60°C), oxygen, a w, and pH to steer kinetics, safety, and flavor. High‐temperature and low‐salt regimes (≈50°C–60°C; 2%–5% NaCl) accelerate proteolysis and can compress timelines from months to weeks by boosting enzymatic reaction rates and substrate solubilization (Aquerreta et al. 2002; K.‐D. H. Chin and Koehler 1986; Kristinsson and Rasco 2000; Redzepi and Zilber 2018; Yongsawatdigul et al. 2007). Elevated temperatures can also intensify Maillard‐derived toasted/caramel notes and reduce the contribution of slow‐growing, aroma‐generating microbiota typical of ambient fermentations (Martins et al. 2000; Tamang et al. 2016).

The addition of enzymes could improve the safety control of the product, enabling a reduction in salt concentration and avoiding the production of BAs, as has already been proven by other authors (Martín‐Miguélez et al. 2026). Other strategies, such as ultrasound‐assisted or high‐pressure fermentation, can also accelerate the maturation period, being able to reduce sodium concentration and increase flavor (Gao et al. 2019, Gao et al. 2020; Gavahian et al. 2022; Morales‐de la Peña et al. 2023; Y. Zhang et al. 2022).

Exogenous Enzymatic Engineering

In addition to natural and microbial enzymes, isolated commercial proteases have become indispensable tools for accelerating hydrolysis and improving process efficiency. Enzyme preparations such as alcalase, papain, and bromelain enable targeted cleavage of protein substrates, producing peptides and free amino acids on compressed timelines. These enzymatic treatments can be combined with controlled fermentation under optimized conditions of temperature, salinity, pH, and oxygen availability to balance enzymatic activity with microbial succession. The addition of enzymes could accelerate the process even more, but longer maturations favor greater aromatic and textural complexity via mixed microbial succession (Abiose et al. 1982; Allwood et al. 2021; M. Y. Jung et al. 2018; Kothe et al. 2024; Lim et al. 2024; Ryu et al. 2021; Song et al. 2018).

The activity of microorganisms for a longer time and at a lower temperatures is sometimes required for gastronomic development and not only for umami. Nowadays, it is possible to perform an infinite variety of aminopeptide sauces, but a design plan should be developed to use different raw materials, types of fermentation, and proteolysis mechanisms to obtain the desired product. Emerging biotechnological innovations focus on engineering microbial consortia and enzyme cocktails designed to enhance flavor complexity and consistency in plant‐based matrices, addressing the rising demand for vegan and clean‐label products. Synthetic biology and metagenomic screening facilitate the identification of novel proteases with unique substrate specificities and stability profiles, facilitating their application in diverse fermentation systems. In addition, the integration of process analytical technologies enables real‐time monitoring and control of fermentation parameters, ensuring reproducibility and quality assurance at an industrial scale.

Nowadays, the intense quest for additive‐free, tasty food leads to the production of free amino acids and small peptides responsible for saltiness, umami, and kokumi enhancement (Ramesh et al. 2025; Yan et al. 2021). However, aminopeptide seasonings can be developed under controlled industrial processes that reflect both technological advances and heightened food safety awareness (Figure 3).

Biocontrol and Hurdle Technologies

The first concern about aminopeptide seasonings should be to avoid the development of pathogenic microorganisms that might proliferate during the whole proteolytic process. The traditional parameters used to control the pathogenic microorganisms are temperature, pH, and a w, which should be monitored and tailored for every product (Peleg 2022). The processing usually recommended by cookbook authors is performed at 60°C (Redzepi and Zilber 2018). This temperature prevents the development of pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus (Juneja et al. 2025), though it has been observed that some strains of other microorganisms, such as C. botulinum, might not be inactivated (Munir et al. 2023). Thus, this technique is usually combined with other strategies, such as the utilization of high salt concentrations, up to 20% (Ghayoomi et al. 2023). Even so, some microorganisms, such as yeasts and molds, can develop in 0.80 a w values or less (Grant et al. 2004).

Some novel production methods are also utilizing microorganisms such as LAB to reduce pH below safe levels (Zhou et al. 2022). The utilization of protective cultures during fermentation can avoid the development of pathogenic or spoilage microorganisms through pH modification but also through other mechanisms, such as inhibition or competition (Atasoy et al. 2024; Martín‐Miguélez et al. 2025). The selection of autochthonous microorganisms to avoid the modification of the sensory characteristics of the fermented product is a strategy currently carried out by some researchers (Moon et al. 2025; Whitaker et al. 2021).

However, the combination of elevated temperatures, high salt concentrations, and low pH can increase lipid and protein oxidation (Mei et al. 1998; Qu et al. 2020; Zhuang et al. 2022). The consumption of ROS and a high sodium intake might result in the development of cancer and cardiovascular diseases (Glorieux et al. 2024; Q. Wu et al. 2023). To deal with this problem, the utilization of antioxidants in our diet through the final product would help avoid oxidative stress (Halliwell 2023). On the other hand, low‐temperature processing with reduced salt concentration might also result in lower ROS levels, though the aminopeptide seasonings would be more microbiologically compromised (Glorieux et al. 2024; Q. Wu et al. 2023). Alternatively, the utilization of vacuum processing has also been proven to reduce the formation of ROS (Musakhanian et al. 2022). Vacuum processing and modified atmosphere packaging have been shown to reduce oxidative stress by limiting oxygen availability, thereby preserving sensory quality and extending shelf life (Yadav et al. 2025). Other innovative techniques, such as sodium substitution, the addition of protective cultures, bacteriocins, or bacteriophages, might avoid the development of pathogenic and spoilage bacteria, as has been proven in other products (Choi et al. 2023; D'accolti et al. 2021; García‐Díez and Saraiva 2021; X. Lin et al. 2021).

The aforementioned solutions could also help control the development of toxigenic compounds such as BAs or mycotoxins, both of which are derived from microbial metabolism and increased through the presence of free amino acids in aminopeptide seasonings (Dabadé et al. 2021; Kieliszek et al. 2021; Paramastuti et al. 2021). First, to avoid mold development, a vacuum has been proven by other authors to be an effective strategy for increasing the shelf‐life of different products (Ribeiro et al. 2021). This technology has widely spread in contemporary kitchens since the blooming of sous vide cuisine (Kathuria et al. 2022), and it might be easily utilized in homemade aminopeptide seasonings. The utilization of protective starters has also been proven by other authors to reduce the load of undesired microorganisms and the concentration of BAs (García‐Díez and Saraiva 2021). The processing of aminopeptide seasonings under low temperatures might also reduce the formation of BAs (Abuhlega and Ali 2022). The utilization of microbial control strategies is required to avoid the production of BAs that takes place during long‐term fermentation (Zhang et al. 2024).

A combination of different biocontrol strategies—commonly known as a multi‐hurdle approach—is essential for developing safe and high‐quality aminopeptide seasonings. By integrating several moderate interventions, such as vacuum processing and protective cultures, it could reduce the necessity to utilize extreme temperature, pH, and a w parameners while still achieving effective microbial control and minimizing the formation of harmful chemical compounds (Elhalis et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2016). Combining multiple, less aggressive barriers allows for better optimization between microbial safety and the maintenance of valuable organoleptic properties, which is critical for consumer acceptance and overall product success (Giri et al. 2011).

Based on the reviewed strategies, temperature, pH, and a w are key to limiting microbial hazards throughout the proteolytic process. Contemporary production integrates multiple hurdle technologies and biocontrol strategies to minimize hazards while preserving traditional flavor profiles. Vacuum processing stands out as a highly effective and affordable technique for inhibiting pathogenic and spoilage organisms, reducing oxidative reactions, and prolonging shelf‐life. Its suitability for both industrial and domestic use enhances its applicability. In addition, the incorporation of selected protective cultures represents a cost‐efficient, natural solution that aligns with consumer preferences for clean‐label products. Manufacturing Legal Requirements

Given the complexity of aminopeptide seasonings with flavoring properties, including those derived from fermentation or enzymatic processes, it is essential to understand the regulatory steps that govern their approval and use. The development and commercialization of these products with high concentrations of free amino acids and small peptides must comply with a well‐defined legal framework designed to guarantee food safety, consumer protection, and fair market practices.

At the global level, the Codex Alimentarius Commission—via the Codex Committee on Vegetable Proteins (CCVP)—establishes baseline definitions and utilization guidelines (such as CXS 174–1989) to harmonize the quality, purity, and safety specifications of plant‐derived protein hydrolysates (FAO‐WHO 1989). The primary regulatory bottleneck for modern aminopeptide formulations involves monitoring and mitigating process‐induced chemical contaminants. Specifically, acid‐hydrolysis methods run the risk of generating toxic chloropropanols, primarily 3‐monochloropropane‐1,2‐diol (3‐MCPD), which forms when residual lipid backbones react with hydrochloric acid under thermal duress. Consequently, international bodies mandate strict threshold limits on these compounds to prevent toxicological exposure.

Regional compliance varies significantly, with certain jurisdictions enforcing highly stringent safety tolerances compared to global baselines. The European Union maintains the most restrictive framework worldwide, with a maximum residue limit of 20–50 µg/kg for 3‐MCPD in aminopeptide seasonings (European Commission 2014). Furthermore, any novel microbial strains or isolated enzymatic processing aids utilized to bypass these chemical hazards must undergo rigorous safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). In contrast, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) traditionally regulates aminopeptide seasonings via the GRAS affirmation pathway, emphasizing strict adherence to current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP). Meanwhile, leading consumer markets in Asia are rapidly modernizing their domestic criteria (e.g., China's GB standards) to restrict BAs and 3‐MCPD accumulation, forcing global manufacturers to transition from traditional acid‐driven processing to enzymatic or autolytic pathways to guarantee cross‐border compliance.

The current European regulatory frameworks for flavoring substances are sufficiently mature to adequately regulate the significant diversity of seasonings that can be used for this purpose in foods. In Europe, all foods, including those with flavoring properties, are primarily regulated by General Food Law Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 (European Parliament and Council 2002), which is designed “to ensure a high level of protection of human life and consumers' interests in relation to food, while ensuring the effective functioning of the internal market.” In addition to these regulations, the use of foods with flavoring properties is specifically regulated by the Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008 (European Parliament and Council 2008), which lays down rules on flavorings and food ingredients with flavoring properties for use in and on foods. It ensures the effective functioning of the internal market while protecting human health, including the protection of consumer interests, fair practices in food trade, and the environment. Within the framework of this regulation, it is essential to define the use of fermented or hydrolyzed seasonings of different natures and with high contents of amino acids and peptides as “food ingredients with flavoring properties,” which may be added to food for the main purpose of enhancing or modifying its flavor.

Their flavoring effect is obtained through culinary processes such as fermentation, use of microbial cultures, or the combination of these, with one or more ingredients in their composition. Food ingredients with flavoring properties may be used in or on foods without an evaluation and approval under Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008 (European Parliament and Council 2008). However, like other food ingredients, aminopeptide sauces are subject to fulfilling requirements set out in the General Food Law Article 14, which states: “food shall not be placed on the market if it is unsafe, and it is the food business operator´s responsibility to ensure food safety” (European Parliament and Council 2002). Aminopeptide seasonings might even be considered Novels Foods by the European Union (European Parliament and Council 2015), which establishes that any food, without a “significant” history of consumption in the EU before May 15, 1997, may be considered a novel food, including food from new sources, new substances used in food, as well as new ways and technologies for producing food, as it has been described previously in relation to fermented soybean extracts (Bell et al. 2017). When a seasoning is considered a novel food, producers must follow the EFSA guidance documents on novel and traditional foods in relation to risk assessment pursuant to Regulation (EU) No 2015/2283 (European Parliament and Council 2015). Before it can be placed on the market, the pre‐market authorization procedure for novel food includes a thorough risk assessment by the EFSA and production of a scientific opinion as per Article 29 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 (European Parliament and Council 2002; Mukherjee et al. 2022).

This section outlined the main legal instruments and procedures applicable to aminopeptide seasonings, from their classification as flavoring substances or food ingredients with flavoring properties under EU law, to the potential consideration of these products as novel foods requiring pre‐market authorization. Thus, if the criteria set out in the regulation are met, the product can be authorized for marketing in the EU.

Conclusion

Aminopeptide seasonings leverage proteolytic transformations from diverse protein‐rich substrates to provide potent umami and kokumi profiles. The industry's transition from heterogeneous, spontaneous fermentation to controlled, industrial biotechnology must be managed carefully to avoid sacrificing the sensory depth that defines these products. While modern strategies such as vacuum processing, the addition of specialized starter cultures, the use of isolated enzymes, and the incorporation of antioxidants offer pathways toward safer and more consistent production, these interventions must be balanced against the potential loss of aromatic complexity found in long‐maturation traditional products. Moving forward, embracing these technological advancements will be essential to meet the evolving challenges of global food security, public health, and environmental conservation, establishing proteolytic fermentation as a cornerstone of future food systems.

Consequently, future research must shift toward mapping explicit mechanistic pathways. Specifically, it remains unresolved how non‐starter LAB metabolically cooperate with dominant starter cultures under multi‐hurdle constraints. Open‐ended inquiries should clarify the exact intracellular and extracellular enzymatic mechanisms responsible for routing bitter hydrophobic amino acids into kokumi‐active γ‐glutamyl and non‐γ‐glutamyl peptides during long‐term aging. Ultimately, mastering these transformations may provide the sensory “key” to unlocking the widespread acceptance of sustainable, health‐promoting, plant‐forward diets.
