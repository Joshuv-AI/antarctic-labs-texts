Correlates of self-reported pregnancy complications: a cross-sectional study in two districts in Northern Ghana

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal health remains a critical indicator of health system performance globally, yet its outcomes remain suboptimal in low-resource settings. In Ghana, pregnancy complications contribute to over 12% of maternal deaths among women aged 15–49 years and are a key driver of maternal morbidity. This study examined the health system and socio-demographic correlates of self-reported pregnancy complications in two rural districts in Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted among 274 women aged 15–49 years in the Wa West and Mamprugu Moagduri districts of Ghana. Data were collected via structured interviewer-administered questionnaires and analysed using STATA 17. Binary logistic regression was applied, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals to determine associated factors of self-reported pregnancy complications.ResultsOverall, 38.7% of the respondents reported experiencing at least one pregnancy complication during their last birth. Stillbirth (63.2%) and miscarriage (26.4%) were the most reported pregnancy complications. Sub-district variations showed sharp differences within and across districts on pregnancy-related complications. Statistically significant socio-demographic correlates of pregnancy complications included being married (AoR = 0.16; 95% CI: 0.04–0.65), primiparous (AoR = 4.26; 95% CI:1.14–15.89), multiparity (AoR = 9.94; 95% CI: 2.80–35.3), higher income (AoR = 2.84; 95% CI: 1.49–5.41) and limited decision-making autonomy (AoR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.37–3.99). Early antenatal care (ANC) initiation (AoR = 2.86; 95% CI: 1.08–7.54), and long distance to health facility (AoR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.13–3.99) were associated with higher pregnancy complications, but facility-based pregnancy confirmation (AoR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.12–0.59) was associated with lower reports of pregnancy complications.ConclusionThe high burden of self-reported pregnancy complications underscores systemic gaps in service delivery, workforce distribution, and governance. Geographic disparities reflect systemic inequities in resource allocation and supervision. Strengthening ANC outreach, empowering women through gender-responsive governance, and leveraging digital health innovations for early detection and referral coordination are essential to improving maternal outcomes and Ghana’s progress toward SDG 3.1.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01960-w.

Background

Maternal and child health remain central to global health priorities, yet achieving optimal outcomes continues to be difficult in low-resource settings and in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1–3]. Pregnancy complications, a key indicator of maternal health, remain unacceptably high in several developing countries [4].

In Ghana, notable progress has been made in maternal and child health interventions over the past two decades [5]. However, many women still face preventable risks during pregnancy, often influenced by socio-demographic disparities. For example, Wiafe et al. [6] reported that 51.8% of pregnant women in Ghana experienced at least one complication. This persists despite national efforts to expand antenatal care (ANC) coverage and reduce financial barriers through the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) [7–9]. Pregnancy complications remain a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality, with more than 12% of maternal deaths among women aged 15–49 attributed to pregnancy-related causes [6]. A systematic review by Punguyire et al. [10] further reported a pooled estimate of perinatal mortality of 44.8 per 1000 births. Geographic disparities in maternal health outcomes are evident. Rural districts such as Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West face persistent shortages of skilled personnel, limited emergency obstetric services, and inadequate referral systems [6]. Although ANC coverage has improved nationally, skilled delivery and postnatal care remain disproportionately low in underserved regions. Delayed recognition of danger signs and weak referral mechanisms contribute to preventable complications such as hemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, and infections [9, 11]. In northern Ghana, long distances to health facilities, inadequate transportation, limited skilled personnel, and widespread poverty hinder timely access to care [12, 13]. Chronic shortages of midwives, obstetricians, and community health workers further constrain early detection and management of complications [14]. While the NHIS is intended to reduce financial barriers, coverage gaps, reimbursement delays, and continued out-of-pocket payments, particularly for diagnostics and emergency transport, limit its effectiveness and disproportionately affect poorer women [7, 8]. The 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) shows that although ANC coverage has improved nationally, skilled delivery and postnatal care remain low in underserved regions [15]. Socio-demographic factors such as maternal age, education, parity, and household wealth significantly influence both the risk of pregnancy complications and care-seeking behaviours [6]. Women with lower educational attainment or in the poorest quintiles are consistently less likely to receive timely prenatal care or deliver with skilled assistance [16].

Understanding the association between self-reported pregnancy complications and socio-demographic factors at the district level is vital for Ghana’s national agenda to improve maternal health and achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1. Evidence from underserved rural areas is particularly important for guiding microplanning, resource allocation, and tailored interventions.

This study presents preliminary baseline findings from the Enhance Community-Health System Communication and Knowledge Improvements for Pregnancy and Newborn Care using AI-tailored solutions (ENCOMPAS) project, implemented in the Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West Districts in northern Ghana. The findings provide insights into individual and district-level determinants of self-reported pregnancy complications, highlighting contextual drivers relevant for improving maternal and newborn health outcomes. These insights are critical for informing Ghana’s efforts toward achieving SDG 3.1 and meeting the targets of the National Maternal Health Strategy (2020–2025) (GNA, 2020).

Methods

Study design

The study employed a cross-sectional design to collect quantitative data from women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in the two ENCOMPAS project districts, Wa West and Mamprugu Moagduri. Our analysis focused on responses to questions on participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, health system characteristics, and self-reported pregnancy complications as captured in the baseline survey study questionnaire of the ENCOMPAS project.

Study sites

The study was conducted in two underserved districts in Ghana: Wa West District (WWD) in the Upper West Region and Mamprugu Moagduri District (MMD) in the North East Region. Wa West, with its capital at Wechiau, was established in 2004 under Legislative Instrument 1751. It lies in the western part of the region, bordered by Burkina Faso to the west and the Northern Region to the south. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the district has a population of 96,957, comprising 45,880 males and 51,077 females.

Mamprugu Moagduri District, created in 2012 from West Mamprusi, is one of six districts in the North East Region, with Yagaba as its capital. Covering a land area of 2,150 square kilometers, it is situated between longitudes 0°35’W and 1°45’W and latitudes 9°55’N and 10°35’N. It shares boundaries with districts in the Northern, Upper West, and Upper East Regions. The 2021 census reports a population of 66,181, with 32,931 males (49.76%) and 33,250 females (50.24%) for the district.

Study population

The study population consisted of women of reproductive age (15–49 years), including pregnant women and women with children under five years residing in selected communities within the two districts (Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West).

Sampling procedure

A multistage sampling technique was used. First, the two districts and sub-districts were purposively selected based on facility coverage and maternal health indicators. Within each sub-district, communities were randomly sampled, followed by systematic sampling of eligible households. In selecting households, a geographical landmark (e.g., community centre or market) was identified in each community. A water bottle was then spun, and the direction the mouth of the bottle pointed was considered the starting point of the survey [17]. After this, every other kth (sampling fraction=number of households/sample size) household in that direction was then entered into. The number k was used based on the sampling fraction derived from the estimated population for the study. A household was defined as a group of people who live under the same dwelling unit and share catering arrangements/services. In each household, one eligible woman of reproductive age (15–49 years) was recruited, once available. However, where more than one eligible woman of reproductive age was in a household, balloting using the lottery method was done to select one for them.

Sample size

The sample size for this study was calculated using OpenEpi to ensure statistical validity across both study districts. The calculation was based on previously reported proportions of pregnancy complications, with parameters set at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. To account for potential non-response, the sample size was upwardly adjusted by 10%, resulting in a final sample size of 274 women aged 15–49 years.

Data collection instruments and procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire developed by the ENCOMPAS research team (refer to supplementary file). The tool captured socio-demographic characteristics, household health decision-making, obstetric history, antenatal care utilisation, and self-reported pregnancy complications. The questionnaire was pre-tested in a neighbouring district among a population similar to that of the study population and refined for clarity and contextual relevance. The questionnaire was mounted on the Kobocollect toolbox for electronic data collection. Before the data collection, the research assistants were trained on the data collection instrument. The data collectors who were fluent in the local languages conducted face-to-face interviews using tablets equipped with the Kobocollect toolbox to ensure data quality and to minimise data entry errors. Supervisors monitored fieldwork daily and conducted random spot checks. The data collection took place in April 2024. The interviews were conducted mostly in the homes of the study participants and at a secluded area where no third party of the household could hear what was being discussed. Each interview lasted for at least 20 to 30 min.

Variables and measures

The primary outcome in this study was self-reported pregnancy complications. For the purpose of this research, a pregnancy complication was operationally defined as any adverse health condition experienced during pregnancy that participants themselves reported as having been identified and confirmed by a health professional. During interviews, women were asked whether they had experienced any health problems while pregnant, and if so, whether these problems had been medically diagnosed by a doctor, nurse, midwife, or other qualified health worker. Only conditions that participants explicitly linked to a professional diagnosis were classified as complications in the dataset. Importantly, the reporting framework allowed for multiple complications to be recorded per respondent. Women could describe more than one condition if they had experienced several distinct complications during the same pregnancy. Each reported complication was captured as a separate entry, enabling analysis of both single and multiple adverse events. This approach ensured that the outcome variable reflected medically recognized complications rather than self-perceived discomforts or minor symptoms, while also acknowledging the possibility of overlapping or recurrent conditions within individual pregnancies. For this study, pregnancy complications encompassed a range of clinically recognized conditions, including but not limited to eclampsia, miscarriage, stillbirth, antepartum or postpartum bleeding, and other medically validated complications. This definition ensured that reported outcomes reflected both the participants lived experiences and professional medical confirmation, thereby strengthening the reliability of the data collected. The independent variables comprised a range of socio-demographic and reproductive health factors hypothesised to influence pregnancy outcomes. These included: Age of women (in completed years), educational attainment, marital status, number of pregnancies (gravidity), parity (number of live births), occupation, household income, timing of ANC initiation, confirmation by health facility, categorized as early (first trimester) or late (second trimester onward), household health decision-making autonomy, defined by whether the woman independently made decisions regarding her health or relied on a partner, distance to the nearest health facility, measured in travel time.

Data analysis

Data collected were exported to STATA version 17 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics summarised respondent characteristics and complication prevalence. Multivariate analysis using binary logistic regressions was used to examine the associations between socio-demographic and health system correlates of pregnancy complications. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant at a 95% confidence interval. The results from the analysis were presented in Crude Odd Ratios (CoR) and Adjusted Odd Ratios (AORs) using tables and graphs.

Prior to fitting logistic regression models, we examined potential multicollinearity among predictor variables. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated using ordinary least squares regression, with values greater than 10 considered indicative of problematic collinearity. All diagnostics indicated acceptable levels of collinearity, supporting the validity of the final models.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC:002/01/25). Written informed consent was obtained from participants before including them in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study. The participants were given code names to conceal their actual identities. All methods and study procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, assured of confidentiality. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were informed that they could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Results

Table 1 summarises the socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants. The majority (56.6%) were aged between 20 and 29 years, followed by 28.1% aged 30–39 years, 10.6% under 20 years, and 4.7% above 39 years. Most participants were married (91.9%), while 7.0% had never married. In terms of educational attainment, nearly half (48.9%) had no formal education, 19.0% had completed primary education, 20.4% had attained junior high school level, 10.6% had reached senior high school level, and only 1.1% had tertiary education. Regarding occupation, 35.4% were housewives, 34.7% were engaged in farming, and 3.7% were unemployed. A majority (67.9%) reported earning less than 500 Ghana Cedis monthly, while 28.8% earned between 500 and 1,000 Cedis. In terms of religious affiliation, 58.2% identified as Muslims, 37.2% as Christians, and 4.4% as African traditionalists. Reproductive history revealed that 78.5% had carried between one and four pregnancies to term, while 17.5% had carried five to ten pregnancies. With respect to parity, 57.3% were multiparous, and 24.8% were primiparous.

The data reveal a marked disparity between the two study districts; a substantial majority of complications (61.7%) were reported by women residing in Mamprugu District, compared to only 11.2% from Wa West District. Figure 3 presents the distribution of self-reported pregnancy complications across sub-districts in Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West districts. In the Wa West District, the Vieri sub-district recorded the highest proportion of complications, accounting for 18.2% of all cases reported within the district. This was followed by Poyentanga and Gurungu, which contributed 11.5% and 10.3% of complications, respectively. At the other end of the spectrum, Dorimon reported the lowest burden, with only 7.1% of cases. In the Mamprugu Moagduri District, the uneven distribution was more pronounced. The Yagaba sub-district emerged as the most affected, with a staggering prevalence of 91.2% of all complications reported in the district. This was followed by Kubori (60.9%) and Yinkpabongo (47.2%), both of which also exhibited elevated complication rates. In contrast, Kunkwa recorded the lowest proportion of complications within Mamprugu, accounting for 17.2% of cases.

Fig. 3Self-reported pregnancy complications by Sub-district in two districts

Self-reported pregnancy complications by Sub-district in two districts

Figure 4 presents the distribution of self-reported pregnancy complications by type across the two study districts. In Wa West District, the most frequently reported complication was miscarriage, accounting for 50.0% of all cases. This was followed by postpartum haemorrhage at 35.7%. Stillbirth was the least reported complication in this district, comprising 14.3% of cases. In contrast, the Mamprugu District exhibited a different pattern. The majority of complications (70.6%) were stillbirths. Miscarriage was the second most common complication, reported by 22.8% of affected women. Other complications included antepartum haemorrhage (33.3%), typically occurring before labor and often associated with placental disorders; postpartum haemorrhage (2.2%); and pre-eclampsia, a hypertensive disorder, which accounted for 1.1% of cases.

Figure 5 illustrates the age-specific distribution of self-reported pregnancy complications among women across the two districts. The findings indicate that the highest proportion of complications (50.7%) occurred among women aged 30 to 39 years, suggesting that this age group may be particularly vulnerable to adverse maternal outcomes. Women aged 20 to 29 years accounted for 36.8% of reported complications, reflecting a substantial burden among younger reproductive-age women as well. Interestingly, 30.8% of complications were reported among women aged above 39 years, highlighting the risks associated with advanced maternal age. Table 2 presents the factors associated with self-reported pregnancy complications among women in the study population. These factors are categorised into individual-level and health system-related determinants. Among individual-level factors, marital status was protective: married women were less likely to experience pregnancy complications compared to those who had never married (AoR = 0.16; 95% CI: 0.04–0.65). Compared to those who had no formal education, women who had primary level (AoR = 0.41; 95% CI:0.18–0.97] and JHS (AoR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.15–0.77) level of education were less likely to experience pregnancy complications. Compared to nulliparous women, primiparous (AoR = 4.26; 95% CI: 1.14–15.89) and multiparous (AoR = 9.94; 95% CI: 2.80–35.36) women were 4.26 and 9.94 times more likely to report complications, respectively. Household decision-making autonomy also showed a significant association; women whose health seeking decisions were dependent on their partners or husbands had increased odds of experiencing complications (AoR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.37–3.99). Those who earned above GHC 500 were also more likely to report pregnancy complications compared to those who earned below 500 (AoR = 2.84; 95% CI:1.49–5.41).

Women who confirmed their pregnancies at a health facility were less likely to experience complications compared to those who relied on home-based confirmation methods (AoR = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.12–0.59). Those who resided far from a health facility (30 to 1 h travel distance) were about 2 times more likely to experience pregnancy complications compared to those who lived close to the health facility (AoR = 2.12; 95%CI = 1.13–3.99). Also, women who initiated ANC late in pregnancy had 2.86 times higher odds of experiencing complications compared to those who began ANC early (AoR = 2.86; 95% CI: 1.08–7.54).

Discussion

Our study revealed differentials in self-reported pregnancy complications among reproductive-age women in two underserved districts of northern Ghana. Overall, 38.7% of women surveyed reported having a pregnancy complication across the two districts. This finding aligns with previous studies in Ghana in a similar context, specifically in the Upper West and Ashanti Regions [9, 18]. However, higher rates reported in urban settings such as Greater Accra (51.8%) [6] suggest that the prevalence of preganacy complications may vary by population characteristics, health system capacity, and definitions used. Our findings may underscore the persistent burden of maternal health challenges in rural contexts and the need for strengthened ANC quality, early detection mechanisms, and targeted interventions. District-level disparities across the two study sites are also suggestive of underlying inequities in health system readiness, resource allocation, and supervisory oversight in these settings. Variations in reported disparities regarding pregnancy complications among women in this study are consistent with national evidence highlighting geographic inequalities in maternal health outcomes [19].

We found significant intra-district disparities in the distribution of self-reported pregnancy complications across sub-districts in Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West districts. These spatial patterns underscore the uneven burden of maternal health risks and highlight the need for geographically targeted interventions. In Mamprugu Moagduri, the Yagaba sub-district reported an overwhelming majority of all complications, followed by Kubori and Yinkpabongo. In Wa West, Vieri accounted for the highest proportion, followed by Poyentanga and Gurungu, while Dorimon reported the lowest. These findings suggest that maternal health risks are not evenly distributed, even within districts, and that certain sub-populations face disproportionately higher burdens. The observed disparities in self-reported pregnancy complications across sub-districts may reflect a combination of contextual and individual-level influences. While our study did not directly measure workforce distribution, service readiness, or governance, prior research in Ghana has documented challenges that plausibly contribute to uneven maternal health outcomes. For example, sub-districts such as Yagaba and Kubori have been described in earlier studies as facing limited emergency obstetric care infrastructure, which can delay the detection and management of complications [20]. Geographic barriers, including poor road networks and long travel distances to health facilities, are also known to restrict timely access to skilled care, particularly during emergencies [21]. In addition, financial constraints, such as transport costs and out-of-pocket payments for diagnostics, persist despite the coverage offered by Ghana’s NHIS [7], disproportionately affecting women in remote and underserved areas. The clustering of complications in specific sub-districts has important implications for maternal health policy and practice. High-burden areas such as Yagaba, Kubori, and Vieri should be prioritized for targeted interventions that respond to local needs. Potential strategies include strengthening mobile antenatal care outreach, expanding the coverage of Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, and improving readiness for emergency obstetric care. These geographically tailored approaches can help reduce disparities by ensuring that resources are directed to the sub-populations most at risk.

Stillbirth emerged as the most prevalent complication, followed by miscarriage. These findings mirror regional and global trends, where stillbirths remain a major contributor to maternal and perinatal morbidity, particularly in rural African settings [22, 23]. In Ghana, stillbirth rates range from 15 to 30 per 1,000 births, with higher rates in tertiary facilities serving rural populations [24]. The high burden of miscarriage also reflects gaps in early pregnancy screening and counseling services [25, 26]. These outcomes highlight critical service delivery gaps, particularly in ANC timeliness, continuity of care, and emergency obstetric readiness. Strengthening ANC outreach and integrating maternal and foetal health strategies that prioritize early risk identification, emotional support, and continuity across pregnancy stages are essential.

Marital status was protective, with married women being less likely to report complications. This aligns with evidence that marital support enhances access to care, financial stability, and adherence to ANC schedules [27, 28]. Married women are more likely to deliver in health facilities and engage consistently with ANC services, both of which are protective against adverse outcomes [29].

This study found that women with primary and Junior High School (JHS) education were significantly less likely to experience pregnancy complications compared to those with no formal education. This finding highlights the protective role of maternal education in shaping pregnancy outcomes and underscores education as a critical social determinant of health. Our findings are similar to evidence from Ghana and that of other parts of sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) showing that educated women are more likely to initiate ANC early, attend recommended visits, and deliver with skilled birth attendants [15, 16, 30]. Our findings could be due to a myriad of explanations. First, educated women are more likely to understand ANC guidelines, recognize danger signs, and adhere to medical advice [31]. Secondly, education empowers women to make independent health decisions, reducing delays caused by partner-controlled decision-making [30]. Also, educated women often have better employment opportunities and financial resources, enabling them to afford transport and healthcare costs [32]. Furthermore, education is strongly associated with early ANC initiation, consistent attendance, and skilled delivery, all of which reduce complications [30, 31].

Parity emerged as a strong predictor, with primiparous and multiparous women being more likely to experience complications. This supports findings from Wiafe et al. [6] and Zeleke et al. [33], which associate higher parity with cumulative physiological strain and delayed care-seeking due to perceived experience. Self-complacency among primiparous and multiparous women may lead to missed opportunities for early detection and management of complications [25, 34].

Contrary to expectations, we found that women earning above GHC 500 reported higher odds of pregnancy complications. This finding may reflect reporting bias and health-seeking behavior rather than a direct causal link. Women with higher incomes are more likely to access health facilities, undergo diagnostic procedures, and report complications that poorer women may not recognize or disclose. Similar patterns have been observed in other studies in Ghana, where socioeconomic inequalities influence not only access to skilled birth attendants but also reporting of maternal health outcomes [35, 36]. A systematic review across Africa also found that socioeconomic status can paradoxically increase detection of maternal near-miss events due to greater health system contact [37].

Household decision-making dynamics were also significant. Women whose health seeking decisions were dependent on their partners had higher odds of complications, echoing studies that link limited autonomy to delayed care-seeking and reduced adherence to maternal health interventions [38, 39]. In rural settings, partner-controlled decisions often affect access to transport and finances, further hindering timely engagement with health services. The influence of household decision-making highlights governance challenges in promoting women’s autonomy. Gender-responsive policies, community education, and male engagement are needed to empower women and improve maternal health outcomes. Our findings also point to the influence of governance and household dynamics. Women whose health decisions were dependent on their partners had significantly higher odds of experiencing complications. This aligns with studies showing that limited autonomy delays care-seeking and reduces adherence to maternal health interventions [40]. Promoting gender-responsive governance, through community education, male engagement, and policy reforms, can empower women to make timely health decisions.

We found in our study that women who confirmed their pregnancies at health facilities were less likely to experience complications compared to those relying on home-based methods. This finding reinforces the importance of early clinical engagement and aligns with evidence that facility-based confirmation promotes timely ANC initiation and risk screening [41, 42]. Home-based confirmation, often based on missed periods or informal testing, may delay care-seeking and reduce opportunities for early intervention [43].

We found a significant correlation between distance to health facility and self-reported pregnancy complications. Women living farther from health facilities (30 min to 1 h travel) were twice as likely to experience complications compared to those living nearby. This aligns with extensive literature showing that geographic access is a critical determinant of maternal outcomes. Poor road networks, long travel times, and limited emergency transport delay care-seeking and contribute to preventable complications [44]. The Ghana DHS (2022) similarly reported that rural women face disproportionate barriers to skilled delivery and timely ANC, resulting in higher maternal morbidity [45].

We found that the timing of ANC initiation was a critical determinant. Women who initiated ANC late had higher odds of complications, consistent with studies in Ghana and across SSA [9, 46]. Early ANC engagement facilitates screening for gestational conditions, health education, and birth preparedness. Delayed initiation often results in missed services and unmanaged risks. Late ANC initiation and high rates of stillbirth reflect gaps in service delivery, particularly in early risk detection and emergency obstetric care. Strengthening ANC outreach, integrating maternal and foetal health strategies, and ensuring continuity of care across pregnancy stages are essential.

Policy Implications for maternal health improvements at subnational levels in Ghana

The findings of this study reveal some contextual, socio-demographic and behavioral determinants that contribute to pregnancy complications in underserved districts, underscoring the urgent need for targeted, data-driven policy responses that align with Ghana’s Maternal Health Strategy (2020–2025), the CHPS initiative, and SDG 3.1, to reduce maternal mortality to fewer than 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030. The findings highlight important intra-district disparities in self-reported pregnancy complications, with certain sub-districts such as Yagaba, Kubori, and Vieri bearing disproportionately higher burdens. These spatial patterns suggest that maternal health interventions in Ghana should not only be designed at the national or district level but also tailored to sub-district contexts where risks are concentrated. Based on the evidence generated, the most immediate policy implication is the need for geographically targeted resource allocation. Prioritising high-burden sub-districts for intensified maternal health programming can help ensure that limited resources are directed where the need is greatest. This may include strengthening routine antenatal care outreach, improving referral pathways, and enhancing community engagement to promote timely care-seeking.

Given the strong association between limited decision-making autonomy and pregnancy complications, the Ghana Health Service (GHS) should invest in community-based education programs that promote male involvement and empower women. These programs should foster shared decision-making, challenge restrictive gender norms, and encourage timely care-seeking behavior. To reduce stillbirths and late-stage complications, the GHS should enhance emergency obstetric care readiness at sub-district and district levels. Establishing functional referral hubs and ensuring 24/7 availability of skilled providers can significantly reduce delays in accessing life-saving care. Geographic disparities in complication rates reflect deeper structural issues in resource allocation and workforce distribution. Policymakers should prioritise maternal health investments in underserved regions, ensuring equitable deployment of midwives, CHVs, and skilled birth attendants.

Limitations and strengths

Our study has some limitations that are worth pointing out. First, the pregnancy complications data were based on self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias or underreporting, especially for sensitive outcomes like stillbirth or miscarriage. Secondly, the use of a cross-sectional design had limited causal inference; associations identified cannot confirm causal relationships between predictors and outcomes. Further, our findings are specific to two districts and may not be representative of other regions in Ghana with different health system dynamics or socio-cultural practices. Despite these limitations, our study holds some strengths. First, the study was conducted in two underserved and resource-limited health districts, Mamprugu Moagduri and Wa West, providing valuable insights into maternal health disparities in regions often underrepresented. Secondly, the study examined a wide range of socio-demographic and behavioural predictors, allowing for a nuanced understanding of risk factors of pregnancy complications within the two districts studied. Furthermore, the identification of sub-district hotspots and behavioural risk factors offers actionable evidence for targeted interventions by the GHS and the Ministry of Health.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

This study highlights the burden, distribution, and determinants of self‑reported pregnancy complications among women in two underserved districts of northern Ghana. We found a prevalence of 38.7%, with notable geographic clustering in Mamprugu Moagduri (particularly Yagaba and Kubori) and Wa West (Vieri). These findings demonstrate that maternal health risks are unevenly distributed even within districts, underscoring the importance of sub‑district level analyses. Individual‑level factors were significantly associated with pregnancy complications. Advanced maternal age, multiparity, and limited decision‑making autonomy increased the likelihood of complications, while marital status appeared protective, likely reflecting social and financial support that facilitates care‑seeking. Early engagement with antenatal care, through timely pregnancy confirmation and initiation of services, was associated with reduced risk of complications, highlighting the value of prompt and consistent maternal health care.

The implications of these findings are practical rather than systemic. High‑burden sub‑districts should be prioritized for intensified maternal health programming, including strengthened antenatal outreach and improved referral pathways. Interventions that empower women in decision‑making and promote supportive involvement of partners may also reduce risks. While broader issues such as workforce distribution, service readiness, and digital innovations are relevant to maternal health in Ghana, they were not directly assessed in this study and should be explored in future research. In sum, addressing pregnancy complications in Ghana requires a multifaceted approach that combines individual empowerment with systemic reform. These efforts are essential for achieving Ghana’s Maternal Health Strategy (2020–2025) and accelerating progress toward Sustainable Development Goal 3.1: reducing maternal mortality to fewer than 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030.
