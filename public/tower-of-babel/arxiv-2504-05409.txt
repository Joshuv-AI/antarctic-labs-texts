First-passage properties of the jump process with a drift. Two exactly solvable cases.
Ivan N. Burenev, Satya N. Majumdar

Abstract
. We investigate the first-passage properties of a jump process with a constant drift, focusing on two key observables: the first-passage time τ \tau and the number of jumps n n before the first-passage event. By mapping the problem onto an effective discrete-time random walk, we derive an exact expression for the Laplace transform of the joint distribution of τ \tau and n n using the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula. This result is then used to analyze the first-passage properties for two exactly solvable cases: (i) both the inter-jump intervals and jump amplitudes are exponentially distributed, and (ii) the inter-jump intervals are exponentially distributed while all jumps have the same fixed amplitude. We show the existence of two distinct regimes governed by the strength of the drift: (i) a survival regime , where the process remains positive indefinitely with finite probability; (ii) an absorption regime , where the first-passage eventually occurs; and (iii) a critical point at the boundary between these two phases. We characterize the asymptotic behavior of survival probabilities in each regime: they decay exponentially to a constant in the survival regime, vanish exponentially fast in the absorption regime, and exhibit power-law decay at the critical point. Furthermore, in the absorption regime, we derive large deviation forms for the marginal distributions of τ \tau and n n . The analytical predictions are validated through extensive numerical simulations.

Introduction

First-passage problems concern the time at which a stochastic process first reaches a boundary or a target. They have a long-standing history, with significant contributions dating back to the 1950s and since then they have been widely studied across various contexts in both mathematics and physics (see e.g.,
[

We consider a jump process with a constant drift, taking place on the positive semi-axis, where the system evolves through a combination of ballistic motion with constant velocity and random jumps. We assume the following:

The process, starting at

The amplitudes of the jumps

(4)

The process stops after first crossing of the origin to the negative side.

2.
The model and the main results

First, we define the process shown in Fig.
1
in a more formal way. Mathematically, it can be expressed as

(3)

X(t)=X_{0}-\alpha\,t+\sum_{j=1}^{n(t)}M_{j},

where
X_{0}
is the initial position,
\alpha>0
is the drift velocity, and
M_{j}
are positive
i.i.d.
random variables distributed with
q(M)
. Here,
n(t)
is the number of jumps that occurred up to the time
t
. We assume that the first jump happens at
t=0
, hence
n(0)=1
, and that the inter-jump intervals
t_{j}
are also
i.i.d.
random variables drawn from
p(t)
.

Before going into the details, it is convenient to gain some intuition and speculate about the expected results. Consider the two extreme cases:

•

If the drift is very strong (
\alpha\to\infty
), then the process rapidly moves toward the origin, and first-passage happens almost immediately. In this limit, we have
\tau=0
and
n=1
.

•

Conversely, if there is no drift (
\alpha=0
), then the process consists solely of positive jumps, so the first-passage never occurs.

This simple observation suggests the existence of two distinct regimes. For sufficiently small
\alpha
, the process remains positive indefinitely with finite probability. On the other hand, for sufficiently large
\alpha
, the process inevitably crosses the origin. We will refer to these two scenarios as the
survival regime
and the
absorption regime
respectively.
It is also natural to anticipate the existence of a critical value
\alpha_{c}
that separates these two behaviors, leading to what we will call the
critical point
.

As a matter of fact, the critical value
\alpha_{c}
and the large
X_{0}
behavior of the means of
\tau
and
n
in the
absorption regime
can be determined by a simple heuristic argument. Suppose that
\alpha
is large enough so that the process eventually crosses the origin. At this moment, we must have

(4)

X(\tau)=X_{0}-\alpha\tau+\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{j}=0.

From (
4
) it is clear that
n
and
\tau
are inherently correlated. Moreover, (
4
) implies the relation between their means:

(5)

\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=X_{0}+\langle M\rangle\,\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}],

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdots|\,X_{0}]
denotes averaging over all possible trajectories starting from
X_{0}
, and brackets
\langle\cdots\rangle
represent averaging over the distributions
p(t)
and
q(M)
,

(6)

\langle f(M,t)\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,f(M,t)\,q(M)\,p(t).

Another simple observation is that if the first-passage happens after
n
jumps, then

(7)

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}t_{j}<\tau<\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

The further from the origin the process starts, the more jumps occur before the first-passage event. Therefore, as
X_{0}\to\infty
, we have
n\to\infty
, and both sums in (
7
) can be approximated by
n\langle t\rangle
. Hence,

(8)

\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\approx}\langle t\rangle\,\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}].

By combining (
8
) with (
5
), we can easily find the asymptotic means of
\tau
and
n
:

(9)

\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\approx}-\frac{X_{0}}{\left\langle M-\alpha t\right\rangle},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\approx}-\frac{\langle t\rangle X_{0}}{\left\langle M-\alpha t\right\rangle}.

Note that
\left\langle M-\alpha t\right\rangle
is nothing but the average displacement per jump, and (
9
) suggests that for the system to be in the absorption regime, this displacement must be negative, meaning that, on average, the process moves toward the origin. This, in turn, suggests that the critical value of the drift velocity
\alpha_{c}
is given by

(10)

\alpha_{c}=\frac{\left\langle M\right\rangle}{\left\langle t\right\rangle}.

Having presented these heuristic arguments, we now proceed to the main results of the paper, which cannot be derived from the such a simple intuitive reasoning.

To characterize the first-passage properties more precisely we need to introduce several key quantities that will be used in the analysis. Throughout the paper, and in line with common practice in the physics literature, we adopt a slight abuse of notation by using the same symbols to denote both random variables and their realizations. While this may introduce some ambiguity from a strictly mathematical perspective, we believe it significantly improves readability by keeping the notation more compact.

The first two quantities are the survival probabilities, defined as:

(11)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\equiv\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,k\,|\,X_{0}],\quad S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\equiv\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\tau}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\tau},n\,|\,X_{0}],

where
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
is the joint probability distribution of the first-passage time
\tau
and the number of jumps
n
before the first-passage, given that the process starts at
X_{0}
.
Specifically,
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
is the probability that the process has remained positive for the first
n
jumps, while
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
is the probability that the process has stayed positive up to time
\tau
. Additionally, we define the probability that the process stays positive indefinitely as

(12)

S_{\infty}(X_{0})\equiv 1-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|X_{0}].

Finally, we introduce two marginal first-passage probability densities,

(13)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}],\qquad\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}].

These are directly related to the survival probabilities (
11
) through:

(14)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=-\partial_{\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0}),\qquad\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=S_{N}(n-1\,|\,X_{0})-S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0}).

In what follows, we show that there are indeed three scenarios with qualitatively different first-passage properties, which can be summarized as follows

(1)

Absorption regime.
The drift is strong
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
, and the process eventually crosses the origin.

•

Both
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
decay exponentially, and
S_{\infty}(X_{0})=0
;

•

We compute the decay rates for the survival probabilities and find the first two moments of
n
and
\tau
, as well as the marginal probability distributions (
13
) for
X_{0}=0
and in the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
.

(2)

Survival regime.
The drift is weak
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
, and the process may stay positive indefinitely.

•

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
approach a constant value
S_{\infty}(X_{0})>0
;

•

We compute the decay rates of the survival probabilities and the first two moments of
\tau
and
n
for the trajectories where the first-passage occurs.

(3)

Critical point.
The drift is critical
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
, marking the transition between the two regimes above.

•

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
decay as a power-law, and
S_{\infty}(X_{0})=0
.

•

We determine the prefactor of the power-law decay for both survival probabilities and analyze the continuous limit behavior as
X_{0}\to\infty
and
\tau\to\infty
(or
n\to\infty
).

More formally, we show that in the
absorption regime
, the survival probabilities decay exponentially

(15)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{\tau}{\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)}},\\
&S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{n}{\xi_{n}(\alpha)}},\end{aligned}\right.

in the
survival regime
, the survival probabilities asymptotically approach a constant with an exponential correction

(16)

\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{\tau}{\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)}},\\
&S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{n}{\xi_{n}(\alpha)}}.\end{aligned}\right.

As the drift velocity approaches the critical value
\alpha\to\alpha_{c}
(from either side), both
\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)
and
\xi_{n}(\alpha)
diverge as

(17)

\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}},\qquad\xi_{n}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}},

which leads to the power-law decay of the survival probabilities at the
critical point

(18)

\alpha=\alpha_{c}:\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{c_{\tau}}{\sqrt{\tau}},\\
&S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{c_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.\end{aligned}\right.

These different behaviors are illustrated schematically in Fig.
2
.

Figure 2.

Schematic plots of the survival probabilities
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
(left) and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
(right).
The solid blue lines correspond to the survival regime, where both survival probabilities exponentially decay to a constant value
S_{\infty}=S_{\infty}(X_{0})
.
The dotted red lines correspond to the absorption regime, where both survival probabilities exponentially decay to zero.
The dashed purple lines correspond to the critical point, where the survival probabilities decay as a power law.

The exact expressions for
\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)
,
\xi_{n}(\alpha)
,
c_{\tau}
,
c_{n}
, and
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
depend on the distributions

and

and will be provided shortly, along with a more detailed description of the first-passage properties in Sec.

and Sec.

. Since these results are rather technical, it is convenient to make some general comments before presenting them.

First, although in this paper we obtain (
15
), (
16
), (
17
) and (

) in the two exactly solvable cases, it is reasonable to expect that this behavior is universal for arbitrary light-tailed distributions
p(t)
and
q(M)
. Such analysis however is a non-trivial task on its own and falls beyond the scope of the present paper.

Second, to further develop intuition for these results, we take a brief detour and discuss the critical phenomena with the two dimensional Ising model serving as a representative example.
This model is a classical model in statistical mechanics that consists of spins
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
located on the sites of the square lattice, with nearest-neighbor interactions between the spins. The probability of a given configuration is:

(19)

\mathrm{Prob}[\{\sigma\}]=\frac{1}{Z}e^{\frac{J}{T}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}},

where
Z
is the partition function,
J
is the interaction strength,
T
is the temperature, and the sum is over all neighboring sites of the lattice. This model is known to have two phases depending on the temperature: an ordered phase at low temperatures and a disordered phase at high temperatures, with a phase transition occurring at
T=T_{c}
.

Above the critical temperature (
T>T_{c}
), thermal fluctuations dominate and
\langle\sigma\rangle=0
. The two-point correlation function in the scaling limit decays exponentially as

(20)

T>T_{c}:\qquad\left\langle\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\underset{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|}{\xi(T)}},

where
\xi(T)
is the temperature-dependent correlation length. This regime corresponds to complete disorder.

Below the critical temperature (
T<T_{c}
), the system exhibits spontaneous magnetization (
\langle\sigma\rangle\neq 0
), and long-range ferromagnetic order. Specifically, in the scaling limit, the two-point correlation function decays to a constant as

(21)

T<T_{c}:\qquad\left\langle\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\sigma\right\rangle^{2}\underset{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-\frac{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|}{\xi(T)}}.

This behavior reflects short-range order. While local regions maintain spin alignment, global coherence diminishes exponentially.

As the critical temperature is approached (from either side), the correlation length diverges as

(22)

\xi(T)\underset{T\to T_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{\left|T-T_{c}\right|},

leading to the power-law decay in the correlation function

(23)

T=T_{c}:\qquad\left\langle\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\underset{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|^{\frac{1}{4}}}.

Comparing (
15
), (
16
) and (
18
) with (
20
), (
21
) and (
23
) we observe a clear analogy:
the drift velocity
\alpha
plays the role of the temperature;
absorption
and
survival
regimes resemble high- and low-temperature behaviors of the Ising model;
\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)
and
\xi_{n}(\alpha)
act as “correlation lengths”.
It is important to emphasize that this is merely an illustrative analogy, which can help build intuition, but there is no connection between the stochastic process (
3

With this remark, we complete our detour and proceed to the results for the two exactly solvable cases.

2.1.
Results in the case I

The first exactly solvable case we consider is the scenario in which both the inter-event times and jump sizes follow exponential distributions

(24)

p(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t},{}\qquad q(M)=\gamma e^{-\gamma M}.

We begin by determining the explicit form of the double Laplace transform of the joint probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
(given by (
108
)). By analyzing this expression, we obtain the following results.

The critical drift, as anticipated in (
10
), is given by

(25)

\alpha_{c}=\frac{\langle M\rangle}{\langle t\rangle}=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}.

Survival regime.

For
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
, the survival probabilities tend to a constant with exponentially small correction, as described by (
16
). The probability of the process remaining positive indefinitely is

(26)

\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\qquad S_{\infty}(X_{0})=1-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{c}}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}-1\right)\gamma X_{0}\right].

The corresponding “correlation lengths” are

(27)

\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\gamma\,(\sqrt{\alpha}-\sqrt{\alpha_{c}})^{2}},\qquad\xi_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\log\frac{(\alpha+\alpha_{c})^{2}}{4\,\alpha\,\alpha_{c}}}.

For the trajectories where the first-passage does occur, we compute the conditional means

(28)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{\alpha+\beta X_{0}}{\alpha(\beta-\alpha\gamma)},

(29)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0},n<\infty]=\frac{\beta+\beta\gamma X_{0}}{\beta-\alpha\gamma},

and the conditional variances

(30)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{\beta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\gamma X_{0}}{(\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{3}},

(31)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0},n<\infty]=\beta\gamma\,\frac{\alpha(\beta+\alpha\gamma)+(\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})X_{0}}{(\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{3}}.

Absorption regime.

For
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
, the survival probabilities decay exponentially, as in (
15
), and the decay rates are again given by (
27
).
In this regime, we find the mean values of
\tau
and
n

(32)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\qquad\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\gamma\,\frac{\alpha+\beta X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},

and the variances

(33)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{\beta+\alpha\gamma+2\beta\gamma X_{0}}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}},

(34)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\beta\gamma\,\frac{\alpha(\alpha\gamma+\beta)+(\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})X_{0}}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}.

Moreover, we compute the probability distribution in the two limiting cases. Specifically, for
X_{0}=0
we obtain the exact form of the joint probability distribution

(35)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}=0]=\frac{1}{\beta}e^{-(\beta+\alpha\gamma)\tau}\;\frac{n}{(n!)^{2}}(\alpha\beta\gamma)^{n}\;\tau^{2n-2}.

Conversely, in the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
, the marginal first-passage probability distributions (
13
) admit large deviation forms

(36)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Phi(z)},\qquad z=\frac{\alpha\tau}{X_{0}}-1,

(37)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Psi(\nu)},\qquad\nu=\frac{\alpha n}{\beta X_{0}},

where the explicit expressions for the rate functions
\Phi(z)
and
\Psi(\nu)
are given by (
165
) and (
177
) respectively.

Critical point.

When
\alpha\to\alpha_{c}
, i.e., when the drift approaches its critical value, the “correlation lengths” diverge as

(38)

\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}},\qquad\xi_{n}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}}.

This divergence leads to a power-law behavior given in (
18
), with coefficients

(39)

c_{\tau}=\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\pi\beta}},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

At the critical point the process consists of identical jumps with finite moments and zero mean. Thus it naturally converges to Brownian motion in the scaling limit. For the survival probabilities, we have the following scaling behavior

(40)

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\beta\tau}}\right),\quad\tau\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed},

(41)

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

This concludes the results for the case of exponential distributions.

2.2.
Results in the case II

In the second exactly solvable case, we consider an exponential distribution for the time intervals, while keeping the jumps fixed

(42)

p(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t},\qquad q(M)=\delta(M-M_{0}).

With fixed jump sizes, the first-passage condition (
4
) simplifies to

(43)

X_{0}-\alpha\tau+nM_{0}=0,

implying a one-to-one correspondence between
n
and
\tau
. This means that one quantity fully determines the other. However, for completeness, we present results for both
n
and
\tau
.

Again, by computing the explicit form (
206
) of the double Laplace transform of the joint probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
and analyzing it, we obtain the results listed below.

The critical drift is given by

(44)

\alpha_{c}=\frac{\langle M\rangle}{\langle t\rangle}=\beta M_{0}.

Survival regime.

For
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
, the survival probabilities approach a constant with exponentially small corrections, as seen in (
16
). The probability that the process never crosses the origin is

(45)

\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad S_{\infty}(X_{0})=1-e^{-\Lambda(\alpha)\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{M_{0}}},\qquad\Lambda(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}+W_{0}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}}\right],

where
W_{0}(z)
is the principal branch of the Lambert
W
-function (see Sec.
5
for details). The “correlation lengths” in (
16
) are

(46)

\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)=\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha_{c}-\alpha-\alpha\log\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}},\qquad\xi_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{c}-\alpha-\alpha\log\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}}.

We again compute the conditional means

(47)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{\alpha\left(1+\Lambda(\alpha)-\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)},

(48)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0},n<\infty]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{M_{0}\left(1+\Lambda(\alpha)-\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)}-\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}},

and the variances

(49)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{M_{0}(X_{0}+M_{0})\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-\Lambda(\alpha)\right)}{\alpha^{2}\left(1+\Lambda(\alpha)-\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{3}},

(50)

\displaystyle\alpha<\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0},n<\infty]=\frac{(X_{0}+M_{0})\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-\Lambda(\alpha)\right)}{M_{0}\left(1+\Lambda(\alpha)-\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{3}}.

Absorption regime.

If
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
, then the survival probabilities vanish exponentially fast as in (
15
) with the “correlation lengths” given by (
46
). The means of
\tau
and
n
are given by:

(51)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{M_{0}+X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\quad\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{\alpha+\beta X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},

and for the variances we have

(52)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}\,\beta M_{0}^{2},\quad\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}\,\beta\alpha^{2}.

The joint probability distribution for
X_{0}=0
is given by

(53)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}=0]=\frac{1}{n!}e^{-\beta\tau}\left(n\,\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{n-1}\delta\left(\tau-n\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}\right),

where
\delta
-function enforces the relation (
43
) between
n
and
\tau
.
In the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
the marginal probability distributions admit large deviation forms

(54)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Phi(z)},\qquad z=\frac{\alpha\tau}{X_{0}}-1,

(55)

\displaystyle\alpha>\alpha_{c}:\quad\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Psi(\nu)},\qquad\nu=\frac{\alpha n}{\beta X_{0}},

with the rate functions
\Phi(z)
and
\Psi(\nu)
given by (
245
) and (
254
) respectively. Note that the large deviation form is the same as in (
36
) and (
37
), but the rate functions are different.

Critical point.

As
\alpha\to\alpha_{c}
, the “correlation lengths” diverge as

(56)

\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}},\qquad\xi_{n}(\alpha)\underset{\alpha\to\alpha_{c}}{\sim}\frac{1}{(\alpha-\alpha_{c})^{2}}.

This leads to a power-law behavior (
18
), with coefficients

(57)

c_{\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\beta}}\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right),\qquad c_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right).

In the scaling limit, for the survival probabilities we have

(58)

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta\tau}}\right),\quad\tau\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed},

(59)

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{c}:\quad

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

This concludes the results for the second exactly solvable case.

3.
An effective discrete-time random walk

In this section we construct a mapping of the original process onto an effective discrete-time random walk performing the trick similar to that used in
[
51
,
52
]
for the run-and-tumble particle in
d
-dimensions and in
[
53
]
for the cost of excursions (see also
[
54
]
).
This mapping enables us to circumvent solving the integral equations that arise in the renewal equation approach.
Below, we first briefly recall the renewal equation method, summarize key results from the literature on the discrete-time random walks, and finally, construct the aforementioned mapping.

3.1.
Renewal equation

The core idea behind the renewal equation formalism is a recurrence relation for the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
. Denote by
M_{1}
the amplitude of the first jump (at
t=0
). The process then follows one of the two mutually exclusive scenarios:

(1)

No further jumps occur (
n=1
). After the first jump the process follows purely ballistic motion with the velocity
\alpha
, leading to the first-passage time of
\tau=(X_{0}+M_{1})/\alpha
.

(2)

At least one additional jump occurs (
n>1
). Denoting by
t_{1}
the time of the second jump, we end up with the same process starting from
X_{0}^{\prime}=X_{0}+M_{1}-\alpha t_{1}
and hence the joint probability distribution of
\tau
and
n
reduces to
\mathbb{P}[\tau-t_{1},n-1\,|\,X_{0}^{\prime}]
.

This simple argument results in the recurrence relation:

(60)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(M_{1})\mathrm{d}M_{1}\;\int_{\frac{X_{0}+M_{1}}{\alpha}}^{\infty}p(t_{1})\mathrm{d}t_{1}\;\delta\left(\tau-\frac{1}{\alpha}(X_{0}+M_{1})\right)\delta_{n,1}\\
+\int_{0}^{\infty}q(M_{1})\mathrm{d}M_{1}\;\int_{0}^{\frac{X_{0}+M_{1}}{\alpha}}p(t_{1})\mathrm{d}t_{1}\;\mathbb{P}[\tau-t_{1},n-1\,|\,X_{0}+M_{1}-\alpha t_{1}],

where we have also accounted for the fact that
M_{1}
and
t_{1}
are random variables distributed with
p(t)
and
q(M)
.

In terms of the generating function

(61)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]

the renewal equation (
60
) reads

(62)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\int_{0}^{\infty}q(M_{1})\mathrm{d}M_{1}\;e^{-\rho\frac{X_{0}+M_{1}}{\alpha}}\int_{\frac{X_{0}+M_{1}}{\alpha}}^{\infty}p(t_{1})\mathrm{d}t_{1}\\
+s\int_{0}^{\infty}q(M_{1})\mathrm{d}M_{1}\;\int_{0}^{\frac{X_{0}+M_{1}}{\alpha}}p(t_{1})\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,e^{-\rho t_{1}}\,Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}+M_{1}-\alpha t_{1}).

Equations (
60
) and (
62
) are integral equations of the Wiener–Hopf type that commonly arise in the study of first-passage properties of continuous-time random walks (see e.g.
[
55
,
56
]
). Such equations are notoriously difficult to solve unless
p(t)
and
q(M)
take specific forms. In the both cases we consider,
p(t)
is an exponential distribution, enabling us to transform (
62
) into a differential equation and subsequently solve it obtaining an explicit expression for
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
.

3.2.
Generalized Pollaczek–Spitzer formula

Consider now a discrete-time random walk, where the jumps
\eta
’s are distributed with some probability density
f_{\text{d}}(\eta)

(63)

X_{j+1}=X_{j}+\eta_{j},\qquad\eta_{j}\leftarrow f_{\text{d}}(\eta).

We use the subscript “d” to avoid the confusion between the entities for the discrete-time random walk and their analogs for the original process (
3
).

Denote by
\mathrm{P}^{+}_{\text{d}}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]
the constrained propagator, i.e., the probability that the random walk starting at
X_{1}
arrives at
X_{2}
after
n
steps while always staying above the origin. Similarly, denote by
\mathrm{P}^{-}_{\text{d}}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]
the time reversed propagator, which corresponds to the change
\eta\mapsto-\eta
. These two propagators are related via

(64)

\mathrm{P}_{\text{d}}^{+}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]=\mathrm{P}^{-}_{\text{d}}[X_{2},X_{1}\,|\,n].

There is a closed expression for the Laplace transforms of these propagators which we will refer to as the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula (see
[
41
,
1
]
and references therein for a review). Specifically,

(65)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{1}\,e^{-\lambda_{1}X_{1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{2}\,e^{-\lambda_{2}X_{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\mathrm{P}^{\pm}_{\text{d}}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]=\frac{\phi^{\mp}_{\text{d}}(\lambda_{1};s)\;\phi^{\pm}_{\text{d}}(\lambda_{2};s)}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}},

where

(66)

\phi^{\pm}_{\text{d}}(\lambda;s)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\frac{1}{\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}k}\log\left[1-sF_{\text{d}}(k)\right]\right],\quad F_{\text{d}}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta\,e^{\mathrm{i}k\eta}f_{\text{d}}(\eta).

For the symmetric jump distribution the result (
65
) was first obtained in
[
57
]
. A relatively simple proof of its generalization to the asymmetric jump distributions can be found in Sec. III.A of
[
39
]
. Strictly speaking, the derivation in
[
39
]
was carried out for the case where
\eta
is symmetric with a constant drift, but the very same derivation is valid for arbitrary asymmetric random walks. It is worth mentioning that although only the propagator
\mathrm{P}_{\text{d}}^{+}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]
, will be used in the subsequent derivations, we provide the analogous expression for the reversed propagator
\mathrm{P}_{\text{d}}^{-}[X_{1},X_{2}\,|\,n]
for completeness. This also facilitates comparison with the proof presented in
[
39
]
, where both propagators play an important role.

To extract the distribution of the number of jumps before the first-passage from the constrained propagator, note that the survival probability
S_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
can be obtained from the constrained propagator
\mathrm{P}^{+}_{\text{d}}[X_{0},X_{2}\,|\,n]
by integrating over the final position
X_{2}
at step
n
, i.e.,

(67)

S_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{2}\,\mathrm{P}^{+}_{\text{d}}[X_{0},X_{2}\,|\,n].

Thus we can find the Laplace transform of the survival probability by taking the
\lambda_{2}\to 0
limit in (
65
),

(68)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{0}\,e^{-\lambda X_{0}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}S_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\phi^{-}_{\text{d}}(\lambda;s)\;\phi^{+}_{\text{d}}(0;s).

At the same time the probability distribution of the number of jumps
n
before the first-passage
\mathbb{P}_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
can be expressed in terms of the survival probability as

(69)

\mathbb{P}_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=S_{\text{d}}[n-1\,|\,X_{0}]-S_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}].

Substituting (
69
) into (
68
), after a simple calculation, yields

(70)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{0}\,e^{-\lambda X_{0}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\text{d}}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1-s}{\lambda}\phi^{-}_{\text{d}}(\lambda;s)\phi^{+}_{\text{d}}(0;s).

Now we construct a mapping of the original process onto a discrete random walk and use (
70
) to obtain an explicit expression for the Laplace transform of
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
.

3.3.
Mapping onto an effective discrete-time random walk

The idea behind the mapping of the original process onto an effective random walk is simple. Let
X_{j}
be the position of the original process (described by (
3
)) just before the
(j+1)
th jump (see Fig.
3
). The position
X_{j}
then evolves according to

(71)

X_{j+1}=X_{j}+\eta_{j},\qquad\eta_{j}=M_{j}-\alpha t_{j}.

This closely resembles a discrete-time random walk as in (
63
), except that we have yet to define the probability distribution of the increments
\eta_{j}
.

A naive approach is to treat
\eta_{j}
as the difference between two independent random variables: the jump amplitude
M_{j}
drawn from
q(M)
and the waiting time
t_{j}
(multiplied by
\alpha
) drawn from
p(t)
.
This simple mapping allows us to apply Pollazcek-Spitzer formula (
70
) and extract the distribution of the number of jumps
n
before the first-passage. However, this approach discards all information about the first-passage time
\tau
. To account for both
\tau
and
n
, a more refined mapping is required.

Figure 3.

Schematic representation of the trajectory of the original process (black) from Fig.
1
, along with its effective random walk counterpart (green) given by (
71
).
The coordinate
X_{j}
of the effective random walk corresponds to the position of the original process right before the
(j+1)
th jump.
Here, the first-passage occurs after
n=7
steps, meaning that the first six positions
X_{1},\ldots,X_{6}
of the effective walk are positive, while
X_{7}
is negative.
Note that the discrete-time walk does not include
t
and the time axis is shown only to clarify the construction.

Both the jumps
M_{j}
and the time intervals
t_{j}
are positive, which allows us to express the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
as

(72)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}M_{n}\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}p(t_{j})q(M_{j})\\
\delta\left(\tau-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(X_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{j}\right)\right)\theta(-X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\theta(X_{j}),

where
X_{j}
are given by (
71
). The
\delta
-function in (
72
) enforces the first-passage condition (
4
).
The product of Heaviside step functions ensures that the first
n-1
coordinates
X_{j}
are positive, while
X_{n}
is negative, i.e., that the first-passage occurs exactly after
n
jumps.

Taking the Laplace transform with respect to
\tau
and changing the integration variable in (
72
) from
t_{j}
to
\eta_{j}
via
\eta_{j}=M_{j}-\alpha t_{j}
(so that
X_{j}
is now function of
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1,\ldots,j-1}
), we obtain

(73)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=e^{-\rho\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha}}\left[c(\rho)\right]^{n}\\
\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}\eta_{n}\;\theta(-X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\theta(X_{j})\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}f(\eta_{j};\rho),

where

(74)

\displaystyle f(\eta;\rho)=\frac{1}{\alpha\;c(\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}}q(M)\;p\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(M-\eta\right)\right),

(75)

\displaystyle c(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}}q(M).

The functions
c(\rho)
and
f(\eta;\rho)
are chosen in such a way, that
f(\eta;\rho)
is normalized to one, which can be easily verified. Integrating over
\eta
we obtain

(76)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\eta;\rho)\mathrm{d}\eta=\frac{1}{\alpha\,c(\rho)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}}q(M)\;p\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(M-\eta\right)\right).

Changing the variable of integration via
\eta\mapsto\tilde{\eta}=\frac{1}{\alpha}(M-\eta)
yields

(77)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\eta;\rho)\mathrm{d}\eta=\frac{1}{c(\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}}q(M)\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\eta}\,p\left(\tilde{\eta}\right).

Recall that
p(t)
is a probability density and hence the second integral in (
77
) is one and comparing the remaining integral with (
75
), we immediately see that

(78)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\eta;\rho)\mathrm{d}\eta=\frac{1}{c(\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}}q(M)\;=1.

Since
f(\eta;\rho)
is positive and normalized to one, it can be interpreted as a probability distribution of the increments
\eta_{j}
.
The integral (
73
) is then nothing but the probability that a discrete-time random walk
X_{j+1}=X_{j}+\eta_{j}
crosses the origin exactly between steps
n-1
and
n
. This probability can be computed using the Pollaczek-Spitzer formula (
70
), with the prefactors in (
73
) inducing the shift of
s
and
\lambda
in (
70
), namely
s\mapsto sc(\rho)
and
\lambda\mapsto\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}
.
Introducing the notation

(79)

\hat{Q}(\rho,s\,|\,\lambda)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{0}\,e^{-\lambda X_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}],

and comparing (
73
) with (
70
),
we arrive at the closed expression for the triple Laplace transform of
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
:

(80)

\hat{Q}(\rho,s\,|\,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}-\frac{1-s\,c(\rho)}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}\;\phi^{-}\!\left(\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha};\rho,s\right)\phi^{+}\!\left(0;\rho,s\right),

where

(81)

\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\;\frac{1}{\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}k}\log\left[1-s\,c(\rho)F(k;\rho)\right]\right]

and

(82)

F(k;\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\mathrm{i}k\eta}f(\eta;\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Rewriting
F(k;\rho)
in terms of the original process rather than the effective random walk, we obtain

(83)

F(k;\rho)=\frac{1}{c(\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\rho\frac{M}{\alpha}+\mathrm{i}kM}q(M)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha kt}p(t).

Suppose, for a moment, that we are interested only in the number of jumps
n
before the first-passage. This corresponds to setting
\rho=0
. In this case
c(\rho)=1
and

(84)

f(\eta;0)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,q(M)\,p\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}(M-\eta)\right)\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,q(M)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,p(t)\,\delta\left(\eta-(M-\alpha t)\right).

Thus, for
\rho=0
, an increment
\eta_{j}
is simply the difference between the jump amplitude
M_{j}
and the waiting time
t_{j}
, as we would expect from the “naive” mapping.

In the rest of the paper we derive the results presented in Sec.
2
from (
80
). Before diving in the computation let us make one final remark. The mapping to an effective random walk described in this section is essentially an intuitive way to motivate the representation (
72
). In principle, one can substitute this representation into the renewal equation (
60
) and then follow the derivation of the Pollaczek-Spitzer formula, as outlined, for example, in
[
57
]
.

4.
Exactly solvable case I. Double exponential distribution

In this section, we focus on a special case where both jumps and waiting times are exponentially distributed,

(85)

p(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t},\qquad q(M)=\gamma e^{-\gamma M}.

In this scenario, the generating function
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
defined in (
61
) can be determined explicitly through two approaches: either by solving the renewal equation (
62
) directly, or by inverting the Laplace transform (
80
). In the following, we perform both computations and demonstrate their equivalence. The resulting expressions are then used to analyze the first-passage properties.

4.1.
Renewal equation approach

With the probability distributions (
85
), renewal equation (
62
) simplifies to

(86)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,\gamma e^{-\gamma M}e^{-\rho\frac{X_{0}+M}{\alpha}}\int_{\frac{X_{0}+M}{\alpha}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\beta e^{-\beta t}\\
+s\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,\gamma e^{-\gamma M}\int_{0}^{\frac{X_{0}+M}{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}t\,\beta e^{-(\beta+\rho)t}\,Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}+M-\alpha t).

This integral equation gives a recurrence relation for the Laplace transform of the joint probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
. This recurrence relation may be solved explicitly by reducing the integral equation to an ordinary differential equation. This is done in four steps. First we compute the integrals in the first term, change the variable of integration in the second via
t\mapsto y=X_{0}+M-\alpha t
and multiply both sides by
e^{(\rho+\beta)X_{0}/\alpha}
. This results in:

(87)

Q(p,s\,|\,X_{0})\,e^{\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}=\frac{s}{1+\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha\gamma}}\\
+s\frac{\gamma\beta}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma M}\mathrm{d}M\;\int_{0}^{X_{0}+M}e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}(M-y)}Q(\rho,s\,|\,y)\mathrm{d}y.

Second step is to take the derivative with respect to
X_{0}
essentially getting rid of the constant term and removing one integration

(88)

\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{0}}\left[Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\,e^{\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}\right]=s\frac{\gamma\beta}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}M\,e^{-\gamma M}e^{\frac{\beta+\rho}{\alpha}X_{0}}Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}+M).

At the thirds step, we once again extract the exponential factor from the integrand by multiplying both sides by
e^{-\frac{\beta+\rho}{\alpha}X_{0}}
and change the variable of integration
M\mapsto Y=X_{0}+M
. This simple calculation yields

(89)

e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{0}}\left[Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\,e^{\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}\right]=s\frac{\gamma\beta}{\alpha}\int_{X_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}Y\,e^{-\gamma(Y-X_{0})}Q(\rho,s\,|\,Y).

Finally, we take yet another derivative with respect to
X_{0}
, and arrive at

(90)

e^{\gamma X_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{0}}\left[e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}-\gamma X_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{0}}\left[Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\,e^{\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}\right]\right]=-s\frac{\gamma\beta}{\alpha}Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}).

This equation, though correct, is not yet in its most convenient form. By expanding and rearranging terms, we obtain a canonical form of a standard second-order linear differential equation:

(91)

\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X_{0}^{2}}+\frac{\rho+\beta-\alpha\gamma}{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{0}}-\gamma\,\frac{\rho+\beta(1-s)}{\alpha}\right]Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=0.

Now that we have derived the differential equation (
91
), we solve it explicitly. The general solution is a sum of two exponentials:

(92)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=A_{+}e^{-\omega_{+}X_{0}}+A_{-}e^{-\omega_{-}X_{0}},

where

(93)

\omega_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\rho+\beta-\alpha\gamma\pm\sqrt{(\rho+\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{2}+4\alpha\gamma\,(\rho+\beta(1-s))}\right).

The only thing left is to fix the constants
A_{\pm}
.
Note that
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
is the Laplace transform of the probability distribution and hence it should not diverge as
X_{0}\to\infty
. Since
\omega_{-}<0
, this boundary condition implies that physical solutions are those with
A_{-}=0
. The constant
A_{+}
is then found by substituting (
92
) into (
86
). This computation gives the final result:

(94)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{s}{1+\frac{\omega_{+}}{\gamma}}e^{-\omega_{+}X_{0}}.

Note, that the above derivation heavily relies on the exact form of the probability distributions and the renewal equation reduces to such simple differential equation only in the case where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential distributions.

4.2.
Effective random walk approach

Now we present an alternative derivation of (
94
) that relies on the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula for the effective random walk (
80
).
First, we construct an effective random walk given by (
74
) and (
83
). The direct computation shows that, for the probability distributions (
85
), the effective random walk is given by:

(95)

F(k;\rho)=\frac{\beta\gamma}{\alpha}\frac{1+\frac{\rho}{\alpha\gamma}}{\left(k-\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)\left(k+\mathrm{i}\left(\gamma+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}\right)\right)},\qquad c(\rho)=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\rho}{\alpha\gamma}}.

The key ingredient in the effective random walk approach is the functions
\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)
. If both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential distributions, then these functions can be found explicitly.

First we integrate (
81
) by parts to obtain a more convenient representation for the functions
\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)
. Specifically, we have:

(96)

\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)\right],

where

(97)

\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)=\mp\log[k\mp\mathrm{i}\lambda]\frac{\partial_{k}F(k;\rho)}{\frac{1}{s\,c(\rho)}-F(k;\rho)}.

We then compute the integrals by extending them into the complex plane of
k
. Let us now describe the analytic structure of the integrands in (
96
).
There is a branch cut originating from the logarithmic term
\log[k\mp\mathrm{i}\lambda]
. Using the exact form of
F(k;\rho)
as in (
95
), we see that the numerator
F^{\prime}(k;\rho)
has two poles at
k=\mathrm{i}\beta/\alpha
and
k=-\mathrm{i}(\gamma+\rho/\alpha)
. The denominator gives rise to two more purely imaginary poles at

(98)

k_{\pm}^{*}(\rho,s)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\alpha}\left(\beta-\alpha\gamma-\rho\pm\sqrt{(\rho+\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}-4s\,\alpha\beta\gamma}\right).

Figure 4.
Analytic structure of
\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)
(left) and
\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)
(right) in (
97
) for the probability distributions (
85
). The integrals over semi-circles vanish when
R\to\infty
.

This analytic structure is schematically shown in Fig.
4
. To compute the integrals we close the contour in the upper-half plane, and, since the integral over semi-circle vanishes at infinity, we find that
\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)
is simply a sum of two residues

(99)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)=2\pi\mathrm{i}\;\mathrm{Res}_{\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)\right]+2\pi\mathrm{i}\;\mathrm{Res}_{k_{+}^{*}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)\right].

For
\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)
we should also account for the branch cut, and hence

(100)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)=2\pi\mathrm{i}\;\mathrm{Res}_{\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)\right]+2\pi\mathrm{i}\;\mathrm{Res}_{k_{+}^{*}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)\right]-\int_{\text{b.c.}}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{+}(k),

where “
b.c.
” stands for the integral over the branch cut, that is given by

(101)

-\int_{\text{b.c.}}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathrm{i}\lambda}^{\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\partial_{k}F(k;\rho)}{\frac{1}{s\,c(\rho)}-F(k;\rho)}\mathrm{d}k.

All terms entering (
99
) and (
100
) can be found explicitly. The residues are straightforward to compute

(102)

\;\mathrm{Res}_{\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)\right]=\mp\log\left[\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\mp\lambda\right)\right],\quad\;\mathrm{Res}_{k_{+}^{*}}\left[\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)\right]=\pm\log\left[k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)\mp\mathrm{i}\lambda\right].

The integrand in (
103
) is a full derivative, hence

(103)

\int_{\text{b.c.}}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)=-2\pi\mathrm{i}\log\left[1-s\,c(\rho)F(k;\rho)\right]\Big|_{\mathrm{i}\lambda}^{\mathrm{i}\infty}=2\pi\mathrm{i}\log\left[1-s\,c(\rho)F(\mathrm{i}\lambda;\rho)\right],

where we used the fact that
F(\mathrm{i}\infty;\rho)=0
. Finally we substitute (
103
) and (
101
) into (
99
) and (
100
). The obtained results are then used in (
96
) to find the closed forms of the functions
\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)
. The final expressions are:

(104)

\phi^{+}(\lambda;\rho,s)=\frac{\lambda+\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)}{\lambda-\beta/\alpha}\frac{1}{1-s\,c(\rho)F(\mathrm{i}\lambda;\rho)},\quad\phi^{-}(\lambda;\rho;s)=\frac{\lambda+\beta/\alpha}{\lambda-\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)}.

Now we have all ingredients for the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula. Since
F(k;\rho)
is a Fourier transform of the probability distribution, we must have
F(0;\rho)=1
, thus substituting (
104
) into (
80
) yields

(105)

\hat{Q}(\rho,s\,|\,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}\;\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)}{\beta/\alpha}\;\frac{\lambda+\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)}.

Recall that
\hat{Q}(\rho,s\,|\,\lambda)
is the triple Laplace transform of the joint probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
, as given by (
79
). At the same time, using the renewal equation approach, we have found the double transform
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
directly. Fortunately, (
105
) can be easily inverted with respect to
X_{0}
leading to

(106)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)\exp\left[-\left(\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)X_{0}\right].

At first glance, this result appears different from those obtained within the renewal equation approach (
94
) and it is essential to verify their equivalence. A simple computation using the exact forms of
k^{*}_{+}
and
\omega_{+}
as in (
98
) and (
93
), shows that

(107)

\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)=\omega_{+},\qquad\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,\mathrm{i}k_{+}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)=\frac{s}{1+\frac{\omega_{+}}{\gamma}}.

Thus (
106
) coincides with (
94
), confirming that the results obtained from the renewal equation approach and the effective random walk approach are indeed equivalent.

4.3.
First-passage properties

Once
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
is found by either solving the renewal equation (
94
) or inverting the Laplace transform in the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula for the effective random walk (
106
), the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
can, in principle, be obtained by inverting the double transform

(108)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau e^{-\rho\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{s}{1+\frac{\omega_{+}}{\gamma}}e^{-\omega_{+}X_{0}},

where
\omega_{+}
is given by (
93
). However, there is no closed-form expression for the inverse transform of (
108
), except when
X_{0}=0
. In this case, first inverting
s\mapsto n
, and then
\rho\mapsto\tau
, yields

(109)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}=0]=\frac{1}{\beta}e^{-(\beta+\alpha\gamma)\tau}\;\frac{n}{(n!)^{2}}(\alpha\beta\gamma)^{n}\;\tau^{2n-2}.

The exact form of the joint probability distribution for
X_{0}
can be used to verify most of the results presented below for arbitrary
X_{0}
(unless they concern the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
). However, since the computations are technical and the results can be obtained by simply setting
X_{0}=0
in the general case, we leave these computations to the reader.

The analysis for
X_{0}\neq 0
commences with computing
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
, the probability of surviving indefinitely. As argued in Sec.
2
, this is a robust quantity that can be used to distinguish the
absorption regime
from the
survival regime
. Recall that according to (
12
) this probability is given by:

(110)

S_{\infty}(X_{0})=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\Big|_{\rho=0,s=1},

which is easily computed by fixing
\rho=0
and
s=1
in (
108
) to be

(111)

S_{\infty}(X_{0})=1-\frac{2\alpha\gamma}{\beta+\alpha\gamma+\sqrt{(\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\beta-\alpha\gamma+\sqrt{(\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{2}}\right)X_{0}}.

From (
111
) it is clear that the behavior of
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
depends on whether
(\beta-\alpha\gamma)
is positive or negative. This is a clear manifestation of the transition happening at
\alpha_{c}=\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{\gamma}}
. Such transition is very natural as discussed in Sec.
2
.
The critical value of the drift coincides with that found by heuristic argument in (
10
), since for the considered probability distributions,
\left\langle M\right\rangle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\gamma}}
and
\langle t\rangle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}
. Rewriting (
110
) in terms of
\alpha_{c}
yields

(112)

S_{\infty}(X_{0})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{c}}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}-1\right)\gamma X_{0}\right],&&\alpha<\alpha_{c},\\
&0,&&\alpha\geq\alpha_{c}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.,\qquad\alpha_{c}=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}.

In other words, there are three distinct cases

(1)

\alpha<\alpha_{c}
,

survival regime
,

(2)

\alpha=\alpha_{c}
,

critical point
,

(3)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}
,

absorption regime
.

See Fig.
5
for the numerical verification of (
112
).

Figure 5.

Survival probability
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
as a function of
X_{0}
for
n=1000
(red circles) and
n=100
(blue squares) computed numerically and exact expression for
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
as in (

) (black dashed line).
The model parameters are
\alpha=1
,

,

. For the details of the simulation see Appendix
A
.

To get a more precise description, we look at the survival probabilities
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
. Using (
14
) we express the Laplace transform of the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
in terms of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as

(113)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\left.\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right)\right|_{s=1},

similarly, for
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
we have

(114)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=\left.\frac{1}{1-s}\left(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right)\right|_{\rho=0}.

Formally inverting (
113
) and (
114
) we obtain

(115)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathrm{d}\rho\;e^{\rho\tau}\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right)\Big|_{s=1},

(116)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}))\Big|_{\rho=0},

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a vertical contour in the
\rho
plane, chosen so that all singularities of the integrand lie to its left, and
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is a counterclockwise circle around the origin in the
s
plane.

The behavior of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
for
\tau\to\infty
and
n\to\infty
can now be inferred from the analytic structure of the integrands (
115
) and (
116
) in the
\rho
and
s
complex planes. Below, we analyze three cases separately.

4.3.1.
Survival regime

If
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
(
\beta>\alpha\gamma
), then the drift is weak and there is a finite probability of the process never crossing the origin
S_{\infty}(X_{0})>0
. In this regime, we expect that both
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
tend to a constant
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
with exponential corrections as in (
16
). Below we analyze the inverse transforms (
115
) and (
116
) and show that the behavior stated in (
16
) is indeed correct. Additionally we compute the “correlation lengths” presented in (
27
) and the conditional means and variances (
28
), (
29
), (
30
), and (
31
).

We start with
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
. The explicit form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
given by (
108
) implies that the integrand in the inverse transform (
115
) has a pole at
\rho=0
and a branch cut

(117)

\rho\in[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]:\qquad\rho_{1}=-(\sqrt{\beta}+\sqrt{\alpha\gamma})^{2},\quad\rho_{2}=-(\sqrt{\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha\gamma})^{2}.

Deforming the contour
\mathcal{C}_{1}\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{2}
as shown in Fig.
6
, and accounting for the contribution of the pole yields

(118)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\;\mathrm{Res}_{\rho=0}\left[\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right)\right]+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\mathrm{d}\rho\;e^{\rho\tau}\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right),

where
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is the contour encircling the branch cut.

Figure 6.
Analytic structure of the Laplace transform of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
(left) and of the generating function of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
(right) in the survival regime for the distributions (
85
).

The residue is straightforward to compute:

(119)

\;\mathrm{Res}_{\rho=0}\left[\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right)\right]=1-\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\beta}e^{-\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\gamma\right)X_{0}}.

Comparing this expression with (
111
) we see, that this is nothing but the survival probability
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
.
To obtain the asymptotic behavior of the branch cut integral in (
118
), we rewrite it as an integral along the real axis

(120)

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\mathrm{d}\rho\;e^{\rho\tau}\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right)\\
=e^{\rho_{2}\tau}\;\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}^{0}\mathrm{d}\rho\;\Delta\Big[\frac{e^{\rho\tau}}{\rho+\rho_{2}}(1-Q(\rho+\rho_{2},1\,|\,X_{0}))\Big],

where
\Delta
denotes the discontinuity across the cut

(121)

\Delta[f(\rho)]\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}[f(\rho-\mathrm{i}\epsilon)-f(\rho+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)].

Since the discontinuity is purely imaginary and the integral in the right hand side is bounded, the second term in (
118
) decays exponentially fast as
e^{\rho_{2}\tau}
at large times. Substituting (
119
) into (
118
) and replacing the integral by its asymptotic behavior
e^{\rho_{2}\tau}
with
\rho_{2}
given by (
117
), we obtain the exponential decay of the survival probability

(122)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-\left(\sqrt{\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}\right)^{2}\tau\right].

This is the behavior presented in (
16
) with the decay rate
\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)=-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho_{2}}})
stated in (
27
). In other words, the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
approaches its asymptotic value exponentially fast. The asymptotic value
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
is given by the residue of the integrand in (
115
) at
\rho=0
and the decay rate corresponds to the rightmost branching point.

Similarly, for
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
, the integrand in the inverse transform (
116
) has poles at
s=0
and
s=1
and a branch cut

(123)

s\in[s_{1},\infty),\qquad s_{1}=\frac{\left(\beta+\alpha\gamma\right)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta\gamma}.

Deforming the contour of integration
\mathcal{C}_{1}\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{2}
and accounting for the pole at
s=1
we again represent the survival probability as a sum of residue and the integral over the branch cut

(124)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=-\;\mathrm{Res}_{s=1}\left[\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(0,s\,|\,X_{0}))\right]\\
+\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(0,s\,|\,X_{0})).

A straightforward computation shows that the residue at
s=1
corresponds to the survival probability
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
as

(125)

-\;\mathrm{Res}_{s=1}\left[\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(0,s\,|\,X_{0}))\right]=1-\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\beta}e^{-\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\gamma\right)X_{0}}=S_{\infty}(X_{0}).

The integral in (
124
) can be represented as an integral of the discontinuity (
121
) over the real line essentially in the same way as in (
120
). Factoring out the branching point by changing the value of integration
s\mapsto ss_{1}
we find that

(126)

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(0,s\,|\,X_{0}))\\
=-s_{1}^{-n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Delta\left[\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s\,s_{1}}(1-Q(0,s\,s_{1}\,|\,X_{0}))\right].

The integral in the right hand side is bounded and hence for large
n
the left hand side decays as
s_{1}^{-n}
(recall that
s_{1}>1
). Substituting (
125
) into (
124
) and replacing the integral in (
124
) term by its asymptotic behavior
s_{1}^{-n}
we find

(127)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}s_{1}^{-n}.

Using the explicit form of
s_{1}
as in (
123
) we finally arrive at

(128)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\log\frac{(\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta\gamma}\right].

This is the behavior (
16
) with the decay rate
\xi_{n}(\alpha)
given by (
27
). Note, that the probability to survive for the infinite time
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
corresponds to the residue at
s=1
and the decay rate is now governed by the branching point
s_{1}
.

It should be mentioned, that the result (
128
) can be obtained if we note that for large
n
the sum in (
114
) can be replaced by an integral essentially changing (
114
) for the Laplace transform of continuous variable. In what follows we will sometimes use this observation instead of analyzing the inversion (
116
) in the complex
s
plane.

From (
112
) it is evident that the probability to survive indefinitely decays exponentially with
X_{0}
. However, when
X_{0}
is close to
0
, a significant fraction of the trajectories do cross the origin. The mean values of
\tau
and
n
for such trajectories are given by

(129)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left.\left[\frac{Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})}{1-S_{\infty}(X_{0})}\right]\right|_{s=1,\rho=0},

(130)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left.\left[\frac{Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})}{1-S_{\infty}(X_{0})}\right]\right|_{s=1,\rho=0}.

Similarly, for the second moments we have

(131)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau^{2}\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}\left.\left[\frac{Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})}{1-S_{\infty}(X_{0})}\right]\right|_{s=1,\rho=0},

(132)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n^{2}\,|\,X_{0},\tau<\infty]=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)\left.\left[\frac{Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})}{1-S_{\infty}(X_{0})}\right]\right|_{s=1,\rho=0}.

Straightforward computation using (
108
) gives the results presented in Sec.
2
, namely, (
28
), (
29
), (
30
), and (
31
).

4.3.2.
Critical point

If
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
(
\beta=\alpha\gamma
), then
S_{\infty}(X_{0})=0
and the decay rates in (
122
) and (
128
) are zeros.
At the level of the analytic structure of the integrands in the inverse transform (
115
) and (
116
) the difference with respect to the survival regime is that the endpoints of the branch cuts coincide with the poles (see Fig.
7
). Thus after the transformation of the contours we cannot separate the contributions from the poles and the contributions from the cuts as was done (
118
) and (
124
). Consequently to extract the large
\tau
(large
n
) behaviors we need to study the expansions of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as
\rho\to 0
(
s\to 1
).

Analytic structure of the Laplace transform of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
(left) and of the generating function of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
(right) at the critical point for the distributions (
85
).

We start with the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
.
First we set
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
in the exact expression for
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as given by (
108
) and then use the representation (
113
) for the Laplace transform of the survival probability arriving at:

(133)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{\rho}\left[1-\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{\gamma X_{0}}{2\beta}\left(\rho+\sqrt{\rho(4\beta+\rho)}\right)\right]}{1+\frac{\rho}{2\beta}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\sqrt{\rho(4\beta+\rho)}}\right].

Large
\tau
behavior of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
is governed by
\rho\to 0
expansion of its Laplace transform. Expanding the right hand side of (
133
) in series we obtain

(134)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\rho\to 0}{\sim}\left(1+\gamma X_{0}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}.

The square root divergence suggests, that
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
decays as
\tau^{-1/2}
. Substituting this ansatz in (
134
) yields

(135)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\pi\beta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}.

The procedure for
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
is similar. By setting
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
in
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
and using the representation (
114
) for the generating function of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
, we find that at the critical point:

(136)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{1-s}\left(1-\left(1-\sqrt{1-s}\right)e^{-\sqrt{1-s}\,\gamma X_{0}}\right).

If
n
is large, the sum may be replaced by the integral. Then repeating the same arguments as for
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
with
\rho
replaced by
-\log s
we find the behavior of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
at large
n
:

(137)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

With (
135
) and (
137
) we prove the power-law decay of the survival probabilities stated in (
18
) and find the constants
c_{\tau}
and
c_{n}
given by (
39
). However, two remarks are in order.

Sparre-Anderson theorem

The first remark is that if
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
(
\beta=\alpha\gamma
), then for
\rho=0
the effective random walk (
95
) is symmetric. It means, that the survival probability for
X_{0}=0
can be computed by Sparre-Anderson theorem. This is a very nice universal result in the theory of the discrete-time random walks that was obtained in
[
58
]
. The theorem states that for a symmetric random walk starting at the origin, the probability to survive up to the
n
th step is given by a simple closed expression

(138)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0}=0)=\binom{2n}{n}2^{-2n}.

The universality of the result (
138
) is that it holds for any distribution of jumps provided that it is symmetric and its cumulative distribution is continuous. For the effective random walk (
95
) both conditions are satisfied, and the Sparre-Anderson theorem holds. Taking the the limit
n\to\infty
in (
138
) we find that

(139)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0}=0)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}},

which coincides with (
137
).

Scaling limit.

The second remark is that at the critical point the effective random walk consists of identically distributed jumps with finite moments and zero mean. Therefore it is natural to expect, that such a process converges to Brownian motion in the scaling limit. The expected scaling is
X_{0}\sim\sqrt{\tau}
(or
X_{0}\sim\sqrt{n}
).
In other words, if both
X_{0}\to\infty
and
\tau\to\infty
with fixed ratio
z=X_{0}/\sqrt{\tau}
, we should have

(140)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim V_{\tau}\left(z_{\text{c}}=\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\right),\quad X_{0}\to\infty,\quad\tau\to\infty.

The scaling function
V_{\tau}(z)
can be found in a same way as was done in
[
36
]
, i.e., by analyzing the Laplace transform

(141)

\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,\lambda)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}X_{0}\,e^{-\lambda X_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0}).

The behavior of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
for large
X_{0}
and
\tau
can be extracted from
\lambda\to 0
and
\rho\to 0
behavior of
\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,\lambda)
. However the scaling
X_{0}\sim\sqrt{\tau}
should be accounted for when constructing the expansion
\rho\to 0
and
\lambda\to 0
.
Substituting the scaling form (
140
) into (
141
) and changing the variables of integration via
z=X_{0}/\sqrt{\tau}
and
y=\rho\tau
, we obtain

(142)

\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,\lambda)\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\to 0\\
\rho\to 0\end{subarray}}{\sim}\frac{1}{\rho^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,e^{-y}\sqrt{y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\rho}}z\sqrt{y}}V_{\tau}(z).

From (
142
) it is clear, that the correct scaling ratio between
\lambda
and
\rho
is
\lambda\sim\sqrt{\rho}
.
At the same time, we can find the explicit form of
\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,\lambda)
by performing the Laplace transform of
S_{T}(\rho\,|\,X_{0})
in (
133
) with respect to
X_{0}
. Then by setting
\lambda=u\sqrt{\rho}
and expanding the resulting expression in series as
\rho\to 0
we obtain

(143)

\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,\lambda=u\sqrt{\rho})\underset{\rho\to 0}{\sim}\frac{1}{\rho^{3/2}}\frac{1}{u\left(1+u\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{\gamma}\right)}.

Comparing (
142
) and the expansion (
143
) obtained from the explicit expression of
\hat{S}_{T}(\rho\,|\,X_{0})
yields an integral equation for the scaling function
V_{\tau}(z)

(144)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,e^{-y}\sqrt{y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{-uz\sqrt{y}}\,V_{\tau}(z)=\frac{1}{u\left(1+u\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{\gamma}\right)}.

This equation was studied in details in
[
36
]
and here we will not repeat the analysis. The solution of (
144
) is
V_{\tau}(z)=\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{z}{2}\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\beta}}\right)
.
Hence for the survival probability we have

(145)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{\beta\tau}}\right),\quad\tau\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

The scaling behavior of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
is obtained by replacing the sum by an integral in (
136
) and repeating the same arguments with
\rho
replaced by
-\log s
. This procedure results in

(146)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

We conclude by numerically verifying the analytical results (
135
), (
137
) and (
145
), (
146
). As is clear from Fig.
8
, the numerical simulations are in perfect agreement with the analytical predictions.

4.3.3.
Absorption regime

In this regime
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
(
\beta<\alpha\gamma
) and
S_{\infty}(X_{0})=0
, i.e., the drift is strong enough and the process eventually crosses the origin.
In terms of the analytic structure of the integrands in the inverse transforms (
115
) and (
116
) of the survival probabilities, the difference is that there are no poles at
\rho=0
and
s=1
, which can be verified by using the exact form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as in (
108
). Consequently, we can deform the contours of integration as in (
118
) and (
124
), but this time there will be no terms with residues. As a result, the survival probabilities at large times vanish exponentially fast with the decay rates governed by the branching points
\rho_{2}
in (
117
) and
s_{1}
in (
123
). In summary, the behavior of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
is given by

(147)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-(\sqrt{\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha\gamma})^{2}\tau\right],

(148)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\log\frac{(\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta\gamma}\right].

This is exactly the result stated in (
15
) with the “correlation lengths” (
27
). Note, that the decay rates (
122
) and (
128
) in the
survival regime
and the decay rates in the
absorption regime
have the same functional form.

In the rest of this section we provide more detailed description of the first-passage properties in the absorption regime.

Moments of
\tau
and
n
.

First of all, since survival probabilities decay exponentially, we expect all moments of
n
and
\tau
to be finite. They can be found by expanding
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
in series with respect to
\rho
and
s
. Specifically, the first two moments of
\tau
are given by

(149)

\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=-\left.\partial_{\rho}Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right|_{\rho=0},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\tau^{2}\,|\,X_{0}]=\left.\partial^{2}_{\rho}Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})\right|_{\rho=0}.

The first two moments of
n
are obtained in a similar way as

(150)

\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\left.\partial_{s}Q(0,s\,|\,E_{0})\right|_{\rho=0},\qquad\mathbb{E}[n^{2}\,|\,X_{0}]=\left[\partial_{s}^{2}+\partial_{s}\right]\left.Q(\rho,s\,|\,E_{0})\right|_{s=1}.

In the scenario where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential we have the explicit form (
108
) of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
. After straightforward computation we find the mean and the variances of the first-passage time
\tau

(151)

\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{1+\gamma X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\qquad\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{\beta+\alpha\gamma+2\beta\gamma X_{0}}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}},

as well as the mean and the variance of the number of jumps
n
that have occurred prior to the first-passage

(152)

\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\gamma\,\frac{\alpha+\beta X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\qquad\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\beta\gamma\,\frac{\alpha(\alpha\gamma+\beta)+(\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})X_{0}}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}.

In particular, the relations (
151
) and (
152
) imply that for large
X_{0}
the mean values of
\tau
and
n
are given by:

(153)

\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{\gamma\,X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\qquad\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{\gamma\beta\,X_{0}}{\alpha\gamma-\beta}.

This is exactly the behavior (
9
) found by the heuristic argument in Sec.
2
.

Correlation coefficient.

As argued in Sec.
2
,
\tau
and
n
are correlated. One way to quantify this is to compute the correlation coefficient

(154)

\mathrm{corr}(\tau,n)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(n\tau)\,|\,X_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\;\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\,\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]}}.

The correlation coefficient can be easily computed from the exact form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
by using the identity

(155)

\mathbb{E}[(n\tau)\,|\,X_{0}]=-\partial_{s}\partial_{\rho}Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\Big|_{\rho=0,s=1}.

After a simple calculation we obtain:

(156)

\mathrm{corr}(\tau,n)=\frac{\sqrt{\beta\gamma}\left(2\alpha+X_{0}(\beta+\alpha\gamma)\right)}{\sqrt{(\beta+\alpha\gamma+2\beta\gamma\,X_{0})(\alpha(\alpha\gamma+\beta)+(\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+\beta^{2})X_{0})}}.

The moments and the correlation coefficient characterize
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
close to the typical values of
\tau
and
n
. However, due to the explicit form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
we can also obtain the marginal probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
in the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
. This is done by applying the large deviation theory (see
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
for a pedagogical review).

Rate function for
\tau
.

Both the mean and the variance of
\tau
grow linearly with
X_{0}
as we have shown in (
153
). This suggests, that the marginal probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
follows the large deviation form

(157)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Phi(z)},\qquad z=\frac{\alpha\tau}{X_{0}}-1.

The scaling variable
z
captures the deviation of
\tau
from its typical values. Linear growth of the mean and variance of
\tau
with
X_{0}
suggests that scale of such fluctuations is proportional to
X_{0}
.
Recall that even in the extreme case, where only a single jump with zero amplitude occurs at
t=0
, the process still requires a time of
X_{0}/\alpha
to reach the origin. This implies that the scaling variable
z
lies in the range
z\in[0;\infty)
.
Note that in order for the large deviation form to recover the Gaussian distribution near the typical value, the scaling variable only needs to be proportional to
X_{0}
; for example, one could use
z^{\prime}=\tau/X_{0}
. The particular choice of
z
in (
157
) is primarily motivated by convenience, as it simplifies the expressions that follow.

The approximation (
157
) essentially means that the probability of observing a rescaled fluctuation
z
decays exponentially with
X_{0}
, and is governed by the non-negative function
\Phi(z)
usually referred to as the
rate function
.

The easiest way to compute the rate function is to substitute the large deviation ansatz into the Laplace transform of the marginal probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]

(158)

\left.Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right|_{s=1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\,\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-X_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\rho(z+1)+\Phi(z)\right)}\mathrm{d}z.

In the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
, this integral is dominated by the minimum of the exponent due to the saddle-point approximation

(159)

\left.Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right|_{s=1}\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-X_{0}\min_{z}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\rho(z+1)+\Phi(z)\right)\right].

At the same time, the explicit form of
Q(\rho,s,|,X_{0})
, derived earlier in (
108
), has the asymptotic behavior

(160)

\left.Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right|_{s=1}\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\,\phi(\rho)},

where

(161)

\phi(\rho)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\rho+\beta-\alpha\gamma+\sqrt{(\rho+\beta-\alpha\gamma)^{2}+4\alpha\gamma\rho}\right).

Equating the exponents in (
159
) and (
160
) implies the relation between
\Phi(z)
and
\phi(\rho)

(162)

\min_{z}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\rho(z+1)+\Phi(z)\right)=\phi(\rho).

This identifies
\Phi(z)
as the Legendre-Fenchel transform of
\phi(\rho)
:

(163)

\Phi(z)=\max_{\rho}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\rho(z+1)+\phi(\rho)\right).

Equation (
163
) together with (
161
) provide the rate function in the parametric form. However, the maximization can be carried out explicitly. Indeed, the maximum of (
163
) is reached at
\rho=\rho^{*}
satisfying
\phi^{\prime}(\rho^{*})=(z+1)/\alpha
. Solving the maximization condition results in

(164)

\rho^{*}=-\beta-\alpha\gamma+(1+2z)\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\beta\gamma}{z(z+1)}}.

Finally, substituting (
164
) into (
163
) we find the rate function
\Phi(z)
:

(165)

\Phi(z)=\gamma\left(\sqrt{z}-\sqrt{(z+1)\frac{\beta}{\alpha\gamma}}\right)^{2}.

This function is convex and non-negative, with a minimum at
z=\frac{\beta}{\alpha\gamma-\beta}
. The behavior of
\Phi(z)
around the minimum characterizes the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
near typical values of
\tau
. The behavior of the distribution for the atypical values of
\tau
is governed by the expansions of
\Phi(z)
for
z\to 0
and
z\to\infty
.
These expansions are given by:

(166)

\Phi(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\beta}{\gamma}-2\sqrt{\frac{\beta\gamma}{\alpha}}\sqrt{z},&&z\to 0,\\
&\frac{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}{4\alpha^{2}\beta\gamma}(z-z^{*})^{2},&&z\to z^{*}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\\
&\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}-\sqrt{\beta}\right)^{2}\,z,&&z\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

From (
166
), we can deduce the behavior of the probability distribution. Close to the typical values, it is a Gaussian

(167)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\tau-\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau]\right)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[\tau]}\right],\qquad\tau\approx\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau],

where

(168)

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau]=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha\gamma-\beta}\,X_{0},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[\tau]=\frac{2\beta\gamma}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}\,X_{0}.

The asymptotic mean and variance (
168
) are consistent with the exact results (
151
) obtained for finite
X_{0}
.

For the left tail of
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
, i.e., atypically small values of
\tau
, we have

(169)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}+2\sqrt{\beta\gamma X_{0}}\,\sqrt{\Delta\tau}\right],\qquad\tau=\Delta\tau+\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha}\ll\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau].

Recall, that the first-passage time cannot be smaller than
X_{0}/\alpha
, and hence it is more natural to use
\Delta\tau
rather than
\tau
as a parameter in (
169
).

For the right tail (atypically large values of
\tau
) we obtain

(170)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\left(\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}-\sqrt{\beta}\right)^{2}\tau\right],\qquad\tau\gg\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau].

Note, that the right tail of the distribution has the same exponential decay as the survival probability (
147
). This is not a coincidence as according to (
11
) the survival probability is given by:

(171)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\tau}\,\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tilde{\tau}\,|\,X_{0}].

For sufficiently large
\tau
, the distribution may be replaced by the asymptotic behavior (
170
) and computing the integral results in (
147
). Note that the decay rate of
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and the asymptotic behavior of
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
were obtained by different arguments, therefore the fact that they coincide may be used as a simple self-consistency check.

The numerical verification of the large deviation approximation is shown in Fig.
9
. Note that the large deviation approximation
157
describes the tails of the distribution for atypical values of
\tau
, which have very small probabilities. To sample these regions, we use Importance Sampling, a strategy for generating rare events (see
[
59
]
for an introduction), allowing us to explore probabilities as small as
10^{-150}
. Details of the simulations are given in Appendix
A
.

Rate function for
n

The mean and the variance of
n
scale linearly with
X_{0}
(
152
). Therefore it is natural to expect that the marginal probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
follows the large deviation form

(172)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Psi(\nu)},\qquad\nu=\frac{\alpha n}{\beta X_{0}}.

The scaling variable
\nu
lies in the range
\nu\in[0;\infty)
. The rate function
\Psi(\nu)
is computed in the same manner as
\Phi(z)
. Specifically, we first introduce another variable
\tilde{s}=-\log s
, substitute the large deviation ansatz into (
108
) and compute the sum in the saddle-point approximation arriving at:

(173)

Q(\rho,e^{\tilde{s}}|\,X_{0})\Big|_{\rho=0}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\tilde{s}n}\,\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\min\limits_{\nu}\left(\tilde{s}\,\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\nu+\Psi(\nu)\right)}.

Then we extract the asymptotic behavior of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
from the exact representation (
108
)

(174)

Q(\rho,e^{\tilde{s}}|\,X_{0})\Big|_{\rho=0}\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\psi(\tilde{s})},

where

(175)

\psi(\tilde{s})=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\beta-\alpha\gamma+\sqrt{(\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}-4\alpha\gamma\beta\,e^{-\tilde{s}}}\right).

Comparing the exponents in (
173
) and (
174
) we see that
\Psi(\nu)
and
\psi(\tilde{s})
are related by the Legendre-Fenchel transform. Inverting this transform yields the parametric representation for the rate function
\Psi(\nu)
, namely

(176)

\Psi(\nu)=\max_{\tilde{s}}\left(-\tilde{s}\,\nu\,\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\psi(\tilde{s})\right).

The maximization in (
176
) can be again carried out explicitly.
This is done by first finding the solution
\tilde{s}^{*}
of
\psi^{\prime}(\tilde{s})=\nu\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
and then substituting it into (
176
).
Performing this algebraic manipulations we find the rate function
\Psi(\nu)
explicitly

(177)

\Psi(\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\gamma\right)+\gamma\,\Omega(\nu)+\frac{\beta\,\nu}{\alpha}\log\left[2\nu\,\Omega(\nu)\right],

where

(178)

\Omega(\nu)=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\beta\,\nu}{\alpha\gamma}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha\gamma}+1\right)^{2}}-\frac{\beta\,\nu}{\alpha\gamma}.

This is a convex non-negative function with minimum at
\nu^{*}=\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\alpha\gamma-\beta}
and the asymptotic behavior

(179)

\Psi(\nu)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\nu\log\nu,&&\nu\to 0,\\
&\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}\gamma}\frac{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}{\beta^{2}+\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}}(\nu-\nu^{*})^{2},&&\nu\to\nu^{*}=\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\alpha\gamma-\beta},\\
&\nu\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\log\frac{(\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta\gamma},&&\nu\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly to the distribution of
\tau
we now use (
179
) to extract the asymptotic behavior of the marginal distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
. Close to the typical values of
n
this distribution is a Gaussian

(180)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(n-\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n]\right)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[n]}\right],\qquad n\approx\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n],

where

(181)

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n]=\frac{\beta\gamma}{\alpha\gamma-\beta}\,X_{0},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[n]=\frac{\beta^{2}+\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}}{(\alpha\gamma-\beta)^{3}}\beta\gamma\,X_{0}.

The asymptotic mean and variance are consistent with the exact results (
152
).

The left tail of the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
(atypically small values of
n
) is given by:

(182)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,X_{0}-n\log n\right],\qquad n\ll\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n]

Note, that the behavior is different from those of
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
as in (
169
), but the constant term is the same. This term essentially gives the probability that there is only one jump at
t=0
and this jump has zero amplitude. For the exponential distribution
p(t)
this probability is nothing but

(183)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau=0\,|\,X_{0}]\approx\mathbb{P}_{N}[n=0\,|\,X_{0}]\approx\int_{\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha}}^{\infty}p(t)\mathrm{d}t=e^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}.

For the right tail of the distribution (atypically large values of
n
) we have

(184)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-n\log\frac{(\beta+\alpha\gamma)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta\gamma}\right],\qquad n\gg\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n].

We complete the description of the marginal probability
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
by noting that as expected the decay in the right tail (
184
) is the same as the decay of the survival probability
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
as given in (
148
). The results of the numerical simulations are shown in Fig.
9
.

5.
Exactly solvable case II. Exponential time distribution, fixed jumps

This section is devoted to another exactly solvable case, where the time intervals are exponentially distributed while all jumps are the same,

(185)

p(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t},\qquad q(M)=\delta(M-M_{0}).

For the choice (
185
) both the renewal equation and effective random walk approaches can be carried out explicitly. Note that since the jumps are fixed,
n
uniquely determines
\tau
by the first-passage condition (
4
),

(186)

X_{0}-\alpha\tau-nM_{0}=0.

Hence the joint probability distribution must be proportional to the
\delta
-function,

(187)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]\sim\delta\left(\tau-\frac{X_{0}+nM_{0}}{\alpha}\right).

This means that we can focus on either one of the marginal probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
, and the other can then be easily recovered. However, we will keep track of both
n
and
\tau
for pedagogical purposes.

5.1.
Renewal equation approach

We now apply the renewal equation approach to the case where the jump amplitudes are fixed. This follows a procedure similar to that in Sec.
4.1
, where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
were exponential distributions.

First, we substitute the probability distributions (
185
) into the renewal equation (
62
).
Due to the fixed jump amplitudes, the integral equation simplifies, and only one integral remains instead of the double one seen in (
86
).
Specifically, we have:

(188)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\,e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}(X_{0}+M_{0})}+\frac{\beta s}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{X_{0}+M_{0}}e^{-\frac{\beta+\rho}{\alpha}(X_{0}+M_{0}-y)}Q(\rho,s\,|\,y)\mathrm{d}y.

To transform this integral equation into a differential form, we take the derivative with respect to
X_{0}
, arriving at:

(189)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}X_{0}}\left[Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\,e^{\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}(X_{0}+M_{0})}\right]=\frac{\beta s}{\alpha}\,e^{\frac{\beta+\rho}{\alpha}(X_{0}+M_{0})}\,Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}+M_{0})

or, equivalently,

(190)

\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}X_{0}}+\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}\right]Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{\beta s}{\alpha}Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}+M_{0}).

In contrast to the previous case with exponential
p(t)
and
q(M)
, which led to a second-order differential equation, here we obtain a first-order delayed differential equation (see e.g.,
[
60
]
). The general solution to (
190
) has the form

(191)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=A\,e^{-\omega X_{0}}.

Substituting this ansatz into (
190
), we find that
\omega
is determined by solving

(192)

-\omega+\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}=\frac{\beta s}{\alpha}e^{-\omega M_{0}}.

The constant
A
in (
191
) is fixed by noting that
Q(\rho,s\,|\,-M_{0})=s
, which is evident from (
188
). Therefore, the explicit expression for the generating function reads:

(193)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\,e^{-\omega(X_{0}+M_{0})}.

Note that, once again, we are able to easily reduce the integral equation (
188
) to a differential equation (
190
) because the inter-jump intervals are exponentially distributed.

To analyze the solutions of the transcendental equation (
192
), we rewrite it as:

(194)

M_{0}=-\frac{1}{\omega}\log\left[\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\rho}{\beta}+1-\omega\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right].

By plotting
M_{0}
as a function of
\omega
(see Fig.
10
), we identify two distinct scenarios:

•

If
s<1
or
\rho>0
, there exists a unique positive solution of (
192
). This solution ensures that the generating function decays exponentially as
X_{0}\to\infty
.

•

If
\rho=0
and
s=1
, the behavior changes.
For
M_{0}\geq\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
, there is a unique positive solution, and the generating function decays exponentially as
X_{0}\to\infty
.
For
M_{0}<\frac{\alpha}{\beta}
, however, the solution for
\omega
obtained from (
194
) is negative. This would lead to an non-physical exponential growth of the generating function as
X_{0}\to\infty
. In this case, we select the trivial solution
\omega=0
of (
192
) instead.

The combined solution for
\omega
in these cases is illustrated in Fig.
10
.

Figure 10.
Plots of
M_{0}(\omega)
for
\rho>0
,
s<1
(left) and for
\rho=0
,
s=1
(right). Physical solution with
\omega\geq 0
is shown by the solid blue line. The non-physical solution for
\rho=0
,
s=1
is shown by the dashed red line.

In fact, (
192
) can be transformed into a standard transcendental equation. Rewriting it as

(195)

\left(\omega-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}\right)M_{0}\;e^{\left(\omega-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}\right)M_{0}}=-\frac{s\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}},

we obtain an equation of the form
xe^{x}=z
, where
z\in[-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{e}});0]
.
This is a well-known transcendental equation whose solutions are expressed in terms of the Lambert
W
-function
[
61
]
.
In particular, it has two real solutions:
x=W_{0}(z)
and
x=W_{-1}(z)
.
In our case, the physically relevant solution corresponds to the principal branch
W_{0}
. Specifically, we have:

(196)

\omega=\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}W_{0}\left[-\frac{s\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}}\right].

The other non-physical solution, represented by the dashed red line in Fig.
10
, that appears for
\rho=0
and
s=1
corresponds to the
W_{-1}
branch.

5.2.
Effective random walk approach

Having determined the Laplace transform of the probability distribution using the renewal equation approach, we now derive the same result by leveraging the effective-time random walk framework introduced in Sec.
3.3
. For the probability distributions defined in (
185
), the effective random walk is characterized, according to (
75
) and (
82
), by:

(197)

F(k;\rho)=-e^{\mathrm{i}kM_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}{k-\mathrm{i}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}},\qquad c(\rho)=e^{-\rho\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}}.

Our next goal is to find the functions
\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)
, which are essential for computing the Laplace transform of the first-passage time distribution (
80
). This procedure is similar to those presented in Sec.
4.2
. Specifically, we first integrate (
81
) by parts

(198)

\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\;\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)\right],\quad\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)=\mp\log[k\mp\mathrm{i}\lambda]\frac{\partial_{k}F(k;\rho)}{\frac{1}{s\,c(\rho)}-F(k;\rho)}.

The integrals are then computed by analyzing their analytic structure in the complex
k
-plane.
Specifically, the integrands
\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)
have a branch cut originating from the logarithmic term
\log[k\mp\mathrm{i}\lambda]
, and a pole at
k=\mathrm{i}(\beta/\alpha)
arising from the numerator
\partial_{k}F(k;\rho)
. Furthermore, the denominator gives rise to infinitely many poles, which are solutions to the transcendental equation:

(199)

\frac{1}{s\,c(\rho)}=F(k;\rho):\qquad k_{\ell}^{*}(\rho,s)=\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}W_{\ell}\left[-s\frac{\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}}\right]\right),

where
W_{\ell}
denotes the
\ell
th branch of the Lambert
W
-function. The analytic structure of
\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)
is shown in Fig.
11
.

A key observation is that all poles except for
k_{0}^{*}
, corresponding to the principal branch of the Lambert
W
-function, lie in the lower half-plane.
Therefore only the pole at
k=k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)
contributes when closing the contour in the upper half-plane.

Figure 11.
Analytic structure of
\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)
(left) and
\mathcal{I}^{-}(k)
(right) in (
198
) for the probability distributions (
185
). The integrals over semi-circles vanish when
R\to\infty
.

Following the same procedure as in Sec.
4.2
, which involves computing the residues at
k=\mathrm{i}(\beta/\alpha)
and
k=k_{0}^{*}
, and carefully accounting for the contribution from the branch cut integral in
\mathcal{I}^{+}(k)
, we arrive at the explicit forms of the functions
\phi^{\pm}(\lambda;\rho,s)
:

(200)

\phi^{+}(\lambda;\rho,s)=\frac{\lambda+\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)}{\lambda-\beta/\alpha}\frac{1}{1-s\,c(\rho)\,F(\mathrm{i}\lambda;\rho)},\quad\phi^{-}(\lambda;s,\rho)=\frac{\lambda+\beta/\alpha}{\lambda-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)}.

Substituting these expressions into the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula for the effective random walk (
80
), we obtain the triple Laplace transform (
79
) of
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
:

(201)

\hat{Q}(\rho,s\,|\,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)}{\beta/\alpha}\frac{\lambda+\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}}{\lambda+\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)}.

Note, that this transform has the same functional form as the one obtained in the first exactly solvable case (
105
), a consequence of the integrands
\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(k)
in (
97
) and (
198
) corresponding to the Fourier transforms of the effective random walks (
95
) and (
197
) having the same analytic structure in the upper part of the complex
k
-plane.

Inverting the transform
\lambda\mapsto X_{0}
in (
201
), we arrive at:

(202)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=\left(1+\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)e^{-\left(\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)X_{0}}.

To verify that we have obtained the same result as in the renewal equation approach (
193
), note that the Lambert function satisfies
W(x)e^{W(x)}=x
, hence

(203)

\left(1+\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)e^{\left(\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)M_{0}}=s.

Applying this identity to (
202
) we obtain

(204)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\,e^{-\left(\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}(\rho,s)\right)(X_{0}+M_{0})}.

Finally, by comparing (
196
) and (
199
), it becomes clear that
\omega=\frac{\rho}{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{*}
, and that (
204
) is indeed equivalent to (
193
). This equivalence confirms that the renewal equation approach and the effective random walk method yield identical results.

5.3.
First-passage properties

We have shown that both the renewal equation approach and the effective random walk approach yield identical expressions for the Laplace transform of the joint probability distribution

(205)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]=Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}).

Specifically, from either (
193
) or (
204
), we obtain

(206)

Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})=s\exp\left[-\left(\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}W_{0}\left[-s\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}}\right]\right)(X_{0}+M_{0})\right].

Below, we analyze (
206
) and extract the first-passage properties, obtaining the results stated in Sec.
2.2
.

Analogously to the case where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential distributions, the transform (
205
) can be inverted explicitly if the process starts at the origin. The inversion relies on the expansion of the Lambert
W
-function for small arguments
[
61
]
:

(207)

W_{0}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-n)^{n-1}}{n!}z^{n},\qquad z\to 0.

First, we use (
207
) to expand (
206
) in series with respect to
s
, which allows us to invert the transform
s\mapsto n
. The subsequent inversion
\rho\mapsto\tau
is then straightforward. The resulting joint distribution takes the form:

(208)

\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}=0]=\frac{1}{n!}e^{-\beta\tau}\left(n\,\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{n-1}\delta\left(\tau-n\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}\right).

The
\delta
-function highlights the deterministic relation (
186
) between the first-passage time
\tau
and the number of jumps
n
before the first-passage.

Figure 12.

\Lambda(\alpha)
from (

), which determines the survival probability
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
as in (
209
). For
\alpha\geq\alpha_{c}
, we have
\Lambda(\alpha)=0
and thus
S_{\infty}(X_{0})=0
, indicating that the process eventually crosses the origin. For
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
,
\Lambda(\alpha)>0
, resulting in a finite probability to survive indefinitely.

The first quantity we compute is the probability to survive at the infinite time (
12
). It is readily computed from (
206
) by setting
\rho=0
and
s=1
:

(209)

S_{\infty}(X_{0})=1-\exp\left[-\Lambda(\alpha)\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{M_{0}}\right],

where

(210)

\Lambda(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}+W_{0}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{c}}{\alpha}}\right],\qquad\alpha_{c}=\beta M_{0}.

The behavior of the survival probability is governed by the function
\Lambda(\alpha)
, plotted in Fig.
12
. This function reveals the existence of three distinct regimes:

(1)

\alpha<\alpha_{c}
,

survival regime
,

(2)

\alpha=\alpha_{c}
,

critical point
,

(3)

\alpha>\alpha_{c}
,

absorption regime
.

The numerical verification of (
209
) is shown in Fig.
13
.

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})

for

(red circles) and

(blue squares) computed numerically and exact expansion for

as in (

) (black dashed line).
The model parameters are

,

,

. For the details of the simulations see Appendix

.

To gain a more refined description of the first-passage properties, we analyze the survival probabilities for the finite times and number of jumps. These are given by the inverse Laplace transforms (
115
) and (
116
):

(211)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathrm{d}\rho\;e^{\rho\tau}\left.\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})\right)\right|_{s=1},

(212)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{1}{s^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-s}(1-Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0}))\Big|_{\rho=0}.

We analyze these survival probabilities separately for each regime. The computations closely follow those in Sec.
4.3
, so we briefly outline the main steps and present the results.

5.3.1.
Survival regime

When the drift is weak (
\alpha<\alpha_{c}
), the process has a finite probability of remaining above the origin indefinitely. In this regime, we analyze the survival probabilities
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
, characterizing how they approach the asymptotic value
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
and obtaining the results (
16
) with the “correlation lengths” given by (
46
).

We commence with the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
. To analyze its behavior at large times, we need to understand the analytic structure of the Laplace transform given by (
211
). A key element is the Lambert
W
-function,
W_{0}(z)
, which appears in the expression for
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as in (
206
). The function
W_{0}(z)
has a branch cut along the negative real axis for
z<-1/e
. This branch cut induces branch cuts in
Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0})
that have a form:

(213)

\rho\in\left(-\infty+2\pi\mathrm{i}\;\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}};\rho_{\ell}\right],\quad\rho_{\ell}=-\beta+\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\left(1+\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)+2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\ell,

where
\ell
is an integer. Consequently the integrand (
211
) has infinitely many cuts and a pole at
\rho=0
(see Fig.
14
).

Figure 14.

Analytic structure of the Laplace transform
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
(left) and of the generating function
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
for the distributions (

) in the survival regime.

To find the asymptotic behavior of the inverse Laplace transform (
211
), we deform the contour
\mathcal{C}_{1}
to encircle the branch cuts. This deformation separates the contribution of the pole at
\rho=0
from the contributions of the branch cuts. Representing the integrals along the cuts as integrals over the real line, we obtain:

(214)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\;\mathrm{Res}_{\rho=0}\left[\frac{e^{\rho\tau}}{\rho}(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0}))\right]\\
+\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\rho^{*}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Delta\left[\frac{\exp\left(\rho\tau+2\pi\mathrm{i}\ell\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\right)}{\rho+2\pi\mathrm{i}\ell\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}}\left(1-Q\left(\rho+2\pi\mathrm{i}\ell\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}},1\,|\,X_{0}\right)\right)\right],

where
\Delta[f(z)]
is the discontinuity across the cut as defined in (
121
), and
\rho^{*}
is the real part of the branching points

(215)

\rho^{*}=-\beta+\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\left(1+\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)

Computing the residue in (
214
), we see that it gives exactly the survival probability
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
,

(216)

\;\mathrm{Res}_{\rho=0}\left[\frac{e^{\rho\tau}}{\rho}(1-Q(\rho,1\,|\,X_{0}))\right]=1-e^{-\Lambda(\alpha)\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{M_{0}}}=S_{\infty}(X_{0}),

where
\Lambda(\alpha)
is given by (
210
).

The remaining term in (
214
) involves the sum of integrals along the branch cuts. Since the discontinuity across each cut is purely imaginary and originates from the
(1-Q)
term, we can rewrite (
214
) as:

(217)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\\
=e^{\rho^{*}\tau}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{(\rho+\rho^{*})\,e^{\rho\tau}\Delta\left[\left(1-Q\left(\rho+\rho^{*}+2\pi\mathrm{i}\ell\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}},1\,|\,X_{0}\right)\right)\right]}{(\rho+\rho^{*})^{2}+\left(2\pi\ell\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\right)^{2}}

The sum of integrals in (
217
) converges. This can be verified by first replacing the integrands by their absolute values and then bounding the discontinuity by
\left|\Delta[1-Q]\right|<2\pi
. Then the leading-order behavior of the difference between
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
and its long-time limit
S_{\infty}(X_{0})
is determined by the prefactor
e^{\rho^{*}\tau}
. Using the explicit form of
\rho^{*}
as in (
215
) we finally obtain

(218)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-\tau\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right].

This is exactly the result stated in (
16
) with
\xi_{\tau}(\alpha)=-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho^{*}}})
given by (
46
).

Now, let us turn to the survival probability
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
. The analytic structure of the integrand in (
212
) is qualitatively the same as that encountered in the case where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential distributions (see Fig.
14
). Namely, there are poles at
s=0
and
s=1
and a branch cut along the real axis

(219)

s\in[s_{1}\;\infty),\qquad s_{1}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta M_{0}}e^{\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-1}.

Repeating the very same arguments as in the case where both distributions are exponential, we find that the survival probability asymptotically tends to a constant

(220)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})-S_{\infty}(X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-1-\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right].

This is the result stated in (
16
) with
\xi_{n}(\alpha)=-\frac{1}{\log s_{1}}
as in (
46
).

Conditional mean and variances for
\tau
and
n
presented in (

), (

), (

), and (
50
) are obtained by substituting (
209
) and (

) into (

), (

), (

) and (

).

5.3.2.
Critical point.

At the critical point (
\alpha=\alpha_{c}
),
\Lambda(\alpha)=0
, hence the survival probability at infinite time (
209
) is zero. The decay rates of the survival probabilities we have computed in the survival regime (
218
), (
220
) are zeros meaning that there is no exponential decay.

The key difference in the analysis of the inverse transforms (
211
) and (
212
) at the critical point is that the pole at
\rho=0
(or
s=1
) coincides with the endpoint of the branch cut. This confluence of singularities alters the asymptotic behavior, and the large
\tau
(large
n
) behavior is now governed by the
\rho\to 0
(or
s\to 1
) expansions of the transforms.

We start with
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
. Using the exact form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as in (
206
) and substituting it into (
113
) we find the Laplace transform of the survival probability:

(221)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(1-\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right)\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\beta}+W_{0}\left[-e^{-\frac{\rho+\beta}{\beta}}\right]\right)\right]\right).

Expanding this expression for small
\rho
reveals a square root singularity

(222)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{-\rho\tau}\,S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}},\qquad\rho\to 0.

This divergence suggests that the survival probability decays as
1/\sqrt{\tau}
. Substituting this ansatz into (
109
) we obtain the large time behavior:

(223)

S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\sim}\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\beta}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}.

Large
n
behavior of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
is found in the similar way. First we substitute the exact form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as in (
206
) into the representation (
114
) for the generating function of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
and arrive at

(224)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^{n}S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})=\frac{1}{1-s}\left(1-s\,\exp\left[-\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right)\left(1+W_{0}\left[-\frac{s}{e}\right]\right)\right]\right).

Then we replace the sum with an integral, and repeat the same procedure as for
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
with
\rho
replaced by
-\log s
, we find the large
n
behavior of
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})
:

(225)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\left(1+\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

Sparre-Anderson theorem.

It is instructive to briefly revisit the scenario where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential. In that case, we were able to compute the survival probability
S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0}=0)
using the Sparre-Anderson theorem (
138
), which predicted a decay of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}
.
However, in the present case of fixed jump amplitudes, as is clear from (
225
), we find a different asymptotic behavior:

(226)

S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0}=0)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}}.

This decay, while still a power law, differs from the decay predicted by the Sparre-Anderson theorem (
139
) by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

This discrepancy, however, represents neither a contradiction nor an error in our calculations.
The Sparre-Andersen theorem (
138
) applies to symmetric random walks with a continuous cumulative distribution function. However, the effective random walk described by (
197
) at the critical point, despite having zero mean displacement, is genuinely asymmetric. This asymmetry makes the Sparre-Anderson theorem inapplicable.

Interestingly, the decay in (
226
) also appears in the survival probability of the symmetric lattice random walk (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). In this case, the process is symmetric but does violate the assumption of a continuous cumulative distribution function.

Scaling limit.

At the critical point, the system exhibits a scaling limit analogous to that observed in the double exponential case (Sec.
4.3.2
). As the underlying calculations are essentially identical, we directly state the resulting scaling behaviors:

(227)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta\tau}}\right),\quad\tau\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed},

(228)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{M_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

Comparing these results to their counterparts obtained in the case where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential, we observe that in both cases, the scaling behavior can be uniformly characterized as

(229)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\frac{\langle t\rangle}{\left\langle(M-\alpha_{c}t)^{2}\right\rangle}}\right),\quad\tau\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed},

(230)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\sim\;\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\left\langle(M-\alpha_{c}t)^{2}\right\rangle}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,\quad\frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{\penalty\ --- fixed}.

Thus, the scaling limit is essentially the same for both cases. This universality arises because, in the scaling limit, the details of the jump distribution become irrelevant, and the process converges to a Brownian motion. As long as the effective random walk has zero mean and finite moments, we expect the Brownian limit to be universally valid, regardless of the specific forms of
p(t)
and
q(M)
.

We conclude the analysis at the critical point by verifying the analytical results (
223
), (
225
), (
227
), and (
228
) by performing numerical simulations (see Fig.
15
).

5.3.3.
Absorption regime

If the drift is strong (
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
), then the process is strongly pulled toward the origin and the first-passage event eventually occurs. In the analytic structure of the inverse transforms (
211
), (
212
) of the survival probabilities this is manifested in the absence of the poles at
\rho=0
and
s=1
. The disappearance of the poles implies that in the leading order the behavior of the survival probabilities is governed by the branching points
\rho^{*}
as in (
215
) and
s_{1}
as in (
219
). Specifically, we have

(231)

\displaystyle S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})\underset{\tau\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-\tau\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right],

(232)

\displaystyle S_{N}(n\,|\,X_{0})\underset{n\to\infty}{\asymp}\exp\left[-n\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-1-\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right].

This is exactly the exponential decay (
15
) with the “correlation lengths” given by (
46
).

Below, we analyze the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}[\tau,n\,|\,X_{0}]
in more detail obtaining the remaining results stated in Sec.
2.2
.
Specifically, we compute the mean and variance of both
\tau
and
n
, and determine the large deviation forms for their marginal probability distributions.

Moments of
\tau
and
n
.

First, we compute the first two moments of
\tau
and
n
to characterize their behavior near typical values. This is achieved by expanding the Laplace transform
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
in series with respect to
\rho
and
s
, then using (
149
) and (
150
) to relate the expansion terms to the moments. From the exact representation (
206
), we obtain for
\tau
:

(233)

\mathrm{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{M_{0}+X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\qquad\mathrm{Var}\left[\tau\,|\,X_{0}\right]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}\,\beta M_{0}^{2},

and for
n
:

(234)

\mathrm{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{\alpha+\beta X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\qquad\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{X_{0}+M_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}\,\beta\alpha^{2}.

The means and variances depend linearly on the initial position
X_{0}
, and diverge as
\alpha
approaches its critical value
\alpha_{c}=\beta M_{0}
.

Correlation coefficient

To quantify the correlation between the
\tau
and
n
, we compute the correlation coefficient

(235)

\mathrm{corr}(\tau,n)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\tau n)\,|\,X_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\;\mathbb{E}[n\,|\,X_{0}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\,\mathrm{Var}[n\,|\,X_{0}]}}.

Using the exact form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
we find that if the jumps are fixed, then

(236)

\mathrm{corr}(\tau,n)=1.

In other words, we have a perfect linear correlation between
n
and
\tau
. This strong relationship arises directly from the first-passage condition (
186
). In simpler terms, it means that for each additional jump the first-passage time increases linearly, with no variance around this value.

Rate function for
\tau
.

The mean and the variance of
\tau
and
n
grow linearly with
X_{0}
. Therefore we expect the marginal probability distribution to follow the large deviation form

(237)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Phi(z)},\qquad z=\frac{\alpha\tau}{X_{0}}-1,

with the scaling parameter
z
lying in the range
z\in[0;\infty)
. This large deviation form is the same as the one in the first exactly solvable case (
157
) but the rate function
\Phi(z)
is different.

To determine the rate function we first substitute the large deviation ansatz into the Laplace transform (
205
) and compute the integral in the saddle-point approximation. Then we compare the result with the exponential behavior extracted from the explicit form of
Q(\rho,s\,|\,X_{0})
as given in (
206
) and equate the exponents arriving at a Legendre transform similar to (
162
). Finally, inverting this transform we find the rate function as the solution of the maximization problem

(238)

\Phi(z)=\max_{\rho}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\rho(z+1)+\phi(\rho)\right),

where

(239)

\phi(\rho)=\frac{\rho+\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}W_{0}\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}(\rho+\beta)}\right].

This maximization can be carried out explicitly. Indeed, the maximum is reached at
\rho^{*}
which is the solution of
\phi^{\prime}(\rho^{*})=\frac{z+1}{\alpha}
.
Note that the Lambert
W
-function satisfies

(240)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}W_{0}(x)=\frac{W_{0}(x)}{x(1+W_{0}(x))}.

Using this identity we find that
\rho^{*}
satisfies

(241)

-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,z-\frac{W_{0}\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}(\rho^{*}+\beta)}\right]}{1+W_{0}\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}e^{-\frac{M_{0}}{\alpha}(\rho^{*}+\beta)}\right]}=0.

Solving (
241
) with respect to the Lambert function yields

(242)

W_{0}\left[-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,M_{0}e^{-M_{0}\frac{p^{*}+\beta}{\alpha}}\right]=-\frac{z}{z+1}.

Recall that the Lambert function
W_{0}(z)
is a solution of
xe^{x}=z
and hence we can reduce (
242
) to

(243)

-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,M_{0}e^{-M_{0}\frac{\rho^{*}+\beta}{\alpha}}=-\frac{z}{z+1}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{z+1}\right],

which is readily solved, yielding

(244)

\rho^{*}=-\beta+\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\left(\frac{z}{z+1}-\log\left[\frac{z}{z+1}\frac{\alpha}{\beta M_{0}}\right]\right).

Substituting (
244
) into (
238
) and using (
242
), after some simplification, we obtain the exact form of the rate function:

(245)

\Phi(z)=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+z\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{M_{0}}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\log\left[\frac{z}{z+1}\frac{\alpha}{\beta M_{0}}\right]\right).

Note that the rate function is very different from the one obtained in the case where both
p(t)
and
q(M)
are exponential distributions (
165
).
This is also a non-negative convex function with a minimum at
z^{*}=\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}}
and the asymptotic behaviors

(246)

\Phi(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\,z\log z,&&z\to 0,\\
&\frac{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}{2\alpha^{2}\beta M_{0}^{2}}(z-z^{*})^{2},&&z\to z^{*}=\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\\
&z\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{M_{0}}-\frac{1}{M_{0}}\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right),&&z\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

From (
246
) we extract the asymptotic behaviors of the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
. Specifically, close to the typical values of
\tau
, the distribution is Gaussian

(247)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\tau-\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau])^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[\tau]}\right],\qquad\tau\approx\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau],

where the asymptotic mean and variance are

(248)

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau]=\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[\tau]=\frac{\beta M_{0}^{2}\,X_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}.

This Gaussian approximation describes the typical fluctuations of the first-passage time. Both the asymptotic mean and the variance are in agreement with the results obtained in (
233
) for an arbitrary
X_{0}
.

The left tail of the distribution (atypically small values of
\tau
) is given by:

(249)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-X_{0}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\,\Delta\tau\log\Delta\tau\right],\quad\tau=\Delta\tau+\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha}\ll\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau].

Comparing this with the left tail obtained in the first exactly solvable case (
169
), we observe a notable difference in the functional form, yet the constant term,
-X_{0}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
, remains identical. This shared constant term reflects a fundamental property of the system: the probability of reaching the origin with minimal delay is governed by the initial waiting time distribution,
p(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t}
, irrespective of the specific jump size distribution.

Consider the event where
\Delta\tau=0
. In this scenario, the process must have reached the origin very quickly, implying that the initial jump was immediately followed by the drift carrying the process to the origin. In other words, to reach the origin at
\tau=X_{0}/\alpha
, the process must take effectively no jumps (strictly speaking, only the jump at
t=0
with zero amplitude). Thus, the probability of this event depends only on the initial waiting time, yielding:

(250)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau=0\,|\,X_{0}]\approx\int_{\frac{X_{0}}{\alpha}}^{\infty}p(t)\mathrm{d}t=e^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}X_{0}}.

For the right tail (atypically large values of
\tau
), we obtain an exponential decay with the same rate as in the survival probability (
231
), essentially confirming (
15
). Specifically, we have:

(251)

\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}-\frac{\alpha}{M_{0}}\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\tau\right],\qquad\tau\gg\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau].

This completes the description of the marginal probability
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
(see Fig.
16
for the comparison with the numerical simulations). Trajectories with atypical values of
\tau
are again generated using the Importance Sampling strategy as explained in more detail in Appendix
A
.

Rate function for
n
.

Given the linear growth of the mean and variance of
n
with
X_{0}
, we expect the probability distribution for the number of jumps
n
to follow the large deviation form:

(252)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\asymp}e^{-X_{0}\Psi(\nu)},\qquad\nu=\frac{\alpha\,n}{\beta X_{0}},

where the scaling parameter
\nu
lies in the range
\nu\in[0;\infty)
. This large deviation form mirrors that of the first exactly solvable case (
172
), albeit with a different rate function
\Psi(\nu)
.

The rate function
\Psi(\nu)
can be determined in two ways. One approach is to use the fact that, for fixed jump amplitudes,
\tau
and
n
are deterministically related via the first-passage condition (
186
). Alternatively, we could perform the derivation used to obtain
\Psi(\nu)
in the first exactly solvable case. However, since
\tau
and
n
are linearly related, this derivation would be almost identical to the one we just provided for
\Phi(z)
. Therefore, we choose the more direct approach and use the first-passage condition (
186
) to determine
\Psi(\nu)
.

Taking the limit
X_{0}\to\infty
in (
187
) leads to the relationship between the scaling variables
z
and
\nu
, i.e.,
z=\nu\,\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}
which allows
\Psi(\nu)
to be obtained from
\Phi(z)
via

(253)

\Psi\left(\nu\right)=\Phi\left(\nu\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right).

Using the exact form (
245
) of the rate function
\Phi(z)
, we
find:

(254)

\Psi(\nu)=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(1+\nu\left(M_{0}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1\right)+\nu\log\left[\frac{\nu}{1+\nu\frac{\beta}{\alpha}M_{0}}\right]\right).

The asymptotic behavior of
\Psi(\nu)
and the marginal probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
then follow directly from (
246
), (
247
), (
249
), and (
251
). For completeness, we provide a summary of these expressions below.

The rate function
\Psi(\nu)
is convex, reaching its minimum at
\nu^{*}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}}
. Its asymptotic behavior is given by:

(255)

\Psi(\nu)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,\nu\log\nu,&&\nu\to 0,\\
&\frac{\beta(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}{2\alpha^{4}}(\nu-\nu^{*})^{2},&&\nu\to\nu^{*}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\\
&\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-1-\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\nu,&&\nu\to\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

Near the typical values of
n
, the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
follows a Gaussian:

(256)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(n-\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n])^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[n]}\right],\qquad n\approx\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n],

where the mean and the variance are

(257)

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n]=\frac{\beta\,X_{0}}{\alpha-\beta M_{0}},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\infty}[n]=\frac{\alpha^{2}\beta\,X_{0}}{(\alpha-\beta M_{0})^{3}}.

The left tail (atypically small values of
n
) is given by:

(258)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-X_{0}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-n\log n\right],\quad n\ll\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n].

For the right tail (atypically large values of
n
) we have an exponential decay with the same rate as for the survival probability (
232
), i.e.,

(259)

\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]\underset{X_{0}\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[-n\left(\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}-1-\log\frac{\beta M_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right],\qquad n\gg\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[n].

This concludes the analysis of the marginal distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}[n\,|\,X_{0}]
in the second exactly solvable case (see Fig.
16
for the comparison with the numerical simulations). With this, we have now completed the analysis of the first-passage properties for both exactly solvable cases.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the first-passage properties of a jump process with a constant drift. By mapping this process onto a discrete-time random walk and applying the generalized Pollaczek-Spitzer formula, we have derived a closed-form expression for the (triple) Laplace transform of the joint probability of
n
and
\tau
. To exemplify this approach, we have performed a detailed analysis of two exactly solvable cases, where calculations could be carried out in a well-controlled manner, and a more standard renewal equation approach is also applicable.

The main advantage of our approach is that, unlike the renewal equation method, it does not require solving integral equations, which is generally a challenging task unless the inter-jump distribution is exponential.
Instead, it provides a closed-form expression for the Laplace transform of the joint distribution of
n
and
\tau
, thereby enabling a systematic asymptotic analysis of systems with arbitrary inter-jump and jump amplitude distributions. This analysis can be performed using techniques developed in
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
38
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
39
,
40
,
37
]
. We emphasize that, although we map the original process onto a discrete-time random walk, this mapping retains information about both the first-passage time
\tau
and the number of jumps
n
before absorption. This level of detail would be lost if one were to directly apply the Pollaczek-Spitzer formula to the original process.

We have shown that the model exhibits two distinct regimes: the
survival regime
and the
absorption regime
, separated by the
critical point
. In the absorption regime, the drift is strong enough to drive the process toward the origin, leading to an exponential decay of the survival probabilities. In the survival regime, where the drift is weak, there exists a finite probability that first-passage never occurs, and the process remains positive indefinitely. In this case, at large times, the survival probabilities asymptotically approach a constant, decaying exponentially. The transition between these two regimes defines the critical point, where the survival probability follows a power-law decay rather than an exponential one. This behavior is reminiscent of phase transitions in the two-dimensional Ising model, with the drift velocity playing a role analogous to that of the temperature.

A natural question that arises is to what extent the results obtained in this paper for two exactly solvable cases can be generalized to arbitrary distributions
p(t)
and
q(M)
. We believe that the general structure illustrated in Fig.
2
, as well as the asymptotic behaviors of the survival probabilities, remains valid, at least for light-tailed distributions. However, a more thorough analytical investigation is required.

Another interesting direction for future work is to extend this study to systems with multiple particles, with or without interactions. In particular, one could focus on the fraction of trajectories that have not crossed the origin up to a fixed observation time and analyze how this quantity is influenced by the initial conditions. We expect to observe the memory effects similar to those reported in
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,

,

Appendix A
Numerical strategy

This appendix provides details on the numerical simulations.

The plots in the survival regime (Fig.
5
, Fig.
13
) and at the critical point (Fig.
8
, Fig.
15
) are obtained through direct trajectory generation. In this method, we fix the number of jumps,
N
, and generate
N
pairs
(t_{j},M_{j})
by independently sampling from the distributions
p(t)
and
q(M)
. This process is repeated to generate
10^{6}
trajectories. The survival probabilities are then calculated as the fraction of trajectories that remain positive up to the
n
th jump (or up to the time
\tau
).

When analyzing the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
, it is crucial to choose a sufficiently large value of
N
to ensure that all trajectories either cross the origin or reach the total time greater than
\tau
. Indeed, if the total time at the
N
th step is smaller than
\tau
, it becomes unclear how such trajectories contribute to the survival probability
S_{T}(\tau\,|\,X_{0})
.
We set
N=1000
for the plots shown in Fig.
5
and Fig.
13
(survival regime), and
N=10\,000
for the plots shown in Fig.
8
and Fig.
15
(critical point).

The plots in the absorption regime (Fig.
9
, Fig.
16
) display the probability distributions up to very small probabilities (
\approx 10^{-150}
). To generate the trajectories with such atypical values of
\tau
(or
n
) we employ Importance Sampling Monte Carlo (see
[
59
]
for a pedagogical introduction). This is a widely used strategy for studying rare event statistics in various systems
[
77
,
78
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
82
,
83
]
. The key idea behind the Importance Sampling is to sample trajectories from a biased distribution. Here we focus on the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]
as the strategy is the same for both observables.

In this paper, we use Importance Sampling with an exponential tilt in the observable. Specifically, we sample
\tau
from the biased distribution

(260)

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}]=\frac{e^{\theta\tau}}{Z(\theta)}\mathbb{P}_{T}[\tau\,|\,X_{0}],

where
\theta
is a parameter (quasi-temperature), and
Z(\theta)
is a normalization constant. The positive (negative) values of
\theta
correspond to increasing the statistical weight of the trajectories with atypically large (small) values of
\tau
.

To sample
\tau
from the biased distribution (
260
), we apply the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm on the space of the trajectories. First, we generate a trajectory
\{(t_{j},M_{j})\}
with a fixed number of jumps
N
. At each step, the algorithm proposes a new trajectory
\{(t_{j}^{\prime},M_{j}^{\prime})\}
by randomly selecting
r
out of the
N
pairs
(t_{j},M_{j})
and resampling them from
p(t)
and
q(M)
. The proposed trajectory is then accepted with the probability

(261)

\{(t_{j},M_{j})\}\mapsto\{(t_{j}^{\prime},M_{j}^{\prime})\}:\qquad p_{\text{acc}}=\min\left(1,\exp\left[\theta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\right]\right),

where
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are the first-passage times corresponding to the trajectories
\{(t_{j},M_{j})\}
and
\{(t_{j}^{\prime},M_{j}^{\prime})\}
. The parameter
r
is chosen to maintain an acceptance rate above
70\%
, with typical values for
r
in the range
[1,10]
. The length of the trajectory
N
is chosen large enough to ensure that all generated trajectories cross the origin. This is particularly important when studying the tail of the distribution for atypically large values of
\tau
(or
n
).
In our simulations we set
N=10\,000
.
For the quasi-temperatures
\theta
