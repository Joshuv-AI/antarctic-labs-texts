On the Local Structure and Approximation Stability of Block Isotropic Gaussian Fields
Munki Jeong, Alexander Strang

Abstract
Skew-symmetric functions are a class of functions defined on a product space M × M M\times M that are antisymmetric with respect to the order of their inputs. In [ 13 ] , the authors proved that non-deterministic skew-symmetric Gaussian fields cannot be stationary or isotropic and proposed an alternative notion: stationarity (isotropy) in each component space. Our work focuses on local quadratic approximations of the associated Gaussian fields. Local quadratic approximations to random fields are random polynomials parametrized by a jointly sampled gradient vector and Hessian matrix. We characterize the distribution of the corresponding random vectors and random matrices. Then, we study the error in the quadratic approximation, which is also a Gaussian field. We investigate the error induced by the quadratic approximation in three senses: the pointwise error, the maximal error over an ellipsoidal region, and the worst-case error for multivariate Gaussian inputs at a given confidence level. Next, we explore the limiting behavior of the worst-case error as the distance between an expansion point and evaluation points approaches zero and infinity. Finally, we study how, as the input dimension increases, the variance of multivariate Gaussian distributions must be restricted to keep the worst-case error bound constant. Keywords: Gaussian Processes, Random Fields, Local Approximation, Gaussian Random Matrices, High-Dimensional Probability, Asymptotic Analysis

Introduction

Skew-symmetric functions are a class of functions defined on the product space
M\times M
, which are antisymmetric with respect to the order of their inputs. These functions generalize real skew-symmetric matrices, which can be viewed as functions defined on the product space of a finite set of natural numbers, to arbitrary index spaces. Skew-symmetric functions are common across the sciences. Notable applications include preference modeling and network flow analysis.

For instance, in preference modeling, skew-symmetric functions represent the relative preference between two choices
A,B\in M
: the preference for choice
A
over choice
B
is the negative of the preference for
B
over
A

[
8
,
3
,
6
]
. Since
[
8
]
introduced a Bayesian nonparametric approach to preference learning via Gaussian processes, extensive research has been devoted to modeling and learning skew-symmetric preference functions. As another example, network flows are modeled as skew-symmetric functions on pairs of vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in M
, where
f(v_{1},v_{2})
denotes the flow from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
, and
f(v_{2},v_{1})=-f(v_{1},v_{2})
denotes the flow in the opposite direction
[
12
,
14
]
. Gaussian fields are also employed here for stochastic modeling
[
13
]
. More generally, Gaussian fields are a useful tool for modeling phenomena when the underlying mechanics are not well understood. This approach is also widely adopted in theoretical physics
[
17
]
.

Many applied sciences study problems parameterized by functions,
f
. For example, most dynamical systems start from an ordinary differential equation model that is expressed
\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=f(x(t))
for some function
f
. Commonly, these problems cannot be solved for a generic
f
. When the solution is continuous in
f
,
f
is smooth, and inputs may be restricted to a small neighborhood, it is common to replace
f
with a truncated expansion,
\tilde{f}
. In many applications,
\tilde{f}
is a second-order Taylor approximation. The user usually proceeds by finding exact solutions to the approximate problem, substituting
\tilde{f}
for
f
. Quadratic expressions are widely used because they offer a tractable yet expressive means of capturing the rich local behavior of the original functions
[
4
]
. We are interested in the accuracy of local quadratic approximations to random models of skew-symmetric functions. For related work, see
[
5
,
13
]
.

We introduce a specific subclass of skew-symmetric Gaussian processes, termed Block Isotropic Gaussian Processes. We characterize the local geometric structure of their derivatives, demonstrate that their quadratic approximations can be viewed as another Gaussian process parameterized by a random gradient and Hessian, and show that these are isotropic Gaussian vectors and Gaussian Orthogonal Ensembles. Finally, we analyze the approximation error between the original Gaussian process and its local quadratic approximation, providing the pointwise error distribution and extending this to a uniform error bound that holds with high probability over an elliptical region and for normally distributed random inputs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the foundations of Gaussian random fields, including stationarity/isotropy, appropriate notions of derivatives, and several theorems characterizing the covariance of derivatives. This is followed by a discussion of random vectors and random matrices, and of their (un)correlation and independence. In Section
3
, we study skew-symmetric Gaussian fields and their properties, focusing in particular on the incompatibility of the general notion of stationarity (isotropy) with skew-symmetry in a product space. We then introduce an alternative notion, which we refer to as block stationarity (isotropy), and describe in detail the properties of block isotropic Gaussian fields. In Section
4
, we derive the quadratic Taylor approximation of block isotropic Gaussian fields at inputs of the form
[z,z]
and characterize the quadratic approximation as a Gaussian field parametrized by Gaussian random vectors and matrices, with prescribed independence structure. Based on this structure, in Section
5
, we give a characterization of the discrepancy between the original Gaussian fields and their quadratic approximation as another Gaussian field. A simple closed-form expression for the covariance function of these Gaussian fields leads to a straightforward characterization of the pointwise error. We then extend the pointwise error result to the maximal error over an ellipsoidal region. We use this to construct a uniform upper bound on the error for random inputs sampled from a multivariate Gaussian distribution. Finally, we describe the limiting behavior of the bound near, and far, from the point of expansion. We conclude the paper by studying how an ellipsoidal region must concentrate about the point of expansion as the underlying dimension increases to maintain the same error characterization.

Preliminaries on Gaussian Random Fields

2.1
Gaussian Random Fields

We present Gaussian fields as described in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
.

Definition 1

(Real-Valued Random Field)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space and
M
be a topological space (the index set). A real-valued random field is a collection of random variables
\{u(x)\}_{x\in M}
, or equivalently, a measurable mapping
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{M}
denotes the space of all real-valued functions on
M
.

In this work, we consider the case where the index set
M
is a
2D
-dimensional Euclidean space, i.e.,
M=\mathbb{R}^{2D}
.
The random field is denoted by
u=\{u_{x}(\omega)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}}
. For a fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
,
u_{x}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued random variable. We will primarily use the notation
u(x)
rather than
u_{x}
unless confusion arises.

Definition 2

(Gaussian Random Field)
.

A random field
\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}
is a Gaussian random field if for any finite set of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, every linear combination of
\big\{u(x_{1}),\dots,u(x_{n})\big\}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}u(x_{i})
follows a univariate Gaussian distribution.

Definition 3

(Equality in Distribution)
.

Two Gaussian random fields
\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}
and
\{v(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}
are said to be equal in distribution (or equal in law) if their finite-dimensional distributions are identical, i.e., for any finite set of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the multivariate vectors
(u(x_{1}),\dots,u(x_{n}))
and
(v(x_{1}),\dots,v(x_{n}))
follow the same distribution:

\displaystyle\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{v(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}.

Arbitrary random fields are not necessarily specified by their first two moments. However, Gaussian fields are entirely determined by their mean and covariance functions.

Definition 4

(Mean and
Cov
ariance Function)
.

For a given Gaussian field
u
, the mean function
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{2D}\to\mathbb{R}
and the covariance function
k_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{2D}\times\mathbb{R}^{2D}\to\mathbb{R}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[u(x)],

\displaystyle k_{u}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\text{\text{Cov}}(u(x),u(y))=\mathbb{E}[(u(x)-\mu(x))(u(y)-\mu(y))],\quad\forall\text{ }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.

We are predominantly interested in the case where the variance is finite. With this in mind, we consider Gaussian fields as elements of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,M)
, or more precisely, we assume that for every fixed
x
,
u(x)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. This ensures that the covariance function
k_{u}
is well-defined and finite everywhere.

Stationarity and isotropy are crucial properties because they imply that the random fields are invariant under translation and orthogonal coordinate transformations, respectively. Introducing symmetries simplifies analysis by reducing the number of free components of the model.

Definition 5

(Stationary and Isotropic Gaussian Field)
.

A Gaussian field
u
is
strictly stationary
if its finite-dimensional distributions are invariant under translations.
That is
\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{u(x+a)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}
.

A Gaussian field
u
is
weakly stationary
if its mean function is constant and its covariance function depends only on the difference vector
x-y
. i.e., there exists
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{2D}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle\mu(x)\equiv\mu\quad\text{and}\quad k_{u}(x,y)=\kappa(x-y).

Further, a Gaussian field is
isotropic
if its mean is constant and its covariance depends only on the Euclidean distance
\|x-y\|
. i.e., there exists
\kappa_{r}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle k_{u}(x,y)=\kappa_{r}(\|x-y\|).

In general, strict stationarity implies weak stationarity, but the converse is not necessarily true. However, for Gaussian fields, the two notions are equivalent because the distribution is fully characterized by the first two moments. Thus, we use the term
“stationary”
without qualification. Notably, isotropic Gaussian fields are always stationary.

Notation 6

(Squared Distance Notation)
.

It is common to express the isotropic covariance function in terms of the squared distance. We posit that there exists a function
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle k_{u}(x,y)=h\big(\|x-y\|^{2}\big),\quad\forall\text{ }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.

(1)

For example,
h(\tau)=exp(-\tau/l^{2})
amounts to the squared exponential covariance function.
In this notation, the smoothness assumption
k_{u}\in C^{2n}(\mathbb{R}^{2D}\times\mathbb{R}^{2D})
is equivalent to
h\in C^{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Proposition 1

(Isotropic Gaussian Field is Orthogonally Invariant)
.

Let
R:\mathbb{R}^{2D}\to\mathbb{R}^{2D}
be an arbitrary orthogonal transformation (e.g., rotation or reflection).
If
\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}}
is an isotropic Gaussian field, then it is distributionally invariant under
Q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\{u(Qx)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\overset{d}{=}\{u(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}}.

Conversely, for a stationary Gaussian field, distributional invariance under all orthogonal transformations implies isotropy.

Proof.

Since Gaussian fields are entirely determined by their mean and covariance functions, it suffices to show that these are invariant.
Let
u
be isotropic. Since
\mathbb{E}[u(Qx)]=\mu=\mathbb{E}[u(x)]
, the mean is invariant.
Furthermore, since
\|Qx-Qy\|=\|Q(x-y)\|=\|x-y\|
, we have:

k_{u}(Qx,Qy)=\kappa_{r}(\|Qx-Qy\|)=\kappa_{r}(\|x-y\|)=k_{u}(x,y).

Thus, the covariance is invariant.

Conversely, assume
u
is stationary and orthogonal invariant. Stationarity implies
k_{u}(x,y)=\kappa(x-y)
. Orthogonal invariance implies
\kappa(Q(x-y))=\kappa(x-y)
for any orthogonal
Q
. This is if and only if
\kappa
is a function of
\|x-y\|
. Hence,
k_{u}
depends only on
\|x-y\|
.
∎

2.2
Differentiability of Gaussian Random Fields

Definition 7

(
L^{2}
Continuity)
.

A Gaussian field
u
is continuous in
L^{2}
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
if for every sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2D}
such that
x_{n}\to x
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big|u(x_{n})-u(x)\big|^{2}\bigg]=0.

Definition 8

(Directional Derivative)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2D}
be a deterministic function . The directional derivative of
u
at
x
in the direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
, denoted by
D_{v}u(x)
, is defined as the limit of the quotient:

\displaystyle D_{v}u(x):=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{u(x+tv)-u(x)}{t}.

In particular, when
v
is the
i
-th standard basis vector
e_{i}
, the directional derivative reduces to the partial derivative
\partial_{i}u(x)
.

Definition 9

(Mean-Square Differentiability)
.

A Gaussian field
u
is differentiable in
L^{2}
(i.e., mean-square sense) at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
, if there exists a random variable
D_{v}u(x)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\frac{u(x+tv)-u(x)}{t}-D_{v}u(x)\bigg|^{2}\bigg]=0,\quad\text{ in every direction }v\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.

Proposition 2

(Interchange of Differentiation and Expectation)
.

If the Gaussian field
u
is differentiable in
L^{2}
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
, then we can interchange the order of differentiation and expectation, i.e.,

\partial_{i}\mathbb{E}[u(x)]=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{i}u(x)],\quad 1\leq i\leq 2D.

Proof.

Let
e_{i}
denote the unit vector in the
i
-th direction. Recall that convergence in
L^{2}
implies convergence in
L^{1}
. Therefore, the definition of mean-square differentiability implies:

\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{u(x+te_{i})-u(x)}{t}-\partial_{i}u(x)\right|=0.

By the linearity of expectation and Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[u(x+te_{i})]-\mathbb{E}[u(x)]}{t}-\mathbb{E}[\partial_{i}u(x)]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u(x+te_{i})-u(x)}{t}-\partial_{i}u(x)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{u(x+te_{i})-u(x)}{t}-\partial_{i}u(x)\right|\to 0.

Thus,
\partial_{i}\mathbb{E}[u(x)]=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{i}u(x)]
.
∎

Proposition 3

(Gaussianity under Linear Operations and Limits)
.

Let
u
be a Gaussian field.

1.

Any finite linear combination of values of
u
is a Gaussian random variable.

2.

The
L^{2}
-limit of a sequence of Gaussian random variables is a Gaussian random variable.

Proof.

Part 1 follows directly from the definition of a Gaussian field (multivariate normality).

For Part 2, let
\{X_{n}\}
be a sequence of Gaussian random variables such that
X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}^{2})
and
X_{n}\to X
in
L^{2}
.

First, recall that convergence in
L^{2}
implies convergence of the first two moments.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
|\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]-\mathbb{E}[X]|=|\mathbb{E}[X_{n}-X]|\leq\|X_{n}-X\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
, so
\mu_{n}\to\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X]
.
Similarly, by the reverse triangle inequality for norms,
\|X_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\to\|X\|_{L^{2}}
, which implies
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{2}]\to\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
. Consequently, the variances converge:
\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\sigma^{2}:=\text{Var}(X)
.

Second, we recall that
X_{n}\to X
in
L^{2}
implies
X_{n}
converges to
X
in distribution (denoted
X_{n}\xrightarrow{d}X
).
Consider the Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDF). Since each
X_{n}
is Gaussian, its CDF is given by
F_{n}(x)=\Phi(\frac{x-\mu_{n}}{\sigma_{n}})
, where
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF.
Since
\mu_{n}\to\mu
and
\sigma_{n}\to\sigma
, and
\Phi
is a continuous function, we have pointwise convergence for all points where the limit CDF is continuous:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).

Note that the right-hand side is the CDF of a Gaussian variable
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
.
Hence the random variable
X
must follow the distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
.
∎

Proposition 4

(Derivative of Gaussian Field)
.

Let
u
be a mean-square differentiable Gaussian field on
\mathbb{R}^{2D}

\partial_{i}u(x)
is a Gaussian random variable.

Proof.

The derivative is defined as the
L^{2}
-limit of the quotient
\frac{1}{t}(u(x+te_{i})-u(x))
. For any fixed
t
, this quotient is a linear combination of Gaussian variables, hence Gaussian. By Proposition
3
, the limit is also Gaussian.
∎

(Sufficient Condition for Mean Square Differentiability)
.

Let
u
be a Gaussian field on

with covariance function

and a differentiable mean function. If the mixed partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}k_{u}(x,y)}{\partial x_{i}\partial y_{j}}

(
Cov

If

2.3
Preliminaries on Random Vectors and Random Matrices

Remark
.

Throughout this work, we consider the gradient and Hessian at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
While
u(z)
is a scalar random variable, the gradient
\nabla u(z)
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}u(z)
is a random matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}
. Rigorously, these are random variables taking values in Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2D})
and
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D})
respectively.
In particular, the inner product is defined as:

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X^{\mathsf{T}}Y],\quad X,Y\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D}),

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D\times D})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\text{trace}(A^{\mathsf{T}}B)],\quad A,B\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}).

Notably, the elements of Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{D\times D})
are Banach space-valued random variables where the norm is defined through the inner product and the metric is induced by the norm.
With this understanding, real-valued, vector-valued, and matrix-valued random variables can be treated in a unified framework.

Notation 14

For

3.2
Block Isotropic Gaussian Fields

While stationarity and isotropy are desirable characteristics for analysis,
Lemma
13
implies that every skew-symmetric non-deterministic Gaussian field is not stationary, so cannot be isotropic either. Nevertheless, analogous notions of stationarity and isotropy are introduced in
[
13
]
. Recall that a skew-symmetric function is defined on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Seeking distributional invariance for each component space separately is reasonable since the inputs
x,y
to
f(x,y)
represent elements of a shared component space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
in most applications. For example, if
f
is a preference function intended to compare elements drawn from the same space, it is natural to interpret each component space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
in the same way and expect them to have the same structure
[
3
]
. Consequently, we consider the invariance under translations and orthogonal transformations that are applied to each component space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
separately, i.e.,
\forall\text{ }a^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\text{ and orthogonal matrix }Q^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
,

\displaystyle\{f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{f(x^{(1)}+a^{*},x^{(2)}+a^{*})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}

(4)

\displaystyle\{f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{f(Q^{*}x^{(1)},Q^{*}x^{(2)})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}

(5)

instead of invariance under arbitrary translations
T_{a}
and orthogonal transformations
T_{Q}
defined on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
,

\displaystyle\{f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{f(T_{a}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}))\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}

\displaystyle\{f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{f(T_{Q}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}))\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}

A stochastic process exhibits block isotropy if it is defined on a product space, and its ensemble is invariant to unitary coordinate changes applied to the component spaces. If
f
is expressed as a difference of base fields
u
, and
u
is isotropic, then
f
is block isotropic. Since we will work exclusively with this parameterization, we will, for brevity, use block isotropic to refer to models expressed as a difference of isotropic base fields.

u
is stationary and isotropic, respectively.

Remark
.

Convention

If the mean function of the base process

is always zero. We will assume that the mean function of the stationary or isotropic base process

4
Local Quadratic Approximation of Block Isotropic Gaussian Processes

The values of random fields are determined by both random and spatial effects. Expressing a random function
f_{x}(\omega)
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, in terms of a Taylor basis
\{T_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
with corresponding random coefficients
\{A_{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
separates these factors:

\displaystyle f_{x}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}(\omega)T_{k}(x)

This representation offers several advantages. First, it provides a clearer intuition, as the random coefficients can be interpreted as amplitudes associated with each basis function. Second, it reduces the study of infinite-dimensional objects to the analysis of countable (or finite) collections of coefficients.

In this work, we focus on the second-order Taylor expansion, as it balances expressivity and tractability
[
4
]
. A quadratic local approximation can capture both local slopes and curvature. Furthermore, the coefficients corresponding to the bases
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are given by the gradient and Hessian, respectively, making the quadratic expansion both contextually interpretable and technically tractable. We omit the dependence on the point about which we perform a Taylor expansion for brevity, unless clarity requires it.

Finally, we present the closed-form expression for the covariance function of the Taylor approximation

k_{\tilde{f}}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle k_{\tilde{f}}\big((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big):=\Cov\big(\tilde{f}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),\tilde{f}(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)\quad\forall\text{ }(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}

Proposition 24

(Covariance function of the Quadratic Approximation)
.

Under the same setting as Theorem
20
and where
h
is the scalar function associated with the block isotropic covariance function
k_{f}
as in
6
, the covariance function of the Taylor approximation
\tilde{f}
at the matched input point

\displaystyle k_{\tilde{f}}\big(x,y\big)

\displaystyle=-4h^{\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(\big\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}\bigg)+4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(\big\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}^{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\text{where }\Delta x=x-z,\Delta y=y-z

Proof.

Recall that the quadratic Taylor approximation at
z=(z^{*},z^{*})
is expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})=g^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\Delta x^{(1)}-\Delta x^{(2)}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg((\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta x^{(1)}-(\Delta x^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta x^{(2)}\bigg)+(\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,2)}\Delta x^{(2)}

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})=g^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\Delta y^{(1)}-\Delta y^{(2)}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg((\Delta y^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta x^{(1)}-(\Delta y^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta y^{(2)}\bigg)+(\Delta y^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,2)}\Delta y^{(2)}

\displaystyle\text{where }\Delta x^{(1)}:=x^{(1)}-z^{*},\quad\Delta x^{(2)}:=x^{(2)}-z^{*},\quad\Delta y^{(1)}:=y^{(1)}-z^{*},\quad\Delta y^{(2)}:=y^{(2)}-z^{*}

We expand
k_{\tilde{f}}(x,y)=\Cov\big(\tilde{f}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),\tilde{f}(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)
term by term.
First, by Theorem

, note that all cross-covariance terms involving

\displaystyle\text{1. }\Cov\big(g^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\Delta x^{(1)}-\Delta x^{(2)}\big),g^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\Delta y^{(1)}-\Delta y^{(2)}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=-4h^{\prime}(0)\bigg(\big\langle\Delta x^{(1)},\Delta y^{(1)}\big\rangle_{2}+\big\langle\Delta x^{(2)},\Delta y^{(2)}\big\rangle_{2}-\big\langle\Delta x^{(1)},\Delta y^{(2)}\big\rangle_{2}-\big\langle\Delta x^{(2)},\Delta y^{(1)}\big\rangle_{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\text{2. }\Cov\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\big((\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta x^{(1)}-(\Delta x^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta x^{(2)}\big),\frac{1}{2}\big((\Delta y^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta y^{(1)}-(\Delta y^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,1)}\Delta y^{(2)}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(||(\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta y^{(1)}||_{2}^{2}+||(\Delta x^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta y^{(2)}||_{2}^{2}-||(\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta y^{(2)}||_{2}^{2}-||(\Delta x^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta y^{(1)}||_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\text{3. }\Cov\big((\Delta x^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,2)}\Delta x^{(2)},(\Delta y^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}}H^{(1,2)}\Delta y^{(2)}\big)

\displaystyle=4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(2\big\langle\Delta x^{(1)},\Delta y^{(1)}\big\rangle_{2}\cdot\big\langle\Delta x^{(2)},\Delta y^{(2)}\big\rangle_{2}-2\big\langle\Delta x^{(1)},\Delta y^{(2)}\big\rangle_{2}\cdot\big\langle\Delta x^{(2)},\Delta y^{(1)}\big\rangle_{2}\bigg)

Let

\displaystyle k_{\tilde{f}}\big((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)=-4h^{\prime}(0)\cdot(A+D-B-C)+4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot(A^{2}+D^{2}-B^{2}-C^{2}+2AD-2BC)

\displaystyle=-4h^{\prime}(0)\cdot\big((A+D)-(B+C)\big)+4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot\big((A+D)^{2}-(B+C)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=-4h^{\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(\big\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}\bigg)+4h^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot\bigg(\big\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}^{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}^{2}\bigg)

∎

5
Error induced by Quadratic Approximation

5.1
Error Fields of Block Isotropic Gaussian Fields

For deterministic functions, the discrepancy between the function and its Taylor approximation increases with the distance from the expansion point.
Then, for random fields, we expect the discrepancy to increase
“stochastically”
with distance.
In general, characterizing the distribution of the error induced by the Taylor approximation of random fields is challenging.
However, for Block Isotropic Gaussian fields, due to their intrinsic structure, the error is also a Gaussian field, and the pointwise error is a mean-zero Gaussian random variable.
The variance of the error process at each input completely determines the distribution of the pointwise error and is available in closed form.
Furthermore, the maximal error over a neighborhood of the location
z^{*}
used as the center of the approximation can be bounded from above with high probability. We extend this bound to randomly distributed inputs drawn from Gaussian distributions centered at
z^{*}
.

Convention
.

As noted in
Convention
, we assume the mean function of skew-symmetric Gaussian fields to be zero unless otherwise stated.

Definition 22

(Error Process)
.

Let
f\sim GF(0,k_{f})
be a skew-symmetric Gaussian field over
\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
k_{f}\in C^{4}((\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D})\times(\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}))
differentiability and
\tilde{f}
be its second-order Taylor approximation at the point
z=(z^{*},z^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Then, we define the error process
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}):=\tilde{f}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})-f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\quad\forall\text{ }(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}

Proposition 26
.

Under the same setting as above, the error process
\mathcal{E}
is also a Gaussian field over
\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
with mean zero.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{f}
is a Gaussian field as shown in
18
and
f
is also a Gaussian field, their difference
\mathcal{E}
is also a Gaussian field by
3
.
Since both
f
and
\tilde{f}
are mean zero,
\mathcal{E}
is also mean zero.
∎

Since
\mathcal{E}
is a mean-zero Gaussian field, its distribution is fully determined by its covariance function.
Hereafter, we assume
f
to be a block isotropic Gaussian field with its covariance function
k_{f}
being four times differentiable in
L^{2}
.

Theorem 27
.

Under the same setting as above, the error process
\mathcal{E}
is invariant under block orthogonal transformation
Q^{b}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{E}(Q^{b}x\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}\overset{d}{=}\{\mathcal{E}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}}.

Proof.

Under the assumptions, both
f
and
\tilde{f}

Then their difference must share the same invariances.
∎

We now present the closed-form expression for the covariance function of the error process
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.,

defined by

\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{E}}\big((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big):=\Cov\big(\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),\mathcal{E}(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)\quad\forall\text{ }(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}.

The covariance function of the error process can be expressed in terms of the covariance function of the base field.
Let
k_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}

Proposition 28
.

Here,

\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{E}}\big(x,y\big)

\displaystyle=2\bigg(h\big(\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}-h\big(\|x-y^{flip}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)+4\bigg(h^{\prime}\big(\|\Delta x\|_{2}^{2}\big)+h^{\prime}\big(\|\Delta y\|_{2}^{2}\big)-h^{\prime}\big(0\big)\bigg)\bigg(\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-4\bigg(h^{\prime\prime}\big(\|\Delta x\|_{2}^{2}\big)+h^{\prime\prime}\big(\|\Delta y\|_{2}^{2}\big)-h^{\prime\prime}\big(0\big)\bigg)\bigg(\langle\Delta x,\Delta y\big\rangle_{2}^{2}-\big\langle\Delta x,(\Delta y)^{flip}\big\rangle_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{E}}\big((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)

\displaystyle=\Cov\big(\tilde{f}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})-f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),\tilde{f}(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})-f(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\ldots+\Cov\big(f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),f(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})\big)

5.2
Pointwise Error Bound

As noted earlier, when
f
is a Gaussian field,
f_{x}
is a real-valued Gaussian random variable. Similarly,
\mathcal{E}(x)
is a Gaussian random variable since
\mathcal{E}
is a Gaussian field, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\big)\quad\forall\text{ }(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}

Since the error at any input is a mean-zero Gaussian random variable, its distribution is entirely determined by its standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})
.

Proposition 29

(Pointwise Variance of the Error Process)
.

Under the same setting as
19
, for fixed
(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the pointwise error
\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a mean-zero Gaussian random variable with its variance given as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}))

\displaystyle=2\bigg(h\big(0\big)-h\big(2\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)-4\bigg(h^{\prime}\big(0\big)-2h^{\prime}\big(\|x^{(1)}|_{2}^{2}+\|x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)\cdot\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+4\bigg(h^{\prime\prime}\big(0\big)-2h^{\prime\prime}\big(\|x^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)\cdot\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|x^{(1)}+x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

Proof.

The result follows directly from
28
by substituting
(y^{(1)},y^{(2)})=(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})
, then each term simplifies as follows:

1.

\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}=0
and
\|x-y^{flip}\|_{2}^{2}=\|x-x^{flip}\|_{2}^{2}=2\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

2.

\|y\|_{2}^{2}=\|x\|_{2}^{2}=\|x^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

3.

\langle x,y\rangle_{2}-\langle x,y^{flip}\rangle_{2}=\langle x,x-x^{flip}\rangle_{2}=|x^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}-\langle x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\rangle_{2}-\langle x^{(2)},x^{(1)}\rangle_{2}+\|x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}=\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

4.

\langle x,y\rangle_{2}^{2}-\langle x,y^{flip}\rangle_{2}^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle_{2}^{2}-\langle x,x^{flip}\rangle_{2}^{2}=\langle x,x-x^{flip}\rangle_{2}\cdot\langle x,x+x^{flip}\rangle_{2}=\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|x^{(1)}+x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}

∎

Theorem 30

(Equivalent Expressions defined over the plane)
.

The pointwise variance in the error process,
\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}
, can be expressed in terms of the magnitudes
r_{1},r_{2}
of, and the relative angle
\theta
between, the inputs.
Specifically,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(r_{1},r_{2},\theta)

\displaystyle=2\bigg(h\big(0\big)-h\big(2(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\theta)\big)\bigg)-4\cdot\bigg(h^{\prime}\big(0\big)-2h^{\prime}\big(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)\cdot\bigg(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\theta\bigg)

\displaystyle+4\cdot\bigg(h^{\prime\prime}\big(0\big)-2h^{\prime\prime}\big(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}\big)\bigg)\cdot\bigg((r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})^{2}-4r_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}cos^{2}\theta\bigg)

Proof.

The pointwise error variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}))
is determined by
x^{(1)},x^{(2)}
solely through their norms and the angle between them.
Therefore, we can adopt the coordinate system such that
x^{(1)}
is aligned with the first axis and
x^{(2)}
lies in the plane spanned by the first two axes without loss of generality.
Then,

\displaystyle x^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(r_{1},0,\dots,0)^{\top}

\displaystyle x^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(r_{2}\cos\theta,r_{2}\sin\theta,0,\dots,0)^{\top}\text{where }r_{1}=\|x^{(1)}\|_{2},r_{2}=\|x^{(2)}\|_{2},\text{and }\theta\text{ is the angle between them.}

The corresponding squared norm of the vector, its sum, and its difference are:

\displaystyle\|x\|_{2}^{2}=\|x^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}=r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\|x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}=r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\theta

\displaystyle\|x^{(1)}+x^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}=r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\theta

The result can be obtained by substituting this in the pointwise variance provided in Proposition
29
.
∎

Definition 23

(Pointwise Error Bound)
.

For the confidenc level
p\in(0,1)
, the pointwise error bound is defined as the
p
-quantile:

\displaystyle b^{*}_{p}\Big(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\Big):=inf\Bigg\{b\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}\bigg[\mathcal{E}\Big(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\Big)\leq b\bigg]>p\Bigg\}

Proposition 31

(Decomposition of the Pointwise Error Bound)
.

\displaystyle b^{*}_{p}\Big(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\cdot Q(p)\text{ where }(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}

\displaystyle Q:\text{Quantile Function for Standard Normal Variable}

Proof.

It follows immediately from the definition of a standard normal variable.
∎

Local expansions are accurate only within specific neighborhoods.
Whenever local expansions are used, it is essential to understand how the worst-case error varies with the size of the neighborhood. We will study this in two ways: first, by considering the maximal error over elliptical neighborhoods centered at
z
, and second, by examining the uniform error bound for randomly chosen inputs drawn from a Gaussian distribution centered at
z
.

5.3
Maximal Error over Ellipsoidal Region

Definition 24

(Maximal Error over the Region)
.

We define
b^{*}_{p}(A)
as the supremum of error over the region
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

\displaystyle i.e.,\text{ }b^{*}_{p}(A):=\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A}b^{*}_{p}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

Due to Proposition
31
, the following equality holds.

\displaystyle b^{*}_{p}(A)=Q(p)\cdot\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

Solving this optimization problem is challenging because the pointwise standard deviation of the error process depends on the function
h
, which determines the smoothness of the original function
f
, but has been left unspecified. So, we provide an upper bound instead of the exact maximum.

Remark
.

The supremum of the pointwise standard deviation,
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
, over a set is monotone increasing with respect to the set inclusion,

\displaystyle A\subseteq B\implies\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in B}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

(7)

The ellipsoidal region defined as
A:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:x^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}x\leq r^{2}\}
allows a simple closed-form expression for the upper bound. We use orthogonal invariance of
\mathcal{E}
as introduced in Theorem
27
and monotonicity with respect to set inclusion. Our strategy is summarized as follows: First, rotate the coordinate system such that
x^{(1)}
is aligned with the first axis and
x^{(2)}
lies in the plane spanned by the first two axes. Second, since the pointwise error is monotone increasing with respect to set inclusion, the supremum is attained when both axes are aligned with the two largest principal axes of the ellipsoid, or equivalently, the two largest eigenvectors of
\Sigma
.

Proposition 32
.

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

\displaystyle\text{ where }A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}:=\bigg\{(t_{1},t_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\frac{t_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}\leq r^{2}\bigg\},\quad A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}:=\bigg\{(t_{1},t_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\frac{t_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\leq r^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\text{ are the two largest eigenvalues of }\Sigma.

Proof.

The first inequality immediately follows from the above argument.
The second follows from set inclusion;
A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\subseteq A^{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1})}
since
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
.
∎

Proposition
32
reduces the original problem to a maximization problem over a circular set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then, we can use the polar expression of
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
provided in Theorem
30
.
The problem is then reduced to the following form.

Find:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq r_{1},r_{2}\leq R_{D}(p^{\prime}),\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}(r_{1},r_{2},\theta),\quad\text{ given }R_{D}(p^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\cdot r^{2}}

Construction of the Upper Bound for the Maximal Error over the Region

From Theorem

5.4
Uniform Error Bound for Randomly Distributed Inputs

In practice, inputs to the random field are often drawn from a probability distribution
\pi
.
We seek a reasonably tight upper bound that holds with high probability
1-\delta

Definition 26

(Uniform Upper Bound for Randomly Distributed Inputs)
.

\displaystyle b^{*}_{\delta}(\pi):=inf\Bigg\{b\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}\bigg[\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq b\bigg]>1-\delta\text{ where }x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pi\Bigg\}

However, this rarely admits concise analytic expressions. Alternatively, we consider the infimum among the maximal error values over every tolerance region that satisfies the confidence level

b^{*}_{(p,p^{\prime})}(\pi):=\inf_{A}\bigg\{\sup_{x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A}b_{p}^{*}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}):A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}\text{ satisfies }\mathbb{P}\left(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A\mid x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pi\right)>p^{\prime}\bigg\}\text{ }

(8)

Notation 27

(Highest Density Region among all Admissible Regions)

If it exists, we denote the smallest region that satisfies the constraint as follows:

\displaystyle A_{p^{\prime}}(\pi):=\bigcap\bigg\{A:\mathbb{P}\left(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\in A\mid x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pi\right)>p^{\prime}\bigg\}

Proposition 36
.

For any

Proof.

Let

and choose a set

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq b\mid x^{(1)}\in A,x^{(2)}\in A\right]

\displaystyle>p,\quad\mathbb{P}\left[x^{(1)}\in A,x^{(2)}\in A\mid x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\pi\right]>p^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq b\mid x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pi\bigg]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\leq b^{*}_{(p,p^{\prime})}(\pi)\right]>\delta\text{ where }x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}(\pi)

5.5

To summarize the uniform upper bound’s behavior for small and large regions, analyze its rate of decay as the radius of the region vanishes (local) and its rate of growth as the radius diverges (far).
Our characterization is based on the properties of the function
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
, provided in Notation
6
.
For the local regime analysis, we rely on the smoothness
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. For the far regime counterpart, the monotonicity
(-1)^{n}h^{(n)}(\cdot)\geq 0
for

is used.

Notation 28
.

We denote the expression for the upper bound with respect to
R>0

r_{1},r_{2}>0

\displaystyle\textbf{Local: }\phi(R)=2\bigg(h\big(0\big)-h\big(8R^{2}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\phi(R)\to-16h^{\prime}(0)R^{2}+O(R^{4})\text{ as }R\to 0

(Limiting Behavior of Uniform Error Bound)

At infinity, the upper bound grows quartically, with the constant determined by the second derivative of

\displaystyle\psi\to 2h\big(0\big)-16h^{\prime}\big(0\big)R^{2}+16h^{\prime\prime}\big(0\big)R^{4}\text{ as }R\to\infty

Theorem

\displaystyle h(0)=\text{Var}\bigg(u(z)\bigg)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{4}\text{Var}\bigg(\nabla^{(1)}_{i}u(z)\bigg)=-\frac{1}{4}\text{Var}\bigg(g(z^{*},z^{*})\bigg)\text{ }\text{ for some }i=1,\dots,D

In summary:

1.

In the local regime, the upper bound decays quadratically, and its magnitude is determined by the variance of the slope of the base field near the point of expansion
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The error bound is larger when the slope of the base field is more variable.

2.

In the far regime, grows quartically, and its magnitude is determined by the variance of the second derivatives of the base field near the point of expansion
z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The error bound is larger when the second derivatives of the base field are more variable.

Note that
h(0)
appears in the limit

R\to 0
. This observation is consistent with the behavior of generic local approximations to random fields whose entries are approximately independent at large distances. Locally, the quadratic approximation can replace the original random function, with the residual vanishing quadratically. When
R=0
, the approximation is exact, so the variance in the process does not appear in the error on small neighborhoods. In the far regime,
f(x,y)
is essentially independent of
f(z+v,z+w)
for any small

, so is independent of the derivatives used to generate the quadratic approximation
\tilde{f}
. Then, the variances add as if the process, and approximation, were sampled independently.

Example: Squared Exponential Kernel

The function

defines the squared exponential covariance function

(see

).
The length scale parameter

admits a straightforward interpretation. If
l
is small, then the covariance function decays rapidly. If
l
is large, then the covariance function deacys slowly, and sampled values remain correlated at long distances.

The derivatives of

,

, and
h^{\prime\prime}(0)=1/l^{4}
.
Small

and

, which implies a high variance in local curvature.
So, shrinking the length scale

increases the variability in the terms that parameterize the quadratic approximation, while reducing the distances needed to decorrelate sampled values of the process, producing an error bound that decays slowly as

approaches zero, and grows quickly as
R
diverges.

5.6
Asymptotic Analysis with respect to the Dimension

In high-dimensional settings, probabilistic results may yield surprising conclusions
[
15
]
. Accordingly, it is important to directly study how systems behave in the limit of large dimension. Here, we will investigate how the accuracy of quadratic approximations to Gaussian fields does, or does not, depend on the ambient dimension.

We’ve characterized the accuracy of the approximation with an error bound that holds with high probability over elliptical regions, and for Gaussian distributed inputs. Our construction involves two sources of randomness: one from randomly sampled from Gaussian field and the other from random inputs distributed according to the probability distribution
\pi
. The construction depends on three interrelated factors: the confidence level
\delta\in[0,1]
, the desired upper bound
b=b^{*}_{\delta}(\pi)
, and the admissible region
A=A_{p^{\prime}}(\pi)
. Each choice of these parameters leads to the following question:

1.

For fixed
\delta
and
b
, how does the admissible region vary?

2.

For fixed
\delta
and
A
, how does the uniform upper bound vary?

3.

For fixed
A
and
b
, how does the confidence level vary?

These are, in effect, equivalent questions. We illustrate the influence of the dimension
D
by focusing on the first.
Contextually, this question can be phrased as follows:
To keep a high-probability bound on the worst-case error constant as the dimension
D
grows, how must the admissible region vary?

If the admissible must shrink rapidly in dimension to maintain the same error control, then quadratic approximations will lose fidelity on neighborhoods of any fixed size, for sufficiently high-dimensional problems. Then, to employ a quadratic approximation in high dimension, an analyst would either need a tighter error bound, a different notion of error, or stronger regularity conditions on the process. In contrast, if the admissible region’s size is independent of the dimension, then we can shift our focus to guaranteeing that inputs stay within the region in high dimension.

Our construction of the uniform bound was based on Proposition
32
. Proposition
32
, allowed, without loss of generality, reduction from
D
dimensions to a standard two-dimensional optimization problem. As a result, the pointwise standard deviation in the error process can be bounded above by a constant that is independent of the dimension
D
for any compact region
A
. When
A
is contained in an ellipsoid, the bound depends only on the lengths of the first and second principal axes of the ellipsoid. It follows that, if we are only concerned with worst-case error bounds on fixed neighborhoods, then we do not need to vary the neighborhood’s radius to account for changes in the dimension
D
. In particular, we can use the same radius, even as
D
diverges.

This optimistic result is countered by the observation that, the volume of a
D
dimensional ball vanishes in the limit as
D
diverges. So, while the worst-case error is bounded above by a bound that holds with high probability, for a region with fixed radius, the neighborhood may need to vary in
D
if we sample inputs at random about the point of expansion, and hope to keep the same fraction of inputs inside the admissible region.

When
\pi
is Gaussian, the admissible region may be represented in two-dimensional Euclidean space by the circular region
R^{2}_{D}(p^{\prime})=\lambda_{1}\cdot\chi^{2}_{D}(\sqrt{p^{\prime}})
. This implies that the geometric constraint imposed by a fixed confidence level and a fixed upper bound restricts only the magnitude of the principal axis; that is, the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
.
The uniform upper bound remains constant at a fixed confidence level only if we consider a “smaller” admissible region, namely one for which the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\propto\big(\chi^{2}_{D}(\sqrt{p^{\prime}})\big)^{-1}

To characterize this trend, we introduce a simple closed-form approximation to the quantile function of the chi-squared distribution, originally proposed by R.A Fisher in
[
11
]
. Among various higher-precision approximations, this one yields an especially simple expression while remaining accurate for degrees of freedom greater than 30, thereby providing a convenient tool for understanding the overall behavior. Specifically, Fisher’s approximation for the
p
-quantile of the
\chi^{2}_{D}
distribution is

\chi^{2}_{D}(p)\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}(p)+\sqrt{2D-1}\right)^{2}.

(9)

Using Fisher’s approximation, we conclude that: to maintain the same confidence level for randomly selected inputs and the same uniform upper bound on the error as the dimension grows, the admissible ellipsoid must shrink, with the magnitude of its principal axis decreasing at the rate
O(D^{-1})
. This result is natural since, the expected squared length of a Gaussian random vector is proportional to the dimension
D
.

6
Conclusion

This manuscript provides a basic distributional characterization of local quadratic approximations to skew-symmetric Gaussian fields and their errors. We’ve established that the first and second order derivatives of a block-isotropic Gaussian are independent, and that the Hessian breaks into GOE and GSOE blocks. We have also shown that the worst-case error in the quadratic approximation on an elliptical region can be bounded from above, with high probability, by a bound that is quadratic in the radius of the region for small radii, then quartic for large radii. The rate of growth in the bound depends on the length scale of the kernel function, and, as a consequence, the smoothness of the process. Naturally, rougher processes produce larger errors for the same radii than smoother processes. Importantly, the maximal approximation error over a compact region is bounded from above with high probability by a bound whose value is independent of the underlying dimension. When the inputs are drawn at random from a multivariate normal, the error bound depends on the dimension only through the sampling of inputs to the approximation.

We propose this work as a reference for future studies based on local approximations to random, skew-symmetric functions.

Distributionally Orthogonal Invariance of Isotropic Gaussian Random Vectors and Matrices Consists of Independent Normal Entries

Lemma 40
.

(Orthogonal Invariance of mean-zero Isotropic Gaussian Random Vectors)
Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a Gaussian random vector with mean zero and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}I_{D}
for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. Then, for any orthogonal transformation
Q:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
,

\displaystyle Qv\overset{d}{=}v

Proof.

Qv
also satisfies the definition of a multivariate Gaussian distribution, and is trivially mean-zero. Since they are Gaussian, it suffices to show that they have the same covariance matrices, as follows.

\displaystyle\Cov(Qv,Qv)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[(Qv)(Qv)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Qvv^{\mathsf{T}}Q^{\mathsf{T}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=Q\mathbb{E}\bigg[vv^{\mathsf{T}}\bigg]Q^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{2}I_{D})QQ^{\mathsf{T}}=\sigma^{2}I_{D}

∎

Lemma 41
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
be a random matrix whose entries are independent normal random variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. Then, for any orthogonal transformation
Q:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Then its distribution is invariant under conjugation by
Q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle Q^{\mathsf{T}}AQ\overset{d}{=}A

Proof.

We show that they have the same Probability Density Function.
Being a random matrix with independent normal entries, the probability density function of
A
is given as

\displaystyle f(A)\propto\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{D}A_{i,j}^{2}\bigg)=\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}}A)\bigg)

For the conjugated matrix
Q^{\mathsf{T}}AQ
, we have

\displaystyle f(Q^{\mathsf{T}}AQ)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}((Q^{\mathsf{T}}AQ)^{\mathsf{T}}(Q^{\mathsf{T}}AQ))\bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(Q^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}QQ^{\mathsf{T}}AQ)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}}A)\bigg)

∎
