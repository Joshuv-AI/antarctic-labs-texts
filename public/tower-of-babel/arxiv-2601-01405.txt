Exact and Approximate Range Queries in Ball Mapper
Jay-Anne Bulauan, John Rick Manzanares

Abstract
Ball Mapper summarizes a finite metric dataset by covering the sample with closed balls centered at selected landmarks and connecting landmarks whose balls share observations. Its construction therefore depends critically on repeated fixed radius range queries, yet the effect of replacing exact queries by approximate search has not been systematically characterized. We formulate Ball Mapper through an abstract range query procedure that separates the mathematical construction from the search backend used to realize it. Under fixed ordering, exact procedures preserve the landmark sequence, cover, graph, and membership-based colorings. For approximate procedures, we derive deterministic bounds on covering radius and landmark separation under additive and multiplicative query errors, prove inclusions for the induced nerve, characterize edge survival through witness redundancy for conservative approximations, and bound perturbations of mean vertex colorings. The accompanying implementation provides independent exact reference backends together with exhaustive and approximate search methods under a common closed ball convention. Experiments on Gaussian, mixture, and noisy curve data across three seeds show that approximation fidelity depends strongly on geometry and that edges supported by multiple witnesses are substantially more robust to missed memberships. At 20,000 observations, the approximate indexes did not outperform exhaustive FAISS Flat search. The results therefore establish a framework for controlled approximation rather than a universal speed advantage, and identify the geometric and combinatorial quantities that govern when approximate range search preserves the Ball Mapper summary.



Keywords:

topological data analysis; metric indexing; high-dimensional search;
simplicial complexes

1
Introduction

Ball Mapper is a method for constructing an interpretable graph summary of a finite metric dataset
(
Dłotko, 2019
)
. Given a scale parameter
\varepsilon>0
, the method selects representative data points, called landmarks, whose
\varepsilon
-balls cover the dataset. Each landmark becomes a vertex, and two vertices are connected when their corresponding balls contain a common data point. The resulting graph gives a coarse description of the organization of the data and can reveal clusters, branches, transitions, and regions with distinct values of an auxiliary variable.

The appeal of Ball Mapper lies partly in its metric formulation. It requires only a dataset, a distance function, and a scale parameter, and it does not assume a particular coordinate representation or probability model. This makes it suitable for exploratory analysis of structured and high-dimensional data. However, the same formulation also creates a computational bottleneck. During the greedy construction of the landmark set, each newly selected landmark requires a range query at a fixed radius to identify all data points lying within distance
\varepsilon
. A direct implementation performs this query by comparing the landmark with every point in the dataset.

The repeated range query computation can dominate the cost of Ball Mapper. The difficulty increases with the number of observations, because more distances must be evaluated, and with the ambient dimension, because each distance computation becomes more expensive. Moreover, geometric indexing methods can lose pruning efficiency in high dimensions, reflecting the broader curse of dimensionality
(
Bellman, 1957
)
. Thus, an efficient implementation of Ball Mapper should account for both the metric structure of the data and the computational cost of evaluating many distances.

The computational methods considered here fall into three broad classes: exact spatial indexes, exhaustive vector search, and approximate vector indexes. For exact spatial search, we consider ball trees and
k
-dimensional trees alongside a direct brute-force reference. A ball tree recursively partitions the dataset into bounded subsets and uses metric bounds to exclude subsets that cannot intersect a query ball
(
Omohundro, 1989
)
. A
k
-dimensional tree instead partitions the ambient coordinate space by recursive axis-aligned splits and prunes regions whose bounding boxes cannot intersect the query ball
(
Bentley, 1975
)
. These structures exploit different aspects of the data geometry, and their effectiveness depends on the extent to which the resulting partitions permit queries to discard observations without individual distance evaluations.

For dense Euclidean data, we also consider Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS), a library for efficient vector search
(
Douze et al., 2025
)
. A Flat index performs exhaustive search: every database vector remains a candidate, but distance computations are implemented using optimized vectorized kernels, multithreading, and cache-conscious memory access. It therefore provides a useful exhaustive backend against which indexed methods can be compared. Because the FAISS computations use finite-precision vector representations, however, points lying very close to the prescribed radius require particular care when exact agreement with the closed-ball definition is required.

We further consider non-exhaustive FAISS indexes based on inverted-file and hierarchical navigable small-world search. An inverted-file (IVF) index partitions the dataset into coarse cells and searches only selected cells, whereas a hierarchical navigable small-world (HNSW) index explores a multilevel proximity graph to locate promising regions of the dataset. Both methods can reduce the number of observations examined by a query, but they may also omit points that belong to the exact query ball. From the perspective of Ball Mapper, such omissions are consequential: they can change ball memberships, alter the greedy landmark sequence, remove witnesses of intersections, and thereby modify the resulting graph and its membership-based colorings.

The effect of approximate search on Ball Mapper requires a range query analysis rather than a conventional nearest neighbor analysis. Ball Mapper requires the set of all observations lying within a prescribed radius, not only a sufficiently close observation or a fixed number of nearest neighbors. Approximation is therefore described through inclusions between exact and approximate range sets. Under additive and multiplicative range errors, we obtain deterministic bounds on the covering radius and minimum separation of the resulting landmark set. For a fixed landmark set, we further derive inclusions between approximate and exact nerves at nearby scales, characterize the survival of graph edges through witness redundancy, and bound perturbations of membership-based vertex colorings.

The empirical part of the paper has three purposes. First, we compare ball tree and exhaustive FAISS Flat implementations of the range query step against the existing
pyBallMapper
implementation
(
Gurnari,
)
. Second, we validate the closed ball convention and the resulting Ball Mapper construction against independent double precision floating point reference and exact spatial backends, including brute force, ball tree, and
k
-dimensional tree. Third, we study the structural effects of approximate range search using IVF and HNSW. The approximation experiments focus not primarily on speed, but on fidelity of ball memberships, landmark selection, witness counts, graph edges and connectivity, and membership based vertex colorings.

We use the standard mathematical and computer science term
oracle
for an idealized procedure that returns the answer to a specified query. In this article, a
range query oracle
returns all observations lying in a requested metric ball. A search backend is a concrete computational realization of such an oracle. This terminology separates the mathematical range set from the data structure, numerical representation, or software library used to compute it.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

1.

We formulate Ball Mapper construction through an explicit range query oracle and prove that, under fixed observation ordering and tie breaking, any exact backend produces the same landmark sequence, cover, graph, and membership based colorings.

2.

We analyze the computational roles of exhaustive and tree based range search
and benchmark BallTree and exhaustive FAISS Flat implementations against
pyBallMapper
.

3.

For additive and multiplicative range errors, we derive deterministic bounds on the covering radius and minimum landmark separation. For a fixed landmark set, we further establish inclusions between approximate and exact nerves at nearby scales, with corresponding consequences for the Ball Mapper graph.

4.

For conservative approximate search, we characterize edge survival in terms of witness redundancy and derive a perturbation bound for mean vertex colorings.

5.

We show empirically that sensitivity to approximate range search depends strongly on data geometry and witness redundancy. At the tested scale of 20,000 observations, the approximate indexes do not provide a runtime advantage over exhaustive FAISS Flat search.

The objective is not to alter the definition of Ball Mapper, but to separate the mathematical construction from the computational mechanism used to realize its metric balls and to make the consequences of approximate range search mathematically explicit.

2
Ball Mapper

This section introduces the Ball Mapper. We first present the theoretical background underpinning the method, then describe the construction of the cover and associated graph. Finally, we discuss the coloring schemes used to encode and analyze auxiliary data.

2.1
Cover Construction

To describe the Ball Mapper algorithm rigorously, we first outline the mathematical setting in which it operates. First, we model the dataset as a metric space. A
metric space

(X,d)
is a set
X
together with a
distance function

d:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying, for all
x,y,z\in X
,

1.

d(x,y)\geq 0
,

2.

d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
,

3.

d(x,y)=d(y,x)
, and

4.

d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.

Given a metric structure on the dataset, the next step is to select a finite set of representative points that captures the geometry of the space at a prescribed scale. This is achieved through the notion of an
\varepsilon
-net.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. In this article, for a real number
\varepsilon>0
, an
\varepsilon
-net
of
X
means a subset
N\subset X
satisfying the closed ball convention used throughout this work:

1.

For every point
x\in X
, there exists
n\in N
with

d(x,n)\leq\varepsilon
.

2.

For any pair of distinct points
n_{1},n_{2}\in N
, we have

d\left(n_{1},n_{2}\right)>\varepsilon
.

Elements of
N
are called
landmarks
.

An
\varepsilon
-net naturally induces a collection of neighborhoods by associating to each landmark a ball of radius
\varepsilon
. Specifically, an
\varepsilon
-net
\left\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\right\}
yields a collection of
closed balls
of radius
\varepsilon

B\left(c_{i},\varepsilon\right)=\left\{y\in X~|~d(c_{i},y)\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

centered at the landmarks
c_{i}
. The union of these balls is said to
cover
the metric space
X
.

The parameter
\varepsilon
controls the scale at which the metric structure of the dataset is examined. Smaller values of
\varepsilon
produce covers with more balls of smaller radius, yielding a finer representation of local geometric features. Larger values result in coarser covers in which local variations are aggregated. In practice,
\varepsilon
is chosen to balance resolution against robustness to sampling noise.

Given a cover of the dataset, a standard way to encode the pattern of overlaps among the covering sets is through an abstract simplicial complex. This construction records not only pairwise intersections but also higher-order overlaps in a combinatorial form.

An
(abstract) simplicial complex
is a pair
(V,S)
where
V
is a set of
vertices
, and
S
is a collection of nonempty finite subsets of
V
satisfying the following:

1.

For every
v\in V
,
\left\{v\right\}\in S
.

2.

If
\sigma\in S
and
\tau\subset\sigma
, then
\tau\in S
.

Elements
\sigma
of
S
are called
k
-simplices
where the
dimension

k=|\sigma|-1
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
nerve
of the cover induced by an
\varepsilon
-net is the abstract simplicial complex defined as follows. The vertex set consists of the landmarks
\left\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\right\}
. A finite subset
\{c_{i_{0}},\ldots,c_{i_{m}}\}
of landmarks spans an
m
-simplex if the corresponding closed balls of radius
\varepsilon
have a nonempty common intersection, that is,

\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}B\left(c_{i_{j}},\varepsilon\right)\cap X\neq\varnothing.

A
k
-skeleton
of a simplicial complex
S
is a simplicial complex containing all simplices of
S
whose dimension is at most
k
. The
Ball Mapper graph
is the
1
-skeleton of the nerve of an
\varepsilon
-net. The
0
-simplices (or
vertices
) are precisely the landmarks, while the
1
-simplices (or
edges
) between two vertices are formed when the associated landmarks have a nonempty intersection.

While higher-order simplices encode multiway intersections among balls, restricting attention to the
1
-skeleton yields a graph representation that is computationally efficient and easy to interpret. This representation preserves the dominant pairwise connectivity structure and is well-suited to exploratory analysis and visualization.

The classical Nerve Lemma
(
Borsuk, 1948
)
identifies conditions under which the nerve of a cover has the homotopy type of the covered space. Ball Mapper instead forms a cover of the observed finite dataset and retains only the
1
-skeleton. We therefore do not invoke the Nerve Lemma as a homotopy-recovery guarantee here; the Ball Mapper graph is treated as a multiscale descriptive summary of observed overlap structure.

2.2
Practical Implementation

Ball Mapper constructs a cover of a finite dataset
X
by computing an
\varepsilon
-net using a greedy algorithm. The procedure is as follows:

1.

Initialize an empty set
N
to hold the landmarks.

2.

While there exist uncovered points in the dataset:

•

Select an uncovered point
x
from the dataset.

•

Add
x
to
N
.

•

Mark all points within distance
\varepsilon
of
x
as covered.

A point is said to be
covered
if it lies within at least one ball centered at a landmark in
N
; otherwise, it is
uncovered
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that the greedy procedure marks precisely the points in the closed range set
R_{\varepsilon}(l)=\{x\in X:d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon\}
whenever a landmark
l
is selected. Then the final landmark set
L
is
\varepsilon
-separated under the strict convention
d(l_{i},l_{j})>\varepsilon
, and is maximal with respect to this property. Consequently,
L
is an
\varepsilon
-cover of
X
.

Proof.

If
l_{i}
is selected before
l_{j}
, then
l_{j}
is still uncovered when it is selected. Hence,
d(l_{i},l_{j})>\varepsilon
, which proves separation. At termination, every point not in
L
has been covered by a selected landmark. Thus, for every
x\in X\setminus L
, there exists
l\in L
such that
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon
. Therefore, no further point can be added to
L
while preserving
\varepsilon
-separation, and the same statement gives the
\varepsilon
-cover property.
∎

This proposition makes explicit that the greedy Ball Mapper landmarks form an inclusion-maximal
\varepsilon
-separated set and simultaneously an
\varepsilon
-cover. The particular maximal set depends on the processing order, but the separation and covering conclusions do not.

This greedy construction depends on the order in which points are processed, and consequently the resulting set of landmarks is not unique.
An alternative approach is
farthest point sampling

(
Gonzalez, 1985
)
. It can be made deterministic after fixing the initial landmark and a deterministic rule for resolving distance ties; without these choices, the resulting landmark sequence need not be unique. The procedure is as follows:

1.

Select an initial landmark
x_{0}\in X
.

2.

Initialize the current landmark set as
L=\{x_{0}\}
.

3.

Find a point

x^{\ast}=\arg\max\limits_{x\in X}\min\limits_{l\in L}d(x,l)

farthest from the current landmark set.

4.

Add
x^{\ast}
to
L
if

\min\limits_{l\in L}d\left(x^{\ast},l\right)>\varepsilon
.

5.

Repeat until no such point exists.

For a fixed initial landmark and deterministic tie-breaking rule, this procedure yields a reproducible landmark sequence. Different initial landmarks or tie resolutions can nevertheless produce different
\varepsilon
-nets. The strict condition in Step 4 is consistent with the closed ball convention used above: a point at distance exactly
\varepsilon
is already covered and is therefore not eligible to become a new landmark. The procedure is typically more computationally expensive than the one-pass greedy construction and is therefore less commonly used in large-scale applications.

2.3
Coloring

In applications, the Ball Mapper is typically used as a scaffold for visualizing auxiliary information associated with the data, such as response variables, labels, or summary statistics. By aggregating such quantities over balls, one can examine how they vary across regions of the data space while respecting its metric structure.

Let
M
be a Ball Mapper graph with landmark set
L
. A
coloring
of
M
is a function
c:L\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
c(l)
encodes aggregated information associated with the closed ball centered at
l\in L
.

Let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function defined on the dataset
X
. For example,
f
can be some response variable or feature. Given a landmark
l_{i}
of
M
, let
X_{i}=X\cap B\left(l_{i},\varepsilon\right)
. A common choice of coloring is given by averaging:

c\left(l_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{|X_{i}|}\sum_{x\in X_{i}}f(x).

This assigns to each vertex a summary statistic of the data points contained in its corresponding ball.

To understand how coloring behaves across the graph, we impose a mild regularity assumption on
f
. Note that
\mathbb{R}
can be viewed as a metric space with the Euclidean distance function
|\cdot|
.

Definition 2.4
.

The function
f
is said to be
k
-Lipschitz continuous
if, for some constant
k
,

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq kd(x,y)

for all
x,y\in X
.

For a Lipschitz continuous function, the difference in the images of any two points is at most proportional to the distance between the points.

The preceding Lipschitz assumption permits a precise analysis of colorings aggregated over balls, but the bound depends on how the color is formed. To include the mean, weighted mean, median, trimmed mean, minimum, and maximum in one framework, it is convenient to use convex aggregation.

Definition 2.5
.

For a nonempty ball membership set
X_{i}
, a
convex ball coloring
is any quantity of the form

c(l_{i})=\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\alpha_{i,x}f(x).

The weights satisfy
\alpha_{i,x}\geq 0
for every
x\in X_{i}
, and
\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\alpha_{i,x}=1
. The ordinary mean is obtained with
\alpha_{i,x}=|X_{i}|^{-1}
. Because
X_{i}
is finite, the minimum and maximum correspond to placing unit weight on a minimizing or maximizing point, and a median or trimmed mean can likewise be represented by suitable nonnegative weights.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be
k
-Lipschitz and let
c
be a convex ball coloring. Then

|c(l_{i})-f(l_{i})|\leq k\varepsilon.

Proof.

Since
l_{i}\in X_{i}
and
d(x,l_{i})\leq\varepsilon
for every
x\in X_{i}
, Lipschitz continuity gives
|f(x)-f(l_{i})|\leq k\varepsilon
. Therefore,

|c(l_{i})-f(l_{i})|=\left|\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\alpha_{i,x}(f(x)-f(l_{i}))\right|\leq\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\alpha_{i,x}|f(x)-f(l_{i})|\leq k\varepsilon.

∎

Corollary 2.7
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.6
, for every
x\in X_{i}
,

|f(x)-c(l_{i})|\leq 2k\varepsilon.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

|f(x)-c(l_{i})|\leq|f(x)-f(l_{i})|+|f(l_{i})-c(l_{i})|\leq 2k\varepsilon.

∎

The factor
2
is necessary for a general aggregated coloring. Although every point in
X_{i}
lies within
\varepsilon
of the landmark, two points in the same ball can be almost
2\varepsilon
apart. Thus, a bound of
k\varepsilon
for
|f(x)-c(l_{i})|
is not valid without an additional restriction such as choosing
c(l_{i})=f(l_{i})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
f
be
k
-Lipschitz and let
c
be a convex ball coloring. If
(l_{i},l_{j})
is an edge of the Ball Mapper graph, then

|c(l_{i})-c(l_{j})|\leq 4k\varepsilon.

If the landmark value coloring
c(l)=f(l)
is used instead, the sharper bound

|c(l_{i})-c(l_{j})|\leq 2k\varepsilon

holds.

Proof.

Let
x\in X_{i}\cap X_{j}
be a witness for the edge. Then

d(l_{i},l_{j})\leq d(l_{i},x)+d(x,l_{j})\leq 2\varepsilon.

For a convex ball coloring, Proposition
2.6
and Lipschitz continuity give

\displaystyle|c(l_{i})-c(l_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq|c(l_{i})-f(l_{i})|+|f(l_{i})-f(l_{j})|+|f(l_{j})-c(l_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq k\varepsilon+2k\varepsilon+k\varepsilon=4k\varepsilon.

If
c(l)=f(l)
, only the middle term remains.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

If the graph distance between
l_{i}
and
l_{j}
is
r<\infty
, then every convex ball coloring generated by a
k
-Lipschitz function satisfies

|c(l_{i})-c(l_{j})|\leq 2k\varepsilon(r+1).

For landmark value coloring, the bound improves to
2k\varepsilon r
.

Proof.

A path of
r
edges gives
d(l_{i},l_{j})\leq 2r\varepsilon
by repeated use of the triangle inequality through edge witnesses. Applying Proposition
2.6
at the two endpoints gives

|c(l_{i})-c(l_{j})|\leq k\varepsilon+2rk\varepsilon+k\varepsilon=2k\varepsilon(r+1).

For landmark value coloring, Lipschitz continuity gives the sharper bound directly.
∎

These results formalize two distinct approximation statements. The vertex color approximates the value of
f
at its landmark to order
\varepsilon
, while the graph controls how rapidly colors can change along edges and paths. Statistics such as variance and range are not convex aggregations on the original scale of
f
and therefore require separate bounds.

When
f
is not Lipschitz, the preceding deterministic variation bounds no longer apply. Sharp color transitions may then occur, and the Ball Mapper graph can be used heuristically to identify regions of qualitatively different behavior. This may happen, for example, when
f
represents class labels in classification problems. In such cases, robust or categorical summaries such as the mode or majority vote are often more appropriate than averaging-based colorings.

In both perspectives, the choice of coloring determines the role played by the Ball Mapper. Smooth colorings emphasize geometric continuity, while nonsmooth colorings expose boundaries and regimes. Neither viewpoint is preferable a priori; each answers a different question about the data.

Lastly, the coloring depends on the choice of landmarks. The greedy
\varepsilon
-net construction is order-dependent and may yield non-unique landmark sets, resulting in non-consistent colorings. Farthest point sampling reduces this source of variability only after its initial landmark and tie-breaking rule are fixed; under those additional choices it is reproducible, but it is not uniquely determined by the data and
\varepsilon
alone.

3
Range Query Backends

Constructing an
\varepsilon
-net requires repeatedly identifying all points within distance
\varepsilon
of a selected landmark. This operation is a
range query
. It is useful to regard the Ball Mapper algorithm as using a range query oracle independently of the underlying search data structure. The mathematical algorithm asks only for the set
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
; brute force, metric trees, vector search libraries, or approximate candidate generators are computational realizations of that oracle. This separation is central to the analysis below: exact backends may change runtime but not the Ball Mapper object, whereas approximate backends change the oracle itself and therefore require stability guarantees.

In the following theorem, we fix the data order, the scale
\varepsilon
, and all tie-breaking rules used by the Ball Mapper implementation.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose two implementations return exactly the same range set
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
for every query point
q
encountered during construction. Then they select the same landmarks in the same order, produce identical ball memberships, and therefore produce the same nerve, Ball Mapper graph, and any coloring computed solely from those memberships.

Proof.

Let
U_{k}^{A}
and
U_{k}^{B}
denote the sets of uncovered observations after
k
landmark selections for implementations
A
and
B
, respectively. We prove by induction on
k
that the two implementations have selected the same first
k
landmarks and that

U_{k}^{A}=U_{k}^{B}.

For
k=0
, no landmarks have been selected and no observations have been covered, so

U_{0}^{A}=U_{0}^{B}=X.

Suppose the claim holds after
k
selections. Since
U_{k}^{A}=U_{k}^{B}
, the common deterministic selection rule chooses the same next landmark
q
in both implementations. By hypothesis, both implementations return exactly the same range set
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
. Consequently,

U_{k+1}^{A}=U_{k}^{A}\setminus R_{\varepsilon}(q)=U_{k}^{B}\setminus R_{\varepsilon}(q)=U_{k+1}^{B}.

Thus the
(k+1)
-st landmark is the same and the uncovered sets remain equal. The induction therefore gives the same landmark sequence for the entire construction.

For each selected landmark, the two implementations also return the same range set, so their ball memberships are identical. The nerve is determined entirely by intersections among these membership sets, and the Ball Mapper graph is its
1
-skeleton. Hence, both are identical. Any coloring determined solely by the membership sets and fixed observation data is likewise identical.
∎

Thus, an exact acceleration changes only the computational route, not the mathematical Ball Mapper object. In floating-point implementations, this statement should be interpreted relative to a fixed numerical comparison rule at the radius boundary.

The associated implementation in this study represents the mathematical closed ball predicate
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon
using an explicit floating-point boundary convention. Let
\mathbb{F}
denote the set of finite numbers representable in the floating-point format used by a given comparison, and let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{F}
. Define

\varepsilon^{+}=\min\{r\in\mathbb{F}:r>\varepsilon\},

that is, the smallest representable floating-point number strictly larger than
\varepsilon
. In the implementation, this adjacent representable value is obtained by a standard
nextafter
operation directed toward
+\infty
. Since no representable floating-point number lies strictly between
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{+}
, a strict comparison of the form

d(x,l)<\varepsilon^{+}

is equivalent, at the level of the represented values, to the inclusive comparison
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon
.

For ordinary radius comparisons, the implementation constructs
\varepsilon^{+}
in double precision. FAISS Euclidean range search instead operates on single-precision squared Euclidean distances and uses a strict threshold. Accordingly, the implementation first represents
\varepsilon^{2}
in single precision and then replaces that value by the next larger representable single-precision number before issuing the FAISS range query. Exact backends are subjected to an explicit closed ball filtering step so that the same mathematical membership criterion is enforced throughout.

This outward rounding is a device for representing a non-strict boundary with an interface that uses a strict floating-point comparison. It does not change the intended mathematical radius. It also does not remove numerical error in the computed distances themselves, and therefore does not by itself guarantee agreement between computations performed in different floating-point precisions.

3.1
Exact Backend Implementations

The implementation uses complementary exact strategies. Scikit-learn BallTree accelerates metric range queries by pruning tree nodes whose metric bounds cannot intersect the query ball. FAISS Flat instead performs an exhaustive scan over dense vectors but reduces the constant cost of distance evaluation through optimized vector and linear algebra kernels. The double-precision floating-point brute force backend serves as the transparent reference oracle, while SciPy cKDTree provides an independent exact Euclidean tree implementation
(
Virtanen et al., 2020
)
. The Ball Mapper construction is independent of these choices; only the backend determines the available metric, numerical representation, and computational strategy. Additional implementation details are given in Appendix
A
.

3.2
Range Query Complexity

Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
be the dataset. Let
L\subseteq X
denote the set of landmarks selected by the greedy Ball Mapper construction, and let
m=|L|
be the number of selected landmarks. Since the landmarks are observations from
X
, the inequality
m\leq n
always hold.

For a landmark
l\in L
, a radius query asks for all observations whose Euclidean distance from
l
does not exceed the prescribed radius
\varepsilon
. The Euclidean distance between
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})
and
l=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{D})
is

d(x,l)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}(x_{j}-l_{j})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

A
point-to-point distance evaluation
means computing this distance, or an equivalent quantity such as its square, for one pair of observations. Computing a Euclidean distance requires examining all
D
coordinates. Its arithmetic cost therefore grows proportionally to
D
.

We use standard asymptotic notation to describe how computational cost grows as the problem size increases. For two nonnegative quantities
f
and
g
, the notation
f=O(g)
means that, apart from a constant factor,
f
is eventually no larger than
g
. Thus
O(g)
gives an asymptotic upper bound. The notation
f=\Theta(g)
means that
f
is bounded both above and below by constant multiples of
g
for sufficiently large problem sizes. Thus
\Theta(g)
describes the order of growth rather than only an upper bound. The constants suppressed by this notation do not depend on the problem-size parameters under consideration.

Since one range query is issued for each selected landmark, the cost of the range query stage depends on both the number
n
of observations and the number
m
of landmarks.

Proposition 3.2
.

An exhaustive implementation evaluates the distance from each of the
m
landmarks to all
n
observations. It therefore performs exactly
mn
point-to-point distance evaluations, and hence
\Theta(mn)
such evaluations. For dense Euclidean vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, each distance evaluation requires
\Theta(D)
arithmetic work, so the total arithmetic cost is
\Theta(mnD)
. Since
m\leq n
, this cost is at most
O(n^{2}D)
.

Proof.

Each of the
m
selected landmarks generates one exhaustive range query. For a fixed landmark, an exhaustive query compares that landmark with all
n
observations, giving
n
distance evaluations. Repeating this for all
m
landmarks gives
mn
evaluations.

For Euclidean data in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, evaluating the distance between two points requires processing their
D
coordinate pairs. The amount of arithmetic per distance is therefore proportional to
D
, giving total work proportional to
mnD
.

Finally, every landmark belongs to the original dataset, so at most
n
landmarks can be selected. Substituting the bound
m\leq n
gives an upper bound proportional to
n^{2}D
.
∎

A flat FAISS index changes the implementation constants through vectorization, blocking, optimized kernels, and parallelism, but it does not change this exhaustive asymptotic count. Its advantage is therefore primarily hardware efficiency. By contrast, a metric tree can avoid many pointwise evaluations when node bounds exclude large subsets of the data. Nevertheless, the worst-case cost of a tree range query is still
O(n)
, so without geometric assumptions the worst-case Ball Mapper range query cost remains
O(mn)
distance evaluations. This distinction is important: empirical speedups and empirical scaling exponents quantify observed performance, whereas asymptotic complexity statements require guarantees that hold over a specified class of inputs.

3.3
Backend Abstraction and Reference Implementation

The accompanying implementation exposes a common range query backend interface. Each backend reports whether it is intended to be exact or approximate, its metric and numerical data type, its execution device, whether batched queries are supported, and whether exhaustive distance vectors are available. The latter capability is required by farthest point sampling, which needs distances from each selected landmark to every observation; approximate indexes that return only candidates are therefore excluded from farthest point sampling when they cannot provide distances to all observations.

The exact CPU validation uses three independent implementations: a transparent double-precision floating-point brute force oracle, scikit-learn BallTree, and SciPy cKDTree for Euclidean data. FAISS Flat is exhaustive over its stored single-precision floating-point vectors and is used as the optimized dense vector backend, but comparisons to the double-precision floating-point reference retain an explicit comparison of memberships near the radius threshold because single-precision floating-point representation can differ for points whose distances from the query lie very close to
\varepsilon
. Approximate adapters include FAISS IVF and HNSW. The software also provides optional hnswlib and NVIDIA cuVS adapters through the same interface; these adapters are part of the implementation architecture but are not included in the experiments reported in this article.

This design has two purposes. First, it makes exactness testable independently of a particular library. Second, it represents the theoretical range query oracle through a common software interface: new backends can be added without changing landmark selection, graph construction, coloring, or the validation routines.

4
Stability Under Approximate Range Queries

FAISS is not intrinsically approximate. Whether a FAISS range query is exact or approximate depends on the index used and on its configuration. In the Ball Mapper construction, this distinction is important because the algorithm requires all points lying inside a prescribed ball, not merely a fixed number of nearest neighbors.

Throughout this section, let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space and let
\varepsilon>0
be the Ball Mapper scale parameter. We now formalize the exact and approximate range query oracles introduced in the Introduction.

Definition 4.1
.

For
q\in X
and
r>0
, the
exact range set
centered at
q
with radius
r
is

R_{r}(q):=\{x\in X\mid d(q,x)\leq r\}.

The map
R_{r}:X\to 2^{X}
given by
q\mapsto R_{r}(q)
is called the
exact range oracle
at radius
r
.

An exact range oracle returns precisely the points in
X
contained in the closed ball of radius
r
centered at the query point. By contrast, an approximate index may return a set that differs from
R_{r}(q)
.

Definition 4.2
.

An
approximate range oracle
at radius
r
is a map
\widetilde{R}_{r}:X\to 2^{X}
given by
q\mapsto\widetilde{R}_{r}(q)
, where
\widetilde{R}_{r}(q)
is the set returned by an approximate range query procedure.

The purpose of this section is to describe how differences between the exact range set
R_{r}(q)
and the approximate range set
\widetilde{R}_{r}(q)
affect the Ball Mapper cover and graph.

4.1
Cover Approximation

We first consider an additive error model. This model assumes that the approximate query is exact except possibly for points whose distance from the query point lies close to the requested radius.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
0\leq\delta<r
. An approximate range oracle
\widetilde{R}_{r}
is said to be
\delta
-accurate
at radius
r
if, for every
q\in X
,

R_{r-\delta}(q)\subseteq\widetilde{R}_{r}(q)\subseteq R_{r+\delta}(q).

Equivalently, every point at distance at most
r-\delta
is returned by the approximate query, and no point at distance greater than
r+\delta
is returned. Only points in the boundary band
r-\delta<d(q,x)\leq r+\delta
can be ambiguous. This shows that a
\delta
-accurate oracle can make errors only near the boundary of the query ball.

Because
X
is finite, this boundary description yields an exact robustness criterion whenever the chosen scale is separated from all observed pairwise distances. Define the global radius margin

\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\min_{q,x\in X}|d(q,x)-\varepsilon|.

If no observed distance equals
\varepsilon
, then
\gamma_{\varepsilon}>0
.

Proposition 4.4
.

If a range oracle is
\delta
-accurate at radius
\varepsilon
and
0\leq\delta<\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, then, for every
q\in X
,

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)=R_{\varepsilon}(q).

Consequently, with fixed data order and tie-breaking, the approximate and exact greedy Ball Mapper constructions are identical.

Proof.

The inequality
\delta<\gamma_{\varepsilon}
implies that no point has distance from
q
in the closed boundary band
[\varepsilon-\delta,\varepsilon+\delta]
. Hence,

R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(q)=R_{\varepsilon}(q)=R_{\varepsilon+\delta}(q).

The defining inclusions of
\delta
-accuracy force
\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)
to equal this common set. The final statement follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Thus, approximate numerical search can still recover the exact Ball Mapper whenever its uncertainty band is smaller than the empirical margin around
\varepsilon
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\rho>0
. A subset
L\subseteq X
is a
\rho
-cover
of
X
if, for every
x\in X
, there exists
l\in L
such that

d(x,l)\leq\rho.

For
\alpha>0
, the set
L
is said to be
\alpha
-separated
if, for every pair of distinct points
l,l^{\prime}\in L
,

d(l,l^{\prime})>\alpha.

An
\varepsilon
-net is simultaneously an
\varepsilon
-cover and an
\varepsilon
-separated set.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose that the greedy Ball Mapper landmark construction is run using a
\delta
-accurate approximate range oracle at radius
\varepsilon
, where
0\leq\delta<\varepsilon
. Let
L\subseteq X
be the computed landmark set. Then the following are satisfied:

1.

L
is an
(\varepsilon+\delta)
-cover of
X
.

2.

L
is
(\varepsilon-\delta)
-separated.

Proof.

Every point of
X
is either selected as a landmark or marked as covered by a previous landmark. If
x
is marked as covered by a landmark
l
, then

x\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l).

Since the oracle is
\delta
-accurate,

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)\subseteq R_{\varepsilon+\delta}(l).

Hence,
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon+\delta
. If
x
is itself selected as a landmark, then
d(x,x)=0\leq\varepsilon+\delta
. Therefore,
L
is an
(\varepsilon+\delta)
-cover.

For separation, let
l,l^{\prime}\in L
be distinct landmarks, and suppose that
l
was selected before
l^{\prime}
. Since
l^{\prime}
was uncovered when it was selected,
l^{\prime}\notin\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)
. Using the inclusion
R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(l)\subseteq\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)
,
we obtain

l^{\prime}\notin R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(l).

Therefore,
d(l,l^{\prime})>\varepsilon-\delta
. Consequently,
L
is
(\varepsilon-\delta)
-separated.
∎

The theorem shows that the output of the approximate greedy construction need not be an exact
\varepsilon
-net. However, if
\delta
is small relative to
\varepsilon
, then the covering and separation scales are perturbed by at most
\delta
.

The same theorem also controls how many landmarks an approximate construction can produce through standard metric packing and covering numbers. Let
N_{X}(r)
be the minimum cardinality of an
r
-cover of
X
, and let
P_{X}(r)
be the maximum cardinality of an
r
-separated subset of
X
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Under the assumptions of the preceding theorem, the approximate landmark set
L
satisfies

N_{X}(\varepsilon+\delta)\leq|L|\leq P_{X}(\varepsilon-\delta).

For exact greedy Ball Mapper, this reduces to

N_{X}(\varepsilon)\leq|L|\leq P_{X}(\varepsilon).

Proof.

Every
(\varepsilon+\delta)
-cover has at least
N_{X}(\varepsilon+\delta)
points, and every
(\varepsilon-\delta)
-separated set has at most
P_{X}(\varepsilon-\delta)
points. Applying the two conclusions of the preceding theorem proves the result.
∎

This identifies one mechanism by which false negatives can increase the number of selected landmarks: weakening separation from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon-\delta
enlarges the admissible packing number.

4.2
Nerve Approximation

We next study how approximate range sets affect the Ball Mapper graph when the landmark set is fixed.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
L\subseteq X
be a finite landmark set. For
r>0
, the
exact nerve at scale
r
is the abstract simplicial complex

\mathcal{N}_{r}(L):=\left\{\sigma\subseteq L:\sigma\neq\varnothing,\ \bigcap_{l\in\sigma}R_{r}(l)\neq\varnothing\right\}.

The corresponding Ball Mapper graph is the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{N}_{r}(L)
and is denoted by
G_{r}(L)
.

When approximate range sets are used, we obtain an approximate nerve. In the stability results below,
0\leq\delta<\varepsilon
, so
l\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)
for every landmark
l
and the returned sets define a simplicial complex on the fixed landmark set.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}
be an approximate range oracle and let
L\subseteq X
be fixed. The
approximate nerve
at scale
\varepsilon
is

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varepsilon}(L):=\left\{\sigma\subseteq L:\sigma\neq\varnothing,\ \bigcap_{l\in\sigma}\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)\neq\varnothing\right\}.

Its
1
-skeleton is denoted by
\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}
is
\delta
-accurate at radius
\varepsilon
. For any fixed landmark set
L\subseteq X
,

\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon-\delta}(L)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varepsilon}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(L).

Consequently,
G_{\varepsilon-\delta}(L)\subseteq\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)\subseteq G_{\varepsilon+\delta}(L)
.

Proof.

For every
l\in L
, by assumption, we have

R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(l)\subseteq\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)\subseteq R_{\varepsilon+\delta}(l).

Therefore, for every nonempty finite subset
\sigma\subseteq L
,

\bigcap_{l\in\sigma}R_{\varepsilon-\delta}(l)\subseteq\bigcap_{l\in\sigma}\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)\subseteq\bigcap_{l\in\sigma}R_{\varepsilon+\delta}(l).

If the left-hand intersection is nonempty, then the middle intersection is nonempty. If the middle intersection is nonempty, then the right-hand intersection is nonempty. This proves the nerve inclusions. Taking
1
-skeleta gives the graph inclusions.
∎

The approximate graph at scale
\varepsilon
is not arbitrary. It lies between two exact Ball Mapper graphs computed at the nearby scales
\varepsilon-\delta
and
\varepsilon+\delta
. This can also be expressed using the edges.

Definition 4.11
.

For two landmarks
l_{i},l_{j}\in L
, the
witness radius
is given by

\tau_{ij}:=\min_{x\in X}\max\{d(x,l_{i}),d(x,l_{j})\}.

The witness radius
\tau_{ij}
is the smallest radius at which some data point lies simultaneously in the two balls centered at
l_{i}
and
l_{j}
. Under the closed ball convention, the edge
(l_{i},l_{j})
belongs to
G_{r}(L)
precisely when

\tau_{ij}\leq r.

As a consequence of Proposition
4.10
, the following statements hold:

1.

If
\tau_{ij}\leq\varepsilon-\delta
, then
(l_{i},l_{j})
is guaranteed to appear in
\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)
.

2.

If
\tau_{ij}>\varepsilon+\delta
, then
(l_{i},l_{j})
is guaranteed not to appear in
\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)
.

3.

An edge between landmarks
l_{i}
and
l_{j}
satisfying
\varepsilon-\delta<\tau_{ij}\leq\varepsilon+\delta
may or may not appear in the approximate graph.

Uncertain edges are precisely those whose witnesses occur near the chosen scale parameter.

The pairwise witness radius extends directly to higher-order simplices. For any nonempty
\sigma\subseteq L
, define

\tau_{\sigma}:=\min_{x\in X}\max_{l\in\sigma}d(x,l).

Then
\sigma\in\mathcal{N}_{r}(L)
exactly when
\tau_{\sigma}\leq r
. Proposition
4.10
therefore has the following equivalent threshold interpretation. If
\tau_{\sigma}\leq\varepsilon-\delta
, then

\sigma\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varepsilon}(L).

If
\tau_{\sigma}>\varepsilon+\delta
, then

\sigma\notin\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\varepsilon}(L).

Hence, the uncertainty band applies to the full nerve, not only to graph edges.

For some approximate methods, an additive range error is not the most natural model. Instead, the computed distance may approximate the true distance multiplicatively.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
0\leq\eta<1
. An approximate distance function
\widetilde{d}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to have
relative error at most
\eta
if

(1-\eta)d(q,x)\leq\widetilde{d}(q,x)\leq(1+\eta)d(q,x)

for all
q,x\in X
.

Given such an approximate distance, the approximate range set is

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\{x\in X\mid\widetilde{d}(q,x)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Proposition 4.13
.

If
\widetilde{d}
has relative error at most
\eta
, then for every
q\in X
,

R_{\varepsilon/(1+\eta)}(q)\subseteq\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)\subseteq R_{\varepsilon/(1-\eta)}(q).

Proof.

Suppose first that
x\in R_{\varepsilon/(1+\eta)}(q)
. Then

d(q,x)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\eta}.

The upper relative error bound gives
\widetilde{d}(q,x)\leq(1+\eta)d(q,x)\leq\varepsilon
. Hence,
x\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)
.

Conversely, suppose that
x\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)
. Then
\widetilde{d}(q,x)\leq\varepsilon
. The lower relative error bound gives
(1-\eta)d(q,x)\leq\widetilde{d}(q,x)\leq\varepsilon
. Therefore,

d(q,x)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\eta},

so
x\in R_{\varepsilon/(1-\eta)}(q)
.
∎

Therefore, the preceding cover, separation, nerve, and graph results also apply in the multiplicative setting. The inner radius is
\varepsilon/(1+\eta)
, and the outer radius is
\varepsilon/(1-\eta)
.

4.3
Range Query Errors

The approximate range set may differ from the exact range set in two basic ways.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
q\in X
. The set of
false negatives
at radius
\varepsilon
is

\operatorname{FN}_{\varepsilon}(q):=R_{\varepsilon}(q)\setminus\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q).

The set of
false positives
at radius
\varepsilon
is

\operatorname{FP}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)\setminus R_{\varepsilon}(q).

False negatives are true members of the exact ball that are not returned by the approximate query. False positives are points returned by the approximate query even though they do not belong to the exact ball.

The two error types affect Ball Mapper differently. During landmark selection, a false negative may leave a point uncovered even though it lies inside an existing landmark ball. Such a point may later be selected as an additional landmark. A false positive may mark a point as covered even though it does not lie inside the exact ball. This can destroy exact
\varepsilon
-coverage.

For a fixed landmark set, false negatives may remove genuine intersection witnesses and hence delete edges. False positives may introduce spurious witnesses and hence add edges.

Definition 4.15
.

For
q\in X
, define the
range recall
and
range precision
by

\operatorname{recall}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\frac{|R_{\varepsilon}(q)\cap\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)|}{|R_{\varepsilon}(q)|},

and

\operatorname{precision}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\frac{|R_{\varepsilon}(q)\cap\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)|}{|\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)|},

whenever the denominators are non-zero.

Range recall measures the proportion of true ball members returned by the approximate query. Range precision measures the proportion of returned points that are true ball members.

A common way to eliminate false positives is to use an approximate index only to generate a candidate set
C_{\varepsilon}(q)\subseteq X
, and then evaluate the exact distance
d(q,x)
for each candidate
x\in C_{\varepsilon}(q)
. The
verified range set
is defined by

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\{x\in C_{\varepsilon}(q)\mid d(q,x)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Since membership is decided using the exact distance, we have

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)\subseteq R_{\varepsilon}(q).

Thus, exact verification eliminates false positives. False negatives may remain if the candidate set
C_{\varepsilon}(q)
omits points belonging to
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
.

Definition 4.16
.

An approximate range oracle is called
conservative
at radius
\varepsilon
if

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)\subseteq R_{\varepsilon}(q)

for every
q\in X
.

Proposition 4.17
.

Suppose that the greedy Ball Mapper construction uses a conservative approximate range oracle at radius
\varepsilon
. Let
L\subseteq X
be the resulting landmark set. Then the following statements hold:

1.

The exact balls of radius
\varepsilon
centered at points of
L
cover
X
.

2.

For a fixed landmark set
L
,
\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)\subseteq G_{\varepsilon}(L)
.

Proof.

If
x
is marked as covered by a landmark
l
, then
x\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)
. Since the oracle is conservative,
x\in R_{\varepsilon}(l)
or
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon
. If
x
is itself selected as a landmark, then
d(x,x)=0\leq\varepsilon
. Therefore, the exact
\varepsilon
-balls centered at landmarks in
L
cover
X
.

Now, suppose
(l_{i},l_{j})
is an edge of
\widetilde{G}_{\varepsilon}(L)
. Then there exists
x\in X
such that

x\in\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})\cap\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{j}).

Since the oracle is conservative,

x\in R_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})\cap R_{\varepsilon}(l_{j}).

Thus,
(l_{i},l_{j})
is also an edge of
G_{\varepsilon}(L)
.
∎

The resulting landmark set
L
need not be
\varepsilon
-separated. Separation may fail because a point
x
satisfying
d(x,l)\leq\varepsilon
may be omitted from
\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l)
by a false negative. Then
x
remains uncovered and may later be selected as another landmark. Hence, two distinct landmarks may lie at distance at most
\varepsilon
.

The graph inclusion in Proposition
4.17
is a statement for a fixed landmark set. If approximate range queries are also used during landmark selection, then the exact and approximate constructions may produce different landmark sets, and the resulting graphs need not have the same vertex set.

4.3.1
Witness Redundancy and Edge Survival

For a fixed landmark set and conservative approximate ranges, graph stability is governed not only by range recall but also by the number of distinct data points witnessing each overlap. Define the exact witness set by

W_{ij}:=R_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})\cap R_{\varepsilon}(l_{j}).

Let
m_{ij}:=|W_{ij}|
denote its cardinality, and let
F_{i}:=R_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})\setminus\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})
be the false negative set for landmark
l_{i}
.

Proposition 4.18
.

Suppose the approximate oracle is conservative and the landmark set is fixed. An exact edge
(l_{i},l_{j})
survives in the approximate graph if and only if

W_{ij}\setminus(F_{i}\cup F_{j})\neq\varnothing.

In particular, the sufficient condition

m_{ij}>|F_{i}\cap W_{ij}|+|F_{j}\cap W_{ij}|

guarantees that the edge survives. A still simpler sufficient condition is
m_{ij}>|F_{i}|+|F_{j}|
.

Proof.

Under conservative search, the approximate common witnesses are exactly

\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})\cap\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{j})=W_{ij}\setminus(F_{i}\cup F_{j}).

The edge survives precisely when this set is nonempty. If every exact witness were removed, then
W_{ij}\subseteq F_{i}\cup F_{j}
, which would imply

|W_{ij}|\leq|F_{i}\cap W_{ij}|+|F_{j}\cap W_{ij}|
.
The contrapositive gives the sufficient condition.
∎

Thus, two edges with the same geometric scale margin can have very different robustness: an overlap supported by many data points can tolerate several missed memberships, whereas an edge with a single witness disappears as soon as that witness is missed by either endpoint. The same argument extends to a simplex
\sigma
: it survives whenever at least one exact common witness is retained by every landmark in
\sigma
. This gives a direct theoretical explanation for the empirical observation that clustered or low-dimensional structured datasets can preserve graph connectivity despite imperfect range recall.

4.3.2
Stability of Average Colorings Under Range Errors

Approximate range queries can change a Ball Mapper visualization even when the landmark set is held fixed, because the color of a vertex is computed from its returned membership set. This effect can also be bounded directly.

Let
X_{i}=R_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})
and
\widetilde{X}_{i}=\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(l_{i})
, and assume that both sets are nonempty. Define the exact mean color by

c_{i}=\frac{1}{|X_{i}|}\sum_{x\in X_{i}}f(x),

and define the approximate mean color by

\widetilde{c}_{i}=\frac{1}{|\widetilde{X}_{i}|}\sum_{x\in\widetilde{X}_{i}}f(x).

Write
r_{i}
and
p_{i}
for the range recall and precision at
l_{i}
, and define the local oscillation

\operatorname{osc}_{i}(f)=\max_{x\in X_{i}\cup\widetilde{X}_{i}}f(x)-\min_{x\in X_{i}\cup\widetilde{X}_{i}}f(x).

Theorem 4.19
.

For every fixed landmark
l_{i}
,

|\widetilde{c}_{i}-c_{i}|\leq\operatorname{osc}_{i}(f)\bigl(1-\min\{r_{i},p_{i}\}\bigr).

If the oracle is
\delta
-accurate and
f
is
k
-Lipschitz, then

|\widetilde{c}_{i}-c_{i}|\leq 2k(\varepsilon+\delta)\bigl(1-\min\{r_{i},p_{i}\}\bigr).

For a conservative oracle,
p_{i}=1
and
X_{i}\cup\widetilde{X}_{i}=X_{i}
, so the sharper specialization is

|\widetilde{c}_{i}-c_{i}|\leq 2k\varepsilon(1-r_{i}).

Proof.

Let
U_{i}
and
\widetilde{U}_{i}
denote the uniform probability measures on
X_{i}
and
\widetilde{X}_{i}
. For two finite uniform measures,

\|U_{i}-\widetilde{U}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=1-\frac{|X_{i}\cap\widetilde{X}_{i}|}{\max\{|X_{i}|,|\widetilde{X}_{i}|\}}=1-\min\{r_{i},p_{i}\}.

The difference between expectations of a function whose range has width
\operatorname{osc}_{i}(f)
is bounded by that width times total variation, proving the first inequality. Under
\delta
-accuracy, both membership sets lie in
R_{\varepsilon+\delta}(l_{i})
, whose diameter is at most
2(\varepsilon+\delta)
; Lipschitz continuity therefore gives
\operatorname{osc}_{i}(f)\leq 2k(\varepsilon+\delta)
. Under conservative search,
\widetilde{X}_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
, so precision equals one and the union is contained in the exact
\varepsilon
-ball.
∎

This separates two sources of visual variation: geometric variation of
f
over the cover, controlled by the coloring results in Section 2.3, and computational variation caused by imperfect range membership, controlled here by recall and precision.

4.4
Approximation Methods in FAISS

FAISS contains several indexing methods
(
Meta AI Research, 2025
)
. Their effect on Ball Mapper can be interpreted through the range set framework introduced above.

A flat index, such as
IndexFlatL2
, compares the query with every stored vector
(
Meta AI Research, 2026
)
. Hence, it computes the exact range set, up to some floating-point precision near the boundary.

First, consider a
non-exhaustive index
. Such an index examines only a subset of the data points during a query. Let
C(q)\subseteq X
denote the set of candidate points examined for the query point
q
. This is a conservative approximation. Since membership is checked using the exact distance
d
, no point outside the exact ball can be returned. Hence, false positives are impossible. However, false negatives may occur.

Examples of this type include inverted file indexes such as
IndexIVFFlat
, where the dataset is divided into cells and only selected cells are searched, and graph-based indexes such as
IndexHNSWFlat
, where the query explores only part of a proximity graph
(
Malkov and Yashunin, 2020
)
.

Second, consider indexes based on compressed distance computation. In this case, a vector
x
is replaced by a compressed representation, and the query uses an approximate distance
\widetilde{d}(q,x)
instead of the exact distance
d(q,x)
. Because
\widetilde{d}(q,x)
may be smaller or larger than
d(q,x)
, the set
\widetilde{R}_{\varepsilon}(q)
need not be contained in
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
, nor does it necessarily contain
R_{\varepsilon}(q)
. Thus, both false positives and false negatives may occur.

Examples of compressed distance indexes include scalar quantization and product quantization
(
Jégou et al., 2011
)
indexes, such as
IndexSQ
and
IndexPQ
.

Finally, some FAISS indexes combine both mechanisms. For example, an inverted file index with product quantization first selects a candidate set
C(q)
and then evaluates approximate distances using compressed vector representations
(
Sivic and Zisserman, 2003
)
. False negatives and false positives, as explained above, may occur.

The main cases can be summarized as follows, following the FAISS index taxonomy
(
Meta AI Research, 2025
)
:

Index type

Source of approximation

Possible range errors

IndexFlatL2

Exact

None

IndexIVFFlat

Only selected cells are examined

False negatives

IndexHNSWFlat

Partial graph traversal

False negatives

IndexPQ
or
IndexSQ

Compressed distance evaluation

False positives and false negatives

IndexIVFPQ

Cell selection and compression

False positives and false negatives

Thus, the deterministic guarantees proved above should be interpreted conditionally. If an approximate FAISS configuration satisfies an additive or multiplicative range set error bound, then the corresponding Ball Mapper cover and graph satisfy the stated guarantees. If no such bound is available, then the approximation should instead be evaluated empirically by comparing it with the exhaustive
IndexFlatL2
baseline, using quantities such as range recall and range precision.

6.5
Interpretation Across Dataset Geometries

The three geometries consistently ordered themselves by sensitivity to approximation. The isotropic Gaussian dataset was the most difficult: ball overlaps were numerous, and moderate range recall loss caused substantial witness and edge loss. The Gaussian mixture was more stable, while the noisy curve was nearly exact under modest search effort. This dependence on geometry was reproducible across all three seeds.

The boundary margin diagnostics should be interpreted more cautiously. The deterministic boundary results describe perturbations controlled by distance from the radius threshold. IVF and HNSW errors arise instead from candidate omission, and in these experiments ball size covaries with both boundary density and the opportunity for missed candidates. We therefore do not treat the observed boundary margin scatter as an independent empirical validation of the deterministic margin theorem. A controlled perturbation experiment would be needed to isolate that effect.

7
Limitations and Scope

The theoretical guarantees are conditional on properties of the range oracle. The additive and multiplicative models do not assert that IVF or HNSW satisfies a universal deterministic error parameter
\delta
or
\eta
; when such a bound is unavailable, fidelity must be measured against an exact reference. Likewise, the conservative behavior observed for the verified IVF and HNSW configurations is an empirical property of the evaluated implementation and should not be generalized to compressed distance indexes, for which false positives can occur.

The approximation experiments use synthetic Euclidean data at
n=20{,}000
and CPU execution. They were designed to isolate geometry, witness redundancy, and range query error rather than to establish application specific performance. Real data may exhibit different density, anisotropy, intrinsic dimension, and coloring behavior. The optional hnswlib and cuVS adapters in the software were not benchmarked in this study, and no GPU speed claim is made. Finally, exhaustive FAISS Flat stores vectors in single-precision floating-point format; its search is exhaustive relative to that representation, whereas the exact validation uses double-precision floating-point brute force, BallTree, and cKDTree to make the closed ball mathematical reference explicit.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

This work separates the mathematical Ball Mapper construction from the range query backend used to realize its metric balls. Theorem
3.1
explains why an exact acceleration can change runtime without changing landmarks, memberships, graph edges, or membership based colorings. Comparisons among the independent brute-force, BallTree, and cKDTree implementations support the consistency of the closed ball computation under the stated numerical convention, while the approximate oracle analysis quantifies how range uncertainty propagates to covering radius, landmark separation, the nerve, edge witnesses, and mean vertex colors.

The approximation study over three seeds yields three main empirical conclusions. Approximation sensitivity is strongly geometry dependent; isotropic Gaussian data are substantially harder than clustered data or data with low intrinsic dimension. Edge stability is governed by witness redundancy, with overlaps supported by multiple witnesses surviving much more reliably than overlaps supported by a single witness. The derived mean color perturbation bound was never violated in the experiments with fixed landmarks.

Approximate indexing did not outperform exhaustive FAISS Flat at
n=20{,}000
. At this scale its value is therefore diagnostic rather than computational: it provides a controlled setting in which to understand fidelity before approximation is used at larger scale. Future work should test substantially larger databases, GPU execution, memory constrained settings, and compressed representations, where a genuine speed–accuracy trade-off is more plausible. Controlled perturbation experiments can separately test boundary margin theory, while real application datasets can determine how local density, witness multiplicity, and auxiliary colorings interact in practice.

Appendix A
Backend Implementation Details

A.1
BallTree Implementation

A
ball tree

(
Omohundro, 1989
)
is a rooted binary tree data structure where each node in the tree represents a ball that contains a subset of the data points. A Python implementation of the ball tree data structure is available through scikit-learn
(
Pedregosa et al., 2011
)
.

The scikit-learn implementation uses a balanced binary tree layout. Each node corresponds to a contiguous subset of the indexed observations and stores BallTree bounding information consisting of a centroid and a radius. During recursive construction, scikit-learn chooses the coordinate with the largest spread in the current node and partitions the node indices around the midpoint so that the two children contain approximately equal numbers of observations. Recursion continues until the prescribed leaf size condition is reached; for the default BallTree interface, the leaf size is 40. Changing the leaf size affects construction cost, memory use, and query speed but not the exact result of a range query
(
scikit-learn developers, 2026
)
. This implementation detail is distinct from alternative BallTree construction algorithms, including farthest point pivot schemes described in the classical literature
(
Omohundro, 1989
)
.

The range query proceeds as follows. Given a query point
q
and a radius
r
, we start at the root node with center
c
and radius
R
.
If the condition

d(q,c)-R>r

is satisfied, then the query ball
B(q,r)
cannot contain any point stored in that node. Indeed, every stored point
x
satisfies
d(c,x)\leq R
, so the reverse triangle inequality gives
d(q,x)\geq d(q,c)-R>r
. This does not mean that
B(q,r)
itself is empty; it means that its intersection with the subset represented by the node is empty. The condition therefore permits the entire subtree to be pruned without examining its individual points.
We then recursively visit each child node and perform the same check. If we reach the leaves, we check the distance of each point in the leaf to determine
B(q,r)
.

Scikit-learn’s BallTree supports a range of built-in metrics through its
valid_metrics
interface, including Euclidean- and Manhattan type metrics. This flexibility makes ball trees suitable for many metric based implementations of Ball Mapper.

A.2
k
-Dimensional Tree Implementation

A
k
-dimensional tree
, commonly called a
k
-d tree
, is a binary tree that organizes observations through recursive coordinate splits
Bentley (1975)
. Each internal node partitions its observations according to their position relative to a hyperplane perpendicular to a coordinate axis. Repeated partitioning produces smaller subsets, which are stored in the leaves.

For observations in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, each node is associated with a bounding box

Q=\prod_{j=1}^{D}[a_{j},b_{j}],

where
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are lower and upper bounds on the
j
-th coordinates of the observations assigned to that node. For a query point
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{D})
, the minimum Euclidean distance from
q
to this box is

d_{\min}(q,Q)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}\max\{a_{j}-q_{j},\,0,\,q_{j}-b_{j}\}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Each term measures the squared distance from a coordinate of the query to the corresponding interval.

A range query with radius
r>0
can exclude the entire node whenever
d_{\min}(q,Q)>r
. Indeed, every observation
x
assigned to the node satisfies
d(q,x)\geq d_{\min}(q,Q)
, so none can belong to the closed query ball. Otherwise, the search examines the child nodes. At leaves that have not been excluded, individual distances determine which observations satisfy
d(q,x)\leq r
. Nodes whose bounding boxes lie entirely within the query ball may be accepted without evaluating individual distances.

The implementation uses SciPy’s cKDTree as an independent Euclidean reference for validation
Virtanen et al. (2020)
. Its query_ball_point method supports Euclidean range queries with
p=2
, while
eps=0
disables approximate search. This software parameter is distinct from the Ball Mapper radius
\varepsilon
.

A.3
FAISS Flat Implementation

For many commonly used metrics, distance computations admit algebraic decompositions that can be exploited for efficiency. We illustrate this for the Euclidean distance. Consider a fixed query point
q
and the
squared Euclidean distance

d(q,x)=\left(\sum_{j}\left(q_{j}-x_{j}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The squared Euclidean distance can be expressed as

d^{2}(q,x)=d^{2}(q,0)+d^{2}(x,0)-2\sum_{j}q_{j}x_{j}.

The term
d^{2}(q,0)
is constant and computed only once per query. The term
d^{2}(x,0)
can be precomputed and stored. Consequently, the dominant cost in evaluating lies in the remaining term. This operation requires operations between
q
and each data point.

FAISS
(
Douze et al., 2025
)
accelerates this computation by exploiting two mechanisms: single instruction, multiple data (SIMD) processing
(
Flynn, 1972
)
and basic linear algebra subprograms (BLAS)
(
Goto and Van de Geijn, 2008
)
. SIMD instructions allow the same arithmetic operation to be applied simultaneously to multiple components of a vector, enabling parallel evaluation of inner products at the hardware level. BLAS is a standardized interface whose implementations are optimized numerical routines for linear algebra. By expressing collections of inner products as matrix–vector or matrix–matrix operations, FAISS leverages highly optimized BLAS routines that are tuned for modern CPU and GPU architectures.

As a result, FAISS reduces the constant factors associated with distance evaluation without altering the underlying algorithmic complexity. In contrast to tree-based data structures, FAISS accelerates the cost per distance computation through low level numerical optimization. Furthermore, FAISS supports parallel execution on modern hardware, which can further speed up computation when sufficient CPU or GPU resources are available.

The implementation studied here uses FAISS for squared Euclidean distance and, optionally, cosine similarity after vector normalization. The theoretical oracle formulation itself is metric agnostic; restrictions on the metric arise from the chosen backend rather than from Ball Mapper.

B.2
Empirical Scaling Analysis

The regression below is an
empirical scaling analysis
, not a proof of asymptotic computational complexity. The fitted exponent depends on the finite range of
n
and
D
, the hardware, implementation, thread count, cache behavior, and the geometry of the sampled data. It should therefore be interpreted together with Proposition
3.2

To summarize finite sample scaling with dataset size, we perform a log–log regression of runtime
T(n)
as a function of
n
separately at fixed dimensions
D
. We assume the empirical relationship

T(n)=kn^{p}

over the tested range. Taking logarithms gives

\log T(n)=\log k+p\log n,

which expresses
\log T(n)
as a linear function of
\log n
at fixed
D
. We estimate this relationship by least squares and call
p
the
empirical scaling exponent
. Thus,
p=1
corresponds to linear empirical growth in
n
over the tested range, while
p=2
corresponds to quadratic empirical growth. These fitted exponents are descriptive and are not asymptotic complexity guarantees.

The quality of the fit is assessed using the
coefficient of determination

R^{2}
defined as

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i}\left(y_{i}-\hat{y_{i}}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i}\left(y_{i}-\overline{y}\right)^{2}}

where
y_{i}
are the observed values,
\hat{y_{i}}
are the predicted values from the regression model, and
\overline{y}
denotes the mean of
y_{i}
. Higher values of
R^{2}
indicate a stronger adherence to a power law scaling relationship.
