The relationship between elderly dietary index and sarcopenia: a case-control study
Authors: Elham Moslemi, Fatemeh Mansouri, Babaqul Khudayqulov, Marzieh Mahmoodi, Seyyed Mohammad Alavi, Melika Mahmoudi-Zadeh, Fatemeh Souni, Moein Askarpour, Mehran Nouri, Zainab Shateri, Mohammad Hossein Dabbaghmanesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the rising number of older adults and the risk of sarcopenia in the new century, no research has assessed the link between sarcopenia and the Elderly Dietary Index (EDI) in this population. Therefore, the goal of the current research was to assess the association between sarcopenia odds and the EDI in older adults in Iran.MethodsThis case-control study, which included 80 participants in each group, utilized data from a population-based cross-sectional study of eligible older adults recruited from healthcare centers in Shiraz, Iran. The diagnostic standards established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia were used to define sarcopenia. For dietary intake assessment over the previous year, a 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire, with good validity and reliability among the Iranian population, was used. Associations between the EDI and other related factors with the odds of sarcopenia were assessed using logistic regression.ResultsIn the adjusted model, each unit increment in the EDI was correlated with lower odds of sarcopenia (odds ratio (OR) = 0.798, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.645–0.986). A greater odds of sarcopenia was observed with each unit increase in age and with higher education levels compared to lower age and education levels (age: OR = 1.204, 95% CI: 1.092–1.327; education: OR = 2.866, 95% CI: 1.131–7.259).ConclusionsThis study assessed adherence to the EDI among older adults and found a negative relationship between the odds of sarcopenia and the EDI.Keywords: Elderly dietary index, Diet, Sarcopenia, Older adults

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related condition characterized by a progressive decline in muscle mass, strength, and physical function. This condition reduces self-sufficiency, increases the risk of falls and hospitalizations, limits mobility, leads to high socioeconomic costs, and elevates mortality [1, 2].

Aging is the principal factor contributing to sarcopenia, with prevalence rates ranging from 11% to 50% in individuals aged 80 years and older, and from 5% to 13% among those aged 60 to 70 years [3]. It is predicted that 2.1 billion people will be affected by sarcopenia by 2050, while the number is expected to reach 1 billion to 1.4 billion by 2030 [4]. Additionally, the prevalence of sarcopenia in Western societies ranges from 1% to 29%, and from 2% to 46% in Asian countries [5, 6]. In Iran, there is limited research on the prevalence of sarcopenia [7, 8]. A comprehensive study conducted in Tehran revealed that the prevalence of sarcopenia among individuals aged over 59 years was 16.5% to 32.5%, depending on the diagnostic criteria applied [9]. Another study reported that 19.7% of males and 13.6% of females were affected by sarcopenia, according to the Bushehr Elderly Health Program [8].

Due to the increasing aging population and the lack of a specific cure for this disease, sarcopenia is currently considered a serious health threat. Sedentary lifestyles, malnutrition, physical inactivity, hospitalization, chronic illness, the use of certain medications, and anabolic resistance are the primary risk factors for sarcopenia [10]. One of the most crucial and modifiable lifestyle factors is diet [11]. To assess the relationship between diet and disease, dietary indices are useful because they evaluate various dietary components, including nutrients, food groups, and individual foods [12]. One such index is the Elderly Dietary Index (EDI), a simple and straightforward instrument for determining the adherence of older adults to specific dietary guidelines [13]. This guideline is a modified food guide pyramid for older adults, and the components of this score are the mean consumption of nine dietary groups and foods, including dairy products, bread, legumes, cereals, vegetables, fruits, fish, olive oil, and meat [14].

By consuming key food groups, older adults can meet recommended intakes of protein, fiber, potassium, calcium, and vitamins K, E, and D [13]. Protein sources such as fish, meat, and legumes are particularly important, as aging is associated with reduced muscle protein synthesis, leading to the loss of lean mass and sarcopenia [14]. Fat-free or low-fat dairy products help fulfill calcium requirements while limiting saturated fat intake [15]. Vegetables and fruits are rich in potassium and vitamins; notably, a cross-sectional study of adults aged 65 and older found that higher consumption of fruits and vegetables was associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia in men, and greater fruit intake was linked to a reduced likelihood of sarcopenia in women [16].

Despite the increasing prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults, no study has examined its association with the EDI in this population. Therefore, the objective of our study was to investigate the relationship between EDI scores and the risk of sarcopenia in Iranian older adults.

Methods

Research population

This case-control study utilized data from a population-based cross-sectional study carried out from August 2017 to February 2018 on 501 eligible older adults recruited from healthcare centers in Shiraz, Iran [17]. Eighty cases diagnosed with sarcopenia and 80 controls without sarcopenia, who were frequency-matched to cases by gender, were chosen based on the following exclusion and inclusion criteria: The inclusion criteria included age ≥ 65, living independently, and walking ability. The exclusion criteria consisted of having a limb prosthesis or any history of pulmonary or severe cardiac disease (severe cardiac disease was defined as heart failure, recent myocardial infarction within the past 6 months, advanced arrhythmias, or other clinically significant cardiac conditions requiring intensive management), musculoskeletal, or neoplastic diseases, uncontrolled metabolic or endocrine disorders (such as type II diabetes and diseases related to the thyroid gland), stroke, or severe neurological diseases (including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease). Furthermore, participants who had not completely answered the questionnaires or were unwilling to take part in this research were excluded.

This research was carried out following the principles set forth in the Helsinki Declaration. Some details of the present study have been previously published [18–20].

Baseline information

During registration, participants were interviewed for their demographic and clinical information, including medication use, sex, age, education, income, smoking history, and medical history. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was utilized to investigate physical activity, a previously validated tool [21]. A digital scale with 100 g precision and a non-elastic tape measure were used to record the weight and height of the participants while they wore light clothing and no shoes.

Diagnostic measures for sarcopenia

Sarcopenia is defined based on the diagnostic standards determined by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), which encompasses the following components: (1) reduced physical function, (2) diminished muscle strength, and (3) decreased muscle mass. According to the AWGS guidelines, older adults exhibiting diminished muscle strength, decreased muscle mass, and/or diminished physical function are categorized as sarcopenic patients [22].

The body composition of the participants was evaluated utilizing bioelectrical impedance analysis (South Korea). The formula used to calculate the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) and values below 7 kg/m² and below 5.7 kg/m² were deemed indicative of decreased muscle mass for men and women, respectively [17, 22]. Muscle strength was quantified by the evaluation of handgrip strength (HGS) while seated with the elbow flexed at a 90-degree angle. Participants performed 3 squeezes on a hand dynamometer (Korea) with both hands, providing fifteen-second intervals between the squeezes. Individuals were recognized as having decreased muscle strength if their maximum HGS value recorded was < 26 kg for males and < 18 kg for females [17, 22]. Physical function was examined via gait speed (GS) during unaided walking over a distance of four meters [GS = distance (meters)/time (seconds)]. The cut-off point of GS < 0.8 m/second was used to identify reduced physical function [17, 22].

All measurements for participants were conducted in the morning, and they adhered to the instructions to maintain their regular dietary and physical activity routines and to avoid intense activities the day prior to the test.

Dietary evaluation and EDI

For dietary consumption assessment over the previous year, a 168-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire with good validity and reliability among the Iranian population was used [23]. Participants provided information on their annual, monthly, weekly, or daily food item consumption during face-to-face interviews, which were converted into daily grams according to household measurements [24]. Daily food intakes were analyzed using modified Nutritionist IV software tailored for Iranians to assess individuals’ energy and macronutrient consumption.

Kourlaba et al. developed the EDI [14] based on food items/groups recommended in the traditional Mediterranean diet and the Modified MyPyramid for Older Adults. The EDI originally included 10 dietary parts: alcohol, dairy products, olive oil, bread, fish, meat, legumes, cereals, vegetables, and fruits. Due to religious and legal prohibitions, the Iranian population may underreport or have infrequent alcohol consumption; hence, it was excluded from the EDI in this study.

For the remaining nine components, we applied the modified method proposed by Darooghegi Mofrad et al. [13]. In this approach, each dietary component was assigned a score from 1 to 4 according to the participant’s daily intake (g/day), with higher scores indicating better adherence to dietary recommendations for older adults. The sum of the nine components constituted the total EDI score, ranging from 9 to 36. The scores of all nine components were then summed to yield the total EDI, ranging from 9 to 36, where greater scores indicate higher overall diet quality.

Statistical analysis

An independent samples t-test was utilized to compare continuous data between cases and controls. Skewed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Additionally, categorical data were compared between the two groups by applying the chi-square test. The associations between EDI and other related factors with the odds of sarcopenia were assessed by logistic regressions (enter method) in the crude model and an adjusted model accounting for several confounders (including age, energy, protein, fat, physical activity, smoking history, and education level). SPSS software (version 24) was utilized for statistical analysis. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in all analyses.

The link between EDI score and the likelihood of sarcopenia in the adjusted and crude models is presented in Table 2. In the crude model, each point increment in the EDI was associated with a lower likelihood of sarcopenia (odds ratio (OR) = 0.748, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.677–0.825). Similarly, in the crude model, a lower chance of sarcopenia was detected with increased energy intake (OR = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.997–0.998), protein consumption (OR = 0.929, 95% CI: 0.906–0.953), and fat consumption (OR = 0.937, 95% CI: 0.911–0.964). However, a higher chance of sarcopenia was found based on each unit alteration in age (OR = 1.141, 95% CI: 1.058–1.230).

A lesser chance of sarcopenia was detected with each point alteration in the EDI score (OR = 0.798, 95% CI: 0.645–0.986) in the adjusted model for age, energy, protein, fat, physical activity, smoking history, and education level. With each incremental year of age and with a higher level of education compared to lower levels, a greater likelihood of sarcopenia was observed (age: OR = 1.204, 95% CI: 1.092–1.327; education: OR = 2.866, 95% CI: 1.131–7.259).

Discussion

It is essential to address age-related disorders like sarcopenia in light of the global trend of an aging population. Diagnosing and treating this disease will enhance people’s quality of life and lower overall healthcare expenditures [25]. The current investigation’s findings illustrated a strong inverse link between sarcopenia in older adults and adherence to the EDI. This means that a lower dietary score was observed in the older adults with sarcopenia. This is the first research, to our knowledge, that assesses the potential link between EDI and sarcopenia.

EDI is a dietary assessment tool developed to evaluate overall diet quality in older adults [13]. It is an adapted version of the Mediterranean diet score, modified to account for the consumption levels of various food groups and specific foods [14, 26]. Compared to the traditional Mediterranean diet score, the EDI incorporates a U-shaped relationship between health outcomes and specific food components and assigns a 4-point score to each component. Given that olive oil and fish consumption is lower among Iranian adults, the EDI may provide a more suitable assessment of diet quality in this population than the Mediterranean diet score [13].

Only three studies have previously assessed the relationship between the incidence of a disease, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), and EDI [13, 14, 26]. A cohort study revealed that greater adherence to EDI was correlated with mortality and a reduced rate of CVD in older adults in England [26]. Another study involving older adults found that a 1-point increase in the EDI is linked to nearly a 10% reduction in the chance of having at least one risk factor for CVD, being hypertensive, or obese [14]. Moreover, a study by Mofrad et al. with 362 older adults Iranian men showed that although elevated EDI was correlated with a reduced risk of obesity and overweight, there was no meaningful association between other CVD risk variables and EDI [13].

Due to the lack of studies about EDI and sarcopenia, research investigating the link between the Mediterranean diet and sarcopenia may provide valuable insights [27–30]. Similar to our study, another study indicated that lesser adherence to the Mediterranean diet was related to a greater risk of sarcopenia (25.9%) [29]. It is noticeable that with increasing age, the rate of sarcopenia is greater in the low-adherence group as well. Moreover, in another study by Isanejad et al., women lost less muscle and lean mass after 3 years when they were more adherent to the Mediterranean diet than other groups [27]. However, in the 7-year follow-up research involving Italian older adults, the combination of aerobic training and greater adherence to a Mediterranean diet did not correlate with a reduced risk of sarcopenia [30]. Also, a systematic review study illustrated that following the Mediterranean diet was generally correlated with increased muscle function and mass; however, the impacts on muscle strength were not meaningful [28].

In our study, older adults without sarcopenia consumed significantly greater amounts of vegetables, fruits, and dairy products compared with those with sarcopenia, while the sarcopenic group showed higher intakes of total fats and breads. These findings suggest that dietary quality plays a critical role in muscle health. Antioxidant vitamins and phytochemicals in fruits and vegetables can decrease inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby enhancing anabolic processes and reducing proteolysis [31, 32]. Dairy foods also provide high-quality proteins in a convenient form, which may support the maintenance of muscle mass [33, 34]. Moreover, unsaturated fats from olive oil, a key element of the EDI, have been shown to promote protein synthesis [35], whereas higher consumption of refined breads and excessive fats may reflect poorer diet quality, contributing to muscle deterioration.

Whole grains and legumes, key components of the EDI, may contribute to a lower sarcopenia risk through several mechanisms. Whole grains contain bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, β-glucan, β-sitosterol, and alkylresorcinols, which can promote muscle anabolism [36]. Additionally, prebiotic fibers in whole grains and legumes support gut microbiota health, an emerging gut–muscle axis implicated in maintaining muscle mass and function in older adults [37–40]. These mechanisms may help explain why adherence to the EDI is associated with a reduced sarcopenia risk in our study.

Protein intake, particularly from fish, appears to play an important role in reducing sarcopenia risk. Adequate dietary protein supports muscle protein synthesis, while insufficient protein intake is linked to reduced muscle mass in older adults [41]. Fish, a central component of the EDI, provides high-quality protein, vitamin D, selenium, and omega-3 fatty acids, all of which contribute to muscle metabolism and have anti-inflammatory effects [42]. These findings align with our results, emphasizing the potential protective role of protein-rich foods in maintaining muscle health.

These dietary patterns are consistent with our findings. Higher EDI scores were associated with lower odds of sarcopenia. Age was positively associated with sarcopenia. Collectively, these findings suggest that stronger adherence to the EDI—characterized by higher intakes of plant-based foods, dairy, fish, and whole grains/legumes—may help protect against sarcopenia in older adults.

The primary strength of the current research is that it is the first study to investigate the association between EDI and the likelihood of sarcopenia in older men and women. Furthermore, the EDI’s four-point scoring system considers equal weight for each dietary group to assess the final score, and comparing among components is possible. However, it has been proven that some food groups impact health outcomes more than others. Another strength is the use of a validated and trustworthy FFQ to estimate food intake. However, recall bias is a limitation of this research, particularly in older adults. Additionally, another potential limitation of this study is the possibility of selection bias, as only independent patients without major comorbidities were included. Another limitation of our study is that the updated 2019 AWGS diagnostic criteria could not be applied, as the primary study from which our sample was drawn was conducted before 2019. Therefore, we used the previously established cutoffs for muscle strength and gait speed, which may differ slightly from the current recommendations.

Researchers have observed that older adults tend to report their routine consumption more than their actual daytime consumption due to a decrease in memory capacity and concentration. Moreover, another weakness of this study is its design. Due to the observational design, a direct cause-and-effect relationship cannot be determined, and the authors only evaluate the relationship between exposure (EDI) and outcome (sarcopenia). Lastly, even if the majority of the significant confounders were taken into account, some complicating factors, such as polypharmacy, may still exist. However, these preliminary results can be considered a foundation for further in-depth research in the future. Subsequent studies should utilize a larger sample size to investigate the impact of EDI on sarcopenia through randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

This study assessed adherence to EDI among older adults with sarcopenia and found a negative relationship between the odds of sarcopenia and EDI. Due to the crucial role of nutritional status, further interventional and prospective studies are needed to determine whether older adults will consume food groups based on the EDI and whether this will decrease the risk of sarcopenia.
