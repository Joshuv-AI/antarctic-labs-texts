Mechanism of PRRSV infection and antiviral role of polyphenols
Authors: Fructueux Modeste Amona, Yipeng Pang, Xingyu Gong, Yanhong Wang, Xingtang Fang, Chunlei Zhang, Xi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPorcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) is associated with the endemic outbreak of fever, anorexia, and abortion in pregnant sows, resulting in an enormous economic impact on the global swine industry. Current mainstream prophylactic agents and therapies have been developed to prevent PRRSV infection; however, they have limited efficacy. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel antiviral strategies to prevent PRRSV infection and transmission. The identification of new PRRSV entry mediators, such as MYH9 and HSPA8; viral apoptotic mimicry; and TIM-induced macropinocytosis, to facilitate infection has led to a novel molecular understanding of the PRRSV infection mechanism, which can be utilized in the development of prophylactic agents and therapies for PRRSV infection. Polyphenols, complex chemical molecules with abundant biological activities derived from microorganisms and plants, have demonstrated great potential for controlling PRRSV infection via different mechanisms. To explore new possibilities for treating PRRSV infection with polyphenols, this review focuses on summarizing the pathogenesis of PRRSV, reviewing the potential antiviral mechanisms of polyphenols against PRRSV, and addressing the challenges associated with the widespread use of polyphenols.KEYWORDS: PRRSV infection, pathogenesis, antiviral mechanism, natural products, polyphenols

Introduction

Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) is an endemic outbreak disease that causes reproductive failure in breeding sows and respiratory disorders in growing pigs. PRRS virus (PRRSV) is an enveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus of the family Arteriviridae within the genus Betaarterivirus, [1,2] that consists of approximately spherical or oval particles with a 50 to 74 nm diameter and exhibits a relatively smooth appearance according to cryo-electron microscopy, [3] has caused enormous economic losses in the swine industry worldwide in the past three decades, because of its persistent mutation and epidemic caused by the high variability of the RNA virus genome. Based on their genetic characteristics, PRRSV is classified into two genotypes, PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2, both of which exhibit a 60% nucleotide identity. [4,5] PRRSV-1 is further divided into four subtypes, while PRRSV-2 is classified into nine lineages (1–9) with an interlineage genetic distance of 11–18%. [6,7] Major preventive strategies currently include the use of an inactivated vaccine and the use of a modified live-attenuated vaccine (MLV). However, pig immunosuppression against PRRSV infection, virus evolution, and multiple recombination events between the wild-type strain and MLV, as well as the limitations of currently approved vaccines, do not offer sufficient protection against the challenge of a heterogeneous strain. This presents a significant challenge to vaccine development. [8–10] Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel antiviral strategies against PRRSV by inactivating the virus from the initial stage of infection.

PRRSV entry into host cells is the essential mechanism for infection. The binding of specific glycoproteins or structures on the virus particle surface to particular cell receptors leads to viral penetration into target cells or fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane and subsequent release of the viral genome. The scavenger receptor CD163 interacts with the GP2a/GP3/GP4 complex to facilitate membrane fusion. [11] Additionally, the E protein, which is embedded in the virus envelope as an ion channel, has been reported to interact with the viral proteins GP2a, GP3, and GP4 to form diverse polymer complexes that promote viral genome release. [12] Given the limited availability of effective vaccines, the interactions between PRRSV glycoproteins and host membrane receptors have become highly attractive targets for intervention to prevent PRRSV infection.

Natural products (NPs), which contain multiple bioactive components with anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and immunoregulatory properties, have been validated for their potential for controlling PRRSV infection via different mechanisms. Polyphenols, the organic molecules containing phenolic hydroxyl groups, are common natural compounds found in plants and have been utilized for their antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, antitumoral, antiviral, and antibacterial effects. [13] Several studies have shown the potential of polyphenols to inhibit PRRSV. [14,15] However, there is a lack of well-documented knowledge on the antiviral mechanisms by which natural compounds, particularly polyphenols, inhibit PRRSV infection. In the present review, we primarily summarize the mechanism of PRRSV infection and review the potential antiviral mechanisms of polyphenols against PRRSV, as well as further elucidate the challenges in the wide application of polyphenols to promote an in-depth understanding and development of polyphenol-based PRRSV therapy.

PRRSV and its molecular mechanism of infection

PRRSV genome structure

The viral genomic RNA is encapsulated by the nucleocapsid (N) protein. Surrounding the N protein, there are four membrane-associated glycoproteins (GPs: GP2a-b, GP3, GP4, and GP5), three non-glycosylated membrane proteins (E, ORF5a, and M), and other membrane proteins that are inserted into the lipid envelope to form complete virions. [16] The PRRSV genomic RNA is a positive-sense molecule ~15.4 kb in length with a polyadenylated 3” end and containing 12 known open reading frames (ORFs): ORF1a, ORF1a,” TF, ORF1b, ORF2a, ORF2b, ORF3, ORF4, ORF5a, and ORF5–ORF7 (Figure 1). Among these ORFs, ORF1a and ORF1b make up the replicase gene located in the 5’-proximal three-quarters of the polycistronic genome, constituting approximately 80% of the genome. [1,20,21] Within ORF1a and ORF1b, the polyproteins pp1a and pp1b encode 17 nonstructural proteins (NSPs), including nsp1α, nsp 1β, nsp 2, nsp2N, nsp2TF, and nsp3-14, which are essential for viral replication. Recently, an alternative ORF was discovered, generating two additional proteins, nsp2TF and nsp2N, which are necessary and sufficient for inducing membrane modifications similar to those in infected cells. [17,21] Additionally, nsp2, traditionally considered a nonstructural protein, is found in multiple isoforms within the viral envelope (ovarian tumor domain protease region, hypervariable region, and C-terminal region), providing new insights into the virus’s structure.[17,22]

The genome structure and topology of the PRRSV envelope proteins. Nonstructural proteins are located at the 5′ end of the genome. These proteins encode two polyproteins, pp1a and pp1ab, which are cleaved into at least 17 nonstructural proteins (NSP), including nsp1α, nsp 1β, nsp 2, nsp2N, nsp2TF, and nsp3-14. Furthermore, nsp2, traditionally considered a nonstructural protein, is present in multiple isoforms within the viral envelope. Structural proteins located near the 3′ ends are linked to the viral envelope (GP2a-b, GP3, GP4, GP5, GP5a, M, N, E) and RNA package. [1,17–19] the topological structure of the PRRSV envelope proteins, according to recent progress in PRRSV, revealed protein interactions, such as those between the two major envelope protein complexes M – GP5 and GP2a-b – GP3–GP4.

Furthermore, key envelope proteins such as GP5, which is encoded by ORF5, and M envelope proteins, which are encoded by ORF6, interact to create heterodimers on the virion surface (Figure 1). These heterodimeric forms typically exist as M-GP5 complexes, which make up more than half of the viral proteins found in PRRSV. These proteins undergo disulphide-linked association within infected cells, which is crucial for the proper trafficking, assembly, and release of progeny virions. [21] Moreover, GP5, a glycoprotein consisting of approximately 200 amino acids with an apparent molecular mass of 26 kDa, [23] represents one of several structurally variable regions within the proteome of PRRSV, making it frequently used for phylogenetic analyses. The minor proteins GP2a, GP3, and GP4 are encoded by ORFs 2–4 and form noncovalent heterotrimers. Additionally, ORF2a, ORF2b, ORFs3–7, and the recently discovered ORF5a encode the structural proteins GP2, E, GP3, GP4, GP5, the matrix protein (M), the nucleocapsid protein (N) and GP5a, respectively, which all constitute the PRRSV virion. [6,17,21,24,25] The non-glycosylated E protein is essential for viral uncoating, likely because of its ion channel characteristics. [12] The non-glycosylated envelope protein GP5a may be essential for virus viability, however its role requires additional investigation.[24]

Mechanism of PRRSV infection

The permissiveness of a cell line to PRRSV infection is determined by the presence or absence of cellular receptors. Despite conflicting data from various studies in the last decade, the mechanism underlying PRRSV infection has not been elucidated. These data led to the identification of nine main entry mediators (Figure 2), namely, heparan sulphate (HS), sialoadhesin (CD169), porcine CD163 (pCD163), the cluster of differentiation 151 receptor (CD151), dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3-grabbing nonintegrin/CD209 (DC-SIGN), nonmuscle-myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin structural domain (TIM), vimentin, and heat shock protein member 8 (HSPA8). [1,26] Because pigs lacking the scavenger receptor cysteine-rich domain 5 (SRCR5) of pCD163 are completely resistant to PRRSV infection, it is evident that the porcine cluster of differentiation 163 (pCD163) serves as the most specific and important cellular receptor for successful infection. [27,28] The receptor for PRRSV, the porcine cluster of differentiation 163 (pCD163) molecule, is a 130 kDa macrophage-specific glycoprotein that belongs to class B of the scavenger receptor cysteine-rich (SRCR) superfamily and possesses one intracellular domain and nine cytoplasmic outer SRCR domains. [29] Among the nine extracellular SRCR domains in pCD163 (Figure 2), SRCR5 was found to be crucial for PRRSV infection, and monocytes/macrophages from pigs expressing pCD163 with deleted SRCR5 were fully protected from PRRSV infection. [28] The surface expression of pCD163 is limited to cells derived from the monocyte-macrophage lineage, serving as the receptor for genotype 1 and genotype 2 PRRSV. [27] Currently, Xu et al. reported that pCD163-3D4/21 cells were permissive to infection by various PRRSV-2 strains, including NADC30-like PRRSV (SD17–38) and NADC34-like PRRSV (Anheal-1), while being nonpermissive to MARC-145 cells.[11]

Putative receptors for PRRSV and structural domain organization of pCD163 (with nine extracellular SRCR domains, two pst-rich domains, a transmembrane region, and an intracellular cytoplasmic tail). The red region (SRCR5) is the domain that participates in PRRSV infection. At least nine infection-related cellular molecules have been recently identified as putative receptors: vimentin, cluster of differentiation 151 receptor (CD151), heparin sulphate (HS), cluster of differentiation 163 (CD163), sialoadhesin (siglec-1; also known as CD169), dendritic cell-specific intercellular-adhesion-molecule-3 grabbing nonintegrin (DC-SIGN, CD209), nonmuscle myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9), heat shock protein member 8 (HSPA8), and the T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain (TIM).

Recent studies using pigs gene-edited via the CRISPR-Cas9 system have shown that pigs lacking functional pCD163 are resistant to PRRSV infection, indicating that this is an essential receptor and confirming that it is an indispensable receptor. [30,31] It was recently discovered that pigs with ligand-binding pouch (LBP) deletions in SRCR5 are resistant to PRRSV-2 infection, which was also confirmed by a cell infection assay in which pCD163 mutants disrupted this region. [32] Taken together, these data demonstrated that the loss of any single disulphide bond in SRCR5 is sufficient to abolish the ability of pCD163 to support PRRSV-2 infection. In recent years, heat shock protein (HSP), a protein primarily found in plant and animal cells, has also been implicated in attachment and internalization during PRRSV infection. Specifically, the coexpression of HSPA8 and pCD163 contributed more to PRRSV infection than did the expression of pCD163 alone. These results indicate that HSPA8 and pCD163 contribute to PRRSV infection. [33] Interestingly, current evidence has demonstrated that MYH9 and pCD163 also play essential roles in PRRSV infection in MARC-145 and porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) cells. This study is the first to identify amino acids 1676–1791 as the key domain of MYH9 responsible for the internalization of PRRSV. [34] Taken together, these findings demonstrate that it is challenging for only one receptor to mediate the entire process of viral infection, even though the use of pCD163 has been reported to be sufficient for converting nonpermissive cell lines to fully permissive cell lines for PRRSV infection. [35] However, it is worth mentioning that there are currently no stable PAM cell lines available that are permissive for PRRSV infection in vitro. Despite being of simian origin, Marc-145 cells are widely utilized for PRRSV isolation, propagation, vaccine production, and basic research due to their high permissiveness to PRRSV infection. [36] Overall, the five stages of viral infection in cells are attachment, internalization, uncoating, replication assembly, and release of the PRRSV virion.

PRRSV entry and internalization into the host cells

The process of PRRSV entry into host cells occurs through a pH-dependent (low pH) membrane-bound receptor-mediated endocytosis. [26] In PAMs, heparan sulphate and sialoadhesin are crucial attachment factors for PRRSV entry, [37] whereas, in MARC-145 cells, CD163 serves as the primary attachment factor. [38,39] Recent evidence supports that PRRSV entry receptors are similar in both PAMs and MARC-145 cells. However, there might be some differences in the utilization of receptor molecules by PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2. [39–41] Primarily, during PRRSV entry into susceptible host cells, the crucial step in this process is the binding of several structural and nonstructural proteins to receptor-mediated endocytosis to boost viral particle adherence and internalization. [41] Initially, there is low-affinity binding between the M-GP5 complex and HS on the cell surface of the PAMs and Marc-145. Subsequently, the PRRSV M-GP5 heterodimer interacts with Sn, which mediates viral internalization. [42] Further evidence supports this affinity binding to facilitate viral internalization. [42–44] Although not necessary for infection, this binding affinity may help concentrate the virus on the cell surface and subsequently facilitate binding to a higher affinity receptor known as pCD163. Interestingly, cellular vimentin, a known receptor for PRRSV, forms a complex with nonstructural protein 2 (nsp2) using the viral nucleocapsid protein as an intermediate. This interaction may be crucial for PRRSV attachment and replication, revealing the specific role of nsp2 in PRRSV replication and immune escape.[36]

Importantly, MYH9 interacts with the GP5 protein of PRRSV through its C-terminal domain. MYH9 acts as a bridge between virus particles attaching to cell surface receptors in either MARC-145 cells or PAMs. This finding claims that the interaction of PRRSV with MYH9 occurs at the early stage of virus infection, and the expression of MYH9 is triggered by PRRSV infection. [45] Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that PRRSV utilizes viral apoptotic mimicry and TIM-induced macropinocytosis to promote infection in both PAMs and MARC-145 cells. Consequently, TIM-1/4 might function only as an attachment factor for PRRSV and an inducer of downstream signalling in micropinocytosis. [46] Importantly, the scavenger receptor pCD163 plays a pivotal role in PRRSV infection by initiating the binding of virus receptors to the GP2a/3/4 complex on the surface of PRRSV, facilitating internalization and uncoating with macrophages [47]. As with many other studies, [48–50] further recent studies confirmed pCD163 as a key receptor in PRRSV infection.[51]

Furthermore, PRRSV was also found to utilize intercellular nanotubes for transporting infectious viral materials like viral RNA, specific replicates, and certain structural proteins into the cytosol of a neighbouring cell. This route of viral transmission involves the interaction of specific viral proteins with cytoskeleton proteins. Importantly, intercellular nanotube transport of viral materials occurs in either MARC-145 cells or PAMs, offering new insights into immune evasion mechanisms and viral pathogenesis. The study revealed that the presence of nanotubes in PAMs was lower than in MARC-145 cells, possibly due to differences in cell morphology and density since PAMs are the primary host cells for PRRSV. Further investigation into which viral proteins could be transported through the nanotubes in MARC-145 cells indicated significant amounts of GP5 proteins and nsp2 were found to be associated with intercellular nanotubes. [52,53] Unfortunately, it is unknown whether GP5 or other viral proteins are directly involved in the transport process of viral materials or are more passive components of nanotubes.

Internalization is primarily initiated by PRRSV entry, where GP4, GP3, and GP2a interact with CD163, thereby dominating viral tropism. Additionally, other factors on the host cell surface also facilitate PRRSV infection. HSPA8 interacts with PRRSV by binding to GP4 through its peptide binding (PB) domain on the cell surface. PRRSV is subsequently internalized into host cells via clathrin-dependent endocytosis (CME), in which HSPA8 also participates. [33] During this process, MYH9 is transported to the cell surface, where it becomes accessible for binding by the PRRSV GP5 protein and interacts with the pCD163 N-terminal domain to enhance virion internalization. [32,54] The interaction between PRRSV GP5 and MYH9 (preferably dimerized MYH9) following attachment both in MARC-145 cells or PAMs also induces polymerization and aggregation of MYH9 to allow myosin filament assembly and acquisition of motor activity. This motor activity then becomes an essential step in endocytosis-mediated PRRSV virion internalization that occurs before the initiation of PRRSV replication is initiated in susceptible cells. [54] Importantly, upon internalization by receptor-mediated endocytosis through small clathrin-like coated early vesicles, PRRSV virion particles are transported to late endosomes for the uncoating process.[55]

PRRSV uncoating

Uncoating is a crucial step in virus infection, during which the lipid envelope is shed, and the viral capsid is disassembled to release the genome and initiate a replication cycle in the cytoplasm. The uncoating process occurs in late endosomes, where an acidic environment triggers fusion between the viral membrane and the endosomal membrane. [55] In this environment, the E-protein ion channels in the viral membrane undergo conformational changes when exposed to low pH in the endosome, allowing ions to enter the virion. This triggers the disassembly of the inner capsid and the release of the viral genome into the cytoplasm, enabling further steps in genome replication and the infection cycle to proceed. [56] Additionally, upon uncoating, the E protein embedded in the virus envelope as an ion channel interacts with the viral proteins GP2a, GP3, and GP4 to form heterogeneous polymer complexes. These complexes promote the shelling of PRRSV-2 and interact with the host proteins cholesterol 25-hydroxylase (CH25H), Cal-1, protein kinase C (PKCδ), and mitochondrial proteins to facilitate the release of the PRRSV genome and regulate the cellular immune response.[12]

PRRSV replication assembly and release

Once the viral genome is released, a series of intracellular modifications occur to complete its replication cycle, including rearrangements of intracellular membrane organelles to generate the replication complex. These modifications involve the formation of perinuclear double-membrane vesicles derived from the endoplasmic reticulum, the synthesis of genomic RNA (gRNA), the transcription of segmented RNA (sgRNA), and the expression of viral proteins. [5,57] In the late stages of replication, mature virions accumulate in intracellular membrane compartments and are subsequently released into the extracellular space through exocytosis. [57] Overall, this mechanism provides a comprehensive understanding of PRRSV infection, leading to new approaches for the design of PRRSV vaccines.

Antiviral molecular mechanisms and molecular structure

The molecular structures of polyphenols

Polyphenols are subdivided into three main classes: flavonoids, stilbenoids, and phenolic acids. Flavonoids, the most abundant class of compounds, consist of two phenyl rings (A and B rings) and an oxygenated heterocyclic ring (C ring). [13] The structure of flavonoids consists of a diphenylpropane (C6–C3–C6, benzo-γ-pyrone) skeleton. Furthermore, when the B ring is moved from position 2 of the C-ring to the carbon atom at position 3, isoflavones are formed, whereas those in which the B ring is linked at position 4 are called neoflavonoids. However, the B ring remains at position 2 of the basic flavane skeleton and can be subdivided into different subgroups depending on the degree of unsaturation and oxidation of the C ring. [58] Phenolic acids are derivatives of benzoic acid (C1–C6 backbone) or cinnamic acid (C3–C6 backbone). Hydroxybenzoic acids are derivatives of benzoic acid with a seven-carbon framework and a C6-C1 structure. Hydroxycinnamic acids are derivatives of cinnamic acids that have the framework of the C6-C3 structure. All these phenolic acids differ among themselves based on the modification of the aromatic ring. Stilbenoids are polyphenols with a 1,2-diphenylethene structure (derivatives of stilbene) and a 14-carbon skeleton (C6–C2–C6 backbone). [13] They exist as monomeric, oligomeric, or polymeric structures. They may also be found free (aglycone) or conjugated as glucosides. Monomers such as resveratrol, pterostilbene, piceatannol, and oxyresveratrol are characterized by the presence of two aromatic rings (A ring and B ring) linked by an olefin. Oligomers are generated from the oxidative coupling of monomers. [59] The general structure of the polyphenol skeleton is shown in Figure 4.

Molecular antiviral mechanisms of polyphenols

Natural compounds are chemical molecules found in plants that have significant biological or pharmacological activity. [60] In recent years, there has been a strong focus on exploring natural compounds as antimicrobial agents, particularly in the search for effective treatments. [61] Bello-Onaghise et al. recently summarized the findings on antiviral strategies used by NPs extracts from traditional Chinese Medicines (TCMs) against PRRSV at different stages of the life cycle, focusing on direct-acting and indirect-acting antivirals. [62] Furthermore, in particular, several small plant-derived polyphenols have been shown to have antiviral effects on viruses, including PRRSV. [15] Importantly, the antiviral effect of polyphenols stems from the interaction between the benzene ring (hydroxyl groups) and viral protein and/or RNA, or their ability to disrupt host cell defence by regulating mitogen-activated protein kinase signalling. [63] Additionally, the use of these natural compounds as entry blockers or inhibitors of protein-protein interactions (PPIs) between PRRSV and CD163 has emerged as a potential antiviral strategy against PRRSV infection. [64] As a result, several bioactive compounds extracted from natural sources, such as polyphenols, have been found to exhibit antagonistic effects on PRRSV. These polyphenols prevent viruses from attaching to receptors or entering cells in vitro, disrupting the process that results in the uncoating of the viral genome. These actions occur mainly through (i) bioactive compound-viral interactions, (ii) increasing the pH of the endocytic pathway, or (iii) modulating the expression of potential receptors. While the antiviral mechanisms may vary depending on the polyphenol molecules, some of these mechanisms are outlined below and summarized in Figure 3.

Antiviral mechanisms of flavonoids

Polyphenols, especially flavonoids such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), are the most abundant catechins in green tea and have been found to inhibit PRRSV replication in MARC-145 cells. [15,65] Previous studies have suggested that the potential mechanisms of viral inhibition include (i) a direct reaction of EGCG with viral particles, causing damage to the virion and blocking virion binding to target cells, and (ii) suppression of viral replication by modifying host and/or viral gene expression [66–69]. Ge et al. [15] demonstrated that treatment of primary PAMs with EGCG confers resistance to viral infection and that multiple factors are responsible for the anti-PRRSV effects of EGCG in MARC-145 cells, including (i) blocking EGCG-treated viruses docking to susceptible cells by downregulating the expression of the PRRSV receptors CD163, MYH9, and HS in a dose-time dependent manner resulting from direct virus-EGCG interaction, (ii) developing resistance in EGCG-pretreated cells through reduced expression of receptors and/or related proteins, and (iii) suppressing PRRSV replication in susceptible cells that were treated with EGCG post-infection. Additionally, EGCG has been reported to block an early step of the hepatitis C virus (HCV) entry process and inhibit viral cell-to-cell transmission [67]. It also exerts its antiviral effect mainly in the early stage of dengue virus infection by interacting directly with the virion [70]. Furthermore, EGCG was also shown to have similar antiviral activities against several unrelated viruses.[71]

Tea polyphenol (TPP) is the collective term for polyphenols found in tea leaves encompasses the total polyphenol content in tea and indicates superior antiviral properties. The primary components of TPP are catechins and their derivatives. [14] For instance, EGCG, which makes up 60–80% of TPP, has previously been recognized for its antiviral impact against PRRSV. [15] TPP has been noted for its various potential mechanisms of viral inhibition, including blocking the attachment and internalization of PRRSV in cells, as well as inhibiting the assembly of viral RTCs after virions enter cells during the virus life cycle. Additionally, TPP strongly inhibited the release of mature PRRSV particles into the extracellular environment. Furthermore, TPP restricts the synthesis of viral nonstructural protein 2 (nsp2), a crucial component of viral replication transcription complexes, which may contribute to the suppression of viral RNA replication. TPP also hinders the PRRSV-induced translocation of NF-κB p65 into the nucleus, thereby suppressing the expression of cytokines, which may contribute to its inhibition of PRRSV. This study further explored the antiviral effects of TPP on different PRRSV strains, including VR-2332, TJM-92 and JXA1, and CHR6. These results indicate that the suppressive effect of TPP on PRRSV is broad-spectrum and not dependent on the strain. [14] Additionally, further studies reported that the inhibitory effect of TPP on coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) may be achieved through a series of mechanisms, including the inhibition of multiple viral targets, the blocking of cellular receptors, and the activation of transcription factors.[63,72]

Proanthocyanidins are a group of naturally occurring polyphenolic bioflavonoids found in fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds, particularly grape seeds, flowers, and bark. [73] Proanthocyanidin A2 (PA2) is a dimer of proanthocyanidin derived from the condensation of catechins and belongs to the family of tea polyphenols. [74] A study reported that PA2 exhibited broad-spectrum and comparable inhibitory activities in vitro with EC50 ranging from 2.2 to 3.2 μg/ml against traditional type 2 PRRSV CH-1a, high pathogenic GD-XH, and GD-HD strains. PA2 strongly inhibited virus replication in PAMs and in Marc-145 cells. Investigating the potential effects of PA2 on PRRSV attachment, internalization as well as progeny virus release, the study found that progeny PRRSV particles were significantly reduced when PA2 was co-incubated with PPRSV infected cells for 2 h at 4°C. Interestingly, further results suggested that the early-stage antiviral effects of PA2 may be mediated directly through PRRSV inactivation or impairment. Unfortunately, the antiviral effects of PA2 on PRRSV, however, did not include the inhibition of viral RNA replication. [74] Furthermore, previous studies have also demonstrated the antiviral mechanisms of proanthocyanidins varying between different virus strains. Gescher et al. showed that proanthocyanidin-enriched extracts from Myrothamnus flabellifolia Welw exhibited antiviral activity against herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) by inhibiting viral binding and internalization. They observed the aggregation of the essential glycoprotein gD from HSV-1 by forming oligomeric structures and speculated that proanthocyanidins might covalently link gD. [75] Similar effects were also reported by Fink et al. on human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1). [76] Additionally, Cheng et al. demonstrated that proanthocyanidin A-1 (an epimer of PA2) suppressed herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) infection by inhibiting viral attachment and internalization, as well as the later stages of HSV-2 infection.[77]

Isobavachalcone (IBC) is a prenylated chalcone derived from the plant Psoralea corylifolia; it is a type of flavonoid with broad-spectrum biological activity against bacteria, fungi, and viruses. [78] According to Wang et al., IBC has been found to block the early stages of the viral life cycle, particularly at the initiation of viral RNA replication. In vitro studies have shown that it effectively inhibits the replication of both HP-PRRSV and the current Chinese epidemic NADC30-like strains without causing significant drug toxicity. A further study reported that IBC inhibits severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) papain-like protease; however, whether IBC inhibits infectious virus was not tested in that study. [79] Yu et al demonstrated that IBC significantly inhibited Pseudorabies virus (PRV) glycoprotein-induced cell-to-cell fusion.[80]

Chalcones (1,3-diaryl-2-propen-1-ones) are an important group of natural compounds that belong to the Flavonoid family. Chemically, chalcones are very similar to flavonoids and are their open and flexible analogs, which are made by connecting two aromatic rings with a three-carbon α-β unsaturated ketone bridge. Despite these similarities, chalcones and flavonoids have some important differences that are noteworthy. Chalcones are more flexible due to having an aliphatic linker in the middle section of the scaffold and creating E and Z structural isoforms, but flavonoids are more rigid structures. [81] DiNap, a synthetic analog of the chalcone flavokawain, has been studied for its potential antiviral activity against PRRSV infection in cell culture systems and in a pig model. DiNap effectively inhibited VR2332 replication in MARC-145 cells and PAMs at concentrations ranging from 0.02 to 0.06 mm [82]. Consistent with these findings, Elkhalifa et al. [83] and Mehdi et al. [81] recently provided a comprehensive summary of the results from studies on the antiviral mechanism of chalcone-based compounds against different viruses.

Sanggenon C (SC) is a flavanone Diels – Alder adduct extracted from the root bark of plants in the mulberry genus. The present study demonstrated that SC effectively inhibited PRRSV infection at a concentration of 10 µM at both the gene and protein levels. Moreover, PRRSV nsp10 activated the NF-κB signalling pathway by inhibiting TRAF2 expression. Further investigation revealed that SC inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signalling pathway by promoting TRAF2 expression, thereby reducing PRRSV infection. [84] Similarly, the study also found that SC efficiently inhibits the replication of porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), porcine circovirus 2 (PCV2), and classical swine fever virus (CSFV) at a concentration of 10 µM, but not PRV replication. [84] Recently, Liu et al. confirmed that SC significantly inhibited the replication of PRRSV in piglets leading to a slight improvement in clinical symptoms.[85]

Naringenin is a flavanone that is most abundant in citrus fruits. [86] In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the biological functions and pharmacological effects of naringenin, which has been found to have a wide range of biological activities. A recent study using the VR2332-like strain showed that naringenin reactivated the RIG-I-MAVS signalling pathway, which was suppressed during the late stage of PRRSV infection, and upregulated the expression of IFN-I and ISGs by targeting RIG-I, an innate immune RNA recognition receptor. [87] These findings demonstrate the potential of naringenin as an effective antiviral drug. In addition, naringenin inhibits HCV secretion and prevents the assembly of infectious virus particles. [88] Studies on HCV, influenza A virus, dengue virus, and Zika virus have further demonstrated that naringenin treatment may be a promising strategy for suppressing viral replication and infection. [89] More notably, naringenin not only exerts a therapeutic effect on COVID-19 by inhibiting its major protease, 3 chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro) [90], and by decreasing the activity of angiotensin-converting enzyme receptors, [91] but it may also modulate the effects of human coronavirus infections by targeting the molecular target, TPC2. [92,93] Naringenin also activates Natural killer (NK) cells’ activity by enhancing NKG2D ligand expression.[94]

Flavonoids such as brazilin, naringenin, brazilein, and catechin are the primary polyphenols derived from Caesalpinia sappan crude (CSC), a well-known medicinal plant from the Leguminosae family found and grown in tropical Asian regions such as southern China. These components of the CSC extract have been shown to inhibit PRRSV replication in MARC-145 cells. [95] Previous research has demonstrated the antiviral activity of CSC extract against PRRSV by inhibiting its replication in vitro in the MARC-145 cell line. [96] However, further investigations are needed to determine the exact mechanism underlying these findings. Furthermore, brazilin from CSC has been also reported to exhibit high activity against the influenza virus (H3N2) in vitro. [97] Additionally, in a recent report by Laksmiani et al., in an in silico model, brazilein and brazilin formed stronger bonds with ACE2, the receptor of SARS-CoV-2. This suggests that these compounds show a high affinity for the target protein involved in coronavirus infection.[98]

Brazilin is the main homoisoflavonoid constituent found in CSC heartwood and exhibits antiviral activity against PRRSV. [95] Studies have shown that brazilin, a major compound in Caesalpinia sappan L. extract, can block PRRSV attachment to the cell surface receptor pCD163 and prevent viral infection. Further research on the binding affinity of compounds for the SRCR5 receptor revealed that, compared with catechin and epicatechin, Brazilin is a more potent and stronger inhibitor of PRRSV infection. This research suggested that inhibiting the interaction of PRRSV-CD163 with brazilin from Caesalpinia sappan may prevent PRRSV infection in pigs. [99] Amit et al. also reported that brazilin from CSC exhibited HIV-1 anti-viral protein R (Vpr) activities at the 10 μM concentration.[100]

Xanthohumol (Xn), a prenylated flavonoid extracted from the hop plant Humulus lupulus L, has been reported to effectively inhibit the early stages of PRRSV infection by preventing PRRSV from attaching to and entering cells. Additionally, it can reduce virally induced oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2–HMOX1 pathway in Marc-145 cells and PAMs, indicating its potential as a therapeutic agent. [101] Subsequent research demonstrated that Xn could also inhibit infection by different subgenotypes of PRRSV in PAMs, with a low 50% inhibition concentration (IC50) value. Additionally, it led to reduced expression of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumour necrosis factor-α in PAMs infected with PRRSV or treated with lipopolysaccharide. [102] Furthermore, Yuxi et al. found that Xn inhibited the main protease (Mpro) activities of SARS-CoV-2 in the enzymatical assays. They also observed that pretreatment with Xn restricted the replication of SARS-CoV-2 and PEDV in Vero-E6 cells. [103] Xn showed a pan-inhibitor activity against alpha- and beta-coronaviruses by targeting Mpro. PRRSV follows a similar assembly process of replicase as coronaviruses, encoding a polyprotein that is cleaved by viral proteins (also known as Mpro and Papain-like protease like SARS-CoV-2). This suggests that Xn may inhibit HIV, HCV, PRRSV, and coronaviruses through a similar mechanism as a protease inhibitor. Additionally, Sai et al. showed that Xn at concentrations of 7.05 µM and 14.11 µM reduced the HCV RNA level to the same level as interferon-alpha. [103] Consistent with these findings, Luigi et al. and Zeng et al. recently summarized the findings of studies on the antiviral mechanism of Xn against positive-sense RNA viruses like HCV, PEDV, PRRSV, and SARS-CoV-2. [104,105]

Scutellarin is a flavone glucuronide derived from Scutellaria barbata and Scutellaria lateriflora. Its pharmacological actions include anti-inflammatory and antiviral effects. Similar results were observed in cells treated with scutellarin and infected with PRRSV. It inhibited the replication of PRRSV by directly inactivating the virus. Additionally, an in vitro study aimed at screening potential anti-PRRSV natural compounds showed that 5-CQA and scutellarin exhibited the highest potential anti-PRRSV activities. [106] Additionally, at a concentration of 433 µM, scutellarin inhibited 48% of the cell-free recombinant HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT) activity. It also caused 82% inhibition of HIV-1 particle attachment and 45% inhibition of fusion at concentrations of 54 µM.[107]

Antiviral mechanisms of phenolic acids

5-O-Caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA) is one of the most common phenolic acid compounds found in many plants. [108,109] Mechanistically, 5-CQA has been reported to directly impede intracellular viral replication by inhibiting the early stages of PRRSV RNA synthesis. Additionally, it has the potential to directly impact the enzyme system responsible for replicating viral genes (RNA) or inhibit the enzyme involved in integrating viral DNA into host DNA (integrase). Wang et al. demonstrated that within safe concentration limits, 5-CQA exhibits inhibitory and blocking effects on PRRSV replication in vitro. [110] Similarly, Cheng et al. reported that the anti-PRRSV activity of 5-CQA could be attributed to its inhibition at an earlier stage of virus replication. In an HIV/AIDS study, derivatives from 5-CQA like 3,4,5–Tricaffeoylquinic acid were found to inhibit HIV-1 integrase with an effective dose (ED) of 1.15 μM and an inhibition concentration (IC) of 0.063 μM. [111] Mcdougall et al. found that dicaffeoylquinic acids (DCQAs) hinder the interaction between HIV-1 gp120 and CD4 receptors, and they also show effectiveness against HIV-1 reverse transcriptase. Additionally, they also reported that only 1-methoxyoxalyl-3,5-DCQA inhibits HIV RNase H. [112] Subsequent studies have shown that 5-CQA displays a 50% inhibition rate against Enterovirus 71, as well as the replication of extracellular DNA and surface antigen of hepatitis B virus. [113] Similarly, Xie et al. reported that a synthesized compound made from caffeic acid derivatives exhibited inhibitory effects on N1 and N2 neuraminidases (NA) of the influenza virus at concentrations of 7.2 μM and 8.5 μM, respectively. The docking results suggested that the dihydroxyphenyl group of the compound synthesized from a caffeic acid derivative could effectively penetrate NA’s active site, forming hydrogen bonds with residues Glu119 and Arg156.[114]

Curcumin, also known as diferuloylmethane, has a chemical formula of (1,7-bis(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)-1,6-heptadiene-3,5-dione). It is a phenolic acid and a natural bioactive compound isolated from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa (family Zingiberaceae) and is commonly known as turmeric. Curcumin is a polyphenol of various organic compounds and has various functions, including antiviral effects. [115,116] In a study involving the treatment of infected MARC-145 cells and PAMs with curcumin, curcumin was found to significantly inhibit the entry of PRRSV-2 into cells by interfering with all post-internalization stages of PRRSV infection. This interference blocked viral membrane fusion and stopped the uncoating of the PRRSV genome in MARC-145 and PAM cells. [117] Furthermore, it has been reported that curcumin may impact membrane fluidity. A previous study found that curcumin decreased the infectivity of HCV particles by reducing the fluidity of viral envelopes, without rupturing virus particles. [118] Since PRRSV particles consist of an isometric core surrounded by a lipid-containing envelope, curcumin could potentially reduce the infectivity of PRRSV by influencing the membrane fluidity of viral envelopes. [117] Computational docking studies showed that the keto-enol and terminal o-hydroxyl group of curcumin have a strong connection to the HIV-1 integrases binding site, which is made up of residues Asp64, His67, Thr66, Glu92, Thr93, Asp116, Ser119, Asn120, and Lys159. In the protease docking, the curcumin structure fits well into the active site, interacting with residues Asp25, Asp29, Asp30, Gly270, Asp290, and Asp300. These findings indicate that o-hydroxyl and/or keto-enol structures play a crucial role in both integrase and protease inhibitory actions in HIV-1 infection. [119] Additionally, Dimas et al. summarized the findings of studies on the antiviral mechanism of curcumin against various types of viruses in cells.[116]

Antiviral mechanisms of stilbenoids

Stilbenoids are a class of nonflavonoid polyphenolic compounds that have been extensively studied in recent decades due to their numerous bioactivities. These compounds act as phyto-alexins, which are secondary metabolites produced by plants as a defence mechanism against pathogens or stress factors. Resveratrol has been the most widely studied stilbenoid in recent decades due to its bioactivities, including its antiviral properties. [59] Additionally, promising results from experimental research have suggested that resveratrol can be used as an antiviral agent against several viruses. [121–123] Mechanistically, studies have demonstrated that resveratrol suppresses herpes simplex virus (HSV) by activating NF-κB within the nucleus in vitro and by regulating the expression of essential immediate-early, early, and late HSV genes, as well as the synthesis of viral DNA. [121] Furthermore, piceatannol (PIC), a tetrahydroxylated stilbenoid also known as 3′‑hydroxy resveratrol, has been reported to have inhibitory effects on various viruses, such as cytomegalovirus, [124] pseudorabies virus [125], and influenza A virus. [126] For instance, PIC has been shown to inhibit the replication of both H1N1 and H3N2 viruses and to block H5N1 pseudovirus infection with low toxicity. PIC can directly act on the envelope of influenza A virus (IAV) to induce the rupture and inactivation of IAV particles. PIC can block membrane fusion by binding to haemagglutinin 2 (HA2) rather than HA1 or the cleavage site of HA0 (HA1 + hA2).127 Unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, there is no published research on the use of natural stilbenoids as PRRSV inhibitors. Consequently, further research is necessary to assess the beneficial effects of various stilbenoids on PRRSV and the underlying antiviral mechanism.

Conclusion and outlook

PRRSV infection begins with the recognition of a specific receptor on the cell, and the ability of the virus to reach the cell depends on the use of these receptors. While there are accumulating data on the interactions between the receptor and PRRSV, there are also conflicting findings. The recent discovery of new PRRSV entry mediators has enhanced the current understanding of the infection process. It is now evident that a single receptor alone cannot facilitate the entire virus infection process, despite reports that pCD163 is capable of converting nonpermissive cell lines to fully permissive ones. In addition, the studies reviewed have demonstrated that polyphenols have a significant antiviral effect on PRRSV, with direct and indirect inhibitory effects on the viral life cycle. This includes blocking viral entry, inhibiting replication, packaging, and assembly, modulating the immune response, and inhibiting pathogenic pathways. Overall, this review outlines the mechanism of PRRSV infection by identifying host cell proteins that interact with various PRRSV proteins discovered in recent years. This review further revealed that polyphenols are potent agents against PRRSV infection and target various stages of the virus life cycle. These data lay the groundwork for the development of new antiviral compounds or vaccines that can specifically target protein receptors and inhibit PRRSV infection.

This review raises important questions for further research, such as understanding the specific mechanism of interaction between natural bioactive compounds in plant extracts and PRRSV virulent proteins and establishing standard procedures to support the efficacy of available polyphenols. Despite several studies on polyphenols, the mechanism of some polyphenols, such as stilbenoids, which have not been well documented in the literature, remains unclear. Research is needed to further understand the anti-PRRSV mechanism of stilbenoids. Additionally, it will be interesting to investigate the role of viral proteins in the intercellular spread of the virus through nanotube connections. The non-glycosylated envelope protein GP5a is encoded by ORF5a, but its role needs additional investigation.

Furthermore, while polyphenols have demonstrated potential for controlling PRRSV infection, their widespread application in treatment is hindered by their low permeability and bioavailability. [127,128] The delivery route significantly impacts a drug’s bioavailability, which refers to the proportion of a drug that enters circulation and can have an active effect. However, nanotechnology has recently shown great success in overcoming the issue of permeability and bioavailability in drug delivery. [60,129] Therefore, polyphenol-containing nanoparticles can enhance the effectiveness of polyphenols in disease treatment and prevention by increasing their permeability and bioavailability. Additionally, targeted nanoparticle delivery of NPs like polyphenols offers a significant advantage in avoiding potential toxicity issues associated with nanoparticles. [60] Moreover, adding adjuvants to polyphenols can also enhance their bioavailability instead of using nanoparticles.
