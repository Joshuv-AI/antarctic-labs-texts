Cutoff for congestion dynamics and related generalized exclusion processes
Ryokichi Tanaka

Abstract
. We consider congestion dynamics with n n players and Q Q resources under the constraint that the number of each resource is κ \kappa and that n < κ ​ Q n<\kappa Q in the regime that n n and κ \kappa diverge but Q Q is fixed with n = ⌊ ρ ​ κ ​ Q ⌋ n=\left\lfloor\rho\kappa Q\right\rfloor for a fixed constant ρ ∈ ( 0 , 1 / 2 ] \rho\in(0,1/2] . We show that the Glauber dynamics and its unlabeled version exhibit cutoff at time ( 1 / 2 ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1/2)n\log n and ( 1 / 2 ) ​ ( 1 − ρ ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1/2)(1-\rho)n\log n in total variation respectively. The unlabeled version is a special case of natural Markov chains for sampling from log M-concave distributions. We also show that a family of Markov chains for uniform sampling on M-convex sets does not necessarily exhibit cutoff.



1.
Introduction

This article aims to study the following special case of
congestion dynamics
.
Let
n
,
Q
and
\kappa
be positive integers.
We consider an evolution of configurations where each one of
n
players adapts one of
Q
resources under the constraint that the number of each resource is
\kappa
and that
n<\kappa Q
.
At each step, one of players chosen uniformly at random releases the current resource and adopt a new one independently with the probability proportional to the number of leftovers.
This model is a special case of Markov chains for sampling from Gibbs distributions (with log-concave potentials) by the Glauber dynamics
[
ALOG+21
]
and
[
Kle23
]
,
and interacting particle systems
[
KL99
]
.
We will discuss the background in Section
1.1
below.

A main focus will be in the regime where the number of players
n
diverges but
Q
is fixed and the ratio
n/(\kappa Q)
is a fixed constant
\rho\in(0,1/2]
up to rounding.
We show that this particular dynamics and its unlabeled version (i.e., players are indistinguishable) reveal abrupt convergences to their unique equilibrium states.
More precisely,
they exhibit
cutoff
at time
(1/2)n\log n
and
(1/2)(1-\rho)n\log n
as
n\to\infty

in the worst case total variation distance respectively.
On the one hand,
the results are expected to hold in a more general setting in the same category we are considering.
On the other hand,
it turns out that the unlabeled process falls into a class of
generalized exclusion processes
on complete graphs (cf.
[
KL99
, Section 4, Chapter 2]
).
The setting extends exclusion processes on these graphs (or, the Bernoulli-Laplace urn model)
for which a cutoff has been studied in
[
DS87
]
,

[
LL11
]
and
[
PP19
]
; see also a more recent result
[
OTS24
]
.

Let us state the precise statements.
The set of
resources
is defined by
S:=\{1,\dots,Q\}
.
For
\sigma=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[n]}\in S^{n}
where
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
,
let

\xi_{v}(\sigma):=\#\big\{i\in[n]\ :\ \sigma_{i}=v\big\}\quad\text{for each $v\in S$}.

In the above
\#
of a set stands for the cardinality.
The space of
configurations
is

{\mathcal{S}}_{n}:=\big\{\sigma\in S^{n}\ :\ \xi_{v}(\sigma)\leq\kappa\quad\text{for all $v\in S$}\big\}.

This is the state space where an evolution takes place,
and each
\sigma
indicates an assignment to each player one of
Q
resources keeping each resource being adapted by at most
\kappa
players.

Let us fix a real
\rho\in(0,1)
.
We define
N:=\kappa Q
and
n=\left\lfloor\rho N\right\rfloor
,
where
\left\lfloor r\right\rfloor
of a real
r
is the largest integer at most
r
.
Let us consider the function
\varphi:\{0,1,\dots,\kappa\}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
defined by

\varphi(s)=\log(\kappa+1)-\log(\kappa+1-s).

The
potential

\Phi:{\mathcal{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is defined by
\Phi(\sigma):=\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{s=0}^{\xi_{v}(\sigma)}\varphi(s)
.
The associated
Gibbs distribution
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
has the form

\mu_{n}(\sigma):=\frac{1}{Z_{n}}e^{-\Phi(\sigma)}\quad\text{where $Z_{n}:=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}e^{-\Phi(\sigma)}$}.

The process on the state space
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
we are considering evolves by the
Glauber dynamics
:
Given a configuration
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
,
first choose
i\in[n]
uniformly at random,
then replace
\sigma
by
\sigma^{\prime}
according to the Gibbs distribution conditioned that
\sigma^{\prime}
coincides with
\sigma
except for
i
.
In this special form of function
\varphi
,
the process coincides with the one described in the beginning (see Section
1.2
for more details).

This defines a Markov chain
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
,
and we call it a
congestion dynamics
.
It is
reversible
with respect to
\mu_{n}
,
i.e.,

\mu_{n}(\sigma)p(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\mu_{n}(\sigma^{\prime})p(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma)

for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.
Moreover, it is
irreducible
,
i.e.,
for every pair
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma
,
there exists
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}

such that
\sigma(t)=\sigma
and
\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}
with a positive probability
if
n<N=\kappa Q
(cf. Section
1.2
).
Further the chain is
aperiodic
since it has a holding probability
p(\sigma,\sigma)>0

for each
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.
This implies that
\mu_{n}
is the unique stationary distribution for the chain,
and the distribution for
\sigma(t)
with an arbitrary initial condition converges to
\mu_{n}

as
t
tends to infinity.
Let
{\bf P}_{\sigma_{0}}
be the distribution of the process
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
with the initial state
\sigma_{0}
.

Let us define the (worst case) total variation
mixing time
for
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.
Let

D_{\rm TV}(t):=\max_{\sigma_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}D_{\rm TV}^{\sigma_{0}}(t)\quad\text{where $D_{\rm TV}^{\sigma_{0}}(t):=\max_{A\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}|{\bf P}_{\sigma_{0}}(\sigma(t)\in A)-\mu_{n}(A)|$}.

For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
we define

T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon):=\min\big\{t\geq 0\ :\ D_{\rm TV}(t)\leq\varepsilon\big\}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Q
be a fixed integer at least two,
\rho
be a fixed real in
(0,1/2]
.
For a positive integer
\kappa
, let
n=\left\lfloor\rho\kappa Q\right\rfloor
.
For the Markov chain
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
,
it holds that for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
there exist constant
C_{\varepsilon}
and
N_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,

\left|T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}n\log n\right|\leq C_{\varepsilon}n.

This in particular establishes cutoff at time
(1/2)n\log n
with window of order
n
.
The term which depends on
\varepsilon
is not in the dominating order
n\log n
but within a smaller order
n
.
For background and other basic results, see
[
LP17
, Chapter 18]
.
We discuss more about this phenomenon and related results in Section
1.1
below.

Associated to the chain of configurations,
we consider its unlabeled version,
namely,
we only count the number of players who adapt resources.
Let us define the
load profile

\xi(t):=(\xi_{v}(t))_{v\in S}\quad\text{where $\xi_{v}(t):=\xi_{v}(\sigma(t))$ for each $v\in S$}.

This defines a Markov chain on the set

\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}:=\left\{\xi=(\xi_{v})_{v\in S}\in\{0,1,\dots,\kappa\}^{S}\ :\ \sum_{v\in S}\xi_{v}=n\right\}.

The chain is irreducible if
n<N=\kappa Q
,
aperiodic and has a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{n}
.
Let
{\bf P}_{\xi_{0}}
be the distribution of the process
\{\xi(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
with the initial state
\xi_{0}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
.
Let us consider the worst case total variation mixing time for
\{\xi(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
against
\pi_{n}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
analogously to the case of
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
Q
be a fixed integer at least two,

\rho
be a fixed real in
(0,1/2]
.
For a positive integer
\kappa
,
let
n=\left\lfloor\rho\kappa Q\right\rfloor
.
For the Markov chain
\{\xi(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
,
it holds that for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
there exist constants
C_{\varepsilon}
and
N_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
,

\left|T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho)n\log n\right|\leq C_{\varepsilon}n.

The load profile chain exhibits cutoff but at time different from the original congestion dynamics of configurations.
In fact, Theorem
1.2
can also be deduced as a time change of the exclusion processes of unlabeled particles on complete graphs
[
LL11
, Theorem 1.1]
(see discussions in Section
1.1
).
It is illustrative to write the statement in its equivalent form:
For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho)\rho N\log N+O_{\varepsilon}(N)\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$}.

This reveals the equivalence between the numbers of players
n
and
N-n
.
Namely,
considering the process
(\kappa-\xi_{v}(t))_{v\in S}
for
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
amounts to the load profile chain with
N-n
players under the same constraint
\kappa
on each resource.
Thus there is no loss of generality to assume that
\rho\in(0,1/2]
.
The Glauber dynamics itself, however, does not (at least apparently) have the same kind of equivalence.
We leave open as to whether Theorem
1.1
holds for all
\rho\in(0,1)
.

1.1.
Background and related results

This work arises from an attempt to find an explicit class of examples which exhibit cutoff
in sampling problems of (discrete counterparts of) log-concave distributions and their generalizations.
There has been a significant progress on understanding mixing times of natural Markov chains with stationary distributions whose generating polynomials are (strongly) log-concave
[
ALOGV19
]
and
[
ALOGV24
]
.
An important class includes the base-exchange chain of a given matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=([N],{\mathcal{I}})
on the ground set
[N]
of rank
n
.
It has been shown that for this chain
T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)=O(n\log(n/\varepsilon))
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

[
ALOG+21
, a special case of Theorem 1]
developed upon
[
CGM21
]
and
[
ALOGV19
]
.
The order is optimal since there is a lower bound
\Omega(n\log n)
, which follows from a coupon collector problem.
Given the results, it is tempting to understand a cutoff for this type of chains,
or to find a reasonably wide class of chains for which we are able to establish an abrupt convergence.

In view of generalizations and algorithmic perspective,
it is natural to consider sampling problems from log M-convex sets or its distribution counterparts in the discrete convex analysis
[
Mur03
]
.
We say that a probability measure
\pi
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q}
is
log M-concave
if
\pi
is supported in the set

\{\xi\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{Q}\xi_{i}=n\}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and the following holds:
For all
\xi
and
\tilde{\xi}
in the support and for every
i\in[Q]
with
\xi_{i}>\tilde{\xi}_{i}
,
there exists
j\in[Q]
such that
\xi_{j}<\tilde{\xi}_{j}
and

\log\pi\big(\xi\big)+\log\pi\big(\tilde{\xi}\,\big)\leq\log\pi\big(\xi-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\big)+\log\pi\big(\tilde{\xi}-\bm{e}_{j}+\bm{e}_{i}\big).

In the above,
\bm{e}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,Q
, denotes the standard basis in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q}
.
The load profile chain on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
has the stationary distribution
\pi_{n}
,
which is obtained by the product of binomial distributions conditioned on
\sum_{v\in S}\xi_{v}=n
(cf. Section
1.2
).
The measure
\pi_{n}
is log M-concave as it is directly checked by this explicit form.
More generally,
if the function
m\mapsto\log(m!)+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\varphi(i)
is convex,
then
\pi_{n}
is log M-concave.
The function
\varphi
may also vary in
v\in S
;
see
[
Mur03
, Example 6.3, Chapter 6]
for a thorough discussion.
It would be interesting to generalize our result in this setting.
We, however, point out that a cutoff does not necessarily occur for an analogous Markov chain on an M-convex set with the uniform stationary distribution.
This corresponds to the case when
\log\pi_{n}
is constant on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
and
n\leq\kappa
(see Section
A
).

From the viewpoint of interacting particle systems,
the load profile chain is a generalization of exclusion process,
where each site is occupied by at most one indistinguishable particle.
Concerning exclusion processes, mixing times on general underlying graphs (not necessarily complete graphs) have been studied in
[
Oli13
]
.
For relaxation times in this context, see a recent work
[
KW23
]
.
In the case of complete graphs on
n
vertices
(or, equivalently the Bernoulli-Laplace urn model),
the exclusion process of
k
-(unlabeled) particles is defined as follows:
Starting from a configuration of particles where each site has at most one particle on the graph,
independently in each step we choose an (undirected) edge uniformly at random and swap the contents of extremes of the edge.
For the unlabeled particles,
a cutoff has been established by
[
DS87
, Theorem 1]

(for the case of
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
particles),
and by
[
LL11
, Theorem 1.1]
.
The proof of the former is representation theoretic whereas that of the latter is probabilistic.
In the latter, it has been shown that for the case of
k_{n}\in[0,n]
particles and
k_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
a cutoff occurs at time
(1/4)n\log n
with window of order
n
.
Also, in the case of
k_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to 0
and
k_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
or in the case of
k_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to l
for some
l\in(0,\infty)
as
n\to\infty
,
a cutoff occurs at time
(1/2)n\log n
with window of order
n
.
Recently, it is announced in
[
OTS24
]

that the (different) limiting profiles have been identified in these three cases.
See also
[
NOT22
]
for other results and recent developments in this direction.

The cutoff for the load profile chain in Theorem
1.2
actually follows by a time change from the above mentioned result in
[
LL11
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Indeed, let us consider the exclusion process of
n
-unlabeled particles on the complete graph on
N
-vertices, and
n/N\to\rho\in(0,1)
, in particular
n/\sqrt{N}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.
Further each vertex is colored by one of
Q
different colors and the number of vertices with a common color is
\kappa
and
N=\kappa Q
.
Independently in each step,
if a chosen edge has the extremes with the same contents (i.e., either both have particles or both have no particles),
then that step is not counted.
This possible skip does not change the
configuration since particles are unlabeled.
Under this time change, the resulting chain is essentially the load profile chain itself.
This speeded-up exclusion process has the mixing time asymptotically equal to

\frac{2n(N-n)}{N(N-1)}\frac{1}{4}N\log N\sim\frac{1}{2}\rho(1-\rho)N\log N.

We produce a self-contained proof of Theorem
1.2
adapted to the present setting.
Some of the technical results obtained along the proof are used to show Theorem
1.1
.

The exclusion process with labeled particles has been studied in
[
LL11
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
PP19
]
.
A different generalization of the Bernoulli-Laplace urn model (for many urns) is discussed in
[
Sca97
]
.

1.2.
More on the setting

Let us describe more in details on the Glauber dynamics in Theorem
1.1
.
The chain is irreducible if
n<\kappa Q
.
Indeed, this follows since given
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
and a distinct pair
i,j\in[n]

the configuration
\sigma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
obtained from
\sigma
by exchanging
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
can be reached within at most
3
steps.
The transition probability is defined by

p(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\frac{e^{-\Phi(\sigma^{\prime})}}{\sum_{\tau_{j}=\sigma_{j},j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}e^{-\Phi(\tau)}}\quad\text{for $\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}$},

if
\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{\prime}
for
j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}
,
and
p(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=0
otherwise.
Recall that
\Phi(\sigma)=\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{s=0}^{\xi_{v}(\sigma)}\varphi(s)
and

\varphi(s)=\log(\kappa+1)-\log(\kappa+1-s)
.
If
\sigma_{i}=v_{0}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=v_{1}
with
v_{0}\neq v_{1}
,
then

p(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\frac{e^{-\varphi(\xi_{v_{1}}(\sigma)+1)}}{e^{-\varphi(\xi_{v_{0}}(\sigma))}+\sum_{v\neq v_{0}}e^{-\varphi(\xi_{v}(\sigma)+1)}}=\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v_{1}}(\sigma)}{n(1+N-n)}.

Furthermore,
we have

p(\sigma,\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+\kappa-\xi_{\sigma_{i}}(\sigma)}{n(1+N-n)}.

For the load profile chain in Theorem
1.2
,
each transition from
\xi
to
\xi^{\prime}
occurs in the following way:
First
v_{-}\in S
is chosen with probability
\xi_{v_{-}}/n
.
Then we have
\xi^{\prime}=\xi
with probability
(1+\kappa-\xi_{v_{-}})/(1+N-n)
,
or
v_{+}\in S\setminus\{v_{-}\}
is chosen with probability

(\kappa-\xi_{v_{+}})/(1+N-n)
and obtain
\xi^{\prime}
from
\xi
by decrementing
\xi_{v_{-}}
by
1
and then incrementing
\xi_{v_{+}}
by
1
.
Equivalently,
choose
v_{-}\in S
with probability
\xi_{v_{-}}/n
.
We define
\xi^{\prime}=\xi
with probability
1/(1+N-n)
and otherwise define
\xi^{\prime}
as follows:
Choose
v_{+}\in S
independently with probability
(\kappa-\xi_{v_{+}})/(N-n)
.
If
v_{-}=v_{+}
, then we define
\xi^{\prime}=\xi
, and if
v_{-}\neq v_{+}
,
then

\xi_{v}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}\xi_{v}-1&\text{if $v=v_{-}$},\\
\xi_{v}+1&\text{if $v=v_{+}$, and}\\
\xi_{v}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Note that the holding (i.e.,
\xi^{\prime}=\xi
) occurs with probability

\sum_{v\in S}\frac{\xi_{v}}{n}\frac{1+\kappa-\xi_{v}}{1+N-n}.

Let us define the distribution
\pi_{n}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
by

\pi_{n}(\xi):=\frac{1}{Z_{n,S,\kappa}}\prod_{v\in S}\frac{1}{\xi_{v}!(\kappa-\xi_{v})!}\quad\text{where $Z_{n,S,\kappa}:=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}}\prod_{v\in S}\frac{1}{\xi_{v}!(\kappa-\xi_{v})!}$}.

In the above we understand
0!=1
.
The chain
\{\xi(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
is reversible with respect to
\pi_{n}
.
Indeed, for each distinct pair
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
with
\xi_{v_{-}}^{\prime}=\xi_{v_{-}}-1
,

\xi_{v_{+}}^{\prime}=\xi_{v_{+}}+1
and
\xi_{v}^{\prime}=\xi_{v}
for
v\neq v_{-},v_{+}
,
we have

\frac{\xi_{v_{-}}}{n}\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v_{+}}}{1+N-n}{\kappa\choose\xi_{v_{-}}}{\kappa\choose\xi_{v_{+}}}=\frac{\xi_{v_{+}}+1}{n}\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v_{-}}+1}{1+N-n}{\kappa\choose\xi_{v_{+}}+1}{\kappa\choose\xi_{v_{-}}-1}.

This implies the required reversibility.

1.3.
Outlines of the proofs

First we discuss Theorem
1.2
on the load profile chain and then mention Theorem
1.1
on the congestion dynamics.
The latter uses some of results obtained along the proof of the former.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
on the load profile chain proceeds as follows.
Recall that
n=\left\lfloor\rho N\right\rfloor
and
\rho\in(0,1/2]
.
For this chain,
the stationary distribution
\pi_{n}
is the product of
Q
copies of binomial distributions on
\{0,\dots,\kappa\}
conditioned on that
\sum_{v\in S}\xi_{v}=n
.
Under
\pi_{n}
,
the normalized vector
(\xi_{v}/N)_{v\in S}
is concentrated around the constant vector
(\rho/Q)_{v\in S}
within the order
O\big(1/\sqrt{N}\big)
.
For the normalized load profile chain,
the time to reach the constant vector within that order is
(1/2)(1-\rho)\rho N\log N
(Lemma
2.3
).
After that period,
we construct a coupling to coalesce the chain and another chain starting from the stationary distribution.
The coupling period takes
O(n)
with high probability (Lemma
2.4
).
This proves the desired upper bound for the mixing time (Theorem
2.5
).
For the lower bound,
we exhibit a worst initial state yielding the tight bound (Theorem
2.6
).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
on the congestion dynamics elaborates this analysis of the load profile chain.
We consider the
load matrix chain

(\xi_{u,v}(\sigma(t)))_{u,v\in S}
.
This counts in a configuration
\sigma(t)
the number of sites in
[n]
where the state is
u
at time
0
and the state is
v
at time
t
(see Section
3
).
After a short
O(n)
time,
it boils down to consider this load matrix chain by exploiting a mean-field nature in this model (Lemma
3.1
).
Analyzing the dynamics for an appropriately normalized load matrix chain,
we show that the time
(1/2)n\log n
is needed to reach a typical matrix within order
O\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)
(Lemma
3.4
).
After that period,
we construct a coupling which coalesces two processes in
O(n)
time with high probability,
combined with the process for the number of vacant sites
(\kappa-\xi_{v})_{v\in S}
(Lemma
3.5
).
This coupling is developed upon the one for the load profile chain in Section
2
.
This leads to the required upper bound for the mixing time (Theorem
3.6
).
For the lower bound, we exhibit a worst initial state and show that the mentioned analysis is optimal by using a variance bound for the load matrix chain (Theorem
3.7
).

Notation

We write
c
,
C
,
C^{\prime}
,
C^{\prime\prime}
, …, for constants whose exact values possibly change from line to line,
and for
c_{\delta}
a constant which depends on the other constant
\delta
to indicate its dependency.
For real valued sequences
\{f(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
and
\{g(n)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
such that
g(n)>0
on non-negative integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,
we write
f(n)=O(g(n))

if there exist constants
C\geq 0
and
N
such that

|f(n)|\leq Cg(n)
for all
n\geq N
.
Further we write
f(n)=o(g(n))
if
f(n)/g(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
For a real
x
,
we mean by
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
the largest integer at most
x
and by
\left\lceil x\right\rceil
the smallest integer at least
x
.
For a set
A
, we write
A^{\sf c}
for the complement set and

2.
The load profile chain

Let us consider the load profile chain
\{\xi(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
and recall
N=\kappa Q
.
The associated filtration is denoted by
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
is the sigma algebra generated by
\xi(s)
for
s=0,\dots,t
.
For each
v\in S
and each
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,
we have

\xi_{v}(t+1)-\xi_{v}(t)=\frac{n-\xi_{v}(t)}{n}\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v}(t)}{1+N-n}-\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{n}\frac{N-n-(\kappa-\xi_{v}(t))}{1+N-n}+M_{t+1},

where
|M_{t+1}|\leq 2
and
{\bf E}\,[M_{t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]=0
almost surely.
Rearranging the terms,
we obtain

\xi_{v}(t+1)-\xi_{v}(t)=\frac{\kappa}{1+N-n}-\frac{N}{1+N-n}\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{n}+M_{t+1}.

(2.1)

For the discussion below,
it is useful to write the equation in the following way:
For each
v\in S
and each
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,

\xi_{v}(t+1)-\xi_{v}(t)=\frac{\kappa}{N-n}-\frac{N}{N-n}\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{n}+\widehat{M}_{t+1},

where
|\widehat{M}_{t+1}|\leq C
and
|{\bf E}\,[\widehat{M}_{t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq C/N
almost surely.
Further
C
is a constant depending only on
\rho
.
Note that
\rho-1/N\leq n/N\leq\rho
and that
N=\kappa Q
where
Q
is a fixed constant.
Abusing the notation
\widehat{M}_{t+1}
and changing the constant
C
,
we have

\xi_{v}(t+1)-\xi_{v}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-\rho)Q}-\frac{1}{(1-\rho)\rho N}\xi_{v}(t)+\widehat{M}_{t+1}.

This leads to

\frac{\xi_{v}(t+1)}{N}-\frac{\rho}{Q}=\left(1-\frac{1}{(1-\rho)\rho N}\right)\left(\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{N}-\frac{\rho}{Q}\right)+\frac{\widehat{M}_{t+1}}{N}.

(2.2)

Simplifying notations,
we write

\Delta(t):=(\Delta_{v}(t))_{v\in S}\quad\text{where $\Delta_{v}(t):=\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{N}-\frac{\rho}{Q}$}.

Let us define

\gamma:=\frac{1}{(1-\rho)\rho}.

Squaring both sides of (
2.2
)
and summing over
v\in S
yields the following:

\|\Delta(t+1)\|^{2}=\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{N}\right)^{2}\|\Delta(t)\|^{2}+M_{t+1}^{\prime},

where
|M_{t+1}^{\prime}|\leq CQ/N
and
|{\bf E}\,[M_{t+1}^{\prime}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq CQ/N^{2}
almost surely.
In the above
\|\cdot\|
denotes the standard Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{Q}
.
Rearranging terms leads to

\|\Delta(t+1)\|^{2}=\left(1-\frac{2\gamma}{N}\right)\|\Delta(t)\|^{2}+\widetilde{M}_{t+1}.

(2.3)

In the above
|\widetilde{M}_{t+1}|\leq CQ/N
and
|{\bf E}\,[\widetilde{M}_{t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq CQ/N^{2}
almost surely.
This shows that for each
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, almost surely

{\bf E}\,[\|\Delta(t+1)\|^{2}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]\leq\left(1-\frac{2\gamma}{N}\right)\|\Delta(t)\|^{2}+\frac{CQ}{N^{2}}.

(2.4)

The following is a general result which we use throughout.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{x_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}

be real valued random variables with the associated filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
.
Suppose that there exist constants
\lambda,C>0
such that for all
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
N>0
,

x_{t+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)x_{t}+M_{t+1}\quad\text{where $|M_{t+1}|\leq\frac{C}{N}$ and $|{\bf E}\,[M_{t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq\frac{C}{N^{2}}$ almost surely}.

Then there exist constants
c,R_{0}>0
such that
for all
R\geq R_{0}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
N>0
,

{\bf P}_{x_{0}}\left(x_{t}\geq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{t}x_{0}+\frac{R}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\leq e^{-cR^{2}}.

Proof.

Let us define inductively
\overline{x}_{0}:=x_{0}
and for
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,

\overline{x}_{t+1}:=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)\overline{x}_{t}+M_{t+1}.

(2.5)

It holds that
x_{t}\leq\overline{x}_{t}
almost surely for each
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Hence it suffices to show the claim for
\{\overline{x}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
, and thus we assume that
\{x_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
satisfies (
2.5
).
By assumption,
almost surely

{\bf E}\,[x_{t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)x_{t}+\frac{C}{N^{2}}.

Therefore it holds that almost surely

\displaystyle{\bf E}\,\left[x_{t+1}-\frac{C}{N\lambda}\,\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)x_{t}-\frac{C}{N\lambda}+\frac{C}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)x_{t}-\frac{C(1-\lambda/N)}{N\lambda}=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)\left(x_{t}-\frac{C}{N\lambda}\right).

Letting
z_{t}:=(1-\lambda/N)^{-t}(x_{t}-C/(N\lambda))
,
we find that
\{z_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
forms a supermartingale with respect to
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
.
Note that by (
2.5
),

\displaystyle|z_{t+1}-z_{t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t-1}M_{t+1}-\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t-1}\frac{C}{N\lambda}+\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t}\frac{C}{N\lambda}\right|

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t-1}\left|M_{t+1}-\frac{C}{N^{2}}\right|\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t-1}\frac{2C}{N}.

Thus the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality yields for all
R\geq 0
,

\displaystyle{\bf P}_{x_{0}}\left(z_{t}\geq z_{0}+\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{-t}R\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\lambda/N)^{-2t}R^{2}}{2\sum_{s=0}^{t}(1-\lambda/N)^{-2s-2}(2C/N)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}N^{2}}{8C^{2}}(1-\lambda/N)^{4}((1-\lambda/N)^{-2}-1)\right)\leq\exp(-cR^{2}N).

Replacing
R
by
R/\sqrt{N}
in the above inequalities,
we obtain

{\bf P}_{x_{0}}\left(x_{t}-\frac{C}{N\lambda}\geq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{N}\right)^{t}\left(x_{0}-\frac{C}{N\lambda}\right)+\frac{R}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\leq e^{-cR^{2}}.

Shifting
R
to
R-C/(\lambda\sqrt{N})
and choosing a smaller
c>0
yields the required inequality.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
Q
be a fixed integer at least two,

\rho
be a fixed real in
(0,1)
.
For a positive integer
\kappa
,
let
N=\kappa Q
and
n=\left\lfloor\rho N\right\rfloor
.
For the chain
\{\sigma(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
,
letting
t_{n}:=\left\lceil(1/2)n\log n\right\rceil
,
we have

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}D_{\rm TV}\left(t_{n}-\left\lfloor\alpha n\right\rfloor\right)=1.

Proof.

Fix an initial state
\sigma_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
satisfying that for all
u\in S
,

\left|\frac{\xi_{u}(\sigma_{0})}{n}-\frac{1}{Q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Note that such
\sigma_{0}
exists for each
n>0
.
By (
3.1
) and the expectations,
it holds that

\displaystyle\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}-{\bf E}\,\frac{\kappa-\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}\right)\frac{\xi_{u}(\sigma_{0})}{n}

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)+\widehat{M}_{u,v,t+1}-{\bf E}\,\widehat{M}_{u,v,t+1}.

In the above
|\widehat{M}_{u,v,t+1}|\leq C
and
|{\bf E}\,[\widehat{M}_{u,v,t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq C/n
almost surely.
Squaring both sides yields the following:

\displaystyle\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}+{\bf E}\,\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\xi_{u}(\sigma_{0})}{n}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\left(-\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}+{\bf E}\,\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}\right)\frac{\xi_{u}(\sigma_{0})}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)+\widetilde{M}_{u,v,t+1}.

In the above
|\widetilde{M}_{u,v,t+1}|\leq C^{\prime}n
and
|{\bf E}\,[\widetilde{M}_{u,v,t+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]|\leq C^{\prime}
almost surely.
Summing over
u\in S
yields the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in S}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}-{\bf E}\,\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{Q}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\sum_{u\in S}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-2\left(\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}-{\bf E}\,\frac{\xi_{v}(t)}{(1-\rho)N}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{u\in S}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)+O(1)+\sum_{u\in S}\widetilde{M}_{u,v,t+1}.

Noting that

\sum_{u\in S}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)=\xi_{v}(t)-{\bf E}\,\xi_{v}(t)
,
we have by the expectations,

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{Q(1-\rho)^{2}N^{2}}{\rm Var}\left(\xi_{v}(t)\right)

\displaystyle-2\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{1}{Q(1-\rho)N}{\rm Var}\left(\xi_{v}(t)\right)+O(1).

We note that for all large enough
n
,

\frac{1}{Q(1-\rho)^{2}N^{2}}<2\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\frac{1}{Q(1-\rho)N}.

Therefore it holds that

\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t+1)\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)+C.

Iterating in
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
shows that since
{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(0)\right)=0
,

\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2t}\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\xi^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(0)\right)+Cn=Cn.

In the normalized form
\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)
,
for each
v\in S
, for all
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and for all large enough
n
,

\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}\left(\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}(t)\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{n}.

In particular, letting

yields

\sum_{u\in S}{\rm Var}_{\mu_{n}}\left(\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,v}\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{n}.

Let us fix an arbitrary
u\in S
, and focus on the case when
v=u
.
By the assumption on the initial state
\sigma_{0}
and by (
2.2
),
for all large enough
n
and all

,

\left|{\bf E}\,\frac{\xi_{u}(t)}{n}-\frac{1}{Q}\right|\leq\frac{C}{n}.

This combined with (

) implies that for all
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,

{\bf E}\,\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,u}(t+1)-\frac{1}{Q}=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\left({\bf E}\,\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,u}(t)-\frac{1}{Q}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Thus by iteration in
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
yields

{\bf E}\,\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,u}(t)=\frac{1}{Q}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{Q}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

In the above we have used
\overline{\xi}^{\sigma_{0}}_{u,u}(0)=1
by the assumption on
\sigma_{0}
.
For

for

,

The estimate on the variance implies that

D_{\rm TV}(t_{\alpha,n})=1-O\left(e^{-2\alpha}\right).

Letting

and then

shows the claim.

Appendix A
Uniform sampling on M-convex sets

Let
Q
and
n
be positive integers.
An
M-convex set

B
is a subset of

\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{Q}x_{i}=n\right\}

such that the following holds:
For all
x,y\in B
with
x\neq y
,
for every
i\in[Q]
with
x_{i}>y_{i}

there exists
j\in[Q]
with
y_{j}>x_{j}
,
satisfying that
x-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\in B
and
y-\bm{e}_{j}+\bm{e}_{i}\in B
.
For example,
for all positive integers
Q
and
n
,
the following set is M-convex:

B^{(Q)}(n):=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{Q}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{Q}x_{i}=n\right\}.

This set coincides with
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
if
n\leq\kappa
;
the state space of load profile chains
for positive integers
\kappa
,
Q
and
n

(for the notation, see Section
1
).
Note that for
n\leq Q
,

{\mathcal{I}}:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})\in\{0,1\}^{Q}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{Q}x_{i}\leq n\right\}

defines a (uniform) matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=\left([Q],{\mathcal{I}}\right)

[
Oxl11
, Example 1.2.7, Chapter 2]
.
Moreover,

B_{{\mathcal{I}}}:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})\in\{0,1\}^{Q}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{Q}x_{i}=n\right\}

forms the set of bases in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The notion of M-convex set generalizes the set of bases in a matroid.
See
[
Mur03
]
for background.

Every M-convex set
B

admits a natural Markov chain with the uniform stationary distribution.
The transition probability is defined as follows:
Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{Q})\in B
.
An
i\in[Q]
is chosen uniformly at random,
and then independently a
j
is chosen uniformly at random from

\Big\{j\in[Q]\ :\ x-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\in B\Big\}.

Note that the above set is not empty since it contains
i
.
Letting
y:=x-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}
in
B

defines the transition from
x
to
y
.
The chain is irreducible since
B
is M-convex,
and is aperiodic since it has a holding probability at each state.
Further it is symmetric.
Indeed,
for
x,y\in B
with
y=x-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}
,
the transition from
x
to
y
occurs with probability

\frac{1}{Q\#\{j\in[Q]\ :\ x-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\in B\}}.

This coincides with the transition probability from
y
to
x
.
In the case when
x_{i}=0
,
then
j=i
almost surely and
y=x
.
Therefore the chain is reversible with respect to the uniform distribution,
which is the unique stationary distribution.
Let us restrict ourselves to the special case when

B=B^{(Q)}(n).

A particular feature of this case is that given a state
x
,
if the first index
i
satisfies that
x_{i}>0
,
then the second index
j
is always chosen uniformly from the whole
[Q]
.
If we identify
B^{(Q)}(n)
with
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
for
n\leq\kappa
,
then
the uniform distribution
{\rm Unif}_{n}
on
\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}
is obtained as follows:
For an arbitrary real
\lambda>0
,

{\rm Unif}_{n}(\xi)=\frac{1}{Z_{\lambda}}\prod_{v\in S}\frac{\lambda^{\xi_{v}}}{1+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^{\kappa}}\quad\text{where $Z_{\lambda}:=\frac{\lambda^{n}}{(1+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^{\kappa})^{Q}}$}\cdot\#\Xi_{n,S,\kappa}.

We consider the lazy version of the chain (i.e. the original process is delayed with
1/2
-holding probability in each step) for an upper bound on the mixing time in total variation.

Proposition A.1
.

For each integer
Q
at least two and for each real
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
n>0
,

T^{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\leq C_{\varepsilon}n^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
and
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
be the lazy Markov chains with the common transition probability,
and we construct a coupling between them in the following.
Suppose that
X_{t}=x
and
Y_{t}=y
.
If
x=y
,
then we run the chains with common transitions.
Otherwise if
x\neq y
,
then we flip an independent fair coin
\chi\in\{{\sf head},{\sf tail}\}
and independently choose
i\in[Q]
uniformly at random.
Let

\left\{{\text{$x=0$ or $y=0$}}\right\}:=\left\{i\in[Q]\ :\ x_{i}\cdot y_{i}=0\right\},\quad\left\{x>y>0\right\}:=\left\{i\in[Q]\ :\ x_{i}>y_{i}>0\right\}.

Similarly, we define

\left\{x=y>0\right\}:=\left\{i\in[Q]\ :\ x_{i}=y_{i}>0\right\},\quad\left\{y>x>0\right\}:=\left\{i\in[Q]\ :\ y_{i}>x_{i}>0\right\}.

Analogously we write
\left\{x>y=0\right\}
and
\left\{y>x=0\right\}
.
It holds that

[Q]=\left\{x>y>0\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{y>x>0\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{\text{$x=0$ or $y=0$}\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{x=y>0\right\}.

In the above some of the sets are possibly empty.

(1) Case

i\in\left\{x>y>0\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{y>x>0\right\}

The second index
j
is chosen from
[Q]
uniformly at random both for
X_{t}
and for
Y_{t}
.

(1a) Case

j\in\left\{x>y>0\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{y>x>0\right\}

If
\chi={\sf head}
,
then we run
X_{t}
to the next with the chosen
i
and
j
, and keep
Y_{t}
.
Namely,

X_{t+1}:=X_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1}:=y.

Further,
if
\chi={\sf tail}
,
then we change the roles of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
and run
Y_{t}
to the chosen
i
and
j
,
and keep
X_{t}
.

(1b) Case

j\notin\left\{x>y>0\right\}\bigsqcup\left\{y>x>0\right\}

If
\chi={\sf head}
,
then we move
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
together with the chosen
i
and
j
.
Namely,

X_{t+1}:=X_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1}:=Y_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j},

and if
\chi={\sf tail}
,
then we keep both, i.e.,
X_{t+1}:=x
and
Y_{t+1}:=y
.

(2) Case

i\in\left\{\text{$x=0$ or $y=0$}\right\}

If
\chi={\sf head}
,
then we run
X_{t}
to the next with the chosen
i
and a chosen
j
according to the rule,
and keep
Y_{t}
.
Namely,

X_{t+1}:=X_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1}:=y.

Further, if
\chi={\sf tail}
,
then we change the roles of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
and run
Y_{t}
to the next with the chosen
i
and a chosen
j
according to the rule, and keep
X_{t}
.

(3) Case

i\in\left\{x=y>0\right\}

The second index
j
is chosen from
[Q]
uniformly at random both for
X_{t}
and for
Y_{t}
.
If
\chi={\sf head}
,
then we move
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
together with the chosen
i
and
j
.
Namely,

X_{t+1}:=X_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{t+1}:=Y_{t}-\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}.

Further, if
\chi={\sf tail}
,
then we keep both, i.e.,
X_{t+1}:=x
and
Y_{t+1}:=y
.

Note that chains
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
and
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
are the lazy versions of the original chain.
Let us define the discrepancy

D(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{Q}|x_{i}-y_{i}|.

We claim that if
X_{t}\neq Y_{t}
,
then
D(X_{t},Y_{t})
increases or decreases nearly equally likely:
First, in
(1a)
, the discrepancy changes by
1
or
-1
with equal probability

\frac{1}{Q^{2}}\#\{x>y>0\}\cdot\#\{y>x>0\}.

and, in
(1b)
,
the discrepancy does not change almost surely.
Next, in
(2)
,
the discrepancy does not increase almost surely.
Finally, in
(3)
,
the discrepancy does not change almost surely.

Let us assume that
x\neq y
.
Note that the following holds:

\left\{x>y>0\right\}=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{y>x>0\right\}=\emptyset\implies\left\{x>y=0\right\}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{y>x=0\right\}\neq\emptyset,

further that

\left\{x>y>0\right\}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{y>x>0\right\}=\emptyset\implies\left\{y>x=0\right\}\neq\emptyset.

These follow since
\sum_{i=1}^{Q}x_{i}=n
by definition of
B
.
In these two cases,
the
(2)
occurs with positive probability, and the discrepancy decreases by
1
or does not decrease with probability at least
1/Q^{2}
respectively.

Summarizing all the above cases,
as far as
X_{t}\neq Y_{t}
,
the discrepancy
D_{t}:=D(X_{t},Y_{t})
satisfies that almost surely

{\bf E}\,[D_{t+1}-D_{t}\mid X_{t},Y_{t}]\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm Var}(D_{t+1}-D_{t}\mid X_{t},Y_{t})\geq\frac{1}{4Q^{2}}.

Let
\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0\ :X_{t}=Y_{t}\}
.
There exists a constant
C_{Q}
such that
{\bf E}\,_{x,y}\tau\leq C_{Q}n^{2}
for all
x,y\in B

[
LP17
, Proposition 17.20]
.
Therefore the Markov inequality implies the claim.
∎

Remark A.2
.

For each fixed
Q\geq 2
,
the order of the bound we obtain is optimal:
If we consider
Q=2
and
n\geq 1
,
the chain is a symmetric nearest neighbor random walk on the path on
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
reflected at the extremes.
This chain does not exhibit cutoff (cf.
[
LP17
, Chapter 18]
).
