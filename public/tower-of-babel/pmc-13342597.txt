Social jet lag and visual acuity decline in junior high students: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuexia Liao, Wenrui Sun, Yiran Zhang, Yun Sun, Qianqian Zhen, Xiaoyu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reduced visual acuity, defined according to the Technical Standard for Physical Examination for Students (GB/T 26343 − 2010) as unaided distance visual acuity below 5.0 on the 5-grade notation scale (equivalent to LogMAR ＞ 0.0), affects more than 40% of junior high school students in China. Lifestyle factors have been implicated in refractive development; however, the role of social jet lag remains unclear. We examined the association between large social jet lag, defined as a sleep midpoint discrepancy exceeding 1 h between school and leisure days, and incident visual acuity decline over one year. In this retrospective cohort study, 903 students from 2 junior high schools in Yangzhou, China were included. Baseline lifestyle-related information was extracted from pre-2020 records. Initial visual acuity was assessed in 2020, with follow-up assessment in 2021. Incident visual acuity decline was defined as a deterioration of at least 1 acuity grade. During follow-up, 77 students (8.5%) experienced visual acuity decline. Large social jet lag, observed in 28.9% of participants, was independently associated with an increased risk of visual acuity decline (adjusted RR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.13–2.78). These findings suggest that social jet lag is associated with visual acuity decline in junior high school students and may represent a relevant lifestyle-related factor in adolescent visual health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51107-8.

Introduction

Reduced visual acuity refers to a subnormal level of visual acuity that is primarily caused by refractive errors such as myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism, and is commonly correctable with glasses or contact lenses. In China, reduced visual acuity is specifically defined as unaided distance visual acuity of less than 5.0 on a 5-grade notation scale in individuals over six years of age, as per the national standard (GB 11533–2011)1,2, which corresponds to a LogMAR visual acuity of 0.0. Among children and adolescents, reduced visual acuity is predominantly driven by refractive errors, particularly myopia1, whose prevalence has increased markedly in recent decades, especially in East Asia. Epidemiological evidence indicates that in urban regions of East and Southeast Asia, the prevalence of myopia reaches approximately 80%–90% among adolescents by the time of high school graduation3. In different regions of China, the overall myopia rate among children and adolescents ranges from 49.6% to 71.3%4. It is projected that 180 million children and adolescents will be affected by vision loss by 2030 in China5.

The eye and ocular adnexa play a key role in regulating the body’s circadian rhythm, while their physiological homeostasis is also influenced by neuroendocrine hormones modulated by circadian cycles. Circadian rhythm disruption has been implicated in ocular growth regulation and refractive development, particularly myopia6,7, through pathways involving retinal signaling and neuroendocrine regulation. Among junior high school students, visual acuity reduction has been associated with sleep deprivation and disrupted circadian rhythms8–10. Sleep deprivation is common among Chinese children and adolescents, affecting 69.8% of those aged 6–17 years11, and has been positively associated with reduced visual acuity among students12. However, it is still unclear whether sleep circadian rhythm disruption results in progressive visual acuity reduction.

Social jet lag, a chronobiological phenomenon related to circadian rhythms, reflects wakefulness disturbances primarily driven by social and behavioral factors13, particularly variations in sleep–wake timing between school days and free days13,14. It has been associated with adolescent growth and development, psychological status, memory function, and obesity15,16. Large social jet lag was defined as an absolute difference of more than 1 h between the midpoint time of sleep on weekdays and that on free days. This condition is prevalent among adolescents17. Roenneberg et al.18, in an international survey of approximately 150,000 participants, reported that large social jet lag was more common in adolescent boys than girls, and that nearly 80% required alarm clocks to wake on weekdays19. The 2018 National Assessment of Compulsory Education Quality in China20 reported that 24.1% of eighth-grade girls and 22.1% of boys suffer from moderately to severely decreased visual acuity. Additionally, 83.5% of junior high school students sleep less than 9 h per night, a situation exacerbated by demanding academic schedules. Academic demands and prolonged evening activities are common among junior high school students and may exacerbate sleep–wake misalignment, thereby contributing to social jet lag. Given these conditions, investigating the impact of social jet lag on changes in visual acuity among junior high school students is warranted.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 1,293 junior high school students participated in this study. Of these, 1,106 students completed the two-year follow-up assessment, and 903 valid questionnaires were ultimately obtained, with a valid response rate of 81.65% (Fig. 1). Key baseline demographic and objective clinical characteristics were largely comparable between groups, except that excluded participants had poorer baseline visual acuity (median LogMAR: 0.30 vs. 0.10, p < 0.001), while age, sex, grade, and BMI did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table S1). Among the 903 respondents, 469 (51.94%) were male, and 479 (53.05%) were from one-child families. In 2021, participants were aged 13–16 (14.73 ± 0.72) years. Compared with 2020, average height, weight, and BMI increased by 3.14 ± 2.95 cm, 4.87 ± 4.62 kg, and 1.08 ± 1.45 kg/m², respectively, while mean visual acuity in the worse eye declined by 0.02 ± 0.07 LogMAR (Supplementary Table S2).

Social jet lag in junior school students

In this retrospective cohort study, 467 (51.7%) students reported they went to bed after 10:00 p.m. on weekdays, and 129 (14.3%) reported very late bedtimes on weekdays. Additionally, 425 (47.1%) reported staying up too late on weekends, and 138 (15.3%) slept very late on weekends according to the Sleep practices questionnaire. A total of 440 (48.7%) students reported weekday naps exceeding 30 min. Social jet lag showed a right-skewed distribution, with a median (IQR) of 0.75 (0.50–1.25) hours and a mean ± SD of 0.89 ± 0.75 h, and 261 (28.9%) students experienced large social jet lag.

Reduced visual acuity among junior school students

In 2020, 48.9% of students had normal vision (LogMAR ≤ 0.0), and 21.3%, 24.9%, and 4.9% had mild (LogMAR 0.1), moderate (LogMAR 0.2–0.4), and severe (LogMAR ≥ 0.5) visual reduction, respectively. In 2021, these proportions were 45.5%, 21.0%, 25.5%, and 8.0%, showing a significant change in distribution (p < 0.05; Fig. 2). Over one year, 77 students (8.5%) experienced an incident decline in visual acuity, with a mean decrease of 0.19 ± 0.12 LogMAR (range: 0.10–0.90). Specifically, 20 progressed from normal to mild, 9 to moderate, 2 to severe; 20 from mild to moderate, 2 to severe; and 24 from moderate to severe. Baseline spherical equivalent refraction (SER) data were available for 891 of 903 participants (98.7%), with a median (IQR) of − 2.63 (− 4.25, − 1.31) D, and low myopia (− 6.00 < SE ≤ − 0.50 D) predominated (80.4%). A statistically significant difference in refractive status distribution was observed between social jet lag groups (χ² = 8.73, p = 0.033; Supplementary Table S3).

Baseline comparisons by social jet lag status

Among the 903 junior high school students analyzed, 642 were classified into the no social jet lag group and 261 into the large social jet lag group. Compared with the large social jet lag group, students without social jet lag had a higher proportion of ninth-grade students (42.2% vs. 31.8%; p = 0.004). Weekday sleep duration did not differ significantly between groups. However, students with large social jet lag had earlier weekday bedtimes (median: 22:00 vs. 22:30; p = 0.018) and longer weekday nap duration (40.0 vs. 30.0 min/day; p = 0.010). On weekends, they exhibited later bedtimes (23:00 vs. 22:00; p < 0.001) and wake-up times (9:00 vs. 7:30; p < 0.001), longer sleep duration (9.5 vs. 9.0 h/day; p = 0.048), and shorter nap duration (0.0 vs. 20.0 min/day; p = 0.004).

Daily screen exposure was longer in the large social jet lag group (102.9 vs. 70.7 min/day; p < 0.001). No significant between-group differences were observed in age, sex, baseline physiological indicators (e.g., BMI and visual acuity), or outdoor activity time (Table 1).

Primary analysis: association between social jet lag and visual acuity decline

In the crude model, large social jet lag was associated with an increased risk of visual acuity decline (RR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.08–2.55; p = 0.022). This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for demographics (Model 2; RR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.16–2.84; p = 0.009) and further adjustment for baseline visual acuity and lifestyle factors (Model 3; RR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.13–2.78; p = 0.013).

Higher grade was independently associated with a greater risk of visual acuity decline (RR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.27–4.20; p = 0.006), as was poorer baseline visual acuity (RR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.22–6.08; p = 0.014). Age, sex, outdoor time, screen time, and baseline BMI were not significantly associated with visual acuity decline (all p > 0.05; Table 2).

Secondary analysis: association of weekday and weekend sleep patterns with visual acuity decline

In secondary analyses, weekend wake-up timing was associated with an increased risk of visual acuity decline (fully adjusted RR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47; p = 0.003). In addition, adolescents with a later weekend sleep–wake pattern (later bedtime and later wake-up time) had a significantly higher risk of visual acuity decline compared with those with earlier weekend timing patterns (RR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.25–3.62; p = 0.005). No significant associations were observed between visual acuity decline and sleep duration, isolated bedtime or wake-up time, or weekday sleep patterns in either unadjusted or fully adjusted models (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)21 analyses retaining all 903 participants yielded comparable estimates in unadjusted (RR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.14–2.80) and adjusted models (RR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.17–2.78), with covariates including age, grade, sex, baseline BMI, worse-eye visual acuity, and lifestyle-related factors (screen time, outdoor activity time). The propensity score distributions showed substantial overlap between social jet lag groups (Supplementary Figure S1), indicating adequate common support. Adequate covariate balance was achieved after weighting (all absolute SMDs < 0.1; Supplementary Figure S3), and the IPTW weight distribution demonstrated reasonable stability with most weights concentrated between 1 and 3 (Supplementary Figure S2), suggesting effective balance without extreme weights. A doubly robust IPTW approach22 further confirmed these findings (RR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.17–2.78). Additional sensitivity analyses using average treatment effect on the treated (ATT: RR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.04–2.58)23 and truncated weights at the 99th percentile24(RR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.16–2.75) yielded similar estimates (Supplementary Table S4).

To assess potential confounding by baseline refractive status, we additionally adjusted for baseline worse-eye SER in the multivariable model among the 891 participants (98.7%) with available SER data. The association between large social jet lag and visual acuity decline remained statistically significant (RR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.10–2.69), similar to the estimate from the same subsample without SER adjustment (RR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.13–2.78). These findings suggest that the association was not materially influenced by baseline refractive status (Supplementary Table S5).

In a further sensitivity analysis restricted to participants with normal baseline visual acuity (n = 441), Firth penalized logistic regression showed that large social jet lag was associated with a higher risk of visual acuity decline (OR = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.20–5.99; corresponding RR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.19–4.72). The association remained statistically significant and consistent with the primary and other sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of reduced visual acuity increased from 51.2% in 2020 to 54.5% in 2021 among junior high school students, which is comparable to previous cohort studies in Chinese school populations reporting prevalence rates of 53.83%–58.95%25. Within the same cohort, 28.9% of students experienced large social jet lag, which was associated with visual acuity decline. Social jet lag is an underrecognized form of circadian misalignment and a growing public health concern among adolescents worldwide16. Previous studies have reported substantial variability in prevalence, with only 13% of adolescents reporting no social jet lag in the Netherlands26, while 46.9% of adolescents in Hong Kong reported large social jet lag27. The relatively lower prevalence observed in our sample may reflect more structured weekend schedules driven by academic demands and parental supervision, resulting in smaller weekday–weekend differences in sleep timing. Large social jet lag has been associated with multiple adverse outcomes in adolescents, including sleep deprivation, daytime sleepiness, poor academic performance28, and behavioral problems29,30.

While limited cross-sectional studies31 have shown that social jet lag in adolescents is associated with an increased risk of self-reported visual acuity decline, our retrospective cohort study provides further support for this relationship. Students with large social jet lag had a 77% higher risk of incident visual acuity decline compared with peers without social jet lag. This association remained robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. Given the high prevalence and recurrent exposure to social jet lag during adolescence32, even a moderate increase in individual risk may translate into a substantial population-level burden of visual acuity decline.

Biologically, ocular physiology is closely regulated by circadian mechanisms responsive to light–dark cycles. Retinal photoreceptors interact with circadian regulatory pathways involving the suprachiasmatic nucleus, melatonin, and dopamine, which contribute to ocular homeostasis, including corneal repair, scleral stability, and antioxidative protection33–42. Additionally, social jet lag–induced weekend catch-up sleep may reduce daytime light exposure, potentially attenuating dopamine-mediated inhibition of axial elongation43, providing a plausible pathway linking circadian misalignment with visual deterioration.

Consistent with this mechanism, later sleep patterns on weekends, encompassing both delayed bedtime and later wake-up time, independently predicted visual acuity decline, with later wake-up time serving as a marker of weekend catch-up sleep. Delayed bedtime may induce circadian phase delay and disrupt nocturnal melatonin secretion, while later wake-up time may further reduce exposure to protective morning light. Although similar patterns may occur on weekdays, earlier and more uniform school start times may partially constrain variability in sleep timing and light exposure. These findings suggest potential trade-offs between weekend catch-up sleep and ocular health. While moderate catch-up sleep (1–2 h) has been associated with reduced depression risk44, prolonged morning sleep may reduce exposure to protective morning light during critical developmental periods, highlighting the need to consider cross-domain health effects when evaluating adolescent sleep behaviors.

Beyond sleep-related factors, grade and baseline visual acuity were significant independent predictors of visual acuity decline. Each one-grade increase was associated with a 2.30-fold higher risk, likely reflecting cumulative increases in academic demands and near-work exposure in higher grades. Early junior high school may therefore represent a period of heightened vulnerability to visual deterioration, underscoring the importance of early preventive strategies before substantial myopic progression becomes established. Poorer baseline visual acuity was also independently associated with subsequent decline. Given that 80.4% of the participants had low myopia at baseline, this likely reflects ongoing myopic progression instead of a true ceiling effect45,46. The discrepancy between the proportion of students with normal unaided visual acuity and those classified as emmetropic may partly reflect accommodative compensation47 and limitations of non-cycloplegic refraction, which can overestimate myopia prevalence48. Additional adjustment for baseline SER in sensitivity analyses only minimally attenuated the primary effect estimate, further suggesting that circadian misalignment may be associated with visual acuity decline independently of pre-existing refractive status.

In contrast, no significant association was observed between established behavioral factors, including outdoor activity and screen time, and one-year visual acuity decline. This finding differs from previous studies on myopia progression49,50 and may reflect differences in follow-up duration or measurement precision, as self-reported behavioral data are subject to measurement error and may be less precise than objective exposure assessments51. However, daily screen exposure was significantly higher in the large social jet lag group than in the no social jet lag group. This pattern suggests that screen time may function more as a behavioral correlate of social jet lag rather than as an independent predictor of visual deterioration. Screen use, particularly during evening hours, may delay sleep onset and reduce morning light exposure52, processes that may be more comprehensively captured by the social jet lag construct than by total screen duration alone. Additionally, aggregated screen time does not distinguish viewing distance or accommodative demand, which may vary substantially across devices53. Together, these findings indicate that social jet lag may reflect a broader pattern of circadian and behavioral misalignment beyond screen duration itself. Regarding other covariates of interest, evidence regarding sex differences in visual acuity progression remains inconsistent. While some studies report no significant difference54,55, others, including a meta-analysis of children aged 7–12 years56 and a cohort study in Western China57, have reported faster progression in girls. Although biometric data indicate that females typically have shorter axial lengths but steeper corneas, potentially compensating for refractive differences58, no significant sex difference was observed in our cohort, which may reflect limited statistical power or relatively short follow-up.

The study period (2020–2021) coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which lifestyle changes occurred, including increased screen exposure, reduced outdoor activities, and altered sleep schedules. These shifts have been linked to accelerated myopia progression in children and adolescents and may partly account for the observed visual acuity decline59. However, the association between large social jet lag and incident visual acuity decline remained significant after adjustment for key lifestyle factors, baseline visual acuity, BMI, and demographic characteristics, and was further supported by propensity score–based sensitivity analyses, suggesting that the relationship was not fully explained by measured covariates. To assess robustness to unmeasured confounding, we calculated the E-value60. The point estimate was 2.94, with a lower confidence bound of 1.51, meaning an unmeasured confounder would need risk ratios of at least 1.51 with both exposure and outcome to explain away the association. Parental myopia history and indoor light exposure were not comprehensively assessed and remain potential unmeasured confounders; nonetheless, the observed association may partly reflect circadian misalignment not fully captured by screen time or outdoor activity alone.

It should be noted that reduced visual acuity was defined according to the Chinese national screening standard (GB/T 26343 − 2010), which classifies unaided distance visual acuity below 5.0 (LogMAR > 0.0) as reduced for screening purposes. This threshold is lower than the WHO definition of vision impairment (presenting visual acuity worse than 6/12 in the better eye)61,62. Accordingly, the prevalence reported here reflects subnormal unaided visual acuity identified through school-based screening rather than clinically diagnosed vision impairment. The primary outcome was incident visual acuity decline, defined as a ≥ 1-grade (0.1 LogMAR) deterioration, capturing early, measurable visual change rather than attainment of a diagnostic threshold.

There were also several limitations to our study. First, the sample was limited to two schools in Yangzhou, China, which affects its generalizability. Excluded participants had worse baseline visual acuity than those included, which may have introduced some degree of selection bias. Second, most of the sleep-related data were self-reported rather than objectively measured, introducing recall bias and potential inaccuracies in estimation. Although the sleep-related items were adapted from prior instruments and pilot-tested with acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.67), the questionnaire was not formally validated and test-retest reliability was not assessed. Refractive status was assessed without cycloplegia, which may overestimate myopia prevalence due to residual accommodation and introduce classification bias. Third, although key behavioral variables were adjusted for, the unique context of the COVID-19 pandemic, including online learning and increased digital screen use, may have introduced residual confounding related to pandemic-related lifestyle changes63. The generalizability of these findings to non-pandemic contexts therefore requires confirmation in future prospective studies. Finally, the retrospective design limits causal inferences.

Despite these limitations, this study identifies social jet lag as a novel and potentially modifiable behavioral factor associated with visual acuity decline in adolescents, with implications for adolescent visual health.

Future studies should include broader samples and employ objective assessments of visual acuity decline. Prospective designs are also needed to clarify the impact of social jet lag on vision. Additionally, examining social and environmental determinants of sleep patterns, such as noise, light exposure, and bedroom conditions, may help identify contributors to visual health.

Conclusions

Junior high school students in eastern China exhibited a high incidence of visual acuity decline. Large social jet lag was associated with an increased risk of visual acuity decline. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining regular sleep–wake schedules and stable light–dark routines during adolescence, and social jet lag as a potentially modifiable target for protecting adolescent visual health.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two junior high schools in Yangzhou, China. Using cluster sampling, all grade 8 and 9 students (27 classes) at baseline (November 2020) were invited to participate.

Eligible participants were students aged 12–16 years who were free from chronic systemic diseases and attended school regularly. Students were excluded if they had a history of significant ocular disorders (e.g., glaucoma, cataracts, retinal diseases, amblyopia, or ocular trauma), neurological disorders, or were taking medications (e.g., glucocorticoids or sedating antihistamines) known to affect sleep, circadian rhythms, or visual acuity, as determined by student and parental self-report at enrollment. Students with incomplete baseline or follow-up examinations or questionnaire data were also excluded from analysis.

At baseline, each participant underwent a standardized physical examination comprising: (1) monocular unaided distance visual acuity testing using the 5-meter standard logarithmic E chart (GB/T 11533 − 2011)2; and (2) anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, from which BMI was calculated (GB/T 26343 − 2010)64. Lifestyle and sleep data were obtained from self-administered questionnaires that students had completed prior to the examination. Follow-up examinations of identical content and protocol were conducted in November 2021 by the same team of certified medical practitioners.

Measurements and variable definitions

Visual acuity examination

Uniform monocular unaided distance visual acuity examinations were conducted by certified medical practitioners. Examinations followed standardized protocols based on the Standard Logarithmic Visual Acuity Scale (GB/T 11533 − 2011)2 and the Technical Standard for Physical Examination for Students (GB/T 26343 − 2010)64.

Visual acuity was classified into four categories65: normal (5.0; LogMAR 0.0), mildly reduced (4.9; LogMAR 0.1), moderately reduced (4.6–4.8; LogMAR 0.2–0.4), and severely reduced (≤ 4.5; LogMAR ≥ 0.5), with reduced visual acuity defined as any grade below normal (< 5.0; LogMAR > 0.0)64. In cases of interocular disparity, the poorer eye was used for analysis.

Incident visual acuity decline was operationally defined as a deterioration of at least one acuity grade between 2020 and 2021.

Body mass index

Following the Technical Code for Students’ Health Examination (GB/T 26343 − 2010)64, height and weight were measured using a standard altimeter (HXBM-19) and electronic scale (SECA-877), respectively. Students were measured without shoes for height and wearing a single layer of clothing for weight. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)².

Sleep and lifestyle assessment

A structured self-administered questionnaire was used to collect information on demographics, lifestyle-related variables, and sleep-related measures. The questionnaire included: (i) general information (age, sex, and number of children in the household); (ii) lifestyle-related variables, including weekly exercise frequency, outdoor activity duration per session (categorized as < 15, 15–30, 31–60, or > 60 min), and average daily time spent using electronic devices (hours per day); and (iii) sleep-related measures.

For analysis, weekly outdoor activity time was estimated by multiplying reported exercise frequency (sessions per week) by the assigned category midpoint (7.5, 22.5, 45.5, and 60 min, respectively). For the open-ended upper category (> 60 min), 60 min was conservatively assigned to avoid overestimation. Average daily screen time was calculated by summing total weekly screen exposure (weekday screen time × 5 plus weekend screen time × 2) and dividing by 7, with values expressed in minutes per day.

Sleep-related measures were assessed using items adapted from the Pediatric Sleep Practices Questionnaire (PSPQ) based on prior literature66. The adapted instrument underwent pilot testing prior to formal administration and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the pilot sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.67).

Specifically, students reported their habitual weekday and weekend bedtimes and wake-up times using a 24-hour format, as well as their usual nap duration after lunch. Sleep opportunity was calculated as the interval between bedtime and wake-up time on school days and weekends, respectively. Social jet lag14 was calculated as the absolute difference between the midpoint of sleep on school days and weekends, with < 1 h classified as no social jet lag and ≥ 1 h as large social jet lag.

Quality control

Measuring equipment (altimeters and electronic scales) was calibrated daily before use. Examinations were conducted by qualified physicians. At the beginning of the survey, 5% of participants were randomly selected for retesting. If more than 10% of retested indicators were clinically or operationally inconsistent with the original results, the examinations were considered unreliable and were repeated.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the Independent Samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%) and compared using the chi-squared test. Ordinal categorical variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

All primary regression analyses were conducted using complete-case data. To examine potential selection bias, selected baseline demographic and objective clinical characteristics were compared between participants included in and excluded from the final analysis.

A modified Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between social jet lag and visual acuity decline. Potential confounders were identified based on prior literature and theoretical considerations, excluding potential mediators67. Specifically, age, grade, sex, baseline unaided visual acuity in the worse eye, baseline BMI, screen time, and outdoor activity time were considered potential confounders. These variables were sequentially adjusted for in multivariable models to control for confounding.

For secondary analyses, weekday and weekend bedtimes and wake-up times were dichotomized at their respective medians into earlier (≤ median) and later (> median) categories. Combined bedtime–wake-up timing patterns were further classified into four categories: earlier bedtime/earlier wake-up time, earlier bedtime/later wake-up time, later bedtime/earlier wake-up time, and later bedtime/later wake-up time. Associations between sleep-related variables and visual acuity decline were examined using univariate and multivariable modified Poisson regression models.

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, propensity score–based analyses were performed using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE), with propensity scores derived from logistic regression including all covariates in the primary model. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD < 0.1), and propensity score overlap was examined to assess common support. Robustness was further evaluated using a doubly robust IPTW model, average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) weighting, and truncation of weights at the 99th percentile. Second, to account for potential confounding by baseline refractive status, worse-eye spherical equivalent refraction (SER) was additionally adjusted for in the modified Poisson regression among participants with available SER data. The worse eye was defined as the eye with lower baseline unaided visual acuity; when equal, the eye with the more myopic spherical equivalent was selected. Participants with missing SER data (n = 12, 1.3%) were excluded from this analysis. Third, to assess whether inclusion of students who habitually wore spectacles influenced outcome ascertainment, a sensitivity analysis was restricted to participants with normal baseline visual acuity (worse-eye LogMAR ≤ 0.0; n = 441). Given the limited number of outcome events in this subgroup (n = 30; events-per-variable = 4.3), Firth penalized logistic regression with profile likelihood CIs was applied to reduce small-sample bias68. Baseline visual acuity was not additionally adjusted for, given the restriction to participants with normal baseline visual acuity. Odds ratios were converted to approximate risk ratios to maintain consistency with the primary analysis69.

All data were verified by professionals and double-entered into the database using EpiData (version 3.1). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and R software (version 4.5.1). All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
