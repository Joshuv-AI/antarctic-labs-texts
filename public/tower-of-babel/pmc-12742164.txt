Data-driven targets for predicting outcomes in patients with HIV infection and tuberculous meningitis in China
Authors: Yuwei Li, Yang Zhou, Pengfei Zhao, Yinzhong Shen, Haolan He, Yan Sun, Yanfen Liu, Qingxia Zhao, Yahong Chen, Jianhua Yu, Ping Ma, Zhimin Chen, Hui Wang, Mingbin Zheng, Yaokai Chen, Min Lei, Weimin Li, Hongzhou Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFor people living with HIV (PLWH) and tuberculous meningitis (TBM), current studies on risk stratification and poor prognosis lack a clear optimal threshold. To address this gap, this study aims to identify an optimal immunological threshold for risk stratification and explore predictors of poor prognosis in this population.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre cross-sectional study enrolling PLWH with TBM from hospitals across eight provinces of China between January 2018 and December 2020. We extracted the demographic and clinical data, discharge outcomes, Medical Research Council staging and CD4+ T-lymphocyte count on admission. CD4 thresholds were determined using restricted cubic splines with knots at quintiles. Multivariable logistic regression of risk factors derived adjusted ORs with 95% CIs.ResultsA total of 201 participants were included in the study. Of these, 173 (86.1%) improved with treatment. Restricted cubic spline analysis identified CD4+ T-lymphocyte count <50 cells/µL as the optimal threshold for predicting poor TBM outcomes; the median CD4+ count was significantly lower in patients who deteriorated (40 cells/µL) than in those who improved (69 cells/µL). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed CD4+ T-lymphocyte count <50 cells/µL and elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) as independent risk factors for adverse outcomes.ConclusionsIn PLWH with TBM, a CD4+ T-cell count below 50 cells/µL defines a critical stratification threshold for clinical risk classification, with elevated BUN serving as an additional prognostic marker. These findings support the prioritisation of severely immunocompromised patients for targeted management and further mechanistic studies.Keywords: AIDS, TUBERCULOSIS

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this multicentre investigation encompassing 201 PLWH who presented with TBM, a CD4+ T-lymphocyte count threshold of less than 50 cells/µL was determined to be the most discriminative biomarker for predicting adverse clinical outcomes, with an adjusted OR of 2.55. Moreover, elevated levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN) emerged as an independent prognostic factor. Notably, microbiological confirmation was achieved in only 31.3% of TBM cases, with the majority diagnosed based on clinical criteria alone. These findings establish a geographically relevant immunological cut-off, facilitating risk stratification among Chinese PLWH with TBM.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study identifies a critical CD4+ T-lymphocyte threshold of <50 cells/µL for predicting poor outcomes in PLWH with TBM, along with elevated BUN as an additional risk factor. Rather than merely reinforcing the known low CD4+-poor prognosis link, this threshold provides a quantifiable node to narrow and standardise HIV-TBM immunopathology research, guiding studies on when central nervous system anti-MTB immunity fails or interacts with renal dysfunction, or whether corticosteroid therapy efficacy varies by CD4+ stratification. In clinical practice, the findings enable targeted risk stratification, prioritising closer monitoring and aggressive interventions for PLWH with CD4+ counts below this threshold, while integrating BUN into prognostic assessments. This study also supports the inclusion of the CD4+ threshold in national guidelines for resource allocation towards high-risk groups and advocates for improved access to sensitive diagnostics in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) remains the most severe and life-threatening form of tuberculosis. Despite accounting for only a small proportion of total TB cases, TBM is associated with disproportionately high mortality, and most of those who survive are left with neurological sequelae. In 2019, among the 7.1 million newly diagnosed TB cases worldwide, approximately 164 000 adults were estimated to have developed TBM, with around one-quarter of these cases occurring in people living with HIV (PLWH).1 While the overall mortality of TBM is approximately 25%, mortality increases to nearly 50% in HIV-coinfected individuals. Most deaths occur within the first 3 months of treatment, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and prompt clinical intervention in this vulnerable population.1 2

Despite its severity, TBM in PLWH remains underreported and often grouped within the broader spectrum of central nervous system (CNS) infections.3 Current treatment strategies follow standard anti-tubercular regimens and include adjunctive corticosteroids to suppress meningeal inflammation.4 5 However, a recent randomised controlled trial by Donovan et al6 found that adjunctive dexamethasone did not improve survival or secondary endpoints in HIV-positive adults with TBM. These findings challenge the current treatment paradigm and underscore the need for more individualised, evidence-based therapeutic approaches in this high-risk population.

Profound immunosuppression, particularly the depletion of CD4+ T-lymphocytes, is a hallmark of HIV infection and a key driver of susceptibility to TB. PLWH with CD4+ counts below 200 cells/µL have a 25-fold greater risk of developing TB than individuals with CD4+ counts >1000 cells/μL.7 A Vietnamese study of 70 PLWH with TBM reported a median CD4+ count of just 67 cells/µL (IQR 19–124), highlighting the degree of immunodeficiency in this population.8 Another Vietnamese study further identified low CD4+ T-cell counts as a risk factor for mortality in HIV-infected patients with TBM, yet it did not define a specific CD4+ threshold for predicting such outcomes.9 However, limited data are available from other geographic regions, and the CD4+ threshold for predicting outcomes in PLWH with TBM remains undefined. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a multicentre, cross-sectional study that consecutively enrolled 201 HIV-seropositive adults with microbiologically confirmed or clinically diagnosed TBM from eight tertiary infectious disease referral centres in eight Chinese provinces. Comprehensive clinical data were collected, including demographic characteristics, baseline CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts, laboratory parameters and treatment outcomes. Using a data-driven approach, we identified an optimal CD4+ T-cell threshold for predicting outcomes and explored other risk factors associated with poor prognosis in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a multicentre cross-sectional study conducted between January 2018 and December 2020. Participants were recruited from eight tertiary infectious disease hospitals located in the capitals of eight Chinese provinces. Demographic characteristics, clinical presentations, laboratory test results, CT, discharge outcomes and disease severity measures were collected from medical records. Disease severity was assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) staging system and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores. Laboratory data included baseline CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts at the time of hospital admission.

The study protocol was reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.10

Diagnostic criteria and disease classification

HIV infection was diagnosed based on the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of HIV/AIDS (2024 Edition),11 and TBM was diagnosed according to the Chinese Clinical Guidelines for Central Nervous System Tuberculosis.12 A definitive TBM diagnosis was established by detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using microscopy, culture or the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, USA). Patients who did not meet microbiological criteria but fulfilled clinical and radiological findings consistent with TBM were classified as having probable or possible TBM, according to a standardised diagnostic scoring system.13 To avoid potential misunderstanding and clarify the composition of the study population, we specify that the study population included patients with definitive, probable and possible TBM cases, who were classified according to the following standardised diagnostic scoring system13:

Definitive TBM: Confirmed by the detection of MTB in CSF using microscopy, mycobacterial culture or nucleic acid amplification tests;

Probable TBM: Clinical manifestations (eg, persistent headache, fever, neurological deficits) and radiological findings consistent with TBM, plus at least one of the following: abnormal CSF parameters (elevated protein, reduced glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis), positive tuberculin skin test/interferon-γ release assay or evidence of active pulmonary tuberculosis;

Possible TBM: Clinical and radiological features suggestive of TBM, with supportive CSF changes but no confirmatory microbiological or immunological evidence.

TBM disease severity was staged according to the modified British MRC criteria,4 as follows:

Stage I: GCS score of 15 without focal neurological deficits.

Stage II: GCS score of 11–14 or 15 with focal neurological deficits.

Stage III: GCS score ≤10.

Discharge Outcome Assessment Criteria:4 5 Clinical outcomes were evaluated at 24 hours postdischarge according to the following standardised framework:

Clinical Improvement: Defined by the fulfilment of at least one of the following quantifiable criteria: (a) alleviation of core symptoms based on a standardised symptom severity scale; (b) ≥2-point increase in the GCS score and (c) significant recovery of neurological deficits.

Clinical deterioration: Defined by the occurrence of any of the following: (a) worsening of core symptoms; (b) ≥2-point decrease in GCS score and (c) death during hospitalisation or within 24 hours postdischarge.

All research personnel received standardised training in data recording methods and assessment criteria. Notably, for discharge outcome assessment, we implemented an independent dual-assessor system: assessments were conducted separately by two qualified neurologists. In cases of disagreement between the two assessors, a third neurologist with a senior professional title was invited to serve as an arbitrator to facilitate a final consensus. Additionally, uniform protocols were consistently employed across all study sites for data collection and outcome assessment to ensure inter-site consistency (online supplemental file).

Statistical analysis

Missing data were imputed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method, as the missing values primarily involved stable baseline clinical and laboratory parameters, and LOCF could reasonably reflect patients’ actual baseline status with minimal additional assumptions. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were summarised as medians with IQRs and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, with between-group comparisons performed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Prior to multivariable logistic regression, we first performed univariable logistic regression analysis for all candidate variables. Variables with a <0.1 in the univariable analysis were selected for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for poor discharge outcomes, with adjusted ORs (aORs) and corresponding 95% CIs estimated. All analyses were conducted with R software (V.4.2.3).

CD4+ T-lymphocyte thresholds were determined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) modelling with knots placed at quintiles. Dichotomised variables were further analysed using continuity-corrected χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. We simultaneously fitted the Youden Index curve and the p value fitting curve, then identified their intersection point to confirm the optimal CD4+ threshold. Outcome distributions were visualised using proportion plots generated with the ggplot2 package (R).14

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 201 adults with HIV who were diagnosed with TBM were included in the study. The eight participating provinces and corresponding enrolment numbers were as follows: Shenzhen (n=7 patients); Shanghai (n=9); Chongqing (n=103); Tianjin (n=4); Fuzhou (n=16); Zhengzhou (n=31); Hangzhou (n=2) and Nanning (n=29) (the larger enrolment in Chongqing reflects its higher local HIV/TBM prevalence and not sampling bias).15 Between January 2018 and December 2020, 295 HIV-positive patients with suspected TBM were admitted. After excluding 4 initial cases, lost to follow-up, 34 (30 undiagnosed TBM, 4 missing outcomes), and 56 with incomplete data, 201 patients with confirmed TBM were finally included (online supplemental figure 1). This cross-sectional study of 201 PLWH with TBM included 63 (31.3%) microbiologically confirmed patients and 138 (68.7%) who were diagnosed clinically. There were 168 males (83.6%) and 33 females (16.4%). At the time of diagnosis, 106 (52.7%) had never received anti-retroviral therapy (ART) and 30 (14.9%) patients tested positive for cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide antigen in CSF. The modified British MRC classified 139 (69.2%) as stage I, 54 (26.9%) as stage II and 8 (4.0%) as stage III (online supplemental table). With anti-TB therapy, 173 (86.1%) improved clinically while 28 (13.9%) deteriorated. No statistically significant association was observed between the various symptoms at admission and outcomes at discharge (online supplemental figure 2). Among the 201 patients, 15 (7.46%) had missing baseline laboratory data, involving CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts (4 cases), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels (6 cases) and total bilirubin levels (5 cases). Missing data were imputed using the LOCF method, with the last available observation (measured 1 day after the time point of missing data) used for filling. Significant differences in clinical parameters were observed between the two outcome groups, including CD4+ T-lymphocyte count, BUN, total bilirubin and body mass index (BMI) (figure 1). Notably, the median CD4+ T-cell count was significantly lower in patients who deteriorated (40 cells/µL, IQR 14.5–86.0) compared with patients who improved (69 cells/µL, IQR 29.0–115.0) (p=0.021).

Patients who deteriorated had significantly worse baseline MRC staging, but did not differ in gender, ART history, cryptococcal infection, HIV viral load or other baseline characteristics (online supplemental table).

Optimal CD4+ threshold for predicting outcomes

The distribution of CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts among patients was skewed, with 71.6% of patients having counts in the range of 0–100 cells/µL (online supplemental figure 3). For the predictive performance of CD4+ T-lymphocyte count on TBM outcomes: the area under the ROC curve was 0.664 (95% CI 0.562 to 0.765), indicating moderate discriminative ability; the Brier score was 0.114 (95% CI 0.085 to 0.139), reflecting good predictive accuracy. The calibration plot of CD4+ count-based prediction aligned with the ideal 45° line, confirming good calibration. A CD4+ T-lymphocyte threshold of 50 cells/µL was identified by restricted cubic spline analysis with quintile-based knots as optimal for predicting TBM clinical outcomes; this threshold was further validated by the intersection of the simultaneously fitted Youden Index curve and p value fitting curve. Specifically, TBM prognosis in PLWH was significantly worse when CD4+ counts were below this level at the time of diagnosis (figure 2).

Risk factors for adverse discharge outcomes

Prior to conducting univariate logistic regression, we first performed χ² tests to compare differences in baseline indicators between the improved and deteriorated outcome groups. On this basis, we initially screened variables with significant group differences as candidate factors for univariate logistic regression, including BMI, abnormalities of lung CT, abnormalities of cerebral imaging, total bilirubin, BUN and CD4+ T-cell counts <50 cells/µL. Additionally, considering clinical relevance and findings from previous studies on HIV-associated TBM outcomes, we further included potentially influential indicators that might affect prognosis but had marginal differences in χ² tests, such as fever, sodium ion concentration, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and abnormal CSF glucose. We then performed multivariable logistic regression, including all variables with a p<0.1 on univariate analysis (online supplemental figure 4A). This multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that poor discharge outcomes were significantly and independently associated with CD4+ T cell counts below 50 cells/µL (aOR=2.55; 95% CI 1.02 to 6.34; p=0.045) and elevated BUN (aOR=1.31; 95% CI 1.09 to 1.57; p=0.004) (online supplemental figure 4B).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest dataset of PLWH diagnosed with TBM (n=201) exclusively, without including patients with other CNS diseases. compared with the 173 patients showing clinical improvement, the 28 patients with deteriorated outcomes had a significantly lower median CD4+ T cell count, lower BMI levels, higher BUN levels and higher total bilirubin levels. Through a data-driven approach employing restricted cubic splines, we identified an optimal threshold CD4+ T cell count of <50 cells/µL for predicting poor outcomes in PLWH with TBM. This finding precisely fills a critical gap in the existing literature: previously, Li et al16 confirmed that a CD4+ T cell count <200 cells/µL is associated with an increased overall risk of HIV-related TB, but did not focus on the TBM subtype; Hai et al,8 in their study of 70 patients in Vietnam, only reported a median CD4+ T cell count of 67 cells/µL, failed to determine prognostic thresholds using standardised methods and had limitations in sample size and geographical representativeness. The CD4+ T cell count <50 cells/µL threshold identified in this study echoes the recommendations of the 2024 edition of the Chinese Guidelines for HIV/AIDS Diagnosis and Treatment11 in terms of clinical significance. This guideline classifies CD4+ T cell count <50 cells/µL as a ‘population with immunocompromise requiring focused attention’, but lacks concrete evidence supporting this in the context of TBM coinfection. Meintjes and Maartens17 noted that immune stratification for HIV-TBM coinfection needs to consider regional population characteristics. The results based on a multicentre sample of 201 cases (covering 8 provinces) provide the first evidence of a TBM-specific CD4+ threshold for the Chinese population, addressing the underrepresentation of East Asian populations in studies from Southeast Asia and Africa.

In this study, only 31.3% of the patients diagnosed with TBM were microbiologically confirmed, while 68.7% of TBM cases were diagnosed presumptively based on clinical and radiological criteria. Bacteriological confirmation in TBM is difficult because of the limited penetration of MTB across the blood–brain barrier,18 but diagnosis using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra has achieved sensitivities of 70%–77%, an improvement over the 50%–60% sensitivity of prior diagnostic tests.4 13 15 However, the widespread implementation of prompt Xpert Ultra testing is challenging, especially in rural or peripheral health facilities where TBM patients often present initially. Combining the Fujifilm SILVAMP TB-LAM urine assay with the Xpert Ultra on CSF has been shown to improve the sensitivity for diagnosing TBM by 11% over Xpert Ultra alone,18 with an additional potential for improved specificity. Given that 50% of HIV/AIDS patients with CD4+ T-cell counts below 50 cells/µL have undiagnosed tuberculosis at autopsy,19 the development of more sensitive point-of-care diagnostic tools remains an urgent priority.

The designation of <50 CD4+ T cells/μL as a threshold for poor prognoses was obtained using RCS, a well-established analytical approach for identifying nonlinear relationships. This severe level of immunodeficiency is associated with a markedly increased susceptibility to MTB coinfection, disorganised or absent granuloma formation and elevated risks of disseminated or extrapulmonary tuberculosis, including TBM.18 Clinically, this translates into complex therapeutic challenges and suboptimal outcomes, as corroborated by our data. While the precise immunopathological mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, our results underscore the importance of further mechanistic studies on CD4+ T-lymphocyte-mediated immune dysfunction in advanced HIV-TBM coinfection.

It is important to note that the CD4+ T-cell count threshold (< 50 cells/µL) identified in our study is intended as a key complementary indicator rather than the sole prognostic factor. In addition to CD4+ T-cell counts, elevated BUN levels and higher initial MRC staging were also significantly correlated with treatment outcomes, and these findings align with prior research. For example, Le et al.9 identified MRC severity grade as a key prognostic marker for PLWH with TBM, which further supports the clinical relevance of these factors in our study. Notably, the association between elevated BUN and poor outcomes in our PLWH/TBM cohort is consistent with insights from urinary metabolic studies in tuberculosis populations. A case in point is a study on advanced TBM in South African paediatric patients,20 which highlighted that derangements in nitrogen metabolism often reflect systemic metabolic stress and end-organ impairment in severe tuberculosis manifestations. Conversely, ART and cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide antigen positivity in CSF, which have been associated with clinical outcomes in other studies,21 did not reach statistical significance in our analysis (cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide antigen positivity in CSF: p=0.698; ART-naivety: p=0.187). This discrepancy may reflect the limitations of cross-sectional designs that lack the longitudinal depth necessary to fully capture dynamic risk factors. Future longitudinal cohorts with frequent sampling may better elucidate these associations.

Despite a relatively large sample size, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits the statistical validity of the causal associations of risk factors with treatment outcomes. Additionally, an inherent limitation of this study is the use of a 24-hour postdischarge time window to define clinical outcomes. This narrow time frame may have underestimated late deterioration events that often occur in PLWH with TBM. To more definitively identify determinants of prognosis, we have initiated a longitudinal prospective treatment cohort of PLWH with TBM, which will be stratified by CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts and will extend follow-up to 12 months postdischarge. This cohort aims to not only validate whether the CD4+ T-cell threshold of <50 cells/µL maintains predictive value for long-term prognosis but also capture late-onset deterioration events that were not assessed in the current cross-sectional study.

In summary, we have identified CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts <50 cells/µL as the optimal threshold for predicting poor treatment outcomes in PLWH with TBM and confirmed this as an independent risk factor for adverse treatment outcomes. This threshold offers a critical stratification marker for clinical research, prioritising severely immunocompromised patients for targeted mechanistic and therapeutic investigations, and holds direct value for clinical practice. Specifically, it guides prioritising active drug concentration measurement or regimen adjustment based on drug resistance testing to optimise efficacy, and enables targeted neurocritical care for prompt management of complications like increased intracranial pressure,21 and assists in judging the timing of adjuvant corticosteroid therapy, while remaining a complement to, not replacement for, comprehensive clinical judgement.
