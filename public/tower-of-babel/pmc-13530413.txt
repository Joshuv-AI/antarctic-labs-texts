Pre-Pregnancy Physical Activity Lowers Risk of Diet-Controlled but Not Insulin-Treated Gestational Diabetes Mellitus: A Korean Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Hye-Ji Han, Hyun Jung Lee, Tae Ho Kim, MinJeong Kwak, Jin Woo Kim, Su Ji Yang, Ju Yeon Kim, Yong Jun Choi, Nari Kim, Ji Hyon Jang, You Jung Han, Jin Hoon Chung, Dong Wook Kwak, Ji Hyae Lim, Hyun Mee Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) presents clinically as either diet-controlled (diet-controlled gestational diabetes mellitus, A1GDM) or medication-controlled (insulin-requiring gestational diabetes mellitus, A2GDM) subtypes. Pre-pregnancy physical activity represents a significant modifiable factor in GDM prevention and management. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the differential effects of physical activity on these two clinical GDM subtypes.MethodsThis secondary analysis utilized data from the Korean Pregnancy Outcome Study, a longitudinal multicenter cohort. From 3,457 singleton pregnant participants, physical activity was assessed at five time points from pre-pregnancy to postpartum using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form. Logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for GDM subtypes according to physical activity. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used for A2GDM analysis.ResultsOf the participants, 231 developed GDM (overall prevalence 6.7%). Among these cases, 198 (85.7%) were diagnosed with A1GDM, and 33 (14.3%) with A2GDM. No significant differences in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity or muscle-strengthening activity were observed across three groups in baseline characteristics. However, Women who performed muscle-strengthening activity before pregnancy had a significantly lower risk of A1GDM (aOR, 0.492; 95% confidence interval, 0.262–0.923), with risk decreasing as activity frequency increased (P for trend = 0.014).ConclusionPre-pregnancy muscle-strengthening activity is associated with significantly reduced risk of A1GDM. These findings highlight the importance of muscle-strengthening activity before conception as a potential strategy for GDM prevention.Keywords: Diabetes, Gestational; Exercise; Longitudinal Studies; Pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is characterized by impaired glucose metabolism during pregnancy, resulting in elevated blood glucose levels and reduced insulin sensitivity. Typically diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, GDM refers to glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy.1 It affects up to 14.7% of pregnancies worldwide, with a prevalence exceeding 10% in Asia, a rate that continues to rise alongside increasing obesity and the aging maternal population.2,3 GDM poses significant short- and long-term health risks for both mothers and their offspring. In mothers, it increases the likelihood of complications such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, macrosomia, and delivery-related challenges. In babies, GDM is associated with a heightened risk of preterm birth, neonatal hypoglycemia, and shoulder dystocia.4,5,6,7,8,9,10

Current prevention and management strategies for GDM primarily rely on lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and physical activity, as no universally accepted pharmacological treatment exists. Although insulin therapy is sometimes used, its effectiveness can be limited by insulin resistance.11 Clinically, GDM is classified into two subtypes based on treatment requirements: diet-controlled gestational diabetes mellitus (A1GDM), which can be managed through nutritional therapy alone, and insulin-requiring gestational diabetes mellitus (A2GDM), which necessitates pharmacological intervention for glycemic control.12 Poorly managed A1GDM can progress to A2GDM, where insulin is used to control blood sugar. Furthermore, women diagnosed with A2GDM during pregnancy face a substantially increased risk of developing T2DM postpartum.11 Despite advances in understanding GDM, the underlying mechanisms and differential responses to treatment between these two clinical subtypes remain poorly understood.

International guidelines recommend that pregnant individuals engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week - activities such as walking, swimming, and low-impact aerobics are commonly suggested.13 Numerous studies have consistently reported the protective effects of adequate physical activity against GDM and its role in enhancing glycemic control.14,15,16,17,18 Our previous study further strengthened the evidence supporting the effectiveness of exercise in GDM prevention, demonstrating a significantly lower risk of GDM among women who participated in pre-pregnancy strength training.19 However, despite the distinct clinical management and treatment strategies for GDM subtypes, there remains a notable lack of research investigating the effects of exercise specifically for each GDM subtype. Therefore, the current study aims to extend our prior findings by stratifying GDM into its two major subtypes including A1GDM and A2GDM and longitudinally analyzing the associations between physical activity and the risk of each GDM subtype. This subtype-specific approach may contribute to more targeted preventive strategies and a deeper understanding of GDM pathophysiology.

METHODS

Study population and design

Data for this study were obtained from the Korean Pregnancy Outcome Study (KPOS), a longitudinal prospective cohort study focusing on adverse pregnancy outcomes, which was conducted at the two secondary hospitals in Seoul, Korea between March 2013 and January 2017.19 Of the 4,537 women enrolled in the cohort, 3,457 singleton pregnancies were included in the present analysis, after excluding women with pre-existing diabetes mellitus. GDM diagnosis followed a two-step screening protocol. Initial screening was performed using a 50 g glucose challenge test (GCT) between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. Women with GCT values ≥ 140 mg/dL proceeded to a diagnostic oral glucose tolerance test. Detailed diagnostic criteria and thresholds are provided in our previous report.19 Women diagnosed with GDM were classified as A2GDM if insulin prescription was documented in their medical records during pregnancy, and as A1GDM otherwise. Based on pregnancy outcomes, participants were categorized into three groups: normal (n = 3,226), A1GDM (n = 198), and A2GDM (n = 33) (Fig. 1).

A1GDM = diet-controlled gestational diabetes mellitus, A2GDM = insulin-treated gestational diabetes mellitus, GDM = gestational diabetes mellitus.

Data were collected at four scheduled visits: visit 1 (approximately 12 gestational weeks [GW]), visit 2 (24 GW), visit 3 (36 GW), and visit 4 (4–6 weeks postpartum). A structured survey regarding pre-pregnancy lifestyle factors was administered during visit 1.

Demographic data

Demographic and clinical characteristics were obtained from the KPOS dataset. Socio-demographic information including maternal age, educational attainment, and household income and health-related behaviors such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, and dietary behavior were collected through structured interviews using questionnaires. Additional clinical information, including obstetric history (e.g., parity, history of GDM, history of previous macrosomia), medical history (e.g., hypertension and polycystic ovary syndrome [PCOS]), and first-degree family history of diabetes, was obtained via self-report and medical records. Dietary behavior was assessed using the Mini Dietary Assessment index which includes 10 items scored on a Likert scale (maximum 50 points), with higher scores indicating healthier dietary behaviors.20

Physical activity measurements

Physical activity was measured using the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).21 Each domain assessed walking, moderate, and vigorous physical activities performed for at least 10 consecutive minutes daily over 7 days. The IPAQ was used to assess participants' physical activity at five time points: pre-pregnancy, visit 1 (first trimester), visit 2 (second trimester), visit 3 (third trimester), and postpartum. Analysis was restricted to subjects with complete IPAQ data at each time points, with missing responses excluded. The number of participants at each time point after accounting for missingness is presented in Fig. 1.

To analyze physical activity data as a continuous variable, metabolic equivalent task (MET) values were applied. These were calculated by multiplying the weekly duration (minutes/week) of walking (3.3 MET), moderate (4 MET), and vigorous (8 MET) activities by their respective MET values. The sum of walking, moderate, and vigorous METs was used to estimate moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) levels. In addition to the standard International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) questionnaire items, we included supplementary questions to assess muscle-strengthening activities performed in the preceding 7 days (e.g., push-ups, sit-ups, dumbbell exercises, weightlifting, and barbell exercises). As in the previous study, this was counted as 5 METs.18,22

Next, the physical activity performance indicators were established based on the criteria outlined in the Korea Health Statistics 2020, and the IPAQ data collected in this study were used to generate performance attainment variables for statistical analysis.23

1. Walking performance: Walking at least 30 minutes per day for at least 10 minutes per session, at least 5 days per week.

1. Walking performance: Walking at least 30 minutes per day for at least 10 minutes per session, at least 5 days per week.

2. MVPA performance: Engaging in 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity, 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity physical activity, or a combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities (1 minute of vigorous-intensity equals 2 minutes of moderate-intensity).

2. MVPA performance: Engaging in 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity, 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity physical activity, or a combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities (1 minute of vigorous-intensity equals 2 minutes of moderate-intensity).

3. Muscle-strengthening activity performance: Performing muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days in the past week.

3. Muscle-strengthening activity performance: Performing muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days in the past week.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations or 95% confidence intervals (CI), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis was used to assess between-group differences in continuous variables. Pearson’s χ2 tests were used for categorical variables.

To estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for the associations between physical activity and GDM subtypes, separate regression models were fitted for each outcome. For A1GDM, standard logistic regression was employed. For A2GDM, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was used to reduce small-sample bias and improve estimation stability. Both models were adjusted for the same covariates: maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), education level, smoking status, hypertension, family history of diabetes, parity, and prior GDM. Dose-response relationships were examined in supplementary analyses by categorizing muscle-strengthening activity frequency (0, 1, ≥ 2 days/week) and testing for linear trends. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05 or a 95% CI that did not include 1.0.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of Cheil General Hospital (IRB No. CGH-IRB-2013–10) and CHA Hospital (IRB No. 2013-14-KNC13–018), and informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment. The current analysis using data from KPOS was additionally approved by CHA Bundang Medical Center (IRB No. CHAMC 2021-04-052).

RESULTS

Study population characteristics

Among the 3,457 participants included in the analysis, 198 were diagnosed with A1GDM, and 33 with A2GDM, resulting in an overall GDM prevalence of 6.7% (Fig. 1). In the demographics of the study population, statistically significant differences were observed across the three groups. Variables showing differences included maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, educational attainment, household income, smoking status, history of hypertension, first-degree family history of diabetes, parity, history of GDM, and history of previous macrosomia (Table 1). Maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI were higher in both GDM groups, with the highest averages observed in the A2GDM group. In contrast, employment status, alcohol consumption, dietary behavior index, and history of PCOS did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

Association between GDM subtype and physical activity

Separate logistic regression models were performed to evaluate whether pre-pregnancy physical activity was independently associated with each GDM subtype, adjusting for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, educational attainment, smoking status, history of hypertension, first-degree family history of diabetes, parity, and history of GDM (Table 4). For A1GDM, meeting the walking performance indicator showed a protective association in crude analysis (odds ratio [OR], 0.703; 95% CI, 0.520–0.951; P = 0.022), but this association was attenuated after adjustment for covariates (aOR, 0.773; 95% CI, 0.567–1.054; P = 0.104). Meeting MVPA performance criteria was not significantly associated with A1GDM risk in either crude or adjusted models. In contrast, pre-pregnancy engagement in muscle-strengthening activities was associated with a significantly lower risk of A1GDM in both crude (OR, 0.456; 95% CI, 0.246–0.846; P = 0.013) and adjusted models (aOR, 0.492; 95% CI. 0.262–0.923; P = 0.027).

DISCUSSION

This secondary analysis of prospective cohort data evaluated the association between physical activity and GDM subtypes. Our findings indicate that pre-pregnancy muscle-strengthening activity was significantly associated with a reduced risk of A1GDM, with a clear dose-response relationship. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between any physical activity indicators and A2GDM risk. Walking performance showed a protective trend for A1GDM in crude analysis, but this association was attenuated after adjustment for covariates.

Previous research has consistently demonstrated the protective effects of physical activity in preventing and managing GDM. International guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per week during pregnancy, primarily emphasizing aerobic activities such as walking, swimming, and low-impact aerobics.13,24,25 In our cohort, the proportion of women who met the recommended threshold varied substantially from pre-pregnancy to postpartum (12.8–71.9%), yet this adherence was not protective against either GDM subtype. A recent meta-analysis examining the role of lifestyle in the prevention of gestational diabetes also reported mixed results regarding the intensity of physical activity by trimester; specifically, light to moderate activity was effective in the first trimester, whereas moderate-to-vigorous exercise in the second trimester showed limited effectiveness.26 This suggests the need for subtype-specific, individualized guidance to prevent and manage GDM.

In contrast, Women who engaged in pre-pregnancy muscle-strengthening activities experienced approximately half the risk of A1GDM compared to those who did not. This finding aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that muscle-strengthening activities may be particularly effective in reducing GDM risk,16,27 potentially through distinct mechanisms compared to aerobic exercise. Given that GDM shares several pathophysiological characteristics with type 2 diabetes mellitus, muscle-strengthening activity may help reduce risk through improvements in glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, adiposity, muscular strength, and body composition.28 Supporting this mechanism, a recent Korean prospective study demonstrated that women with GDM who had higher muscle mass showed significantly lower insulin resistance after delivery and reduced progression to postpartum type 2 diabetes mellitus, independent of body fat mass.29 This finding reinforces the critical role of skeletal muscle as a metabolically active organ in glucose metabolism and diabetes prevention.

However, no significant association was observed for A2GDM. This null finding should be interpreted with caution as it may primarily reflect limited statistical power due to the small number of A2GDM cases rather than a true absence of effect. The wide CIs observed for A2GDM analyses reflect the statistical limitations inherent to small sample sizes. While it is possible that A1GDM and A2GDM differ in their responsiveness to physical activity interventions, our study cannot definitively address this question. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating observations that require validation in larger studies with sufficient power to detect associations in rarer outcomes such as A2GDM.

These findings have important clinical implications for GDM prevention. Pre-pregnancy muscle-strengthening activity appears to reduce A1GDM risk and may inform the development of prevention strategies. The protective mechanism may partly operate through improved glucose regulation before conception. Recent evidence shows that even modest elevations in pre-pregnancy fasting glucose within the normal range are associated with increased GDM risk.30 This suggests that metabolic benefits of pre-pregnancy muscle-strengthening activity may begin before pregnancy and contribute to GDM prevention through enhanced glycemic control. Current guidelines provide limited specific recommendations regarding muscle-strengthening exercise for women planning pregnancy. Our study suggests that initiating muscle-strengthening activity before conception may be particularly beneficial for preventing A1GDM. Further research is needed to establish optimal exercise protocols and to clarify whether similar benefits extend to A2GDM prevention. Pre-pregnancy counseling should consider incorporating muscle-strengthening activity as part of comprehensive GDM prevention efforts.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large prospective cohort and comprehensive physical activity data collected longitudinally from pre-pregnancy through postpartum. By assessing physical activity across multiple time points, we were able to examine both the timing and type of activity in relation to GDM risk. Furthermore, stratifying outcomes by GDM subtype offers novel insights into potential differential effects. The observed dose-response relationship for muscle-strengthening activity and A1GDM strengthens the evidence for a potential protective effect and demonstrates consistency across different levels of exposure.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate associations but cannot establish causality, even though the prospective design supports temporality. Second, pre-pregnancy physical activity was assessed retrospectively during early pregnancy using the self-reported IPAQ-SF, which may be subject to recall bias and over-reporting. Self-reported measures may have led to misclassification of physical activity physical activity performance status. Third, the small number of A2GDM cases resulted in limited statistical power and wide CIs that reflect substantial uncertainty in the effect estimates. The null findings for A2GDM should be interpreted with caution and do not necessarily indicate the absence of an association. Fourth, missing data and participant attrition across time points may have affected the precision of our estimates. Finally, the study did not explore the combined effects of physical activity with other modifiable lifestyle factors. Future studies employing objective measures of physical activity and comprehensive lifestyle assessments are needed to validate and expand on these findings.

In conclusion, pre-pregnancy engagement in muscle-strengthening activity was associated with a reduced risk of A1GDM in a dose-dependent manner, supporting its potential role as a target for GDM prevention. The lack of significant association for A2GDM likely reflects limited statistical power rather than a true null effect, though this requires confirmation in larger studies. These findings emphasize the importance of pre-pregnancy physical activity, particularly Muscle-strengthening activity, in GDM prevention strategies. Future research should employ larger sample sizes, objective activity measures, and prospective intervention designs to further clarify the causal relationships and subtype-specific effects of physical activity on GDM risk.
