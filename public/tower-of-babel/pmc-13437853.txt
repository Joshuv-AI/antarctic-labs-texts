Predictive value of Slug-positive circulating tumor cells for neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in mesenchymal malignancies
Authors: Niezhenghao He, Shilei Liu, Haowen Cui, Junwei Feng, Weimin Liang, Bao Yao, Fusha Amu, Baoxiu Xu, Qingxin Fan, Peng Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the predictive value of Slug-positive circulating tumor cells (CTCs) for responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in mesenchymal malignancies.MethodsProspective observational analysis of 59 patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Peripheral blood was collected pre-treatment for CTC classification. Chemotherapy response was defined as complete response or partial response. Logistic regression identified factors associated with poor response. A combined model integrating Slug+ epithelial CTCs (ECTCs) and Slug+ mesenchymal CTCs (MCTCs) was developed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis assessed predictive performance; Cox regression evaluated long-term outcomes.ResultsResponders had lower Slug+ MCTCs (2.12 ± 1.84 vs. 3.85 ± 2.25) and higher Slug+ ECTCs. The combined model showed superior predictive ability (AUC = 0.873) versus single CTC subtypes. Patients with predicted probability > 0.62 were responders. Cox analysis indicated the model predicted long-term prognosis when Slug+ MCTCs and Slug+ ECTCs were not simultaneously zero.ConclusionsPhenotypic heterogeneity influences neoadjuvant chemotherapy response. High Slug+ MCTCs and low Slug+ ECTCs indicate insensitivity. The combined model showed promising performance for identifying treatment response and potential prognostic relevance in this prospective cohort.Keywords: Slug-positive circulating tumor cells, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy response, Phenotypic heterogeneity, Predictive model

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has become an integral component of the multidisciplinary management of various solid tumors, including mesenchymal malignancies. By reducing tumor burden prior to surgery, NAC aims to improve resectability and ultimately enhance long-term outcomes (Sidaway 2025). However, substantial inter-individual heterogeneity exists in therapeutic responses to standard NAC regimens. While some patients achieve significant tumor regression, others derive little benefit or exhibit primary resistance, which may delay definitive surgical intervention and expose patients to unnecessary treatment-related toxicity (Pasquali et al. 2025). Therefore, identifying reliable biomarkers capable of predicting NAC responsiveness before treatment initiation remains a critical unmet need in precision oncology.

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs), which are shed from primary or metastatic lesions into the peripheral blood, represent a minimally invasive biomarker that reflects the biological behavior of tumors in real time (Yang et al. 2025). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated associations between CTC burden and tumor stage, metastatic potential, and prognosis across multiple solid malignancies (Janjua et al. 2025). Nevertheless, the clinical utility of CTC enumeration alone in predicting treatment response is limited, suggesting that intrinsic phenotypic heterogeneity among CTCs may play a decisive role in therapeutic sensitivity.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a fundamental biological process that endows tumor cells with enhanced migratory capacity, invasiveness, and resistance to cytotoxic therapies (Zhang et al. 2025). Slug (SNAI2), a key EMT-associated transcription factor, has been implicated in tumor progression, chemoresistance, and adverse clinical outcomes (Singh et al. 2024). Recent studies have highlighted that EMT-related phenotypes within the CTC population, particularly Slug-positive mesenchymal CTCs (Slug+ MCTCs), may be preferentially associated with treatment resistance and aggressive tumor behavior (Khan et al. 2024; Srinivasan et al. 2025). However, the predictive value of Slug-positive CTC subpopulations, including both epithelial and mesenchymal phenotypes, for NAC response in mesenchymal malignancies has not been systematically investigated.

In this prospective observational study, we analyzed clinical data from patients with mesenchymal malignancies who received standard NAC at our institution. Pre-treatment peripheral blood samples were collected to characterize Slug-positive epithelial CTCs (Slug+ ECTCs) and Slug-positive mesenchymal CTCs (Slug+ MCTCs). The primary objectives were to evaluate the associations between these CTC subpopulations and NAC response, to establish a combined predictive model integrating Slug+ ECTCs and Slug+ MCTCs, and to assess its performance in predicting both short-term treatment efficacy and long-term prognosis. By delineating the clinical relevance of CTC phenotypic heterogeneity, this study aims to provide evidence-based insights to support individualized neoadjuvant treatment strategies in patients with mesenchymal malignancies.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-center prospective observational study was conducted at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Patients with mesenchymal malignancies who received standardized neoadjuvant therapy between September 2020 and February 2024 were consecutively enrolled. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of distant metastasis before initiation of treatment (n = 15), (2) severe comorbidities (n = 13), (3) coexistence of other malignant tumors (n = 5), (4) noncompliance with prescribed treatment or changes in therapeutic regimens during treatment (n = 6), and (5) loss to follow-up (n = 5) (Fig. 1). In accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, all patients received the corresponding first-line recommended treatment regimen. Patients with osteosarcoma received standard MAP therapy (methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin). Patients with Ewing’s sarcoma received VDC/IE therapy (vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide therapy alternating with ifosfamide and etoposide therapy). For patients with synovial sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma or fibrosarcoma, an anthracycline-based chemotherapy regimen was used as the first-line treatment approach. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital (approval number: [2024]498) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study, and patients were permitted to opt out of the research.

Study design and workflow diagram

Materials

Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant tubes, red blood cell lysis buffer, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 4% paraformaldehyde were purchased from Solaibao (Beijing, China). The CanPatrol® CTC classification system, automated CanPatrol® analysis system, size-based nanomembrane filtration devices, and filtration membranes were purchased from SurExam (Guangzhou, China). Protease, capture probes targeting EpCAM, Vimentin, and Slug, hybridization buffer, 0.1 × saline sodium citrate buffer, DAPI, and fluorescently labeled detection probes were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA).

Sample preparation

Peripheral blood samples (10 mL) were collected from patients in EDTA anticoagulant tubes prior to the initiation of NAC. To minimize potential epithelial contamination during venipuncture, the first 2 mL of blood was discarded. All samples were processed within 4 h of collection. Red blood cells were first removed using a red blood cell lysis buffer, and the remaining nucleated cells were subsequently resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline containing 4% paraformaldehyde and fixed for 5 min.

CTCs detection

The fixed cells were subjected to identification and classification of CTCs using the CanPatrol® CTC classification system (Mao et al. 2021; Long et al. 2022). Briefly, the fixed cell suspension was loaded into the filtration system, and CTCs were enriched using a size-based nanomembrane filtration method based on the size differences between CTCs and leukocytes. The cells retained on the membrane were then analyzed by multiplex mRNA in situ analysis (MRIA) based on branched DNA signal amplification. Specifically, the retained cells were pretreated with protease and hybridized with capture probes targeting EpCAM, Vimentin, and Slug at 42 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the membrane was washed with 0.1 × saline sodium citrate buffer to remove unbound probes. Signal amplification was then performed by adding amplification reagents, followed by the addition of fluorescently labeled detection probes to enable CTC identification and phenotypic classification. Red fluorescent probes were used to detect EpCAM mRNA expression, green fluorescent probes for Vimentin mRNA, and purple fluorescent probes for Slug mRNA, while nuclei were counterstained with blue DAPI. Fluorescence signals and CTCs enumerated were initially analyzed using the automated CanPatrol® analysis system and subsequently verified manually under a fluorescence microscope.

Peripheral blood samples from healthy donors were used as negative controls. To minimize observer bias associated with subjective fluorescence interpretation, each sample was independently assessed by two investigators in a blinded manner. In cases of discrepant results, a third investigator performed an additional review to reach a final determination, thereby ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the results.

Definition of CTC phenotypes

CTC phenotypes were classified based on fluorescence labeling results as follows: epithelial CTCs (ECTCs) were defined as EpCAM-positive and Vimentin-negative cells; mesenchymal CTCs (MCTCs) were defined as EpCAM-negative and Vimentin-positive cells; and hybrid CTCs (HCTCs) were defined as cells co-expressing both EpCAM and Vimentin. Slug-positive CTCs were defined as CTCs exhibiting detectable purple fluorescence signals.

Data collection

Patient data were systematically extracted from both electronic and paper-based medical records and subsequently compiled into a structured electronic database. The collected variables covered the following key domains: (1) demographic and anthropometric characteristics, including age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), (2) tumor-related clinical features, including maximum tumor diameter, metastatic status, pathological diagnosis (e.g., osteosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, liposarcoma, Ewing’s sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, and others), and Enneking staging, (3) CTC-related parameters, including total CTC counts and subgroup analyses of ECTCs, MCTCs, and HCTCs, and (4) molecular phenotypic characteristics, including the numbers of Slug-positive CTCs and their corresponding subpopulations (Slug+ CTCs, Slug+ ECTCs, Slug+ MCTCs, and Slug+ HCTCs). These variables were collected to explore the associations between clinical characteristics, CTC profiles, and treatment response. Follow-up data were obtained from outpatient records and telephone follow-up. Progression-free survival was defined as the time from diagnosis to documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Overall survival was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow-up. The last follow-up date was September 2025.

Definition of NAC response

The therapeutic response to NAC was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. Tumor response was classified based on radiological assessment into complete response, partial response, stable disease, and progressive disease. In this study, complete response and partial response were defined as an effective response to NAC, reflecting objective tumor regression. In contrast, stable disease and progressive disease were categorized as an ineffective response, indicating a lack of meaningful tumor shrinkage or disease progression. This dichotomized classification was applied uniformly across all patients and served as the primary criterion for subsequent efficacy-related analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, while ordinal variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. All P values were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05. To identify independent factors associated with chemotherapy response, both univariate and multivariate analyses were performed. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of chemotherapy response. Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, a combined model was established to project treatment response. The predictive performance of the model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. Progression-free survival and overall survival were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the included patients

The demographic, clinical, and CTC characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Table 1. A total of 59 patients with mesenchymal malignancies were enrolled, including 27 patients in the ineffective group and 32 patients in the effective group according to the response to NAC. No significant intergroup differences were observed with respect to age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), sex distribution, tumor size, or pathological diagnosis (all P > 0.05). However, the proportion of patients without distant metastasis was significantly higher in the effective group than in the ineffective group (P = 0.032), and Enneking staging also differed significantly between the two groups (P = 0.031). Regarding CTC analysis, patients in the ineffective group exhibited significantly higher MCTC counts compared with those in the effective group (P = 0.002). Slug+ ECTC counts were significantly higher in the effective group than in the ineffective group (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Logistic regression analysis of NAC response

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with the efficacy of standard NAC. Among the circulating tumor cell-related variables, significant associations were observed for specific Slug-positive CTC subpopulations. Slug+ MCTCs counts were significantly associated with NAC response (P = 0.02), whereas Slug+ ECTCs counts demonstrated a strong association with treatment efficacy (P < 0.01). Variables showing statistical relevance in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariate binary logistic regression model. Both Slug+ MCTC and Slug+ ECTC counts remained independently associated with the response to NAC after adjustment for potential confounders (both P < 0.01). Specifically, Slug+ ECTC emerged as an independently associated factor of chemotherapy efficacy, whereas Slug+ MCTC retained an independent association with treatment response in the opposite direction (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that Slug-positive CTC subpopulations, rather than total CTC burden, were independently associated with NAC response in patients with mesenchymal malignancies.

Predictive performance of Slug+ CTCs for NAC response

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative performance of Slug-positive CTC subpopulations for the efficacy of NAC used the area under the curve (AUC). Slug+ ECTCs demonstrated good discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.815). In contrast, Slug+ MCTCs showed limited predictive performance (AUC = 0.422). Notably, the combined model incorporating Slug+ ECTCs and Slug+ MCTCs showed better discriminatiEve performance, yielding an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI 0.703–0.927), which was higher than that of either marker alone. These results indicate that the integration of Slug+ CTC subpopulations may improve discrimination of treatment response to standard NAC in patients with mesenchymal malignancies. The optimal cutoff value of the combined model was 0.62 according to the Youden index. In this cohort, patients with a predicted probability > 0.62 were classified as having an effective response to chemotherapy (Fig. 3).

Model validation

To explore the preliminary applicability of the Slug+ CTC-based predictive model, we validated its performance in six patients who completed follow-up. Among these patients, only one case (Patient 2) showed a discrepancy between the predicted and actual outcomes, resulting in an overall predictive accuracy of 83% (Table 2). Immunofluorescence characterization revealed distinct CTC subpopulation profiles and Slug expression patterns between the two representative patients (Fig. 4a). For ease of use, we visualized the predicted probabilities corresponding to different counts of Slug⁺ MCTCs and Slug⁺ ECTCs using a heatmap (Fig. 4b). Consistent with the model output, quantitative assessment of tumor size demonstrated a marked tumor reduction in patient 1 after NAC, while patient 2 showed only limited tumor shrinkage (Fig. 4c and d).

Predictive value of Slug⁺ CTCs for survival

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses demonstrated that the actual treatment response was significantly associated with patient outcomes. The median follow-up duration was 37.5 months (range, 4.0–52.0 months). Patients with an effective actual response showed markedly improved progression-free survival (P = 0.002) and overall survival (P = 0.002) compared with those with an ineffective response (Fig. 5a and d). When patients were stratified according to the predicted treatment response, no significant differences were observed in either progression-free survival or overall survival in the overall cohort (Fig. 5b and e). However, after excluding patients whose Slug+ ECTC and Slug+ MCTC values were both zero, the predicted treatment response was able to significantly discriminate survival outcomes. In this refined cohort, patients predicted to be effective exhibited significantly prolonged progression-free survival (P = 0.03) and overall survival (P = 0.041) compared with those predicted to be ineffective (Fig. 5c and f). These findings indicate that the predictive model shows improved prognostic performance after removing patients with absent Slug-positive circulating tumor cell subpopulations, highlighting the importance of Slug+ ECTCs and MCTCs in stratifying treatment response and survival risk.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically investigated the predictive value of Slug-positive CTCs subpopulations for the response to standard NAC in patients with mesenchymal malignancies. We found that Slug+ ECTCs and Slug+ MCTCs exhibited opposite and independent associations with chemotherapy response, highlighting the biological and clinical heterogeneity within Slug-positive CTCs. Second, a combined predictive model incorporating both Slug+ ECTCs and Slug+ MCTCs demonstrated superior discriminative performance compared with either marker alone. Finally, after excluding patients lacking Slug-positive CTCs, the model showed significant prognostic value for progression-free survival and overall survival. Collectively, these findings indicate that Slug-positive CTC subpopulations represent biologically meaningful determinants of neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in mesenchymal malignancies.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a complex biological process that plays a central role in tumor initiation and progression. During EMT, epithelial cells lose their original cell polarity and cell–cell adhesion properties and acquire mesenchymal characteristics (Zhang et al. 2025). Slug (also known as SNAI2), a member of the SNAIL family, is a zinc-finger transcription factor that serves as a key regulator of EMT (Singh et al. 2024). Mechanistically, Slug promotes EMT by repressing E-cadherin expression, thereby disrupting tight intercellular junctions, while simultaneously upregulating N-cadherin, enhancing cellular motility and facilitating tumor cell migration and metastasis (Srinivasan et al. 2025). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Slug is closely associated with EMT, tumor aggressiveness, and unfavorable prognosis in cervical, hepatocellular, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancers (Kaufhold and Bonavida 2014). However, these studies are almost exclusively conducted in epithelial malignancies. In contrast, mesenchymal malignancies are characterized by an intrinsic mesenchymal phenotype, and their metastatic behavior relies more directly on cell motility, invasiveness, and hematogenous dissemination. While Slug expression has been extensively evaluated in tumor tissues, its functional significance in CTCs, the key mediators of hematogenous metastasis, remains unexplored in mesenchymal malignancies.

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs), which detach from primary or metastatic lesions and enter the bloodstream, are widely recognized as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers across multiple cancer types (Sundling and Lowe 2019). Cristofanilli et al. reported that baseline CTC levels represent one of the strongest predictors of both progression-free survival and overall survival. In patients with early-stage breast cancer, the presence of ≥ 5 CTCs per 30 mL of blood is associated with significantly worse prognosis (Cristofanilli et al. 2004). Similarly, in non-metastatic colorectal cancer, a CTC count ≥ 1 per 7.5 mL of peripheral blood predicts a higher risk of recurrence and metastasis and poorer survival outcomes (Bork et al. 2015). In patients with non-small cell lung cancer, elevated total CTC counts and cytokeratin-negative CTC counts are associated with reduced survival and increased risk of distant metastasis (Bian et al. 2020). Moreover, dynamic longitudinal monitoring of CTCs has been shown to independently predict response to neoadjuvant therapy, recurrence risk, and survival outcomes in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) (Ko et al. 2023).

In the present study, we found that a higher baseline level of Slug-positive epithelial CTCs (Slug+ ECTCs) was associated with a higher likelihood of response to NAC. One possible explanation is that elevated Slug expression in this context may reflect tumor cells that still retain epithelial characteristics, rendering them more susceptible to cytotoxic chemotherapy. This hypothesis is supported by previous studies. In 2013, Yu et al. reported that patients with a higher proportion of epithelial-phenotype CTCs exhibited more favorable clinical outcomes (Yu et al. 2013). More recently, Sun et al. demonstrated that patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma who had higher preoperative ECTC levels experienced better prognoses (Sun et al. 2019). As another terminal phenotype of EMT, MCTCs have been associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poorer outcomes in multiple studies, a finding further supported by our results. Pan et al. reported that MCTCs were more frequently detected in patients with FIGO stage II disease, pelvic lymph node metastasis, lymph vascular invasion, and deep stromal invasion (Pan et al. 2019). Furthermore, MCTC-centered features have been successfully used to construct highly effective predictive models for metastatic risk in renal cell carcinoma (Guan et al. 2024).

The enhanced predictive accuracy of the combined Slug+ ECTC and Slug+ MCTC model reflects the balance between chemotherapy-sensitive and chemotherapy-resistant tumor cell states within the circulating compartment. In addition, this model enables stratified prediction of survival outcomes in patients with detectable Slug-positive tumor cells. This observation further supports the role of Slug+ CTCs as indicators of clinically relevant tumor plasticity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the CanPatrol® platform used in this study offers practical advantages in clinical settings, including a shorter detection cycle and relatively lower cost, its performance in terms of accuracy, consistency, and cross-platform comparability still requires further validation when compared with more established and standardized CTC detection methods such as the CellSearch® system. Therefore, future prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, incorporating more advanced and standardized CTC detection technologies, are warranted to further validate our findings and enhance the robustness, reliability, and clinical applicability of the conclusions. Mesenchymal malignancies encompass a heterogeneous group of tumors, and subtype-specific effects could not be fully explored. Finally, future studies should explore alternative or complementary predictive strategies for patients without detectable Slug-positive CTCs.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that Slug-positive CTC subpopulations are robust predictors of neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in mesenchymal malignancies. Slug+ ECTCs and Slug+ MCTCs show independent and opposite associations with treatment efficacy, underscoring the biological heterogeneity of CTCs. A combined model integrating these two markers achieved strong predictive performance. Although predictive power was limited in patients without detectable Slug-positive CTCs, exclusion of these cases significantly improved survival discrimination. Overall, Slug-positive CTC subpopulations, rather than total CTC burden, represent key determinants of chemotherapy response and prognosis, supporting their potential as noninvasive biomarkers for personalized treatment decision-making in mesenchymal tumors.
