Elementary discrete diffusion/redistancing schemes for the mean curvature flow
Antonin Chambolle, Daniele De Gennaro, Massimiliano Morini

Abstract
. We consider here a fully discrete and explicit scheme for the mean curvature flow of boundaries, based on an elementary diffusion step and a precise redistancing operation. We give an elementary convergence proof for the scheme under the standard CFL condition h ∼ ε 2 h\sim\varepsilon^{2} , where h h is the time discretization step and ε \varepsilon the space step. We discuss extensions to more general convolution/redistancing schemes.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we analyse a basic
explicit
fully discrete (in space and time) scheme for computing the mean curvature
flows of curves and surfaces which are boundaries of sets. This approach belongs to
the family of convolution-generated motions, yet it is much more elementary. It
consists in alternating an explicit approximation of the heat equation, with time-step
h>0
, with a redistancing
operation on rescaled regular grids
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, for
\varepsilon>0
. It is heuristically well known that this kind of approach works,
and such algorithm has been proposed many times in the literature
as a natural, more precise extension of the celebrated Merriman-Bence-Osher (MBO in short)
approach
[
21
]
,
starting from (the first Algorithm in)
[
14
]
,
where error estimates are established (see also
[
15
,
22
]
which mostly use the
technique to simulate higher-order flows, and
[
13
]

which implements a method, introduced and analysed in
[
14
, Sec. 5.3]
,
for simulating large-scale multiple grain evolution in materials).
Indeed, as clearly pointed out in
[
14
]
, despite its computational efficiency, the MBO algorithm has some well-known limitations. In particular, if the spatial discretization is not refined alongside the time step, pinning phenomena may appear. More generally, significant discretization errors may arise in the computed evolution. To address these issues, it is crucial to use a discretization method that allows for a more precise representation of the interface position within the grid.
Unlike the characteristic functions used in the original MBO scheme, signed distance functions can be represented more accurately on uniform grids, even at subgrid resolutions, thanks to their Lipschitz continuity.

The algorithm we propose aims to address these issues by combining a diffusion step with a redistancing one, as proposed in
[
14
]
— yet in a fully discrete formulation. In the first part of the paper, instead of considering the MBO-type diffusion step, based on the discrete heat kernel, we directly consider the Euler explicit scheme for the discrete heat equation. The redistancing step also changes, as we now use the redistancing operator we recently introduced in
[
7
]
.
In our first main result, Theorem
2.3
, we show the convergence of such a fully discrete scheme towards a generalized solution to the mean curvature flow.
We then generalize this result in two directions. From Section
4
on, we modify the diffusion step to allow for general kernels
K^{\varepsilon}
depending on the space discretization parameter
\varepsilon
and satisfying natural assumptions. Precisely, the kernels
K^{\varepsilon}
are required to be non-negative, symmetric and of mass one. Moreover, their second moment (which is assumed to be vanishing as
\varepsilon\to 0
) intrinsically defines the time step
h
as a function of the space step
\varepsilon
(see (
4.4
)). Finally, the kernels are assumed to suitably approximate the Laplacian. Such an approximation property is imposed in terms of the pointwise convergence of the discrete Fourier transform of the kernels to the symbol of the Laplacian (see (
4.5
)), which allows for straightforward verification in many practical examples, as detailed in Section
6
. Under these assumptions, we can show that our diffusion/redistancing scheme converges, see Theorem
4.6
.

We then study a modification of the discrete redistancing operator we defined in
[
7
]
to account for a nonlinearity
\gamma
in the scheme. The idea is that, in some applications, it may be more interesting to consider evolving profiles of the form
\gamma(\,\textnormal{sd})
rather than the signed distance function itself, for instance
in the context of Allen-Cahn-based approximation schemes for the mean curvature flow
[
2
,
6
]
. We show how to design such a scheme in the instance where
\gamma
is the Modica-Mortola
(or Cahn-Hilliard) optimal profile. We refer to Section
5
for a more detailed explanation of the heuristic. Considering the general kernels
K^{\varepsilon}
previously introduced for the diffusion step, we are able to show in Theorem
5.4
that the proposed
diffusion/nonlinear redistancing scheme converges. This seems to give a partial explanation for the good results produced by fully learned approaches for the mean curvature flow, introduced in
[
6
]
.

We are not aware of any previous rigorous convergence result for a fully discrete similar
scheme towards mean curvature flows, as the steps go to zero,
with precise bounds on the possible ratio between the time and space steps, even in the basic instance of the very simple Euler explicit scheme.
The previously mentioned references
consider a time-discrete setting, but continuous in
space, and then assume that if the space step is small enough, one will approximate the continuous process.
The point is that analysing the convergence of a fully discrete
approximation requires a quite precise control
of the redistancing operation, which is in general unavailable.
There are, however, some works addressing this issue
[
23
,
20
]
for MBO-type algorithms (see also
[
31
]
for a similar study in the level-set framework). In the first reference, a study of convergence of the fully discrete MBO scheme is presented in dimension 2, showing convergence towards viscosity solutions to the mean curvature motion under the assumption that
\varepsilon=o(h)
, while pinning is present whenever
h=o(\varepsilon)
. The second reference studies the instance of general graphs, supported on a fixed submanifold
M
. The authors consider MBO-type algorithms where the convolution kernels approximate
the heat kernel corresponding to a weighted Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
. The convergence of this scheme towards mean curvature flows on the manifold
M
is proved under some technical assumptions on the kernels, and for
\varepsilon=o(h^{\frac{3}{2}})
.

In our case, by introducing a particular form of (brute force)
redistancing (actually,
already introduced in
[
7
]
for the study of discrete crystalline
curvature flows), defined by means of inf and sup-convolution
formulas,
we address this issue in a simple way and establish convergence
of various (similar) approaches, with steps depending on the
convergence to the heat equation of the convolution step.
Our definition of a redistancing operator is computationally more expensive than
standard PDE based approach computed using fast-marching type
technique
[
26
,
28
,
25
,
12
,
27
]
, yet whose
definition make less clear the actual error estimates
and regularity properties of the distance that they compute (see Section
8.1
for a practical method to reduce the computational cost).
Note that in
[
7
]
, we were studying an implicit
approach for the crystalline mean curvature flow and could show that, in some situation,
one could completely uncouple the time and space steps and
still get consistency of the scheme. This is not the case here,
as any redistancing produces some (spatial) error which can
accumulate (in time) as the steps go to zero, if the time-step
is not always larger than the spatial error of the redistancing, that is
h\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
.
We show however that this is the only limitation.

The paper is structured as follows.
We first study the discrete
diffusion/redistancing scheme
built upon the standard discretization of the Laplace operator on a grid in Sections
2
-
3
.
Then, in Section
4
we generalize the convergence result to rather general symmetric,
non-negative convolution kernels.
In Section
5
we introduce and study the instance of nonlinear redistancing operators.
We show some examples of kernels fitting in our framework (as the heat kernel, or the kernel associated to the implicit Euler’s scheme) in Section
6
and some numerical experiments in Section
7
.
In Section
8
, we provide some concluding remarks.

2.
A simple algorithm

2.1.
Laplacian operator and redistancing on
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.

Laplacian

The first scheme we present is based on the resolution of an
explicit
scheme for the
heat flow, based on a standard discretization of the Laplacian. We will see
in Section
4
how to generalize to other convolution
or averaging type operators.

Given
v=(v_{i})_{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}
a real-valued function on the discrete grid
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
,
N\geq 1
,
we introduce the discrete Laplace operator:

(\Delta_{\varepsilon}v)_{i}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(v_{i+\varepsilon e_{n}}-2v_{i}+v_{i-\varepsilon e_{n}})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(v_{i+\varepsilon e_{n}}+v_{i-\varepsilon e_{n}})-\frac{2N}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{i}.

It is well known, and straightforward to show that if
\eta
is a smooth (
C^{2}
) function
defined near a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\eta^{\varepsilon}=(\eta(\varepsilon i))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}
, then
(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\eta^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon i}\to\Delta\eta(x)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, whenever
\varepsilon i\to x
.

Redistancing

As in
[
14
]
, the scheme will alternate a step of the discrete heat-flow and a redistancing operation. The redistancing is a general and important
issue in the approximation of geometric flows, and there are many ways to
address it, usually based on the resolution of a Hamilton-Jacobi equation,
either static or evolutionary
[
26
,
19
]
.
However, in general, the method is studied in the continuous
setting and one assumes that the spatial discretization is
fine enough so that the scheme which is used approximates
well enough the continuum limit.

We analyse here the global convergence of such schemes as the
space and time steps go to zero. In that case, most approximate
redistancing schemes to not seem robust enough to yield
consistency of the evolution with the continuous limit.
In some sense, we need to compute an “exact” discrete
distance to the evolving set.
This is why we propose, as in our previous work
[
7
]
, an inf/sup-convolution
based approach.

In practice, our method takes a
1
-Lipschitz function over
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
and returns a proxy for
the distance to the zero level set, which takes into account the values of the initial function and
keeps a (more or less good) sub-pixel accuracy.
Assuming that
(u_{i})_{i}
is
1
-Lipschitz:

|u_{i}-u_{j}|\leq|i-j|\quad\forall i,j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N},

we define the redistancing functions
\,\textnormal{sd}^{\pm}[u]
by letting for
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\,\textnormal{d}^{+}[u]_{i}:=\inf_{j:u_{j}<0}u_{j}+|j-i|\,,\\
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u]_{i}:=\sup_{j:u_{j}\geq 0}\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{j}-|j-i|\end{cases}

and

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\,\textnormal{d}^{-}[u]_{i}:=\sup_{j:u_{j}>0}u_{j}-|j-i|\,,\\
\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}[u]_{i}:=\inf_{j:u_{j}\leq 0}\,\textnormal{d}^{-}_{j}+|j-i|.\end{cases}

When not ambiguous, we drop the dependence “
[u]
” in the notation.

The function
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}
,
\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}
can be seen as discrete “signed distance functions”
to respectively the set
\{i:u_{i}<0\}
and
\{i:u_{i}\leq 0\}
.
In some sense,
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}
,
\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}
are respectively the largest
and smallest distance functions which we may use in our
scheme, and proving convergence
for these choices will yield convergence for any other choice
which lies in between. A good choice
for a more precise distance is, for instance, to consider the average
(\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}+\,\textnormal{sd}^{-})/2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The distance function
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}
may also be defined as follows:

(1)

\,\textnormal{d}^{+}
is the largest
1
-Lipschitz function such that
\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}\leq u_{i}
when

u_{i}<0
, and in particular
\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}=u_{i}
if
u_{i}<0
and
\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}\geq u_{i}
if
u_{i}\geq 0
,

(2)

\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}
is the smallest
1

\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}_{i}\geq\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}

when
u_{i}\geq 0
, and in particular
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}_{i}=\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}\geq u_{i}
if
u_{i}\geq 0
and
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}_{i}\leq\,\textnormal{d}^{+}_{i}=u_{i}

if
u_{i}<0
.

We also deduce that
\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{+}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow u_{i}\geq 0

\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{+}<0\Leftrightarrow u_{i}<0
.

A similar, symmetric statement holds for
\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}
, see
[

∎

This makes more precise the previous statement about “signed distance function”:

On the other hand, let

),

for

2.2.
Algorithm

We analyse here the following algorithm.
Given an initial closed set
E^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we set
E^{0,\varepsilon}:=\{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}:i+[0,\varepsilon)^{N}\cap E^{0}\neq\emptyset\}
, and let
u^{0,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}^{0,\varepsilon}
be a
1
-Lipschitz function
on
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
such that
\,\textnormal{sd}^{0,\varepsilon}_{i}<0
on
E^{0,\varepsilon}
, and
>0
else. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define:

(2.5)

\frac{u_{i}^{k+1,\varepsilon}-\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k,\varepsilon}}{h}=(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon})_{i},

where the time-step
h
will be precised right after, as a function of
\varepsilon
.
We then let

(2.6)

\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u^{k+1,\varepsilon}],

applying (
2.1
) to
u=u^{k+1,\varepsilon}
(alternatively, we could use (
2.2
)).

We fix
\theta\in]0,1]
and let
h=\theta\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2N}
.
Then (
2.5
) becomes:

u_{i}^{k+1,\varepsilon}=(1-\theta)\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k,\varepsilon}+\frac{\theta}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\,\textnormal{sd}_{i+\varepsilon e_{n}}^{k,\varepsilon}+\,\textnormal{sd}_{i-\varepsilon e_{n}}^{k,\varepsilon}\right),

where
(e_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:
this guarantees that
u_{i}^{k+1,\varepsilon}
is
1
-Lipschitz
as a convex combination of
1
-Lipschitz functions, so that the algorithm makes sense.

In particular from Lemma
2.1
we see that where
u^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{i}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{i}\geq 0
,

(2.7)

\frac{\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k+1,\varepsilon}-\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k,\varepsilon}}{h}\geq(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon})_{i},

while where
u^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{i}<0\Leftrightarrow\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{i}<0
,

(2.8)

\frac{\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k+1,\varepsilon}-\,\textnormal{sd}_{i}^{k,\varepsilon}}{h}\leq(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon})_{i}.

We see that approximately, we have built a signed distance function
which is a supersolution of the heat equation where positive,
and a subsolution where negative, which is consistent with the
distance function to a set evolving by its mean curvature.
Our first result is the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the function
(d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i})
, defined for

and

(where

denotes the

such that

is the distance function
to a supersolution of the (generalized)
mean curvature flow starting from
E^{0}
,
and

is the distance function to a supersolution starting from

{E^{0}}^{\complement}
. In particular, if the mean curvature flow
E(t)
starting
from

is unique, then

converges to the signed distance
function to

, up to extinction.

Here, by generalized solution, we mean a solution in the viscosity
sense, as defined in
[

,

,

, or, equivalently,
in the distributional sense introduced in
[

3.
Convergence of the algorithm

3.1.
Notions of generalized mean curvature flow

We consider the classical generalized sub/superflows which
have been defined since
[
17
]
by viscosity solutions
or barriers
[
5
]
, yet the characterization
which is best adapted for showing consistency of our schemes
is the one involving the (signed) distance function to the interface,
introduced in
[
30
, Def. 5.1]
(which essentially is saying
that the flow is characterized by the property that the distance
to the evolving boundary should be a supersolution of the heat flow
away from the boundary), and the distributional variant
in
[
10
, Def. 2.1]
which was introduced for the study of nonsmooth
flows (but it is equivalent in the smooth case). We recall the definition
in
[
10
, Def. 2.1]

(see also
[
8
, Def. 2.2]
), specified to the case of the standard mean curvature flow, which is
less common than
[
30
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
E^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a closed set. Let
E
be a closed
set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,+\infty)
and for each
t\geq 0
denote

E(t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(x,t)\in E\}
. We say that
E
is a supersolution
of the mean curvature flow (in short, a superflow) with initial datum
E^{0}
if

(a)

E(0)\subseteq E^{0}
;

(b)

for all
t\geq 0
, if
E(t)=\emptyset
then
E(s)=\emptyset

for all
s>t
;

(c)

E(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}}}{{\rightarrow}}E(t)
as
s\uparrow t
for all
t>0

(Kuratowski left continuity);

(d)

setting
d(x,t)=\dist(x,E(t))
for
(x,t)\in(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}))\setminus E
where

T^{*}:=\inf\{t>0:E(s)=\emptyset\text{ for }s\geq t\},

then the following inequality holds in the distributional sense in
(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}))\setminus E

(3.1)

\frac{\partial d}{\partial t}\geq\Delta d.

A subsolution (in short, subflow) is the complement of a supersolution, and we say that
E
is a solution if it is a supersolution while
\mathring{E}
is a subsolution.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that in definition
[
8
, Def. 2.2]
, an additional requirement on
\Delta d
is imposed, namely:
\Delta d
is a Radon measure in
(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}))\setminus E
, and
(\Delta d)^{+}\in L^{\infty}(\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}):d(x,t)\geq\delta\})
for every
\delta\geq 0
. However, when
d
is the distance induced by the Euclidean norm, this condition is automatically satisfied, thanks to the following standard semiconcavity estimate. Let
d(x,t)\geq\delta>0
,
y
be the projection of
x
on
\partial E(t)
and
h
small enough (
|h|\leq\delta/2
). Then

d(x+h,t)-2d(x,t)+d(x-h,t)\leq|x+h-y|-2|x-y|+|x-h-y|\\
=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-|t|)\frac{1}{|x-y+th|}\Big(I-\frac{(x-y+th)\otimes(x-y+th)}{|x-y+th|^{2}}\Big)h\cdot h\,dt\\
\leq c|h|^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1-|t|}{(x-y+th)}\,dt\leq\frac{c}{\delta}|h|^{2},

from which follows that the distributional second spatial derivatives of
d
are uniformly bounded above by
\frac{c}{\delta}
in the region
\{d\geq\delta\}
.

It has been proven in
[
10
]
(see also
[
9
]
) that generically (up to fattening) there is a unique solution starting from a set
E^{0}
;
in addition, it is proved in
[
10
, Appendix]
that (
3.1
) holds in the viscosity sense
and that this definition is equivalent to
[
30
, Def. 5.1]
.

We prove that the sequence of distance functions produced
by the algorithm defines, in the limit, generalized solutions
in the this sense.
This will follow from (i) an estimate on the motion of a ball which yields enough
control on
d^{\varepsilon}
in time to show compactness, (ii) an elementary
consistency argument. We point out that the only true difficulty for generalizing
this result is point (i),
which is where the precision of the redistancing plays a major role.

3.2.
Evolution of a ball

As said, a crucial point for proving the convergence of the method, is to control the behaviour
of the algorithm when
\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}_{i}
represents a ball of radius
R>0
. For this,
given
\theta\in]0,1]
, we let for
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
,

(3.2)

u_{i}=|i|-R,\quad v_{i}=(1-\theta)u_{i}+\frac{\theta}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(u_{i+\varepsilon e_{n}}+u_{i-\varepsilon e_{n}})

and let
d=\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[v]
. Then, we show:

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists a dimensional constant
C\geq 1
such that if
\varepsilon/R
is small enough (depending
only on the dimension), then for every
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}

d_{i}\leq|i|-R+\frac{C}{R}\varepsilon^{2}=|i|-R+\frac{2NC}{\theta R}h

Here as before
h,\varepsilon,\theta
are linked by the relationship
\theta=2Nh/\varepsilon^{2}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume
\theta=1
.
We first observe that

v_{i}=-R+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|i+\varepsilon e_{n}|+|i-\varepsilon e_{n}|.

We remark that if
|i|\geq 2\varepsilon
,
e\in\{e_{n},-e_{n}:n=1,\dots,N\}
,

(3.3)

|i+\varepsilon e|=|i|+\varepsilon\frac{i\cdot e}{|i|}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\frac{1}{|i+t\varepsilon e|}\left(I-\frac{(i+t\varepsilon e)\otimes(i+t\varepsilon e)}{|i+t\varepsilon e|^{2}}\right)\varepsilon e\cdot\varepsilon e\,dt\\
\leq|i|+\varepsilon\frac{i\cdot e}{|i|}+\frac{1}{|i|-\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}

so that

v_{i}\leq|i|-R+\frac{1}{|i|-\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

If we assume that
\varepsilon\leq\min\{1,R/2\}
, then for
|i|\leq R-\varepsilon
,

v_{i}<0
. Hence, since by Lemma
2.1

it is enough
to estimate
d_{i}^{+}
at points

i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
v_{i}\geq 0
, we assume
|i|\geq R-\varepsilon
. For all such
i
we have:

(3.4)

\,\textnormal{d}^{+}[v]_{i}:=\inf_{j:v_{j}<0}v_{j}+|j-i|\leq\inf_{j:\frac{R}{2}+\varepsilon\leq|j|\leq R-\varepsilon}v_{j}+|j-i|\leq\inf_{j:\frac{R}{2}+\varepsilon\leq|j|\leq R-\varepsilon}|j|-R+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{R}+|j-i|,

assuming
\varepsilon\leq R/8
so that the set of
j
’s is not empty.
Consider
j
with
\frac{R}{2}+\varepsilon\leq|j|\leq R-\varepsilon
, close to the
segment
[0,i]
: if
\tilde{\jmath}
is the projection of
j
onto
[0,i]
, one has

|j|+|j-i|=\sqrt{|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}+|\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}}+\sqrt{|\tilde{\jmath}-i|^{2}+|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}}\\
\leq|\tilde{\jmath}|+\frac{|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}}{2|\tilde{\jmath}|}+|\tilde{\jmath}-i|+\frac{|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}}{2|\tilde{\jmath}-i|}=|i|+\left(\frac{1}{2|\tilde{\jmath}|}+\frac{1}{2|\tilde{\jmath}-i|}\right){|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}}

If
\varepsilon/R
is small enough (depending only on
N
), we can find

j,\tilde{\jmath}
such that
|j-\tilde{\jmath}|^{2}\leq N\varepsilon^{2}
and
R/2\leq|\tilde{\jmath}|\leq 3R/4
,
so that
|\tilde{\jmath}-i|\geq R/4
. We obtain for such a choice:

(3.5)

|j|+|j-i|\leq|i|+\frac{3N}{R}\varepsilon^{2}

This shows that where
d^{+}_{i}
is non-negative, it is
less than
|i|-R+\frac{3N+1}{R}\varepsilon^{2}
as soon as
\varepsilon/R
is small enough. As
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}
is the smallest

1
-Lipschitz function larger than
d^{+}_{i}
where it is non-negative (Lemma
2.1
),
this achieves the proof.
∎

3.3.
Consistency of the algorithm

In this section as before,
\theta\in]0,1]
is fixed and
the small parameters
\varepsilon,h
are linked through
h=\theta\varepsilon^{2}/2N
.
We investigate the limit of the scheme as
\varepsilon,h\to 0
.

Let
E^{0,\varepsilon},u^{0,\varepsilon}
be defined as in Section
2.2
, so that
u^{0,\varepsilon}
,
1
-Lipschitz,
with
E^{0,\varepsilon}=\{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}:u^{0,\varepsilon}_{i}<0\}
and
{(E^{0,\varepsilon})}^{\complement}=\{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}:u^{0,\varepsilon}_{i}\geq 0\}
.
Note that as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

E^{0,\varepsilon}\to E
and
{(E^{0,\varepsilon})}^{\complement}\to{(\mathring{E}^{0})}^{\complement}
in Hausdorff distance. If we consider a “nice” initial closed set
E^{0}
, such that
E^{0}
is the closure of its interior (and
\partial E
has empty interior), and is not empty, nor its complement, we can also choose as
E^{0,\varepsilon}
the set
E\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
We let
\,\textnormal{sd}^{0,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u^{0,\varepsilon}]
. We run the algorithm, obtaining a sequence

\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}
of “signed distance functions”.

As in the statement of Theorem
2.3
, we let for each
t\geq 0
,
d^{\varepsilon}(t)=\,\textnormal{sd}^{[t/h],\varepsilon}
and then let,
for
t>0
,
E_{\varepsilon}(t)=\{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}:d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i}<0\}
.
Then we let
E_{\varepsilon}=\{(i,t)\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times[0,+\infty):i\in E_{\varepsilon}(t)\}

and
F_{\varepsilon}=\{(i,t)\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times[0,+\infty):i\not\in E_{\varepsilon}(t)\}
.
We find a subsequence such that both
E_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to E

and
F_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to{A}^{\complement}
in the Kuratowski
sense in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,+\infty)
, where
A
is an open set and

E
a closed set. Observe that
A\subset E
.
We let

T^{*}=\inf\{t>0:E\cap(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(t,+\infty))=\emptyset\text{ or }{A}^{\complement}\cap(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(t,+\infty))=\emptyset\}\in[0,+\infty].

Note that it may (in general will) happen that after some time,

u^{k+1,\varepsilon}
defined by (
2.5
) becomes positive (or negative),
in which case
\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}
(defined by (
2.6
)) will be
+\infty

(respectively,
-\infty
) and the corresponding sets
E_{\varepsilon}(t)
(or
{E_{\varepsilon}(t)}^{\complement}
)
will be empty: in the limit, this corresponds to times which are past the extinction
time
T^{*}
of
E
or
{A}^{\complement}
.

Concerning the distances, we see that
if for some
k
,
\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}_{i}\geq R>0
at
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, then
\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}_{j}\geq R-|j-i|

(as it is
1
-Lipschitz). Therefore, using Lemma
3.3
and the fact that
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[-u]=-\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}[u]\geq-\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u]
, we get

\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}\geq\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[-v_{\cdot-i}]=-\,\textnormal{sd}^{-}[v_{\cdot-i}]\geq R-|\cdot-i|-\frac{C}{R}h,

where
v
has been defined in (
3.2
). Therefore, iterating the argument we show that for some
C\geq 1

depending only on the dimension, if
\varepsilon
is small enough,

\,\textnormal{sd}^{\ell,\varepsilon}_{i}\geq R-\frac{C}{R}(\ell-k)h

for
\ell\geq k
and as long as the right-hand side is larger than
R/2
(that is,
\ell h-kh\leq R^{2}/2C
).

This may also be written as

(3.6)

d^{\varepsilon}(s)_{i}\geq d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i}-\frac{C}{R}(s-t+h)

for
d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i}\geq R>0
,
0\leq t\leq s\leq CR^{2}
for some constant
C
depending
only on the dimension. A similar, symmetric statement holds if
d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i}\leq-R<0
.
In particular, by our choice of
E^{0}
, one has
T^{*}>0
.

The estimate (
3.6
) allows to reproduce the proof of
[
10
, Prop. 4.4]

and find that except for a countable set of times the function

\sum_{i\in\varepsilon_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{N}}d^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{i}(t)\chi_{i+[0,\varepsilon)^{N}}

converges locally uniformly to some function
d(x,t)
which
is locally finite for
t<T^{*}
and such that its positive and negative parts
respectively satisfy (thanks to (
2.3
), (
2.4
)):

(3.7)

d^{+}(\cdot,t)=\dist(\cdot,E(t))\quad\text{ and }\quad d^{-}(\cdot,t)=\dist(\cdot,{A}^{\complement}(t)),

where
E(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(x,t)\in E\}
and
A(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(x,t)\in A\}
,
for
t<T^{*}
. Moreover, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the functions
\dist(x,E(\cdot))
and
\dist(x,{A}^{\complement}(\cdot))
are
left-continuous and right-lower-semicontinuous. Equivalently, the maps
E(\cdot)
and
{A}^{\complement}(\cdot)
are left-continuous and right-upper-semicontinuous with respect to the
Kuratowski convergence. Finally,
E(0)=E^{0}
and
A(0)=\mathring{E^{0}}
.
In addition,
d(\cdot,t)\equiv+\infty
or
-\infty
for all
t>T^{*}
.

From (
3.7
) one has in particular that
{E}^{\complement}\cap(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}))=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}):d(x,t)>0\}
while

A=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}):d(x,t)<0\}
, and

\displaystyle d^{+}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\max\{0,d^{\varepsilon}_{i_{k}}(t_{k})\}:\varepsilon_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni(i_{k},t_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}(x,t)\right\}

\displaystyle-d^{-}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\min\{0,d^{\varepsilon}_{i_{k}}(t_{k})\}:\varepsilon_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni(i_{k},t_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}(x,t)\right\}

are the classical relaxed half-limits (see for instance
[
3
,
4
]
).

At this point there are two elementary directions to prove the convergence
of the algorithm. One can establish the consistency with the
viscosity approach of
[
30
]
, showing that
d
is a viscosity
super-solution of the heat equation in
\{d>0\}
(and a subsolution
in
\{d<0\}
), or equivalently the
consistency with respect to the distributional
Definition
3.1
.
We describe both proofs, as the first one is more natural
from a historical point of view, yet we found easier to generalize
the second to non-compactly supported kernels (see Section
4
).

The viscosity approach

For the viscosity point of view, the idea is to consider a smooth
test function
\eta(x,t)
with
\eta\leq d
,
\eta(\bar{x},\bar{t})=d(\bar{x},\bar{t})>0
, and assume without loss of generality that the contact point is unique
[
11
]
.
Then, it is standard that for small
\varepsilon_{k}
, there is

i_{k}\to\bar{x}
,
i_{k}\in\varepsilon_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, and
t_{k}\to\bar{t}
such that
for all
t>0
and
i\in\varepsilon_{k}\mathbb{Z}^{N}
:

\eta_{k}(i,t)=\eta(i,t)+(d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k})_{i_{k}}-\eta(i_{k},t_{k}))\leq d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t)_{i},\quad\eta_{k}(i_{k},t_{k})=d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k})_{i_{k}}>0.

We have:

\eta_{k}(i_{k},t_{k})=d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k})_{i_{k}}\geq(1-\theta)d_{i_{k}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k}-h_{k})+\frac{\theta}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(d_{i_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}e_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k}-h_{k})+d_{i_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}e_{n}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t_{k}-h_{k})\right)\\
\geq(1-\theta)\eta_{k}(i_{k},t_{k}-h_{k})+\frac{\theta}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\eta_{k}(i_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}e_{n},t_{k}-h_{k})+\eta_{k}(i_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}e_{n},t_{k}-h_{k})\right),

so that:

\frac{\eta_{k}(i_{k},t_{k})-\eta_{k}(i_{k},t_{k}-h_{k})}{h_{k}}\geq\left(\Delta_{\varepsilon_{k}}\eta_{k}(\cdot,t_{k}-h_{k})\right)_{i_{k}}.

Using that
\eta
is smooth and passing to the limit, we recover:

\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}(\bar{x},\bar{t})\geq\Delta\eta(\bar{x},\bar{t}),

showing that
d^{+}
is a viscosity supersolution of the heat equation
in
\{d>0\}
.

The distributional approach

On the other hand, the variational point of view is tackled as follows,
considering rather a test function

\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}({E}^{\complement}\cap(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}));\mathbb{R}_{+})
. The support
U_{\eta}

is at distance from
E
, hence for
\varepsilon_{k}
small enough it
is also at positive distance from
E_{\varepsilon_{k}}
so that

d^{\varepsilon_{k}}
is bounded from below by a positive number on
\overline{U}_{\eta}\cap(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times[0,+\infty))
.
Thanks to (
2.7
), it follows that

\frac{d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t)_{i}-d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t-h_{k})_{i}}{h_{k}}\geq(\Delta_{\varepsilon_{k}}d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t-h_{k}))_{i}

for
(i,t)\in\overline{U}_{\eta}
, hence

\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\left(\frac{d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t)_{i}-d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t-h_{k})_{i}}{h_{k}}-(\Delta_{\varepsilon_{k}}d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t-h_{k}))_{i}\right)\eta(i,t)dt\geq 0.

Rearranging the sums, this is also:

\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\left(\frac{\eta(i,t)-\eta(i,t+h_{k})}{h_{k}}-(\Delta_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\eta(t+h_{k},\cdot))_{i}\right)d^{\varepsilon_{k}}(t)_{i}dt\geq 0.

In the limit (since
\eta
is smooth, and
d^{\varepsilon_{k}}
converges uniformly for almost each time), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(-\partial_{t}\eta-\Delta\eta)d\,dxdt\geq 0

so that

(3.8)

\frac{\partial d}{\partial t}\geq\Delta d\quad\text{ in }\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T^{*}):d(x,t)>0\}),

that is in the sense of distributions (or measures). In the same way, we have:

(3.9)

\frac{\partial d}{\partial t}\leq\Delta d\quad\text{ in }\quad\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T^{*}):d(x,t)<0\}).

4.1.
Convolution kernels

Our idea is now to generalize the algorithm in (

\partial E(t)

Assume that

satisfies (

Remark 4.3

We remark that the Euler’s explicit algorithm considered in the previous Section

falls in the present framework. We fix a time step

converging to

and we start by observing that (

) (with

), with the kernel

given by

Here
(\delta_{j})_{i}=1
if

and

otherwise and we require

.
Note that

, it is symmetric,

and

h:=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}|j|^{2}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}=\frac{\tau}{N\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\varepsilon e_{k}|^{2}=\tau,

so that, in particular, (

) is satisfied. Lastly, let us check (

\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}e^{-2\pi ij\cdot\xi}=\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\cos(2\pi j\cdot\xi)=1+\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\cos(2\pi\varepsilon e_{k}\cdot\xi)-1),

as

4.3.
Control of the balls

To obtain the convergence of the algorithm with the general kernels

, we first need to show
an equivalent of the estimate in Lemma

(to obtain
compactness in time of the distances).
We first show the following estimate.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

satisfy (

), (
4.3
), (
4.4
), and let

.
Then for any

(K^{\varepsilon}*|\cdot|)_{i}\leq|i|+\frac{8N}{R}h

Proof.

We write:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}K_{j}|i-j|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|j|\geq R/2}K_{j}(|i|+|j|)+\sum_{|j|<R/2}K_{j}|i-j|

\displaystyle=|i|+\sum_{|j|\geq R/2}K_{j}|j|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|j|<R/2}K_{j}(|i-j|+|i+j|-2|i|).

We have, using (

\sum_{|j|\geq R/2}K_{j}|j|\leq\frac{2}{R}\sum_{|j|\geq R/2}K_{j}|j|^{2}\leq\frac{4Nh}{R}.

For
|i|\geq 3R/4

\sum_{|j|<R/2}K_{j}(|i-j|+|i+j|-2|i|)\leq\frac{2}{R}\sum_{|j|<R/2}K_{j}|j|^{2}\leq\frac{4Nh}{R}

∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Assume

4.4.
Consistency

We will show the following convergence result,

as a corollary.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

, the function

Again, we expect that with our assumptions, it should not be

It seems however that proving consistency with the distributional solutions of Definition

Proof.

We start from the inequality

\frac{\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{j}-\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}_{j}}{h}\geq\left(\frac{K^{\varepsilon}-\delta}{h}*\,\textnormal{sd}^{k,\varepsilon}\right)_{j}

in any

\frac{d^{\varepsilon}(t+h)-d^{\varepsilon}(t)}{h}\geq\frac{K^{\varepsilon}-\delta}{h}*d^{\varepsilon}(t)

Thanks to the estimate in Corollary

and

is a superflow,

{A}
a subflow, and in case

By symmetry of the statements,

\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}({E}^{\complement}\cap(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T^{*}));\mathbb{R}_{+})

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{d^{\varepsilon}_{j}(t+h)-d^{\varepsilon}_{j}(t)}{h}\,dt\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}d^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\,dt

The convergence of the left hand side does not raise any difficulty:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{d^{\varepsilon}_{j}(t+h)-d^{\varepsilon}_{j}(t)}{h}\,dt=-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}d\,dxdt,

and we wish to show convergence also for the
right hand side:

(4.10)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}d^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d\Delta\eta\,dxdt.

We note that there exists
C>0
such that, for all

such that the support of
\eta(\cdot,t)
is
not empty,

|d_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t)|\leq C(1+|j|)
.
Let
R_{0}
such that
\text{spt}(\eta)\subset\subset B(0,R_{0})\times(0,T^{*})
. If
\varepsilon>0
is
small enough then for any

and

,

(4.11)

\varepsilon^{N}\left|\sum_{j^{\prime}:|j^{\prime}|>R}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}d^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\right|=\varepsilon^{N}\left|\sum_{j:|j|<R_{0}}\eta(j,t)\sum_{k:|j-k|>R}\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\delta_{k}}{h}d^{\varepsilon}_{j-k}(t)\right|\\
\leq|B(0,R_{0})|\|\eta\|_{\infty}\sum_{k:|k|>R-R_{0}}\frac{|k|^{2}K^{\varepsilon}_{k}}{h}\frac{C(1+|k|+R_{0})}{|k|^{2}}\leq 2N\frac{3C|B(0,R_{0})|\|\eta\|_{\infty}}{R-R_{0}}\to 0

as

(uniformly in
\varepsilon
), using (
4.4
) for the first inequality.

We fix
t\in(0,T^{*})
and we now show that for any
\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,

(4.12)

\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Delta\eta(x,t)\varphi(x)\,dx

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}=\varepsilon^{-N}\int_{j^{\prime}+[0,\varepsilon)^{N}}\varphi\,dx
for any

.
Denoting
u^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}
,
we observe that
u^{\varepsilon}\in\ell^{1}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N})\cap\ell^{\infty}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}),\varphi^{\varepsilon}\in\ell^{1}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N})
, and that
Plancherel’s formula holds:

\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}u^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}=\int_{[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}}\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}}(\xi)d\xi.

This follows from the calculation

\int_{[0,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}]^{N}}\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}}(\xi)d\xi=\varepsilon^{2N}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\int_{[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}}u^{\varepsilon}_{j}e^{-2i\pi j\cdot\xi}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}e^{2i\pi j^{\prime}\cdot\xi}\,d\xi=\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j}u^{\varepsilon}_{j}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j}

which obviously holds for finite sums, and then in the limit.
We denote
(\eta^{\varepsilon}_{j})_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}

.
Then, we have:

\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}e^{-2i\pi(j+(j^{\prime}-j))\cdot\xi}=\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\frac{\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)-1}{h}

where as before

.
In particular thanks to (

),
|\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)|\leq 4\pi^{2}N|\xi|^{2}|\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)|
, and thanks to (

),

\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\to-4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\widehat{\eta}(\xi,t)

for all

,
where

.
Lemma
A.1
then shows that

. We note that the constants in these estimates depend

In particular there exists

which shows that

\left|\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j^{\prime},t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}-j}-\delta_{j^{\prime}-j}}{h}\right|=|u_{j}^{\varepsilon}|=\left|\int_{[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}}\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi\dot{j}}d\xi\right|\leq CM

for some dimensional constant

.

On the other hand, the functions
\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}
are such that for any

,

|\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)|\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\quad\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\to\widehat{\varphi}(\xi).

From Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem we deduce:

\int_{[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}}\widehat{u^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}}d\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}-4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\widehat{\eta}(\xi,t)\overline{\widehat{\varphi}}\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Delta\eta(x,t)\varphi(x)\,dx.

This shows (
4.12
).

We now fix
\delta>0
and consider a spatial cut-off

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}:|j^{\prime}|>R}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}d^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\,dt\right|\leq\delta,

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j,t)\frac{K^{\varepsilon}_{j-j^{\prime}}-\delta_{j-j^{\prime}}}{h}\varphi^{\varepsilon}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Delta\eta(x,t)d(x,t)\,dxdt

By the locally uniform convergence of

,

5.
Nonlinear redistancing: applications to deep learning based approximation algorithms

In this section give
a partial explanation to the good numerical results produced in
[
6
]

by fully learned approaches for the mean
curvature flow.
There, the authors propose to learn a Neural Network
whose structure mimicks a convolution-redistancing scheme.
Starting from a discrete 2D or 3D image,
supposed
to approximate a phasefield
u(x,t)=\gamma(\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\textnormal{sd}_{E}(x,t))
, where
\gamma
is the Modica-Mortola optimal profile and
\sigma>0
, their simplest network is implementing a convolutional filter followed by a nonlinear operation.
The convolutional filter
approximates
\rho*u
for some (learned) nonnegative kernel
\rho
, while the nonlinear operation is supposed to rebuild
from the convolution a profile
V\approx\gamma(\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\textnormal{sd}_{E}(x,t+h))

after a time-step
h>0
.
Except for the non-linearity, which
is a pointwise update, this is quite close to the
process described in this paper.
Our approach seems to justify that learning the filter
is easy and robust: indeed, we have seen that basically
any symmetric enough filter is able to approximate the
mean curvature flow, with an appropriate redistancing (see Remark
4.2
).
In addition, it is not difficult to check that this
flow is also characterized by the property that

v
defined above is a super/sub solution of
the heat flow in/out the evolving shape, see below for details. It means that one
could imagine to replace the signed distance function with such a non-linear profile in the scheme.
In this section, we show how to make this heuristic rigorous.

5.1.
Algorithm and control of the balls

We denote
\gamma
the optimal profile of some Cahn-Hilliard (a.k.a. Modica-Mortola) energy

\int\frac{\sigma}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{\sigma}\int W(u)dx

where
0<\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
W
is a (smooth) two-wells potential, with
W(t)>W(-1)=W(1)
for
t\not\in\{-1,1\}
. This means
that
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to(-1,1)
is the minimizer of

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2}(\gamma^{\prime}(s))^{2}+W(\gamma(s))ds

with
\gamma(0)=0
,
\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\gamma(s)=\pm 1
, which satisfies both
\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=W^{\prime}(\gamma(s))
and
\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{2W(\gamma(s))}

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
see for instance
[
2
]
. For
simplicity, we use the standard choice
W(t)=(1-t^{2})^{2}/2
. In this instance, one finds that

\gamma(s)=\tanh(s)

and that, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=1-\gamma^{2}(s)\geq 0,\quad\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)=-2\gamma(s)\gamma^{\prime}(s)\leq 0.

We consider a kernel
K^{\varepsilon}
satisfying (
4.2
), (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and define
the following algorithm. Given

u^{0,\varepsilon}:=\,\textnormal{sd}^{0,\varepsilon}
defined upon an initial closed set
E^{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we define for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

(5.1)

u^{k+1,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}[\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(u^{k,\varepsilon}))].

Let us note that the algorithm above is well-defined, in the sense that if
u:\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is 1- Lipschitz and
K^{\varepsilon}
is non-negative and
\sum_{i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}K^{\varepsilon}_{i}=1
then
\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(u))
is still 1-Lipschitz. Indeed, consider
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
1-Lipschitz and
t\in(0,1)
. We have

\left\lvert\frac{d}{dx}\gamma^{-1}(t\,\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x)))\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{t\gamma^{\prime}(f(x))f^{\prime}(x)+(1-t)\gamma^{\prime}(g(x))g^{\prime}(x)}{1-\big(t\,\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x))\big)^{2}}\right\rvert\\[6.45831pt]
\leq\frac{t\gamma^{\prime}(f(x))+(1-t)\gamma^{\prime}(g(x))}{1-\big(t\,\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x))\big)^{2}},

where we used that
\gamma^{\prime}\geq 0
. By convexity,

(t\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\gamma(g(x)))^{2}\leq t\gamma(f(x))^{2}+(1-t)\gamma(g(x))^{2}
so that

\frac{t\gamma^{\prime}(f(x))+(1-t)\gamma^{\prime}(g(x))}{1-\big(t\,\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x))\big)^{2}}\leq\frac{t\gamma^{\prime}(f(x))+(1-t)\gamma^{\prime}(g(x))}{1-t\,\gamma(f(x))^{2}-(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x))^{2}}.

Recalling
\gamma^{\prime}=1-\gamma^{2}
we get

\Big\lvert\frac{d}{dx}\gamma^{-1}(t\,\gamma(f(x))+(1-t)\,\gamma(g(x)))\Big\lvert\leq\frac{1-t\gamma^{2}(f(x))-(1-t)\gamma^{2}(g(x))}{1-t\gamma^{2}(f(x))-(1-t)\gamma^{2}(g(x))}=1.

Thus, considering
u,v:\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
1-Lipschitz, we can extend them to functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and by the estimate above we deduce that
\gamma^{-1}(t\,\gamma(u)+(1-t)\,\gamma(v))

is 1-Lipschitz on
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}.
This allows to show that
\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(u))
is 1-Lipschitz on
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}.
Therefore, the term
\,\textnormal{sd}[\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(u))]
appearing in (
5.1
) is well-defined.

We then prove an estimate on the evolution speed of balls.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
h(\varepsilon)\geq\varepsilon^{2}
. There exist two constants
R_{0},c>0
, such that the following holds. For every
R\in(0,R_{0})
and for

small enough it holds

(5.2)

\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}\Big[\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(|\cdot|-R))\Big]\leq|\cdot|-R+c\tfrac{h}{R}.

Proof.

Let us fix
0<R_{0}\leq 1
such that
\gamma^{\prime}(R_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}
. We denote

v:=K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(|\cdot|-R).

We start remarking that the thesis
follows once we show that in the region
\{v\geq 0\}
it holds

(5.3)

\text{d}^{+}[\gamma^{-1}(v)]\leq|\cdot|-R+\tfrac{ch}{R},

as then
\text{sd}^{+}
is the smallest 1-Lipschitz function lying above
\text{d}^{+}
where it is non-negative.

We will in fact prove the following inequality

(5.4)

v\leq\gamma(|\cdot|-R+\tfrac{ch}{R})\qquad\text{for all }|i|\in(\tfrac{R}{4},\tfrac{R}{2}),

for
R\leq R_{0}
and
h,\varepsilon
small.

Proof of (
5.2
) assuming (
5.4
).

If (
5.4
) holds, then we deduce that

(5.5)

v\leq 0\quad\text{ in }B_{R/2}\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}.

Indeed, from (
5.4
) this inequality already holds in the region
(B_{R/2}\setminus B_{R/4})\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. Then, consider a point
i\in B_{R/4}\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
: there exists
j
such that
|i-j|\leq R/4+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon
and
|j|\in(R/4,R/4+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon)
. By the 1-Lipschitz property, we obtain

v_{i}\leq v_{j}+\tfrac{R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon\leq\gamma\left(-\tfrac{3R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon+\tfrac{ch}{R}\right)+\tfrac{R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon.

Moreover, assuming
\varepsilon,h
small so that
\gamma^{\prime}(-\tfrac{3R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon+\tfrac{ch}{R})\geq\gamma^{\prime}(-R_{0})\geq 1/2
, by the equation above and the convexity of
\gamma
in
\{\gamma\leq 0\}
we get

v_{i}\leq\gamma\left(-\tfrac{3R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon+\tfrac{ch}{R}\right)+\gamma^{\prime}\left(-\tfrac{3R}{4}+\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon+\tfrac{ch}{R}\right)\left(\tfrac{R}{2}+2\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon\right)\leq\gamma\left(-\tfrac{R}{4}+3\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon+\tfrac{ch}{R}\right).

The right-hand side can be made negative by choosing
\varepsilon,h
sufficiently small. This concludes the proof of (
5.5
).

With (
5.5
) in hand, we can follow the argument from Lemma
3.3
to establish (
5.3
), and thus complete the proof of the lemma. Specifically, since (
5.5
) holds in
B_{R/2}\cap\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, it remains to prove (
5.3
) for
|i|\geq R/2
. For such
i
, we apply the definition of
\text{d}^{+}
from (
2.1
), restricting the infimum to the region where
|j|\in(R/4,R/2)
. Using the bound from (
5.4
), we obtain an estimate analogous to the right-hand side of (
3.4
). Since
|i|
is bounded from below, the conclusion follows exactly as in Lemma
3.3
.

Proof of (
5.4
).
To show (
5.4
), we fix
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
|i|\in(R/4,R/2)
and estimate

(5.6)

\begin{split}v_{i}=(K^{\varepsilon}\ast\gamma(|\cdot|-R))_{i}&\leq\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i-j|-R)+\sum_{|j|\geq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\Big(\gamma(|i|-R)+|j|\Big)\\
&\leq\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i-j|-R)+\sum_{|j|\geq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i|-R)+\tfrac{8h}{R},\end{split}

where in the first inequality we used that
\gamma
is 1-Lipschitz and in the second one we used (
4.4
). We need to estimate the term

(5.7)

\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i-j|-R).

Recalling (
3.3
), for
i,j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
|i|\geq R/4
and
|j|\leq R/8
, it holds

|i\pm j|\leq|i|\pm\frac{i\cdot j}{|i|}+\frac{1}{|i|-|j|}\frac{|j|^{2}}{2}\leq|i|\pm\frac{i\cdot j}{|i|}+\frac{4}{R}|j|^{2}.

Therefore, we can rewrite the term (
5.7
) as follows:

(5.8)

\begin{split}\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i-j|&-R)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}(\gamma(|i-j|-R)+\gamma(|i+j|-R))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}(\gamma(|i|-R-\tfrac{j\cdot i}{|i|}+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2})+\gamma(|i|-R+\tfrac{j\cdot i}{|i|}+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2})).\end{split}

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is bounded from above, by a Taylor development we get

\gamma(x+y)=\gamma(x)+\gamma^{\prime}(x)\,y+\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x+sy)(1-s)\,ds\leq\gamma(x)+\gamma^{\prime}(x)\,y+c|y|^{2}.

We thus use this estimate to get

\gamma(|i|-R+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2}\pm\tfrac{j\cdot i}{|i|})\leq\gamma(|i|-R+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2})\pm\frac{j\cdot i}{|i|}\,\gamma^{\prime}(|i|-R+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2})+c|j|^{2}\\
\leq\gamma(|i|-R)\pm\frac{j\cdot i}{|i|}\,\gamma^{\prime}(|i|-R+\tfrac{4}{R}|j|^{2})+|j|^{2}(c+\tfrac{4}{R})

where in the last inequality we used the 1-Lipschitz property. Plugging the inequality above in (
5.8
) and using that
|i|\geq R/2
and
R\leq 1
, we have bounded the term (
5.7
) as follows

\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i-j|-R)\leq\sum_{|j|\leq R/8}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}\gamma(|i|-R)+\frac{ch}{R},

where
c
is a positive constant.
Therefore, by (
5.6
) we get

(K^{\varepsilon}\ast v)_{i}\leq\gamma(|i|-R)+\frac{ch}{R},

where
c
denotes a positive constant. Since
|i|\in(R/4,R/2)
then
\gamma^{\prime}(|i|-R)\geq\gamma^{\prime}(-R_{0})\geq 1/2
, and therefore

(K^{\varepsilon}\ast v)_{i}\leq\gamma(|i|-R)+2c\frac{h}{R}\gamma^{\prime}(|i|-R)\leq\gamma(|i|-R+\tfrac{2ch}{R}),

where the last inequality holds by convexity as long as
|i|-R+\tfrac{2ch}{R}\leq 0
, which holds for
h
small.
∎

5.2.
Consistency

The link between the algorithm (
5.1
) and the motion by mean curvature is provided by the following variant of Soner’s characterization of the mean curvature flow
[
30
]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\sigma>0
and consider
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T)
for
T>0
, a closed set. Set
d(x,t):=\dist(x,E(t))
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
t\in[0,T)
,
where
E(t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(x,t)\in E\}
.
Then

d
is a viscosity supersolution to the heat equation
in
{E}^{\complement}\cap\{t>0\}

if and only if
u(x,t):=\gamma(d(x,t)/\sigma)
is a
viscosity supersolution to
the Allen-Cahn equation:

(5.9)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}W^{\prime}(u)

in the same set.

Proof.

The proof is standard and follows from the fact that if
\varphi(x,t)

is a smooth test function touching from below the graph of
u(x,t)

at
(\bar{x},\bar{t})
, then
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{t})\in(-1,1)
so that
in a neighborhood of
(\bar{x},\bar{t})
,
\tilde{\varphi}(x,t):=\sigma\gamma^{-1}(\varphi(x,t))
is a smooth
test function touching from below the graph of
d(x,t)
. Assuming that
d
is a viscosity supersolution in
\{d>0\}
, one has

\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{x},\bar{t})\geq\Delta\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{x},\bar{t}).

Moreover, it holds
\varphi(x,t)=\gamma(\tilde{\varphi}(x,t)/\sigma)
and, at
(\bar{x},\bar{t})
we have

\partial_{t}\varphi=\frac{1}{\sigma}\gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma}\right)\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}\geq\frac{1}{\sigma}\gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma}\right)\Delta\tilde{\varphi},

since
\gamma
is increasing. Now, note that

\Delta\varphi=\Div\left[\tfrac{1}{\sigma}\gamma^{\prime}(\tfrac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma})\nabla_{x}\varphi\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}(x,t)}{\sigma}\right)|\nabla_{x}\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma}\right)\Delta\tilde{\varphi}.

Since
d(\cdot,\bar{t})
is a distance function, it is standard that
a smooth contact from below at
x=\bar{x}
implies that

|\nabla_{x}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{t})|=1
. Thus, using

\gamma^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}(\gamma)
we conclude

\frac{1}{\sigma}\gamma^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma}\right)\Delta\tilde{\varphi}=\Delta\varphi-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}W^{\prime}\left(\gamma\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}}{\sigma}\right)\right)=\Delta\varphi-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}W^{\prime}(\varphi)

The thesis follows. The reverse implication is shown in the same way.
∎

As a consequence of the previous lemma, if
u
is a viscosity
super solution to (
5.9
) in
\{u>0\}
, it is a subsolution in
\{u<0\}
, and the set
\{u=0\}
does not develop fattening, then
\{u\leq 0\}

evolves by mean curvature.
We can show, however, a stronger result.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T)
,
T>0
, be a closed set
and set
d(x,t):=\dist(x,E(t))
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
t\in[0,T)
,
where
E(t):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:(x,t)\in E\}
,
and
v(x,t)=\gamma(d(x,t)/\sigma)
for some given
\sigma>0
.
Then if
v
is a viscosity supersolution to the
heat equation in
{E}^{\complement}\cap\{t>0\}
, then
also
d
is (and hence, by Lemma (
5.2
)
v

is a supersolution to Allen-Cahn in the same set),
and
E
is a
superflow for the mean curvature motion.

Proof.

Indeed, if
\varphi
touches the graph
of
d
from below at some point
(\bar{x},\bar{t})\in\{d>0\}
,
we have
\tilde{\varphi}:=\gamma(\varphi/\sigma)
touches
the graph of
u
from below at the same point and

\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{x},\bar{t})\geq\Delta\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{x},\bar{t}).

In addition,
|\nabla_{x}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{t})|=1
.
Hence, as before, at
(\bar{x},\bar{t})
it holds

\partial_{t}\varphi\geq\Delta\varphi+\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\varphi/\sigma)}{\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi/\sigma)}

Note that
\gamma^{\prime\prime}/\gamma^{\prime}=W^{\prime}(\gamma)/\sqrt{2W(\gamma)}=(\sqrt{2W})^{\prime}(\gamma)
, that is, with our choice,

-2\gamma
, hence

\partial_{t}\varphi\geq\Delta\varphi-\tfrac{2}{\sigma}\tilde{\varphi}.

We recall that, still with our choice,
\gamma(s)=\tanh(s)
, so that the last term
is
\frac{2}{\sigma}\tanh(\tfrac{1}{\sigma}d(\bar{x},\bar{t}))

and thus
d
is a supersolution of

\partial_{t}d\geq\Delta d-\sqrt{2}\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{ in }\{d>0\}.

Yet this implies that
d
is also a supersolution of the
heat flow in
\{d>0\}
.
Indeed, for each
s>0
, the level set
\{d=s\}
evolves
faster than the mean curvature flow plus the constant speed
-\sqrt{2}\,s/\sigma^{2}
, which implies that

\partial_{t}d\geq\Delta d-\sqrt{2}\,s/\sigma^{2}

in
\{d>s\}
, for any
s>0
(in the viscosity sense). The claim follows.
∎

Starting from the algorithm (
5.1
), the idea is then to define

v^{k,\varepsilon}:=\gamma(u^{k,\varepsilon})

and characterize the evolution of the function
v^{k,\varepsilon}
. Indeed, for every
j
in the region where
\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}>0
it holds

\frac{v^{k+1,\varepsilon}_{j}-v^{k,\varepsilon}_{j}}{h}\geq\frac{\gamma\Big(\gamma^{-1}(K^{\varepsilon}\ast v^{k,\varepsilon})_{j}\Big)-v^{k,\varepsilon}_{j}}{h}=\left(\frac{K^{\varepsilon}-\delta}{h}*v^{k,\varepsilon}\right)_{j},

where
\delta=(\delta_{j})_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}
is defined by
\delta_{0}=1
,

\delta_{j}=0
for
j\neq 0
. We write this inequality as

\frac{v^{\varepsilon}(t+h)-v^{\varepsilon}(t)}{h}\geq\frac{K^{\varepsilon}-\delta}{h}*v^{\varepsilon}(t)

for any
t\geq 0
where
v^{\varepsilon}(t):=v^{[t/h],\varepsilon}
.
Thanks to the estimate in Lemma
5.1
, reasoning as in Section
4.4
we obtain the same compactness
as in
[
10
, Prop. 4.4]
for the function
d^{\varepsilon}:=u^{[t/h],\varepsilon}
. Therefore, up to a subsequence,
d^{\varepsilon}
converges
locally uniformly in space and for all times but a countable number (and until an extinction time
T^{*}\in(0,+\infty]
), to

d(x,t)=\dist(x,E(t))-\dist(x,{A}^{\complement}(t))
, where
E
is the Kuratowski limit of
\{(j,t):d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{j}\leq 0\}

and
{A}\subset E
the complement of the Kuratowski limit of
\{(j,t):d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{j}\geq 0\}
. Moreover, up to a subsequence, by definition the function
v^{\varepsilon}
converges locally uniformly in space and for all times but a countable number to
\gamma(d)
, with
d
as above.

From this, we can follow the proof of Section
4.4
to conclude that

\partial_{t}\gamma(d)\geq\Delta\gamma(d)

in
E^{\complement}
, in the distributional sense. Using Lemma
5.3
we then conclude that
\gamma(d)
is a supersolution of the Allen-Cahn equation, and thus
d
is a supersolution of the heat equation. This means that
E
is a superflow. We have shown the following result.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
E^{0}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and consider the algorithm (
5.1
). As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the function
d^{\varepsilon}
, defined for
t\geq 0
and
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}

by
d^{\varepsilon}(t)_{i}=u^{[t/h],\varepsilon}_{i}
, converge up to subsequences,
for almost all times and locally uniformly in space to a function

d(x,t)
such that
d^{+}=\max\{d,0\}
is the distance function
to a supersolution of the (generalized)
mean curvature flow starting from
E^{0}
,
and
d^{-}
is the distance function to a supersolution starting from

{(E^{0})}^{\complement}
.

6.
Applications: discrete heat flow, implicit Laplacian

In this section we list two examples of numerical schemes whose associated resolvent operator is a kernel satisfying the assumptions listed in Section
4.2
. Note that the kernel associated to the explicit Euler scheme has been investigated in Section
2.2
.

Implicit Laplacian scheme

We start by considering the fully discrete implicit Euler’s scheme that reads as follows. The idea is to use as kernel
K^{\varepsilon}

associated to the resolvent operator for

(6.1)

u-\tau\Delta_{\varepsilon}u=g\quad\text{ in }\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}.

Note that if
g\in\ell^{2}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N})
, there exists a unique solution
u\in\ell^{2}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N})
of the equation above, and a comparison principle holds.
Rewriting (
6.1
) in the Fourier variable we deduce that
K^{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{1-\tau\widetilde{\Delta_{\varepsilon}}},

where, with a slight abuse of notation,
\widetilde{\Delta_{\varepsilon}}
denotes the Fourier transform of the kernel associated to
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
.
Recalling that

(6.2)

\widetilde{\Delta_{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\cos(2\pi\varepsilon\xi\cdot e_{k})-1),

see Remark
4.3
, we obtain

\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\cos(2\pi\varepsilon\xi\cdot e_{k})-1)}.

Note that the regularity of the Fourier transform of the kernel implies that
K^{\varepsilon}
is rapidly decaying, so that
K^{\varepsilon}\ast d
is well-defined when
d\in Lip(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}).

Therefore, we can define our algorithm as

\begin{split}&u^{k+1,\varepsilon}=K^{\varepsilon}\ast d^{k,\varepsilon}\\
&\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}[u^{k+1,\varepsilon}],\end{split}

where the first equation above implies that

u^{k+1,\varepsilon}-\tau\Delta_{\varepsilon}u^{k+1,\varepsilon}=d^{k,\varepsilon}\quad\text{ in }\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}.

Positivity of
K^{\varepsilon}
follows from the comparison principle holding for (
6.1
), while symmetry follows from the symmetry of
\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}
. Moreover, since
\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(0)=1,
it holds
\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}K^{\varepsilon}_{j}=1
. It remains to check that the time-step
\tau(\varepsilon)
coincides with the natural time-step
h(\varepsilon)
defined in (
4.4
).
In order to compute
h
, we recall (
4.4
’) i.e.
h=-\frac{\Delta\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(0)}{8\pi^{2}N}
. By a straightforward computation we check that

\Delta\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(0)=-8N\pi^{2}\tau,

hence
h=\tau
. In particular, (
4.4
) is satisfied. Lastly, using the explicit expression of
\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}
, one can check that (
4.5
) holds.

We conclude by noting that, while the hypothesis on the kernel
K^{\varepsilon}
are satisfied for any
\tau(\varepsilon)=h(\varepsilon)
going to 0 as
\varepsilon\to 0
, Corollary
3.3
requires
h(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}.
In conclusion, if
h(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}
, the theory developed in the previous section applies and Theorem
4.1
holds.

Discrete heat flow scheme

Another interesting example is the discrete heat flow. We define
S_{\varepsilon}(t)
the semigroup generated by the discrete Laplacian, that is
S_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)u=v
with
v
solving

(6.3)

\begin{cases}v_{t}-\Delta_{\varepsilon}v=0\quad\text{ in }\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\times[0,+\infty)\\
v(0)=u,\end{cases}

for
u\in\ell^{2}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N})
.
We consider a parameter
\tau=\tau(\varepsilon)
with
\tau\to 0
, and define the kernel
K^{\varepsilon}
as satisfying
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)u=K^{\varepsilon}\ast u
for every
u\in\ell^{2}(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}).

To find a solution to the equation (
6.3
), we pass to the Fourier variable in space

\dot{\widetilde{v}}(\xi,t)-\widetilde{\Delta_{\varepsilon}}(\xi)\widetilde{v}(\xi,t)=0,\quad\text{ in }[0,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon})^{N}\times[0,+\infty),

where
\widetilde{\Delta_{\varepsilon}}
is defined in (
6.2
).
Therefore, we find
\widetilde{S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)u}(\xi)=\widetilde{v}(\xi,\tau)=\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}\widetilde{u}(\xi)
where

\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\exp\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\cos(2\pi\varepsilon\xi\cdot e_{k})-1)\right).

Then, positivity of
K^{\varepsilon}
follows from the comparison principle, symmetry and condition (
4.3
) (equivalent to
\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(0)=1
) are easily checked, while an explicit computation of
\Delta\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}(0)
, arguing as for the previous example, yields
h=\tau
, and thus also (
4.4
) is satisfied. Similarly, condition (
4.5
) is a simple computation using that
h=\tau
.

Again, note that the regularity of
\widetilde{K^{\varepsilon}}
implies that
K^{\varepsilon}
is rapidly decaying, so that
K^{\varepsilon}\ast d
is well-defined when
d\in Lip(\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}).
Therefore, we can define our algorithm

\begin{split}&u^{k+1,\varepsilon}=K^{\varepsilon}\ast d^{k,\varepsilon}\\
&\,\textnormal{sd}^{k+1,\varepsilon}=\,\textnormal{sd}[u^{k+1,\varepsilon}].\end{split}

As before, if
h(\varepsilon)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}
, the theory developed in the previous section applies and Theorem
4.1
holds.

8.1.
Restricting the computations on a strip

In this subsection we consider the following modification of the redistancing operations
\,\textnormal{d}^{\pm},\,\textnormal{sd}^{\pm}
defined in (
2.1
), (
2.2
). Consider a (fixed) positive parameter
M>0
, and assume that
M>\varepsilon
. Given
E\subseteq\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, we define
S_{M}E:=\{i\in E:\exists k\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}\setminus E,|i-k|\leq M\}.

For
u\in X_{\varepsilon}
be 1-Lipschitz and
i\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
we set:

(8.1)

\begin{cases}\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]_{i}:=\inf_{j\in S_{M}\{u<0\}}u_{j}+|j-i|\,,\\
\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]_{i}:=\sup_{j\in S_{M}\{u\geq 0\}}\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}_{j}-|j-i|\end{cases}

and analogously
\,\textnormal{d}^{M,-},\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,-}.
Clearly, the two functions defined above are both 1-Lipschitz. By the same arguments used in Lemma
2.1
, one can show that
\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]=u
in
S_{M}\{u<0\}
and
\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]\geq u
in
\{u\geq 0\}
, thus
\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]=\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}\geq u
in
S_{M}\{u\geq 0\}
and
\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]\leq\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]\leq u
in
S_{M}\{u<0\}
. In particular,

(8.2)

\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]\leq u\text{ in }S_{M}\{u<0\},\quad\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]\geq u\text{ in }S_{M}\{u\geq 0\},

showing a local comparison between
\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]
and
u
in the “strip”
S_{M}\{u<0\}\cup S_{M}\{u\geq 0\}
, instead of the global one satisfied by
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u].
Furthermore, we can show that this redistancing operation relates well with
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u]
, at least in the strip.

Lemma 8.1
.

Given
u\in X_{\varepsilon}
1-Lipschitz, for every
i\in S_{M}\{u<0\}\cup S_{M}\{u\geq 0\}
it holds

(8.3)

\displaystyle\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]_{i}\leq\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u]_{i}+\frac{4N}{M}\varepsilon^{2},

(8.4)

\displaystyle|\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]_{i}-d_{i}|\leq\sqrt{N}\varepsilon,

where
d=\min_{j\in\{u\geq 0\}}|i-j|-\min_{j\in\{u<0\}}|i-j|.

Proof.

Let
i\in S_{M}\{u\geq 0\}
, then
\,\textnormal{sd}^{M,+}[u]_{i}=\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]_{i}
and
\,\textnormal{sd}^{+}[u]_{i}=\,\textnormal{d}^{+}[u]_{i}
. Moreover, by definition it holds
\,\textnormal{d}^{+}[u]\leq\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]
on
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. We thus estimate, using that
u_{k}-u_{j}\leq|k-j|
,

\,\textnormal{d}^{M,+}[u]_{i}-\,\textnormal{d}^{+}[u]_{i}\leq\sup_{j\in\{u<0\}}\inf_{k\in S_{M}\{u<0\}}|k-j|+|k-i|-|j-i|\\
\leq\sup_{j\in\{u<0\}\setminus S_{M}\{u<0\}}\inf_{k\in S_{3M/4}\{u<0\}\setminus S_{M/4}\{u<0\}}|k-j|+|k-i|-|j-i|\leq\frac{4N}{M}\varepsilon^{2}

where we used

\sup_{j\in S_{M}\{u<0\}}\inf_{k\in S_{M}\{u<0\}}|k-j|+|k-i|-|j-i|=0,

and for the last inequality we reasoned as for the last inf in equation (
3.4
) (see the proof of (
3.5
)). On the other hand, for
i\in S_{M}\{u<0\}
by definition of

8.2.
Relating our redistancing operator
sd
with fast solvers for distance functions

The main drawback of our proposed scheme is the computation cost of the redistancing operator
sd
, although the previous Section
8.1
showed how to reduce computations in practice while maintaining consistency.
Ideally, one would use well-known fast numerical solvers for computing the distance function to a given set in our setting. A natural candidate is the class of fast-marching techniques
[
26
,
28
,
25
,
12
,
27
]
.
However, a major challenge lies in the lack of precise error estimates and regularity properties for the distance function computed with these methods, which are crucial in our framework. In particular, it is unclear whether the key inequality in item (2) of Lemma
2.1
holds (up to controlled errors) when using such schemes.

On the one hand, the error can generally be estimated as
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, as shown in
[
24
, Sec. 9]
. On the other hand, a precise convergence rate for the scheme is only required in Lemma
3.3
, where we compare the redistancing operator applied to a distance function that is smooth outside a single point (the signed distance to a ball). In this specific context, higher-order implementations of the fast-marching scheme
[
29
,
1
]
could provide interesting insights.

Appendix A
An estimate on the decay of the Fourier
transform of discretized rapidly decaying functions

Let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
we let
\eta^{\varepsilon}_{j}=\eta(j)
for
j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
(that is,
\eta^{\varepsilon}=\eta_{|\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}
), and
we consider the
(1/\varepsilon)
-periodic Fourier transform

\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{j\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\eta(j)e^{-2\pi ij\cdot\xi}.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
p>N
, there exists
C
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
:

(A.1)

\sup_{\xi\in[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}}|\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}-\widehat{\eta}|\leq C\varepsilon^{p}

and

(A.2)

|\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)|\leq\frac{C}{1+|\xi|^{p}}

for any
\xi\in[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}
.

Proof.

By Poisson’s summation formula,
\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}
is the
(1/\varepsilon)
-periodic version of
\widehat{\eta}
,
that is:

\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\widehat{\eta}(\xi+j).

Since
\widehat{\eta}
is in the Schwartz class,
for
p\geq 1
, there exists
C
such that for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\widehat{\eta}(\xi)\leq\frac{C}{1+|\xi|^{p}}.

Consider
\xi\in[-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}]^{N}
, that is,
|\xi|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}
.
We write:

\widehat{\eta^{\varepsilon}}(\xi)=\widehat{\eta}(\xi)+\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\widehat{\eta}(\xi+j)

and

e:=\left|\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\widehat{\eta}(\xi+j)\right|\leq\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\frac{C}{1+|\xi+j|^{p}}\leq\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\frac{C}{|\xi+j|_{\infty}^{p}}

possibly increasing the constant
C
in the last term. We have

|\xi+j|_{\infty}^{p}\geq 2^{1-p}|j|_{\infty}^{p}-|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}.

For
|j|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
,
|\xi|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{2}|j|_{\infty}
,
hence

2^{1-p}|j|_{\infty}^{p}-|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}\geq 2^{-p}|j|_{\infty}^{p}\geq\frac{1}{2}(2^{-p}|j|_{\infty}^{p}+|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}).

Hence, assuming
p>N
,

e\leq\sum_{j\in\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\frac{C}{2^{-p}|j|_{\infty}^{p}+|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{N},j\neq 0}\frac{C2^{p}\varepsilon^{p}}{|j|_{\infty}^{p}+2^{p}\varepsilon^{p}|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}}\leq C\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{2^{p}\varepsilon^{p}j^{N-1}}{j^{p}+2^{p}\varepsilon^{p}|\xi|_{\infty}^{p}}\leq C\varepsilon^{p}

(increasing again
C
), which shows (
A.1
).
In addition,
one can estimate the sum as follows: if
p>N
, for any
a\in(0,1]
,

\sum_{j\geq J_{p}}\frac{j^{N-1}}{a^{p}+j^{p}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{N-1}}{a^{p}+t^{p}}dt

for some
J_{p}\geq 1
(which is such that
t\mapsto\frac{t^{N-1}}{a^{p}+t^{p}}
is
decreasing for
t\geq J_{p}
, hence one may use
J_{p}=(N-1)^{1/p}
).Then,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{N-1}}{a^{p}+t^{p}}dt=\frac{1}{a^{p}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{N-1}}{1+(t/a)^{p}}dt=a^{N-p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{s^{N-1}}{1+s^{p}}ds.

It follows

e\leq\frac{C}{|\xi|^{p}}+C2^{p}\varepsilon^{p}(2\varepsilon|\xi|_{\infty})^{N-p}=\frac{C}{|\xi|^{p}}+\varepsilon^{N}\frac{C}{|\xi|^{p-N}}.

We deduce (
A.2
) (since
p
is arbitrary).
∎
