A lesion-responsive ZIF-8/zinc–myricetin nano-biointerface remodels mitochondrial–immune crosstalk for spinal cord repair
Authors: Yuqi Zhao, Zhihao Xia, Tao Xie, Renfeng Liu, Shenghang Liu, Rongjin Luo, Shenglong Wang, Dageng Huang, Liang Yan, Huimin Hu, Cheng Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) creates a hostile lesion microenvironment characterized by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory metabolic remodeling, and pyroptotic cell death, which collectively restrict neural repair. Here, we engineered a lesion-responsive ZIF-8/zinc–myricetin nano-biointerface, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, to coordinate redox regulation, mitochondrial protection, and immune microenvironment remodeling after SCI. Astaxanthin was encapsulated within a ZIF-8 core to improve cargo protection and enable pH/ROS-responsive release, while a zinc–myricetin coordination shell was constructed as a redox-active interfacial layer. The resulting nanoplatform exhibited a well-dispersed core–shell structure, responsive release behavior, broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging activity, and favorable cytocompatibility. In activated microglia, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR reduced intracellular and mitochondrial oxidative stress, restored mitochondrial bioenergetics, alleviated glycolysis-biased immunometabolic remodeling, and attenuated inflammatory activation together with NLRP3/GSDMD-associated molecular signaling linked to pyroptosis. In oxidatively injured neuronal cells, the nano-biointerface preserved mitochondrial membrane potential, improved respiratory function, rebalanced mitochondrial dynamics, and supported neuronal survival and neurite outgrowth. In a rat SCI model, local administration attenuated acute oxidative-inflammatory injury, improved lesion microenvironment remodeling, preserved axon–myelin structures, and was associated with improved electrophysiological and locomotor outcomes. These findings identify AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as a responsive bioactive nanomaterial that bridges interfacial redox chemistry and mitochondrial–immune regulation for spinal cord repair.Keywords: Nano-biointerface, Nanomedicine, Spinal cord injury, Mitochondrial bioenergetics, Immunometabolism, NLRP3 inflammasome

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating disorder of the central nervous system that frequently results in extensive neuronal loss, axonal disruption, and persistent sensory and motor deficits [1,2]. The limited repair capacity after SCI is not determined solely by the initial mechanical insult; it is further shaped by a secondary injury cascade that rapidly develops within the lesion site [[1], [2], [3]]. After injury, excessive oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory activation, metabolic failure, and regulated cell death interact with one another and progressively reshape the local microenvironment [1,3,4]. This evolving pathological niche restricts neuronal survival, axonal regeneration, remyelination, and ultimately functional recovery. Therefore, an effective therapeutic strategy for SCI should not only target an isolated pathological event, but also remodel the hostile lesion microenvironment in a coordinated manner.

Among the interconnected events involved in secondary injury, mitochondrial dysfunction represents a central pathological node. Following SCI, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) disrupt mitochondrial ultrastructure, impair electron transport, reduce adenosine triphosphate production, and collapse mitochondrial membrane potential [1,5,6]. Disturbed mitochondrial fission and fusion further fragment the mitochondrial network and aggravate bioenergetic failure, forming a self-amplifying cycle of redox imbalance and metabolic deterioration [5,6]. Importantly, mitochondrial damage is not merely a downstream consequence of injury. Mitochondria-derived ROS can activate the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 inflammasome, promote gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis, and enhance the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1β [7]. In parallel, mitochondrial respiratory impairment is closely linked to inflammatory metabolic remodeling in microglia/macrophages, which commonly shifts these cells toward a glycolysis-dominant and pro-inflammatory state [8,9]. These findings indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction lies at the intersection of oxidative stress, inflammatory amplification, immunometabolic imbalance, and pyroptotic cell death after SCI.

This pathological complexity partly explains why many current interventions have shown limited therapeutic efficacy. Conventional antioxidants, anti-inflammatory agents, and nanocarrier-based delivery systems have produced encouraging outcomes in preclinical SCI models, but most approaches act on only one segment of the secondary injury cascade [10]. Such single-target strategies are often insufficient to achieve durable control over the lesion microenvironment, particularly when therapeutic molecules suffer from poor aqueous stability, limited bioavailability, rapid clearance, or inadequate responsiveness to pathological cues [11]. Recent biomaterial studies further emphasize the importance of reconstructing the lesion microenvironment rather than targeting a single pathological component. Spatiotemporally programmed delivery systems have been developed to coordinate antioxidant protection with neural regenerative cues across the evolving post-SCI microenvironment, while nanofibrous hybrid hydrogels can provide permissive substrates for axonal regeneration, inflammatory modulation, and remyelination [12,13]. A more effective material-based strategy should integrate microenvironment-responsive delivery with intrinsic bioactivity, thereby allowing one platform to regulate multiple linked pathological processes, including redox imbalance, mitochondrial failure, inflammatory metabolic remodeling, and pyroptotic signaling [3,10,11].

Astaxanthin (AST) and myricetin (MYR) are attractive candidates for such a combined strategy. AST is a natural carotenoid with potent antioxidant and mitochondrial protective activities. It can attenuate lipid peroxidation, preserve mitochondrial membrane integrity, and reduce oxidative stress-associated cellular injury [[14], [15], [16]]. MYR, a natural flavonoid, has been reported to regulate mitochondrial homeostasis, suppress inflammasome activation, and attenuate inflammatory signaling. These complementary bioactivities suggest that their coordinated delivery may offer broader protection against secondary injury than either molecule alone. However, both compounds are limited by poor stability, low aqueous solubility, and inefficient retention in vivo, which restrict their therapeutic performance in the injured spinal cord [[17], [18], [19]]. Therefore, a rational delivery platform is needed to improve their availability while enabling activation within the pathological microenvironment.

Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) is a pH-responsive metal–organic framework that has been widely used for drug encapsulation because of its high loading capacity, structural tunability, and acid-sensitive degradation [20]. In the context of SCI, this feature is particularly useful because the injured tissue exhibits pathological acidosis and oxidative stress [1,3,21]. Meanwhile, metal–phenolic coordination networks provide a versatile strategy for constructing bioactive interfacial coatings [22,23]. Polyphenol-based shells can improve colloidal stability, participate in redox regulation, and interact dynamically with pathological stimuli [24]. In particular, a zinc–myricetin coordination shell may not only serve as a protective outer layer, but also provide a redox-active interface that contributes to ROS scavenging and inflammatory regulation [23].

We therefore designed AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as a lesion-responsive redox-active nano-biointerface that integrates pathological cue-responsive cargo delivery with interfacial redox regulation. In this architecture, astaxanthin serves as the protected therapeutic cargo, while the ZIF-8 core provides cargo protection and acidic/oxidative microenvironment-responsive release, thereby supporting antioxidant and mitochondrial protection. In parallel, the zinc–myricetin coordination shell reconstructs the particle surface as a redox-active interfacial layer that modifies surface physicochemical properties and contributes to ROS regulation and material–cell interactions. Rather than simply co-localizing two antioxidant components, this core–shell design was intended to combine responsive astaxanthin delivery with additional interfacial redox activity, thereby enabling coordinated regulation of oxidative stress, mitochondrial homeostasis, immunometabolic remodeling, and inflammasome- and pyroptosis-associated molecular signaling. We subsequently investigated whether this integrated nano-biointerface could remodel the mitochondrial–immune microenvironment after SCI and thereby support neuronal preservation, axon–myelin repair, and functional recovery (Scheme 1).

Fabrication of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR and its bioactive regulation of the injured spinal cord microenvironment.

Astaxanthin was encapsulated within a ZIF-8 core, followed by surface coordination of myricetin with zinc ions to form a dynamic metal–phenolic shell, yielding the lesion-responsive redox-active nano-biointerface AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. The ZIF-8 core enables pathological microenvironment-responsive astaxanthin release, whereas the zinc–myricetin shell provides a redox-active biointerface for reactive oxygen species scavenging. After local administration into the injured spinal cord, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR responds to the acidic and oxidative lesion microenvironment and acts as a bioactive regulator. In activated microglia/macrophages, it reduces intracellular and mitochondrial oxidative stress, restores mitochondrial bioenergetics, alleviates glycolysis-biased immunometabolic remodeling, and attenuates NLRP3/GSDMD-associated inflammatory signaling linked to pyroptosis. In injured neurons, the nanoplatform preserves mitochondrial homeostasis, rebalances mitochondrial dynamics, and promotes neuronal survival and neurite regeneration. By coupling mitochondrial restoration with immunometabolic reprogramming, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR remodels the hostile lesion microenvironment and promotes axonal repair, remyelination, and functional recovery after SCI.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of ZIF-8

ZIF-8 nanoparticles were prepared by solution precipitation. Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (100 mg, 0.336 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (10 mL) to form solution A. In parallel, 2-methylimidazole (55.2 mg, 0.672 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (20 mL) under magnetic stirring to form solution B. Solution A was added dropwise to solution B under vigorous stirring at room temperature (25 °C), and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 h. The resulting white precipitate was collected by centrifugation (14 000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with methanol, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight.

Synthesis of AST@ZIF-8

AST-loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles were synthesized using the same Zn2+/2-MIM stoichiometric feed ratio. Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (100 mg, 0.336 mmol) and 2-MIM (55.2 mg, 0.672 mmol) were separately dissolved in methanol. AST (5 mg) was introduced during the ZIF-8 assembly process, and the reaction mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (14 000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with methanol, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight.

Synthesis of AST@ZIF-8@Zn–MYR

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was obtained by coating AST@ZIF-8 nanoparticles with a Zn2+-myricetin (MYR) coordination layer. Briefly, AST@ZIF-8 (20 mg) was dispersed in methanol (10 mL) by ultrasonication. Zinc acetate (5 mg) and MYR (5 mg) were separately dissolved in methanol (5 mL each) and added sequentially to the AST@ZIF-8 suspension under magnetic stirring at room temperature, followed by one drop of aqueous ammonia. After stirring for 1 h, the product was collected by centrifugation (14 000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with methanol, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight.

Transmission electron microscopy and elemental mapping

Nanoparticle morphology and microstructure were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA). Samples were dispersed in methanol by ultrasonication, dropped onto carbon-coated copper grids, and air-dried at room temperature before imaging. TEM images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. Elemental mapping was performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) attached to the TEM system to determine the distribution of C, O, N, and Zn in AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR.

Dynamic light scattering

Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Samples were dispersed in deionized water at 0.1 mg mL−1 and sonicated for 5 min before measurement. Analyses were performed at 25 °C with a scattering angle of 173°. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

Zeta potential measurement

Zeta potential was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Nanoparticles were dispersed in deionized water at 0.1 mg mL−1, sonicated for 5 min, and analyzed at 25 °C using folded capillary cells. Three independent measurements were performed for each sample.

X-ray diffraction

Crystal structure was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D/MAX 2550, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Powder samples were scanned over a 2θ range of 5–40° at a rate of 5° min−1.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Samples were mixed with KBr, pressed into pellets, and scanned from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 scans per sample.

UV–vis absorption spectroscopy

UV–vis spectra were recorded using a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Samples were dispersed or dissolved in appropriate solvents and scanned over 350–600 nm. For concentration-dependent measurements, AST solutions at different concentrations were analyzed under the same conditions.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

Surface elemental composition and chemical states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Nexsa G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). Samples were mounted on conductive substrates, and both survey and high-resolution spectra were collected. Binding energies were calibrated against the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV.

Colloidal and interfacial physicochemical stability

The colloidal and interfacial physicochemical stability of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was evaluated by monitoring changes in hydrodynamic diameter and ζ-potential over 7 days. Nanoparticles were dispersed at 0.1 mg mL−1 in deionized water, 10% FBS, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and maintained under the same incubation conditions throughout the observation period. Hydrodynamic diameter and ζ-potential were measured at days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 using a dynamic light scattering and electrophoretic light-scattering analyzer. Prior to each measurement, the nanoparticle suspensions were gently mixed to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Measurements were performed at 25 °C, and three independent measurements were obtained for each condition and time point. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Drug loading capacity

The loading content of AST in the nanoparticles was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Briefly, an accurately weighed amount of AST@ZIF-8 or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was completely disrupted in an appropriate organic solvent to release the encapsulated AST. The suspension was vortexed and sonicated under light-protected conditions to ensure complete extraction of AST, followed by centrifugation to remove insoluble residues. The resulting supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to HPLC analysis.

AST was quantified using a reversed-phase HPLC system equipped with a UV/photodiode-array detector. Chromatographic separation was performed on a C18 reversed-phase column under the validated analytical conditions for AST. AST was identified by comparison of its retention time and characteristic absorption profile with those of an authentic AST standard. Quantification was performed using an external calibration curve prepared from serial concentrations of AST standards.

In vitro drug release

The release profile of AST was evaluated under different conditions, including physiological pH (7.4), acidic pH (5.0), and oxidative conditions in the presence of H2O2. Nanoparticles (2 mg) were dispersed in release medium (10 mL) and incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. At predetermined time points, samples were centrifuged (14 000 rpm, 10 min), and the supernatants were collected for analysis. An equal volume of fresh medium was added after each sampling to maintain sink conditions. Released AST was quantified by UV–vis spectroscopy at 480–500 nm, and cumulative release percentages were calculated accordingly.

Electron paramagnetic resonance analysis

The radical-scavenging activity of AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was evaluated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy. Superoxide radicals (O2•-) were generated using a xanthine/xanthine oxidase system containing 10 mM xanthine and 1 U mL−1 xanthine oxidase. Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were generated by a Fenton reaction system composed of 1 mM Fe2+ and 4 mM H2O2 in 25 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). In both systems, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO, 100 mM) was used as the spin-trapping agent. AST@ZIF-8 or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was added at a final concentration of 320 μg/mL. After incubation, reaction mixtures were transferred into capillary tubes and analyzed by EPR. Radical-scavenging activity was quantified from the reduction in EPR signal intensity relative to the corresponding control.

Reactive oxygen species scavenging assays

The ROS-scavenging activity of AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was evaluated against multiple oxidants, including superoxide anion (O2•-), hydroxyl radical (•OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), DPPH, and ABTS•+.

For O2•- scavenging, radicals were generated using a xanthine/xanthine oxidase system, and scavenging activity was determined from the decrease in radical-associated signal intensity relative to the untreated control. For •OH scavenging, radicals were generated by a Fenton reaction, and scavenging efficiency was evaluated from the attenuation of radical signals after nanoparticle treatment. For H2O2 scavenging, nanoparticle dispersions were incubated with a defined concentration of H2O2, and residual H2O2 was quantified using a commercial colorimetric assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Free-radical scavenging was further assessed using DPPH and ABTS assays. For the DPPH assay, nanoparticle dispersions at different concentrations were mixed with 0.1 mM DPPH ethanol solution and incubated in the dark for 30 min, followed by absorbance measurement at 517 nm. For the ABTS assay, ABTS radical cations were generated by reacting ABTS with potassium persulfate, and the resulting solution was diluted to an absorbance of approximately 0.7 at 734 nm before use. Nanoparticle samples were then added and incubated for 10 min, after which absorbance at 734 nm was recorded.

For all assays, scavenging efficiencies were calculated from the decrease in absorbance or signal intensity relative to the corresponding controls. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell culture

Rat pheochromocytoma PC12 cells were obtained from the Stem Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100×, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Mouse BV2 microglial cells were obtained from the same source and maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under the same conditions.

Primary hippocampal neurons were isolated from newborn Sprague–Dawley rat pups (postnatal day 0–3) according to a previously reported protocol. Briefly, hippocampal tissues were dissected under sterile conditions and digested with 0.125% trypsin (25200-072, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 25 min. Digestion was terminated with DMEM/F12 medium (11320033, Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS, followed by gentle trituration to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were seeded onto poly-D-lysine-coated glass-bottom confocal dishes (801001, NEST, China) at approximately 1 × 104 cells cm−2. After 4 h, the medium was replaced with Neurobasal-A medium (10888-022, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 2% B27 (16954044, Gibco, USA), and half of the medium was refreshed every 3 days. Three independent neuronal preparations were used for the primary-neuron experiments, with each independent isolation considered one biological replicate. Neuronal purity was evaluated by Tuj1 immunofluorescence staining and calculated as the percentage of Tuj1-positive cells among total DAPI-positive cells, yielding a purity of 92.4 ± 5.5%.

Cell viability assay

The cytocompatibility of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; C0037, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) assay. PC12 cells, BV2 cells, and primary hippocampal neurons were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to attach for 12 h. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR at various concentrations. After 12 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for an additional 30 min at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Live/dead assay

PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured overnight. PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons were exposed to 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h. The H2O2-containing medium was then removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS and treated with Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12 h. Cell viability was subsequently evaluated by Calcein-AM/propidium iodide staining. After gentle washing with PBS, fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 Thunder wide-field fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The percentages of live and dead cells were quantified using ImageJ. The experiment was repeated independently three times.

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular oxidative stress was assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; S0033S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and dihydroethidium (DHE; S0063, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). PC12 and BV2 cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes and cultured overnight. For the oxidative-injury model, PC12 cells were exposed to H2O2 at a final concentration of 100 μM for 6 h. The H2O2-containing medium was then removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS before incubation with fresh medium containing Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12 h. For the inflammatory activation model, BV2 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng mL−1) for 12 h and subsequently treated with the indicated formulations for an additional 12 h. At the end of treatment, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA or DHE working solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After three washes with PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 Thunder fluorescence microscope under identical exposure and acquisition settings for all groups. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using ImageJ. The experiment was independently repeated three times, and the mean fluorescence intensity obtained from the analyzed fields in each independent experiment was used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

Mitochondrial ROS detection

Mitochondrial superoxide production in PC12 and BV2 cells was measured using MitoSOX Red (S0061M, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). PC12 cells were exposed to H2O2 at a final concentration of 100 μM for 6 h. The H2O2-containing medium was then removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS before incubation with fresh medium containing Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12 h. BV2 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng mL−1) for 12 h and subsequently treated with the indicated formulations for an additional 12 h.

At the end of treatment, cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red working solution at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. After three washes with PBS, fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 Thunder fluorescence microscope under identical exposure and acquisition settings for all groups. MitoSOX fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

The experiment was independently repeated three times, and the mean fluorescence intensity obtained from the analyzed fields in each independent experiment was used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

Mitochondrial membrane potential analysis

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in PC12 and BV2 cells was evaluated using an enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (C2003S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. PC12 cells were exposed to 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h. The H2O2-containing medium was subsequently removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS before treatment with Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12 h. BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) for 12 h and subsequently treated with the indicated formulations for an additional 12 h.

At the end of treatment, cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After washing three times with JC-1 staining buffer, fluorescence signals corresponding to JC-1 aggregates (red) and JC-1 monomers (green) were acquired using a Leica DMi8 Thunder fluorescence microscope under identical acquisition settings for all groups. The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was calculated using ImageJ. A decreased red-to-green fluorescence ratio was interpreted as mitochondrial membrane depolarization, whereas an increased ratio indicated preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential. The experiment was independently repeated three times, and the mean value obtained from the analyzed fields in each independent experiment was used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

Seahorse XF Mito Stress Test and glycolysis stress test

Cellular bioenergetics in BV2 and PC12 cells was analyzed using a Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sensor cartridges were hydrated overnight in Seahorse XF Calibrant at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. Assay templates were generated using Wave software. PC12 cells were exposed to 100 μM H2O2 for 6 h, after which the H2O2-containing medium was removed and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS. The cells were then treated with Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12 h. BV2 cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) for 12 h and subsequently treated with the indicated formulations for an additional 12 h. At the end of treatment, the culture medium was replaced with the corresponding Seahorse assay medium, and the cells were equilibrated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 45–60 min before measurement. For the Mito Stress Test, cells were incubated in Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, with the pH adjusted to 7.4. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was recorded under basal conditions and after sequential injections of oligomycin (2 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (1 μM each). Basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, proton leak, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial respiration were calculated using the Seahorse XF Report Generator. For the Glycolysis Stress Test, cells were equilibrated in glucose- and pyruvate-free Seahorse XF base medium supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, with the pH adjusted to 7.4. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was recorded after sequential injections of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (2 μM), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 50 mM). Non-glycolytic acidification, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were calculated according to the manufacturer's instructions. At the end of each Seahorse run, total protein content in each well was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to total protein content and expressed as pmol min−1 mg protein−1 and mpH min−1 mg protein−1, respectively. Seahorse measurements were performed in three independent biological experiments. Wells displaying abnormal baseline signals, injection failure, or values outside the predefined quality-control range were excluded before analysis. The remaining technical wells within each independent experiment were averaged to generate one biological replicate value. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Wave software version 2.6 and the Seahorse XF Report Generator.

ATP assay

Because Seahorse analysis reflects ATP-linked respiratory flux rather than intracellular ATP content directly, cellular ATP levels were further quantified using a luciferase-based luminescence assay (CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Assay, Promega, USA). Following the same treatment paradigm used for Seahorse experiments, cells were incubated with the reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. Luminescence was recorded after 10 min using a SpectraMax Paradigm microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Where indicated, ATP values were normalized to total protein content and expressed as nmol mg protein−1 or as relative values normalized to the sham group.

Cellular uptake analysis

To assess cellular uptake, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR nanoparticles were labeled with Cy5. Cy5 (MCE) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 1 mg mL−1 stock solution and mixed with AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR at a mass ratio of 1:200. The mixture was incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Excess free dye was removed by three rounds of centrifugation at 12 000 × g with PBS washing to obtain Cy5-labeled AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR.

For membrane staining, PC12 cells were pre-incubated with DiI (2–5 μg/mL) for 5–10 min at 37 °C, washed with PBS, and allowed to recover in serum-containing medium for 10 min. Cells were then incubated with Cy5-labeled AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR for 12, 24, or 36 h. At each time point, cells were washed with PBS to remove extracellular nanoparticles, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10–15 min at room temperature, and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 Thunder wide-field microscope. Excitation/emission wavelengths were set at 650/670 nm for Cy5 and 555/590 nm for DiI. Mean Cy5 fluorescence intensity and its colocalization with DiI were quantified using ImageJ. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

In vitro analysis of microglial polarization

BV2 cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry to evaluate the effects of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR on microglial polarization. Cells were seeded overnight and stimulated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) to induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype or with interleukin-4 (IL-4, 20 ng mL−1) to induce a reparative phenotype, followed by treatment with Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, or AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as indicated.

For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD68 together with CD86 or CD206. After washing, cells were incubated with the corresponding species-appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by DAPI counterstaining. Complete information on the primary and secondary antibodies, including host species, manufacturer, catalogue number, fluorophore where applicable, and working dilution, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

For flow-cytometric analysis, treated BV2 cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against CD11b, CD68, CD86, and CD206 according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Data were acquired using a BD Biosciences flow cytometer (USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

A sequential gating strategy was applied consistently to all samples. First, cellular events were displayed on an FSC-H versus SSC-H density plot, and the major BV2 cell population was selected using the E1 gate to exclude low-scatter debris and markedly aberrant events. Events within the E1 gate were subsequently displayed on a CD11b-Pacific Blue-H versus FSC-H plot, and CD11b+ BV2 cells were selected using the R2 gate. Polarization-associated populations were then analyzed within the gated CD11b+ BV2 population. CD68+CD86+ and CD68+CD206+ populations were quantified as percentages of the gated CD11b+ BV2 parent population. The same gating hierarchy and gate boundaries were applied consistently across all experimental groups. Flow-cytometry experiments were independently repeated three times.

Immunofluorescence staining of GSDMD and NLRP3 in BV2 cells

Immunofluorescence staining of TOMM20/SIRT3, TOMM20/DRP1, TOMM20/MFN1, and TOMM20/MFN2 in PC12 cells

PC12 cells were seeded on sterile glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Establishment of the SCI rat model

Female Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 180–220 g) were obtained from an accredited laboratory animal center. All animal procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee and performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg kg−1). After shaving and disinfecting the dorsal region, a midline incision was made to expose the T8 spinal segment. A total of 90 rats were included in the in vivo experiments. The experimental groups comprised Sham, SCI, Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. Rats in the Sham group underwent exposure of the T8 spinal cord without contusion or local injection. In the SCI and treatment groups, a moderate clip-compression injury was induced by applying a microvascular clip to the exposed T8 spinal cord for 30 s.

Immediately after SCI induction, the indicated formulations were locally administered into the lesion site in a total volume of 10 μL per rat. The reference concentration of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was selected on the basis of preliminary in vitro concentration-screening experiments. AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR at 25 μg/mL maintained favorable cytocompatibility in BV2 cells, PC12 cells, and primary hippocampal neurons and was therefore selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. This concentration served as the reference formulation concentration for local administration and was not derived from a formal EC50 determination or systemic dose-conversion calculation. The administered doses were subsequently normalized according to the experimentally determined drug-loading contents of the formulations. HPLC analysis showed that the AST loading contents of AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR were 7.28 wt% and 1.47 wt%, respectively, while the MYR loading content of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was 12.03 ± 0.40 wt%. Accordingly, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was administered at 25 μg/mL, corresponding to 0.25 μg of formulation per rat and delivering 3.675 ng AST and approximately 30.08 ng MYR. To achieve an equivalent AST dose, AST@ZIF-8 was administered at 5.05 μg/mL, corresponding to approximately 50.5 ng of formulation and 3.675 ng AST per rat. The Free drugs group received AST at 0.3675 μg/mL together with MYR at 3.0075 μg/mL in the same 10-μL injection volume, corresponding to 3.675 ng AST and approximately 30.08 ng MYR per rat. Thus, the AST dose was matched across the Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups, whereas the MYR dose was additionally matched between the Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups. The SCI group received an equal volume of vehicle. The selected dose should therefore be regarded as a biologically active and well-tolerated working dose within the present experimental setting rather than an optimized therapeutic dose, because a formal in vivo dose–response study was not performed.

Microinjections were performed using a 26-gauge needle (inner diameter 0.25 mm, outer diameter 0.46 mm, 30° bevel; Shanghai Difar Instruments, China) connected to a microinjection pump at a constant rate of 1 μL min−1 over 10 min. The needle was maintained in position for an additional 5 min and then slowly withdrawn to minimize reflux and additional mechanical disturbance. Thus, the SCI and treatment groups were matched for needle insertion, injection volume, injection rate, and local vehicle/procedure exposure, differing only in the administered formulation. Postoperative care included manual bladder expression three times daily until spontaneous urination resumed.

Flow cytometric analysis of microglial and infiltrating myeloid populations in spinal cord tissue

Spinal-cord flow-cytometric analysis was performed to characterize the relative abundance of resident microglial and infiltrating myeloid populations and, subsequently, to evaluate the polarization-associated phenotype of resident microglia. Rats from the Sham, SCI, Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups were included in this analysis, with n = 3 independent animals per group. At 7 days after surgery, an approximately 6-mm spinal cord segment centered on the lesion site was collected from each animal. Tissues were minced and mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, followed by filtration to remove large tissue debris. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in PBS and processed for antibody staining according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cells were stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against CD45, CD11b, CD86, and CD206. Flow-cytometric data were acquired using a BD Biosciences flow cytometer (USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software. The same gating hierarchy and gate-setting criteria were applied consistently across all five experimental groups.

For the initial analysis of CNS myeloid-cell composition, the major cellular population was first selected on an FSC-H versus SSC-H plot to exclude low-scatter debris and markedly aberrant events. CD45 fluorescence intensity was then evaluated to distinguish two populations. Cells exhibiting relatively low CD45 expression (CD45low; denoted as CD45+ in the gating plots) were operationally defined as resident microglia, whereas cells exhibiting markedly higher CD45 expression (CD45high; denoted as CD45++) were considered to predominantly represent infiltrating monocyte/macrophage-lineage cells. The relative abundance of these two populations was quantified, and the CD45low/CD45high ratio was calculated to assess the predominance of resident microglia over infiltrating macrophage-lineage cells within the injured spinal cord.

Based on this population-level analysis, subsequent polarization-associated analysis was specifically performed within the CD45low resident microglial compartment. Events within the CD45low gate were further analyzed for CD11b together with the polarization-associated markers CD86 or CD206. CD11b+CD86+ cells were quantified as a pro-inflammatory microglial population, whereas CD11b+CD206+ cells were quantified as a reparative microglial population. The percentages of these populations were calculated relative to the gated CD45low microglial parent population. The same gating hierarchy and gate boundaries were applied consistently across the Sham, SCI, Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

Spinal cord tissues were harvested at 7 days post-injury, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer system, and concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For transcriptome analysis, 1 μg of high-quality total RNA per sample was used for library preparation with the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Libraries were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp for adapter trimming and quality filtering. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome in a strand-specific manner using HISAT2, and transcript assembly was performed with StringTie based on reference gene annotations. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with |log2 (fold change) | > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the GSVA package with predefined gene sets. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

In vivo ROS chemiluminescence imaging

To assess ROS production after SCI, in vivo chemiluminescence imaging was performed on day 3 post-injury using the L-012 probe. Animals were fasted for 4 h before imaging, with free access to water, and anesthetized with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane throughout the procedure. Fresh L-012 working solution was prepared in sterile saline and administered by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 25 mg kg−1. Imaging was performed immediately using the AniView600 system (BLT, China) in open-filter mode. Regions of interest were defined at the injury site, and signal intensities were quantified to evaluate ROS levels in the spinal cord.

Behavioral assessment

Hindlimb locomotor function was evaluated using the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor rating scale, which ranges from 0 (no observable hindlimb movement) to 21 (normal locomotion). BBB assessments were performed at baseline (week 0) and weekly at weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 after surgery.

Gait analysis was performed one day before tissue collection using the Green Walk system (China), which consists of a green LED-backlit platform, a narrow passage, and a high-speed camera. Rats were acclimated and trained for 3 consecutive days before terminal functional assessment followed by tissue collection. Each rat traversed the walkway three times while locomotion was recorded from below. The accompanying software automatically quantified stride length, stride time, stride frequency, running speed, swing time, and maximum paw contact area.

Motor evoked potential and electromyography recording

At 8 weeks post-injury, motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were recorded to assess descending motor pathway conduction. Rats were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg kg−1, intraperitoneally) and positioned in a stereotaxic frame. A stimulating electrode was positioned over the motor cortex, a reference electrode was placed subcutaneously, and a recording electrode was positioned in the gastrocnemius muscle of the hindlimb. Transcranial electrical stimulation was delivered using single monophasic square-wave pulses (0.5 mA, 0.5 ms duration, 1 Hz). Evoked responses were recorded from the gastrocnemius muscle, band-pass filtered at 20–1000 Hz, and analyzed using MATLAB. The peak-to-peak amplitude and response latency of the MEPs were quantified as electrophysiological indicators of descending motor pathway conduction.

Hindlimb electromyographic (EMG) activity was additionally recorded from the gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior muscles using noninvasive surface electrodes during voluntary movement in a controlled environment. EMG signals were band-pass filtered at 20–1000 Hz and analyzed for signal amplitude using MATLAB. All electrophysiological recordings and analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups.

Tissue collection and preparation for immunostaining

In vivo immunostaining was performed at 7 days and 8 weeks post-injury. Rats were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium and transcardially perfused with ice-cold heparinized saline (10 U mL−1), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Spinal cords were carefully dissected, post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 48 h, and incubated in 30% sucrose until fully equilibrated. Tissue segments approximately 8–10 mm in length centered on the lesion site were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and cryosectioned into continuous 16 μm slices, which were stored at −20 °C until use.

Quantitative real-time PCR

To independently validate representative differentially expressed genes identified by RNA sequencing, spinal cord tissues were collected from independent animals at 7 days after SCI under the same experimental conditions used for transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was extracted from spinal cord tissues using TRIzol reagent (R0016, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically based on the OD260/OD280 ratio. For reverse transcription, 200 ng of total RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan) with a combination of oligo(dT) and random hexamer primers.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (RR390A, Takara, Japan). Hematological and biochemical analysis

Before blood collection, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane to minimize stress and ensure procedural safety. Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used. For repeated measurements over time, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison test was applied. For comparisons between two groups in RNA-seq validation or other pairwise analyses, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used when data were normally distributed; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Engineering and physicochemical characterization of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR nano-biointerface

To construct a bioactive nanoplatform capable of integrating responsive drug delivery with interfacial redox activity, astaxanthin was first encapsulated within the ZIF-8 framework, followed by surface coordination between myricetin and zinc ions to form a zinc–myricetin metal–phenolic shell (Fig. 1A). This stepwise assembly generated the core–shell nanoplatform AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, in which the ZIF-8 core served as a protective reservoir for hydrophobic astaxanthin, whereas the zinc–myricetin shell provided a chemically active outer interface for subsequent interaction with the oxidative lesion microenvironment.

Astaxanthin was encapsulated within the ZIF-8 framework, followed by surface coordination of myricetin with zinc ions to form a zinc–myricetin metal–phenolic shell. (B) TEM and HAADF images of ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, with corresponding elemental mapping of O, Zn, N, and C. (C, D) DLS-derived hydrodynamic size distributions and quantitative hydrodynamic diameters of AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. (E) Zeta potentials of ZIF-8, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. (F) XRD patterns of ZIF-8, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. (G) FT-IR spectra of myricetin, astaxanthin, ZIF-8, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. (H) UV–vis absorption spectra of astaxanthin, AST@ZIF-8, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, and ZIF-8. (I) Concentration-dependent UV–vis absorption spectra of astaxanthin. Transmission electron microscopy showed that pristine ZIF-8 nanoparticles displayed a typical polyhedral morphology with relatively smooth surfaces (Fig. 1B). After astaxanthin loading and zinc–myricetin coating, the resulting AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR particles retained the overall ZIF-derived morphology but exhibited a rougher surface and increased contrast, consistent with cargo incorporation and outer shell formation. High-angle annular dark-field imaging and elemental mapping further confirmed the spatial distribution of oxygen, zinc, nitrogen, and carbon throughout the particles, indicating successful integration of the inorganic ZIF-8 framework and organic functional components within the final nanoplatform.

Dynamic light scattering analysis further characterized the change in hydrodynamic size during stepwise nanoparticle construction. AST@ZIF-8 exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 148.20 ± 0.80 nm with a PDI of 0.108 ± 0.007. Following Zn–MYR surface coordination, the hydrodynamic diameter increased to 171.62 ± 0.49 nm, with a similarly low PDI of 0.102 ± 0.005 (Fig. 1C and D), consistent with the formation of an additional surface coordination layer. Both formulations exhibited low polydispersity indices, indicating narrow particle-size distributions and good colloidal dispersity. Zeta-potential analysis further showed a pronounced surface-charge change after Zn–MYR modification, shifting from a positive value for ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8 to a negative value for AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR (Fig. 1E). The increase in hydrodynamic diameter together with the surface-charge reversal therefore provides complementary evidence of successful surface modification by the zinc–myricetin coordination layer.

X-ray diffraction analysis showed that the characteristic diffraction peaks of ZIF-8 were retained after astaxanthin loading and zinc–myricetin shell formation (Fig. 1F), indicating that the crystalline framework of ZIF-8 remained largely preserved during the fabrication process. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy further supported the incorporation of the functional components into the final nanoplatform (Fig. 1G). Free myricetin exhibited a broad absorption band in the 3200–3500 cm−1 region, mainly attributable to phenolic O–H stretching, together with characteristic vibrations in the 1500–1700 cm−1 region associated with its aromatic and oxygen-containing groups. ZIF-8 displayed characteristic imidazolate-related absorption bands around 1600 cm−1 and within the 1000–1500 cm−1 fingerprint region. These ZIF-8-associated spectral features remained detectable in AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, indicating preservation of the underlying framework after cargo loading and surface modification. Meanwhile, changes in the broad O–H-associated region and the fingerprint region of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR relative to the individual components were consistent with an altered local chemical environment after formation of the Zn–MYR shell. Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy further showed AST-associated absorption features in both AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR (Fig. 1H). In addition, AST exhibited concentration-dependent absorbance behavior over the tested concentration range (Fig. 1I), providing the basis for subsequent spectrophotometric quantification of drug loading.

The surface chemical composition of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was further characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The high-resolution Zn 2p spectrum displayed distinct Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 signals, confirming the presence of zinc species in the final formulation (Fig. 1J). The C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s spectra showed carbon-, nitrogen-, and oxygen-containing chemical environments consistent with the ZIF-8 framework and incorporated organic components (Fig. 1K–M). The XPS survey spectrum further demonstrated the coexistence of Zn, O, N, and C in AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR (Fig. 1N). Collectively, the FT-IR and UV–vis spectral features, together with the XPS, elemental-mapping, hydrodynamic-size, and ζ-potential results, support the successful construction of the Zn–MYR-modified AST@ZIF-8 core–shell nano-biointerface.

Taken together, the increase in hydrodynamic diameter after Zn–MYR modification, together with the surface-charge reversal, electron-microscopy observations, elemental mapping, and spectroscopic signatures, supports the successful construction of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as a structurally integrated core–shell nano-biointerface rather than a simple drug-loaded ZIF-8 particle. The preserved ZIF-8 crystallinity indicates that the underlying framework remained largely intact during surface modification and continued to serve as a pH-labile reservoir for astaxanthin, whereas the Zn–MYR coordination shell introduced a distinct surface interface characterized by altered physicochemical properties. This shell is expected to function not only as a stabilizing layer but also as a chemically active interface capable of interacting with ROS-enriched pathological microenvironments. Thus, the resulting material architecture provides the structural basis for coupling lesion-responsive cargo release with interfacial redox regulation.

Dual-responsive release behavior and redox-active interfacial activity of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR

Having established the successful construction of the core–shell nanoplatform, we next investigated whether AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR could maintain colloidal stability, undergo pathological microenvironment-responsive cargo release, and exert broad-spectrum extracellular antioxidant activity. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the nanoplatform was designed to respond to acidic and oxidative conditions while scavenging multiple reactive oxygen species through the combined actions of the ZIF-8 core and the zinc–myricetin redox-active shell.

We first evaluated the colloidal and interfacial physicochemical stability of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR under physiologically relevant conditions. Dynamic light scattering showed only a modest increase in hydrodynamic diameter over 7 days in H2O, 10% FBS, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), without pronounced particle aggregation (Fig. 2B). Complementary ζ-potential measurements showed that the particles maintained an overall negative surface potential throughout the 7-day observation period, with only moderate changes and no surface-charge reversal (Fig. S1A). Together, the limited variations in hydrodynamic diameter and ζ-potential support good colloidal stability and preservation of the overall physicochemical characteristics of the Zn–MYR-modified particle interface under the tested conditions.

Drug-loading analysis further quantified the composition of the formulations and provided the experimental basis for subsequent equivalent-dose comparisons. HPLC analysis showed an AST loading content of 7.28 wt% in AST@ZIF-8 and 1.47 wt% in AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, while the MYR loading content of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was 12.03 ± 0.40 wt% (Fig. 2C). These experimentally determined loading values were used to normalize the AST and MYR doses in the subsequent free-drug and nanoformulation groups. The decrease in apparent AST loading after Zn–MYR modification reflects the substantial change in formulation composition during shell construction; because loading-content measurements alone cannot distinguish shell-associated mass increase from processing-associated cargo loss, this reduction was not attributed exclusively to a dilution effect caused by shell formation.

We then examined whether the nanoplatform could be activated under SCI-relevant conditions. Under physiological pH (7.4), astaxanthin release remained limited throughout the observation period. In contrast, both acidic conditions (pH 5.0) and oxidative conditions in the presence of H2O2 markedly accelerated drug release (Fig. 2D). Among these conditions, acidic medium produced the fastest and greatest release, whereas H2O2 also substantially promoted cargo liberation relative to neutral medium. These findings support pathological cue-responsive AST release while indicating comparatively restricted cargo liberation under physiological conditions.

To further characterize the environmental responsiveness of the Zn–MYR-modified interface, MYR release was evaluated under physiological and simulated SCI-relevant conditions. At pH 7.4, cumulative MYR release remained relatively limited, reaching 25.6 ± 1.2% at 72 h. Oxidative stimulation increased MYR release to 35.8 ± 1.3% at pH 7.4 + H2O2, whereas mild acidification further enhanced release to 47.8 ± 1.6% at pH 6.5. The greatest release was observed under combined acidic and oxidative conditions, reaching 66.9 ± 1.8% at 72 h (Fig. S1B). These findings indicate that the Zn–MYR-modified interface remains comparatively resistant to MYR liberation under physiological conditions but becomes increasingly labile in response to acidic and oxidative cues resembling the post-SCI microenvironment.

Zn release from the Zn-containing core–shell architecture was subsequently quantified by ICP-MS under the same environmental conditions (Fig. S1C). At 72 h, the released/dissolved Zn concentration remained relatively low under physiological conditions, reaching 8.97 ± 0.36 μM at pH 7.4. The presence of H2O2 at neutral pH increased Zn release to 11.97 ± 0.40 μM, whereas mild acidification to pH 6.5 resulted in 15.65 ± 0.54 μM released Zn. Under the combined acidic and oxidative condition, which represents the most aggressive simulated pathological environment examined in this study, the released Zn concentration reached 24.55 ± 0.66 μM at 72 h. These findings demonstrate that Zn liberation from the core–shell architecture is limited under physiological conditions but increases in response to acidic and oxidative cues.

Given the concentration-dependent biological effects of Zn in neural tissue, we further examined whether the experimentally observed Zn-release range was compatible with neuronal viability. Primary hippocampal neurons were exposed to increasing concentrations of Zn2+ (0–50 μM), followed by CCK-8 analysis (Fig. S1D). Cell viability remained close to the untreated level at low Zn2+ concentrations and was approximately 90% at 25 μM, a concentration slightly above the maximum Zn level measured in the release assay (24.55 ± 0.66 μM). Notably, this concentration is also close to the low-to-moderate micromolar Zn range previously used in neurogenic and regenerative cell models. ZnCl2 at 10 μM has been reported to promote neurite outgrowth during neuronal differentiation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells, whereas Zn concentrations up to 20 μM have been used to promote neural differentiation in an SCI-oriented ZIF-8 study [25,26]. These reports, together with the preserved viability of primary neurons observed here at 25 μM, support the cytocompatibility of the Zn concentrations generated within the tested release window. In contrast, further increases in Zn2+ concentration were accompanied by a progressive reduction in neuronal viability, with more pronounced decreases observed at 40 and 50 μM. These results indicate that the Zn concentrations generated by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR within the tested 72-h in vitro release window remained within a concentration range that did not cause pronounced cytotoxicity in primary neurons under the present experimental conditions.

Because Zn is present in both the ZIF-8 core and the Zn–MYR coordination layer, the ICP-MS measurements represent the overall release of soluble Zn-containing species and cannot distinguish shell-derived Zn from Zn released during ZIF-8 degradation. Moreover, ICP-MS quantifies elemental Zn rather than free Zn2+ speciation, and Zn concentrations measured in an in vitro release medium cannot be directly equated with free Zn2+ concentrations within injured spinal cord tissue in vivo. Accordingly, the present data support the cytocompatibility of the experimentally observed in vitro Zn-release range but do not define a universal therapeutic or neurotoxicity threshold for Zn in vivo.

Taken together, the MYR- and Zn-release profiles demonstrate relatively restrained component liberation under physiological conditions and enhanced release under SCI-relevant acidic and oxidative stimulation. The accompanying neuronal cytocompatibility analysis further indicates that even the highest Zn concentration detected under the tested release conditions remained within a range that was well tolerated by primary hippocampal neurons. These findings complement the hydrodynamic-size and ζ-potential stability data and collectively support short-term physicochemical stability under physiological conditions together with controlled microenvironment-responsive component release.

We next assessed the antioxidant activity of the nanoplatform. The ROS-scavenging capability of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was first visually confirmed by the ABTS and DPPH assays, in which the characteristic colors of both radical solutions progressively faded after treatment (Fig. 2E). Electron paramagnetic resonance analysis further demonstrated that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR efficiently quenched both superoxide anion and hydroxyl radicals. Specifically, the characteristic DMPO/•OOH and DMPO/•OH signals were markedly attenuated after treatment with AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR and were reduced more strongly than in the AST@ZIF-8 group (Fig. 2F and G), indicating that the zinc–myricetin-coated formulation provided superior radical-scavenging activity.

Consistent with the EPR results, colorimetric analyses showed clear concentration-dependent antioxidant behavior. AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR progressively increased the clearance of ABTS•+ and DPPH• with increasing concentration (Fig. 2H and I). A similar dose-dependent trend was also observed for O2•-, H2O2, and •OH, with radical clearance efficiencies rising steadily across the tested concentration range (Fig. 2J–L). These findings indicate that the nanoplatform is capable of scavenging several representative reactive oxygen species rather than acting selectively on a single oxidant.

Importantly, direct comparison between AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR revealed the functional contribution of the outer coordination shell. Across all tested ROS-scavenging assays, including ABTS•+, DPPH•, O2•-, H2O2, and •OH, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR consistently showed stronger antioxidant activity than AST@ZIF-8 alone (Fig. 2M–Q).

Because both formulations contain the AST-loaded ZIF-8 core whereas only AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR contains the Zn–MYR outer interface, the stronger antioxidant performance of the latter is consistent with an additional functional contribution of the Zn–MYR interface beyond carrier-mediated AST delivery. Accordingly, the outer coordination layer is more appropriately regarded as a redox-active material interface rather than solely as a passive stabilizing coating. This comparative formulation design therefore establishes a structure–function relationship linking surface reconstruction with enhanced material-level antioxidant activity.

However, because isolated Zn-, MYR-, and shell-only controls were not included, the individual contributions of Zn coordination, MYR-associated redox chemistry, and shell-mediated surface interactions cannot be quantitatively separated. Accordingly, the improved performance of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR supports an additional functional contribution of the Zn–MYR interface but does not establish formal synergy between the core and shell components.

Overall, the combined stability, component-release, and ROS-scavenging analyses support an integrated core–shell material concept in which the ZIF-8 core provides protected and pathological cue-responsive AST delivery, whereas Zn–MYR modification contributes a relatively stable yet environmentally responsive redox-active interface. These material-level properties provide the physicochemical basis for examining the subsequent treatment-associated changes in mitochondrial homeostasis, immunometabolic remodeling, and inflammasome-associated signaling.

Before proceeding to the cellular experiments, we assessed the cytocompatibility of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR in BV2 cells, PC12 cells, and primary hippocampal neurons using a CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. S2, BV2 cell viability remained above 90% at concentrations up to 50 μg/mL, whereas PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons were comparatively more sensitive and maintained >90% viability at 25 μg/mL. Accordingly, 25 μg/mL was selected as a conservative, cytocompatibility-based working concentration for subsequent in vitro experiments across the different cell models. This concentration was selected to minimize material-associated cytotoxicity and standardize treatment conditions and should not be interpreted as a pharmacodynamically optimized therapeutic concentration.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR coordinates microglial redox–mitochondrial and immunometabolic remodeling and attenuates NLRP3/GSDMD-associated signaling

Activated microglia are central mediators of the secondary injury cascade after SCI, in which oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic remodeling, inflammatory polarization, and inflammasome activation are tightly interconnected.

Microglial inflammatory states are increasingly recognized as being coupled to cellular metabolism. Impaired mitochondrial respiration can coexist with enhanced glycolytic dependence, while dynamic mitochondrial adaptation contributes to microglial phenotypic flexibility [8,[27], [28], [29]]. Recent SCI studies further indicate that modulation of glutamine metabolism, α-ketoglutarate availability, and oxidative phosphorylation can favor a more reparative macrophage state [30], whereas pathological endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondria crosstalk can disturb mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis and amplify inflammatory signaling [31]. Against this background, we examined whether the redox-active properties of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR extended to coordinated regulation of mitochondrial and immunometabolic responses.

To determine whether AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR could regulate this coupled pathological response, lipopolysaccharide-stimulated BV2 cells were used as an in vitro inflammatory microglial model. For concise figure labeling, AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR are denoted as AZ and AZM, respectively, in the figures. We first examined mitochondrial oxidative stress. Lipopolysaccharide stimulation markedly increased mitochondrial superoxide production, as indicated by the strong MitoSOX fluorescence signal in BV2 cells (Fig. 3A). Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 partially reduced this signal, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced the most pronounced suppression, suggesting that the zinc–myricetin-coated nanoplatform more effectively attenuated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulation in activated microglia. Consistent with this result, supplementary DCFH-DA and DHE staining showed that lipopolysaccharide also increased total intracellular reactive oxygen species and intracellular superoxide levels, both of which were markedly reduced after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. S3). These results indicate that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR alleviates oxidative stress at both the global intracellular and mitochondrial levels.

Because mitochondrial reactive oxygen species overload is closely associated with mitochondrial depolarization and structural injury, we next evaluated mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 staining. Compared with control cells, lipopolysaccharide-treated BV2 cells showed a clear shift from JC-1 aggregates to monomers, reflecting mitochondrial membrane potential loss (Fig. 3B and Fig. S3). This depolarization was partially improved by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8, but was more effectively reversed by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the protective effect of the nanoplatform at the ultrastructural level. Control cells displayed mitochondria with relatively intact morphology and organized cristae, whereas lipopolysaccharide stimulation induced mitochondrial swelling, enlargement, vacuolization, and cristae disruption (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis showed that lipopolysaccharide increased mitochondrial area and the proportion of damaged mitochondria, while reducing the cristae integrity score (Fig. 3D–F). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR significantly alleviated these abnormalities, indicating that the nanoplatform preserved mitochondrial structural integrity under inflammatory stress.

We then investigated whether this mitochondrial protection was accompanied by metabolic remodeling. Extracellular acidification rate analysis revealed that lipopolysaccharide markedly enhanced glycolytic activity in BV2 cells, reflecting a shift toward a glycolysis-dominant inflammatory metabolic state (Fig. 3G). Quantitative analysis showed increases in non-glycolytic acidification, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve after lipopolysaccharide stimulation (Fig. 3H–K). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly reduced these glycolytic parameters compared with the lipopolysaccharide group, and its effect was stronger than that of Free drugs or AST@ZIF-8. Complementary oxygen consumption rate analysis further showed that lipopolysaccharide impaired mitochondrial respiration, as reflected by reductions in basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity. These respiratory parameters were partially restored after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. S4). The parallel reduction in glycolytic activation and recovery of mitochondrial respiration therefore indicates that treatment was associated not only with reduced oxidative stress but also with partial normalization of the metabolic state of activated BV2 cells.

The immunometabolic changes were further reflected in microglial phenotype. Immunofluorescence staining showed that lipopolysaccharide strongly increased the CD68+CD86+ inflammatory microglial population (Fig. 3L). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly reduced CD68/CD86 co-expression, indicating attenuation of the pro-inflammatory phenotype. Flow cytometry further confirmed this trend. Within the gated CD11b+ BV2 population, LPS stimulation markedly increased the proportion of CD68+CD86+ cells, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment substantially reduced this inflammatory population (Fig. 3N–P). Conversely, the proportion of CD68+CD206+ cells within the CD11b+ BV2 parent population was increased after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 3O–Q), consistent with a shift toward a more reparative microglial phenotype. Consistently, supplementary western blot analysis showed reduced iNOS expression and increased Arg-1 expression after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment, while ELISA analysis revealed decreased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, together with increased levels of interleukin-4 and interleukin-10 (Fig. S6). These concordant phenotypic and metabolic changes are consistent with a shift away from an inflammatory activation state toward a more reparative immunophenotype. They also support a close relationship between redox–mitochondrial regulation and microglial inflammatory-state remodeling, although they do not establish which of these processes is mechanistically upstream.

Since mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and metabolic stress are important upstream triggers of inflammasome activation, we further examined pyroptosis-related signaling. Under lipopolysaccharide plus adenosine triphosphate stimulation, BV2 cells showed strong CD68/GSDMD and CD68/NLRP3 fluorescence signals, indicating activation of inflammasome-associated pyroptotic pathways (Fig. 3R and S). Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 moderately reduced these signals, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced a stronger inhibitory effect. Western blot analysis further confirmed that lipopolysaccharide plus adenosine triphosphate markedly increased the expression of NLRP3, GSDMD, and interleukin-1β, while AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR significantly suppressed these pyroptosis-related proteins (Fig. 3T and U). Normalized NLRP3 expression increased markedly from 0.033 ± 0.006 in the Control group to 0.827 ± 0.031 following LPS plus ATP stimulation. Treatment with Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 reduced NLRP3 expression to 0.627 ± 0.015 and 0.637 ± 0.021, respectively, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced a more pronounced reduction to 0.357 ± 0.015. A similar pattern was observed for total GSDMD, which increased from 0.050 ± 0.010 in the Control group to 0.723 ± 0.021 after inflammatory stimulation and decreased to 0.523 ± 0.025, 0.520 ± 0.026, and 0.367 ± 0.015 in the Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups, respectively. IL-1β showed a comparable trend, increasing from 0.021 ± 0.010 in Control cells to 0.670 ± 0.020 after LPS plus ATP stimulation, followed by reductions to 0.497 ± 0.015, 0.490 ± 0.020, and 0.257 ± 0.012 after treatment with Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR, respectively. These concordant immunofluorescence and protein-level changes support attenuation of NLRP3/GSDMD-associated inflammatory signaling by the complete nano-biointerface. The parallel improvement in mitochondrial status and inflammasome-associated signaling is biologically plausible given the established sensitivity of NLRP3 activation to mitochondrial oxidative stress [7,32]. However, the present experiments do not establish a direct causal relationship between mitochondrial recovery and inflammasome attenuation. Moreover, total GSDMD abundance does not directly reflect its proteolytic activation, and neither the pore-forming GSDMD-N fragment nor functional membrane permeabilization was assessed. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR inhibits GSDMD-dependent pyroptotic cell death; rather, they indicate attenuation of an NLRP3/GSDMD-associated molecular signaling profile linked to pyroptosis. Taken together with the concurrent reductions in intracellular and mitochondrial ROS, preservation of mitochondrial integrity, partial restoration of respiratory metabolism, attenuation of glycolysis-biased remodeling, and shift toward a more reparative inflammatory phenotype, these findings support AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as an active redox–immunometabolic regulator. Mitochondrial restoration should nevertheless be regarded as a convergent treatment-associated feature rather than a proven upstream mechanistic hub, because the temporal sequence and functional dependencies among redox regulation, mitochondrial recovery, immunometabolic remodeling, and inflammasome-associated signaling were not directly established.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR preserves neuronal redox homeostasis, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and mitochondrial dynamics

We next investigated whether AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR could protect neuronal cells against oxidative injury. PC12 cells were used as an in vitro neuronal model to evaluate cellular uptake, intracellular redox regulation, mitochondrial function, and mitochondrial dynamics under hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress.

Time-dependent fluorescence imaging confirmed progressive cellular association and uptake of the nanoplatform. Cy5 fluorescence signals increased at 12, 24, and 36 h after incubation and showed clear spatial overlap with DiI-labeled cellular membranes and pericellular regions (Fig. 4A). This time-dependent accumulation indicates that the nanoplatform can effectively interact with and enter neuronal cells, providing the basis for intracellular antioxidant and mitochondrial protective effects.

We then established a hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative injury model in PC12 cells. DHE staining showed that hydrogen peroxide markedly increased intracellular superoxide levels, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR substantially reduced the DHE fluorescence signal compared with the untreated injury group (Fig. 4B). DCFH-DA staining showed a similar pattern for total intracellular reactive oxygen species: hydrogen peroxide exposure induced strong green fluorescence, which was attenuated by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 and was most effectively suppressed by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR (Fig. 4C). MitoSOX staining further demonstrated that hydrogen peroxide caused pronounced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulation, while AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly reduced mitochondrial superoxide generation (Fig. 4D). Supplementary biochemical analysis supported these fluorescence results, showing that hydrogen peroxide increased malondialdehyde levels and depleted intracellular glutathione, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR reduced lipid peroxidation and restored antioxidant capacity (Fig. S7). These data indicate that the nanoplatform relieves oxidative stress at both cytosolic and mitochondrial levels.

Because mitochondrial oxidative stress is closely associated with loss of membrane potential and structural injury, we next evaluated mitochondrial integrity. Flow cytometric analysis of JC-1 staining showed that hydrogen peroxide sharply reduced the population of cells with intact mitochondrial membrane potential, indicating severe mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 4E). This change was partially improved by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced the strongest recovery. Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed mitochondrial protection at the ultrastructural level. Control cells displayed relatively intact mitochondria with organized cristae, whereas hydrogen peroxide induced mitochondrial swelling, vacuolization, and cristae disruption (Fig. 4F). Quantitative analysis showed increased mitochondrial damage, reduced cristae integrity, and enlarged mitochondrial area after oxidative injury, all of which were significantly alleviated by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 4G–I). These results suggest that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR preserves mitochondrial structure and membrane potential under oxidative stress.

We further asked whether this structural protection translated into improved mitochondrial bioenergetics. Seahorse mitochondrial stress testing showed that hydrogen peroxide profoundly impaired mitochondrial respiration in PC12 cells, as reflected by reduced oxygen consumption rate after sequential injection of oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A (Fig. 4J). Quantitative analysis confirmed that oxidative stress decreased basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial respiration, while disturbing proton leak and lowering cellular ATP production (Fig. 4K–Q). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR significantly restored basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and cellular ATP levels, indicating recovery of mitochondrial respiratory function and energy production. Supplementary ECAR analysis further showed that hydrogen peroxide induced a compensatory glycolytic shift, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR reduced this metabolic compensation (Fig. S7), consistent with restoration of a more balanced cellular energy state.

Finally, we examined whether the recovery of mitochondrial bioenergetics was accompanied by coordinated changes in mitochondrial homeostasis and dynamics. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that H2O2 exposure markedly reduced SIRT3 and the fusion-related proteins MFN1 and MFN2, while increasing the fission-related protein DRP1 within TOMM20-positive mitochondria (Fig. 4R–U). These alterations were partially reversed by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 and were most prominently restored by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. Western blotting further confirmed these treatment-associated changes (Fig. 4V and W). Normalized SIRT3 expression decreased markedly from 0.800 ± 0.029 in Control cells to 0.252 ± 0.018 following H2O2 exposure. Treatment with Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 partially restored SIRT3 expression to 0.444 ± 0.016 and 0.462 ± 0.021, respectively, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced a more pronounced recovery to 0.656 ± 0.034. In contrast, DRP1 expression increased from 0.073 ± 0.016 in the Control group to 0.717 ± 0.025 after oxidative injury and was reduced to 0.507 ± 0.023, 0.503 ± 0.035, and 0.337 ± 0.021 in the Free drugs, AST@ZIF-8, and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups, respectively. A corresponding recovery was observed for the mitochondrial fusion-related proteins. MFN1 increased from 0.137 ± 0.007 in the H2O2 group to 0.242 ± 0.008, 0.253 ± 0.011, and 0.357 ± 0.018 following the respective treatments, while MFN2 increased from 0.153 ± 0.008 to 0.269 ± 0.009, 0.281 ± 0.012, and 0.397 ± 0.020, respectively. In each case, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced a greater restoration than AST@ZIF-8 alone, supporting a broader improvement in mitochondrial homeostasis rather than modulation of a single mitochondrial regulator.

SIRT3 is a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase involved in antioxidant defense, mitochondrial ROS control, membrane integrity, and mitochondrial quality regulation [[33], [34], [35]], and SIRT3-associated signaling has also been implicated in mitochondrial protection in spinal cord ischemia–reperfusion and traumatic SCI [[36], [37], [38]]. In the present study, recovery of SIRT3 occurred concurrently with reduced mitochondrial ROS, restoration of membrane potential and respiratory capacity, improved mitochondrial ultrastructure, increased MFN1/MFN2 expression, and suppression of DRP1. The convergence of these changes suggests that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR promotes a coordinated restoration of mitochondrial redox, bioenergetic, and dynamic homeostasis, with SIRT3 representing a biologically relevant component of this treatment-associated mitochondrial phenotype. However, because SIRT3 inhibition or genetic loss-of-function was not performed, these findings do not establish SIRT3 as a necessary mediator or support a strictly SIRT3-dependent mechanism. Rather, mitochondrial recovery should be interpreted as a convergent feature of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment, consistent across both activated microglial and oxidatively injured neuronal models, while the causal hierarchy linking mitochondrial restoration to subsequent inflammatory and reparative responses remains to be determined.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR protects PC12 cells from oxidative injury and promotes neurite outgrowth

We next investigated whether AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR could protect neuronal cells and preserve neuronal growth-related phenotypes under oxidative stress conditions relevant to SCI. To this end, hydrogen peroxide-induced injury models were established in PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons.

We first examined cell survival. Calcein-AM/PI staining showed that hydrogen peroxide exposure caused substantial cytotoxicity in PC12 cells, as evidenced by reduced Calcein-positive live cells and increased PI-positive dead cells (Fig. 5A). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly improved cell survival compared with the untreated injury group and showed a stronger protective effect than Free drugs or AST@ZIF-8. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant recovery in PC12 cell viability after treatment (Fig. 5H). A similar protective trend was observed in primary hippocampal neurons. Hydrogen peroxide challenge markedly reduced neuronal survival, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR preserved a substantially larger viable cell population (Fig. 5E–L). These findings indicate that the nanoplatform effectively alleviates oxidative cytotoxicity in both neuronal model cells and primary neurons.

We then asked whether the improved survival was accompanied by reduced apoptosis-associated injury. Caspase-3 immunostaining showed that hydrogen peroxide markedly increased the proportion of Caspase-3-positive PC12 cells (Fig. 5B). This increase was attenuated by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8, and was further reduced by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR. Quantitative analysis confirmed that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR most effectively suppressed the oxidative stress-induced increase in Caspase-3-positive cells (Fig. 5I), supporting an anti-apoptotic effect of the nanoplatform under oxidative injury conditions.

To determine whether neuronal phenotype was also preserved, we next examined structural and growth-associated markers in PC12 cells. Hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative injury markedly reduced the expression of the neuronal markers Tuj1 and MAP-2 and was accompanied by disruption of neurite-associated cellular morphology (Fig. 5C). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR partially restored these signals and preserved a more continuous and organized neuronal architecture. Quantitative analysis showed an increased number of MAP-2+/Tuj1+ PC12 cells per visual field after treatment (Fig. 5J). Consistent with this result, oxidative stress also reduced the expression of neurofilament and GAP43, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR preserved both NF and GAP43 signals more effectively than the comparison groups (Fig. 5D). Quantification further confirmed recovery of GAP43+/NF+ PC12 cells after treatment (Fig. 5K). These results suggest that the nanoplatform not only improves cell survival but also helps maintain neuronal differentiation-related and growth-associated phenotypes under oxidative stress.

PC12 cells were used as a reproducible neuronal-like model for standardized assessment of oxidative mitochondrial injury, whereas primary neurons were included to validate whether the protective effect of the nanoplatform could be extended to non-transformed neuronal cells with neurite-regenerative capacity. This combined in vitro design allowed us to evaluate both mitochondrial protection and neurite/axon-associated repair responses under oxidative stress. We further evaluated neurite growth in primary hippocampal neurons. Representative Tuj1 staining showed that hydrogen peroxide markedly impaired neurite extension and simplified neuronal morphology, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR preserved a more elaborate neuritic architecture with visibly improved neurite extension (Fig. 5F). A similar trend was observed in the wider neuronal network images, in which hydrogen peroxide caused a sparse and fragmented neurite pattern, while AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR maintained a denser and more continuous neuronal network (Fig. 5G). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR significantly increased both the total number of neurites and total neurite length compared with the hydrogen peroxide group, with better overall performance than free drugs or AST@ZIF-8 (Fig. 5M and N).

These findings extend the mitochondrial observations in Fig. 4 by demonstrating that improvement of redox and mitochondrial status occurs in parallel with greater neuronal survival and preservation of growth-associated phenotypes. The primary-neuron experiments are particularly relevant because they confirm that the protective effect is not restricted to the PC12 model. Nevertheless, preservation of Tuj1, MAP-2, NF, and GAP43-associated signals and enhanced neurite growth in vitro should not be interpreted as direct evidence of functional circuit reconstruction. Rather, these findings demonstrate preservation of cellular properties that are permissive for subsequent neuronal structural repair.

Local retention and acute lesion microenvironment remodeling after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment

We next examined the in vivo behavior of the locally administered formulations and their association with regulation of the acute lesion microenvironment after SCI. To first characterize local cargo retention, intact AST concentrations in spinal cord tissue were quantified at 1 h, 1 d, 3 d, and 7 d after injection. At 1 h, AST was readily detected in all AST-containing treatment groups. Thereafter, free AST declined rapidly, whereas AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR maintained higher tissue AST concentrations during the early post-injury period. Notably, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR showed the greatest AST retention at 1 and 3 days. By day 7, tissue AST concentrations had markedly decreased in all groups, indicating progressive clearance rather than persistent accumulation (Fig. S8B). These findings suggest that nanoformulation prolongs early local AST exposure, with additional Zn–MYR surface modification associated with greater retention during the first several days after administration.

Total Zn content in spinal cord tissue was further quantified by ICP-MS at the same time points. Although a modest elevation was observed shortly after administration of the Zn-containing formulations, total tissue Zn remained within a relatively narrow range thereafter and did not progressively accumulate through day 7 (Fig. S8A). Because endogenous Zn constitutes a substantial fraction of total spinal cord Zn and ICP-MS does not distinguish endogenous Zn from formulation-derived Zn, these measurements were interpreted primarily as evidence against sustained excessive Zn accumulation rather than as a direct measure of intact nanoparticle retention or in vivo Zn–MYR shell persistence.

Having established sustained early local AST exposure without progressive tissue Zn accumulation, we next evaluated whether treatment was accompanied by favorable modulation of the acute lesion microenvironment. Analyses were performed at 7 days post-injury, a stage characterized by robust oxidative stress, inflammatory myeloid activation, neuronal apoptosis, and inflammasome-associated signaling. Based on the material characterization, in vitro findings, and the above tissue-retention measurements, we investigated whether local administration of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was associated with attenuation of these early pathological features in vivo.

Oxidative stress at the lesion site was first evaluated by L-012 chemiluminescence imaging. SCI induced a strong chemiluminescent signal in the injured region, indicating marked reactive oxygen species accumulation. Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 reduced this signal to some extent, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced the most pronounced reduction (Fig. S9). This result is consistent with the broad-spectrum antioxidant activity observed in vitro and suggests that the zinc–myricetin-coated nanoplatform effectively lowers the oxidative burden during the acute phase of SCI.

We then assessed inflammatory myeloid polarization in the injured spinal cord. Immunofluorescence staining showed extensive accumulation of IBA-1-positive cells around the lesion epicenter after injury, accompanied by strong iNOS expression, indicating a prominent pro-inflammatory myeloid response (Fig. 6A). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly reduced IBA-1/iNOS co-expression compared with the untreated SCI group and the weaker treatment groups. In parallel, IBA-1/Arg-1 staining showed that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR increased the Arg-1-positive reparative population within the lesion region (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in IBA-1+iNOS+ cells and a concomitant increase in IBA-1+Arg-1+ cells after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 6G and H). Flow cytometric analysis of dissociated spinal cord tissues further supported this trend. SCI increased the proportion of CD11b+CD86+ cells and reduced the reparative CD11b+CD206+ population, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR shifted the CD11b+ myeloid compartment toward lower CD86 expression and higher CD206 expression (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that the nanoplatform attenuates acute inflammatory polarization and promotes a more reparative myeloid phenotype in vivo.

Flow-cytometric analysis of dissociated spinal cord tissues further characterized the cellular basis of the inflammatory response. Based on CD45 fluorescence intensity, two distinct CNS myeloid populations were identified, with CD45low cells operationally defined as resident microglia and CD45high cells predominantly representing infiltrating monocyte/macrophage-lineage cells. Quantitative analysis showed that the CD45low population markedly predominated over the CD45high population, with a CD45low/CD45high ratio of 17.48 ± 2.861 across three independent animals (Fig. S10). This marked numerical predominance suggests that resident microglia constitute the major myeloid population contributing to the inflammatory response within the examined lesion environment, although a contribution from infiltrating macrophages cannot be excluded.

We therefore further examined polarization-associated phenotypes within the CD45low resident microglial compartment. SCI increased the proportion of CD11b + CD86+ microglia while reducing the CD11b+CD206+ population. In contrast, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment decreased the proportion of CD11b+CD86+ cells and increased CD11b+CD206+ cells within the CD45low microglial population (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR is associated with attenuation of pro-inflammatory microglial activation and a shift toward a more reparative microglial phenotype in the injured spinal cord.

We next evaluated neuronal apoptosis within the lesion area. Caspase-3/NeuN double staining revealed a marked increase in Caspase-3 signal after SCI, together with a reduction in NeuN-positive neurons, indicating substantial neuronal apoptotic injury (Fig. 6C). Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 provided partial protection, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR most effectively reduced Caspase-3 fluorescence and preserved NeuN-positive neuronal cells in the lesion region. Quantitative analysis confirmed decreased Caspase-3 intensity and increased NeuN-positive cells after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 6I and J). These data suggest that the nanoplatform confers early neuroprotection in the injured spinal cord microenvironment.

Because oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are closely linked to inflammasome activation, we further examined pyroptosis-related signaling in CD68-positive inflammatory cells. SCI markedly increased GSDMD and NLRP3 fluorescence within CD68-positive regions, indicating activation of inflammasome-associated pyroptotic pathways (Fig. 6E and F). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR substantially reduced both CD68/GSDMD and CD68/NLRP3 signals compared with the untreated injury group. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that GSDMD and NLRP3 fluorescence intensities were significantly decreased after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 6K and L). Cytokine analysis further supported the improvement of the acute inflammatory milieu. IL-1β immunofluorescence and ELISA assays showed that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 (Fig. S9).

The in vivo findings therefore reproduce several treatment-associated features identified in BV2 cells, including reduced oxidative burden, attenuation of inflammatory myeloid activation, a shift toward reparative marker expression, and lower NLRP3/GSDMD-associated molecular signals. This cross-model consistency supports coordinated remodeling of the acute redox–immune lesion environment. However, as in the cellular experiments, these parallel changes do not establish that ROS reduction or mitochondrial restoration directly causes microglial phenotype remodeling or attenuation of inflammasome-associated signaling. The in vivo evidence therefore supports coordinated association rather than a linear causal cascade.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR promotes axon–myelin preservation and lesion tissue remodeling after SCI

We next investigated whether the early microenvironmental protection conferred by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR could translate into improved structural repair after SCI. To this end, histological staining, immunofluorescence analysis, and ultrastructural evaluation were performed to assess tissue preservation, neuronal survival, axonal integrity, and myelin repair.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed severe tissue destruction in the untreated SCI group, characterized by extensive lesion formation, disrupted tissue architecture, and marked loss of spinal cord parenchyma compared with the sham group (Fig. 7A). Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 partially alleviated these pathological changes, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced the most evident preservation of tissue continuity and lesion architecture. These findings indicate that the nanoplatform reduces secondary tissue destruction after SCI.

Neuronal preservation was then examined by Nissl staining. After SCI, the number of Nissl-positive neurons was markedly reduced, and many of the remaining neurons exhibited shrunken cell bodies and disrupted Nissl substance, indicating substantial neuronal degeneration (Fig. 7A). In contrast, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment preserved a higher density of Nissl-positive neurons with better maintained morphology. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR group showed the strongest neuronal preservation among all treatment groups (Fig. 7D).

We next evaluated myelin preservation and repair. Luxol fast blue staining revealed pronounced demyelination in the injured spinal cord, as reflected by the marked reduction in myelin-associated staining within the lesion region (Fig. 7A). This loss was partially alleviated by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR produced the most obvious recovery of Luxol fast blue-positive myelinated tissue. Quantitative analysis further confirmed a significant increase in Luxol fast blue-positive myelin area in the AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR group compared with the untreated SCI group (Fig. 7E), suggesting improved myelin preservation or remyelination after treatment.

Immunofluorescence staining further supported this reparative effect. After SCI, neurofilament, myelin basic protein, and Olig2 signals were markedly reduced, indicating axonal disruption, myelin loss, and impaired oligodendrocyte-associated support (Fig. 7B). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment increased neurofilament-positive axonal fibers, enhanced myelin basic protein expression, and elevated the Olig2-associated signal within the lesion region. Quantitative analysis confirmed significant recovery of myelin basic protein, Olig2, and neurofilament fluorescence intensity after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 7F–H). These results indicate that the nanoplatform promotes axonal preservation together with oligodendrocyte-associated myelin repair.

The improvement in myelin integrity was further validated by transmission electron microscopy. In the untreated SCI group, axons showed obvious degeneration and were surrounded by loose, collapsed, or disorganized myelin sheaths (Fig. 7C). By contrast, the AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated group displayed more regularly wrapped and structurally compact myelin sheaths, with ultrastructural features closer to those observed in the sham group. Quantitative analysis showed that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR significantly increased the number of myelinated axons and reduced the g-ratio compared with the untreated SCI group (Fig. 7I and J), supporting improved remyelination and myelin sheath integrity.

Taken together, the histological, immunofluorescence, and ultrastructural findings indicate greater preservation of neuronal, axonal, oligodendrocyte-associated, and myelin structures after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment. These results extend the early redox–inflammatory findings by showing that attenuation of acute secondary injury occurs in parallel with a more favorable structural phenotype at the later stage. Because the study does not directly distinguish preservation of pre-existing myelin from newly formed myelin in every assay, the changes are most appropriately interpreted as improved myelin preservation/remodeling rather than unequivocal de novo remyelination.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR supports neural tissue and extracellular-matrix remodeling after SCI

We next investigated whether the early anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects of AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR were accompanied by improved neural tissue remodeling at the lesion site. To this end, spinal cord sections were analyzed for neuronal/axonal markers, astrocytic activation, synaptic-associated signals, descending serotonergic fibers, and extracellular matrix components.

Immunofluorescence staining of Tuj1, NeuN, and GFAP first revealed marked differences in neural tissue preservation and glial scar formation among the groups (Fig. 8A). In the untreated SCI group, Tuj1-positive neural fibers were sparse and discontinuous across the lesion region, indicating severe disruption of axonal architecture. NeuN-positive neurons were also markedly reduced, reflecting substantial neuronal loss after injury. In parallel, GFAP staining was strongly enhanced around the lesion border, consistent with reactive astrocytosis and glial scar formation. Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 produced partial improvement, but their effects remained limited. By contrast, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment markedly increased Tuj1-positive fiber density and preserved a larger population of NeuN-positive cells within the injured region, while reducing excessive GFAP accumulation at the lesion border (Fig. 8D–F). These results suggest that the nanoplatform not only preserves neuronal and axonal structures, but also attenuates scar-associated astrocytic activation, thereby creating a tissue environment more permissive to neural repair.

We then examined whether this structural preservation was accompanied by recovery of synaptic-associated and descending pathway-related signals. Synaptophysin staining was strongly reduced after SCI, indicating pronounced synaptic loss within the lesion region (Fig. 8B). AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR markedly restored synaptophysin fluorescence compared with the untreated injury group and the weaker treatment groups (Fig. 8G), suggesting improved preservation or reconstruction of synaptic structures. Similarly, 5-hydroxytryptamine-positive descending serotonergic fibers were markedly disrupted after SCI, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR increased 5-hydroxytryptamine fluorescence within and around the lesion region (Fig. 8B–H). These changes are consistent with improved preservation or reorganization of synaptic-associated structures and descending serotonergic projections [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. However, recovery of Synaptophysin or 5-HT immunoreactivity does not directly demonstrate formation of new functional synapses or de novo reconstruction of descending neural circuits. Direct circuit-level evidence would require approaches such as anatomical tracing, electrophysiological connectivity mapping, or other dedicated functional-connectivity analyses.

Because extracellular matrix organization strongly influences axonal extension and tissue repair after SCI, we further assessed laminin, fibronectin, and neurofilament expression in the lesion area (Fig. 8C). After injury, the extracellular matrix became disorganized, with abnormal fibronectin deposition and reduced laminin-associated support. Neurofilament-positive axonal structures were also markedly disrupted. AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment increased laminin fluorescence and restored neurofilament-positive axonal architecture, while reducing excessive fibronectin-associated scar-like deposition compared with the untreated injury condition (Fig. 8I–K). Importantly, post-SCI extracellular-matrix regulation should not necessarily be interpreted as simple scar elimination. Emerging biomaterial studies indicate that mechanoregulation of fibroblast patterning can reorganize fibrotic extracellular matrix into a more aligned architecture that supports axonal extension [43]. Accordingly, the increased laminin-associated support and reduced excessive fibronectin-associated deposition observed here are better interpreted as constructive matrix remodeling toward a tissue state that may be more permissive for axonal extension and myelin repair.

Thus, AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR was associated with coordinated neural and extracellular-matrix remodeling. Nevertheless, the direct contribution of these matrix changes to functional neural connectivity remains to be established.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR promotes electrophysiological conduction, neuromuscular preservation, and locomotor recovery after SCI

We next determined whether the structural and microenvironmental improvements induced by AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR translated into functional recovery after SCI. To this end, electrophysiological conduction, skeletal muscle preservation, neuromuscular activity, gait performance, and longitudinal locomotor function were systematically evaluated.

Electrophysiological assessment revealed marked impairment of descending motor pathway conduction after SCI. Representative MEP recordings showed robust evoked responses in the Sham group, whereas SCI markedly reduced MEP amplitude and prolonged response latency (Fig. 9A). Compared with the untreated SCI group, the treatment groups showed varying degrees of electrophysiological improvement, with AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR displaying the most pronounced recovery. Quantitative analysis showed that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment was associated with increased MEP peak-to-peak amplitude and shorter response latency (Fig. 9E and F), consistent with improved descending motor pathway conduction within the examined experimental window.

Because impaired neural conduction after SCI is commonly accompanied by denervation-related muscle atrophy, we next examined hindlimb skeletal muscle preservation. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed that SCI caused pronounced myofiber atrophy, irregular fiber morphology, and enlarged interstitial spaces in skeletal muscle tissue (Fig. 9B). These pathological changes were partially alleviated by Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment resulted in more compact and organized muscle fibers with better preserved tissue architecture. Consistently, the AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR group showed significantly higher muscle wet weight and larger muscle fiber area than the untreated SCI group (Fig. 9G and H), suggesting attenuation of denervation-associated muscle degeneration.

Neuromuscular activity was further evaluated by EMG recordings from the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles. SCI markedly reduced EMG activity in both hindlimb muscles, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment was associated with more prominent electrical activity compared with the untreated SCI group (Fig. 9C). Quantitative analysis further showed increased EMG amplitudes in both the tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 9I and J), indicating improved neuromuscular activation.

We then evaluated gait performance using footprint and two-dimensional gait analyses. Untreated SCI animals displayed disorganized stepping patterns, reduced paw contact, and unstable gait trajectories (Fig. 9D). Free drugs and AST@ZIF-8 produced limited improvement, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated animals showed more regular stepping patterns and larger paw print areas, indicating improved hindlimb support and locomotor coordination. Quantitative analysis showed that stride time, which was prolonged after SCI, was reduced after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment (Fig. 9K). Additional gait parameters, including paw contact and coordination-related indices, also showed improvement after treatment (Fig. S11), supporting enhanced locomotor stability.

Longitudinal behavioral assessment further confirmed functional recovery. The Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan score remained low in untreated SCI animals throughout the observation period, whereas AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated animals showed a progressive and sustained improvement in hindlimb locomotor performance over 8 weeks (Fig. 9L). The concordance among MEP conduction, hindlimb EMG activity, skeletal-muscle preservation, gait performance, and longitudinal BBB scoring supports an overall treatment-associated improvement in neurological function.

Importantly, these functional changes occurred in parallel with the histological and molecular improvements described above, but the current experimental design does not demonstrate that any individual molecular, matrix, axonal, or myelin alteration directly caused the observed functional recovery [44]. The data therefore support an association between a more favorable post-injury tissue state and improved neurological performance rather than a simple linear structure–function causal relationship.

Transcriptomic profiling supports coordinated redox–immune–matrix remodeling

To further characterize the molecular response associated with AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment, RNA sequencing was performed as a focused comparison between injured spinal cord tissues from the untreated SCI and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated groups. Principal component analysis showed clear separation between the two groups, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 39.7% and 29.9% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 10A), indicating that treatment was associated with a distinct transcriptional state within the injured spinal cord. Differential-expression analysis further identified multiple treatment-associated transcriptional alterations involving immune regulation, stress responses, extracellular-matrix organization, and tissue remodeling (Fig. 10B–D). Representative genes shown in the volcano plot and heatmap satisfied the predefined criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05.

Among these transcripts, Oas3 and Serpinb1b represented immune/stress- and inflammation-associated expression changes, respectively, whereas Ibsp reflected an extracellular-matrix-associated alteration. To independently assess the reproducibility of selected RNA-seq findings, Oas3, Serpinb1b, and Ibsp were further examined by qRT-PCR using independent spinal cord samples collected at 7 days after SCI. The direction of change for all three genes was consistent with that observed in the RNA-seq dataset (Fig. S12), supporting the reproducibility of these representative treatment-associated transcriptional alterations. These measurements were used to validate selected expression patterns rather than to imply that the individual genes directly mediated the therapeutic phenotype.

We next examined pathway-level changes to determine whether the transcriptional response converged with the cellular and tissue phenotypes observed above. ssGSEA revealed altered immune-related enrichment in AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated tissue compared with untreated SCI tissue (Fig. 10C), consistent with the treatment-associated shift in the local immune transcriptional state. This pattern broadly paralleled the in vivo immunofluorescence and flow-cytometric findings showing reduced inflammatory myeloid activation and increased reparative marker expression. Gene Ontology analysis further showed enrichment of biological processes related to defense responses, regulation of innate immunity, response to bacterium, and negative regulation of immune-system processes, while antioxidant- and oxidoreductase-related molecular functions were also represented (Fig. 10F). These changes were broadly concordant with the reduced oxidative burden and improved mitochondrial homeostasis observed at the cellular and tissue levels.

KEGG analysis further identified enrichment of NOD-like receptor signaling, IL-17 signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, and phagosome-related pathways (Fig. 10E). Rather than pointing to a single dominant downstream mechanism, these pathway-level changes suggest a coordinated transcriptional response involving inflammatory regulation, innate immunity, redox-related processes, and cell–matrix interactions. In particular, the NOD-like receptor-related signature was consistent with the observed attenuation of NLRP3-associated inflammatory signaling, whereas enrichment of ECM–receptor interaction was concordant with the histological changes in laminin-, fibronectin-, and axon-associated tissue organization. The qRT-PCR-confirmed alteration in Ibsp further supported the reproducibility of an ECM-related transcriptional response, while Oas3 and Serpinb1b provided representative validation of immune/stress- and inflammation-associated expression changes.

Taken together, the RNA-seq and independent qRT-PCR analyses provide complementary molecular support for the coordinated redox–immune–matrix remodeling observed after AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR treatment. Importantly, however, the transcriptomic dataset was intentionally restricted to a focused comparison between untreated SCI and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR-treated tissues. It therefore cannot reconstruct the complete SCI-versus-Sham transcriptional trajectory, distinguish transcriptional effects attributable specifically to AST delivery, the ZIF-8 carrier, or the Zn–MYR interface, or establish direct causal relationships between individual differentially expressed genes, enriched pathways, and the corresponding therapeutic phenotypes. Accordingly, these transcriptomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory molecular evidence supporting the broader treatment-associated phenotype rather than as definitive pathway-specific mechanistic proof.

AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR shows favorable cytocompatibility and preliminary systemic tolerability within the 8-week observation window

The cells remained predominantly Calcein-positive throughout the observation period, with only sparse propidium iodide-positive dead cells detected (Fig. 11A). Quantitative analysis showed that cell survival remained close to 100% at all tested time points, with no significant difference among the 1-, 3-, 5-, and 7-day groups (Fig. 11C). These results indicate that AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR did not induce detectable delayed cytotoxicity under the tested in vitro conditions.

We next examined systemic tolerability after local administration within the 8-week observation window. H&E staining of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, showed preserved tissue architecture across all groups (Fig. 11B). Hematological and serum biochemical analyses further showed no evident treatment-associated abnormalities in white blood cell count, hemoglobin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, serum creatinine, or blood urea nitrogen (Fig. 11D–I). These findings support cytocompatibility and systemic tolerability within the examined 8-week period rather than comprehensive long-term biosafety.

Notably, several recent preclinical SCI nanomaterial and biomaterial studies have similarly evaluated systemic tolerability over approximately 8 weeks using major-organ histology together with hematological or serum biochemical indices [[45], [46], [47], [48]], whereas shorter safety observation periods, including approximately 28 days, have also been reported [49]. Thus, the present 8-week assessment falls within the observation range adopted by several contemporary preclinical SCI biomaterial studies. Nevertheless, intermediate-term safety assessments should not be considered equivalent to comprehensive chronic biosafety. Long-term material fate, degradation, biodistribution, accumulation, clearance, and delayed toxicity remain important unresolved considerations for translational SCI nanomedicine [50].

The present study did not directly quantify the spatiotemporal degradation and clearance of the ZIF-8 core or Zn–MYR coordination interface. In particular, the local fate of Zn-containing degradation products and imidazolate-derived components, together with their potential redistribution or accumulation in peripheral organs, remains to be established. Future studies should therefore incorporate time-resolved ICP-MS analysis of Zn in the lesion, circulation, and major organs, complemented by structural or imaging approaches to characterize material retention, degradation, redistribution, and clearance. Extended follow-up cohorts (≥3 months) will additionally be required to evaluate persistent microglial/macrophage activation, astrocytic reactivity, foreign-body responses, chronic neurotoxicity, and delayed systemic effects.

Overall interpretation and translational considerations

Collectively, the present findings support a material concept in which AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR functions not simply as a passive carrier but as a lesion-responsive redox-active nano-biointerface. The ZIF-8 core provides protected and pathological cue-responsive AST delivery, whereas the Zn–MYR modification contributes an additional redox-active surface interface. This architecture is associated with treatment responses spanning extracellular ROS regulation, mitochondrial preservation, immunometabolic remodeling, attenuation of inflammasome-associated signaling, neuronal protection, and axon–myelin preservation. Such integrated material–biology regulation may be particularly relevant to SCI because oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, immune activation, regulated cell-death signaling, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and neural disconnection evolve concurrently and interact within the injured lesion [[50], [51], [52]].

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. First, although the present study consistently showed treatment-associated improvements in oxidative stress, mitochondrial bioenergetics and dynamics, cellular metabolism, inflammatory-state remodeling, and NLRP3/GSDMD-associated signaling, the current experimental design does not establish a strict temporal or causal hierarchy among these processes. Mitochondrial restoration should therefore be interpreted as a convergent feature associated with treatment rather than as a proven upstream mechanistic hub. Similarly, the concurrent recovery of SIRT3 expression, mitochondrial function, and fission–fusion-related proteins does not establish SIRT3 as a necessary mediator because targeted loss-of-function or pharmacological perturbation experiments were not performed. The reductions in NLRP3, total GSDMD, and IL-1β likewise support attenuation of inflammasome- and pyroptosis-associated molecular signaling, but do not demonstrate direct inhibition of GSDMD-dependent pyroptotic cell death in the absence of GSDMD cleavage, membrane permeabilization, or other functional readouts of pyroptosis.

Second, the dosing strategy and molecular profiling remain exploratory in several respects. The reference working concentration was selected primarily from preliminary cytocompatibility and biological-response screening rather than from a systematic dose–response optimization, and a formal in vivo dose-ranging study was not performed. Accordingly, the administered dose should be regarded as a biologically active and well-tolerated working dose within the present experimental setting rather than an optimized therapeutic dose. In addition, the transcriptomic analysis was restricted to the SCI and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR groups. Although this design identifies treatment-associated transcriptional alterations, it cannot reconstruct the complete injury-associated transcriptional trajectory or distinguish transcriptional changes specifically attributable to the Zn–MYR interface. Multi-group and time-resolved transcriptomic analyses will therefore be required to more precisely resolve formulation-specific and stage-dependent molecular responses.

Third, although direct comparisons between AST@ZIF-8 and AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR consistently support an additional functional contribution of the Zn–MYR-modified interface, the individual roles of Zn coordination, MYR-associated redox chemistry, and shell-mediated surface interactions remain incompletely resolved. Because isolated Zn-, MYR-, and shell-only controls were not included, these contributions cannot be quantitatively separated, and the present data should not be interpreted as establishing formal synergy among the individual components. Future studies incorporating component-specific controls and targeted perturbation strategies will be needed to define the structure–function relationship of the outer coordination interface more precisely.

From a translational perspective, the present study provides evidence of cytocompatibility, local therapeutic efficacy, and systemic tolerability within the 8-week observation window, but does not establish comprehensive long-term biosafety or clinical readiness. Importantly, the newly added time-resolved measurements of chromatographically detectable intact AST and total tissue Zn provide direct information on local cargo retention and tissue Zn burden during the first 7 days after administration. These measurements indicate prolonged early AST exposure after nanoformulation and no progressive accumulation of total Zn within the injured spinal cord. However, they do not constitute a comprehensive biodistribution or material-fate analysis. Intact AST quantification does not directly trace the persistence of the complete nanoplatform, whereas ICP-MS measures total elemental Zn and cannot distinguish endogenous Zn from formulation-derived Zn or resolve the respective contributions of the ZIF-8 core and Zn–MYR coordination layer. Thus, the long-term degradation, redistribution, and clearance of the complete core–shell architecture remain to be established.

Future studies should therefore incorporate formulation-specific labeling together with quantitative analysis of the lesion site, circulation, and major organs to define the long-term distribution and clearance of the nanoplatform and its degradation products. Particular attention should be given to the fate of Zn-containing species and imidazolate-derived components and to their potential redistribution or accumulation outside the spinal cord. Although the 8-week observation period is consistent with that used in several recent preclinical SCI nanomaterial studies, longer follow-up periods will be required to assess chronic neurotoxicity, persistent microglial/macrophage activation, astrocytic reactivity, foreign-body responses, and delayed systemic effects. Finally, although lesion-directed local administration may facilitate integration with surgical management of SCI, its administration timing, formulation volume, dosing frequency, lesion-site coverage, and procedural compatibility require further optimization. Collectively, systematic dose-ranging studies, time-resolved mechanistic perturbation, multi-group transcriptomic analysis, formulation-specific material tracking, and long-term safety evaluation will be necessary to more precisely define the mechanistic boundaries, therapeutic window, and translational potential of this nano-biointerface.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed AST@ZIF-8@Zn-MYR as a lesion-responsive redox-active nano-biointerface that integrates pathological cue-responsive cargo delivery with interfacial redox regulation. This design links material-level responsiveness with coordinated mitochondrial–immune microenvironment remodeling after SCI, providing a material-based strategy for microenvironment-oriented spinal cord repair.
