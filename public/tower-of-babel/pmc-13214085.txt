Multi-parametric profiling of plasma-derived extracellular vesicles reveals a disease-associated molecular signature supporting a liquid biopsy approach in myelofibrosis
Authors: Ghazal Narimanfar, Husam B. R. Alabed, Alessia Brancolini, Roberto Maria Pellegrino, Martina Barone, Claudia Venturi, Hillary Catellani, Giovanni Torsello, Luca Pestarino, Andrea Cavalli, Alessandra D’Addio, Alessia Tieghi, Regina Grillari-Voglauer, Francesca Palandri, Lucia Catani

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyelofibrosis (MF) is a rare and severe myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by bone marrow fibrosis, cytopenia, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and systemic inflammation. The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in MF pathogenesis by promoting fibrotic remodeling and supporting malignant clone survival. Among the key mediators of intercellular communication within the TME are extracellular vesicles (EVs), which transport bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. EVs derived from both malignant and stromal cells might contribute to disease progression and niche remodeling in MF. However, their role in MF TME has still to be defined.MethodsEVs were isolated from the platelet-poor plasma of MF patients and age- and sex-matched controls using size-exclusion chromatography. EVs were then characterized by transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and Western blotting for canonical markers, following with the surface protein profiling using bead-based flow cytometry, and lipidomic profiling by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Functional assays assessed the impact of EVs on α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression in Transforming Growth Factor (TGF)-β–stimulated fibroblasts.ResultsEVs from MF patients displayed typical EV features (CD9, TSG101, and ALIX positivity) and showed a significant increase in median size compared with control counterparts, suggesting altered vesicle biogenesis. Lipidomic analysis identified 136 species across 12 lipid classes, revealing a distinct MF-specific lipid signature characterized by reduced sphingomyelin levels and increased diacylglycerol, ether-linked phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine species, as well as enhanced acyl chain unsaturation, indicative of membrane remodeling under inflammatory stress. Surface profiling analysis revealed that MF-derived EVs exhibited a selective depletion of the endothelial marker CD146. Functionally, control-derived EVs significantly suppressed α-SMA expression in TGF-β–stimulated fibroblasts, whereas MF-derived EVs failed to exert this effect, suggesting a loss of regulatory function.ConclusionThe integrated biophysical, molecular, and functional characterization of circulating EVs in MF revealed a distinct signature marked by enlarged vesicle size, altered lipid and protein composition, and reduced ability to counteract a fibrosis-related phenotype. Background

Myelofibrosis (MF) is a rare clonal disorder of hemopoietic stem cells characterized by aberrant proliferation of megakaryocytic and granulocytic lineages, progressive bone marrow fibrosis, and, ultimately, osteosclerosis (Kreipe et al. 2012; Kuter et al. 2007). Clinically, MF manifests with debilitating constitutional symptoms, splenomegaly due to extramedullary hematopoiesis, cytopenia, and shortened survival primarily driven by disease progression and leukemic transformation. MF occurs either as primary (de novo) myelofibrosis (PMF) or as secondary MF evolving from essential thrombocythemia (ET) or polycythemia vera (PV). Among the Philadelphia-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), MF represents the most aggressive form and predominantly affects older adults (Garmezy et al. 2021; Gangat and Tefferi 2020). Approximately two-thirds of MF patients harbor the Janus Kinase 2 (JAK2) V617F mutation, one-quarter carry calreticulin (CALR) mutations; and around 10% with either the myeloproliferative leukemia virus (MPL) mutation or ‘triple negative’ status (Cordua et al. 2019). Constitutive activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway is a hallmark of MF pathogenesis (Garmezy et al. 2021; Gangat and Tefferi 2020; Passamonti and Mora 2023).

Beyond genetic drivers, chronic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a key pathogenic feature of the TME (Luca et al. 2024; Fisher et al. 2021). Dysregulated production and activity of multiple inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokines are associated with MF (Geyer et al. 2015) and correlate with the degree of marrow fibrosis (Tefferi 2000; Le Bousse-Kerdiles and Martyre 1999), systemic symptom burden (Squires 2013), and overall survival (Tefferi and Vainchenker 2011). Persistent chronic inflammation not only provides a selective advantage to the neoplastic clone but also sustains the fibrotic microenvironment (Fleischman et al. 2011; Sollazzo et al. 2016). Bone marrow fibrosis in MF is characterized by extensive deposition of collagen fibers and expansion of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)–positive stromal cells, reflecting a complex interplay between malignant hematopoietic and stromal compartments (Tefferi 2000; Kramann and Schneider 2018).

It is known that, along with inflammatory cytokines, extracellular vesicles (EVs) are part of the inflammatory network (Buzas 2023). EVs are nanosized particles released by virtually all cell types that mediate intercellular communication and contribute to the regulation of tumor and stromal microenvironments (Kalluri and McAndrews 2023). Based on size and biogenesis, they are broadly classified as small EVs (S-EVs, 30–200 nm) and large EVs (L-EVs, 0.2–10 µm) (Thery et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019; Tricarico et al. 2017). Circulating EVs are detectable in various biological fluids, including blood (Nazimek et al. 2015), and their cargo, comprising nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and metabolites, mirrors the physiological or pathological state of their cells of origin (Doyle and Wang 2019). Consequently, EV-based liquid biopsy approaches have advanced our understanding in several key aspects of cancer management (Melo et al. 2015), biomarker discovery (Jansen et al. 2009; Zhao et al. 2019), immunotherapy (Asleh et al. 2023), and response prediction (Jansen et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2020; Irmer et al. 2023).

Recent studies have highlighted the role of EVs in MF pathobiology. Elevated levels of circulating EVs have been observed in the peripheral blood of MF patients (Caivano et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2017), with megakaryocyte-derived EVs correlating with disease aggressiveness (Barone et al. 2019). Moreover, circulating EVs from triple negative MF patients exhibit tumor-promoting properties in vitro (Forte et al. 2021), while monocyte-derived EVs carry pro-inflammatory cytokine cargo (Barone et al. 2020). Despite these findings, the comprehensive molecular characterization of EV cargo in MF and its contribution to disease-specific fibrosis remain largely unexplored. Moreover, although EVs have been implicated in organ and tissue fibrosis (Yang et al. 2024), their specific role in MF-associated fibrotic processes has not been fully elucidated.

To gain a deeper understanding of the role of EVs in MF, we performed an integrated characterization of circulating EVs, combining surface protein and lipidomic profiling with functional assays of fibrotic activity. This approach aimed to elucidate the molecular features and potential pathogenic roles of EVs in MF, while assessing their utility as non-invasive biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Patient recruitment and sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 27 patients diagnosed with MF and 24 age- and sex-matched controls. For each participant, blood was drawn into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated tubes and serum separator tubes (BD Vacutainer®, Becton Dickinson, USA). Patient characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Patients were at diagnosis or at least after 3 months from cytotoxic therapy. Overall, 78% of patients (n = 21) were positive for the JAK2V617F mutation. Fifteen patients were diagnosed as PMF and 8 as post-ET and 4 as post-PV MF. Bone marrow reticulin fibrosis was graded according to WHO guidelines (Swerdlow et al. 2017). Three patients were classified as pre-fibrotic MF and 5 patients had a grade 3 marrow fibrosis. Palpable splenomegaly was observed in 63% of the patients with palpable spleen at a median of 9 cm below the costal margin (range: 3–26 cm). Eight patients had a cytopenic phenotype (Hb < 10 g/dl and/or PLT < 100.000/mmc and/or WBC < 4000/mmc). International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) risk stratification was as follows: Low risk (n = 8, 30%), intermediate-1(n = 9, 33%), Intermediate-2 (n = 7, 26%), high-risk (n = 3, 11%).

The research was approved by the institutional review board of the Area Vasta Emilia Centro (AVEC) Ethical Committee (CE AVEC 1010/2021/Sper/AOUBo). Patients with MF and controls were recruited at IRCCS Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria in Bologna, Ospedale S. Maria delle Croci in Ravenna and IRCCS Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria in Reggio-Emilia. Clinical samples and data were collected after written informed consent was obtained.

Plasma and serum preparation

After discarding the first 2 ml of blood, blood samples were collected and processed within 120 min after withdrawal. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was obtained by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 15 min at room temperature (RT). PRP was then centrifuged at 2500 × g for 15 min and 10.000 × g for 15 min at RT. The platelet-poor plasma was then aliquoted to protein low binding tubes (Eppendorf, Germany) and stored at − 80 °C. In parallel, serum from controls was obtained by centrifugation of whole blood at 1000 × g for 15 min at RT and stored at − 80 °C.

EV isolation from plasma

Plasma samples were thawed at RT, and EV isolation was performed using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), following the manufacturer’s instructions (iZON Science, Oxford, UK) and as previously described (Forte et al. 2023). Briefly, EV isolation was achieved using a qEVoriginal/70 nm Gen 2 SEC column (Izon), followed by ultrafiltration. Prior to sample loading, the column was equilibrated with an adequate volume of 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, 500 µL of processed plasma was carefully loaded onto the top of the column according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following elution with 1 × PBS, four consecutive fractions of 0.4 mL each were collected and pooled, resulting in a total EV-containing volume of 1.6 mL. The pooled EV fractions were then concentrated by ultrafiltration using 30 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) Amicon Ultra-2 centrifugal filters (Millipore, Merck, USA) by centrifugation at 4,000 × g at RT, in order to maximize EV yield. Purified and concentrated EVs were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. All samples were processed using the same standardized isolation workflow, ensuring methodological uniformity across the study.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

Particle enumeration and size distribution were assessed by using the NanoSight LM10 system (NanoSight Ltd.). This technology equipped with a 405 nm laser and NTA 2.3 analytic software. All samples were diluted in ultrafiltered PBS to obtain the optimal particle concentration for NTA measurements (30–120 particles per frame).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the isolated EVs was performed as follows: samples were processed by adding one drop of solution (about 25 µl) on 400 mesh holey film grid; after staining with 2% uranyl acetate (for 2 min), the sample was observed with a Tecnai G2 (FEI) transmission electron microscope operating at 120 kV. Images were captured with a Veleta (Olympus Soft Imaging System) digital camera.

Determination of protein content of EVs

The protein concentrations of isolated EVs were quantified using BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sample were diluted 1:5 in PBS in a final reaction volume of 200 µl. The color was developed within 30 min at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using the Glomax Multidirection System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA).

Western blotting

Protein lysates were normalized and 25 µg total protein per lane were loaded and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‒ polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (NuPAGE™ Bis–Tris Mini Protein Gels, 4–12%, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. As a control, serum of controls and whole cell lysates from the MOLM-13 (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany; ACC-554) and HUVECs (PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany, C12203) cell lines were prepared. Briefly, cells were washed in PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) on ice for 30 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at − 20 °C.

The following antibodies were used against CD9 (1:1000, Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), TSG101 (1:1000, BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA), ALIX (1:1000, Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Calnexin (1:1000, Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Flotillin-1 (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and CD146 (1:500, ABclonal, Massachusetts, USA). Following this, goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse HRP conjugated secondary antibody (System Biosciences) in 1: 10,000 dilution was used. SuperSignalTM West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for detection by a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

EV lipid extraction and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC/MS) analysis

The EV lipidome was quantified using an untargeted lipidomic approach as previously described (Forte et al. 2024). Briefly, samples were prepared by using 50 µL of sample mixed with 1000 µL of MMC solution (MeOH/MTBE/CHCl₃ 1.33:1:1) containing SPLASH lipidomix internal standard (Pellegrino et al. 2014). Lipidomic analysis was performed using an LC–MS/Agilent 6530 QTOF mass spectrometer coupled to an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For the LC, an Ascentis Express ES-C18 column (Supelco, 10 × 2.1 mm, 2 µm particle size, 90 Å pore size) maintained at 55 °C was used. The MS analysis was performed in positive and negative ionization modes. The raw LC–MS data were processed using MS-DIAL 4.9 (Tsugawa et al. 2020) for peak picking, alignment, and annotation. The lipid semi-quantification was based on the internal standard used.

Multiplex bead-based flow cytometry analysis of EV surface proteins

Surface EV protein expression was analyzed using the MACSPlex Exosome Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch-Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay detects 37 membrane surface epitopes using fluorescently labeled beads coated with specific antibodies, along with two isotype controls (mIgG1 and REA) to ensure signal specificity. Briefly, EV concentrations were quantified indirectly by determining protein content, and 4 µg protein of each sample were used for the assays as previously described (Narimanfar et al. 2025). The method was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions with no modifications. Exosomal surface epitope expression was quantified as median APC fluorescence intensity. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was evaluated for each capture bead subsets and backgrounds were corrected by subtracting matched buffer control values. Markers with Median Fluorescent Intensity of the APC signal lower than the respective isotype controls were considered non-detected.

Fibrosis assay

For analysis of potential effects of EV preparations on myofibroblast differentiation (pro-fibrotic activity), the human dermal fibroblast cell line fHDF/TERT166 (Evercyte, Vienna, Austria) was treated with 100 pg/ml Transforming Growth Factor-ß1 (TGF-ß1, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), as described previously (Brancolini et al. 2026). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well Black Microclear plates (Greiner) and kept in serum-reduced (0.5%, Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) DMEM/Ham´s F12 medium (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) for 24 h. Cells were then treated with TGF-ß1 in the presence or the absence of EVs isolated from MF patients and controls at different concentrations (5 × 108 or 1 × 109 EVs/ml) at day 1, 3 and 6. PBS was used as negative and the src family kinase inhibitor PP2 (2 µM, Avantor/VWR, Vienna, Austria) as positive control. 24 h after the last treatment, cells were analyzed for expression of α-SMA using immunofluorescence staining. Therefore, paraformaldehyde-fixed cells (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) were incubated with anti- α-SMA [1A4] antibody (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) followed by incubation with donkey anti-mouse AF594 secondary antibody (1:500, Jackson ImmunoResearch, Baltimore, USA). Fluorescence signal from α-SMA induction was measured on a Spark® multimode microplate reader (Tecan) with an excitation at 580 nm and an emission at 615 nm. Unstimulated, TGF-β stimulated and TGF-β + PP2 stimulated fHDF/TERT166 cells were then washed twice with PBS Ca2 +/Mg2 + and stained for 15 min at RT with Vybrant dye cycle violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:2500 dilution in PBS Ca2 +/Mg2 +, 100 µl/well). The staining solution was removed; cells were rinsed with PBS Ca2 +/Mg2 + and kept light-protected at 4 °C until imaging and automatic-cell counting with an EVOS M5000 microscope.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The median and range of each treatment group were calculated for all experiments. Data were analyzed with Graphpad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were statistically analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test to compare the two groups. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC) were generated and calculated to evaluate the discriminative ability of each marker. Lipid statistical analysis, including differential analysis and functional evaluation, was performed using LipidOne (Alabed et al. 2024). The data matrix (including lipidomic, clinical, and protein data) was analyzed using MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) with normalization "by median" and "autoscaling". The Correlation function in MetaboAnalyst was applied to the normalized data using Spearman correlation. Correlations were filtered with a p-value < 0.05. P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are indicated in the graphs as reported by the analysis software: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Plasma-derived EVs from MF patients exhibit increased size

To characterize circulating EVs in MF, EVs were isolated from platelet-poor plasma of MF patients and age- and sex-matched controls using SEC and characterized in accordance with MISEV 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024). Western blot analysis confirmed enrichment for EVs, as evidenced by the presence of the canonical EV-associated proteins TSG101, ALIX, and the tetraspanin CD9, while the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin was undetectable, excluding contamination from cellular debris (Fig. 1A). TEM revealed the characteristic cup-shaped, lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles, consistent with small EV morphology (Fig. 1B). NTA demonstrated that EVs from MF patients exhibited a significantly larger median size (113.4 nm; range 71.3–130.9 nm) compared with those from controls (100.05 nm; range 72.6–123 nm; P < 0.02), while EV concentrations remained comparable between groups (Fig. 1C, D). These findings confirm that plasma-derived EVs from MF patients display the defining structural characteristics and size range of EVs; however, they exhibit a distinct increase in vesicle size compared with controls.

A distinct lipidomic profile characterizes the MF EVs

To determine whether circulating EVs from MF patients exhibit distinct molecular cargo, we performed untargeted lipidomic profiling of plasma-derived EVs from MF patients and controls. A total of 136 lipid species across 12 lipid classes were annotated and semi-quantified (see the Supplementary Table 2 for the entire lipidomic dataset). Compared with controls, MF-derived EVs displayed a significant enrichment in diacylglycerols (DGs), free fatty acids (FAs), and ether-linked forms of phosphatidylcholine (PC-O) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), whereas sphingomyelins (SMs) were markedly reduced (Fig. 2A). A more in-depth analysis at the molecular species level allowed for a sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) revealing a clear separation between MF and controls samples (Fig. 2B). To identify the lipid species driving this discrimination, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis highlighted 15 lipid species with the strongest contribution to group separation. Notably, SM 16:1;O2_16:0, SM 16:1;O2_22:0, SM 18:1;O2_24:0, and SM 18:1;O2_22:0 were significantly decreased in MF-EVs, while PC-O 18:0_20:4, PC-P 20:0_20:3, PC-O 16:0_20:4, and SM 16:1;O2_18:1 were increased (Fig. 2C). The Heat-map analysis of hierarchical clustering of EV lipid species displays the top 30 lipid species differentially expressed in EVs from MF patients and Controls (Fig. 2D). In addition, volcano plot shows lipids compounds that differ significantly between the two groups according to FC > 0.5 and P < 0.05 in positive and negative mode (Fig. 2E). ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of individual lipid species, as expressed by the AUC. Among the sphingomyelins, SM 16:1;O2_16:0 showed the highest performance (AUC = 0.84, P = 0.001), followed by SM 16:1;O2_22:0 (AUC = 0.80, P < 0.004). Among the phosphatidylcholines, PC-O 18:0_20:4 and PC-P 20:0_20:3 exhibited the strongest discriminative power (AUC = 0.96, P < 0.0001 and P < 0.001, respectively), followed by PC-O 16:0_20:4 (AUC = 0.90, P < 0.001) (Data not shown).

Although the overall acyl-chain distribution was similar between groups, MF-EVs showed a significant enrichment in species containing acyl chains with four double bonds, indicating greater lipid unsaturation (Fig. 3A). Taken together, these data demonstrate that circulating EVs from MF patients possess a distinct lipidomic fingerprint, characterized by sphingomyelin depletion and accumulation of ether phospholipids and polyunsaturated species, suggesting disease-associated remodeling of EV membrane composition and biophysical properties.

Multiplex surface protein profiling uncovers loss of CD146 EV subsets in MF

Given that EV-mediated communication is driven by both lipid and protein cargo, we next profiled the surface protein landscape of plasma-derived EVs from MF patients and controls. To this end, we employed a bead-based flow cytometry assay to characterize EV surface markers and explore their potential relevance in MF pathophysiology. Among the 37 surface proteins, 33 were detected above the detection threshold in EVs from MF patients and controls. CD3, CD56, CD25 and CD142 markers were not detected on EVs from either group. Several markers were consistently expressed in both groups, reflecting the heterogeneous cellular origins of circulating EVs. In particular, strong signals were observed for platelet-derived markers (CD62P, CD41b, CD42a), immune-related markers (CD24, HLA-DRDPDQ, HLA-ABC, CD8), and the endothelial and perivascular mesenchymal stromal cells marker CD105 (Fig. 4A). Meantime, both cohorts exhibited robust expression of canonical EV markers CD9, CD63, and CD81, with a trend toward higher expression in MF-derived EVs; however, the wide inter-patient variability precluded statistical significance (Fig. 4B). Comparative analysis revealed that two markers, ROR1 and CD146, were significantly reduced in MF-derived EVs compared with controls (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A).

Specifically, as shown in Fig. 4C, CD146 surface marker expression was significantly lower in EVs from MF patients relative to controls. EV preparations from MF patients and controls were analyzed to evaluate CD146 expression, with CD9 and Flotillin-1 used as EV markers and HUVEC lysate serving as a positive control for CD146.

Overall, these data demonstrate that circulating EVs from MF patients display a distinctive surface protein profile, characterized by reduced abundance of CD146- and ROR1-positive vesicles. The endothelial and perivascular mesenchymal stromal marker CD146, identified as the most significant molecular surface protein, was used for the multivariate ROC analysis and showed a promising area under the curve (AUC = 0.84) (Fig. 4E).

Correlation patterns in MF EVs reveal disease-associated biomarker candidates

We next investigated whether specific lipid species were associated with clinical features of MF patients and with protein markers expressed on MF-derived EVs.

Discussion

Here we aimed to characterize circulating EVs from patients with MF in vitro, with the goals of identifying clinically relevant biomarkers, assessing their potential utility as a liquid biopsy tool, and elucidating disease-related mechanisms. This study provides a comprehensive molecular and functional characterization of circulating EVs in MF, uncovering a distinct EV signature that integrates structural, lipidomic, proteomic, and clinical dimensions. Through multi-omics profiling and functional assays, we demonstrate that EVs from MF patients are not merely disease by-products but active participants in disease pathogenesis and fibrotic progression.

Consistent with the emerging role of EVs in hematologic malignancies, we showed that plasma-derived EVs from MF patients retain canonical EV markers (CD9, TSG101, and ALIX) while exhibiting a significant increase in size compared with control counterparts. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, both hallmarks of the disease, are known to influence vesicle formation and membrane composition (Kobiela et al. 2023) and may therefore account for the increased heterogeneity of EVs observed in our study. Notably, though the increase in EV size observed in MF patients compared with controls is modest, it may still be biologically relevant. Small shifts toward larger EV diameters can reflect disease-associated changes in vesicle biogenesis, membrane composition, or cargo loading driven by chronic inflammation. Such changes may influence EV stability, uptake, and functional interactions within the bone marrow niche.

Our lipidomic profiling provides novel insights into the metabolic remodeling underlying MF-derived EVs. Compared with controls, MF EVs displayed a clear lipid signature characterized by reduced SM content and enrichment in DG, PC-O, and PE species, along with increased acyl chain unsaturation. This lipid remodeling points toward membrane fluidity and oxidative stress, consistent with enhanced vesicle fusion potential and altered cellular signaling. SM depletion may have profound consequences for vesicle curvature and raft organization, potentially impairing EV stability and uptake (Verderio et al. 2018; Shah et al. 2026). Notably, an escalating number of studies reviewed by Liu et al. (Liu et al. 2024) have evinced that sphingolipid play a crucial role in tissue fibrosis. Consistent with our data, decreased amount of sphingolipids has been associated with alcohol-related liver fibrosis and cystic fibrosis (Thiele et al. 2023; Bal Topcu et al. 2023). In addition, PE and SM are key components of the phospholipid bilayer and play a crucial role in membrane stability and integrity (Vance and Tasseva 2013; Ramstedt and Slotte 2002). A change in their content could indicate a destabilization of the membrane or a change in the cell signaling processes of EVs. Together, these findings highlight EV lipid remodeling as a key feature of the MF microenvironment, providing new molecular clues into the altered metabolic and inflammatory state of the disease.

We also observed an enrichment of PC-O in EVs from MF patients. Recent work has established that these ether lipids are a hallmark of EVs (Fyfe et al. 2023), and their elevation here points to a potential EV-specific signature in MF. These lipids constitute a class of bioactive molecules involved in multiple cellular processes, not only structural but also related to signaling, oxidative stress and inflammatory response (Dean and Lodhi 2018). Ether phospholipids have been linked to oxidative stress resistance and may reflect adaptive remodeling of parental cells exposed to chronic inflammatory and fibrotic signaling. Consistently, an increased degree of unsaturation (i.e., a greater number of double bonds) and longer chain are associated with more fluid and dynamic membrane properties, potentially impacting the stability, functionality, and cellular interactions of EVs. Furthermore, a high degree of unsaturation in EV lipids may modulate key inflammatory pathways, thereby influencing pathological signaling in recipient cells (Baccouch et al. 2023; Holopainen et al. 2019). Surface protein profiling further delineated a distinct MF-EV phenotype. Notably, MF-EVs showed reduced abundance of CD146, a molecule involved in vascular/stromal and endothelial niche (Crisan et al. 2008), and inflammation regulation (Shih 1999), and ROR1, a tumor-associated receptor implicated in hematopoietic and stromal signaling (Congrains et al. 2021). This coordinated depletion suggests a disruption of endothelial, mesenchymal stromal, and neoplastic communication networks in MF.

Given that endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of MF pathophysiology, the absence of CD146-positive EVs may reflect impaired vascular homeostasis and/or a shift in the cellular contributors to the circulating EV pool. While CD146 overexpression has been linked to tumor progression in solid malignancies (Oka et al. 2012; Wang and Yan 2013), evidence from the bone marrow microenvironment indicates that reduced CD146 expression in mesenchymal stromal cells is associated with endosteal niche localization, acquisition of a pro-fibrotic phenotype, and promotion of MF pathology (Tormin et al. 2011). Importantly, CD146 is not endothelial-restricted markers and may also reflect altered activation states of perivascular mesenchymal stromal cells (Crisan et al. 2008). Similarly, ROR1 showed reduced expression in MF EVs. This observation aligns with previous studies in hematologic malignancies such as acute leukemia and chronic lymphoid leukemia, where reduced ROR1 levels in EVs were consistently reported using bead-based flow cytometry (Forte et al. 2024; Li et al. 2022).

Importantly, we identified clear correlations between specific EV lipid species and clinical features, bridging molecular alterations to disease manifestations. Lipid species such as PC16:0_16:0, PC18:0_20:4, and PC18:0_22:5 showed positive associations with splenomegaly and marrow fibrosis, suggesting that lipid remodeling parallels disease burden and stromal remodeling. Moreover, the enrichment of PC18:0_20:4 and PE P-18:0_20:4 in EVs from secondary MF patients suggests that lipidomic profiles may discriminate between disease subtypes and reflect disease evolution from ET or PV. These findings highlight the potential of EV lipidomics as a minimally invasive tool for disease stratification and monitoring.

At the molecular level, our correlation analyses reveal close interconnections between EV lipid species and canonical EV surface markers. We identified 14 lipid species that significantly correlated with tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81, with consistent patterns across Cer, SM, and PC classes. Notably, five lipid species (Cer 18:1;1;O2_24:0, LPC-O-18:1, PC 18:0_20:4, SM 16:1;O2_16:0, and SM 18:1;O2_24:0) were correlated with all three tetraspanins, suggesting that these lipids may play structural or regulatory roles in EV formation and identity. The inverse correlations between sphingomyelin and ceramide species and tetraspanin expression imply that membrane composition directly influences tetraspanin clustering and EV topology. Conversely, positive correlations between LPC-O and PC-O species and EV markers may reflect membrane remodeling that favors tetraspanin-enriched microdomain stability. These lipid–protein associations provide mechanistic insights into how altered lipid metabolism in MF might reprogram EV assembly, cargo loading, and intercellular signaling.

Functionally, we provide the first evidence that EVs from MF patients lose the anti-fibrotic capacity observed in EVs from controls. EVs are known to play roles in cancer-related inflammation and in modulating fibrotic activity (Brigstock 2021). However, a key unanswered question is whether, and how, circulating EVs contribute to fibrotic remodeling in MF. Our study provides functional evidence that MF EVs may indeed participate in the generation and/or maintenance of fibrosis. While control-derived EVs suppressed α-SMA expression in TGF-β–stimulated fibroblasts, MF EVs failed to exert this regulatory effect, indicating a functional reprogramming toward a pro-fibrotic phenotype. This loss of anti-fibrotic signaling likely contributes to the fibrotic remodeling of the bone marrow niche, as EVs circulate between hematopoietic, endothelial, and stromal compartments. Consistently, lipid and protein biomarkers such as SM and CD146, which in this study are significantly reduced in MF EVs compared with control counterparts, have been previously associated with tissue fibrosis (Thiele et al. 2023; Tormin et al. 2011; Shea and Tager 2012). The observed correlation between fibrosis severity and specific lipid alterations, particularly in polyunsaturated PC species, further supports the hypothesis that EV lipid composition is not merely a biomarker but a mechanistic driver of fibrogenesis. However, we acknowledge that the current data remains hypothesis-generating in nature; further validation through “rescue” experiments is required to confirm whether Sphingomyelin depletion directly impairs the fibroblast fibrosis activity.

Together, these findings establish a mechanistic link between EV lipid composition, surface protein identity, and fibrotic activity in MF. The integrated lipidomic–proteomic correlations suggest that EV membrane composition governs cargo sorting and biological function. The observed clinical associations further strengthen the case for EV lipidomics as a liquid biopsy tool for disease assessment. Beyond diagnostic applications, targeting the pathways that regulate EV lipid remodeling, such as sphingomyelinase activity or ether lipid biosynthesis, may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to restore EV homeostasis and limit fibrosis.

Our study has several limitations. Given the rarity of MF, the cohort size was necessarily modest, limiting statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses, and reducing the robustness of clinical correlations. Accordingly, the clinical associations reported here should be considered exploratory until validated in larger, independent patient cohorts. Importantly, the correlations observed between EV surface proteins and lipid species reflect trends at the bulk EV population level rather than direct molecular co-localization within individual vesicles; therefore, they should be interpreted cautiously. Consequently, these correlations cannot establish whether specific proteins and lipid species are present on the same EVs. More refined approaches, such as affinity-based EV isolation combined with targeted lipidomics, will be required to resolve direct protein–lipid relationships and further validate these observations. Future studies integrating cell-specific EV tracing, single-vesicle profiling, and longitudinal patient sampling will help confirm these findings and better define their relevance for disease evolution and therapeutic response. Finally, we deliberately focused on primary human-derived samples to ensure that our findings reflect the cellular crosstalk and the unique inflammatory milieu of the human MF niche—features that are often difficult to fully recapitulate in in vivo or in vitro systems. Nevertheless, further functional studies in established MPN models (e.g. everthelJAK2V617F mice) will be a valuable next step to explore the specific pro-fibrotic or pro-inflammatory roles of these EVs.

Conclusion

This study provides insights into the role and cargo complexity of circulating EVs in MF, revealing coordinated and disease biology-related alterations in their protein and lipid composition alongside fibrosis-related functional abnormalities. These changes, detectable through peripheral blood, suggest the potential to reduce reliance on invasive bone marrow biopsies in MF. By integrating EV-associated protein and lipid signatures, we outline a novel, minimally invasive liquid biopsy framework for MF monitoring. This combinatorial approach may enhance the specificity and sensitivity of EV-based readouts and generate testable hypotheses regarding disease-associated molecular pathways and potential therapeutic targets.

Importantly, these findings advance our understanding of EV-mediated mechanisms and warrant validation in larger, well-defined patient cohorts to confirm their reproducibility and assess their full clinical relevance.
