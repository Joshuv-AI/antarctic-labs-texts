Hybrid model for classifying Indo Aryan and Tamil texts from historic manuscripts
Authors: P. M. Dinesh, Dhanalakshmi Gopal, S. Vignesh, S. Sheeba Rani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ancient manuscripts, especially Indo-Aryan and Tamil texts have the complex linguistic structure in their manuscripts with historical differences. The present paper is a BERT-Li Scribe Hybrid Model of the historic Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscript classification. The model combines the contextual embedding of BERT, which learns the semantic links in the text, and LiScribe, a specialized sequence model that learns features, and linguistic patterns of Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts at a character level. The sample of 1,055 manuscripts of the Library of Congress and University of Hamburg is a perfect combination of Indo-Aryan and Tamil texts. The model that is offered provides the classification of two large groups, such as Indo-Aryan (Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati) and Tamil, which guarantees the level of classification on a family level and specific scripts. Training is performed using categorical cross-entropy loss and Adam optimizer with learning rate scheduling with dropout layers used to avoid overfitting with noisy historical data. The model, which was coded in Python and deployed with the help of such libraries as TensorFlow and PyTorch, demonstrated a high overall classification accuracy of 97.61%, being able to distinguish between the Indo-Aryan and Tamil texts at the same time. The attention mechanism also increases the concentration on the important features by the model even in the degraded manuscripts. This mixed methodology proves the usefulness of the combination of deep learning and linguistic feature extraction to the correct classification of historical manuscripts.

Introduction

Manuscripts are invaluable historical resources, offering scholars, historians, and readers a direct glimpse into the lives, cultures, and ideas of earlier times12. These manuscripts were written on various materials, such as papyrus, parchment, vellum and early papers that dated from the ancient to medieval ages which includes various themes from religion, philosophy science, literature, law and practical life34. They are important source of information regarding the socio-political system of the societies, evolution of language and literature and the growth of knowledge systems. Historic Manuscripts have significance in History, literary books, artwork, linguistics, and other aspects. As words and products of material history, manuscripts are not only texts and but are also imbued with the physical history of the technologies that made them56.

Perhaps, the most interesting aspect of the manuscripts of historical value is that they are conveyors of knowledge that has always followed the growth of societies and cultures, their accomplishments and values. Before the printing press, manuscripts were usually hand written which means that every copy created was a completely separate artifact7. Often this rendered them unique, including marginal notes, illustrations, bindings, and all down to the material upon which they were written. These manuscripts were mainly employed in learning, research as academic as well as scholarly tools, in religious and legal writing, and documentation and as central in defining and advancing social and intellectual thought systems8. Some of the greatest manuscripts that have come down from antiquity and the Middle Ages were produced in monastic scriptoria; the labour was done by monks who could spend years copying texts and under frequently trying circumstances. These manuscripts are of great historic value since they contain some of the oldest copies of the bibles, Greek and Roman classics, scientific works and religious tracts and other historic documents9. They also provide a wealth of data regarding the orality of the knowledge transfer in preindustrial societies, the development of the scripts, and the actual linguistic changes for centuries.

Historic manuscripts also contain indications about the historical development of languages and scripts. Manuscripts in Latin and Greek highlight changes from majuscule, or capital, writing to minuscule or lowercase writing that facilitated faster writing and the legibility of more text in a given area10. During the early medieval era, the Carolingian minuscule script made writing readable and consistent across most part of Europe with further improving the script to ease the process of copying texts. Script style differs from region to region; hence manuscripts give palaeographers the opportunity to know where the text started and the various regions or culture it has gone through11. There is also much that can be learned about how knowledge was spread and scribes and scholars worked to keep texts alive and passed on. However, in medieval Europe the monasteries and universities were the main source of education where scribes were used in writing processes and what was produced was manually handwritten. These copying monopolies was highly susceptible to error and alteration or outright addition, and such characteristics can be of benefit to the present-day scholars, particularly with regard to the social setting in which the copies were made. In the Islamic world, sometimes scholars paraphrased certain texts and then wrote marginal notes, or glosses, alongside new commentary notes which were then included in the new copies. Thanks to this phenomenon of marginalia—writing in the margins, new scholars are able to feedback the changes in thought and its reception over the course of time12.

In Asia, manuscript traditions developed in different lines. Paper was invented in China at the early centuries of the Common Era, which enhanced manuscript production also leading to a growth in literature science and arts. When it came to making manuscripts, the Chinese did so in scrolls, and not only could a scroll contain the written word but also the stroke work and figurative art as well13. Likewise, in India the religious and philosophical works were originally recorded in palm leaf and this went on for many centuries. The example from the past may be the Buddhist sutras in which the texts were handwritten; manuscripts were the tools that Buddhism used to spread across Asia. Another change that came into the society was with the introduction of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg in mid-fifteenth century which lead to the decline of the use of manuscripts prepared by scribes. The printing press revolutionized the mass production of books and therefore made knowledge more available in the society mostly freeing the society from having to produce manuscripts most of the time14. But when printing press came into existence, manuscripts were still written for rituals and aesthetics more over religious purposes. Thus, illuminated manuscript continued to be an elite kind of art and received many commissions even in the period of the Renaissance. To the modern scholar, historic manuscripts are not only documents and ideas but also valuable for both cultural and artistic significance. Most are located in libraries, museums, and personal possession, and the citizens have the responsibility to preserve and research them. Technological developments enabled the conversion of manuscripts into digital forms, and therefore people have an opportunity to find them on the Internet. However, confronting the received ideas about print culture with the tangible evidence provided by manuscripts is still essential for studying the materials, techniques, and circumstances of their making15. Indo-Aryan and Tamil are two literary and cultural genres originating from historic manuscripts that are the most important in South Asia. These manuscripts contain information on the history and origins, philosophy, religion, literature and lifestyle in the regions of their origin16. Indo-Aryan languages are derived from Sanskrit and developed into a set of regional languages such as Hindi, Bengali or Marathi, Tamil on the other hand is a part of the Dravidian language family and has historical continuity with a script and literature traditions which is different from that of the Indo-Aryan languages. The descendants of the Vedic Sanskrit, the Indo-Aryan languages are possessed with an ancient history of manuscripts. The first documents belonging to this tradition are the Vedic texts dating to around 1500 BC17. They were passed from generation to generation in an oral form, and the existing scripts of the Vedas go back to medieval times on birch bark or palm leaves. Tamil occupies the unique status of being one of the oldest surviving languages globally; it features an extensive and vivid manuscript tradition that dates back more than two thousand years1819.

Unlike the Indo Aryan texts which have used Sanskrit, Tamil literature evolved in the Dravidian language family on its own. Indeed, Tamil manuscripts are an important source to learn about the cultural, religious, and literary history of South India and Sri Lanka. Being uniquely credible, Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts from handwritten manuscripts are indispensable to learn South Asian cultural and intellectual heritages. It is immaterial whether it is the Sanskrit epics and the Vedic scripts of the Indo-Aryan tradition or the Tamil Sangam poetry and the Bhakti hymn these manuscripts offer a window to past. These texts should be preserved and studied and should also be digitized so that people of the future generation can also read these great traditions that have already influenced the SI literature and religion for thousands of years. Traditional approaches for text classification struggle with the complexity of these manuscripts, which often exhibit noise, script variations, and archaic language forms. To address these challenges, we propose the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model, which combines the strengths of deep learning with traditional linguistic models. BERT, a powerful contextual language model, provides a robust mechanism for understanding the relationships between words, while LiScribe extracts character-level features that are essential for handling the unique properties of historic Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts.

Related works

It is rather difficult to find certain writers in manuscripts and it is a crucial issue for the analysis of authorship of certain historical texts. A novel deep learning solution was presented for the biometric identification of writers within such texts using data-driven features and neural networks. In contrast to previous conventional practices that involved hand selecting the features of the handwritten historical documents, this approach learns the features on its own that separate the two classes20. The presented method functions systematically that involve manuscript pre-processing followed by image denoising and binarization by adopting complex techniques. Key-point identification is then performed automatically by utilizing Harris corner detection and clustering to further segregate the regions of interest. The last approach is using transfer learning for classification of features derived from document patches and then combining them in a way that will increase accuracy through majority vote method. Through ICDAR 201721 experimental results, the effectiveness and stability of the framework, especially in the case of small amounts of training data, which indicates the application of historical manuscript authorship attribution.

Identification of writers in historical manuscripts is still a difficult process that is widely used in various occupations, including palaeography or forensic document examination. In this research, the primary approach of distinguishing between different writers of historical documents is through the use of texture-based features. The proposed approach utilizes two specific features: oriented Basic Image Features (oBIF) and hinge features that describe the structure of text written by hand22. A new matching method for these features based on moments is then proposed to minimize a measure of similarity. This technique makes it possible to match writing samples with the writer with lots of accuracy. The evaluation of the proposed moment distance-based approach on the International Conference on Document Analysis and Recognition (ICDAR 2017)21 dataset is illustrated by the outcomes of a series of exhaustive experiments, which testify to the high efficiency of the applied solution. This work also extends the previous approaches for solving the authorship of historical manuscripts and offers a helpful tool for palaeographers and researchers in engineering systematic approaches towards historical manuscripts.

Computerization of scripts in historical documents is a crucial assignment for historians who want to determine the backgrounds of manuscripts from prehistoric culture. The authors introduce an advanced feature selection deep learning system for improving script classification accuracy. Starting with the dataset of Competitions on Classification of Latin Medieval Manuscripts (CLaMM)23, the materials are subjected to denoising and binarization to pre-process the manuscript images as part of the evaluated methodology24. Harris corner detection and k-means clustering are then applied in order to locate important regions in the manuscripts and to exclude unrelated areas that is patches, from training. These patches are indeed classified as before using convolutional neural networks (CNN), however, a majority vote mechanism is used in an attempt to better improve the document-level script classification process. It proved helpful in differentiating between one script and the next, with the system recording an 89.2% accuracy level. This research is very useful to the field of history in that it provides an efficient and accurate method of script identification so that historians can continue their study of old manuscripts.

Credible dating of manuscripts is an important factor to determine the context of the past events and historical memory. Thus, the study of deep learning and specifically vision transformers reveal new opportunities to improve the dating of manuscripts. Therefore, this work examines the possibilities of vision transformers in historical manuscript analysis while providing a complex framework for dating manuscripts. Some preliminary steps such as noise elimination and binarization of the information are applied aiming at making the data clear in the subsequent detection stages, the feature detection is done using Harris Corner detection method and the last step is clustering25. The system is specifically tailored for finding relevant patches, which enrich the dataset and improve the quality of models. The pre-trained deep learning model of transfer learning is used to identify a manuscript’s date of creation. The effectiveness of the system is confirmed on medieval paleographical scale (MPS)26 historical document data in terms of Mean Absolute Error (MAE), achieving an error of 3.97 years at the document-level analysis. This work brings a breakthrough in donate dating of manuscripts and reveals the possibility of using vision transformers in this field.

The identification of script in historical manuscripts is crucial in analysis with regard to historical contexts and stories. This work proposes an approach of developing an automated system for the identification of the script of historical documents using deep learning approach on CLaMM dataset23. Some of the techniques carried out on the manifold of images include denoising and binarization to fit the manuscript images into the subsequent processing. Both the Harris corner detector for key point detection and k-means clustering for grouping the points take place to give out important features27. Such features are extracted in a patch-based scheme and the patch is classified using a combination of CNN and a more novel architecture known as the Visual Transformer. The comparison of two architectures shows that the vision transformers surpass CNNs obtaining the accuracy of 91.99%. This work advances the field by proposing vision transformers for photo-chemical manuscript assessment; for script identification, this methodology outperforms existing approaches and paves the way for augmenting history document conservation and analysis.

Methodology

The methodology for this study involves the development of a BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model to classify Indo-Aryan and Tamil texts from historical manuscripts. The process begins with dataset collection, including 1,055 manuscripts sourced from the Library of Congress and University of Hamburg, followed by text extraction using OCR tools and manual transcription where necessary. Preprocessing steps like tokenization, noise removal, and normalization are applied to prepare the data. The model combines BERT’s contextual embeddings and LiScribe’s linguistic feature extraction to capture both high-level semantic and script-specific details.

Dataset collection

The BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model include 1,055 manuscripts from both Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscripts collections. This dataset contains sufficient information to train a strong classification model that can easily partition texts from these two different language families. The Indo-Aryan manuscripts, sourced from the "South Asian Manuscripts: Indo-Aryan Languages” section of the collections at LOC, encompass such languages as Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, and Gujarati, which are written with scripts like Devanagari, Bengali and Gujarati, respectively28. Such texts are as wide as the religious writing, literature and law, and they depict the Indo-Aryan language family versatility in terms of language and culture. Tamil manuscripts from the University of Hamburg collection depicting the classical Tamil language as a tradition of literary and religious writings is added29. These scripts written in Dravidian script which is completely different than Indo-Aryan in terms of accent and script from the textual side they provide us over view of Dravidian language family. The use of both Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscripts guarantees a diverse dataset containing a wide range of languages in terms of their dialect, style, and scripts, which allows the model to learn from the variations. Text extraction

The first stage of data preprocessing is involved in text acquisition where OCR tools employed for converting the manuscripts to machine-readable form. OCR tools are capable of recognizing a number of scripts of South Asian area of which are applicable for many of the Indic scripts like Devanagari for Hindi and Marathi, Bengali and Tamil scripts. Since a large number of these manuscripts were historical in nature and as the years passed, these manuscripts faced the issue of degradation, certain errors, such as misrecognized characters, some text portions, which were unclear, or improper formatting were introduced during the OCR process. For the cases where OCR failed to generate high quality outputs, only transcription of certain passages was necessary in order to achieve high levels of accuracy. This step was particularly important for such scripts as Devanagari, Bengali, Gujarati, Oriya etc. because classical inscriptions employ scripts that are different from the modern or even earlier forms of the modern scripts in many ways.

Data cleaning

Following text extraction was data cleaning where the concern was on eliminating other noise, special characters and other artifacts is brought about by the OCR process. Most frequent OCR mistakes include misidentification of characters; this was more evident where one script resembled the other. For instance, the Devanagari script resembled the Bengali script; other causes of mistakes included faded and damaged print. New line codes, excessive space along with other unwanted characters were also removed so that the text was free from any unwanted stuff. Further, text normalization was conducted in order to eliminate several spelling and punctuation mistakes due to future text sorting which varied in historical literature. Normalization facilitated comparability especially with regard to samples that contained multiple versions of a language or a dialect. The aim was to provide the texts at most clean and uniform as possible being, however, important to avoid the loss of semantics and morphology in the source texts which was critical during model training. Tokenization

After cleaning the data, the text undergoes tokenization in which the text is divided into tokens or sub-units of text. In this case, the sub word tokenization was adopted for this work; for instance, the Word Piece tokenization that is mostly used in BERT. This form of tokenization was especially useful for dealing with difficult languages and scripts as breaking words up into segments helped maintain meaningful subword particles to in managing the vocabulary. This was particularly helpful for South Asian languages since there are certain affixes and compound words which are frequently used in these languages and subword tokenization more closely mirrors the actual structure of words than other forms of tokenization. By dividing the manuscripts into smaller sections and taking tokens as input, the model could consume the manuscripts while selecting an optimal size of the feeding tokens that would not distort the context and semantic of the manuscripts. This was a critical step for both, the IA as well as the TI, since the scripts in both the cases are so rich and complex. Model architecture design: BERT-LiScribe hybrid model

Due to these different and complex features of the historical manuscripts, the architecture for the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model is developed in a way that is capable of handling them. This two-part framework incorporates BERT, one of the most advanced contextual language models, and LiScribe, a sequence model that is developed to better understand the distinct features of these manuscripts. Through these two components, the model is able to overcome problems related to historic language differences, noisy manuscripts, and other complexities due to different scripts.

BERT component

The BERT component of the model employs the BERT multilingual model (mBERT) which is perfectly designed to understand multiple languages including the languages falling within the Indo Aryan family and the Tamil family. Fine-tuning mBERT on the manuscript dataset guarantees that the model adapts to various features of these old texts for instance, change in spelling, grammar and syntax. Unlike several other machine learning models, BERT creates contextual embeddings which define further into the relations between the words, making it an eminent model for historical resentments tied to the language. These embeddings enable the model to understand context-dependent meanings of words, even if they were used in different forms or referred to some archaic historical epoch.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} represent an input sequence (a text or a sentence from the manuscript) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{BERT}\left(x\right)$$\end{document} represent the function that generates BERT contextual embeddings from the input text. The output of the BERT component is a set of contextual embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{BERT}:$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{BERT}={f}_{BERT}\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

In our experiment, we operate the multilingual BERT (mBERT) base model that produces dimensionality 768 embeddings. These embeddings represent the contextual and semantic linkages among words in the manuscript text.

Moreover, the positional encoding in BERT makes the information of the manuscripts’ word order or structure remain intact. This is especially required during the processing of the texts which are historical since word order may greatly influence meaning. Positional encoding assists in preserving relationships between words and thus the current and historical structures of the manuscripts’ sentences are properly interpreted.

LiScribe component

The LiScribe component is built from the ground up as a sequence model that is fine-tuned on the Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts. While BERT seems to capture the overall correlation between the surrounding words and the target word, LiScribe pays attention to the nuance of the languages that learn character-level features and higher-level syntactic and morphological patters in these writing systems. For detection of graphemes, phonemes, syllabic structures and script variations LiScribe uses n-gram, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and Conditional Random Fields (CRFs). These methods enable LiScribe to identify frequent patterns and phonetic features which are especially important in historical works containing regional or rather old forms of the language.

The LiScribe component extracts specific linguistic and script features, such as graphemes, phonemes, and syllabic structures. The LiScribe element picks off particular linguistic and written elements like graphemes, phonemes, syllabic structures. The result is a 512-dimensional feature vector, tuned to strike between detailed script-specific representation and computation efficiency.

However, LiScribe employs the use of language-related rules that assist in identifying signs from the ancient forms of the scripts like Old Tamil or Archaic Devanagari. These rules are necessary to filter out manuscript texts which could be spelled, grammatically or script-styled differently from the modern craftsmanship, so that the model of classification could understand them properly. This capability is particularly useful in identifying manuscripts in languages that are very close in the Indo Aryan group or in different periods of Tamil manuscripts.

LiScribe is also specially tailored to process the character level complexity and morpho-phonemic regularities of Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts, particularly as in older manuscripts. On the inside, LiScribe uses a three-layered sequence model architecture, including a character embedding layer, a come-back GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) and a linguistic decoder module that has incorporated Conditional Random Fields (CRF).

The first stage involves embedding each character in input sequence into a dense vector representation by a character-level trainable embedding matrix. This embedding uses sub-word morphology like ligatures, affixes and glyph variations in the script. We use to denote character sequence in a manuscript where characters are learned to an embedding. In the second step, the embedded sequence is unfolded through a Bi-GRU layer that allows modeling forward and backward dependencies between characters. This bi-directionality plays a very important role when modeling agglutinative suffixes in Tamil and the Sandhi constructions within Indo-Aryan languages. GRU model is the good alternative to LSTM due to fewer parameters and relatively the same temporal modeling abilities. The concatenated hidden states are then used in the last step: a CRF decoder uses probabilistic transitions between character classes for learning structural dependencies. This enables LiScribe to give graphemes, phonemes and syllable boundaries high-confidence labels in either noisy or ambiguous environments. During training, the CRF loss promotes the best label sequences and, therefore, robust to irregular spacing, poor-quality characters, or archaic orthographic variants. LiScribe integrates dense embeddings, recurrent modeling and structured prediction to learn a high-fidelity representation of the script features both dimensions of time and language. Vectorizing provides these outputs, which are then merged with the semantic embeddings of BERT to make a final classification.

Training and optimization

The fine-tuning and computational optimization of the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model are the critical steps that enable the model to identify the Indo-Aryan as well as the Tamil written from the historical manuscript. It is the process of selection of training dataset, right loss function, optimization methods and regularization to maximize the model accuracy without significant overfitting and bias.

Training dataset

To train the model effectively, the entire dataset is divided into three distinct subsets: they are known as training set, validation set, and test set. Training set which is formed 70% of the data to build the model and make refinements on the internal parameters of the model based on the output error of each iteration. The validation set (15%) is used for adjusting of hyperparameters and monitoring of the model’s performance during training process in order to predict the overfitting and improve the model’s parameters. Lastly, the test set (15%) used to evaluate the success of the trained model to perform well when tested on unseen data. While developing these subsets, it is always important to ensure that a fair amount of attention has been paid to the Indic and Dravidian traditions. Such characteristics means that if there is an imbalance in the distribution, the training will be prejudiced, and the model will tend to favor one language family to the detriment of the other. This balance also helps to exercise the model responses to many scripts, dialects and texts from both the Indo-Aryan and Tamil sources, making the model better and with more generalization.

Loss function

For this task, a Categorical Cross Entropy loss function is used since the aim is to do multiclass classification. The cross-entropy loss is used as a way of quantifying the distance between the probability distribution of the texts and the actual language. This loss function is typically used in classification where the model must output a probability of more than one classes, for example Hindi, Bengali, Tamil and other languages in this case. By trying to minimize this loss function, the model will make better predictions of language class because it means the function wants the model to have a high probability at the correct set of outputs and low probability at the incorrect set of outputs.

Optimizer

The Adam optimizer was used during back propagation to update the weights in the model. Adam is another optimization algorithm used in deep learning because it is efficient for providing appropriate learning rates for each parameter based on first and the second moment of the gradients it receives. The formula is derived as a combination of SGD or Stochastic Gradient Descent together with RMSProp which will make the convergence much smoother. Second, learning rate scheduling is employed to adjust the learning rate for training in a dynamic way. It stops the optimizer from oscillating around the minimum of the loss function and also in the fine-tuning of the model the learning rate is gradually tapered off to allow the model get better solution.

Regularization

Since the model may have overfitting problems especially owing to the small dataset and noisy nature of historical manuscripts and degraded text and variable script, there is a need for regularization. Among the most useful regularization techniques used, the addition of dropout layers can be called. During training, Dropout randomly ‘drops’ or deactivates a fraction of neurons, making the model to learn more generalized and distributed representations. This prevents the model from overfitting that is leaning too heavily on some specific features or patterns present in the training dataset and serves to enhance the model’s capacity to perform well on new data. Thus, dropout helps the model not reach for memorization of the training data – which is always a disadvantage when handling smaller datasets. In this technique along with other methods like weight decay and early stoppage it allows the model to be flexible yet free of overfitting thus being able to capture the feature of text important for classification.AlgorithmBERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model494 Input.

Novelty of the work

The novelty of this work lies in the development of a BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model that uniquely combines deep learning with traditional linguistic modelling to classify historical Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscripts. Unlike existing models that primarily rely on either deep learning or handcrafted features, this hybrid approach integrates BERT’s contextual embedding’s, which capture the semantics and relationships between words, with LiScribe, which focuses on character-level features, phonetic structures, and linguistic rules specific to ancient scripts. This dual approach offers several advantages over existing methods. Traditional models struggle with noisy, degraded manuscripts and often fail to handle the complexities of multi-lingual and multi-script historical texts. Meanwhile, conventional deep learning models like BERT, while powerful, may miss the detailed script-specific features critical for distinguishing ancient forms of languages. The proposed model, by incorporating both contextual understanding through BERT and script-specific detail through LiScribe, excels in handling noisy and degraded manuscripts, as well as capturing linguistic variations and historical nuances in texts. The added attention mechanism further enhances the model’s ability to focus on relevant text segments in noisy or partially damaged documents, a significant improvement over existing model. Additionally, the model’s ability to classify not only the broad language families but also the finer subcategories of scripts and dialects demonstrates its versatility and superior performance compared to traditional approaches.

Results and discussions

The proposed BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model implemented using Python, leveraging powerful libraries such as TensorFlow and PyTorch for deep learning, and additional packages for natural language processing and data handling. The model was trained and evaluated in a system running on Windows, configured with an Intel® Core™ i9 processor 14901E (36 M Cache, up to 5.60 GHz) to handle the computationally intensive tasks of fine-tuning the BERT model and running the custom LiScribe feature extraction. The system also features Intel® Arc™ Pro A60 Graphics, which accelerates the processing of large datasets and improves model performance by speeding up calculations during training and inference. With 8 GB of RAM, the system efficiently manages the data-preprocessing pipeline, including OCR, tokenization, and feature extraction for a large number of historical texts. This hardware configuration ensures that the model runs smoothly, handling both the complexities of deep learning and the demands of processing historical manuscripts with high computational efficiency. The combination of Python’s flexibility and the powerful Windows configuration allows the model to process and classify texts in a timely manner, achieving robust performance even with limited hardware resources. In order to determine the efficiency of the suggested BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model, we used the traditional measures of the classification: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and error rate. Accuracy is the percentage of the correct manuscripts to the total samples, which gives a measure of the model power as a whole. Accuracy measures the rate of the percentage of the number of manuscripts correctly identified within a language category over the total sample projected within the language category, which focuses on the ability of the model to reduce the occurrence of false positives. Recall is the fraction of manuscripts correctly recognized in a language category divided by the fraction of the number of samples who actually belong to the language category in the model, hence the capacity of the model to reduce false negatives. The F1-score is a measure of trade-off between the recall and the precision, which is a harmonic mean of the two or is especially used when the datasets are unbalanced. Error rate is the percentage of misclassified samples and is anti-proportional to accuracy. Increased accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores, and reduced error rates are suggestive of more overall model performance. The combination of these metrics gives a holistic picture of classification performance, where improvements in all of them indicate that the proposed hybrid model is not only more likely to make correct prediction but also able to tolerate noisy and degraded manuscripts than the current baselines.

The function of the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model in terms of classifying the Indo-Aryan and Tamil texts primarily rests with the deep contextual understanding afforded through the BERT model in tandem with a detailed, dedicated linguistic scribe customized for the task. The results inferred from the model fit the predictions made from it as a result of the integration of these two interrelated methods. In the BERT component, the manuscript text is input and breaks each word down into contextual embedding’s that are the building blocks of context comprehending. This is particularly useful when dealing with historic texts, their understanding is not only dependent on the words as they are, but also their context in the document and the period and dialect of writing. BERT reinforces the fact that it’s embedding’s allows for even texts written in formal ways and seldom occur formally contextualized with other texts. This component is beneficial in the identification of other technically refined features like, syntactic structures, grammatical orders and historical (dialects). Error analysis: understanding failure cases

Although the overall performance of the proposed BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model was high, the analysis of errors was performed to understand the character of misclassifications and learn more about the model limits. This discussion contrasted incorrect predictions done by BERT-LiScribe against a set of baseline methods such as RoBERTa, mBERT, CNN, and LSTM. By manual inspection of the misclassified outputs, we could identify four main failure cases: script similarity confusions, degraded input manuscript, dialectal over lapping, and OCR induced noise. Like the similarity of scripts between related Indo-Aryan scripts (mainly between Bengali and Devanagari), one of the most common causes of misclassification. Transformer-based models like RoBERTa and mBERT tended to mix Hindi and Bengali texts in the ones based on Sanskrit-derived vocabulary used universally in the regional languages. As an example, a Bengali document containing classical lexicon was incorrectly labelled as the Hindi document by RoBERTa, but BERT-LiScribe correctly labelled it through the character-level distinction using LiScribe. This demonstrates that contextual embedding as is, are not enough in addressing closely related forms of scripts and they need to be supplemented by script-sensitive models.

The second common problem was the result of poor quality of manuscripts, most notably in historical Tamil palm-leaf texts. Both CNN and LSTM models, that mainly depend on visual or sequential patterns often failed to correctly classify these texts when their characters were distorted due to ink spilling or physical degradations. To illustrate this, fragments of fading Tamil text with poetry in classical forms could be erroneously interpreted as Marathi by a CNN model, presumably due to the incorrect readings of fragmented syllabic symbols. The BERT-LiScribe model, having the opportunity to utilize both semantic understanding and recognition of syllabic features, succeeded in classifying such examples on most occasions, albeit when noise was extreme it became a challenge. The third difficulty was noted in writings where archaic, or local dialectal forms, are used; in early Hindi, and Sanskritized Marathi texts. As these texts represented the words that are not commonly used nowadays, models trained mostly on the modern language corpora became confused. In other cases, mBERT did not accurately classify early Hindi religious texts as Sanskrit because of lexical overlap, but our LiScribe component identified them as being Sanskrit in the context of phoneme-pattern identification combined with morphology.

The hardest and most recurring failure mode was the mistakes made by the Optical Character Recognition (OCR) system particularly in manuscripts that had ligatures destroyed or characters confused by smudge, blotches, or script worn away. The output of such artifacts caused incomprehensible streams of tokens, which BERT and LiScribe were incapable of getting right. To the example, a severely corrupted Tamil inputs with unconnected glyphs resulted in low-confidence or wrong output in all models. Whereas BERT-LiScribe demonstrated increased robustness compared with the baselines, it is the case when handling inputs where the linguistic structure itself has been destroyed beyond repair. To conclude, it was observed in error analysis that BERT-LiScribe managed to avert most of the failures present in the transformer-only models or CNN/LSTM-based models, especially with script ambiguity and moderate degradation. Nevertheless, the model continues to perform poorly in cases when the quality of OCR is extremely poor or when the texts have a lot of code-switching or multilingual statements. These constraints indicate that in the future, it would be best to improve by implanting OCR confidence scores into the classification pipeline, adding language segregation mechanisms to documents with mixed scripts and adding more training data to the low-resource dialects that could be used to enhance model generalization. It would be even more feasible to see such refinements raised toward enabling the model to further take advantage of the broad dynamic range available in historical manuscript collections.

Practical and theoretical implications

The BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model proposed to be used in the classification of historical Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscripts, can be of great practical value as well as theoretical contribution to the field of computational linguistics, digital humanities, and machine learning. Practically, the system presents a good option of automating classification of noisy, multilingual and script diversified historical texts which would normally require a lot of manual work by linguistic and historical scholars. The high accuracy rate of 97.61% as well as impressive precision, recall, and generalization rates proves the BERT-LiScribe to be an efficient model ready to be implemented in an array of digital library technology, archival digitalization program, and heritage conservation interfaces. The big collection of unclassified or inadequately annotated manuscripts maintained by institutions like museums, national libraries, and academic repositories can include this model into their pipeline to make cataloguing of large volumes faster, and enable linguistic indexing. It can also be used to treat fragile or incomplete manuscripts since its resistance to frailed-texts is multi-dimensional, providing researchers an aiding tool in researching, translating, and recovering text. Since the pace of digitizing the past is picking up in South Asia and elsewhere, this approach can help greatly to lighten the annotation load, make the resources more widely accessible, and also democratize language resources as an educational and preservation tool.

Theoretically, the hybrid architecture presents a new paradigm in multimodal linguistic modelling that integrates deep context language understanding of BERT with handcrafted phonetics, graphemic, and syntactic feature extraction of LiScribe. This two-stream architecture not only outperforms the traditional use of complete deep learning-based but also shows that the combination of structural and semantic knowledge is better in terms of performance when it comes to complex script classification. The addition of attention mechanism to adjust the fused characteristics also leads to expand the body of knowledge on multimodal learning to adapt to new data in the low-resource, and historically variable language settings. Further, effective use of Multi-modal Factorized Bilinear (MFB) fusion here strengthens the current literature that points toward the effectiveness of complex bilinear fusion methods in deploying heterogeneous features.

The model also drives theoretical innovation in the domain of multilingual document processing by resolving the distinctive role of language families with shared lexical origins, non-linear writing systems as well as diachronic orthographic variation. It proposes a model that can be applied to other underrated scripts like Sinhala or Kannada or Pali and that would welcome the application of linguistic theory to explain model interpretability and improve their performance. These results are relevant to the wider discussion of explainable AI in heritage informatics alongside the suggestion that hybrid modelling methods are capable of realizing high accuracy levels and transparency in handling data regarding historically and linguistically rich data. Overall, the BERT-LiScribe model does not only offer a practical solution and but also to the real-world problem of automated manuscript classification, it also has the potential to offer a research infrastructure which enables subsequent research to effectively intertwine the extremes of classical linguistic approaches and modern developments applied in the field of deep learning. It is the step forward in the production of computational treatment of the historical and culturally important texts and its practical effectiveness against theoretical innovativeness.

Future scope: computational efficiency and model optimization

Although the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model has higher classification performance on historical Indo-Aryan and Tamil manuscripts, it has a relatively high implementation cost; hence limited opportunities in practice-related, resource-constrained applications. The current configuration is more appropriate in high-performance computing systems with the speed of processing a manuscript of about 170 ms and a memory footprint of 750 MB. Nevertheless, a great number of archival organizations, mobile platforms or digitalization projects in developing areas may have to use their resources in terms of limited hardware and connectivity. To overcome this, in future research, one shall seek to optimize the proposed model to file off lightweight versions that not only retain classification accuracy but also have a much smaller latency and resource usage. There are a number of tactics that can be utilized in order to attain this aim. On the one hand, the general model compression methods like parameter pruning, quantization, weight pruning, and sharing can be performed to both the BERT and LiScribe parts, and those approaches do not significantly degrade the quality of the inference. Second, we can promote efficient deployment of the model on edge devices via knowledge distillation of the full BERT model to a smaller encoder (i.e. DistilBERT or TinyBERT with a smaller LiScribe encoder). Besides, there are dynamic neural routing mechanisms that could be programmed into LiScribe through which only relevant feature paths will be powered up and run depending on the complexity of the input, thus so that unnecessary computation is minimized. Lastly, researching something like low-rank attention, linear transformers, or efficient variants of attention can also help to alleviate the transformer block overhead in BERT.

Although the loss function is categorical cross-entropy, the optimizer is Adam with learning rate decay scheduled (standard and effective selection to apply with any multi-class classification), the performance of the BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model can be enhanced by investigating the additional combinations. Alternative trade-offs between losses, speed of convergence, generalization, resistance to overfitting, can be greatly modified by the choice of loss functions and optimization algorithms, especially in small and noisy data domains like historical manuscripts. It is possible to imagine future experiments with focal loss, which tries to handle the class imbalance problem by down-weighing data that is being classified well and pushing the model to make efforts on harder (usually under-represented), data points. This would be especially useful when applying the model to the rare dialects or underrepresented scripts in subsequent databases. Also, label smoothing cross entropy may help with generalization, looking to avoid the overconfidence of the model in prediction which happens in linguistically ambiguous or degraded text inputs. Regarding optimizers, even though Adam has been shown to be robust, one should consider the alternative, Nadam (the Nesterov-based optimizer) or Ranger (a combination of RAdam and Lookahead optimizers) or LAMB (Layer-wise Adaptive Moments). These optimizers have better convergence and regularization behaviour particularly with deep transformer-based systems.

Conclusion and future work

The BERT-LiScribe Hybrid Model achieved a high classification accuracy of 97.61%, illustrating the potential of hybrid approaches that combine deep learning with linguistic modelling for handling complex historical texts. The integration of BERT for contextual understanding and LiScribe for character-level and linguistic feature extraction proved highly effective, even with noisy and aged manuscripts. The model’s ability to accurately classify both Indo-Aryan and Tamil scripts offers valuable support for historians and linguists studying these texts. However, further improvements could address challenges such as degraded texts and multi-lingual manuscripts. Future work should focus on refining the attention mechanism to better handle manuscripts with multiple languages and exploring the inclusion of additional South Asian languages and scripts to expand the model’s applicability. Incorporating advanced data augmentation techniques for historical manuscripts and exploring enhanced Transformer-based architectures could also further improve the model’s robustness and accuracy. This model serves as a foundation for further development in the automated classification of complex historical manuscripts, with potential applications in larger datasets and other linguistic families.
