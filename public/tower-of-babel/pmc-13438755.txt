Integrating artificial intelligence tools in health research
Authors: Gemma Postill, Jana Sedlakova, Stefano Tancredi, Frerik Smit, Sintieh Ekongefeyin, Andreas M Baumer, Arnaud Chiolero, Jürgen Bernard, Laura C Rosella, Viktor von Wyl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisciplines conducting health research rely on discipline-specific workflows that are distinct from data science, the discipline which develops most artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled research tools. Here we reflect on the impact of such differences in workflows and provide practical advice for leveraging AI-enabled research tools in health research. Further, we provide recommendations for how to prepare the next generation of health researchers for the inevitable influx of research integrating AI-enabled tools.Subject terms: Business and industry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Scientific community

Introduction

With recent technological advancements, artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled research tools are increasingly being used, to support, and even conduct, clinical and population health research1,2. These tools include those that assist with research objective generation, data preparation, analyses execution, data visualization, and research output summarization3. In theory, humans plus AI-enabled research tools would be better than either alone4. Indeed, the quality of AI-enabled research tools and the collaborations produced with them are rapidly evolving, particularly for tools supporting quantitative analysis of tabular health data5. Key advancements in AI-enabled research tools include improvements in models (e.g., deeper chain-of-thought reasoning, multimodal architectures, and fewer hallucinations) and the user interfaces6–9. Yet, concerns still remain regarding the quality and future directions of AI-enabled health research10. So, with better models and user interfaces, why has AI-enabled research not yet surpassed human research and how can researchers use the existing tools?

Disciplines conducting clinical and population health research, such as epidemiology, traditionally rely on discipline-specific workflows distinct from those of data science. Many AI-enabled research tools, in contrast, originate and are trained on software code bases from data science (e.g., the interdisciplinary field that draws on computational, statistical and mathematical fields, and subject expertise to generate knowledge from data) and, therefore, reflect the workflows and language of data science. Although there is potential to leverage these AI-enabled tools to accelerate or augment clinical and population health research, their application may change the research workflow in ways not apparent to researchers, resulting in poor-quality research outputs not aligned with current disciplinary standards. How should we leverage tools for research not structured around the typical research workflow?

In an international collaborative effort, we sought to (1) reflect on how the research workflows of health research and data science contrast, (2) provide practical advice for leveraging AI-enabled research tools in clinical and population health research, and (3) reflect on the broader training implications for future clinical and population health researchers. To provide concrete, actionable advice, we focus on the discipline of quantitative epidemiology (a discipline of health research focused on distribution, occurrence, and determinants of health and disease in a population) as it is our shared expertise; however, these insights can be generalized to other disciplines that conduct health research. Similar challenges in structuring, governing, and operationalizing data science workflows in health research have been documented across a range of international contexts, and highlight common issues related to data access, reproducibility, governance, interdisciplinary coordination, and translation, which all intersect in complex ways with AI tools for analysis, suggesting these challenges are not context-specific but globally shared11–14.

Contrasting the workflows of epidemiology and data science

To understand the implications of embedding AI into health research workflows, the first step is to identify the commonalities and differences between a specific health research discipline (e.g., epidemiology) and data science. By having a clear picture of the differences in research workflow structure, one can better leverage the benefits of AI-enabled research tools, minimize their potential harms, and produce high-quality research.

Epidemiology

Epidemiologists are trained to generate or use existing data deliberately, guided by study design, and ideally from well-defined populations (Fig. 1). There is much emphasis on the method of collection and purpose of the data (e.g., “designed” versus “organic” data)15. Research questions typically fall into three categories: descriptive, predictive, or causal16. In answering these questions, the emphasis is placed on developing rigorous protocols that specify research questions and analytic plans in advance17. This includes clearly defining target and study populations, as generalizability and transportability of study findings are key elements of the epidemiology workflow18. These practices reflect the generally high stakes of clinical and population health research19, where poor scientific practices and biases can lead to erroneous findings that fail to inform improvements in health outcomes and may ultimately harm populations20.

Data science

While there is a range of technical expertise within data science, the common understanding of data scientists is that they accept data from collaborators in some real-world setting and leverage their computational skills to generate data-driven insights and support decision-making (Fig. 1). As such, most data scientists work with data without an explicit step to conceptualize the study design21. Data scientists identify problems relevant to available data and transform data into useful outputs for downstream tasks based on domain-specific needs. This process of iterative exploration and transformation is central in preparing for the analysis and often the most resource- and time-intensive phase22. During this phase, data scientists ensure that data is accessible, governed, curated, and structured before analyses begin. The actual data analysis phase can be multi-faceted, reflecting a range of technical depth and roles, as well as iterative. The development of AI-enabled research tools by AI engineers is one branch of data science analysis. The final step in the data science workflow involves integrating subject matter expertise to make data meaningful and credible for their tasks and needs in decision-making.

Advice for how to integrate AI-enabled tools for epidemiological research

Given the cultural and methodological differences between epidemiology and data science, the use of AI-enabled tools in epidemiological research raises concerns regarding how it may impact a study’s validity, rigor, transparency, and replicability. However, by recognizing such differences and striving for common understanding, we can begin to ground AI-enabled tools in rigorous epidemiological research design while also embracing the efficiency and power that these tools can bring. The advice provided below is thereby intended to serve as a guide for helping us achieve this wider objective.

Never assume shared meaning; familiarize yourself with the terminology of data science and ensure appropriate interpretation of AI-enabled research tools

Many commonly used terms and concepts (e.g., modeling, prediction, and validation) carry different meanings in epidemiology and other computational disciplines (Table 1). Likewise, different terms may be used across these disciplines to mean the same thing. These differences can hinder collaboration, lead to confusion, and cause misinterpretation when AI-enabled tools are applied to research tasks23–25. For example, in epidemiology, (statistical) significance is commonly tied to statistical hypothesis testing, with strict pre-specified thresholds (e.g., p < 0.05). In contrast, in data science, significance might refer to the weight or influence of a feature (corresponding to a variable in epidemiology) in a predictive model. In the context of AI-enabled research tools, terminology differences are even more problematic, as some tools allow the use of natural or lay language rather than code to run analyses, with the intention of making data analysis more efficient and accessible. Consideration of ontological frameworks and metadata structures within health research is also foundational for the integration of AI-enabled tools into clinical and population health research workflows. Shared ontologies and metadata enable consistent representation of clinical and epidemiological concepts across heterogeneous data sources26. Only in ensuring consistent meaning and application of health data (e.g., quality control and data cleaning), alongside appropriate translation of that meaning to data science, can we enable semantically aware integration of AI-enabled research tools27,28.

We recommend systematically setting aside time to clarify the key concepts and terminology of the other discipline. Terminology-matching is the approach in which one can respond to a collaborator by rephrasing what is heard with their interpretation of the terminology29–31. Such an approach requires translation-like resources (Table 1) as a foundation for facilitating meaningful dialog about methods and expectations across disciplines.

Break down goals and processes into tasks and steps that are clearly documented

AI systems and humans both tend to struggle when faced with too much data and analytic complexity. Analysis goals that are too broad or high-level often lead to ambiguity, misinterpretation, and errors. For instance, when users interact with large language models using vague or oversized prompts, the system may produce outputs that do not align with user intent (Box 1). This misalignment stems from the wide space of possible interpretations and decisions embedded in complex tasks. Such is analogous to how human decision-making (and research) can deteriorate when overwhelmed by multifaceted goals without clear structure32–35. With current AI-enabled research tools, especially generative ones, failing to manage this complexity leads to inconsistent and non-reproducible behavior36.

We recommend deconstructing complex problems, including the data processing, into smaller, well-scoped steps (Fig. 1). Both human users and AI models benefit from clear well-defined and self-contained tasks. In language models, this is reflected in the effectiveness of chain-of-thought prompting, where systems internally decompose high-level prompts into intermediate steps. This not only improves results but also allows users to trace the reasoning path and verify individual steps. When designing human-AI collaboration (mixed-initiative systems), the idea is to define autonomy, competence, and responsibility at the level of individual atomic actions37,38. This granularity can improve control, transparency, and trust39. It may also be desirable to design workflows that make decomposition explicit, either through user input or by supporting AI self-decomposition, so that decision-making remains observable and revisable. In designing these, considering how they compare to data science workflows can facilitate understanding of the differing assumptions that might be embedded in an AI tool.

We tested an AI-enabled tool that leverages multiple large language models to assist users with statistical analyses. The tool allows users to upload datasets and interact with the AI through a chatbot interface to summarize data, generate visualizations, and run specific analyses. It provides step-by-step output along with the corresponding Python code. We used the AI-enabled tool to answer the following causal research question: “What is the causal effect of current smoking on heart attack?” The tool was instructed to address this question using publicly available survey data. We provided two prompts.

Our first prompt was simple and deliberately did not include any specific instructions regarding how we would have approached the analysis ourselves, replicating the kind of prompt that a student or an inexperienced researcher might use. “I have uploaded a dataset, and I’d like you to use it to answer the following research question: ‘What is the causal effect of current smoking on having a heart attack?’ Please tell me step by step what you would do and then run the analyses for me.”

The AI tool outlined the steps to address the research question and proceeded with the analysis. It identified the exposure and outcome variables, described the data, and ran a logistic regression model, interpreting the results. Code was provided for each step of the analysis.

The DAG was also not used by the model in the subsequent analytical steps. After having produced the DAG, the tool attempted to run the analysis but was not able to convert a string to a numerical value, failing to complete the task. This was unexpected, as the same issue had not occurred with the prompt 1.

Exercise Reflection. This simple experience highlighted some strengths and limitations of using AI for data analysis. The tool generated reasonable analytical steps, explained its methods clearly, and provided usable Python code. However, there were substantive errors in these outputs (particularly those unique to epidemiology, such as DAGs). When outputs are plausible but incorrect, they risk reinforcing misconceptions and giving users, especially those with limited expertise, a false sense of security that can ultimately hinder rather than support learning.

Define the purpose of an AI-enabled tool with respect to the workflow step it is applied to and the degree of human involvement

An important distinction in the use of AI-enabled research tools concerns (1) the workflow building block that is AI-enabled and (2) the degree of AI versus human involvement40. When humans and AI are paired without clarity about how decisions are being made and at what level of autonomy, collaboration tends to underperform regardless of how well their capabilities could theoretically complement each other10. Lack of clarity on an AI-enabled tool’s role in the research workflow can lead to a pedagogical research trap, where researchers underuse critical thinking skills and lose efficiency gains40.

Opportunities exist to integrate AI in each step of the research workflow41. However, not all steps in a scientific workflow carry equal risk, nor do they require the same level of judgment or responsibility. Each step in the health research workflow is aligned with specific overarching scientific principles such as reliability, reproducibility, or validity. Likewise, as is done with automated vehicles42, AI-enabled tools (agents) can be classified into levels of autonomy (Fig. 2), depending on the amount of control the user wants to give the tools. Rather than blindly applying AI to a step in the research workflow, we must deliberately decide which tasks can be safely and ethically supported by or delegated to AI-enabled research tools and which require more nuanced human judgment and oversight. As such, these level assignments are also an expression of trust into AI-autonomy, given current AI capabilities, analysis objectives, error tolerance, and liability.

Making the application of AI-enabled tools and human oversight decisions transparent and defensible is essential for building trust in research conducted with the support of AI and for producing high-quality science, especially given the volume of data currently available43. The respective roles of humans and AI in carrying out the research task should also be clearly spelled out in journal publications and conference papers, rather than being hidden due to stigma around the use of AI-enabled tools. In doing so, we recommend specifying, and ideally prespecifying within protocols, the AI-enabled tools used, as well as the tool’s purpose, limitations, and user knowledge or skill applied in the human-AI collaboration3.

Do not assume how AI-enabled tools work

Effective human-AI collaboration requires users to understand how these tools work so they can apply the appropriate level of skepticism. AI-enabled tools carry implicit assumptions about epistemology (i.e., the theory of how knowledge is generated), acceptable approaches to output production, and acceptable levels of automation44,45. These assumptions are not always apparent, may not align with epidemiology, and can impact the overall quality and validity of health research3. For example, seemingly transparent models (i.e., models that provide rationales or programming code showing how an analysis was internally conducted) may still mislead users by creating an illusion of human understanding when it may not be how the answer was generated46,47. The use of AI-enabled tools that are epistemically opaque (e.g., in how confounders were selected for an analysis) can also impede the actualization of key scientific principles of transparency and reproducibility. Knowledge of the data-generating process is a key strength in current epidemiology training programs and is foundational to the use of epidemiology for understanding and remediating health disparities. Thus, researchers must develop new skills to evaluate the ability of AI-enabled research tools to provide context-sensitive epistemic elements, such as by applying discipline-specific knowledge to evaluate the selection of a variable adjustment set48.

Reject, revise, and accept; approach AI-enabled workflow steps like a peer review

Validation of the usefulness of an AI-enabled tool for its intended purpose is important; validation of its output for a specific task is equally important. No tool is perfect; yet the limitations of AI-enabled tools are new and may not be noticed. For example, generative AI models (e.g., ChatGPT) often produce plausible-sounding outputs that can be incorrect (i.e., hallucinations), misleading, or subtly biased49. Generative model outputs are also very heavily tied to the quality of the prompt provided49. These limitations challenge the notion that these tools can provide authoritative guidance or independently act on tasks50. However, without training on use of AI for research, students may assume generative tools are authoritative or intelligent in a human sense, when in fact they rely on probabilistic patterns rather than understanding51.

Thus, AI-enabled research tools require a paradigm shift in which researchers and trainees must simultaneously correct the AI-enabled tool while leveraging it for their specific analysis goal. Navigating this new division of labor requires new skills and a reminder of the need to maintain researcher accountability. Practically speaking, researchers must learn to “peer-review” their AI-enabled tools, just as they would scrutinize the work of a colleague (Box 1). This means not only verifying results but also understanding how they were produced, what assumptions were embedded into the system, and what limitations must be communicated to audiences by the authors who take accountability for the research. Researchers can also use chain-of-thought prompting (as previously mentioned) or repeat prompting with varied terminology. In the latter, a researcher would evaluate the consistency of the model’s output in each iteration of the prompt. In this way, using AI limits the risk of shortcut thinking while offering opportunities for deeper engagement with the data and methods, which may be complemented by more efficient output generation. As a secondary benefit, training researchers to review, interrogate, and contextualize AI-generated outputs might also help remediate the human cognitive biases that exist (e.g., when humans overstate their own knowledge or blindly trust heuristics). Using AI-enabled research tools in this way could thus amplify human insight, rather than replace or obscure it.

Practice self-reflection in the use of AI-enabled research tools

Reflecting on the long-term implications of using AI-enabled tool is part of a broader commitment to preserving the integrity and depth of scientific inquiry. Researchers and students alike should critically reflect upon their motivations, needs, and assumptions when choosing to use AI-enabled research tools. Specifically, they should ask themselves: Should I use an AI-enable tool here and what role is the tool playing in the overall research workflow? The proportionality principle in this context can be a good guide: the benefits of using a new tool should outweigh its risks, and they should also outweigh the benefits of the method it replaces52. Researchers must be able to pause and interrogate their own decision-making: Am I relying on this output? What assumptions underly the AI’s model? Could alternative interpretations or methods be more appropriate here? Such reflective practices do not merely safeguard against error; they form the basis of epistemic responsibility and scientific integrity.

For trainees, this is particularly important, as they may lack the experience and methodological proficiency to assess the quality of these tools, the appropriateness of the analytic techniques applied, or to detect errors53. Relying too early on AI-enabled tools that outsource key cognitive tasks will hinder the development of competencies essential to critically evaluating their outputs and achieving long-term research success. We advise that students must first develop foundational health research skills such as critical appraisal, study design, and conceptual reasoning.

Implications for teaching and training health researchers

Guidance put forth in this paper has implications for the skills needed by health research trainees (Table 2). In the context of AI-enabled research tools, educational programs must instruct trainees on how to breakdown the research processes of their own disciplines, as well as data science, into modular workflows (Fig. 1). Health research trainees should learn how to coordinate, not just execute, the steps of a research workflow with AI-enabled tools. This includes clarifying how responsibility is actioned at different levels of human–AI collaborations and how to detect and address errors in AI outputs.

AI artificial intelligence.

Equally important is the need to teach with AI-enabled tools (vs. only about AI) so that trainees gain firsthand experience with their capabilities and limitations54,55. Integrating tool use into the learning process can demystify the technology, sharpen students’ evaluative skills, and foster a mindset of shared accountability between human researchers and machine assistants56,57. Educating trainees is particularly important, as their exposure to and collaboration with AI-enabled tools occurs whilst they are developing research skills and experience. Case studies and opportunities to practice as well as receive feedback on their ability to critically appraise the outputs of AI-enabled tools can equip students with the necessary skills to become successful independent researchers (Table 2).

Revamping education in light of technical advancements is not a new phenomenon. Previously, with the growth of statistical software systems (i.e., SAS, R, and STATA), epidemiological education shifted from emphasizing hand calculation to computer-enabled statistical tests. Doing so involved many iterations of identifying (and instructing) how one should trust a software’s output. We can learn from this experience and apply analogous principles to the integration of AI-enabled tools in health research (Table 2).

Embracing and preparing for change

With the increasing use of AI-enabled tools in clinical and population health research areas, we face an urgent need to reflect on how we structure and teach their integration into existing research workflows. Effectively leveraging them in existing research workflows requires understanding the research workflows of the disciplines that develop AI-enabled research tools (data science) and of the health research disciplines that use those tools. In applying these tools to health research disciplines, reflection is needed on terminology differences, the modular components that constitute research workflows, the components of research workflows that AI should support, and the technical structure of the AI-enabled research tools. Application of these tools must be complemented with self-reflection on the ultimate role they serve, peer-review of model outputs, and transparent reporting of their use. Leveraging AI-enabled tools for health research in this way, with humans at the center and health research standards upheld, can support the continued production of high-quality, robust, ethical, and impactful health research. By highlighting key considerations for the integration of AI-enabled tools in health research our findings provide a useful starting point for researchers, educators, and trainees, enabling the development of concrete frameworks for validating AI outputs in high-stakes health research contexts like the one addressed in the paper.
