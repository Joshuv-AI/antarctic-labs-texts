Magnetic field generation in mergers of massive main sequence stars
Sebastian T. Ohlmann ,, Fabian R. N. Schneider, Friedrich K. Röpke, Rüdiger Pakmor, Philipp Podsiadlowski, Volker Springel

Abstract
Magnetic fields are found in many astrophysical objects, ranging from galaxy clusters to the interstellar medium of galaxies and magnetars. Strong magnetic fields are also observed in massive stars, but it is still unclear how they are generated. There are different theories for their origin: the magnetic fields could be fossil fields from star formation, they could be generated in dynamo processes during stellar evolution, or they could be generated during a merger of two stars. Here, we show how magnetic fields are generated in simulations of mergers of massive main-sequence stars, conducted with the 3D magnetohydrodynamics code Arepo . In these simulations, a 9 ​ M ⊙ 9\,M_{\odot} and a 8 ​ M ⊙ 8\,M_{\odot} main sequence star merge, whereby the more massive star transfers mass to the less massive one until it is finally disrupted, forming a core–disk structure. During the initial mass transfer, magnetic fields start to be amplified in the accretion stream, saturating after the disruption at an equipartition level. Correspondingly, the magnetic energy starts being present at small scales, growing to larger scales during the merger. During the merger, the magnetic pressure is much smaller than the gas pressure, thus the dynamical impact of the magnetic fields on the merger is small. After the merger, both are similar in some regions and the magnetic field shows a large-scale structure that is mostly toroidal and that is expected to be stable. Thus, our simulations show that magnetic fields in massive stars can originate from a merger of two main sequence stars.



1
Introduction

The merger of two stars is a common phenomenon in stellar astrophysics and may invoke compact objects such as neutron stars and black holes, but also stars during their main nuclear-burning cycles. While the former are observed, e.g., in gravitational-wave events
(
Abbott et al. 2016
;
Abbott et al. 2017
)
, the latter lead to outbursts that are called luminous red novae with V1309 Sco and V838 Mon being prototypical events
(
Munari et al. 2002
;
Tylenda et al. 2011
)
. Merged stars may be rejuvenated
(
Hellings 1983
;
Braun & Langer 1995
;
Dray & Tout 2007
;
Schneider et al. 2016
, e.g.)
and can appear as blue stragglers in star clusters and stellar associations
(
Hills & Day 1976
, e.g.)
. Some of the most massive stars known might in fact be merger remnants
(
Schneider et al. 2014
)
and also the Great Eruption of
\eta
Car in the 1840s that created the Homunculus Nebula has been suggested to be the result of a merger event
(
Smith et al. 2018
;
Hirai et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. Some merged stars may also give rise to unusual supernova progenitors such as the surprising blue supergiant that lead to SN 1987A
(
Podsiadlowski & Joss 1989
;
Podsiadlowski et al. 1990
;
Podsiadlowski et al. 1992
;
Morris & Podsiadlowski 2007
;
Menon & Heger 2017
)
while others are thought to be the progenitors of interacting and even superluminous supernovae
(
Justham et al. 2014
)
. Still, the merging phase itself and also the evolution and final fates of merged stars leave many open questions.
Schneider et al. 2025
present a general introduction to stellar mergers and the related common-envelope phase.

Mergers can be induced in various ways: by dynamical encounters in dense stellar systems such as star cluster (cf. “head-on collisions”), by orbital decay of binaries in dense interstellar/circumbinary media such as in AGN disks or during the formation of stars, by orbital dynamics in triple-star systems and other multiples (e.g. von Zeipel–Lidov–Kozai mechanism), and by mass transfer in isolated binary stars. The latter channel is supposed to lead to mergers for about 25% of all massive stars
(
Sana et al. 2012
,
\gtrsim 10\,M_{\odot}
;)
such that – when integrating over a constant star-formation rate in a stellar population like in the Milky Way – about 10% of all massive stars are expected to be remnants of mergers from isolated binary evolution
(
de Mink et al. 2014
)
. Indeed, the binary evolution computations of
Henneco et al. 2024
show that at least 12% of massive, mass-transferring binaries will merge, and this fraction goes up to
{>}\,23\%
if fully conservative mass transfer is assumed
(
Henneco 2025
)
.

Ever since the discovery of surface magnetic fields in the A-type star 78 Vir by
Babcock 1947
, the origin of strong surface magnetism in massive OBA stars is debated. It has long been speculated that strong and large-scale magnetic fields are produced in stellar mergers and that they can persist over the long evolutionary timescales of stars
(
Ferrario et al. 2009
;
Wickramasinghe et al. 2014
, see e.g.)
. Today, direct simulations of essentialy every flavor of stellar mergers and other dynamic stellar phases show the efficient amplification of magnetic fields. This is the case in mergers of binary white dwarfs
(
Zhu et al. 2015
;
Pakmor et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, white dwarfs and neutron stars
(
Morán-Fraile et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, binary neutron stars
(
Kiuchi et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, low- and high-mass main-sequence (MS) stars (e.g.
Schneider et al. 2019
, hereafter
69
;
Ryu et al. 2024
;
Vynatheya et al. 2025
) and also common-envelope events
(
Ohlmann et al. 2016b
;
Ondratschek et al. 2022
;
Vetter et al. 2024
;
Gagnier & Pejcha 2024
, e.g.)
.

The mergers of main-sequence (MS) stars offer a way to understand the origin of strong surface magnetic fields observationally found in about 10% of OBA stars
(
Landstreet 1992
;
Donati & Landstreet 2009
;
Fossati et al. 2015
;
Schöller et al. 2017
;
Grunhut et al. 2017
)
. For example, a merger hypothesis has been put forward to explain the magnetic stars
\tau
Sco (
Schneider et al. 2016
;
69
), HR 2949
(
Schneider et al. 2016
)
, and HD 148937
(
Frost et al. 2024
)
. All three stars show clear rejuvenation that can be understood within a merger scenario, and HD 148937 is additionally surrounded by a bipolar nebula that appears to be the smoking gun for debris ejected in a merger event. In the case of
\tau
Sco, we conduct three-dimensional (3D) magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations of the merger of an initially
9\,M_{\odot}
and
8\,M_{\odot}
MS star and show strong magnetic field amplification and that the post-merger evolution of the star can explain many of the observed properties of
\tau
Sco
(
69
)
. If the merger-generated magnetic fields can persist until the pre-supernova stage of
\tau
Sco, the iron core-collapse of the progenitor likely leads to the formation of a highly magnetic neutron star, a so-called magnetar
(
Varma et al. 2023
)
.

Here, we provide further analysis of the magnetic field amplification in the merger of the two MS stars originally presented in
69
and the spin evolution of the merger remnant. In particular, we show how small-scale magnetic fields generated by the magneto-rotational and Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities are converted into large-scale magnetic fields via large-scale fluid motions in the rotating post-merger star. We describe our computations in Sect.
2
and present our main findings in Sect.
3
. The results are discussed in Sect.
4
and we conclude in Sect.
5
.

2
Numerical methods

The initial conditions for the merger
simulations in this paper are from stellar models computed with the stellar evolution
code
Mesa
(Sect.
2.1
) and represent a merger induced by binary star evolution. The hydrodynamics simulations of
the merger are carried out using the moving-mesh magnetohydrodynamics code
Arepo
(Sect.
2.2
).

2.1
Stellar evolution calculations

An
8\,M_{\odot}
and a
9\,M_{\odot}
MS star are
evolved to an age of 9 Myr without rotation, using the stellar evolution code

Mesa

(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
, version 9793,)
. The computations use an initial helium mass
fraction of
Y=0.2703
and solar-like metallicity
Z\,{=}\,0.0142

(
Asplund et al. 2009
)
. We use the Ledoux criterion for convection and
exponential convective core overshooting with a parameter of

f_{\text{ov}}=0.019
. Mixing-length theory
(
Henyey et al. 1965
)
is used with a
mixing-length parameter of
\alpha_{\mathrm{MLT}}=1.8
and a semi-convective
efficiency of
\alpha_{\mathrm{sm}}=1.0
, but not applying the so-called MLT++
scheme in
Mesa
. We use the so-called “Dutch” wind mass-loss prescription with
an efficiency factor of 1.0.

2.2
Hydrodynamics simulations

We carry out the hydrodynamics simulations using the finite-volume
moving-mesh code
Arepo

(
Springel 2010
)
with updates improving the
convergence
(
Pakmor et al. 2016
)
. We solve the ideal MHD equations using an HLLD
Riemann solver
(
Miyoshi & Kusano 2005
)
and the Powell scheme for handling the
divergence constraint
(
Powell et al. 1999
)
; for more details and tests of the
implementation, see
Pakmor et al. 2011
and
Pakmor & Springel 2013
. We couple the
OPAL equation of state
(
Rogers et al. 1996
;
Rogers & Nayfonov 2002
)
to the hydrodynamics
which is the same as used in
Mesa
in the relevant part of the
temperature–density plane.

For the merger simulations, we need to create initial conditions that consist of
two stars that are about to merge. For these single stars, we generate stable
models by following the method outlined in
Ohlmann et al. 2017
. We compute the
stellar structure with
Mesa
and map it to a grid with cell centers on spherical
shells distributed according to a HEALPix tesselation. Then, we run a relaxation
because slight mismatches between pressure gradients and gravitional forces can
induce spurious velocities that need to be damped away. We carry out the relaxation over ten dynamical timescales
as given by the sound crossing time to ensure that the models remain stable.

In a second step, the initial conditions for a merger are derived from these
stable stellar models. In principle, they should be set up such that the stars
just begin Roche lobe overflow. Such conditions, however, lead to a stable
system that will not merge in the simulation for many orbits. In an additional
step, initial conditions are generated that lead to a faster merger by adding an
angular momentum loss term until a certain separation is reached. From there
on, the simulation is continued without the loss term to capture the dynamics of
the merger.

We can gain additional information on the merging process by tracking the origin
of material during the merger. To this end, we add two scalars to the
initial conditions that are passively advected with the flow. We set each of the
scalars to 1 inside one of the stars and 0 everywhere else. Because we advect the
scalars in the simulation, their final value gives the mass fraction of a cell
originating from the corresponding star.

3
Results

A set of simulations has been run to study how the magnetic field is amplified in
MS star mergers. These simulations use initial stellar models that are
stable over a few dynamical timescales (Sect.
3.1
). During
the merger, the simulations show that the more massive primary star is disrupted
around the secondary, forming a core–disk structure
(Sect.
3.2
). Magnetic fields are amplified in the
simulations, first on small scales and later reaching large-scale structures
(Sect.
3.3
). These fields are mostly toroidal in the final
merger product which shows a core–disk structure where the core originates
mainly from the secondary and the disk mainly from the primary star
(Sect.
3.4
). During the evolution, the simulations show
almost no ejection of mass (Sect.
3.5
).

3.1
Initial models

Because the MS stars are quite compact compared to giants presented in

Ohlmann et al. 2017
, the method of Sec.
2.2
allows the generation of models that
are stable in the hydrodynamical simulations for many dynamical timescales.
This can be seen in Fig.
1
, where spherically averaged profiles of the density,
the Mach number, and the deviation from hydrostatic equilibrium are shown at
different times during the relaxation run. One can see that the density profile
(Fig.
1
, top panel) remains the same in the interior of the
star; only the boundary smears out slightly, which is to be expected due to the
insufficient resolution in these low-density regions. For the same reason, the
Mach numbers are slightly larger at the edge of the star, while the Mach number in the
interior remains below
10^{-3}
(Fig.
1
, middle panel).
The hydrostatic equilibrium (
\nabla p=\rho\mathbf{g}
) is fulfilled to
better than a few percent in most parts of the star
(Fig.
1
, bottom panel).

L1. This stream hits the secondary and leads to shear flows near the surface of

Directly after the merger, the cores of the stars are still separate (see
central part of Fig.

symmetric merger product (Figs.

(see Fig.

material from the primary increases outwards. The post-merger

During the rapid mass transfer leading up to the merger, magnetic fields are
strongly amplified. Due to the complex flow patterns, it is difficult to unambiguously
identify the amplification mechanisms. The maximum magnetic field strength grows exponentially for
some time over several orders of magnitude until it saturates at about

10^{8}
G, independent of the different initial conditions
(Fig.
5
, upper panel).
Fits to an exponential function yield amplification times of 0.2 d to 1 d.
The time evolution of the magnetic field in Fig.
6

(first row)
shows that the initial amplification takes place in the region where the
accretion stream hits the secondary. Strong shear flows induce Kelvin-Helmholtz
instabilities, winding up the magnetic fields. The thickness of this layer is about

0.8\,R_{\odot}
.

The amplification continues in the mass stream from the primary to the secondary
as it creates shear flows outside the Roche lobes of both stars
(Fig.
6
at
t=-2.0
d). At this stage, we find that the flow
is unstable to the magneto-rotational instability (MRI,
Balbus & Hawley 1998
).
We estimate the length and time scale of the
fastest growing MRI channel in
our simulations
(
Rembiasz et al. 2016
, cf.)
: this yields a timescale of about
0.5 d and
a wavelength of about
0.1\,R_{\odot}
which is similar to the scales in our simulation
(time scale: 0.2–1 d, length scale:
0.8\,R_{\odot}
). Moreover, the strong amplification
of the magnetic field starts as soon as the wavelength of the fastest growing MRI channel is resolved by
a few hundred cells.

When the stellar cores merge,
the magnetic field is distributed throughout the merger product, leading to a
nearly symmetric distribution of the field strength
(Fig.
6
,
t>0
d). Due to the differential rotation
during the merger process, the field structure is mostly toroidal: the fraction
of the toroidal magnetic energy is about
80-85\%
of the total
magnetic energy (for more details, see Sect.
3.4
).

The amplification is slowing down and reaches saturation at later times, when
the ratio of magnetic to turbulent kinetic energy approaches the percent level
and comes closer to equipartition (Fig.
5
, middle panel).
As a proxy for the turbulent kinetic energy, we use the kinetic energy in
the radial and
z
direction,
E_{\mathrm{kin},r,z}

the large-scale angular motion (see the comparison in
Fig.

energy is injected at small scales (about

3
–

obtained by a fit to

for

between 0.1 and

and are 1.38,

, and
-0.78
for

-2.0\,

and
5.0\,

when the amplification nears saturation. As mentioned there, large-scale
magnetic fields seem to appear when the amplification process saturates (see
also
Ebrahimi et al. 2009

The impact of the magnetic fields on the dynamics of the merger can be
characterized by the ratio of magnetic pressure to gas pressure

\beta^{-1}=p_{B}/p_{\mathrm{gas}}
(see Fig.
6
, bottom row).
During the mass transfer leading up to the
merger,
\beta^{-1}
stays below 1%, thus the effect of the magnetic fields on
the dynamics of the merger is very small. Only after the merger, the ratio of
the magnetic pressure rises in some parts up to values between 0.3 and 1,
indicating that the magnetic fields have a stronger impact on the dynamics
of the merger product, especially on the differential rotation.

As already seen during the evolution of the merger (cf. Fig.
4
), the very central part of the merger product
originates from the secondary star, whereas the primary star is disrupted around
the core.
Only the inner
\sim 2\,M_{\odot}
of the product are
dominated by material from the secondary, the rest of the core stems to similar
parts from both stars. The disk, however, is predominantly formed from material
of the primary star. This distribution is expected from the entropy and thus buoyancy profiles
of the progenitor stars: due to the similar mass and hence also similar
evolutionary stage, the entropy is similar and mixing is expected over large
parts of the profile. Because the secondary has a lower mass, its
entropy is slightly smaller and thus its material dominates the central part.
This behaviour is similar to the case of direct collisions as explored by

Glebbeek & Pols 2008
and
Glebbeek et al. 2013
, especially their case S.

Figure 10:
The magnetic field structure of the merger product at
t=6
d is shown
using line integral convolution. On top of the magnitude of the magnetic
field (color-coded), the field lines are visualized in the corresponding plane
by convolving a white noise texture with a kernel along the field lines.

The magnetic field structure of the final merger product is visualized in
Fig.
10
. The field structure is mostly toroidal; the
energy of the toroidal magnetic field component is about 80-85% of the total
magnetic energy. In this range, magnetic field configurations are expected to be
stable in stars
(
Braithwaite & Spruit 2004
;
Braithwaite & Nordlund 2006a
)
. Moreover, the configuration remains stable
after the final merger and this time already corresponds to a few
Alfvén timescales. However, in the regime of fast rotation (
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{A}}
), the evolutionary timescale of the magnetic field is
\tau_{\mathrm{evol}}\sim\tau_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}\Omega
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{A}}
is the Alfvén timescale
(
Braithwaite & Cantiello 2013
)
. Hence, great changes in the magnetic field are not expected to be seen over the timescales simulated here after the field has reached an equilibrium configuration. We expect Ohmic decay to operate on timescales that are
comparable to the lifetime of the star (for more details, see

69
). Hence, the magnetic field of the star should
remain present at least until it reaches the main sequence. Modeling the
subsequent stellar evolution in more detail
shows that the merger product could evolve into a MS star with strong
magnetic fields, similar to
\tau
Sco (
69
;
Schneider et al. 2020
).

The merger process leads to a denser and hotter core than a comparable
16.9 M
⊙
MS star in full equilibrium: heat is mostly generated in the
central region, leading to a larger internal energy in the core.
The angular momentum redistribution
leads to large rotational velocities outside the core
(Fig.
9
), thus the kinetic energy is larger compared to the
internal energy outside the core, especially in the thick disk.
This makes sense because the
angular momentum of a binary increases outwards (
J\propto\sqrt{a}
), whereas
the orbital energy decreases outwards (
E_{\mathrm{orb}}\propto a^{-1}
).
This redistribution of energies is important to predict 1D merger products that
can be used for further stellar evolution modeling (see also
Sect.
4.2
).

3.5
Dynamical ejecta

Because the further evolution of the merger product and also its optical display
might be influenced by dynamical ejecta, we compute how much mass is unbound at
the end of the simulation. To this end, we sum the mass of all cells in the
simulation for which the total energy is positive. To get an upper limit,
contributions from the potential, kinetic and also internal energies are taken
into account. After the merger settled into the core–disk structure, at
t=6
d,
about
0.02\,M_{\odot}
of material are unbound, corresponding to 0.14% of the
total mass of the system. Not much mass is ejected compared to other stellar
mergers (e.g., common envelope phases, see
Ohlmann et al. 2016a
), also because
the binding energy of the system is still quite large with about

-4\times 10^{50}\,\text{erg}
. The unbound mass moves outwards with an average
radial velocity of about 180 km/s and is predominantly ejected near the orbital
plane with a distribution that shows some tails to the polar directions.

4
Discussion

4.1
Impact of numerical parameters

The main numerical parameters that affect the simulation outcome are the
resolution, the initial distance and the magnetic field configuration.
To assess the impact of the resolution, we ran model 1 with about 10 times more
cells than model 2 (cf. Table
2
). Because the adaptive
mesh leads to cells with nearly constant mass, this leads to a higher resolution
in volume by a factor of 10, which roughly corresponds to a factor of 2 in each
spatial dimension. Overall, these two simulations show quite similar behavior in
their orbital evolution (Fig.
2
). The evolution of the
magnetic field amplification differs slightly for the two models
(Fig.
6
): the amplification
sets in later for model 2 with less cells.
The final field strength and configuration, however, are quite similar which
demonstrates that they are robust with respect to the resolution.

The initial distance influences the outcome as more angular momentum is present
in the system for larger initial distances. In principle, the simulation should
start at Roche-lobe overflow; however, this is prohibitively expensive in
computational time because the mass transfer progresses only slowly in the
beginning, leading to a long time to the actual merger. As already described, we
remove angular momentum artificially in order to obtain a starting configuration
that leads to a merger more quickly. In reality, the system would have more
angular momentum, leading probably to a longer mass loss via L2 and L3 in the
phase prior to the actual merger. The largest impact of this is probably on the
detailed structure of the outer part of the disk. To assess the impact of a
larger initial separation, we ran model 3 with a larger initial distance. As one
can see in Fig.
2
, it takes longer until the cores finally
merge, but the overall evolution is similar. Also, the magnetic field evolution
(Fig.
6
) shows a slightly different shape, but the
final field strength and configuration are similar. Thus, we expect that the
main conclusions are not affected by the detailed choice of this parameter, but
that it mainly affects the outermost structure of the disk.

In addition, we ran a simulation without magnetic fields at all to assess their
impact on the orbital evolution (model 4). The orbital evolution is very similar
to model 2 with the same resolution (see Fig.
2
), thus the
impact of the magnetic fields on the dynamical evolution is small. Moreover, the ratio of
magnetic pressure to gas pressure is only about 1% in the phase leading up to
the merger. This indicates that the magnetic fields do not greatly influence
the dynamics of the gas in this phase.

A typical numerical issue in MHD simulations is fulfilling the
divergence constraint
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
. In principle, the continuous equations of MHD
preserve this constraint for initial conditions fulfilling it. However,
discretization errors also lead to errors in
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}
. As explained
in
Pakmor & Springel 2013
, we use the Powell method
(
Powell et al. 1999
)
to
control the errors in the divergence. We have checked that the errors in our
simulations behave very similarly as in the tests shown there (see
Fig.
11
): averages over
larger regions are small and typically adjacent cells show different signs in
the error, indicating that the large-scale impact of these local errors is
small. The volume-weighted average of the relative divergence error is

7.0\times 10^{-4}
,
-1.6\times 10^{-4}
,
8.9\times 10^{-3}
, and

6.2\times 10^{-2}
for the times given in Fig.

.

4.2
Comparison to other simulations

Glebbeek et al. 2013
have conducted a series of stellar merger simulations as head-on
collisions and subsequent stellar evolution calculations for a range of masses
and evolutionary points. They used an SPH code with
130\,000
particles per star
whereas we use a grid-based approach with 1 to 2 million cells per star.
Moreover, we include MHD effects, which is the major novelty in our simulations.
By simulating head-on collisions, they avoid dealing with rotation in the merger
product and also with angular momentum transport. The collision may lead to more
heating compared to the merger in our simulations. Moreover, there is probably
no disk forming around the merger product in their simulations due to the
missing angular momentum. In their simulations, the equal-mass collisions eject
between 4% and 8% of the total mass (see their Figure 3), whereas in our
simulation, only 0.14% of the mass is unbound (compare Sect.
3.5
).
This might be due to the difference in the initial conditions as material can
escape more easily in head-on collisions, whereas in our merger simulations a
large disk forms that is bound. In our simulation, the core is dominated by
material from the secondary star,
corresponding to “case S” in
Glebbeek et al. 2013
. As explained there, this
can be attributed to the lower buoyancy in the secondary star. They find “case
S” for relatively unevolved primaries at moderate mass ratios, which would also
apply to our case.

Glebbeek & Pols 2008
have analyzed stellar collision products. However, they do not
conduct hydrodynamic simulations, but they use the entropy
sorting method from
Lombardi et al. 2002
to compute the merger
products and then they focus on the further evolution.

Lombardi et al. 2002
use results from earlier collision simulations using an
SPH code to construct a model for predicting the outcome of such a collision.
This model mainly sorts the material by its entropy but takes into account more
effects such as mass loss and shock heating. For our simulations, comparing the
merger product with an entropy-sorted mix of the initial stellar models, one can
clearly see similar effects: the entropy in the simulated merger is larger
especially in the center and in the outermost parts. Simple entropy sorting of the initial stellar models does not provide the right structure of the merged stars. Shock heating, chemical mixing and changes in the total mass of the merged star and thereby in density and temperature modify the entropy of the original fluid elements. Still, the final merged star has an outward increasing entropy profile as is expected for buoyantly stable stratifications
(
Landau & Lifshitz 1987
)
.
The models from
Lombardi et al. 2002
are based on earlier collision
simulations, amongst others the off-axis collisions conducted by

Sills et al. 2001
. They use an SPH code with about
100\,000
particles for the
full simulation and target low-mass stars,
mainly to study blue stragglers similar to
Lombardi et al. 2002
and
Glebbeek & Pols 2008
.

Ryu et al. 2024
simulate parabolic head-on collisions of low-mass stars with non-zero impact parameters similar to those mentioned above but they also use the moving-mesh MHD code
Arepo
and find strong magnetic field amplification. Such collisions contain less angular momentum than mergers in circular binaries studied here and thus form not so massive disk-like structures in the merger remnant as found here. The magnetic field amplification mechanism is otherwise in qualitative agreement with that found in our inspiral mergers of circular high-mass binaries: the collision mergers invoke several contact/grazing encounters before the final coalescence and in each phase small-scale dynamos amplify the magnetic fields. From the work presented in
Ryu et al. 2024
, however, it remains unclear whether also large-scale magnetic fields are formed.
Vynatheya et al. 2025
compare head-on collisions of massive stars with binary mergers of the same stars. They find systematic differences between those, in particular they find that merger remnants have signs of an ordering of the magnetic field similar to our simulation, that is absent for collision remnants. They focus on a qualitative analysis of the final state of their collision and merger remnants. The latter seem consistent with our simulation.

It is remarkable that the magnetic field amplification process in mergers of MS stars is so similar to that in mergers of white dwarfs and neutron stars presented in
Pakmor et al. 2024
and
Kiuchi et al. 2024
, respectively. Also in these binary white-dwarf and neutron-star mergers small scale magnetic fields are produced by a turbulent dynamo and magnetic energy is then transferred to larger scales by a large-scale, MRI-driven dynamo. A similar situation is also found in neutron star–white dwarf mergers
(
Morán-Fraile et al. 2024
)
and common-envelope phases of low-mass and high-mass stars
(
Ondratschek et al. 2022
;
Vetter et al. 2024
)
.
Despite the similarities, all these simulations feature outflows that are magnetically driven, whereas our simulations do not show such outflows. This might be because we could not follow our simulations for long enough after the merger.

5
Conclusions

We have studied in detail the magnetic-field amplification in the merger of a
9
and
8\,M_{\odot}
main-sequence star in an isolated binary system and the properties of the resulting merged star. The simulations were first described in
69
to explain observations of the magnetic massive star
\tau
Sco. The focus of this study is on the magnetic field amplification process, the magnetic field geometry, the mixing during the merger and the rotational configuration of the resulting merger product. The main findings can be summarised as follows.

•

We see the formation of a star–torus structure in which the core of the initially less-massive
8\,M_{\odot}
secondary star is in the centre and the initially more-massive
9\,M_{\odot}
primary star is distributed around it. The material in the torus originates mostly from the
9\,M_{\odot}
primary. The rotationally-supported torus has a mass of
{\approx}3\,M_{\odot}
and holds
{\approx}\,60\%
of the initial angular momentum of the binary.

•

There is hardly any dynamic mass loss and only
{\approx}\,0.14\%
of the total mass is ejected.

•

We observe robust magnetic-field amplification by more than ten orders of magnitude. The magnetic-field growth is initially sourced by turbulent energy in the merger and then by large-scale, ordered azimuthal fluid motions. In the end, the magnetic field energy reaches a super-equipartition level with respect to the kinetic energy in the radial and
z
-direction, i.e. our proxy of the turbulent energy. The final outcome appears robust and does not depend on the initial separation of the binary, the numerical resolution and the strength of the seed magnetic field. We thus conclude that the memory of the initial seed magnetic field is lost.

•

The magnetic field is initially amplified by small-scale turbulent dynamos invoking the Kelvin-Helmholtz (shear) and magneto-rotational instabilities. Later, the large-scale, ordered azimuthal fluid motions drive another dynamo process that pumps the magnetic energy from small (
{\approx}\,0.1\,R_{\odot}
) to large (
{\approx}\,5\,R_{\odot}
) scales. The magnetic energy power spectrum in the final merger structure follows that of the kinetic energy and the merger remnant is threaded by a large-scale magnetic field.

•

The final magnetic-field geometry is characterised by a large-scale poloidal and toroidal structure with a still non-negligible small-scale component. The field structure resembles those found by
Braithwaite & Spruit 2004
and
Braithwaite & Nordlund 2006b
to be stable over thermal up to possibly nuclear timescales.

•

The spherically symmetric and pressure-supported central parts quickly approach solid-body rotation and transition into a Keplerian-like rotation profile in the torus.

This work is only the beginning of a detailed exploration of the complex physics in stellar mergers and many questions remain unanswered. The stability of the amplified magnetic fields needs to be assessed and the further evolution of the star–torus structure will determine the exact properties of merged stars such as their rotational velocities and surface chemical chemistry
(
Schneider et al. 2020
, see also)
. Addressing these questions is essential to further understand the properties of magnetic massive stars and their possible highly-magnetic white-dwarf and neutron-star remnants (cf. polars and magnetars).
