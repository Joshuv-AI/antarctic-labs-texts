RV-PA Uncoupling
Authors: Khung Keong Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Predicting which patients would respond favorably to mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) in secondary (functional) mitral regurgitation (FMR) has been the subject of much research and discussion. The early debate over the discordant results of MITRA-FR1 and COAPT2 studies led to the concept of proportionate and disproportionate FMR3, and the importance of an enlarged left ventricular. The more recent RESHAPE-HF2 study4 has further deepened our understanding, underscoring that these 3 groups of patients have subtle differences and represent a continuum of worsening heart failure and differences in extent of MR. With newer pharmacologic therapies like Entresto and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2-inhibitors, the armamentarium for heart failure is now more potent, and patients are benefiting from the 4 pillars of therapy. Patients with severe FMR who continue to have significant symptoms are those who may benefit from M-TEER. Yet some patients continue to do poorly despite procedural success in reducing FMR. How then do we identify these patients?

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Miyahara et al5 describe their findings from the Japanese OCEAN-Mitral registry. This consented cohort of patients with FMR treated with M-TEER yields knowledge that deepens our understanding of FMR and the physiological interplay in the failing heart. The study has significant limitations that are well articulated by the investigators, with significant missing data and the absence of a core echocardiography laboratory as notable ones. Several statistical methods are also used to account for these missing data. Another major concern is that the post-TEER echocardiogram seems to be from within the same admission, and follow-up echocardiogram subsequently is not available. Notwithstanding these considerations, the key findings are that impaired right ventricular-pulmonary artery (RV-PA) coupling after M-TEER is independently associated with a higher risk of composite outcomes in patients with ventricular FMR, particularly those with pulmonary hypertension. Importantly, the investigators also found that impaired coupling pre-TEER was not associated with composite outcomes—although it was a predictor of impaired coupling post-TEER. Impaired coupling post-TEER in the pulmonary hypertension group was also associated with worse outcomes compared with those with pulmonary hypertension.

These findings enhance our understanding of how M-TEER may benefit patients with FMR. Perhaps the most striking observation is that the 2 groups of patients—those with impaired coupling post-TEER and those with preserved coupling—were starkly different at baseline. The impaired group was more likely to have prior cardiac surgery, have atrial fibrillation or flutter, NYHA functional class III/IV, worse EuroSCORE 2 and Society of Thoracic Surgeons score, worse renal function, and higher N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide. Their echocardiogram parameters were generally also worse, with lower ejection fraction, (much) larger left atria, worse RV function, higher systolic PA pressures, and a greater proportion of patients with significant tricuspid regurgitation. Collectively, these demographic, biomarker, and echocardiographic features suggest that the patients with impaired coupling post-TEER are simply sicker patients whose hearts are further along the downward trajectory of heart failure. Although the investigators have accounted for these differences with statistical adjustments, there are likely unmeasured or unmeasurable markers. From a clinical standpoint, a clinician would recognize these patients with a group of FMR patients anyway as higher risk and would likely predict them as having a worse outcome over time. At the same time, having recognized these patients as being higher risk, a clinician would nonetheless still offer M-TEER to ameliorate symptoms and perhaps change the clinical trajectory. The subsequent assessment of impaired coupling post-TEER would not be able to retrospectively alter the decision to perform M-TEER.

What this study shows is that in patients with advanced heart failure, uncoupling post-TEER likely identifies a sicker group of patients. In such patients, intensifying medical treatment after M-TEER would be critical. In patients who continue a downward clinical spiral, it may be important to identify them early for consideration for more advanced heart failure therapies like left ventricular assist device or heart transplant.

It was surprising that uncoupling pre-TEER was not a predictor of outcomes despite several studies demonstrating its utility as a prognostic marker.6,7 It is possible that these earlier studies from the West, with the earlier adoption of TEER technologies, have now influenced Japanese practice and perhaps influenced patient selection for offer M-TEER in patients with lesser degrees of right ventricular dysfunction. Regardless, the fact that uncoupling pre-TEER was predictive of uncoupling post-TEER is consistent with the overall conceptual framework that patients who are further along the heart failure trajectory are likely to do worse. It also reinforces what cardiac surgeons have known for a long time—that a weakened right heart threatens the whole heart.

Where do we go from here? Beyond being a prognostic marker, does acute RV-PA uncoupling by itself have any causal effect on worse clinical outcomes? Would interventions that protect the right ventricle or pretreatment of pulmonary hypertension with advanced pharmacologic agents or even new interventional therapies reduce the risk of uncoupling? These questions remain unanswered today. Miyahara’s paper is another valuable piece of the puzzle but much more work remains.
