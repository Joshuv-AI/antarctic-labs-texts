Secondary findings after multi-gene panel testing in 7,388 patients with suspected cancer predisposition syndrome
Authors: Eva Avsec, Ana Blatnik, Vita Šetrajčič Dragoš, Petra Škerl, Vida Stegel, Mateja Krajc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur study focused on the individuals’ preference for disclosure of secondary findings (SF) in hereditary cancer genes (HCG) and the frequency and type of SF after multi-gene panel testing (MGPT) in individuals with a suspected cancer predisposition syndrome (CPS). SF are defined as pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (P/LPV) that were not anticipated and without a known correlation to the primary indication for genetic testing. 7,388 individuals with a suspected CPS were tested in a 6-year period 2019–2024. SF in HCG were reported in agreement between clinical and laboratory geneticists. Data about the reported SF and preference to receive SF was acquired from patient medical documentation. Our study showed that majority of patients (98.8%) opted-in for disclosure of SF. We identified 42 SF (0.57% of all tested individuals) in 41 individuals. 11 were found in the HCG from the ACMG list, 28 in HCG not included in ACMG list and 3 were discovered in mosaic form. Our study provides important insight into frequency and type of SF in HCG in a cohort of individuals with suspected CPS. Further studies on larger cohorts are needed to better define the usefulness and potential risks of reporting SF in HCG in individuals without personal/family history of cancer.Keywords: Secondary findings, Genetic testing, Hereditary cancerSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Genetics, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Rising use of panel, exome, and genome analyses with next-generation sequencing has resulted in increased detection of secondary findings (SF). American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) defines SF as pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (P/LPV) in genes, which are a part of predefined list of potentially medically actionable genes and are deliberately searched for when a genetic test is performed, but are not related to the primary indication for genetic testing1. The first list was published in 2013 and has been updated several times since1–5. The latest ACMG 3.3 list with 84 genes was published in May 20256 and includes 28 hereditary cancer genes (HCG). According to the ACMG, this is a minimum list of genes intended to reduce morbidity and mortality, rather than a comprehensive list of all potentially actionable genetic results. They also acknowledged that some laboratories might include additional genes in their reports. The term SF should be distinguished from the term incidental finding, which refers to a result of a genetic test that is unexpected, unrelated to the primary indication for genetic testing and was discovered by chance and not during deliberate screening2.

Large population studies7–12 show the frequency of all SF to be between 1 and 7%, while frequency of SF in HCG is estimated to be 0.4–3.1%13. There is lack of data on frequency of SF in HCG after multi-gene panel testing (MGPT) for suspected cancer predisposition syndrome (CPS), even though panel analysis is a common practice in cancer-related genetic testing14.

According to different authors7,12,15, majority of patients who undergo genetic testing opt-in for disclosure of medically actionable SF and this is also true for patients, tested for suspected CPS16,17. Data on patient preference is still limited in the setting of MGPT for suspected CPS and was reported by Nambot et al., where 100% patients opted-in for disclosure of SF16. Analysis of a smaller Slovenian cohort similarly showed 98.4% of tested individuals consented to receiving SF in this setting18.

In oncology, the extensive use of exome sequencing or multi-gene panel analyses to investigate CPS has uncovered P/LPV in HCG not directly related to the initial focus of genetic testing. Additionally, tumour tissue testing can reveal somatic variants as well as potential germline SF in HCG19,20. This consequently results in increased detection of potentially clinically significant germline P/LPV beyond the expected yield of targeted germline testing.

The interpretation of SF in HCG in cancer patients can be particularly complex. Most often, the spectrum of cancers associated with a specific CPS is relatively well defined and carriers are considered to be at population risk for all other cancers. But in many cases the causal relationship between a variant and the observed cancer cannot be definitively established or ruled out without additional supportive molecular evidence such as immunohistochemical staining or genetic analysis of tumour tissue. The spectrum of cancers associated with P/LPV in specific HCG continues to evolve. On the other hand, interpretation of P/LPV in HCG without a known personal and/or family history of cancer is complicated as well considering the uncertainties regarding penetrance in HCG identified to date. With these questions unanswered, there is an ongoing need for further research regarding SF in HCG.

Aim of the study

In this retrospective single-centre study we aimed to assess the frequency and type of SF in HCG after MGPT for suspected CPS. We also assessed the patient preference within the subgroup of patients who signed a consent form for reporting SF after its implementation in January 2020.

Materials and methods

Patients and genetic testing

Our study is a single-centre retrospective study. 11,477 patients were referred to our Department of Clinical Cancer Genetics, Institute of Oncology Ljubljana, in a 6-year period from January 2019 to December 2024. 4089 patients were excluded from our study due to different reasons: 1,854 individuals were offered targeted genetic testing for a known familial P/LPV variant, 214 had targeted genetic test for variants discovered in their tumour tissue and 2021 individuals did not fulfil the criteria for genetic testing at the time of consultation and were therefore not offered genetic testing. Genomic DNA was extracted from the patients’ peripheral blood samples as described previously21. MGPT was performed using next-generation sequencing with the TruSight Hereditary Cancer panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as described previously21 in 7,388 individuals with suspected CPS at the Department of Molecular Diagnostics, Institute of Oncology Ljubljana. All 7,388 panel-tested patients were adults, aged 18 years or older and had a suspected CPS due to their personal and/or family history of cancer. All cancer diagnoses in patients and their families were verified in the national Slovenian Cancer Registry.

Ethics declaration

Our study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and all later revisions. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians for molecular genetic analyses and publication of results. We confirm that all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Approval for studies that include a retrospective analysis of genetic and clinical data from tested individuals for the presence of germline mutations that confer a moderate to high risk of cancer, was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Oncology (Ref. No. ERIDNPVO-0025/2020, dated October 2, 2020).

Definition of secondary findings

We defined findings as secondary if they were not anticipated after personal and family history assessment and clinical examination at the pre-test genetic consultation. Our policy for reporting SF findings is reporting all potentially actionable P/LPV in HCG included in the TruSight Hereditary Cancer panel (Illumina) based on the consensus between specialists of laboratory and clinical genetics. Carriership of heterozygous P/LPV for autosomal recessive diseases is not reported as a SF. Clinical geneticist orders the test for specific subset of genes according to the primary indication for genetic testing. Primary indication for MGPT and the virtual panels for every indication are listed in Table 1. All other findings are considered secondary and are deliberately searched for every time a patient opts-in for disclosure of SF.

To assess the frequency and type of reported SF and the preference of the patients for receiving SF in HCG, data were collected from patient medical documentation at our Department of Clinical Cancer Genetics. From 20 January 2020 a written consent form for opting-in for reporting SF in HCG was introduced at our clinic. Before this date, SF in HCG were reported to all patients, tested with next-generation sequencing.

A thorough analysis of family trees with verification of cancer diagnoses in the Slovenian Cancer Registry was performed in individuals with reported SF. Reporting all cancer diagnoses to Slovenian Cancer registry is mandatory by law in Slovenia since 1950. When individuals’ relatives live abroad, the diagnoses of cancer can only be confirmed by obtaining medical documentation of the relatives in question.

For the purpose of this analysis, we categorized all SF into 4 groups. Group 1 consists of P/LPV in HCG from the ACMG 3.3 minimum list6. Group 2 includes P/LPV in HCG that are not included in the ACMG 3.3 list but are potentially medically actionable. We further divided them into 3 subgroups: 2a, 2b and 2c. Group 2a includes P/LPV in HBOC genes, group 2b includes P/LPV associated with familial prostate cancer and group 2c includes P/LPV in other potentially actionable HCG. Group 3 includes mosaic forms of SF. Group 4 includes patients carrying APC(NM_000038.5):c.3920T > A variant, which represents a potential risk factor for developing colon cancer. According to latest recommendations this variant is considered non-actionable in non-Ashkenazi Jewish population22- we therefore do not report this variant as SF anymore and we have deliberately not analysed Group 4 further. Results

Preference for disclosure of SF

Consent form for SF disclosure was implemented at our clinic on January 20, 2020. In the observation period 6,388 (out of 7,388 patients in our cohort) signed this form. 6313 (98.8%) of patients opted-in for reporting of SF, 75 patients chose to opt-out and did not wish to receive SF.

Frequency and type of secondary findings

We reported 42 SF (0.57% of all tested individuals) in 41 individuals. We further divided them into 3 groups as presented in Fig. 2. Additionally, we reported a risk factor in APC gene (c.3920T > A) as a SF in 23 individuals (0.31% of all tested individuals) – group 4, which we have deliberately not analysed further as described in Methods.

Discussion

Our aim was to assess the frequency and type of SF in HCG after MGPT for suspected CPS in individuals with personal and/or family history of cancer and the patient preference to know these findings.

Our reported frequency of SF in HCG of 0.57% is lower than the frequency of SF in HCG individuals with suspected CPS of 2%, previously published by Henn et al.23, where the observed cohort was much smaller, but comparable to the frequency of 1% described by Horiuchi et al.17 and 0.6% by Nambot et al.16 in larger cohorts. Our observed frequency of reported SF of 0,57% excludes the risk factor in APC gene, which we reported until 2023. The majority (67%) of our SF were reported in HCG, not included in the ACMG 3.3 list, but are still potentially actionable.

Spectrum of SF described in our study is different than SF reported in previous studies13, where most SF were found in high risk HBOC and Lynch genes - the most common CPS, prevalent in approximately 2% of the human population24. In comparison, we found more P/LPV in moderate HBOC risk genes and a risk factor for prostate cancer as well as in genes for rare CPS with specific clinical features, that are sometimes hard to recognize at initial genetic consultation (Table 2).

The difference between previously described SF in HCG and our findings can partially be attributed to our inhouse rules for reporting SF as described in Methods (not only HCG on the ACMG list). Lower frequency of highly penetrant SF could also reflect our thorough assessment of personal and family history of cancer due to the verification of all cancer diagnoses in the Slovenian Cancer Registry and our intentional inquiry about possible non-cancerous signs and symptoms of CPS. It should be kept in mind that identification of CPS solely on patient reported personal and family history of cancer is limited, since some cancer diagnoses cannot be verified due to lack of personal data or relatives living abroad. Furthermore, reduced penetrance of certain variants even in HCG typically considered highly penetrant, may result in negative personal and/or family history of cancer.

Sometimes the reported SF in HCG are not necessarily secondary. Performing additional medical investigations such as tumour tissue staining and thorough family history analysis can give us valuable additional information that makes discovered P/LPV not secondary anymore, but rather not expected with available data at initial pre-test genetic counselling. This is often true for P/LPV in very rare CPS25.

One of our additional goals was to assess the patient preference for reporting SF in HCG following the implementation of consent form for SF disclosure in January 2020. Our results are in concordance with the previously published papers13. A great majority of patients in our cohort wished to receive SF in HCG. The share of individuals who opt-in for disclosure of SF is amongst the highest described in comparable studies, which can be attributed to the fact that our tested individuals are cancer patients and/or they have close relatives, who are cancer patients. We assume they are concerned about cancer diagnoses and wish to be familiar with any potential risk for developing any cancer type.

The number of unexpectedly discovered CPS in our cohort was not negligible. These findings could significantly impact individuals’ lives, the positive side being the possibility of surveillance and risk reductive measures, which could lead to cancer prevention and/or earlier cancer diagnoses, and the negative side being the possibility of anxiety and long-term emotional disorders. A significant concern for optimal management of healthy individuals after reporting SF in HCG there is often a lack of practical guidelines and recommendations for surveillance, often attributed to the lack of information regarding psychological impact and the effectiveness of proposed surveillance measures. The limited data so far13 show the patients benefit from disclosure of SF in HCG without significant psychological burden. In addition, finances are limited in existing healthcare systems – cost benefit was proven for high-risk SF for HBOC and Lynch syndrome26,27 but not for other, less penetrant, moderate risk genes. Healthcare professionals should proceed with caution when managing patients with SF in HCG with a negative family history of the cancer type associated with the SF, since risk assessment is especially difficult in these individuals. Factors such as lower penetrance, unknown protective factors, or even issues with paternity could influence the real cancer risk.

In this study, we describe what is essentially opportunistic screening for CPS in individuals with personal and/or family history of cancer. It is crucial to do a thorough pre-test genetic consultation with explanation of the consequences of SF in HCG. These individuals should see a specialist for risk communication, and they should be referred to surveillance and/or risk reduction measures and also offered psychological support. This holds true for every individual in such a situation, so good organisation and availability of further surveillance programs must be established. Opportunistic screening not only requires financial resources for genetic testing but also creates a significant strain on both medical personnel and healthcare finances.

To sum up, we discovered 42 SF in 41 individuals in potentially medically actionable HCG, and all the individuals were offered post-test genetic appointment about the CPS, additional medical investigation for better risk assessment if needed, surveillance programs according to the available guidelines and their relatives were offered an option of cascade testing for reported SF.

Our perspective is that opportunistic screening and reporting of SF in HCG, even those not considered on the ACMG minimum list, is valuable. These individuals can be offered surveillance in accordance with the guidelines and recommendations available at the time of disclosure, same as for primary findings. For example, female carriers of P/LPV in ATM and CHEK2 can be referred for breast surveillance (annual mammography and annual breast MRI) and male carriers of HOXB13 P/LPV can undergo prostate cancer screening with yearly prostate-specific antigen measurements28. In addition, cascade genetic testing may be offered to their relatives.

Apart from the possibility of optimal management of patients, it could also contribute to better understanding the cancer spectrum, expressivity, and penetrance in various CPS. However, there is still lack of data on the long-term psychological impact and benefits for the patients and on the other hand the extent of financial/personnel burden on existing healthcare systems associated with proposed surveillance and risk reduction measures. Our intention is to follow these patients in the long run to see the impact of SF disclosure and effectiveness of patient management.

We consider the size of our cohort and the reliability of our data as all diagnoses of cancer were verified in the national cancer registry to be among the strengths of our study. On the other hand, the fact this is a retrospective analysis focusing on patients who underwent limited panel testing for cancer-related genes only, could be seen as a limitation as our results cannot be automatically extrapolated to other fields of clinical genetics.

Conclusion

Our study provides important insight in SF in HCG when the primary indication for genetic testing is suspected CPS. Thorough analyses of personal and family history of cancer can provide additional information about the spectrum of cancer types in specific CPS. Further studies on larger cohorts are needed to better define the value of reporting SF in HCG in individuals without personal/family history of cancer. Evidence of its cost-benefits could offer a valuable piece of information before implementing population screening for CPS, in accordance with government health policy.
