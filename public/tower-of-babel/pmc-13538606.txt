Hierarchical multi-label structuring of Japanese SOAP clinical notes with large language models
Authors: Ziheng Zhang, Minghui Tang, Feng Han, Hidenori Kitai, Kenji Hirata, Katsuhiko Ogasawara, Satoshi Konno, Kohsuke Kudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMuch clinically salient information in Japanese electronic medical records is recorded in SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) notes, which contain a lot of unstructured text preventing the further usage for analysis. Structuring such unstructured text is essential for secondary data use and clinical research. This study aimed to develop a schema-guided, hierarchical multi-label (15 first-level and 58 second-level labels) framework for structuring respiratory medicine SOAP notes using a locally deployed large language model (LLaMA-3.1-8B adapted with QLoRA). 200 randomly selected notes were annotated and split in two for training and test. Performance was assessed through atomic-cell matching under both strict and tolerant similarity metrics, achieved a micro-averaged F1 of 0.49 (strict) and 0.59 (Levenshtein> 0.7). Common and standardized fields such as vital signs were reliably structured, while ambiguous boundaries and rare labels remained challenging. These findings demonstrate that schema-guided hierarchical multi-label frameworks can support the structuring of Japanese clinical notes and serve as a foundation for downstream research and quality-improvement workflows.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Electronic medical records (EMRs) contain both structured fields (e.g., laboratory test values, medication prescriptions) and unstructured free-text notes that capture rich contextual details of patient care1. A significant portion of clinically relevant information is locked in these narrative texts, which are difficult to process due to their narrative format, specialized jargon, and variability in writing style2–5. Therefore, converting free-text clinical notes into structured data is essential for supporting clinical decision-making and enabling secondary uses such as outcomes analysis and phenotyping. It has become increasingly important in the era of artificial intelligence (AI) and data-driven medicine6,7.

To address the challenge of structuring free text EMRs, previous studies have applied various natural language processing (NLP) approaches. Traditionally, these approaches have included rule-based systems and machine learning models, which attempt to extract structured information by encoding domain knowledge or learning from annotated corpora8–11. While rule-based methods can capture explicit heuristics, they often lack generalizability across institutions and documentation styles12. Machine learning and early transformer-based methods such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) require substantial annotated datasets, which are costly and labor-intensive to obtain in the medical domain13,14. These limitations are further amplified in Japanese, where the absence of whitespace, widespread abbreviations, and frequent embedding of English medical terms make parsing especially difficult15.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a promising avenue for tackling unstructured clinical text16–18. Generative LLMs (e.g., GPT-3/4/5, LLaMA3/4) can integrate domain knowledge and handle long contexts through zero- or few-shot prompts, without task-specific retraining19–21. Early studies show LLMs can directly extract patient information from raw, unstructured notes with encouraging accuracy22. However, concerns remain: LLM outputs may introduce factual errors, omit subtle details, or deviate from predefined schema formats23–25. Additionally, controlling the structure of LLM outputs remains difficult, as these models are inclined to free-text generation, so ensuring that extracted information strictly follows a predefined schema or JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format requires additional strategies26,27. These observations underline the need for careful prompt design and structuring constraints when applying LLMs to clinical note processing.

Another practical issue is the labeling strategy. Flat, single-level classification tends to mishandle overlapping or borderline sections, producing incoherent or incompatible assignments. Hierarchical classification, by contrast, enforces parent-child consistency28. Although it cannot fully resolve overlaps or borderline ambiguities inherent in clinical narratives, it would improve structural coherence relative to flat baselines in many domains28. For schema-constrained generation, recent work has shown that guiding LLMs to respect target structures can reduce format drift and spurious fields26. Building on these insights, a multi-level schema may provide both coarse section cues and a focused set of permissible subfields, which could help models localize content and reduce off-schema outputs.

To ground these considerations in a concrete setting, we focus on the SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) note format, a widely used documentation style for recording patient encounters29. In Japan, many hospitals and clinics use SOAP in their EMRs to ensure that key information is captured under standardized headings, and the same four sections serve as the organizing principle30. Although SOAP organizes content by section, entries within each section remain free text, even abbreviated or uneven details31. Specifically, SOAP notes of respiratory medicine cover a diverse spectrum of common conditions (e.g., pneumonia, asthma, lung cancer, interstitial lung disease), ensuring both variety and clinical relevance. In addition, they typically include standardized fields such as Vital Signs while also containing rich unstructured symptom descriptions, making them a suitable and representative testbed for evaluating schema-guided structuring. We therefore target SOAP notes of respiratory medicine as its internal variability makes the benefits and limitations of schema-guided approaches observable in practice.

In this study, we aimed to develop and evaluate a hierarchical, schema-guided approach for structuring Japanese SOAP notes of respiratory medicine using LLMs. From a methodological perspective, this study explores schema design, prompt engineering, and domain adaptation strategies for clinical information extraction. From an applied perspective, it evaluates the feasibility of LLM-based systems as support tools for structured data entry in electronic medical records, highlighting their potential benefits and current limitations.

Methods

Task overview

The study focuses on Japanese SOAP notes from the department of respiratory medicine, with the objective of identifying and categorizing key elements such as Chief Complaint, History, Vital Signs, Laboratory Examination, and Treatment Plan. We designed a two-level hierarchical multi-label schema. The First-level labels represent broad clinical categories, while the Second-level labels provide finer granularity, corresponding to specific medical fields (e.g., body temperature, chest X-ray findings, or medication names). The model input consisted of SOAP notes, and the output was constrained to a nested JSON object containing the extracted First-level and Second-level labels together with their corresponding contents. Data and annotation

Data sources and cohort

SOAP notes were used from the Department of Respiratory Medicine at Hokkaido University Hospital. The collection period spanned from February 22, 1968 to March 26, 2024, involving over several thousand patients and tens of thousands of clinical records. The predominant diagnoses included emphysema, lung tumors, bronchial asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, and pneumonia, which are among the most common conditions in respiratory medicine. From the full corpus, we randomly sampled 200 SOAP notes using Python’s built-in random module. This sample size was pre-specified to balance annotation feasibility with dataset diversity, and is comparable to prior Japanese clinical NLP corpora (typically comprise 100-200 documents) given the substantial human effort required for expert annotation32.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hokkaido University Hospital (approval no. 024-0206). Only patient IDs were accessed; no patient names were recorded. To preserve the originality of the texts, no de-identification or preprocessing was applied prior to annotation or modeling.

Label schema

We developed a two-level hierarchical multi-label schema tailored to SOAP notes. The First-level comprises 15 broad categories (e.g., Chief Complaint (CC), Vital Signs, Physical Examination, Treatment Plan), while the Second-level further specifies 58 fine-grained medical fields (e.g., Temperature (BT), Blood Pressure (BP), X-ray Imaging, Oral administration) as shown in Table 1. This label schema was initially derived from prior studies29,30, and then refined under the guidance of a respiratory medicine physician to adapt the framework to the characteristics of Japanese SOAP notes in the local respiratory medicine setting. For clarity of presentation, only the English label names are shown in the main text and tables; however, in the actual annotation process, all labels were used in a Japanese-English bilingual form, ensuring consistency with the original clinical documentation while maintaining cross-lingual interpretability (see Supplementary Table S1 for the complete bilingual label list).

Annotation

20 notes were first pilot-annotated under the direct supervision of the respiratory medicine physician, who helped establish standard exemplars. Based on these annotated notes, two medical AI researchers from the Faculty of Medicine (a faculty member and a Ph.D. candidate) then annotated the remaining 180 notes according to the schema and rules, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. All annotations were subsequently reviewed and necessary corrections were made by the respiratory medicine physician.

Annotation was conducted using Label Studio (version 1.13.0, HumanSignal Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA)33. Across the 200 notes, we recorded 259,366 characters (≈ 1,300 characters per note on average) and 4,609 annotations (≈ 23 annotations per note; each annotation ranged from a few characters to several hundred).

Prompt design and model training

We selected the open-source large language model LLaMA 3.1-8B Instruct released by Meta AI34 as the base model because the LLaMA family provides strong performance and scalability, and it supports local deployment, which is critical to ensure data privacy and compliance with ethical regulations in clinical research35.

We guided the model with a schema-constrained system prompt that enumerated the two-level label hierarchy and specified a nested JSON output format. The prompt emphasized that labels must exactly match the whitelist and that outputs must be valid JSON starting with “{” and ending with “}”. We avoided template text that could leak gold content. Model fine-tuning was performed using QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation; implemented in the Hugging Face Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) library, version 0.5.0, Hugging Face Inc., New York, NY, USA) in combination with the Hugging Face PEFT framework36,37. The training followed the QLoRA approach, in which the base model was loaded in 4-bit quantization38 via bitsandbytes (version 0.43.0, an open-source library for 8-bit and 4-bit optimization of large models) and fine-tuned with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules. Training scripts were implemented using the Hugging Face Transformers (version 4.52.4) and PyTorch (version 2.6.0, CUDA 12.1) library39. To optimize memory usage and efficiency, we enabled gradient checkpoint and employed bfloat16 (Brain Floating Point 16-bit) precision during training. The maximum input length was set to 4,096 tokens, with a learning rate of 3e-4, and training proceeded for 8 epochs. We randomly selected 100 examples as the training set and the remaining 100 examples as the evaluation set from the labeled dataset. Additional implementation and environment details are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Note that, the systematic hyperparameter optimization was not performed.

All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper Pro 3955WX CPU, an NVIDIA A6000 GPU (48 GB memory), 251 GB RAM, and Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS. All scripts were written in Python (version 3.10.12). Parameter-efficient fine-tuning was implemented with PEFT (v0.5.0), with multi-GPU training management via Accelerate (version 0.29.1, a Hugging Face framework for distributed and mixed-precision training).

Inference

After model training, we applied the same System Prompt to run inference on the trained model using the remaining 100 annotated SOAP notes. For each SOAP note, the full text of the note was input to the LLM along with the fixed prompt template. We set temperature = 0 to enforce deterministic, reproducible outputs, and the maximum generation length = 4,096 tokens to accommodate long structured responses. The System Prompt constrained generation to a nested JSON that adheres strictly to our predefined schema. Outputs that failed to conform to valid JSON syntax or exhibited structural inconsistencies (including completeness) were automatically flagged and recorded, enabling subsequent analysis and correction. To contextualize the contribution of domain adaptation in our schema-guided framework, we additionally evaluated the base LLaMA 3.1-8B model without fine-tuning using the same prompt template, decoding settings, and test set as the fine-tuned model; this setting was treated as a zero-shot schema-guided baseline because no task-specific input-output examples were included in the prompt.

Evaluation methods and metrics

To comprehensively assess performance on the structured medical information extraction task, we conducted quantitative evaluation at three levels: overall performance, per-label performance, and off-schema label analysis. The evaluation unit was an atomic cell, which was defined as a single aligned field-value pair in the structured output. This notion is conceptually related to atomic decomposition used in long-form factuality evaluation40. Before evaluation, all inference outputs underwent structural and format validation. We retained only outputs that could be successfully parsed as valid JSON and were not truncated, ensuring completeness and validity of the evaluation set.

Field definition

Each label–content pair at the lowest level of the schema (an “atomic cell”) was treated as an evaluation field. For simplicity, all subsequent analyses refer to these units as “fields”. According to the property of the content, two fields were defined.

Overall performance evaluation

We compared all model-generated atomic cells (including off-schema labels) with the gold standard, and reported micro-averaged Precision, Recall, and F1 under each of the four matching strategies above. In addition to raw outputs, we recomputed overall micro-averaged metrics after whitelist filtering, which removes off-schema labels while leaving true positives and false negatives unchanged. Together, the raw results represent the end-to-end performance of the model without external validation, whereas the filtered results illustrate the attainable performance when lightweight schema validation is applied.

Results

Input/output completeness

During training, 19 out of 100 samples were truncated due to exceeding the context window, with the longest sequence reaching 8,125 tokens. Regarding the output completeness, 24 were truncated among the 100 test cases due to exceeding the token limit. Subsequent analyses of overall performance, label level results, and off-schema patterns were conducted on these 76 complete cases. For the baseline (no fine-tuning) setting, 87 test outputs were parseable as valid JSON, while 13 were invalid.

Overall performance

Table 2 summarizes the overall extraction performance of the model. As the matching criteria were gradually relaxed from Strict to Normalized, then to Jaccard similarity> 0.7 and Levenshtein similarity> 0.7, both Precision and Recall consistently improved, leading to higher F1-scores. Under the strictest criterion, the model achieved an F1-score of 0.49, with Precision = 0.48 and Recall = 0.50. When evaluated with the most lenient criterion (Levenshtein similarity> 0.7), the F1-score increased to 0.59, with Precision = 0.58 and Recall = 0.61.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated a hierarchical, schema-guided framework that guides LLMs to perform hierarchical multi-label extraction of clinical information from Japanese SOAP notes, a task that involves handling heterogeneous, free-text content while conforming to a predefined schema. We designed a two-level annotation schema covering both high-level categories and fine-grained fields, applied QLoRA fine-tuning to a LLaMA 3.1-8B model, and developed an evaluation framework that assessed outputs at the level of atomic field-value pairs under multiple matching criteria.

Overall performance was considered reasonable given the multi-label and hierarchical nature of the task, the small corpus size (200 annotated notes), and the linguistic variability of Japanese clinical text. The model achieved a micro-averaged F1 of 0.49 under strict matching, which improved to 0.59 with a Levenshtein similarity > 0.7 including off-schema labels. In addition, a secondary analysis that removed off-schema labels demonstrated consistent gains: precision improved while recall remained unchanged, leading to higher overall F1-scores across all matching criteria. This contrast highlights that a substantial share of performance loss in the raw evaluation was attributable to schema-invalid predictions, and that lightweight schema validation could directly improve effective precision in practice.

For overall performance at First-level, the micro-averaged F1 was 0.47 under strict matching and 0.63 with Levenshtein matching; at the more granular Second-level, strict F1 reached 0.61 and likewise rose to 0.67 with Levenshtein matching, suggesting that once off-schema labels were removed, the model performed robustly for high-frequency fine-grained categories. This gap between strict and more tolerant criteria suggests that many predictions were close to the gold standard but differed in minor ways (e.g., formatting, symbols, or scope). Consequently, looser matching yielded higher recall and improved F1-scores, indicating that tolerant evaluation may better capture the model’s effective understanding. The baseline experiment indicates that the base model without domain-specific adaptation performs poorly in this schema-constrained structuring task, even when evaluated on parseable outputs. This finding suggests that fine-tuning (or other domain adaptation strategies) is necessary for reliable structured extraction under strict privacy-preserving, on-premise deployment constraints.

Regarding the performance at the label level, the model excelled at identifying some labels but struggled with others. For example, it recognized the Problems list and Imaging Examination labels with high reliability (strict F1 0.78 and 0.67, respectively), because these contents often contain distinctive cues. In contrast, it had difficulty with labels like Current Medications, Allergies and History. The History category (e.g., past medical or social history) was rarely detected, likely due to its infrequent or subtle presence in the notes and possibly because such information might be documented in free text without clear section headers. The Japan Coma Scale (JCS), a scoring system for consciousness level used exclusively in Japan45, appeared only once in our test set and was not predicted (F1=0). Targeted augmentation for rare sublabels should further stabilize performance where downstream workflows depend on recall. The Chief Complaint (CC) and History of Present Illness (HPI) were intermediate in performance (strict F1 0.25-0.30), and even under normalized matching their F1 remained only around 0.3-0.4. This suggests a genuine challenge in distinguishing those two closely related sections. It is plausible that content from a brief CC and the beginning of the HPI can overlap, making it hard for the model to decide which label to assign. This tendency also appears in annotation. For example, considering the Japanese phrase “no significant change”, if it appears at the beginning of a consultation note, an annotator might label it as part of the CC (indicating that the patient has no new complaints), while another might treat it as part of the HPI (as it relates to the patient’s current state). Such inconsistencies in labeling make it difficult for the model to learn a distinct pattern, reflected in the lower precision and recall for both labels. In particular, certain labels showed drastic improvements when evaluated with partial-credit metrics, highlighting a specific type of model error. A striking example is Laboratory Examination: under strict matching the F1 was only 0.18, but with Levenshtein similarity > 0.7 it jumped to 0.87. In other words, the model often output content very similar to the gold text, but not exactly the same string. The results of the performance at the label level suggests that a substantial proportion of extracted field–value pairs are close to the physician-verified reference, particularly for standardized and frequently documented items (e.g., vital signs and common laboratory fields). In practical terms, the system may be useful for secondary data use such as cohort identification, registry construction, and pre-populating structured fields to support downstream analytics and quality-improvement workflows. However, the system is not intended to replace clinical documentation, and rare labels and semantically overlapping categories (e.g., CC vs HPI) still require human review.

To our knowledge, there are no directly comparable studies that perform hierarchical multi-label structuring of Japanese SOAP notes, which limits the possibility of head-to-head accuracy comparisons. Prior Japanese clinical NLP work has largely focused on single-level extraction tasks or binary classification, typically with smaller corpora and different evaluation setups (e.g., NTCIR (NII Testbeds and Community for Information access Research) MedNLP tasks), and English benchmarks like the i2b2 (Informatics for Integrating Biology and the Bedside) typically report higher F1 in single-level settings (e.g., F1 scores of approximately 0.85 for i2b2 concept extraction and 0.74 for relation extraction when evaluated under gold concept assumptions), reflecting simpler label spaces and evaluation pipelines32,46. Our primary objective was to develop and evaluate a schema-guided large language model framework capable of structuring Japanese SOAP notes into a hierarchical, multi-label representation. We operationalized “feasibility” as producing valid, non-truncated JSON for most notes, and achieving field-level accuracy sufficient for standardized, high-frequency items after validation. In our evaluation, 76/100 notes yielded complete outputs; micro-F1 reached 0.49 (strict) and 0.59 (Levenshtein > 0.7), improving to 0.54/0.65 with a simple whitelist filter, and standardized sections such as vital signs and common laboratory items attained > 0.8 and even > 0.9 under tolerant matching. The model’s performance on many sections (like Problems, Vital Signs, Imaging Examination) suggests it was able to parse the Japanese content effectively. These results indicate that, with lightweight schema control, the pipeline achieves practical feasibility for downstream use while clarifying where errors remain.

The use of a hierarchical taxonomy influenced the model’s behavior in beneficial ways. By organizing predictions into First-level and Second-level categories, the model maintained a logically consistent structure. This aligns with the known constraint in hierarchical classification that when a subclass is predicted, all its superclasses should be automatically included47. Compared to a traditional “flat” multi-label classifier which would treat all labels independently, it is reported that hierarchical classifiers usually outperformed flat classifiers or at least avoid more serious misclassification errors28. In our case, the hierarchy likely improved precision by narrowing the model’s focus: once the model identified a top-level category (e.g. Vital Signs), it could limit its second-level predictions to that context, reducing spurious labels. This is reflected in the Second-level performance, which was fairly high for many subcategories despite the additional specificity. Furthermore, the hierarchical method mirrors the way clinicians structure medical notes, which may have implicitly helped the model organize information more naturally. Beyond improving consistency, the hierarchical design also ensures both scalability and interpretability: it enables extraction at a higher categorical level while still supporting fine-grained, field-level analysis, which is particularly valuable for downstream tasks such as clinical information retrieval and risk prediction. This dual-level flexibility enhances the practical utility of language models in medical text processing.

Regarding off-schema, most off-schema labels were repairable label issues rather than fabricated clinical facts. Only around 5% were over-specifications that created an unnecessary third level. Only one case completely missed the two-tier structure. A possible solution to reduce such errors could be integrating a post-processing step to validate and correct the model’s output against the allowed label set. Another approach could be to employ few-shot examples or fine-tuning so that the model adheres more strictly to the exact labels. Although label normalization is feasible given the predefined whitelist, we intentionally did not apply automatic post-hoc normalization in the primary evaluation. Our objective was to evaluate the model’s intrinsic ability to adhere to the schema and to characterize off-schema label errors without masking them via heuristic correction. In practical deployments, lightweight normalization (e.g., whitelist mapping) could further improve effective precision and usability and will be explored in future work.

Our study has several limitations. First, model choice was constrained by hardware, particularly the available context window: larger models such as LLaMA-3.3-70B or LLaMA-4-17B were impractical for on-premise deployment under long input windows, and even the selected LLaMA-3.1-8B was limited to a maximum context length of 4,096 tokens, which occasionally led to output truncation and may have capped performance. Second, the dataset comprised 200 SOAP notes from a single respiratory department; while randomly sampled, this scope may limit generalizability across institutions and specialties, and borderline categories (e.g., CC vs. HPI) remained ambiguous. Third, although schema control reduced errors or off-schema labels (n = 302) still occurred, highlighting the need for stricter constrained decoding. Fourth, our evaluation emphasized micro-averaged F1 and tolerant string similarity, which may overstate practical correctness; future work should include macro-averaged metrics and expert adjudication. Fifth, we did not perform extrinsic evaluation with clinical experts or external validation on data from other institutions, which are important to establish robustness, clinical usability, and operational impact. Finally, hyperparameters were not tuned using a validation set, and training was conducted with a fixed configuration, which may result in suboptimal performance. Future work will explore systematic hyperparameter tuning using a dedicated validation set or cross-validation on larger multi-institutional datasets.

This study shows that a schema-guided LLM can structure Japanese SOAP notes using a hierarchical multi-label formulation. It achieved a decent performance on a challenging information extraction task without specialized training data for each label, leveraging the language model’s capabilities, while challenges remained for ambiguous boundaries and rare labels, as well as for controlling off-schema labels generation.

Conclusion

This study developed a hierarchical multi-label LLM-based framework for structuring heterogeneous information from Japanese SOAP notes of respiratory medicine. By integrating a two-level schema and schema-constrained prompting, the method demonstrated feasibility and practical potential for structuring non-English clinical text. The framework produced reliable outputs for frequent and standardized fields, while challenges remained for ambiguous boundaries and rare labels, as well as for controlling off-schema labels generation. Overall, the study establishes a baseline for applying LLMs to Japanese clinical documentation and highlights the importance of schema control and rigorous evaluation.
