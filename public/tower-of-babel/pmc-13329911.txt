Contemporary management of fetal sustained supraventricular tachycardia guided by prenatal echocardiography
Authors: Anindya Ghosh, Chenni S. Sriram
Published: 2026

Fetal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) complicates 0.5% of all pregnancies and makes up to three quarters of all fetal arrhythmias. Timely diagnosis and treatment prevents fetal heart failure, hydrops, intra-uterine fatalities, and premature delivery with its attendant consequences. The risk of subsequent neuro-developmental complications (roughly 5% of infants overall and in 37.5% with fetal hydrops) and considerations for interventional approach (small size, vascular access) limits the options to pharmacological management of these arrhythmias. Fortunately, even hydrops fetalis is favorably amenable to resolution with appropriate therapy and long-term prognosis is good [1].

In the this issue of IPEJ, Ciriello et al. present their ten-year experience at a tertiary center in Southern Italy in diagnosing and managing fetal sustained SVT. Prenatal echocardiography findings determined the SVT subtype which allowed for tailored antiarrhythmic drug therapy. The results are reported, focusing on treatment effectiveness and outcomes [2].

The study included 32 fetuses diagnosed with sustained SVT over ten years. Data collated encompassed SVT type, gestational age at diagnosis, presence of structural heart disease, fetal heart failure or hydrops, maternal features, and other treatment outcomes. Fetal echocardiography involved advanced ultrasound techniques to analyze atrial and ventricular contractions on M-mode and pulsed-wave doppler as atrio-ventricular (AV) relationship surrogates on surface electrocardiogram. SVT was classified based on VA/AV interval and mechanism, including AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT), ectopic atrial tachycardia (EAT), atrial flutter (AFlu), and permanent form junctional of junctional reciprocating tachycardia (PJRT). Treatment utilized antiarrhythmic drugs used either alone or in combination [flecainide (F), digoxin (D), sotalol (S)] with doses (Flecainide 300-400 mg/day, digoxin 1 mg loading dose followed by 0.25-0.5 mg/day, sotalol 80 mg bid) titrated according to response and maternal safety. Postnatal management included clinical and echocardiographic assessments.

The cohort included 32 individual fetal SVT cases (16 AVRT, 7 EAT, 8 AFlu, and 1 PJRT) with average gestational age at diagnosis of 28.5 weeks and maternal age of 31.6 years. Three fetuses had structural heart disease (ventricular septal defect, truncus arteriosus, and cardiac rhabdomyoma). Roughly 41% (13/32) cases displayed signs of fetal heart failure without hydrops fetalis [8 (25%); AVRT in 4, EAT in 2, AFlu in 2] or hydrops [5 (16%; AVRT in 3, EAT in 1, AFlu in 1]. Eleven of these 13 patients were pharmacologically treated.

Maternal antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy was initiated for 27 (84%) fetal SVT cases. Monotherapy (F in 9, D in 7, and S in 2) sufficed in 18 cases. Three received sequential therapy when the first AAD failed (D→F in 2, D→S in 1). Polytherapy (D + F in 1, D + S in 4, D→F + S in 1) was required in 6 cases. Five fetuses were not treated because of loss to follow-up (n = 2), late presentation resulting in delivery (n = 1), and urgent caesarian section for hydrops fetalis (n = 2).

Therapy (n = 27) achieved complete rhythm control in 74% (n = 20) and partial in 24% (n = 6; AFlu in 4 cases), and failure with intra-uterine death with hydrops in 1 case with AVRT. EAT (n = 6) had 100% resolution with flecainide monotherapy (n = 4) or with sequential therapy (D→F in 1, D→S in 1). AVRT (n = 13) responded moderately. This included monotherapy with digoxin (n = 4; complete remission), flecainide (n = 5; complete remission in 4 and partial remission in 1), and sotalol (n = 1; partial remission). Sequential/polytherapy was required in 3 cases [complete remission with D→F in 1 and D→F + S in 1; failure with D + F therapy culminating in intra-uterine death/hydrops in 1]. AFlu (n = 7) showed complete remission only in 3 cases. Treatment included digoxin (complete and partial remission in 1 case each), sotalol (n = 1; complete remission), and D + S (complete remission in 1 and partial remission in 3). The singular case of PJRT showed complete remission with digoxin.

Fetal heart failure/hydrops fetalis was adequately controlled in 91% [10/11; complete remission in 7 (64%), and partial remission in 3 (27%)] with the exception of a singular intra-uterine death noted above. Sequential/polytherapy was instituted in 5 (45.5%) cases [AVRT in 3 (complete remission with D→F and D→ F + S in 1 case each, IUD/Hydrops in D→F in 1 case); AFlu in 3 (Complete and partial remission with D + S respectively in 2 cases, partial remission with digoxin)]. All other cases responded to monotherapy.

Maternal side effects were mild, including minimal ECG changes and symptoms like weakness. Adjustments in drug doses effectively managed adverse effects. Of the 26 treated live-born infants, 10 (38%) experienced spontaneous arrhythmia resolution without postnatal therapy. Earlier gestational age at diagnosis predicted higher likelihood of postnatal resolution. Fetal heart failure or hydrops did not significantly predict persistent postnatal arrhythmia. Postnatal therapy with beta blockers, flecainide, or sotalol was effective, with good overall outcomes. One neonatal death with underlying structural heart disease was unrelated to arrhythmia.

This study adds to the already existing body of evidence on the management of fetal SVTs. The data is most parts in line with the current standard of care of fetal SVTs. Fetal echocardiography is the first choice for prompt and easy diagnosis of fetal SVT. Fetal magnetocardiography and fetal electrocardiography are also advanced diagnostic modalities and recommended for diagnosis. Hand-held doppler for ambulatory fetal heart rate monitoring can prove to be a cheaper alternative for detecting recurrences [3]. Pharmacotherapy is usually with a single drug with combination therapy recommended for failure and in recurrences. Although initially used as first line, digoxin is better used as an adjunct, particularly in failure and better avoided as a standalone therapy in hydrops. This is owing to its limited penetrability in hydrops fetalis. The choice of first line therapy is also pursuant on the initial classification of the SVT type. Flecainide fairs better for both short and long VA tachycardias (AVRT, EAT and PJRT) while sotalol is increasingly preferred for AFlu [1,3,4].

Along with the inherent limitations of a retrospective single center study with small sample size, this study is confounded by the lack of a standardized management protocol which is mostly discretionary. Nevertheless, the clinical course for majority of patients is well-defined and authors must be congratulated for their detailed data analysis.

In summary, echocardiography-guided maternal therapy using routine AADs (digoxin, sotalol and, more often flecainide) alone/sequentially/in combination is highly effective for fetal SVT (most commonly AVRT). Early diagnosis and treatment achieve better control. Fetal heart failure/hydrops can be successfully managed with combination therapy if needed. It does not necessarily predict persistent postnatal arrhythmia.
