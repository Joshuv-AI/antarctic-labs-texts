Unambiguous randomness from a quantum state
Fionnuala Curran

Abstract
Intrinsic randomness is generated when a quantum state is measured in any basis in which it is not diagonal. In an adversarial scenario, we quantify this randomness by the probability that a correlated eavesdropper could correctly guess the measurement outcomes. What if the eavesdropper is never wrong, but can sometimes return an inconclusive outcome? Inspired by analogous concepts in quantum state discrimination, we introduce the unambiguous randomness of a quantum state and measurement, and, relaxing the assumption of perfect accuracy, randomness with a fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes. We solve these problems for any state and projective measurement in dimension two, as well as for an isotropically noisy state measured in an unbiased basis of any dimension. In the latter case, we find that, given a fixed amount of total noise, an eavesdropper correlated only to the noisy state is always outperformed by an eavesdropper with joint correlations to both a noisy state and a noisy measurement. In fact, we identify a critical error parameter beyond which the joint eavesdropper achieves perfect guessing probability, ruling out any possibility of private randomness.



1
Introduction

Quantum physics is inherently random. This quality is baked into the very pillars of quantum theory, via the superposition principle and the Born rule: the result of measuring a pure state in any basis in which it is not diagonal is unpredictable. Complications arise, however, if we replace the pure state with a mixed or
noisy
one. Mixed states possess a second form of randomness arising from ignorance, since they can be physically realised as one of infinitely many mixtures of pure states. In an adversarial scenario, we must assume that this extra
side information
, though unknown to the experimentalist, is known by a malevolent eavesdropper, who uses it to guess the measurement outcomes. What is the extent of the eavesdropper’s knowledge? This question, motivated by foundational interest, as well as the recent proliferation of quantum random number generators
[
20
,
31
]
, is the focus of the present work.

A standard way to quantify randomness is to consider the probability that the eavesdropper guesses the outcomes correctly, optimised over all possible decompositions of the mixed state
[
30
]
. This quantity has operational significance, as it can be related directly to the conditional min-entropy
[
28
]
, which lower bounds the number of (statistically
and
private) random bits that can be extracted from the measurement outcomes
[
35
,
29
]
. Recently, the maximal intrinsic randomness of any quantum state has been solved by maximising the conditional min-entropy over all projective
[
32
]
or general
[
1
]
measurements. Typically, it is supposed that the eavesdropper makes a guess on
every
measurement round, incurring, inevitably, some fraction of incorrect guesses, which she aims to minimise. But what if the eavesdropper wanted a higher, or even perfect, level of accuracy?

Inspired by an analogous concept in quantum state discrimination
[
25
,
8
,
34
,
26
]
, we introduce the
unambiguous
randomness of a quantum state and measurement pair, as quantified by the optimal guessing probability of an eavesdropper who makes no errors, but who is allowed to output an additional,
inconclusive
outcome. Unambiguous quantum state discrimination has already been considered in the context of quantum key distribution
[
10
,
9
]
. Since the unambiguous discrimination of an ensemble of pure states is only possible when they are linearly independent
[
4
]
, given a rank-one projective measurement, an eavesdropper’s unambiguous guessing probability is non-zero only for mixed states of full rank. The
maximal
unambiguous randomness of the state is then found by minimizing this guessing probability over all measurement bases. In this work, we solve this problem for any state, providing a sufficient condition for the optimal measurement.

Further, again adapting ideas from quantum state discrimination
[
3
,
43
]
, we present an intermediate scenario wherein the eavesdropper maximises her guessing probability subject to a fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes (FRIO), a term coined in
[
2
]
. The FRIO randomness of a quantum state and measurement pair, for a fixed parameter
Q
, is then given by the optimal guessing probability of an eavesdropper who returns an inconclusive outcome with probability
Q
. Once solved, this quantity can be inverted to find the optimal guessing probability given a fixed rate of error
[
21
]
, a scenario with its own counterpart in state discrimination
[
41
,
18
]
. We solve the unambiguous and FRIO randomness for any qubit state and projective measurement, revealing an intuitive, geometric optimal decomposition by the eavesdropper.

We devote special attention to the setting wherein a noisy state, defined as a pure state affected by isotropic noise, is measured in a basis that is unbiased to its own diagonal basis. This could be related, for example, to randomness generation using single photons distributed across several paths
[
17
,
15
]
. We solve the FRIO randomness in this scenario for any amount of noise, in any finite dimension. As an application, we consider the more realistic picture where the state
and
the unbiased measurement are noisy. The eavesdropper, now correlated to both devices, can perform a
joint
decomposition of the state and measurement
[
14
,
7
,
37
]
. Generalizing an example in
[
6
]
, we show that such a ‘joint noise’ eavesdropper always outperforms her ‘single noise’ counterpart given the same global amount of noise, and given any upper bound imposed on the probability of error. In fact, beyond a critical error value that is no greater than a half, the ‘joint noise’ eavesdropper achieves perfect guessing probability, ending all hopes of private randomness. This case study warns that, even in a wholly device-dependent setting, with perfect statistical randomness and stringent constraints on the guessing accuracy, an eavesdropper might still gain some (or even total) information about the outcomes, due to noise.

The paper is laid out as follows. In Section
2
, we set up our two scenarios, presenting the semidefinite programs (SDPs) for unambiguous and FRIO randomness. In Section
3
, we give our results. Concretely, in Section
3.1
, we find a simple analytic solution for the maximal unambiguous randomness of any state, which translates to a lower bound on the probability of unambiguous discrimination for any ensemble of linearly independent pure states. In Section
3.2
, we find the unambiguous and FRIO randomness of any state and projective measurement in dimension two, while in Section
3.3
, we find the FRIO randomness of any noisy state when measured in a basis unbiased to its diagonal basis. In Section
3.4
, we lower bound the guessing probability of a ‘joint noise’ eavesdropper, and prove that it always exceeds that of a ‘single noise’ eavesdropper, for an unbiased state and measurement pair. In Section
4
, we present our conclusions and ideas for future work. The majority of proofs and derivations are relegated to the Appendix.

2
Set-up

In both of our randomness scenarios, an experimentalist (who we call Alice) performs a measurement on a system, represented by a mixed state
\rho
, which is positive semidefinite (
\rho\succcurlyeq 0
) and has trace strictly less than one. Similarly, we represent her measurement by a Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM),
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{j}\}_{j}
, where the POVM elements
M_{j}
are positive-semidefinite (
M_{j}\succcurlyeq 0
) and sum to the identity (
\sum_{j}M_{j}=\mathbbm{1}
). Throughout most of this work, we consider rank-one projective measurements, which have the form
M_{j}=\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
, where
\{\ket{m_j}\}
is an orthonormal basis in finite dimension
d
.

We allow the eavesdropper, Eve, to hold a purification of the state
\rho
, i.e., she holds the state
\sigma=\tr_{S}\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}_{SE}
, for some joint state
\ket{\Psi}_{SE}
such that
\rho=\tr_{E}\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}_{SE}
, where the subscripts
S
and
E
represent Alice and Eve’s respective Hilbert spaces. Eve chooses the optimal measurement on her local system to try to infer Alice’s measurement outcomes. From the Schrödinger-Gisin-HJW theorem
[
36
,
16
,
24
]
, we know that a distant party who performs local measurements on the purification of a state can steer any ensemble of states to the other party. It is equivalent, then, to consider a ‘classical’ eavesdropper, who simply chooses a decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
for the state, where
\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}=1
and
\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}=\rho
. In each measurement round, Eve (unlike Alice) knows which state, say
\rho_{j}
, is actually used, and guesses the most probable outcome, say
j
, accordingly. As a final step to reconcile the ‘classical’ and ‘quantum’ eavesdropping scenarios, we assume that the states in the decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d).

Since we demand that Eve guess Alice’s outcomes either unambiguously or with a FRIO parameter
Q
, she must include an ‘inconclusive’ state
\rho_{0}
in the decomposition, with a corresponding probability
p_{0}
. The decomposition then has the form

\rho=p_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\rho_{j}\,.

(1)

For unambiguous randomness, we need
\rho_{j}=\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
. Eve’s guessing probability
P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)
(named for ‘unambiguous discrimination’) is then given by the SDP

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{p_{j}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle p_{j}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\rho\succcurlyeq\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\,.\quad

The maximal unambiguous randomness of a state, given by
P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\rho\right)
, is then found by
minimising

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)
over all measurement bases.

1

Following
[
32
]
, we consider only projective measurements, since we allow Alice no access to additional quantum states or other sources of randomness. We show in Appendix
A.2
that coarse graining a projective measurement does not increase the unambiguous randomness, so we are free to restrict to rank-one projective measurements.

When Eve has a fixed probability
Q
of giving an inconclusive outcome, her guessing probability
P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)
is given by the SDP

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\langle m_{j}|\rho_{j}|m_{j}\rangle

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}=\rho\,\quad

\displaystyle p_{0}=Q\,.\quad

More details on the derivation of the SDPs (
2
) and (
3
) are given in Appendix
A
. We can now proceed to the results.

3
Results

3.1
Unambiguous randomness of any state

Theorem 1
.

The maximal unambiguous randomness of any state
\rho
is given by the guessing probability

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\rho\right)=d\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(4)

We can prove the lower bound by considering the decomposition

p_{0}\rho_{0}=\rho-\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad p_{j}\rho_{j}=\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(5)

We show in Appendix
C
that the bound (
4
) can be achieved by measuring in a basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
for which
\ket{\umin}
, the eigenvector of the state
\rho
corresponding to its minimum eigenvalue, satisfies

\absolutevalue{\langle\umin| m_j \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(6)

This includes bases that are unbiased with respect to the eigenbasis
\{\ket{u_j}\}
of
\rho
. We also show by counterexample that this condition, while sufficient, is not necessary to saturate the bound.

The Schrödinger-Gisin-HJW theorem
[
36
,
16
,
24
]
allows us to relate a randomness scenario, where Alice holds
\rho
, receives the decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
and measures in
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, to a state discrimination scenario, where Eve holds
\rho
, receives the ensemble
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
and performs the measurement
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
, where

E_{\mu}=p_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,,\qquad\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_j}\,.

(7)

(See Appendix
B
for more detail on this equivalence.)
Consider the set of
d
linearly independent states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
, and define
\ket{\xi_j}=\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ket{\phi_j}
.
Since the
d
operators
\outerproduct{\xi_j}{\xi_j}
have rank one, are positive semidefinite and sum to the identity in dimension
d
, the vectors
\{\ket{\xi_j}\}
must define an orthonormal basis, so we find

\langle\xi_{i}|\xi_{j}\rangle=\sqrt{\eta_{i}\eta_{j}}\,\langle\phi_{i}|\rho^{-1}|\phi_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,\,j}\,.

(8)

We can then define the measurement
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
, with

E_{0}=\mathbbm{1}-\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\rho^{-1}\,,\qquad E_{j}=\eta_{j}\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\rho^{-1}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}\rho^{-1}\,,

(9)

which is suitable for unambiguous discrimination because

\tr\left(E_{i}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}\right)=\frac{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)}{\eta_{j}}\,\delta_{i,\,j}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(10)

This gives the bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\tr\left(E_{j}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}\right)=d\,\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(11)

A sufficient condition for which this measurement is optimal is

\eta_{j}\absolutevalue{\langle\umin| \phi_j \rangle}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(12)

We summarize the above in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
be an ensemble of
d
linearly independent vectors
\ket{\phi_j}
with prior probabilities
\eta_{j}
, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}=\rho
. Then the probability for unambiguous state discrimination is lower bounded by

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)\geq d\,\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(13)

3.2
Qubits

In dimension two, we can represent the state
\rho
and the measurement elements
M_{j}
by the Bloch vectors
\vec{r}
and
\pm\vec{m}
, where

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad M_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\vec{m}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,2\}\,,

(14)

where
\vec{\sigma}
is a vector of Pauli matrices,
\vec{\sigma}=\left(\sigma_{x},\,\sigma_{y},\,\sigma_{z}\right)
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is a rank-one measurement,
\absolutevalue{\vec{m}}^{2}=1
, and since
\rho
is mixed,
\absolutevalue{\vecr\,}^{2}<1
.
Without loss of generality, we introduce a ‘normal’ vector
\vec{n}
that is orthogonal to
\vec{m}
, i.e.,
\vec{m}\cdot\vec{n}=0
, and write
\vec{r}
as

\vec{r}=m\vec{m}+p\vec{n}\,,\qquad m^{2}+p^{2}<1\,,

(15)

where we further assume that
m
and
p
are non-negative. (As a slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes refer to
\vec{r}
as the state and to
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
as the measurement.)
We can write Eve’s decomposition in terms of Bloch vectors
\{\vec{r}_{\mu}\}
as

\vec{r}=p_{0}\vec{r}_{0}+p_{1}\vec{r}_{1}+p_{2}\vec{r}_{2}\,.

(16)

In the unambiguous case, we must have
\vec{r}_{1}=+\vec{m}
and
\vec{r}_{2}=-\vec{m}
.

3.2.1
Unambiguous randomness

Corollary 2.1
.

The maximal unambiguous randomness of a qubit state
\vec{r}
, with
r=\absolutevalue{\vecr\,}
, is

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\vec{r}\,\right)=1-r\,,

(17)

and is achieved if and only if the measurement is unbiased to the diagonal basis of the state.

The bound is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1
, using
\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-r\right)
. That it is saturated by any measurement unbiased to the diagonal basis of the state (i.e., when
m=0
) follows, since the condition (
6
) then holds. To show that Eve can achieve a strictly higher guessing probability when
m\neq 0
, consider the following decomposition (which is not necessarily optimal),

\vec{r}_{0}=\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\,\vec{n}+m\vec{m}\,,\qquad\vec{r}_{j}=\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\vec{m}\,,

(18)

with

p_{0}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\,,\qquad p_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-p_{0}\right)\left(1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m\right)\,,

(19)

for
j\in\{1,\,2\}
. We find the bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)\geq 1-\frac{p}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\,.

(20)

Using
m^{2}=r^{2}-p^{2}
, for fixed
r
, the right-hand side of (
20
) decreases monotonically with
p
, so when
p<r
(i.e., when
m\neq 0
), we have

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)>1-r\,.

(21)

We now find the unambiguous randomness of any state
\vec{r}
and measurement
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
.

.

The unambiguous randomness generated by measuring a state
\vec{r}

\{\pm\vec{m}\}

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=\begin{cases}1-p\,,\quad&m+p\leq 1\,,\\
\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,,\quad&m+p>1\,.\end{cases}

\{\pm\vec{m}\}

3.2.2
FRIO randomness

The maximum probability
Q_{\textnormal{max}}
of an inconclusive outcome given a state
\vec{r}
and a measurement
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
is

Q_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=1-P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)\,,

(26)

so from Theorem
3
, we find

Q_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=\begin{cases}p\,,\quad&m+p\leq 1\,,\\
\frac{p^{2}+\left(1-m\right)^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,,\quad&m+p>1\,.\end{cases}

(27)

(From hereon we assume the state and measurement are fixed, and drop the explicit dependence of
Q_{\textnormal{max}}
on
\vec{r}
and
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
.) We also introduce a critical value for
Q

Q_{\textnormal{crit}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-p}\,.

(28)

We then solve the FRIO randomness with parameter
Q
, where
0<Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
.

Theorem 4
.

For a fixed rate
Q
of inconclusive outcomes, the FRIO randomness generated by measuring a qubit state
\vec{r}
, of the form (
15
), in the basis
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
is given by

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}1-Q+\sqrt{\left(1-Q\right)^{2}-\left(p-Q\right)^{2}}\,,\quad&Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\\
1-Q+m-\frac{Q}{r^{2}}\left(Am-p\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}\right)\,,\quad&Q>Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\end{cases}

(29)

where

A=1-\frac{Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\left(1-p\right)}{Q}\,.

(30)

We sketch the proof here, with full details in Appendix
D.3
. As before, Eve’s optimal strategy is split into two cases. We first define the states

\vec{q}_{j}=\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\sqrt{1-q^{2}}\,\vec{m}+q\vec{n}\,,\qquad q=\frac{p-Q}{1-Q}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,2\}\,.

(31)

Our first case,
Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
, is equivalent to
\vec{r}\in\{\vec{n},\,\{\vec{q}_{j}\}\}

\vec{r}
admits a decomposition with
\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{n}
and
\vec{r}_{j}=\vec{q}_{j}

p_{0}=Q\,,\qquad p_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q+\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\right)\,,\qquad j\in\{1,\,2\}\,.

(32)

The guessing probability then follows. This decomposition is shown in Figure

\vec{r}\notin\textnormal{conv}\left(\{\vec{q}_{j}\},\,\vec{n}\right)

\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{u}\,,\qquad\vec{r}_{1}=\vec{v}\,,\qquad p_{0}=Q\,,\qquad p_{1}=1-Q\,,\qquad p_{2}=0\,,

(33)

where

\vec{v}=\frac{1}{1-Q}\left(\vec{r}-Q\vec{u}\right)

\frac{1}{2\left(1-m\right)^{2}}\left(p\sqrt{2T}+\sqrt{\left(1-r^{2}\right)\left(1-m-2T\right)}\right)^{2}\,,\quad&T<T_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\end{cases}

0<T<T_{\textnormal{max}}\,,\qquad T_{\textnormal{max}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1-p^{2}}\right)\,.

(38)

Noisy states

We define a noisy state

Unambiguous randomness

3.4
Randomness with shared noise

We now consider a scenario where Alice has a noisy state
\rho_{\varepsilon}

and
a noisy measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}=\{M_{j}\}_{j}
, where

M_{j}=\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\right)\,,

(45)

and, as before, we assume that the basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
is unbiased to the state
\ket{\phi}
. We follow a model introduced in
[
14
]
and developed in
[
7
,
37
,
6
]
, where an eavesdropper has quantum correlations with
both
the state and the measurement device. Eve’s optimal strategy is difficult to solve in this case, but we can lower bound her guessing probability by finding that of a ‘classical’ eavesdropper, who can
simultaneouly
decompose the state
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and the measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}

[
37
]
. Concretely, the eavesdropper sends the states
\{\ket{\psi_\mu}\}
and the measurements
\{\mathcal{N}_{\nu}\}
according to a joint probability distribution
\{p_{\mu,\,\nu}\}
, choosing the most probable outcome in each case, where
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}=\{N_{x,\,\nu}\}_{x}
are POVMs (
N_{x,\,\nu}\succcurlyeq 0
and
\sum_{x}N_{x,\,\nu}=\mathbbm{1}
for all
\nu
).

By choosing a joint strategy for the ‘classical’ Eve, in Appendix
F
, we find a critical noise value
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
such that Eve has perfect guessing probability when
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
. For any finite dimension
d
,

\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}=\frac{d}{2\left(d+\sqrt{d}\right)}\,.

(46)

The value of
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
increases with
d
and is always strictly less than a half. Generalizing a qubit example in
[
6
]
, we now compare the case where both the state and the measurement have noise
\varepsilon
to the case where Alice has a perfect noiseless measurement
\mathcal{M}
and a
noisier
state
\rho_{\delta}
, where

\delta=\varepsilon\left(2-\varepsilon\right)\,,\qquad\varepsilon=1-\sqrt{1-\delta}\,.

(47)

The total noise parameter
\delta
is chosen such that the Born rule is preserved across the two scenarios, i.e.,

\tr\left(\Delta_{\delta}\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{ \phi}\right)\,M_{j}\right)=\tr\left(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right)\,\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(M_{j}\right)\right)\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(48)

Now, if we restrict Eve’s probability of error in both scenarios to be less than or equal to

, where
0\leq T\leq T_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\delta\right)
, we can prove that Eve achieves a strictly higher guessing probability when she has joint noise. We do this in Appendix
F
by constructing a piecewise strategy for Eve in the joint noise case (which is not necessarily optimal) and comparing it to
P_{\,\textnormal{T}}\left(\rho_{\delta},\,\mathcal{M}\right)
from (
44
), which we know to be optimal. A comparison of the strategies is shown for the qubit case in Figure

. We sum up this discussion with the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a rank-one projective measurement in dimension
d
that is unbiased to a state
\ket{\phi}
, and let
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\cdot\right)
be a depolarizing noise channel. Further, let a ‘joint’ eavesdropper have correlations to the state
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\ket{\phi}\right)
and the measurement
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
, while a ‘single’ eavesdropper has correlations only to the state
\Delta_{\delta}\left(\ket{\phi}\right)
, where the total noise
\delta
is given by (
47
). Then, for any
\delta
and any upper bound on the probability of error, the probability that the ‘joint’ eavesdropper correctly guesses the outcomes of
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
on
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\ket{\phi}\right)
is
strictly higher
than the probability that the ‘single’ eavesdropper correctly guesses the outcomes of
\mathcal{M}
on
\Delta_{\delta}\left(\ket{\phi}\right)
.

4
Conclusions

We have introduced the unambiguous randomness of a quantum state and measurement pair, as quantified by the guessing probability of a correlated eavesdropper who makes no error, but who sometimes returns an inconclusive outcome. Relaxing the ‘unambiguous’ constraint, we also formulate randomness with a fixed rate
Q
of inconclusive outcomes. Both concepts draw from ideas in quantum state discrimination, and exploit the symmetries between the task of state discrimination and the scenario wherein an experimentalist generates randomness using characterized, but noisy, devices. Our results, which centre on qubit states and noisy states in any finite dimension, bound the randomness generated in these typical device-dependent scenarios, given an eavesdropper with arbitrarily high guessing accuracy. In particular, our example of a noisy state with an unbiased noisy measurement shows that correct modelling of noise in one’s devices is crucial to avoid over-estimating the amount of private randomness generated. Our work invites future generalizations to states that are not full rank, where the eavesdropper might use maximal confidence measurements
[
5
]
in the absence of unambiguous ones, and to more general measurements.

A.2
Unambiguous randomness

Let’s imagine an eavesdropper who sends an ensemble
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
to Alice, with
\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}=\rho
. Alice then measures her state
\rho
in an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
of dimension
d
, where we write her POVM as
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{j}\}_{j}
,
M_{j}=\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
. Since the guessing probability must be unambiguous, Eve is forced to send the states
\rho_{j}=\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
, and to reserve one state
\rho_{0}
for her inconclusive outcome, such that

p_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\rho_{j}=\rho\,.

(55)

Since all the states must be positive semidefinite, we have the condition

p_{0}\rho_{0}=\rho-\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\rho_{j}=\rho-\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\succcurlyeq 0\,.

(56)

Our optimisation problem is then

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{p_{j}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}

(57)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle p_{j}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\rho\succcurlyeq\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\,.\quad

This can be converted to an SDP of the standard form by introducing the matrix

L=\sum_{j}p_{j}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}

(58)

and enforcing the conditions

L\succcurlyeq 0\,,\qquad\langle m_{i}|L|m_{j}\rangle=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;i\neq j\,,\quad i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(59)

The SDP is

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{L}{\textnormal{maximize}}

\displaystyle\tr L

(60)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle L\succcurlyeq 0\!\!\!

\displaystyle\rho\succcurlyeq L

\displaystyle\langle m_{i}|L|m_{j}\rangle=0\!\!\!

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;i\neq j\,,\;\;i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

Comparing this SDP to the standard form (
A.1
), we identify the map
\Phi_{ij}\left(\cdot\right)=\langle m_{i}|L|m_{j}\rangle
for
i\neq j
, and its adjoint map
\Phi^{\dagger}_{ij}\left(\cdot\right)=\left(\cdot\right)\outerproduct{m_j}{m_i}
. The dual problem is then

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{Z,\;\{y_{ij}\}}{\textnormal{minimize}}

\displaystyle\tr\left(Z\rho\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle Z\succcurlyeq 0\!\!\!

\displaystyle Z\succcurlyeq\mathbbm{1}-\underset{i\neq j}{\sum_{i,\,j=1}^{d}}y_{ij}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_i}\,,

where
\{y_{ij}\}
are real numbers. The primal problem is strictly feasible, as we could use the variables

L=\left(\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)-\epsilon\right)\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad 0<\epsilon<\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,,

(62)

and similarly for the dual, we have the strictly feasible variables

Z=\alpha\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad\alpha>1\,,\qquad y_{ij}=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(63)

Given an optimal variable
L
, we can recover Eve’s optimal mixing probability distribution as

p_{0}\rho=\rho-L\,,\qquad p_{j}=\langle m_{j}|L|m_{j}\rangle\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(64)

A.2.1
Maximal randomness

We assume throughout that Alice makes a measurement
\mathcal{M}
in an othnonormal basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
. We can show that Eve’s guessing probability would not decrease if Alice coarse-grained her measurement, i.e., if she performed a measurement
\hat{\mathcal{M}}=\{\hat{M}_{a}\}_{a}
with
n<d
elements

\hat{M}_{a}=\sum_{j\in S_{a}}M_{j}\,,

(65)

where
M_{j}=\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
and
\{S_{a}\}
are mutually exclusive subsets of
\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
such that

\sum_{a=1}^{n}\,\sum_{j\in S_{a}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,.

(66)

Note that the inconclusive outcome 0 is not an element of any set
S_{a}
.
If
\{p_{j},\,\rho_{j}\}
is Eve’s optimal decomposition for the original measurement
\mathcal{M}
, we can define a coarse-grained decomposition
\{\hat{p}_{a},\,\hat{\rho}_{a}\}
with

\hat{p}_{a}\hat{\rho}_{a}=\sum_{j\in S_{a}}p_{j}\rho_{j}\,.

(67)

This decomposition still satisfies the unambiguous condition, as

\tr\left(\hat{p}_{a}\,\hat{\rho}_{a}\hat{M}_{b}\right)=\sum_{j\in S_{a}}\,\sum_{k\in S_{b}}p_{j}\tr\left(\rho_{j}M_{k}\right)=\hat{p}_{a}\delta_{a,\,b}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;a,\,b\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,n\}\,.

(68)

Eve’s guessing probability is then lower bounded by

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\hat{\mathcal{M}}\right)\geq\sum_{a=1}^{n}\hat{p}_{a}\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{a}\hat{M}_{a}\right)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\hat{p}_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}=P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)\,.

(69)

When deriving the
maximal
unambiguous randomness of a state, then, which we take to be the optimisation over all projective measurements, we are free to restrict to rank-one projective measurements, since coarse graining a projective measurement won’t increase its randomness.

A.3
Randomness with a fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes

Here, we will again imagine our eavesdropper Eve sending the ensemble
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}

to Alice, who measures in the orthonormal basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
. This time, Eve will return an inconclusive outcome with probability
p_{0}=Q
, with
0<Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
, where
Q_{\textnormal{max}}
is the optimal
Q
in the case of unambiguous randomness. The optimisation problem is the following,

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\langle m_{j}|\rho_{j}|m_{j}\rangle

(70)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}=\rho\,\quad

\displaystyle p_{0}=Q\,.\quad

This can be converted to a standard SDP by defining

\tilde{\rho}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}\otimes\outerproduct{\mu}{\mu}+\underset{\mu\neq\nu}{\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}}G_{\mu\nu}\otimes\outerproduct{\mu}{\nu}\,,\qquad\tilde{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\otimes\outerproduct{j}{j}\,,

(71)

where
\{G_{\mu\nu}\}
are arbitrary Hermitian matrices that don’t contribute to the guessing probability. The matrix
\tilde{\rho}
is Hermitian, and can only be positive semidefinite if all of its principal submatrices are positive semidefinite (see e.g.
[
23
, Observation 7.12]
), so to make sure that
\rho_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0
for all
\mu
, it is sufficient to enforce
\tilde{\rho}\succcurlyeq 0
. To ensure that the new variable
\tilde{\rho}
has the form (
71
), we also have to impose the constraints

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{\mu}\right)\;\tilde{\rho}\;\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\ket{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\,,

(72)

\displaystyle\tr\left(\tilde{\rho}\;\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}\right)

\displaystyle=Q\,.

The SDP in standard form is then

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\tilde{\rho}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\tr\left(\tilde{\rho}\,\tilde{M}\right)

(73)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}\succcurlyeq 0\!\!\!

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{\mu}\right)\;\tilde{\rho}\;\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\ket{\mu}\right)=\rho

\displaystyle\tr\left(\tilde{\rho}\;\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}\right)=Q\,.\quad

We identify the maps

\displaystyle\Lambda\left(\cdot\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{\mu}\right)\;\cdot\;\left(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\ket{\mu}\right)\,,

(74)

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\cdot\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\left(\cdot\right)\;\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}\right)

and their adjoint maps

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\dagger}\left(\cdot\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\cdot\right)\otimes\mathbbm{1}\,,

(75)

\displaystyle\Omega^{\dagger}\left(\cdot\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\cdot\right)\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}\,.

We can then write the dual as

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{G,\,w}{\textnormal{minimize}}

\displaystyle\tr\left(G\rho\right)-wQ

(76)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle G\otimes\mathbbm{1}-w\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}-\tilde{M}\succcurlyeq 0\,,

where
w
is a real number.
Noting that all of the terms in the constraint are diagonal in the second Hilbert space
\{\ket{\mu}\}
, we can instead write

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{G,\;w}{\textnormal{minimize}}

\displaystyle\tr\left(G\rho\right)-wQ

(77)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle G\succcurlyeq w\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle G\succcurlyeq\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

Both primal and dual problems are strictly feasible, as we could take the variables

\rho_{\mu}=\rho\,,\qquad p_{0}=Q\,,\qquad p_{j}=\frac{1-Q}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\quad j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(78)

for the primal and

G=\left(1+\epsilon\right)\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad\epsilon>0\,,\qquad w=0

(79)

for the dual. The complementary slackness conditions are

\tilde{\rho}\left(G\otimes\mathbbm{1}-w\mathbbm{1}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}-\tilde{M}\right)=0\,,

(80)

or, equivalently,

p_{0}\rho_{0}\left(G-w\mathbbm{1}\right)=0\,,\qquad p_{j}\rho_{j}\left(G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\right)=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(81)

Summing the condition
G\succcurlyeq\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
over all
j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we find
G\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d}
, so
G
must have full rank. Since
G
is positive definite,
G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
can have at most one non-positive eigenvalue (see e.g.
[
23
, Corollary 4.3.9]
), so we conclude that all nontrivial states
\rho_{j}
must be rank one for all
j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
(otherwise we have
p_{j}=0
).

Randomness.

In the randomness scenario, Alice holds a state

The terms

where

\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
if Eve measures her part of
\ket{\Psi}
with the POVM
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}=\{F_{\mu}^{*}\}_{\mu}
, where
\left(*\right)
denotes complex conjugation in the basis
\{\ket{u_j}\}
. The POVM elements
E_{\mu}
and the states
\rho_{\mu}
are related by

p_{\mu}\rho_{\mu}=\sqrt{\rho}\,E_{\mu}^{*}\sqrt{\rho}\,.

(84)

Since we know that, in Eve’s optimal decomposition for a fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes, the states
\rho_{j}
are rank one for
j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we conclude that the POVM elements
E_{j}
are also rank one,
E_{j}=\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}
(note that the vectors
\{\ket{e_j}\}
are not necessarily normalized). We can then write the guessing probability (
70
) in terms of Eve’s POVM
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\absolutevalue{\langle m_j | \sqrt{\rho} | e_j^{*} \rangle}^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle E_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}E_{\mu}=\mathbbm{1}\,\quad

\displaystyle\tr\left(E_{0}\,\rho\right)=Q\,.\quad

Meanwhile, when Alice measures her local state in
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, Eve receives the steered states

\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{\phi_j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle m_{j}|u_{i}\rangle\sqrt{\rho}\ket{u_i}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_j^{*}}\,.

(86)

Since the state
\rho
is full rank in the space spanned by the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, every probability
\eta_{j}
is non-zero, and the states
\{\ket{\phi_j}\}
are linearly independent.

State discrimination.

Let’s say we have an ensemble of
d
linearly independent states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
satisfying

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}=\rho\,.

(87)

The state
\rho
has rank
d
because the states
\{\ket{\phi_j}\}
are linearly independent, so we can invert it to define the set

K_{j}=\eta_{j}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(88)

The elements
K_{j}
are rank one and positive and they sum to the identity, so, since there are
d
of them spanning a
d
-dimensional basis,
\{K_{j}\}_{j}
must be a measurement in some orthonormal basis
\{\ket{\xi_j}\}
, where we write
K_{j}=\outerproduct{\xi_j}{\xi_j}
. We can then write the states as

\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{\xi_j}\,.

(89)

Comparing this form to (
86
), we see that the ensemble
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
could have arisen from a measurement in the basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}=\{\ket{\xi_j^{*}}\}
applied to Alice’s part of the purification (
83
). From (
89
), we can also conclude that

\sqrt{\eta_{j}\eta_{l}}\,\langle\phi_{j}|\rho^{-1}|\phi_{j}\rangle=\delta_{j,\,l}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j,\,l\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(90)

To unambiguously discriminate her states, Eve must make a measurement
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
of the form
[
4
]

E_{j}=P_{j}\outerproduct{\phi^{\perp}_j}{\phi^{\perp}_j}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\qquad E_{0}=\mathbbm{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}E_{j}\,,

(91)

where
\{\ket{\phi^{\perp}_j}\}
are the reciprocal states of
\{\ket{\phi_j}\}

[
4
,
11
]
, i.e., the unique (unnormalized) states that satisfy

\langle\phi^{\perp}_{i}|\phi_{k}\rangle=\delta_{i\,k}\,\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;i,\,k\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(92)

From
[
4
, Equation 2.10]
, the SDP for her optimal guessing probability is then

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{P_{j}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}P_{j}

(93)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle P_{j}\geq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\succcurlyeq\sum_{j=1}^{d}P_{j}\outerproduct{\phi^{\perp}_j}{\phi^{\perp}_j}\,.\quad

Using (
89
) and (
90
), we have
\ket{\phi_j^{\perp}}=\eta_{j}\rho^{-1}\ket{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ket{m_j^{*}}
,
such that Eve’s POVM operators can be rewritten as

E_{j}=\eta_{j}P_{j}\,\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\outerproduct{m_j^{*}}{m_j^{*}}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,,\qquad E_{0}=\mathbbm{1}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}E_{j}

(94)

and the SDP as

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{P_{j}\}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}P_{j}

(95)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle P_{j}\geq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\rho\succcurlyeq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}P_{j}\outerproduct{m_j^{*}}{m_j^{*}}\,.\quad

If we replace
p_{j}=\eta_{j}P_{j}
,
we have recovered exactly the form of the SDP (
57
) for unambiguous randomness. This means if we find a mixing probability distribution
\{p_{j}\}
that’s optimal for Eve when Alice has the state
\rho
and makes a measurement in the basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, we have also found the optimal variables
\{\eta_{j}P_{j}\}
to define Eve’s measurement for state discrimination, of the form (
94
), when she receives the states
\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{ \phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_j^{*}}
.

Similarly, given an ensemble of pure states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
, the SDP for state discrimination with a fixed rate
Q
of inconclusive outcomes is
[
2
,
3
]

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\langle\phi_{j}|E_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle

(96)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle E_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}E_{\mu}=\mathbbm{1}\,\quad

\displaystyle\tr\left(E_{0}\,\rho\right)=Q\,.\quad

Subbing in
\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_j^{*}}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle m_{j}^{*}|\sqrt{\rho}\,E_{j}\sqrt{\rho}|m_{j}^{*}\rangle

(97)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle E_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}E_{\mu}=\mathbbm{1}\,\quad

\displaystyle\tr\left(E_{0}\,\rho\right)=Q\,.\quad

Following similar reasoning to that in
A.3
, we can derive the corresponding dual problem
[
13
,
12
]

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{F,\,y}{\textnormal{minimize}}

\displaystyle\tr F-yQ

(98)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle F\succcurlyeq y\rho

\displaystyle F\succcurlyeq\sqrt{\rho}\outerproduct{m_j^{*}}{m_j^{*}}\sqrt{\rho}

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

where
y
is a real variable.
The complementary slackness conditions are

E_{0}\left(F-y\rho\right)=0\,,\qquad E_{j}\left(F-\sqrt{\rho}\outerproduct{m_j^{*}}{m_j^{*}}\sqrt{\rho}\right)=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(99)

Summing up all the inequality constraints on
F
over all
j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we find
F\succcurlyeq\frac{1}{d}\rho
, so
F
must be full rank. Since
F
is positive definite,
F-\sqrt{\rho}\outerproduct{m_j^{*}}{m_j^{*}}\sqrt{\rho}
can have at most one non-positive eigenvalue (see e.g.
[
23
, Corollary 4.3.9]
), so we conclude that all nontrivial POVM elements
E_{j}
must be rank one for all
j
,
E_{j}=\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}
. We can then rewrite the primal problem as

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)=\;\;\underset{\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\absolutevalue{\langle m_j^{*} | \sqrt{\rho} | e_j \rangle}^{2}

(100)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle E_{\mu}\succcurlyeq 0\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}E_{\mu}=\mathbbm{1}\,\quad

\displaystyle\tr\left(E_{0}\,\rho\right)=Q\,,\quad

which is exactly equivalent to (
85
). Returning to (
82
) and (
86
), this means that if a decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
is optimal for the randomness problem (
70
), when Alice holds
\rho
and measures it in
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, then the POVM
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
will be optimal for Eve in the state discrimination problem (
96
), when she receives a linearly independent set of
d
states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
, where

E_{\mu}=p_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,,\qquad\sqrt{\eta_{j}}\ket{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\ket{m_j}\,.

(101)

Appendix C
Maximal unambiguous randomness for any state

We want to find the maximal unambiguous randomness of any quantum state
\rho
, given by the minimisation of the unambiguous guessing probability (
57
) over all rank-one projective measurements
\mathcal{M}
,

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\rho\right)=\min_{\mathcal{M}}P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)\,.

(102)

Here we prove Theorem
1
from the main text in two steps.

Theorem 1
.

The maximal unambiguous randomness of any state
\rho
is given by the guessing probability

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\rho\right)=d\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(103)

Proof.

Lower bound.
For any measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, Eve could use the decomposition

p_{0}\rho_{0}=\rho-\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad p_{j}\rho_{j}=\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(104)

This gives us a lower bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}\right)\geq d\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(105)

Upper bound.
Denote the eigenvector of
\rho
corresponding to its minimum eigenvalue as
\ket{u_\textnormal{min}}
, and choose a measurement
\mathcal{M}_{\textnormal{min}}
such that the basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
is unbiased to
\ket{\umin}
, i.e., such that

\absolutevalue{\langle\umin| m_j \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(106)

The projector
\outerproduct{\umin}{\umin}
can then be expanded as

\outerproduct{\umin}{\umin}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d}+\underset{i\neq j}{\sum_{i,\,j=1}^{d}}\langle m_{i}|u_{\textnormal{min}}\rangle\langle u_{\textnormal{min}}|m_{j}\rangle\outerproduct{m_i}{m_j}\,.

(107)

The following are valid variables for the dual problem (
61
),

Z=d\outerproduct{\umin}{\umin}\,,\qquad y_{ij}=-d\langle m_{j}|u_{\textnormal{min}}\rangle\langle u_{\textnormal{min}}|m_{i}\rangle\,.

(108)

These variables give us the lower bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}_{\textnormal{min}}\right)\leq d\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,.

(109)

Since the upper and lower bounds match, we have proven Theorem
1
.
∎

Sufficient condition.

We have shown that having
\ket{\umin}
unbiased to the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
is a sufficient condition to achieve maximal unambiguous randomness, but in the following example, we show that it is not necessary. Consider the qutrit state
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}\outerproduct{u_i}{u_i}
, with eigenvalues arranged in decreasing order, and with degeneracy such that
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)
.
Take the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
with

\displaystyle\ket{m_1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\ket{u_1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{u_2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\ket{u_3}\,,

(110)

\displaystyle\ket{m_2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\ket{u_1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{u_2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\ket{u_3}\,,

\displaystyle\ket{m_3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\ket{u_1}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\ket{u_3}\,.

We notice that the
maximum
eigenvector
\ket{u_1}
is unbiased with respect to the basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, but we cannot construct a minimum eigenvector

\ket{\umin}=\alpha\ket{u_2}+\beta\ket{u_3}\,,\qquad\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\beta}^{2}=1

(111)

such that
\ket{\umin}
is unbiased to
\{\ket{m_j}\}
. Nonetheless, we can prove that we saturate the bound in Theorem
1
with this state and measurement pair by choosing the following variables for the dual problem (
61
),

Z=\frac{3}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\outerproduct{u_1}{u_1}\right)\,,\qquad y_{ij}=\frac{3}{2}\langle m_{j}|u_{1}\rangle\langle u_{1}|m_{i}\rangle\,.

(112)

State discrimination.

Let’s take any linearly indendent ensemble of
d
pure states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
which sum to
\rho
. Our previous result implies that

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}\right)\geq d\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\,,

(113)

which proves Theorem
2
in the main text. The decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
in the previous section is equivalent to the measurement
\{E_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
, with

E_{0}=\mathbbm{1}-\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\rho^{-1}\,,\qquad E_{j}=\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)\eta_{j}\rho^{-1}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}\rho^{-1}\quad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(114)

The sufficient condition for optimality becomes

\eta_{j}\absolutevalue{ \langle\umin| \phi_j\rangle}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho\right)}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(115)

Theorem
2
could also have been proven by subbing
q_{i}=\eta_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
into
[
40
, Theorem 1]
, noting from (
89
) that the state overlaps satisfy

\langle\phi_{i}|\phi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta_{i}\eta_{j}}}\langle\xi_{i}|\rho|\xi_{j}\rangle\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(116)

where
\{\ket{\xi_i}\}
is an orthonormal basis.

Appendix D
Qubits

D.1
General properties

We will represent our qubit state
\rho
as a Bloch vector
\vec{r}
,

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad\absolutevalue{\vecr\,}^{2}<1\,,

(117)

where
\vec{\sigma}=\left(\sigma_{x},\,\sigma_{y},\,\sigma_{z}\right)
is a vector of Pauli matrices. Let’s fix the measurement basis as
\pm\vec{m}
and introduce a ‘normal’ state
\vec{n}
to which
\vec{m}
is perpendicular, i.e.,

\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{m}_{j}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad\vec{m}_{j}=\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\vec{m}\,,\qquad\underset{i\neq j}{\sum_{i,\,j=1}^{2}}\outerproduct{m_i}{m_j}=\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,.

(118)

Without loss of generality, we will write our state as

\vec{r}=m\vec{m}+p\vec{n}\,,\qquad r^{2}=p^{2}+m^{2}<1\,,

(119)

with
0\leq p,\,m<1
, since the state must be full rank. The square roots
\rho
and
\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
are given by

\sqrt{\rho}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\Big(\mathbbm{1}+\gamma\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\Big)\,,\qquad\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\gamma\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad\gamma=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}{r^{2}}\,,

(120)

where we have

\gamma^{2}r^{2}=2\gamma-1\,,\qquad\left(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\left(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)=r^{2}\mathbbm{1}\,.

(121)

Note also the useful property

\big(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\big)\big(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\big)=\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}\,\mathbbm{1}+i\big(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\big)\cdot\vec{\sigma}

(122)

for any Bloch vectors
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
, such that

\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)=\left(1+2\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}+a^{2}\right)\mathbbm{1}+\left(2\left(1+\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}\,\right)\vec{a}+\left(1-a^{2}\right)\vec{b}\,\right)\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,.

(123)

Eve receives the ensemble of states
\{\eta_{j},\,\ket{\phi_j}\}
given by

\eta_{j}\outerproduct{\phi_j}{\phi_j}=\sqrt{\rho}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\sqrt{\rho}=\frac{1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m}{4}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{s}_{j}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,

(124)

where

\vec{s}_{j}=\frac{\gamma\left(1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\gamma m\right)\vec{r}+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\left(1-\gamma\right)\vec{m}}{\gamma\left(1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m\right)}\,

(125)

(note that complex conjugation was not needed here since we work in the real plane of the Bloch ball).
The prior probabilities for the states are then

\eta_{j}=\frac{1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m}{2}\,,

(126)

while the squared overlap between the states is

\absolutevalue{\langle\phi_1 | \phi_2 \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\vec{s}_{1}\cdot\vec{s}_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1-r^{2}-p^{2}}{1-m^{2}}\right)=\frac{p^{2}}{1-m^{2}}\,,

(127)

such that

\absolutevalue{\langle\phi_1 | \phi_2 \rangle}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\,.

(128)

We can then write the parameters
p
and
m
in terms of
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
and
\absolutevalue{\langle\phi_1 | \phi_2 \rangle}
as

m=\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\,,\qquad p=2\sqrt{\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}\,\absolutevalue{\langle\phi_1 | \phi_2 \rangle}\,.

(129)

In terms of the steered states
\vec{s}_{1}
and
\vec{s}_{2}
, we have

\vec{m}=\frac{a_{2}\eta_{1}\,\vec{s}_{1}-a_{1}\eta_{2}\,\vec{s}_{2}}{1-\gamma}\,,\qquad\vec{n}=\frac{b_{1}\eta_{2}\,\vec{s}_{2}-b_{2}\eta_{1}\,\vec{s}_{1}}{p\left(1-\gamma\right)}\,,

(130)

with

a_{j}=\gamma\left(1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\gamma m\right)\,,\qquad b_{j}=m\gamma\left(1+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\gamma m\right)+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\left(1-\gamma\right)\,.

(131)

Given the optimal decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\vec{r}_{\mu}\}
in the randomness picture, the optimal state discrimination measurement has elements

E_{\mu}=p_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{\mu}\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{p_{\mu}\left(1-\vec{r}\cdot\vec{r}_{\mu}\right)}{1-r^{2}}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\vec{e}_{\mu}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,

(132)

with

\vec{e}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\gamma\left(1-\vec{r}\cdot\vec{r}_{\mu}\right)}\left(\gamma\left(1-\gamma\left(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{r}_{\mu}\right)\right)\vec{r}-\left(1-\gamma\right)\vec{r}_{\mu}\right)\,.

(133)

D.2
Unambiguous guessing probability

From the conditions on the decomposition
\{p_{\mu},\,\rho_{\mu}\}
in (
57
), we find that
\vec{r}
is decomposed in terms of Bloch vectors as

\vec{r}=p_{0}\vec{r}_{0}+p_{1}\vec{m}_{1}+p_{2}\vec{m_{2}}\,.

(134)

Corollary
2.1
, proved in the main text, tells us that the maximal intrinsic randomness of a qubit state
\vec{r}
is given by the guessing probability

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\vec{r}\right)=1-r\,,

(135)

and is achieved only when
m=0
. We will now prove Theorem
3
from the main text, which gives the unambiguous randomness for any qubit state
\vec{r}
and measurement
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
.

Theorem 3
.

The unambiguous randomness generated by measuring a state
\vec{r}
, of the form (
15
), in the basis
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
is given by

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=\begin{cases}1-p\,,\quad&m+p\leq 1\,,\\
\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,,\quad&m+p>1\,.\end{cases}

(136)

Proof.

We split the proof of (
136
) into two cases.

When
m+p\leq 1
.
This condition implies that
\vec{r}\in\textnormal{conv}\left(\{\pm\vec{m}\},\,\vec{n}\,\right)
, so we can find a decomposition of
\vec{r}
into
\pm\,\vec{m}
and
\vec{n}
,

\vec{r}=p_{0}\vec{n}+p_{1}\vec{m}_{1}+p_{2}\vec{m}_{2}\,,

(137)

where we set
\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{n}
. Since
\vec{m}_{1}
and
\vec{m}_{2}
are antiparallel, we must have
p_{0}=p
. This gives us the lower bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\geq 1-p\,.

(138)

Choosing the following variables for the dual problem (
61
),

Z=\mathbbm{1}-\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\,,\qquad y_{12}=y_{21}=1\,,

(139)

it is easily shown that
Z\succcurlyeq 0
and that

Z-\mathbbm{1}+\underset{i\neq j}{\sum_{i,\,j=1}^{2}}y_{ij}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_i}=Z-\mathbbm{1}+\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}=0\,,

(140)

so these variables are valid. They give the upper bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\tr\left(Z\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\right)=1-p\,.

(141)

Since the upper and lower bounds match, we have found the optimal unambiguous guessing probability.

When
m+p>1
.
In this case, we neglect the second outcome entirely, setting
p_{2}=0
, such that the decomposition is

\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{u}\,,\qquad\vec{r}_{1}=\vec{m}\,,

(142)

where

p_{0}\vec{u}=p\vec{n}+\left(m-p_{1}\right)\vec{m}\,,\qquad p_{1}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,,\qquad p_{0}=1-p_{1}\,.

(143)

We can then lower bound the guessing probability by

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\geq\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,.

(144)

Consider the following variables for the dual problem (
61
),

Z=\frac{1-p_{1}}{1-m}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\frac{\vec{r}-p_{1}\vec{m}}{1-p_{1}}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad y_{12}=y_{21}=\frac{p}{1-m}\,.

(145)

We have
Z\succcurlyeq 0
, since

\absolutevalue{\frac{ \vecr-p_1 \vec{m} }{1-p_1}}^{2}=1\,,

(146)

and

Z-\mathbbm{1}+\underset{i\neq j}{\sum_{i,\,j=1}^{2}}y_{ij}\outerproduct{m_j}{m_i}=\frac{m-p_{1}}{1-m}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\vec{m}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\succcurlyeq 0\,,

(147)

where positive semidefiniteness holds because

m-p_{1}=\frac{p^{2}-\left(1-m\right)^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}=\frac{\left(1+p-m\right)\left(m+p-1\right)}{2\left(1-m\right)}>0\,,

(148)

where we use the inequality
m+p>1
. These variables give us the upper bound

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\tr\left(Z\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\right)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,.

(149)

Since the upper and lower bounds match, we have found the optimal unambiguous guessing probability, and proven Theorem
3
.
∎

D.3
Fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes

Since we now know the unambiguous guessing probability given a qubit state
\rho
, described by (

\{\ket{m_j}\}
, described by (

Q_{\textnormal{max}}
, the maximum rate of inconclusive outcomes given the optimal guessing strategy. From (

Q_{\textnormal{max}}=\begin{cases}p\,,\quad&m+p\leq 1\,,\\
\frac{p^{2}+\left(1-m\right)^{2}}{2\left(1-m\right)}\,,\quad&m+p>1\,.\end{cases}

(151)

We will also define a critical value for
Q
,
Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
, by

Q_{\textnormal{crit}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-p}\,.

(152)

Note that

Q_{\textnormal{max}}-Q_{\textnormal{crit}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\left(m+p-1\right)\left(1+m-p\right)}{2\left(1-p\right)}\;\,\leq 0\,,\quad&m+p\leq 1\,,\\
\frac{\left(m+p-1\right)\left(m+p-r^{2}\right)}{2\left(1-m\right)\left(1-p\right)}>0\,,\quad&m+p>1\,.\end{cases}

(153)

Since
Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
, this means
Q<Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
whenever
m+p\leq 1
, while when
m+p>1
, there are two distinct regions of
Q
,
0<Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
and
Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\leq Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
. Notice, further, that
Q<p
holds whenever
Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
. When
m+p\leq 1
, this is trivial, since
Q_{\textnormal{max}}=p
and
Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
. When
m+p>1
, we have

Q_{\textnormal{crit}}-p=-\frac{\left(m+p-1\right)\left(1+m-p\right)}{2\left(1-p\right)}<0\,,

(154)

so
Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}<p
.
We are now ready to prove Theorem
4
from the main text.

Theorem 4
.

For a fixed rate
Q
of inconclusive outcomes, the FRIO randomness generated by measuring a qubit state
\vec{r}
, of the form (
15
), in the basis
\{\pm\vec{m}\}
is given by

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\pm\vec{m}\}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}1-Q+\sqrt{\left(1-Q\right)^{2}-\left(p-Q\right)^{2}}\,,\quad&Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\\
1-Q+m-\frac{Q}{r^{2}}\left(Am-p\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}\right)\,,\quad&Q>Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\end{cases}

(155)

where

A=1-\frac{Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\left(1-p\right)}{Q}\,.

(156)

Proof.

When
Q\leq Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
.
As argued above,
Q<p
in this region. Choosing
p_{0}=Q
and
\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{n}
, we decompose the state
\vec{r}
as

\vec{r}=Q\vec{n}+p_{1}\vec{r}_{1}+p_{2}\vec{r}_{2}\,,

(157)

where

\vec{r}_{j}=\sqrt{1-q^{2}}\,\vec{m}_{j}+q\vec{n}\,,\qquad q=\frac{p-Q}{1-Q}\,,\qquad p_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q+\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j+1}m}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\right)\,.

(158)

This decomposition is valid because
0<q<1
and

p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q-\frac{m}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\right)=\frac{\left(1-p\right)\left(Q_{\textnormal{crit}}-Q\right)}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}\left(\left(1-Q\right)\sqrt{1-q^{2}}+m\right)}\geq 0\,.

(159)

We can then bound the guessing probability from below by

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}p_{j}\left(1+\vec{r}_{j}\cdot\vec{m}_{j}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q+\sqrt{\left(1-Q\right)^{2}-\left(p-Q\right)^{2}}\right)\,.

(160)

Consider the following variables for the dual problem (
77
),

G=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{2\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{g}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad\vec{g}=-\frac{1-\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{q}\vec{n}\,,\qquad w=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{1-q}{1+q}}\right)\,.

(161)

These variables satisfy the necessary constraints, since we have

G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\sqrt{1-q^{2}}\left(\frac{q}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\vec{n}+\left(-1\right)^{j+1}\vec{m}\right)\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\succcurlyeq 0\,,

(162)

which is positive semidefinite because

\absolutevalue{\frac{q}{\sqrt{1-q^2}} \vec{n} - \left( -1 \right)^{j+1} \vec{m}}^{2}=\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}\,,

(163)

and

G-w\mathbbm{1}=\frac{q}{2\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\succcurlyeq 0\,.

(164)

These variables give the upper bound

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\tr\left(G\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\right)-wQ=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q+\sqrt{\left(1-Q\right)^{2}-\left(p-Q\right)^{2}}\right)\,.

(165)

Since the upper and lower bounds coincide, we conclude that this is the optimal guessing probability.

When
Q>Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
.
This condition implies that
m+p>1
. For convenience, we define

A=1-\frac{1-r^{2}}{2Q}=1-\frac{Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\left(1-p\right)}{Q}\,.

(166)

The variable
A
increases with
Q
, so in the range
Q_{\textnormal{crit}}<Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}
, we have

p<A<1-\frac{\left(1-m\right)\left(1-r^{2}\right)}{p^{2}+\left(1-m\right)^{2}}\,.

(167)

We decompose
\vec{r}
as

\vec{r}=Q\vec{u}+\left(1-Q\right)\vec{v}\,,

(168)

where we choose
\vec{r}_{0}=\vec{u}
and
\vec{r}_{1}=\vec{v}
, with

\vec{v}=v_{1}\vec{m}+v_{2}\vec{n}=\frac{1}{1-Q}\left(\vec{r}-Q\vec{u}\right)

(169)

and

\vec{u}=u_{1}\vec{m}+u_{2}\vec{n}\,,\qquad u_{1}=\frac{Am-p\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}}{r^{2}}\,,\qquad u_{2}=\frac{Ap+m\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}}{r^{2}}\,.

(170)

We first prove that the square root
\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}
is well-defined. This is so because

r-A=\frac{\left(1-r\right)\left(1+r-2Q\right)}{2Q}>\frac{\left(1-r\right)\left(1+r-2Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)}{2Q}=\frac{\left(1-r\right)^{2}\left(r+m\right)}{2Q\left(1-m\right)}>0\,.

(171)

We also have
\vec{r}\cdot\vec{u}=A
, such that
\vec{v}
is pure. The variable
u_{1}
increases continuously with
A
, so, writing it explicitly in terms of

u_{1}\left(Q\right)=\frac{1}{2Qr^{2}}\left(\left(2Q-1+r^{2}\right)m-p\sqrt{\left(1-r^{2}\right)\left(4Q\left(1-Q\right)-1+r^{2}\right)}\right)\,,

(172)

we have

u_{1}\left(Q\right)>u_{1}\left(Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\right)=\frac{p\left(m-\sqrt{r^{2}-p^{2}}\right)}{r^{2}}=0\,,

(173)

and

u_{1}\left(Q\right)<u_{1}\left(Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)=\frac{p^{2}-\left(1-m\right)^{2}}{2Q_{\textnormal{max}}\left(1-m\right)}=\frac{m-1+Q_{\textnormal{max}}}{Q_{\textnormal{max}}}\,.

(174)

Similarly, the variable
u_{2}
decreases continuously with
A
, because

\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial A}=\frac{p^{2}-A^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}\left(p\sqrt{r^{2}-A^{2}}+Am\right)}<0\,,

(175)

so, expressing
u_{2}

u_{2}\left(Q\right)=\frac{1}{2Qr^{2}}\left(\left(2Q-1+r^{2}\right)p+m\sqrt{\left(1-r^{2}\right)\left(4Q\left(1-Q\right)-1+r^{2}\right)}\right)\,,

(176)

u_{2}\left(Q\right)<u_{2}\left(Q_{\textnormal{crit}}\right)=1\,,\qquad u_{2}\left(Q\right)\geq u_{2}\left(Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)=\frac{p}{Q_{\textnormal{max}}}\,.

(177)

We can see then that the decomposition in (
169
) and (
170
) interpolates between the decomposition (
157

Q_{\textnormal{crit}}
and the decomposition (
142

\displaystyle m-u_{1}\left(Q\right)

\displaystyle>m-u_{1}\left(Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)=\frac{\left(1-m\right)\left(1-Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)}{Q_{\textnormal{max}}}>0\,,

(178)

\displaystyle u_{2}\left(Q\right)-p

\displaystyle>u_{2}\left(Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)-p=\frac{p\left(1-Q_{\textnormal{max}}\right)}{Q_{\textnormal{max}}}>0\,,

so, from (

u_{1}<v_{1}

u_{2}>v_{2}

u_{2}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{2}>0\,.

(179)

The decomposition in (
169

170

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\vec{r},\,\{\vec{m}_{j}\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q\right)\left(1+\vec{v}\cdot\vec{m}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-Q+m-Qu_{1}\right)

(180)

For the upper bound, consider the following variables for the dual problem (

G=\frac{g}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{g}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\,,\qquad\vec{g}=\frac{\vec{m}-\left(g-1\right)\vec{v}}{g}\,,

g=1+\frac{u_{2}}{u_{2}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{2}}\,,\qquad w=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{u_{2}-v_{2}}{u_{2}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{2}}\right)\,.

(182)

Notice that the vector
\vec{g}

(189)

Appendix E
Noisy states

In any finite dimension
d
, we consider states of the form

\rho_{\varepsilon}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\mathbbm{1}\,.

(207)

For future convenience, we define the terms

A_{\varepsilon}=d-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)\,,\qquad\mathbbm{1}_{\neq\phi}=\mathbbm{1}-\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\,,

(208)

such that

\rho_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{d}\left(A_{\varepsilon}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{\neq\phi}\right)\,,\qquad\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sqrt{A_{\varepsilon}}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\neq\phi}\right)\,.

(209)

For any basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
that’s unbiased to the state
\ket{\phi}
, we have the useful properties

\langle m_{j}|\rho_{\varepsilon}|m_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\,,\qquad\langle m_{j}|\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}|m_{j}\rangle=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(210)

E.1
Unambiguous guessing probability

As a special case of Theorem
1
, we know that

P_{\,\textnormal{UD}}^{*}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)=\varepsilon\,.

(211)

This bound is saturated, for example, when the state
\ket{\phi}
is unbiased to the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, as then we could represent
\rho_{\varepsilon}
in a basis
\{\ket{u_j}\}
that is unbiased with respect to
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, which ensures that
\absolutevalue{\langle\umin| m_j \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d}
for all
j
. This proves Corollary
4.1
.

We know, as a special case of
[
32
, Theorem 1]
, that the maximal randomness from a noisy state in the minimum error (
Q=0
) scenario is

P_{\,\textnormal{ME}}^{*}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\,,

(212)

so Eve’s probability of error, given the optimal strategy, is in the range

0<P_{\textnormal{err}}<1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\,.

(213)

E.2
Fixed rate of inconclusive outcomes

Here we assume that the state
\ket{\phi}
is unbiased to the measurement basis
\{\ket{m_j}\}
, and for simplicity, we set it as
\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ket{j}
. The parameter
Q
lives in the range
0<Q<1-\varepsilon
. We will now prove Theorem
5
from the main text.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\rho_{\varepsilon}
be a noisy state in dimension
d
, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a measurement in a basis to which the state
\ket{\phi}
is unbiased. Then the FRIO randomness, for a fixed rate
Q
of inconclusive outcomes, is given by

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(\sqrt{d\left(1-Q\right)-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}+\left(d-1\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\,.

(214)

Proof.

We begin with the lower bound. Consider the decomposition

p_{0}=Q\,,\qquad p_{j}=\frac{1-Q}{d}\,,\qquad\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\,,\qquad\rho_{j}=\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(215)

with

\ket{\psi_j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d(1-Q)}}\left(\left(\sqrt{A_{\varepsilon}-Qd}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)\ket{\phi}+\sqrt{d\varepsilon}\,\ket{m_j}\right)\,.

(216)

Notice that, from (
209
),

\ket{\psi_j}=\sqrt{d\rho_{\gamma}}\ket{m_j}\,,\qquad\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-Q}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(217)

where the parameter
\gamma
has the range
\varepsilon<\gamma<1
.
The states
\{\ket{\psi_j}\}
are normalized, since

\langle\psi_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle=d\,\langle m_{j}|\rho_{\delta}|m_{j}\rangle=1\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(218)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}

\displaystyle=d\rho_{\gamma}=\frac{d}{1-Q}\left(\left(1-Q-\varepsilon\right)\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\mathbbm{1}\right)\,,

(219)

so the decomposition is valid. It gives us the lower bound

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\absolutevalue{ \langle\psi_j | m_j \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1-Q}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}

(220)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(\sqrt{d\left(1-Q\right)-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}+\left(d-1\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\,.

From the other direction, consider the following variables for the dual problem (
77
),

G=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d}\,\rho_{\gamma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,,\qquad w=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{\sqrt{dA_{\gamma}}}\,

(221)

with
\gamma
as above. The variable
G
is positive definite by construction, so
G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}
can have at most one non-positive eigenvalue (see e.g.
[
23
, Corollary 4.3.9]
). We have

\left(G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\right)\ket{\psi_j}=\sqrt{d}\left(\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d}-\langle m_{j}|\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}|m_{j}\rangle\right)\ket{m_j}=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(222)

so
G-\outerproduct{m_j}{m_j}\succcurlyeq 0
for all
j
. The other condition is equivalent to
\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(G\right)\geq w
. We have

\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(G\right)=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d}\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\left(\rho_{\gamma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{\sqrt{dA_{\gamma}}}=w\,,

(223)

so we conclude that
G\succcurlyeq w\mathbbm{1}
. Since the variables are valid, they give us the upper bound

\displaystyle P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tr\left(G\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)-wQ=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d}\left(\tr\left(\rho_{\gamma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{Q\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{A_{\gamma}}}\right)

(224)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d\sqrt{d}}\left(\frac{A_{\varepsilon}-Qd}{\sqrt{A_{\gamma}}}+\left(d-1\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-Q\right)\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}}{d\sqrt{d}}\left(\frac{A_{\gamma}}{\sqrt{A_{\gamma}}}+\left(d-1\right)\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)=\frac{1-Q}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,.

Since the upper and lower bounds match, we can conclude that (
214
) is the optimal guessing probability, proving Theorem
5
.
∎

Let’s now translate the previous results to the case where have a fixed probability
T
of error. On one hand, we can write (
214
) as

P_{\,\textnormal{Q}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\frac{1-Q}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad 0<Q<Q_{\textnormal{max}}

(225)

where
Q_{\textnormal{max}}=1-\varepsilon
.
The probability of error is then

P_{\textnormal{err}}=1-Q-\frac{1-Q}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,.

(226)

The extreme points of
T
are
T=0
, at
Q_{\textnormal{max}}
, and
T_{\textnormal{max}}
, corresponding to
Q=0
, where

T_{\textnormal{max}}=1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}=\frac{d-1}{d^{2}}\left(\sqrt{d-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\,.

(227)

If we fix
P_{\textnormal{err}}=T
, we can invert (
225
) to find
Q
in terms of
T
as

Q\left(T\right)=1-T-\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}\,.

(228)

This function decreases continuously with
T
, as expected, so we have

Q\left(T\right)<Q\left(0\right)=1-\varepsilon=Q_{\textnormal{max}}

(229)

and

Q\left(T\right)>Q\left(T_{\textnormal{max}}\right)=1-T_{\textnormal{max}}-\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(\sqrt{d-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}+\left(d-1\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}=0\,,

(230)

so
Q\left(T\right)
has the correct range.
We can then solve the optimal guessing probability with a fixed probability
T
of error,

P_{\,\textnormal{T}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}\,,\qquad 0<T<T_{\textnormal{max}}\,.

(231)

Appendix F
Noisy states and measurements

We will now consider a scenario where Alice holds the noisy state
\rho_{\varepsilon}

and
a noisy measurement, given by
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}=\{M_{x,\,\varepsilon}\}_{x}
with

M_{x,\,\varepsilon}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\outerproduct{m_x}{m_x}+\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\mathbbm{1}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(232)

where
\{\ket{m_x}\}
is an orthonormal basis and, as before,

\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\ket{m_x}\,.

(233)

Note that

\langle\phi|M_{x,\,\varepsilon}|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\,,\qquad\langle\phi|\sqrt{M_{x,\,\varepsilon}}|\phi\rangle=\frac{\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}}{d}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(234)

and that

\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\ket{m_x}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\sqrt{A_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)\ket{\phi}+\sqrt{d\varepsilon}\ket{m_x}\right)\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(235)

We assume that Eve can simultaneously decompose
\rho_{\varepsilon}
into states
\{\ket{\psi_\mu}\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}
into POVMs
\{\mathcal{N}_{\nu}\}
according to a joint probability distribution
\{p_{\mu,\,\nu}\}
. The measurements
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}=\{N_{x,\,\nu}\}_{x}
must be valid, i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{x,\,\nu}\succcurlyeq 0\,,\qquad\sum_{x=1}^{d}N_{x,\,\nu}=\mathbbm{1}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\quad\nu\in\{0,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(236)

while the joint conditions are (see
[
37
, Equation 5]
)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}p_{\mu,\,\nu}\outerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_\mu}=\rho_{\varepsilon}\,,

(237)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}p_{\mu,\,\nu}N_{x,\,\nu}=M_{x,\,\varepsilon}\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}p_{\mu,\,\nu}\langle\psi_{\mu}|N_{x,\,\nu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle=\tr\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\,M_{x,\,\varepsilon}\right)\,.

For convenience, we denote Eve’s decomposition by the shorthand
\mathcal{D}=\{\{p_{\mu,\,\nu}\},\,\{\ket{\psi_\mu}\},\,\{\mathcal{N_{\nu}}\}\}
. Given some decomposition
\mathcal{D}
that satisfies the constraints (
237
), Eve chooses her outcome deterministically based on the inputs
\mu
and
\nu
using a guessing function

f\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(238)

The outcome
f\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=0
is inconclusive, as before, so Eve’s guessing probability is given by

P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho,\,\mathcal{M}|\mathcal{D},\,\,f\right)=\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}p_{\mu,\,\nu}\,p\left(f\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)|\mu,\,\nu\right)\,,

(239)

where
p\left(y\,|\mu,\,\nu\right)
is the conditional probability of achieving outcome
y
given inputs
\mu
and
\nu
. When
y=0
, the conditional probability is trivially zero for any
\mu
or
\nu
, since there is no outcome zero, while for all
x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we have

p\left(x|\mu,\,\nu\right)=\langle\psi_{\mu}|N_{x,\,\nu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle\,.

(240)

Eve’s guessing probability probability when she makes a ‘classical decomposition’, as in this framework, is never greater than if she had joint quantum correlations with both the state and the measurement
[
37
]
, according to a model introduced in
[
14
]
and developed in
[
37
,
7
,
6
]
. We will not attempt to find Eve’s
optimal
(quantum or classical) guessing probability in the joint noise case; instead, we will choose a decomposition
\mathcal{D}
and a guessing function
f
to
lower bound
Eve’s joint guessing probability, given any upper bound
T
on her error probability.

We begin by introducing a critical value for the noise
\varepsilon
,

\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}=\frac{d}{2\left(d+\sqrt{d}\right)}\,.

(241)

Generalizing a joint decomposition from
[
6
]
, we will use the following states
\ket{\psi_\mu}
and measurements
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}=\{N_{x,\,\nu}\}_{x}
with different probability distributions
\{p_{\mu,\,\nu}\}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\mu}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\ket{\phi}\,,\qquad\hskip 209.12791pt&\mu=0\,,\\
\sqrt{d\rho_{\gamma}}\ket{m_\mu}\,,\qquad&\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\end{cases}

(242)

\displaystyle N_{x,\,\nu}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\outerproduct{m_x}{m_x}\,,\quad&\nu=0\,,\\
d\sqrt{M_{x,\,\gamma}}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\sqrt{M_{x,\,\gamma}}+\frac{d-1}{d}\gamma\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\neq x}-\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq x}}{\psi_{\neq x}}\right)\,,\quad&\nu=x\,,\\
\sqrt{M_{x,\,\gamma}}\left(\ket{m_x}-\ket{m_\nu}\right)\left(\bra{m_x}-\bra{m_\nu}\right)\sqrt{M_{x,\,\gamma}}\,,\quad&\nu\neq x\,,\;\;\;\nu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\end{cases}

where

\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-Q}\,,\qquad\mathbbm{1}_{\neq x}=\mathbbm{1}-\outerproduct{x}{x}\,,\qquad\ket{\psi_{\neq x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\underset{y\neq x}{\sum_{y=1}^{d}}\ket{y}\,.

(243)

Here,
Q
is just a parameter in the range
0\leq Q\leq 1-\varepsilon
that’s chosen by Eve. Notice that, when
\nu=0
, we have

\sum_{x=1}^{d}N_{x,\,0}=\mathbbm{1}\,,

(244)

while for all
\nu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we have

\underset{x\neq\nu}{\sum_{x=1}^{d}}N_{x,\,\nu}=\frac{1}{d}\left(A_{\gamma}\mathbbm{1}_{\neq\nu}-\sqrt{A_{\gamma}\gamma\left(d-1\right)}\left(\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq\nu}}{m_\nu}+\outerproduct{m_\nu}{\psi_{\neq\nu}}\right)+\gamma\left(d-1\right)\outerproduct{m_\nu}{m_\nu}\right)\,,

(245)

and

\sqrt{M_{x,\,\gamma}}\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\sqrt{A_{\gamma}}\ket{m_x}+\sqrt{\gamma\left(d-1\right)}\ket{\psi_{\neq x}}\right)\,.

(246)

The POVMs
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}=\{N_{x,\,\nu}\}_{x}
in (
242

are positive semidefinite and

\sum_{x=1}^{d}N_{x,\,\nu}=\left(1-\delta_{\nu,\,0}\right)N_{\nu,\,\nu}+\underset{x\neq\nu}{\sum_{x=1}^{d}}N_{x,\,\nu}=\mathbbm{1}\qquad\textnormal{for all }\;\;\nu\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

(247)

For future convenience, note too that

\underset{\nu\neq x}{\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}}N_{x,\,\nu}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(d-1\right)A_{\gamma}\outerproduct{m_x}{m_x}-\sqrt{A_{\gamma}\,\gamma\left(d-1\right)}\left(\outerproduct{m_x}{\psi_{\neq x}}+\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq x}}{m_x}\right)+\gamma\mathbbm{1}_{\neq x}\right)\,,

(248)

such that

\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}N_{x,\,\nu}=N_{x,\,x}+\underset{\nu\neq x}{\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}}N_{x,\,\nu}=dM_{x,\,\gamma}\,.

(249)

We also have the useful properties

\bra{\phi}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\neq x}-\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq x}}{\psi_{\neq x}}\right)=\langle m_{x}|\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\neq x}-\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq x}}{\psi_{\neq x}}\right)=0\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(250)

so we will find that the projector
\mathbbm{1}-\outerproduct{\psi_{\neq x}}{\psi_{\neq x}}
does not contribute to our relevant conditional probabilities. For any
x\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{x}|N_{x,\,x}|\psi_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=d^{2}\absolutevalue{\langle m_x | \sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}} \sqrt{M_{x, \, \gamma}} | \phi\rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d^{3}}\left(A_{\gamma}+2\left(d-1\right)\sqrt{A_{\gamma}\,\gamma}-\gamma\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}

(251)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}-\gamma\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}\,

and, when

and
\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}

\langle\psi_{\mu}|N_{x,\,\mu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle=d\absolutevalue{ \langle m_\mu| \sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}} \sqrt{M_{x, \, \gamma}} \left( \ket{m_x} - \ket{m_\mu} \right) \rangle}^{2}=\frac{1}{d^{3}}\left(A_{\gamma}-2\sqrt{A_{\gamma}\,\gamma}-\gamma\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}\,,

(252)

such that

\sum_{\mu=1}\langle\psi_{\mu}|N_{x,\,\mu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle=\langle\psi_{x}|N_{x,\,x}|\psi_{x}\rangle+\underset{\mu\neq x}{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}}\langle\psi_{\mu}|N_{x,\,\mu}|\psi_{\mu}\rangle=\left(\tr\rho_{\gamma}\right)^{2}=1\,.

(253)

The relevant conditional probabilities are

\displaystyle p\left(\mu|\mu,\,0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

(254)

\displaystyle p\left(\nu|0,\,\nu\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{M_{\nu,\,\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;\nu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

\displaystyle p\left(\mu|\mu,\,\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}-\gamma\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}\quad

\displaystyle\textnormal{for all}\;\;\mu\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,.

254
), her guessing probability is then

P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{D},\,f\right)=\frac{1-Q}{d}\left(\,\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}p\left(\mu|\mu,\,0\right)+\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}p\left(\nu|0,\,\nu\right)\right)=\frac{2\left(1-Q\right)}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\,.

(281)

The probability of giving an inconclusive outcome is

P_{\textnormal{inc}}=\sum_{\mu,\,\nu=0}^{d}p_{\mu,\,\nu}\,\delta_{f\left(\mu,\,\nu\right),\,0}=2Q-1\,,

(282)

such that the probability of error is

P_{\textnormal{err}}=1-P_{\textnormal{inc}}-P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{D},\,f\right)=2\left(1-Q\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\right)\,.

(283)

We should then choose the parameter
Q
such that this error probability matches the chosen parameter
T
. The correct value for
Q
is

Q\left(T\right)=\frac{2\left(d-1\right)\left(1-\varepsilon\right)-dT-2\sqrt{2T\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}}{2\left(d-1\right)}\,,

(284)

which decreases strictly with
T
(note that
Q\left(0\right)=1-\varepsilon
).
This leaves us with the guessing probability

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{D},\,f\right)

\displaystyle=1-P_{\textnormal{inc}}-P_{\textnormal{err}}=2-2Q\left(T\right)-T

(285)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)+T+2\sqrt{2T\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}}{d-1}=\frac{\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}}{d-1}\,.

As a final step to proving that this decomposition is valid, we still have to show that
Q\left(T\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
when
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
and
T\geq T_{1}
, so that the mixing probability distribution
\{p_{\mu,\,\nu}\}
is well-defined. Let’s define another parameter,

T_{2}=\frac{d-1}{d^{2}}\left(\sqrt{A_{2\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\right)^{2}=1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{2\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\,,

(286)

which satisfies

Q\left(T_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,.

(287)

Since the function
Q\left(T\right)
in (
284
) decreases strictly with
T
, we have

Q\left(T\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;0\leq T\leq T_{2}\,,

(288)

so this decomposition is valid for all
T\leq T_{2}
. If we can show that
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
then we can show that the decomposition is valid for all
T<T_{1}
, as desired.
We have

T_{2}-T_{1}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{2\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}>0\,,

(289)

where we use the inequality (
277
). The guessing probability (
281
) is then achievable in this region.

F.3
Single versus joint noise

We have found a lower bound on Eve’s joint guessing probability for all
\varepsilon
and
T
, which we summarize as

P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}\right)\geq\begin{cases}1\,,\quad&\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\\
\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}\,,\quad&\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\;\;T\geq T_{1}\,,\\
\frac{\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}}{d-1}\,,\quad&\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}\,,\;\;T<T_{1}\,.\end{cases}

(290)

We will now compare her performance to that in the single noise case, where only the state is noisy. In order to compare these two cases, we will assume in each case that a total amount of noise
\delta
is shared between the devices. In the single noise case, this is all contained in the state
\rho_{\delta}
, which is measured in
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x}
,
M_{x}=\outerproduct{m_x}{m_x}
, while in the joint noise case, there is an amount
\varepsilon
of noise in each device, where

\varepsilon=1-\sqrt{1-\delta}\,,\qquad\delta=\varepsilon\left(2-\varepsilon\right)\,.

(291)

The value
\varepsilon
is chosen such that

\tr\left(\Delta_{\delta}\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right)\,M_{x}\right)=\tr\left(\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right)\,\Delta_{\varepsilon}\left(M_{x}\right)\right)\qquad\textnormal{for all}\;\;x

(292)

for any state
\ket{\phi}
and any measurement
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x}
for which
\tr M_{x}=1
for all
x
. Remember that from (
231
), for a fixed
T
, the optimal guessing probability in the single noise case is

P_{\textnormal{single}}\left(\rho_{\delta},\,\mathcal{M}\right)=\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\delta\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}\,,\qquad 0\leq T<T_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\delta\right)\,,

(293)

where

T_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\delta\right)=1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\delta}}\right)^{2}\,

(294)

(note that we now include the extreme points
T=0
and
T=T_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\delta\right)
).
This guessing probability is only perfect when
\delta=1
, so when
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
, the joint guessing probability is clearly higher.
Let’s define the difference

\left(*\right)=P_{\textnormal{joint}}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\,\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{D},\,f\right)-P_{\textnormal{single}}\left(\rho_{\delta},\,\mathcal{M}\right)\,.

(295)

When
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\textnormal{crit}}
and when
T_{1}\leq T\leq T_{\textnormal{max}}\left(\delta\right)
, using (
268
), we have

\displaystyle\left(*\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{d-1}\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\delta\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}

(296)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\left(\tr\sqrt{\rho_{\delta}}\right)^{2}>0\,,

since where in the final inequality, we use
T<T_{\textnormal{max}}
, the inequality (
277
) and

2\varepsilon-\delta=2\varepsilon-\varepsilon\left(2-\varepsilon\right)=\varepsilon^{2}>0\,.

(297)

When
0\leq T<T_{1}
, using (
285
), we have

\displaystyle\left(*\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2\varepsilon\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}}{d-1}-\frac{\left(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{\delta\left(d-1\right)}\right)^{2}}{d-1}

(298)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta}}\left(2\sqrt{\frac{T}{d-1}}+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta}\right)\left(2\varepsilon-\delta\right)>0\,.

We have shown, then, that in the case of an unbiased state and measurement pair, for any parameter
T
, dimension
d
and total noise
\delta
, the guessing probability of a correlated eavesdropper is strictly higher when the state and measurement are
both
noisy, rather than the state alone. This proves Theorem
6
from the main text.
