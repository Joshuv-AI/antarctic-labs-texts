Clinical Care and Referral Pathways for Food Security Screening in Health Care: A Scoping Review
Authors: Sydney Yearley, Ronit Ridberg, Allie Tatarian, Lizbeth Moreno Loaeza, Saverio Feudo, Saahil Patel, Molly Duncan, Dariush Mozaffarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood and nutrition insecurity are linked to poor health outcomes and disparities, yet how health care systems implement screening and referrals remains poorly understood.MethodsWe searched for studies evaluating screening and referral processes for food and nutrition insecurity for patients of all ages in US health care settings. Searches were performed through May 2025 in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Cochrane (via Ovid), Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health (EBSCO), and Social Interventions Research and Evaluation Network Evidence and Resource Library. Studies were included if they reported both a screening process and referral mechanism. Findings were synthesized narratively.ResultsOf 11 406 records identified, 136 studies met the inclusion criteria; all screened for food insecurity, and none screened for nutrition insecurity. Most studies screened general populations across age, sex, race, and ethnicity, with few restricting by socioeconomic status or clinical conditions. Screening primarily used tools embedded within broader social determinants of health screenings (46%) and integrated into electronic health records (48%), typically in outpatient settings (49%). Screening methods varied, most commonly self‐administered by patients (21%) or by clinic staff (20%) and were unspecified in one fifth of studies (21%). A total of 129 studies described 39 unique referral strategies across 4 categories: community‐based resources (46%), health care–embedded services (35%), federal nutrition programs (12%), and Food Is Medicine interventions (7%), with 46% spanning multiple categories. While about half of studies (46%) reported referral rates, only about one third (37%) reported referral completion.ConclusionsThese findings highlight current practices and evidence gaps, informing priorities to strengthen screening and referral systems that advance food security, nutrition, health, and health equity.Keywords: food security, nutrition security, social determinants of healthSubject Categories: Social Determinants of Health, Disparities

Food insecurity, a household's limited or uncertain access to adequate food, is associated with adults with higher rates of multiple chronic conditions, preventable hospitalizations, and health care use 1 , 2 and in children with malnutrition, obesity, poor academic performance, increased hospitalizations, anxiety, and depression. 3 In 2023, 18 million US households (13.5%) reported food insecurity at some point over the year, disproportionately affecting lower‐income households, children, people of color, and rural communities. 4 Nutrition insecurity, a related but distinct construct, refers to a lack of consistent access to, availability of, and affordability of nourishing foods that support health and help manage disease and is independently associated with increased prevalence of chronic conditions and sociodemographic disparities. 5 , 6

Given its implications for health and equity, food insecurity screening in clinical settings is recommended by several organizations. 7 , 8 , 9 The American Heart Association Healthcare by Food Advisory on Food Is Medicine (FIM) emphasizes the need for health care professionals to screen for food and nutrition insecurity, counsel patients on referral options (like FIM), and integrate standardized screening and referral processes into the electronic health record (EHR). 10 At the same time, the US Preventive Services Task Force does not endorse routine screening, citing insufficient evidence on how best to respond to a positive screen, a key gap that reflects ongoing uncertainties in the research. The potential impact of screening will be influenced by both screening implementation approaches and referral systems for those screening positive. 11 For example, individuals may be referred to local food pantries or other community resources; to health care–embedded resources like care management, patient navigators, or social workers; to federal nutrition programs like the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP); Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children; school meals; or meals for older adults; or, in more recent years, to FIM interventions like produce prescriptions, medically tailored groceries, or medically tailored meals. Screening tools for food insecurity include the validated 2‐item Hunger Vital Sign (HVS), 12 , 13 , 14 now integrated and available for use in Epic and other EHR systems. In comparison, screening for nutrition insecurity remains in early stages but with evidence for its independent clinical relevance. 6

Importantly, the current state of food or nutrition insecurity screening and referral pathways in health care is not well understood. 15 A 2017 review identified 22 health care–based programs completed through August 2016 that screened adults aged ≥50 years for food insecurity and connected them to resources, such as referrals for community‐based food supports, assistance with applications to federal nutrition programs, patient education and skill building, or distribution of produce vouchers. 16 Other reviews have focused on tool development, validation, and implementation. 17 , 18 For example, one review reported that the HVS is the most commonly used and validated screening tool, yet overall screening rates in health care remain low. 17 Furthermore, a 2019 review reported that while screening is generally accepted across clinical settings, targeted interventions are needed to improve uptake. 18 These studies demonstrate that screening tools are feasible and acceptable, but important gaps remain: There has been no updated review of health care–based screening and referral practices since August 2016, and no synthesis exists that captures implementation across all age groups, beyond the older adult population.

To address these key knowledge gaps, we conducted a scoping review to identify and characterize the evidence on how food and nutrition insecurity screening is implemented in clinical settings in the United States, and to summarize the referral pathways, interventions, and resources used to address positive screens. Although we searched the literature for both food and nutrition insecurity, no eligible studies assessing nutrition insecurity were identified in the literature search. Accordingly, this review synthesizes the evidence for food insecurity screening and referrals only.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.

Data Availability

All extracted data used in this review are provided in Tables S1 through S4. The data collection form is described in the Data Abstraction and Evaluation section, and the Excel version is available upon request. Analyses were conducted in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); however, no unique analytic code was generated, as analyses consisted of descriptive summary statistics and visualizations. The full search strategy is available in Supplemental Methods.

Institutional Review Board Statement

This study involved the synthesis of previously published and publicly available data and did not involve interaction with human subjects; therefore, it was not considered human subjects research and did not require institutional review board approval.

Search Methods for Identification of Studies

Four databases were searched through May 2025, including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Cochrane (via Ovid), Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health (EBSCO), and Social Interventions Research and Evaluation Network Evidence and Resource Library. The detailed search strategy is reported in the Supplemental Methods. Search terms for food insecurity were modified from prior reports, 19 while nutrition insecurity was searched using stand‐alone terms. No studies assessing nutrition insecurity were identified. A detailed summary of study selection, including inclusion and exclusion criteria at each stage, is provided in the Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews flow diagram (Figure S1). The full review protocol was published online in advance of the search and is available for reference; no amendments were made after its publication. 20

Article Selection

To be eligible, studies must have (1) assessed risk of food or nutrition insecurity using a screening tool in a health care setting (eg, outpatient, inpatient, urgent care/emergency); and (2) used at least 1 strategy to connect individuals screening positive to resources, programs, or interventions designed to address identified food or nutrition insecurity. Given differences in other nations' health care practices, we excluded studies conducted outside the United States or published in a language other than English; and given our interest in contemporary practices, we excluded studies published in or before 2000. Studies could screen patients of any age (pediatrics, adults, or geriatrics), sex, race and ethnicity, US geographic region, or socioeconomic status. Titles and abstracts were reviewed by a single investigator (S.Y., L.M., S.F., or S.P.), and 252 articles were identified for full‐text review (Figure S1). Abstracts without full‐text manuscripts were eligible (applying to 2 conference abstracts and 1 phase I trial protocol), though these provided limited extractable data. During full‐text review, each article was evaluated independently and in duplicate by 2 investigators (S.Y., L.M., S.F., S.P., or M.D.). Discordant inclusion decisions were reviewed by both investigators and by a third investigator when consensus could not be reached. Following review, a total of 136 studies met eligibility criteria (Tables S1 through S3).

Data Abstraction and Evaluation

Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by 2 investigators (S.Y., L.M., S.F., S.P., or M.D.) using a predetermined digital extraction form. Any discordance was reviewed by both investigators and by a third investigator when consensus could not be reached. Extraction fields included study publication details (study title, study authors, and year of publication), target population (age, sex, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and defining clinical conditions); health care setting for screening (type, location, size); screening administration (tool type, tool validation, whether the tool was integrated within or external to the EHR, and who performed the screening); referral strategy as based on the immediate referral destination—the first point of contact a patient was connected to following a positive screen—rather than further downstream services that may have been subsequently accessed (referral modalities); percentage/number of individuals who screened positive who received a referral; and percentage/number of individuals who followed up on the referral placed. Findings were reviewed, presented, and evaluated through narrative synthesis.

Results

Study Selection

Of 11 406 records identified, 252 were evaluated using full‐text review, and 136 studies met the inclusion criteria (Figure S1, Tables S1–S3). Exclusions are detailed in Figure S1.

Characteristics of Target Population

All 136 identified studies screened for food insecurity; none screened for nutrition insecurity (Table 1). Participants' characteristics were reported broadly. Studies often targeted youth aged 0 to 17 years (34% [n=46]), adults aged ≥18 years (31% [n=41]), or all ages (25% [n=34]). Less frequently, studies did not specify age (7% [n=10]) or used other overlapping categories (3% [n=5]). Most studies (94% [n=128]) included both men and women; 6% (n=8) screened only women. No studies restricted screening on the basis of racial or ethnic backgrounds. Likewise, most studies (86% [n=117]) did not impose specific socioeconomic eligibility criteria, allowing for a more generalizable assessment of food security. A minority of studies focused on individuals enrolled in Medicaid (7% [n=10]), Medicare (1% [n=1]), or either (1% [n=2]), while a few others used alternative indicators of socioeconomic status not captured by these programs, such as being uninsured (4% [n=6]). Most studies (79% [n=108]) did not restrict screening to any specific clinical condition; a minority focused on particular health conditions such as diabetes (4% [n=5]), COVID‐19 (1% [n=2]), cancer (1% [n=2]), cardiovascular disease (1% [n=1]), or mental health disorders (1% [n=1]). Others targeted different conditions not listed above (8% [n=11]) or evaluated multiple conditions (4% [n=6]).

Characteristics of the Health Care Setting for Screening

In about half the studies (49% [n=66]), screening occurred in outpatient clinics (Table 2). Other locations included hospital inpatient settings (13% [n=18]), urgent care/emergency departments (10% [n=14]), federally qualified health centers (10% [n=13]), and community health centers (7% [n=9]); with the remainder conducted in other settings (7% [n=9]), across multiple site types (3% [n=4]), or pharmacies (2% [n=3]). Most studies (65% [n=88]) did not report the size of the health care setting. Among those that did, 19% (n=26) were health care entities serving >50 000 patients per year, 11% (n=15) served 10 001 to 50 000, 3% (n=4) served 1001 to 10 000, and 2% (n=3) served 1 to 1000 patients annually. The identified studies were conducted across 25 states and Washington, DC, most commonly in Massachusetts (13% [n=18]), Ohio (10% [n=14]), and North Carolina (9% [n=12]) (Figure S2).

Characteristics of Screening Administration

The tool for food security screening was most frequently integrated within a broader social determinants of health (SDOH) screening tool (46% [n=62]), with the specific tool not further reported (the HVS is most commonly integrated into Epic), followed by the HVS used outside a broader SDOH screener (38% [n=51]), the US Department of Agriculture Household Food Security Survey (8% [n=11]), and other miscellaneous tools (3% [n=4]) (Table 2). Some studies (4% [n=6]) did not specify the tool used, and 1% (n=2) used multiple tools. The great majority of screening tools were validated (78% [n=106]), while 15% (n=20) were not, and 7% (n=10) did not specify validation status.

Survey administration was most commonly (48% [n=65]) integrated into the EHR system, followed by separate surveys either as paper handouts or electronically but not administered in the EHR (23% [n=31]); 1% (n=1) used both methods, and the remainder (29% [n=39]) did not specify the administration method. In comparable proportions of studies, patients completed the screening independently (21% [n=28]), clinic staff assisted with survey completion (20% [n=27]), or the mode of administration was not specified (21% [n=29]). The remaining administrators varied widely including research assistants (8% [n=11]); care managers, case workers, or social workers (5% [n=7]); physicians (4% [n=6]); nurses (3% [n=4]); and medical students (1% [n=2]). In 9% (n=12) of studies, multiple staff types administered the screening, and in 7% (n=10), personnel with other job titles were involved.

Referral Strategies

Among 136 studies, 129 described 39 unique referral strategies (Figure 1; Table S4), organized across 12 domains. These included community‐based resources (46% [n=89] studies), followed by health care–embedded services (35% [n=67]), federal nutrition programs (12% [n=23]), and FIM interventions (7% [n=14]). Because 46% of studies (n=59) studies used multicomponents strategies including >1 domain, the totals exceed the number of individual studies.

Among studies that implemented community‐based referrals either as stand‐alone approaches or as part of multicomponent strategies (46% [n=89]), the most common referral was to off‐site food resources alone, primarily community food pantries, where participants were directly connected by the health care system conducting the screening (22% [n=20]). This was followed by referrals involving care management, patient navigators, or social workers with a resource guide (17% [n=15]), and referrals providing only a resource guide (16% [n=14]). The remaining 2 of the 5 most frequently used community‐based referral strategies included referrals to both off‐site food resources and SNAP connection/education (7% [n=6]), and referrals to a community health worker only (6% [n=5]).

Among studies that included health care–embedded referrals, either as stand‐alone strategies or in combination with approaches from other domains (35% [n=67]), the 5 most common referral types were referrals to care management, patient navigators, or social workers with a resource guide (22% [n=15]); care management, patient navigators, or social workers alone (19% [n=13]); on‐site food pantry or food provisions only (eg, meal vouchers for the hospital cafeteria or a 1‐time emergency food box) (6% [n=4]); and 3 strategies each reported equally: on‐site food pantry or food provisions combined with a resource guide, and education and connection to off‐site food, and produce prescription programs and education (each 4% [n=3]).

A smaller subset (18% [n=23]) assisted patients with referrals to federal nutrition programs, either alone (35% [n=8]) or in combination with community (35% [n=8]), health care (13% [n=3]), both community and health care (9% [n=2]), or both community and FIM (9% [n=2]) resources. Among federal nutrition programs referrals (n=23), 74% (n=17) focused on SNAP, 9% (n=2) on Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children, and 17% (n=4) on both.

Despite growing national interest in and use of FIM interventions nationally, 15 referrals to FIM were less commonly reported (11% [n=14]). Most referrals were to produce prescription programs (36% [n=5]) and medically tailored groceries (36% [n=5]), followed by medically tailored meals (14% [n=2]), or to a combination of 2 FIM programs (14% [n=2]). All but 1 of these 14 studies integrated these FIM referrals within a broader, multicomponent strategy that also included referrals to community resources, health care–based services, or federal nutrition programs. Unlike the overall trend in which most studies did not focus screening on a clinical condition, 9 of the 14 studies that referred food‐insecure individuals to FIM interventions specifically screened patients with a defined clinical condition.

Trends Over Time

Between the 2 most common screening tools, the HVS was implemented earlier, while broader SDOH tools emerged later and have become increasingly common (Figure 2A). There were no clear temporal trends in regards to targeting food insecurity screening to certain clinical conditions or socioeconomic status (Figures S3 and S4). Referral Rates and Follow‐Up Among Food‐Insecure Patients (Implementation/Process Outcomes)

Reporting on referral placement, whether patients who screened positive were subsequently provided with a referral to relevant resources, and reporting on referral completion, whether those who received a referral subsequently followed up and successfully accessed or engaged with the referred service, were inconsistent across studies. These measures represent implementation or process outcomes and serve as proxies for referral effectiveness, rather than measures of clinical outcomes or changes in food security status. Almost half of studies reported the proportion or number of food‐insecure patients who received a referral (46% [n=63]), and about one third reported follow‐up or completion rates among those referred (37% [n=50]).

Discussion

This scoping review of food and nutrition insecurity screening practices and referral strategies in health care settings identified several novel findings. All included studies screened for food insecurity, but none addressed nutrition insecurity. Most studies targeted general patient populations without limitations according to age, sex, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, or clinical condition. Screenings were reported in 25 states and Washington, DC, a fairly broad cross‐section of the nation. Screening was most commonly administered in outpatient clinics and using tools embedded within broader SDOH assessments (the use of which is increasing over time). Referral pathways most often lead to community‐based resources, such as off‐site food pantries, and health care system resources, such as care management, patient navigators, or social workers with a resource guide or alone. Among different types of referrals, FIM interventions were the most recent to emerge.

The most commonly identified food security screening tools were embedded in existing SDOH assessments (likely using the HVS) or used the HVS alone, presenting opportunities and concerns. The HVS has high sensitivity (>97%) but only moderate specificity (74%–83%) for identifying food insecurity, resulting in overestimation of affected individuals; moreover, it primarily captures economic strain rather than broader barriers to healthy eating. In addition, some screening tools relied on nonvalidated questions within SDOH screening frameworks. This risks misclassifying patients, leading to missed opportunities for referral (false negatives) or inappropriate allocation of referral efforts (false positives). Because these limitations leave certain barriers to healthy eating unrecognized, incorporating nutrition insecurity screening could provide complementary insights to better identify at‐risk patients.

No study included in our review incorporated screening for nutrition insecurity, despite growing recognition that food and nutrition security are complementary concepts. 21 The questions used to assess food insecurity, for example, help identify economic strain and limited resources, rather than poor nutrition per se, 22 and also do not identify common barriers to healthy eating outside of cost, such as related to knowledge, cooking skills, transportation, access, or physical ability. 23 Looking ahead, the recently validated 2‐item Nutrition Security Screener, tested in >20 000 adults across 5 diverse US populations, may provide a useful tool for addressing these gaps. The Nutrition Security Screener identifies individuals facing challenges to healthy eating who do not have food insecurity and also individuals facing greater challenges to healthy eating among those who have food insecurity. Nutrition insecurity assessed by the Nutrition Security Screener is independently associated with self‐reported physical and mental health outcomes, even accounting for food security status, and helps identify specific barriers to healthy eating. 6 Incorporating nutrition insecurity screening alongside food insecurity assessments could be an important future direction for health care practice and research, helping to identify individuals experiencing nutrition insecurity in addition to food insecurity.

Reporting on referral outcomes was limited; few studies indicated what proportion of patients who screened positive for food insecurity received a referral, or of those, how many actually followed up. Importantly, even these measures are a reflection of initial implementation success, rather than whether the efforts ultimately improved food security or health outcomes. This indicates a need to evaluate both initial and long‐term outcomes of screening and referral programs.

While referrals to community‐based services were common, such referrals can require patients to overcome structural barriers like contacting the community‐based service, transportation needs, time constraints, limited food offerings, and concern around stigma, each potentially limiting effectiveness, especially in most marginalized populations. 24 , 25 , 26 Health care–based referrals can connect patients to resources within the community and also federal nutrition programs like SNAP and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children, 16 potentially increasing effectiveness compared with community referral alone. However, the effectiveness of these strategies has not been compared. Isolated information or connections to federal nutrition programs were a less common strategy. Although the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children is associated with better nutrition and health outcomes in mothers and their children, underenrollment among eligible participants remains a challenge 27 also seen in SNAP, where only 78% of eligible individuals enroll. 28 These gaps highlight the potential for health care–based assistance to increase enrollment in these programs, particularly when paired with professional care management or navigation services.

Referrals to FIM interventions began appearing after 2017 and were ultimately reported in 7% of all studies overall and 8% of studies conducted from 2017 onward. FIM was almost always implemented alongside other referral strategies, consistent with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' guidance for Medicaid Section 1115 demonstration waivers that requires provision of nutrition services like FIM to be coordinated with referrals to federal nutrition programs. 29 Most screening programs that used FIM also focused on a clinical condition, also consistent with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services guidance for FIM in Medicaid. Given the promise of FIM interventions to address both food and nutrition insecurity, our findings support the need for additional reporting and evaluation of their use as a clinical care strategy to address individuals with positive screens.

Our scoping review significantly extends prior work. To date, only 1 prior review examined food insecurity screening and referral in US health care settings, and it focused solely on adults aged ≥50 years and on studies published through 2016. 16 In addition, that study relied on a nomination‐based approach, soliciting programs through expert networks, Internet searches, and self‐ or third‐party nominations, followed by manual eligibility screening by reviewers to identify studies for inclusion in their analysis, as opposed to our Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses–concordant scoping review including multiple electronic databases and duplicate screening and data extraction to comprehensively identify and evaluate relevant studies. We evaluated screening across the life span, rather than focusing solely on older adults. While the earlier review reported use of the HVS in isolation as the most common tool, we identified screening in more recently published studies to be commonly embedded within broader SDOH tools. This signals a shift toward integrated social needs assessments, as well as an opportunity to incorporate nutrition insecurity screening. Moreover, we observed that most studies targeted general patient populations, rather than those with specific chronic conditions, which this review found, reflecting a broader recognition of food insecurity's relevance to overall health. Most of the identified studies screened broad, rather than specific at‐risk, populations. While this helps normalize attention to social needs, reduces stigma, and also can identify food insecure individuals that might otherwise be missed, programs focused on at‐risk populations, such as those on Medicaid or Medicare, may more efficiently identify those likely to benefit most. 30 A targeted focus on high‐risk groups may also help reduce disparities in food security, nutrition, and health outcomes. The relative effectiveness of universal versus targeted food and nutrition screening is an important area for future study. Finally, we identified 3‐fold more types of distinct referral strategies and mapped how these have evolved over time, including the rise of FIM interventions.

Collectively, these findings reveal that while food insecurity screening is increasingly implemented across health care settings, substantial evidence and practice gaps remain. Screening tools and processes vary widely, underscoring the need for standardization and implementation research to identify best practices. With 39 different referral strategies identified, relative effectiveness in promoting patient engagement and improving outcomes is unknown, warranting comparative and experimental studies. Furthermore, standardized reporting on referral effectiveness is not available, for either short‐ or long‐term outcomes. The growing use of FIM represents a novel intervention to address food and nutrition insecurity, but more evidence is needed on the integration between screening and referral systems and FIM treatments. Importantly, given disparities in food security and diet‐related diseases, research and reporting should be conducted through an equity lens. While many studies report basic patient characteristics, very few conducted stratified analyses to examine whether outcomes differ across different sociodemographic groups. Addressing this gap is a first step toward understanding existing disparities and informing best practices that ensure equitable screening and benefit. Finally, cross‐sector research is needed to understand how reimbursement and policy levers can sustain these processes as part of routine health care.

This investigation has several strengths. We conducted a broad and systematic search across multiple databases, reducing the likelihood of missed articles. We developed and implemented pragmatic inclusion and exclusion criteria, increasing generalizability. Full‐text article review and data extraction were performed independently and in duplicate, minimizing misclassification and augmenting validity. We identified studies with patients of diverse ages, sexes, racial and ethnic backgrounds, and socioeconomic status across varied geographic regions and health care settings, capturing a wide range of US populations and clinical contexts. We focused on interventional studies that screened for food or nutrition insecurity within health care and connected patients to resources, strengthening the clinical salience of our findings.

Potential limitations should be considered. We used published reports including abstracts, and it is likely that other screening practices are being used without being reported. Nonetheless, our findings reflect the current state of available published evidence on screening and referral practices for food and nutrition insecurity. Reporting on the effectiveness of screening and referral strategies was inconsistent, with the vast majority of studies lacking data on referral placement rates and referral follow‐up among food‐insecure patients, limiting understanding of their true impact. We focused on studies within the United States, and future research should consider screening practices in other nations.

In conclusion, our findings characterize the current state of reported food and nutrition insecurity screening and referral processes within US health care settings. These results highlight the different tools, strategies, settings, and referral strategies taking place. These findings inform ongoing clinical practice priorities aimed at advancing food security, nutrition, health, and health equity in the United States.
