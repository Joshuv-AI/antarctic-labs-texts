From rejection to formative development: recalibrating decisions for under-resourced authors
Authors: HA Tafenzi, R Belbaraka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeer review and editorial triage together are the cornerstones of scientific credibility yet their combined cracks are increasingly visible. As real-world data and digital health technologies have become increasingly important sources of evidence generation, many promising submissions from under-resourced settings receive terse desk rejections or nonspecialist reviews that obscure remediable methodological and reporting issues, limiting learning and career progression. We propose a compact, author-initiated, equity-centred remediation pathway—the mentorship escrow—that integrates upstream supports, a core remediation window, and a downstream pathway, converting borderline submissions into time-bounded and capacity-building interventions. We outline practical implementation steps, pilot design considerations, and measurable equity and quality metrics; discuss trade-offs across components; and address variable constraints including mentor availability, funding, potential selection bias, and risks of dependency, proposing mitigation strategies to ensure scalable, sustainable capacity building that preserves editorial standards while broadening the circulation of locally relevant evidence.Key words: editorial decisions, methodology, peer review, RWD

Introduction

Many readers may question the critique that follows, which draws in part on my experience as an in-training medical oncology researcher from a low- and middle-income country (LMIC). Early in my career, when digital health technologies, electronic health records, and cancer registries were increasingly central to evidence generation, producing original research was constrained, firstly, by local academic and logistical realities,1 with reliance on handwritten medical records and fragmented registries to produce real-world data, often prone to bias if established reporting guidelines that determine the perceived legitimacy and evaluability of a study2 were not rigorously applied; and secondly, even if these frameworks were followed, inequities persisted with terse desk rejections without diagnostic guidance and reviewer reports that were either cursory or written by nonspecialists, missing, therefore, substantive but fixable problems. By contrast, one later submission, rejected but accompanied by detailed, constructive editorial and reviewer feedback, transformed not only the manuscript and my approach to research but also my confidence to pursue further work; without it, several subsequent projects would likely have stalled.

Peer review and editorial triage together are the cornerstones of scientific credibility yet their combined cracks are increasingly visible,3, 4, 5 particularly vis-à-vis submissions with redundant or previously addressed study objectives, small sample sizes, inadequate data abstraction and/or statistical application, and poorly framed study reports—aspects that are more prevalent in resource-constrained settings, amplified by the lack of methodological support, collaborative networks, and scientific guidance. Designed both to assess validity, novelty, and significance and to serve as a formative corrective process that helps authors strengthen methods and presentation, these evaluation steps are self-correcting when both roles function.6,7 Nevertheless, when the processes fail, it results in an uneven, opaque, and unjust scholarship, which disproportionately harms authors and early-career investigators in LMICs. Through the following commentary, we urge a systematic rethink of how we evaluate manuscripts, vis-à-vis under-resourced authors, by redesigning incentives, increasing transparency, and building capacity so that desk decisions and peer reports are fairer, clearer, and genuinely instructive.

The lived problem: Gatekeeping without guidance

Journals legitimately differ by discipline, scope, editorial priorities, metrics, and capacity to protect focus and reader expectations.7 However, problems arise when the selection process for initial consideration and peer review becomes mechanistic gatekeeping tools rather than diagnostic, formative steps that steward scientific work.

Real-world studies are recognised as complementary data or evidence of under-represented patients and missed opportunities within the trial population—by generating new insights clarifying, for instance, the real-world effectiveness and safety of therapies or the impact of integrating new approaches into the patient care pathway to optimise quality of life or guide clinical decisions.8 Although safeguarding the integrity of these results through rigorous editorial and peer review is mandatory, otherwise would risk undermining scientific credibility, perpetuating bias, and allowing potentially valuable evidence to be dismissed or misinterpreted, yet, despite the critical role of evaluation, many first decisions arrive as formulaic desk rejections, ‘not suitable for publication’ or ‘declined due to lack of novelty and/or small sample size’, with no diagnostic detail or clear indication.9 Authors are left uncertain whether deficiencies are fundamental (irrecoverable), methodological (requiring specialist input), or presentational (fixable with better writing or additional analyses). That ambiguity wastes effort, demoralises investigators, blocks incremental improvement,10 and loses opportunity for learning and career progression, particularly for many LMIC researchers, which is considered a structural barrier rather than an isolated frustration that narrows whose questions are answered and whose evidence informs policy.11

Editorial triage is shaped by multiple, sometimes competing, factors such as the handling editor’s workload and subject-matter knowledge; the journal’s scope, capacity, audience, and metrics12,13; and, consciously or unconsciously, the perceived prominence of the authors or their institutions. At the same time, these practicalities carry measurable trade-offs, as many handling editors balance large submission volumes and short turnaround expectations and volunteer time alongside clinical, teaching, and research duties.14,15 Many publishers list formal qualifications for editorial posts (PhD/MD plus a substantial publication record ± experiences) but relying on publications solely is an imperfect proxy for assessing editorial competence for the reasons that articles are often collaborative and productivity does not automatically translate into the specialised methodological or editorial skills required to evaluate diverse submissions. Consequently, editors may risk misjudging manuscripts not because of poor intent but because selection criteria and time pressures do not always align with the detailed demands of technical appraisal, which biases indirectly the manuscript selection process, as it is customarily known that manuscripts including well-known authors from prestigious institutions may receive different handling compared with lesser-known researchers, thus limiting opportunities for clarification or guided revision,16 unless editorial processes explicitly guard against whatever bias and document work suitability and relevancy.

A parallel breakdown occurs at the reviewer level, particularly among those who lack domain-specific, methodological, or statistical expertise or the ability to analyse, think, and interpret work beyond their own practice and resource setting. Such reviewers may assess manuscripts through an inappropriate lens, overlooking contextual constraints that shaped the study. In many LMIC settings, limited diagnostics, constrained therapeutic options, economic pressures, and reliance on retrospective clinical records influence study design and interpretation.17 A specialist reviewer’s value lies in accurately understanding the manuscript’s aims, critically evaluating the methods, and clearly flagging gaps with actionable, context-sensitive advice. A constructive review interprets results in light of authors’ constraints and day-to-day clinical practice and provides a concise, section-by-section appraisal that lists strengths, weaknesses, and prioritised, actionable suggestions. Crucially, reviewers should not recommend rejection solely because a manuscript lacks novelty or contains multiple fixable issues, as their role is formative, to guide correction by a more experienced specialist, not to eliminate work based on addressable shortcomings. For instance, nonspecialist reviewers can therefore miss substantive contextual strengths (i.e. pragmatic relevance or innovative use of limited data) while disproportionately flagging superficial issues. Conversely, they may fail to identify subtle methodological flaws that a specialist statistician or methods reviewer would catch, without ignoring the legitimacy, candidacy, and knowledge capacity of the person to review the manuscript.

Those same workload pressures make deep, bespoke appraisal difficult at scale and push journals towards heuristics, scope, novelty, and reputation that save time but risk excluding valuable, fixable work from under-resourced settings. For instance, in real-world clinical oncology, where publication decisions influence guideline development, funding priorities, and ultimately patient care, the consequences are tangible, particularly for LMIC researchers who, most of the time if not all, lack funded laboratory access, methodological support, and formal mentorship networks with international references for iterative improvement, though relying on perseverance rather than infrastructure based on retrospective, real-world datasets and depend on formative editorial feedback to learn publication standards. In such a case, when journals fail to provide even minimal, actionable guidance at the desk-decision or reviewer stage, they cut-off a vital route for capacity building, narrowing who can contribute to the scientific agenda and which problems receive attention.

Setting aside predatory or fraudulent services, which are an important separate concern, the growing number of online courses that teach authors how to target the ‘right’ journal does little to improve the underlying quality of research from low-resource settings. The problem is not journal choice but methodological and academic writing gaps, with a lack of specialist support. A skilled mentor helps prioritise fixable weaknesses, translate constrained clinical realities into transparent methods and limitations, and shape clearer narratives for reviewers and editors. Without this capacity building, advice on where to submit may speed the submission process but will not polish authors’ skills, nor materially increase the likelihood of acceptance. For these reasons, mentorship is a logical and necessary complement to any training with author perseverance to learn.

Mentorship escrow for promising submissions

Back in the literature, several initiatives have sought to improve manuscript evaluation by pairing less-experienced mentees with seasoned mentors to cultivate a pipeline of more capable editors and reviewers and to secure future editorial capacity.18, 19, 20, 21 The results, however, have been mixed, with randomised trials, meta-analyses, and prospective studies reporting limited or inconsistent gains, attributing the lack of clear improvement to factors such as participants’ high intrinsic motivation leading to self-directed learning, the intrinsic difficulty of teaching peer-review skills, and high baseline reviewer competence among trainees.22, 23, 24 By contrast, a separate evaluation found that a structured, 6-month mentored reviews, improved review quality as measured by the modified Review Quality Index and increased participants’ self-reported understanding of publication science, with strong mentor satisfaction.25

As a consequence, we argue that mentorship is most effective when it is focused, time-limited, and carefully targeted on the basis of combining negative and positive points concluded from previously listed randomised and prospective studies and a recent evaluation.26 Building on that insight, familiar elements of mentored review and editorial support can be repackaged into a compact, author-initiated, equity-centred remediation route we call the ‘mentorship escrow’. The escrow synthesises existing practices, mentored peer review, coreview training, targeted statistical checks, and transfer-with-advice pathways into a single, time-bounded intervention that centres the author’s revision process and produces a short mentor note to inform editorial adjudication. In other words, the idea sits between a moral case (fixable inequities) and an operational fix (pilots and mentors), and that middle ground often raises questions about feasibility, fairness, cost, and unintended consequences.

Dedicated to researchers from LMICs, the philosophy of the ‘mentorship escrow’, adapts the standard manuscript-handling framework—from technical check, including artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted verification when appropriate, to final editorial decision—while parametrising the decision process. In practice, eligible manuscripts are those initially selected by editors as ‘borderline’ or ‘revise with guidance’, defined as those showing promising insights with potentially fixable methodological or reporting deficiencies, routing them to a short, focused remediation pathway, converting borderline desk rejections into a single, tightly scoped advisory interaction that helps authors address fixable problems without diluting selection standards, with the purpose of protecting the review track, restoring opportunity for remediable manuscripts, and strengthening authors’ methodological and reporting skills while protecting editorial quality by keeping mentorship explicitly remedial and limited in scope and duration, by either creating an independent section within the submission system for authors from countries classified as LMIC by the World Bank or through a manual selection process during technical checks or initial editorial decision.

An experienced handling editor convenes a small, designated team of senior mentors and (trained) mentees to produce a single, actionable note that identifies discrete revision tasks. Authors are given a clearly defined, time-bounded window and submit the revised version with a point-by-point response; the handling editor then adjudicates by sending the revision for full peer review or issuing a constructive rejection template-based while explicitly noting that the escrow is remedial and does not guarantee acceptance (Figure 1).

However, several important points should be highlighted. Firstly, the judgements of a manuscript’s ‘promise’ are inherently variable and can be shaped by editors’, mentors’, and reviewers’ career incentives, research priorities, and personal interest in LMIC work. If the same borderline submission is seen by different editors, each may interpret its potential differently, which can skew how suitability is assessed. These on their own raise another disadvantage, from the fact that it’s time-consuming for stakeholders, and under conditions of high submission volume and minimal editorial staffing, this can introduce selection bias towards manuscripts that appear immediately ‘promising’ rather than those with remediable flaws, without talking about the expertise requirements that need to be fulfilled because accurate appraisal often depends on specialist methodological or statistical skills that are unevenly available.

Secondly, time and expertise constraints have been debated for decades, and when reviewers are overburdened, the proportion of substantive, correctly flagged methodological problems falls markedly.27 One pragmatic response is to establish a small cadre of dedicated, paid, or volunteer editorial mentors from senior scientists, retired academics, and/or experienced clinicians, who can commit a few hours per week to focused, remedial reviews, thereby protecting volunteer reviewers from unsustainable workloads, with a transparent conflict-of-interest disclosure, selection criteria, and ongoing monitoring aiming to ensure it supplements rather than substitutes for rigorous peer review, recreating, therefore, the gatekeeping the escrow principle seeks to remedy.

Thirdly, although anonymised, double-blinded editorials and reviewer reports may seem a reasonable approach to assure fairness and flexibility in editorial selection and peer-review aid, this practice raises another issue. In fact, double anonymisation is not a sound or legitimate method for the reasons that, firstly, it often fails in practice because author identity can be inferred from study context, geographic setting, or citation patterns; secondly, it risks obscuring accountability by removing transparency about reviewer expertise and potential conflicts of interest; and finally, it is inadvertently disadvantageous to researchers from LMICs, whose work may be dismissed without recognition of its local relevance or methodological constraints, creating, therefore, a false sense of neutrality while perpetuating structural inequities.

Fourthly, in real-world studies where sample size, in addition to the previously highlighted aspects, is paramount, ensuring adequate statistical power and population validity is critical to credible inference. AI, nowadays, offers powerful assistance, rapid, low-cost equalising tools that support study design conception, literature synthesis, data management, exploratory analyses, and manuscript drafting,28 although algorithmic assistance does not substitute for rigorous planning. Allowing such a tool to be used as front-line support constitutes both an advantage and a disadvantage. Although the advantage lies within guiding under-resourced authors towards more (partially)—acceptable work, improving adherence to reporting guidelines and reducing common statistical errors—thereby implicitly alleviating editorial and mentor workloads—the disadvantages are substantial when deployed by unqualified authors who lack, on the basis, methodological oversights and adequate AI knowledge-manipulation—to explain the purpose, adapt the algorithm, and polish the writing—given the fact that these models are inherently prone to hallucination and superficial pattern recognition,29,30 implying amplified inequities rather than reducing them. From editorial and reviewer perspectives, coupling AI-driven diagnostic tools with predefined templates tailored to the specific manuscript context can help move beyond perfunctory desk rejection—whether issued by editors before peer review or by reviewers during the process—and ensure quickness, contingent on being appropriately validated, context-sensitive, and embedded within transparent editorial workflows by addressing (i) methodological deficiencies, highlighting issues in study design, data extraction, quality control, definition of core objectives and endpoints, and statistical analysis; (ii) reporting deficiencies, focusing on manuscript structure, completeness of data reporting, adequacy of methodological details, and transparency throughout; and (iii) transfer pathways, in which AI can guide authors towards journals better aligned with the manuscript’s scope. Nevertheless, as several major publishers, such as Elsevier and Springer Nature, offered such a tool, the feature in itself failed to provide adequate manuscript matches optimally to journal scope and ultimately wasted authors’ time.

Fifthly, although the proposed framework primarily guides LMIC researchers in generating adequate real-world manuscript data and ensuring careers and skills are to be polished, the increasing volume and diversity of submissions inevitably complicate editorial decision-making, especially under conditions of limited editorial and reviewer capacity, and could let the authors rely on journal diagnostics rather than investing in themselves. Presubmission clinics, either offered by the publisher or as an independent private service, could serve as an optimal solution to achieve fairer decisions by diagnosing remediable deficiencies before manuscript formal submission while providing equitable support, thereby reducing time-consuming adjudication and improving efficiency. However, the central challenge lies in identifying the accurate vetting process that should be adapted, evaluation criteria, optimal paid service, and, importantly, who will provide the accurate diagnostic support, as qualified experts are limited primarily by time constraints to assist manuscripts at scale. Even if funding were allocated to recruit dedicated staff, such initiatives risk attracting fraudulent actors offering illegitimate feedback or superficial evaluations for financial gain. In other words, presubmission clinics may, in practice, duplicate the role already envisioned for mentorship escrow but with higher costs and less emphasis on building long-term capacity and experience. For this reason, a more sustainable an alternative would be to develop structured manuscript-development programmes tailored to overcome known resource barriers, beginning with a diagnostic phase to identify specific gaps and then providing targeted support using the resources authors already have, such as patient medical records, longitudinal follow-up data, and contextual variables, operating as independent, capacity-building initiatives, not only serving immediate academic needs by improving manuscript quality but also fostering career development while enhancing scientific rigour and health system relevance by generating deeper insights into the true care pathways, treatment responses, and toxicity profile.

Sixthly, equally important to consolidating upstream support is reviewer training programmes focused on equity, global health relevance, and unconscious bias mitigation, offered to those interested in overcoming real-world oncology inequities through a blended model combining a case-based curriculum—covering contextual appraisal of study design, common methodological pitfalls in resource-constrained settings, and bias mitigation—asynchronous e-modules, short synchronous master classes, regional in-person hubs, and supervised coreview opportunity pairings of early-career reviewers with experienced mentors. Crucially, the impact of training would be magnified when coupled with regional editorial boards and reviewer networks composed of investigators with lived LMIC experience to advise on scope and feasibility, nominate and mentor local reviewers, and ensure that triage decisions reflect local data realities, care pathways, and implementation constraints. In parallel, trained reviewers should be integrated into editorial workflows, preferentially assigned to manuscript-development programmes and mentorship escrow cases, and their feedback must iteratively feed back into continuous improvement of desk-rejection templates and validate AI diagnostic outputs.

The final point addresses manuscripts judged mismatched or borderline by the handling editor and/or reviewers following revisions. In such a case, with explicit author consent, the manuscript should enter a time-limited developmental-review phase, in which reviewers or trained mentors annotate priority fixes, indicate salvageability, and provide a concise development checklist. The full transfer package—original submission, anonymised reviews, editorial rationale, the development checklist, and metadata on revision scope and recommended target journals—can then be securely transferred to a curated network of cascade titles or partner journals. Transfers must preserve reviewer credit and accountability, operate under transparent conflict-of-interest policies, and maintain audit trails; outcomes including time to resubmission, acceptance rates, improvements in reporting quality, and equity metrics by region should be tracked to refine templates, reviewer training, and journal-matching algorithms. Additionally, AI may assist by prepopulating checklists and suggesting candidate journals but human validation is essential to avoid inappropriate transfers and to ensure developmental feedback remains context-sensitive and constructive.

Limitations

Before implementation, the proposed framework—mentorship escrow—faces practical real-world challenges stemming from the scarcity of qualified and experienced editorial staff and volunteer mentors. Those who are available risk rapid burnout if asked to absorb substantial, ongoing remedial work, and many journals, although willing in principle to revise handling strategies for LMIC authors, are unwilling or unable to fund supplemental mentorship, which threatens scalability. Additionally, even though the model proposes manuscript-development programmes to address remediable issues, a one-size-fits-all programme risks being inaccurate or ineffective, as LMICs exhibit heterogeneous limitations depending on local infrastructure, academic capacity, language and editorial support needs, baseline data availability, and regulatory context variations, so that only rigorous local diagnosis before programme rollout would secure accuracy of the framework while laying a foundation for durable and accurate capacity building. Additional risks include legal and ethical constraints on cross-border data sharing, the administrative burden of monitoring and collecting equity metrics where governance capacity is limited, and the potential for special remediation tracks to be perceived as stigmatising unless carefully framed as capacity building rather than charity. There is also a real danger of mission creep or dependency, in which external remediation substitutes for local capacity development and uncertainty about sustaining activities beyond initial pilot funding. These limitations justify a staged, diagnostic pilot approach with explicit mitigation measures.

The ultimate question remains, how can pilot success be expected to generalise without sustained funding, robust governance, and deliberate design for scalability?

The implementation

Based on the identified critical escrow points and discussed limitations, the implementation requires a modest, collaborative pilot, beginning with low-cost, high-impact steps that establish at initial, a clearer diagnostic desk decisions, developmental-review checklists, standardised rejection templates that point authors to concrete issues, and a transfer metadata form while building a manuscript-development programme with routine signposting to reporting and methodological resources before any scale-up. Parallel pilots should trial short, time-limited mentorship interventions for genuinely borderline submissions, using a small cadre of accredited mentors, either paid or honoraria-supported, drawn from senior scientists, retired academics, and experienced clinicians, with transparent selection and conflict-of-interest policies for mentors and reviewers; pair this with language-editing partnerships and regional mentor hubs to address writing and infrastructure gaps; an accreditation or vetting process for any manuscript-development providers; and mandatory audit sampling of mentor reports, all to protect quality and trust.

To be sustainable and trustworthy, the initiative requires being subsidised by major international societies (i.e. European Society for Medical Oncology, International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, and American Society of Clinical Oncology) with transparent conflict-of-interest policies, tangible incentives (recognition, Continuing Medical Education credits, and modest honoraria), and being subjected to routine performance audits with equity metrics including but not limited to regional concordance in triage, changes in desk-rejection rates, time to resubmission, progression to full review, acceptance rates, and downstream publication outcomes, in addition to a clear pilot timeline and success thresholds that determine scale-up. Finally, all operational details from funding sources to governance structures, accreditation criteria, conflict of interest, safeguards, and the pilot evaluation plan should be documented in an implementation appendix so stakeholders can assess feasibility and replicate successful elements responsibly while preserving editorial standards.

Conclusion

Moving beyond the one-standard-fits-all manuscript-handling process is critical, necessitating deliberate implementation, continuous surveillance, and committed adoption. Building on the foundations laid in this manuscript, the next phase is to translate these insights into a practical, forward-leaning blueprint—one that anticipates paradigm shifts in equitable publishing and readies the field for their rapid, evidence-based translation into practice. Because journals are constrained by capacity or policy, we nonetheless encourage them to adopt fairer, clearer, and more formative selection practices so LMIC researchers can learn from reviews, improve their work, and contribute effectively, supporting the wider circulation of locally relevant knowledge while endorsing and sustaining regional research ecosystems. To catalyse this change, we call on professional societies, publishers, and funders to convene and resource an initial pilot consortium to harmonise standards, develop a consensus evaluation, and produce shared templates, enabling fairer placement of locally relevant evidence while strengthening regional research capacity.
