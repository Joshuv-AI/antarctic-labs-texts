Rapid Eye Movement Sleep-Related Indicators with Promising Performance in Diagnosing Narcolepsy
Authors: Min Wu, Xinyan Zhang, Jiafeng Ren, Xiao Li, Shirley Xin Li, Junying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe dysregulation of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is an intrinsic nature of narcolepsy. This study aimed to evaluate the performances of REM sleep-related clinical features and the parallel objective indicators for distinguishing narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) and, more specifically, narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) from patients with hypersomnias.Patients and MethodsDrug-free patients with excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) were consecutively recruited between January 2020 and March 2023. All the patients were categorized into three groups, including NT1, NT2, and EDS controls. The REM sleep-related clinical features and REM sleep parameters were compared across these three groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were applied to quantify the diagnostic performances of REM sleep-related indicators in differentiating NT1 and NT2 from patients with hypersomnias, with bootstrap resampling to stabilize cut-offs and related metrics.ResultsA total of 206 patients were enrolled in this study (NT1 = 87, NT2 = 27, EDS controls = 92). In the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT), a daytime REM sleep duration of ≥ 11.91 (7.90–18.75) minutes for NT1 (AUC: 0.943 [0.908–0.974], sensitivity: 0.87 [0.76–0.95], specificity: 0.93 [0.84–1.00]) and ≥ 8.41 (3.00–12.50) minutes for NT2 (AUC: 0.922 [0.852–0.973], sensitivity: 0.90 [0.77–1.00], specificity: 0.88 [0.73–0.96]) yielded optimal differentiation from other hypersomnias. Stratified subgroup ROC analyses focusing on NT1 vs IH (AUC: 0.948 [0.897–0.986], cut-off: 12.67 [0.50–18.50] min, sensitivity: 0.85 [0.72–0.99], specificity: 0.97 [0.80–1.00]) and NT2 vs IH (AUC: 0.918 [0.819–0.986], cut-off: 7.18 [1.75–17.50] min, sensitivity: 0.89 [0.60–1.00], specificity: 0.86 [0.67–1.00]) further support the promising discriminatory potential of this marker. Additionally, ROC analyses showed high AUC values for any REM sleep without atonia (RSWA) index during MSLT in discriminating NT1 (AUC: 0.882 [0.826–0.930], cut-off value: 36.61% [3.71–71.81%], sensitivity: 0.91 [0.81–0.98], specificity: 0.80 [0.69–0.89]) and NT2 (AUC: 0.865 [0.796–0.924], cut-off value: 18.83% [7.41–43.85%], sensitivity: 0.99 [0.90–1.00], specificity: 0.75 [0.65–0.85]) from other hypersomnias. Comparable separation performance was also seen in separate NT1 vs IH (AUC: 0.885 [0.772–0.968], cut-off: 42.81% [3.71–82.58%], sensitivity: 0.86 [0.67–0.99], specificity: 0.86 [0.65–1.00]) and NT2 vs IH (AUC: 0.863 [0.701–0.974], cut-off: 20.09% [7.41–82.29%], sensitivity: 0.97 [0.71–1.00], specificity: 0.77 [0.54–1.00]) subgroup analyses. The co-occurrence of REM sleep-related symptoms of sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder exhibited a high specificity in diagnosing patients with NT1 (AUC: 0.711 [0.647–0.773], specificity: 0.87 [0.80–0.94]).ConclusionThe daytime REM sleep duration, any RSWA in MSLT, and REM sleep-related symptoms represent promising candidate biomarkers in distinguishing between narcolepsy subtypes and other hypersomnias, with particular utility for identifying NT2.

Introduction

Narcolepsy is a rare neurological sleep disorder characterized by symptoms of excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), disrupted nighttime sleep (DNS), and frequently reported abnormal rapid eye movement (REM) sleep dissociation (including cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and muscle tone instability during sleep).1–3 The average annual incidence of narcolepsy was 0.79 per 100,000 person-years from 1990 to 2017 in mainland China.4 Despite its low population incidence, this condition carries a heavy clinical and socioeconomic burden in China. Patients commonly suffer compromised school performance, limited daily functioning, and impaired social participation, which in turn lead to educational interruption, employment difficulties and constrained career development.5 Narcolepsy is classified into narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) and narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) in the International classification of sleep disorders, third edition, text revision (ICSD-3-TR).6 NT1 is defined when there exists a low level of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) hypocretin-1 (Hcrt-1) and/or cataplexy, while NT2 is diagnosed when Hcrt-1 levels are normal and cataplexy is absent.6 Nonetheless, the identification and diagnosis of narcolepsy are complicated by various challenges.7 For example, one major difficulty is the atypical presentation of cataplexy in some patients, such as a slight drooping of the eyelids, brief episodes of slurred speech, or transient facial muscle weakness, which can be mistaken for other conditions.3,8 Additionally, cataplexy may occasionally occur or present with ambiguous manifestations in some cases, leading to delayed diagnosis.3 Another significant challenge is the low compliance with the CSF Hcrt-1 test, which results in underutilization of this critical diagnostic measure and inaccurate diagnosis of narcolepsy, particularly NT1.9

Furthermore, differentiating NT2 from other hypersomnias poses significant challenges in clinical practice due to overlapping symptoms, especially the common EDS complaint.7 The current diagnostic criteria rely heavily on polysomnography (PSG) and the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT), which often fall short in effectively differentiating NT2 from idiopathic hypersomnia (IH).10 The two major indicators in the diagnosis criteria, sleep onset latency and the occurrence of REM sleep, can be inconsistent and non-specific, leading to potential misdiagnoses. Accordingly, there is an urgent need to improve diagnostic strategies for narcolepsy, ensuring more accurate and reliable identification of NT2.

Patients with narcolepsy exhibit dysregulation of REM sleep, manifesting as poor circadian timing of REM sleep and frequent transitions into REM sleep.11,12 For example, patients often unexpectedly enter REM sleep and fall asleep at any time of the day, bypassing the usual non-rapid eye movement (NREM) stages. Thus, REM sleep-related clinical features and REM sleep parameters might be the reliable markers in differentiating narcolepsy from other hypersomnias. Several studies have suggested that REM sleep without atonia (RSWA) during nocturnal PSG could be a potential diagnostic biomarker for narcolepsy.13–16 However, the nocturnal RSWA index has shown unstable sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing narcolepsy,14,17 and an earlier research only adopted this indicator to distinguish narcolepsy patients from healthy controls rather than other hypersomnias.16 Additionally, the role of RSWA during the daytime MSLT has been largely overlooked, despite its potential significance in identifying narcolepsy. Previous researchers also focused on the nocturnal sleep onset REM period (nSOREMP), but this biomarker had low sensitivity for NT1 diagnosis.18,19 Recently, a study reported that SOREMPs in daytime continuous PSG were reliable in identifying pediatric NT1 from patients with central disorders of hypersomnolence and individuals with subjective EDS.20 Likewise, the daytime REM sleep duration could be a good neurophysiological indicator in identifying hypocretin-deficient patients from other hypersomnias,8 but relevant validation studies in NT2 are lacking.

Therefore, in previous studies, RSWA captured in MSLT remained insufficiently characterized across narcolepsy subtypes, while current evidence on daytime REM sleep duration is largely restricted to hypocretin-deficient narcolepsy with no systematic validation in NT2 populations. Accordingly, the goals of this study were as follows: (1) to determine the differences in REM sleep-related clinical features and REM sleep parameters in both PSG and MSLT among patients with NT1, NT2, and EDS controls; (2) to explore potential diagnostic markers based on REM sleep-related indicators for distinguishing narcolepsy subtypes, particularly NT2, from other hypersomnias.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

Consecutive patients referred to the Sleep Medicine Center of West China Hospital with suspected hypersomnolence were prospectively selected between January 2020 and March 2023. The diagnostic procedures included a systematic interview and clinical evaluation, a 7-day sleep log to determine sleep-wake habits, PSG, and MSLT. Patients with narcolepsy were diagnosed by sleep and neurological specialists according to the ICSD-3 criteria,21 and then categorized into NT1 and NT2 subgroups. Other participants with subjective EDS (Epworth Sleepiness Scale score > 10) and/or objective EDS (mean SL ≤ 8 min) were classified as the EDS control group. Patients were excluded if their EDS was due to a specific neurological disease (eg, neurodegenerative diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, or central nervous system infections) (Figure 1). Ultimately, 206 consecutive patients with EDS (NT1, NT2, and EDS controls) were included in the present study. Measures

Sample Characteristics and Clinical Evaluation

Demographic characteristics of all participants were collected, including sex, age at onset of initial symptoms (EDS and/or cataplexy), age at diagnosis, age at recruitment, body mass index (BMI), EDS family history, occupation, and residence. Besides, each participant underwent a comprehensive face-to-face clinical evaluation by the clinician. Subjective nighttime sleep was assessed by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). PSQI scores range from 0 to 21, and a total score above 5 was considered as poor sleep quality.22 The ISI is a screening questionnaire for insomnia, with “moderate to severe insomnia” if > 14.23 The subjective EDS was determined using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)24 > 10, and a score > 14 was regarded as “moderate to severe EDS”.23 The Ullanlinna Narcolepsy Scale (UNS) was applied to assess the symptoms of the narcolepsy, with a maximum total score of 44, and higher scores indicate more severe symptoms.25 The presence of REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) symptom was evaluated using the RBD questionnaire-Hong Kong (RBDQ-HK), where a total score ≥ 18 suggested clinically meaningful RBD manifestations.26 Symptoms of anxiety and depression were measured with the Self-Rated Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rated Depression Scale (SDS), respectively. Standardized total scores ≥ 50 on the SAS27 and ≥ 53 on the SDS28 indicate clinically significant anxiety and depressive symptoms. The validated Chinese versions of the above-mentioned scales were utilized with appropriate copyright licenses, and established cut-off values from corresponding Chinese validation studies were adopted in the present study. Sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations were recorded by the researcher during the clinical interview. In addition, the comorbidities were also evaluated and recorded, eg, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, thyroid dysfunction, and psychosis.

PSG and MSLT

All patients underwent an overnight video-PSG and an MSLT in the following day. PSG and MSLT were performed at the standard sleep laboratory and manually scored by a qualified sleep technician, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines.29 The sleep recordings were performed with the signals from electroencephalography (F4-M1, C4-M1, O2-M1, F3-M2, C3-M2, O1-M2), bilateral electrooculography (ROC-M1, LOC-M2), electrocardiography, submentalis and bilateral anterior tibialis electromyography (EMG), nasal and oral airflow, thoracic and abdominal breathing, oxygen saturation, and body position. All participants did not take any REM sleep suppressing medications or sedating drugs for at least 14 days and maintained a nighttime sleep duration of more than 7 hours per day for at least 7 days, which was verified through standardized sleep diary records. The following PSG parameters were collected: total sleep time (TST), sleep latency (SL), REM sleep latency (RSL), sleep efficiency (SE), percentages of sleep stages (N1, N2, N3, and REM), number of REM sleep periods (REMPs), arousal index, wake after sleep onset (WASO), apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), mean oxygen saturation (Spo2), periodic limb movements index (PLMI), tonic RSWA index, phasic RSWA index, and any RSWA index.

The MSLT consisted of 5 fixed naps opportunities (9:00, 11:00, 13:00, 15:00, 17:00) separated by a 2-hour interval. For each nap trial, the participants were given 20 minutes to fall asleep. Each nap was terminated after 20 minutes if no sleep onset occurred; if sleep was detected, recording continued for an additional 15 minutes. The mean SL, mean RSL, REM sleep duration, SOREMPs, tonic RSWA index, phasic RSWA index, and any RSWA index were computed for the 5 naps. A daytime SOREMP was defined as a REM period appearing within the 20 minutes of a nap.

RSWA Quantification

All REM sleep periods captured during the PSG and MSLT were identified and reviewed in epochs by a sleep technician blinded to participants’ classification. RSWA was calculated in accordance with the AASM manual, version 2.6.29 To assess intra-rater reliability, the technician re-scored the first 20 sleep recordings to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was 0.923, indicating excellent intra-rater reliability for RSWA quantification. We scored REM sleep tonic activity if at least 50% of the stage REM sleep epoch had EMG amplitude at least 2 times greater than the REM sleep atonia level. In a 30-second epoch of REM sleep divided into 10 sequential 3-second mini-epochs, we identified epochs of REM sleep phasic activity if at least 5 (50%) of the mini-epochs contained bursts of transient muscle activity (lasting 0.1–5.0 seconds and at least 2 times greater than the REM sleep atonia level). We scored any EMG activity if the activity with a minimum amplitude 2 times greater than the REM sleep atonia level regardless of the duration of the activity. EMG activities were excluded when they occurred alongside arousal, obstructive or central respiratory events, bruxism, or gross body movements induced by the technician. The numerator referred to the total count of RSWA events (tonic/phasic/any) scored throughout PSG or MSLT recordings, with no time unit applied. Given that multiple separate RSWA events could emerge within a single minute of REM sleep, the resulting RSWA index value was permitted to surpass 100%.

Subjective and Objective REM Sleep Characteristics

The subjective REM sleep-related clinical features include cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD. The objective REM sleep parameters in PSG and MSLT include RSL, REM%, REMPs, tonic RSWA index, phasic RSWA index, any RSWA index, REM sleep time during MSLT, mean RSL, and SOREMPs.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software Version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were described as counts and percentages; continuous variables were expressed as either mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for skewed continuous variables. Comparisons of categorical variables were made by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were analyzed using t-tests or non-parametric equivalents (Mann–Whitney U-test). To compare the diagnostic performance of PSG and MSLT measures, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed. The highest Youden Index [(specificity + sensitivity) − 1] was used to define the optimal threshold. Areas under the curve (AUC) were computed to quantify diagnostic performance. Direct pairwise AUC comparisons using a bootstrap-based approach were performed to assess the incremental diagnostic values of candidate indicators by comparing the AUC of the base model (MSL and SOREMP count) with those of the extended models (base model + candidate indicator). To improve the stability of ROC thresholds and diagnostic estimates in small subgroups, internal validation was performed using 2000 bootstrap resamples with replacement for all subgroup ROC analyses. Adjustment of P values (ie, Pʹ values) using the false discovery rate (FDR) was performed. Statistical significance was set at Pʹ < 0.05.

In this study, all ROC analyses were categorized into primary and exploratory analyses. The primary analyses targeted three core discriminatory markers to distinguish narcolepsy subtypes from other hypersomnias: the any RSWA index in MSLT, REM sleep duration in MSLT, and combined REM sleep-related symptoms. All remaining ROC models were designated as exploratory analyses, encompassing PSG-derived tonic, phasic, and any RSWA indices, MSLT-derived tonic and phasic RSWA indices, MSL, MRSL, and SOREMPs.

Variables in PSG and MSLT

Comparisons of objective sleep variables across patients with NT1, NT2, and EDS controls are shown in Table 2. Patients with NT1 had disrupted nocturnal sleep architecture with shorter SL and RSL, more REMPs, and higher PLMI than those in the EDS control group. When compared with NT2, NT1 had lower SE, a higher arousal index, and greater WASO. Conversely, the NT2 group showed sleep architecture similar to that of the EDS control group. Analysis of ROC Curves

ROC analyses for the diagnoses of NT1 and NT2 were performed using REM sleep parameters that differed significantly between groups. As shown by the ROC curves in Figure 3, the any RSWA index in MSLT with the cut-offs of 36.61% (3.71–71.81%) and 18.83% (7.41–43.85%) effectively discriminated the NT1 group (AUC: 0.882 [0.826–0.930], sensitivity: 0.91 [0.81–0.98], specificity: 0.80 [0.69–0.89], Pʹ < 0.001) and the NT2 group (AUC: 0.865 [0.796–0.924], sensitivity: 0.99 [0.90–1.00], specificity: 0.75 [0.65–0.85], Pʹ < 0.001) from the EDS control group. We further conducted separate ROC analyses comparing NT1 vs IH and NT2 vs IH. For discrimination between NT1 and IH, any RSWA index in MSLT yielded significant diagnostic performance (cut-off: 42.81% [3.71–82.58%], AUC: 0.885 [0.772–0.968], sensitivity: 0.86 [0.67–0.99], specificity: 0.86 [0.65–1.00], Pʹ < 0.001). For differentiating NT2 from IH, the any RSWA index in MSLT also showed favorable diagnostic performance (cut-off: 20.09% [7.41–82.29%], AUC: 0.863 [0.701–0.974], sensitivity: 0.97 [0.71–1.00], specificity: 0.77 [0.54–1.00], P = 0.003) (Figure 4). Other RSWA indices (ie, tonic, phasic, and any RSWA) in both PSG and MSLT yielded favorable specificity but relatively low sensitivity in differentiating NT1 from other hypersomnias, as illustrated in Appendix Figure A1. Furthermore, the RSWA indices in PSG offered satisfactory diagnostic performance to separate patients with NT2 from EDS controls (Appendix Figure A2). Unfortunately, all RSWA indices showed limited diagnostic efficacy in the diagnosis of narcolepsy subtypes (NT1/NT2), as presented in Appendix Figure A3. REM sleep time in MSLT, with cut-offs of 11.91 (7.90–18.75) min and 8.41 (3.00–12.50) min, also performed well in discriminating patients with NT1 (AUC: 0.943 [0.908–0.974], sensitivity: 0.87 [0.76–0.95], specificity: 0.93 [0.84–1.00], Pʹ < 0.001) and NT2 (AUC: 0.922 [0.852–0.973], sensitivity: 0.90 [0.77–1.00], specificity: 0.88 [0.73–0.96], Pʹ < 0.001) from the EDS controls (Figure 5). Additional ROC comparisons of NT1 vs IH and NT2 vs IH revealed that REM sleep duration in MSLT exerted significant diagnostic value for differentiating both pairs, with a cut-off of 12.67 (0.50–18.50) min (AUC: 0.948 [0.897–0.986], sensitivity: 0.85 [0.72–0.99], specificity: 0.97 [0.80–1.00], Pʹ < 0.001) for NT1 vs IH and a cut-off of 7.18 (1.75–17.50) min (AUC: 0.918 [0.819–0.986], sensitivity: 0.89 [0.60–1.00], specificity: 0.86 [0.67–1.00], Pʹ < 0.001) for NT2 vs IH (Figure 6). However, direct AUC comparisons (base- vs extended-model comparison) showed that any RSWA index in MSLT and the daytime REM sleep duration did not provide significant incremental diagnostic value beyond standard MSLT parameters (MSL and SOREMP count). Other REM sleep parameters (ie, MSL and SOREMPs) showed relatively low values in distinguishing NT1 from NT2 due to the inconsistent sensitivity and specificity (Appendix Figure A4). Discussion

Our study assessed the diagnostic performance of subjective REM sleep-related clinical features and objective REM sleep parameters for discriminating NT1 and, in particular, NT2 from a clinical EDS cohort. We found that the daytime REM sleep duration was a promising indicator for distinguishing narcolepsy from other hypersomnias, with balanced sensitivity and specificity at cut-offs of ≥ 11.91 min for NT1 and ≥ 8.41 min for NT2. Furthermore, the any RSWA during MSLT could serve as a potential marker for patients with NT1 and NT2. Additionally, subjective REM sleep-related clinical features (the co-occurrence of sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD) may provide auxiliary diagnostic clues for discriminating NT1 from other patients with hypersomnias.

There are various reasons for EDS, including hypersomnias of central origin (such as narcolepsy, IH, and Kleine-Levin syndrome), OSA, insufficient sleep, depression, and comorbid medical and psychiatric disorders.30 The updated diagnostic criteria in ICSD-3-TR still determine narcolepsy subtypes (NT1 vs NT2) based on CSF Hcrt-1 levels and/or the presence of cataplexy.6 However, it would be hard to discriminate the narcolepsy type when the CSF Hcrt-1 test is unavailable or the symptom of cataplexy is atypical. Our study found that patients with NT1 exhibited the most severe clinical symptoms compared to the other two groups. These results are consistent with the existing evidence showing that patients with NT2 have generally less severe symptoms than those with NT1.12 These findings suggest that clinical symptoms might serve as diagnostic references for NT1 with atypical cataplectic attacks. In terms of the diagnosis of NT2, PSG and MSLT are essential tests,31,32 and the number of SOREMPs is the key point to identify NT2 (≥ 2 SOREMPs) from IH (< 2 SOREMPs).6 However, there are some conditions leading to certain ambiguous results. For instance, the REM sleep rebound effect could potentially increase false positive diagnoses of NT2, particularly among individuals with shift work and sleep restriction.6 In addition, daytime SOREMPs are not specific for narcolepsy diagnosis,19 on the basis of evidence that 16–48% of normal adolescents also exhibit 1–2 SOREMPs.33 In clinical practice, it is difficult to distinguish patients with NT2 from those with other hypersomnias due to similar clinical manifestations. Consistently, our results showed no discernible differences in the severity of clinical symptoms between patients with NT2 and EDS control groups. Thus, more reliable subjective and objective indicators are urgently needed for detecting narcolepsy from other hypersomnias.

REM sleep is normally characterized by muscle atonia, rapid eye movements, dreaming, and activated electroencephalogram.34 In narcolepsy, NT1 is characterized by significantly reduced or undetectable CSF Hcrt-1 levels due to the irreversible and severe loss of hypocretin neurons.12,35 Hypocretin neurons innervate some specific brain regions involved in suppressing REM sleep,36 and their deficiency could result in the full intrusions of REM sleep (ie, SOREMPs) during wakefulness in patients with NT1.37 Accordingly, a recent study reported a novel finding that mean REM sleep duration during MSLT can serve as a biomarker for hypocretin-deficient narcolepsy in clinical settings.8 Consistently, in the current study, we also found that daytime REM sleep duration could effectively identify both NT1 and NT2 from EDS patients, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 85%. However, we have to note that the cut-off for NT1 identified in the present study is higher than that reported in a previous study (11.91 min vs 4.1 min).8 We suppose that such inconsistency in cut-off values might stem from multiple confounding factors including discrepant REM scoring criteria, different medication washout standards, divergent age distributions of enrolled patients, and disparate diagnostic approaches of NT1. Thus, a well-designed multi-center study with a more precise stratification of the sample cohort is expected to verify the diagnostic value of daytime REM sleep duration in hypersomnias. More importantly, we demonstrated that REM sleep time in MSLT can effectively identify NT2 from EDS controls and further from IH, even though NT2 exhibited a greater degree of heterogeneity with a diverse clinical phenotype.38,39 Some researchers propose that NT2 may be caused by a partial loss of the hypocretin neurons accompanied by relatively normal levels of CSF Hcrt-1,12 but resulting in abnormal REM sleep physiology.14 Given the absence of CSF Hcrt-1 testing in our cohort, we could not rule out patients with potential hypocretin deficiency among NT2 cases defined by the absence of cataplexy, which may partially account for the altered REM sleep features observed in this subgroup. However, the neuropathophysiology mechanism underlying abnormal REM sleep regulation in NT2 remains unclear. Notably, REM sleep duration in MSLT is readily accessible as a worthwhile diagnostic indicator for narcolepsy. Thus, it is advisable to regard the daytime REM sleep duration as a candidate marker pending prospective multi-center validation before its inclusion into routine diagnostic procedures in future clinical practice.

RSWA is a neurophysiological hallmark of typical RBD and serves as the mandatory criteria for confirming an RBD diagnosis.6,34 According to the AASM manual, the types of RSWA include the presence of excessive sustained muscle activity (tonic activity), excessive transient muscle activity (phasic activity), and any EMG activity in REM sleep.29 Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that patients with narcolepsy have a higher proportion of RSWA than those in controls.14,15,40,41 These findings primarily focused on nocturnal tonic and phasic RSWA, but indicated that the sensitivity and specificity values of RSWA were unstable. In regard to the pediatric narcolepsy diagnosis, a previous study reported that a threshold of ≥ 1% nocturnal RSWA index (including tonic and phasic activity) yielded a sensitivity of 88.2% and specificity of 60.9%, while a cut-off of ≥ 8% yielded a specificity of 95.7% and sensitivity of 52.9%.14 In the present study, we also demonstrated that nocturnal RSWA indices (tonic, phasic, and any) are specific yet lack sufficient sensitivity as diagnostic biomarkers for narcolepsy. Remarkably, we found that daytime any RSWA index represents a candidate biomarker for diagnosing narcolepsy (NT1/NT2) with promising sensitivity and specificity relative to other PSG- and MSLT-derived RSWA indices, though prospective multi-center validation is still needed. Any RSWA is defined as an activity with a minimum amplitude that is two times greater than the atonia level in REM (or the lowest amplitude in NREM), regardless of its duration.29 Hence, the any RSWA reflects the comprehensive EMG activity rather than solely tonic and phasic activities. Moreover, RSWA is better quantified during MSLT recordings than during overnight PSG. Therefore, any RSWA index from MSLT deserves greater attention, which may improve the diagnostic accuracy for narcolepsy. It is well known that the RSWA in RBD is explained by the dysfunction in the brainstem circuits responsible for maintaining muscle atonia and suppressing behaviors during REM sleep.42 Nevertheless, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the two medical conditions (isolated RBD vs RSWA/RBD in narcolepsy) are completely different. It is still a hotly debated issue whether RSWA/RBD is an intrinsic feature or an affiliated symptom in narcolepsy.43

However, it should be noted that direct AUC comparisons revealed that neither daytime REM sleep duration nor any RSWA index in MSLT yielded significant incremental diagnostic value beyond standard MSLT parameters. This non-significant finding is methodologically predictable. Since patient classification relied primarily on MSL and SOREMP count, these conventional metrics already contained core discriminative information for narcolepsy subtyping. Neither candidate indicator supplied independent predictive information beyond these grouping criteria; thus, neither metric improved diagnostic performance in our cohort. Collectively, these data suggest that daytime REM sleep duration and any RSWA index in MSLT offer no extra diagnostic value when patients are stratified using routine MSLT thresholds. Nonetheless, both metrics may still aid clinical evaluation for patients with suspected hypersomnia presenting ambiguous or inconclusive MSLT findings.

With regard to these core symptoms of narcolepsy (eg, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD), such manifestations are known as REM sleep-related features. To date, the diagnostic value of these REM sleep-related symptoms in narcolepsy has not been fully elucidated, with the exception of cataplexy.44 Noteworthily, our findings indicated a high specificity in diagnosing NT1 by the combination of sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD. Previous studies have also shown that the prevalence and/or frequency of sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD are higher in NT1 than in those with NT2 and other hypersomnias.40,44,45 Hypocretin deficiency is responsible for the symptoms of sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations besides cataplexy in NT1.46 Further evidence is still needed to gain deeper insights into the neural mechanisms underlying the loss of hypocretin signaling causing abnormal REM sleep-related symptoms in narcolepsy. In summary, the above findings highlight the potential diagnostic value in identifying narcolepsy from other hypersomnias by combining the subjective REM sleep-related clinical features with the objective REM sleep parameters (daytime REM sleep duration and any RSWA in MSLT).

In the current study, patients with NT1 had more severe DNS (manifesting as lower SE, a higher proportion of light sleep, more frequent arousals, and longer WASO41) when compared with those in the NT2 group. The result is consistent with a previous report stating that up to 95% of patients with NT1 experience DNS.3 DNS could impair sleep quality47 and may also exert negative impacts on quality of life, mood, and metabolic and cardiovascular functions.48 Nevertheless, in this study, patients with NT1 and EDS controls had comparable degrees of sleep fragmentation, which might be ascribed to the fact that approximately one third of EDS controls consisted of patients with OSA. A general explanation for DNS in NT1 is based on the theory that the loss of the hypocretin neurons could result in nocturnal spontaneous awakenings.17 However, DNS in narcolepsy has not received the same level of attention as EDS, and its underlying mechanisms and clinical management strategies remain poorly characterized.49

Our study has several strengths. First, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of subjective and objective REM sleep-related indicators to improve the differential diagnosis of narcolepsy from other hypersomnias. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated some reliable markers derived from MSLT and clinical REM-related features, which are available through routine sleep assessments. Second, this research revealed that daytime REM sleep duration and any RSWA yield favorable sensitivity and specificity for differentiating NT2 from patients with similar EDS symptoms. The present study also has several limitations. First, this study lacks a healthy control group. However, this drawback does not significantly impact our findings, since we mainly aimed to distinguish NT1 and NT2 from other hypersomnias. Second, the sample size of the NT2 group was relatively small. It is well-established that narcolepsy is a rare disease, and NT2 represents 15–25% of the clinical narcoleptic population.21 A multi-center cohort with a larger sample size is needed to verify our results. Last, the absence of CSF Hcrt-1 measurement constitutes a major limitation of our study, owing to poor patient acceptance of this invasive procedure.50 Given that much of our mechanistic interpretation centers on hypocretin-related pathophysiology, the lack of direct CSF Hcrt-1 measurements weakens the validity of our inferences regarding endogenous hypocretin levels across participants. In addition, all candidate markers were extracted from the MSLT used for diagnostic grouping, which may introduce incorporation bias and overestimate diagnostic performance. Moreover, classification based on the presence of cataplexy might mislabel individuals with atypical cataplexy as NT2. Nevertheless, none of the patients with NT2 progressed to NT1 throughout the 4-year follow-up period. All participants attended clinical visits every three months for routine evaluation, and they underwent repeated PSG and MSLT annually.

Conclusion

Our study evaluated the diagnostic value of subjective REM sleep-related clinical features and objective REM sleep parameters for discriminating patients with narcolepsy, particularly NT2, from those with other hypersomnias. We propose the daytime REM sleep duration and any RSWA index from MSLT as promising markers for narcolepsy diagnosis, particularly for NT2. In addition, the co-occurrence of sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and RBD may act as a supportive clue to screen patients with NT1. These findings provide new insights into clinical practice to improve diagnostic accuracy within the scope of hypersomnolence.
