Abstract
. In [ 7 ] , we defined several classes of degrees that are high in senses related to computable structure theory. Each class of degrees is characterized by a structural feature (e.g., an isomorphism) that it can compute if such a feature exists. In this paper, we examine each of these classes and characterize them based on what they do if no such object exists. We describe, in particular, reticent, loquacious, and collegiate senses of being high. These, respectively, reflect the case where a computation from the degree can give output only if the desired feature exists, the case where it will give output of some kind whether or not the feature exists, and the case where the degree will either compute the feature or the best available approximation to it.



1.
Introduction

If we tell you a joke, our intended outcome is that you laugh. If we are successful, that is exactly what will happen. On the other hand, there are many possible outcomes if we are unsuccessful in telling a joke. You could groan, have no reaction, or even become angry.

In

In giving talks about

1.2.
Plan of the Paper

For various notions of structural highness, we consider three variants. In Section
2
, we describe degrees which are high in a
reticent
sense, including a review of some material from
[
7
]
, but adding a new result on when reticent highness for isomorphism for a class implies reticent highness for isomorphism in general.

In Section
3
, we consider a new variant, the degrees which are
loquaciously
high — that is, the highness is witnessed by a total functional in a way we will make precise. This section leaves open two problems that we consider very interesting.

Section
4
describes a form of graceful degradation in the form of degrees which are
collegiately
high. These degrees, if unable to compute what we had hoped they might, will compute some approximation to it. The degrees are graded by the quality of the approximation. We find that, globally, the hierarchy of notions collapses, but that at least on Abelian
p
-groups the hierarchy is proper.

This leaves the reticent and loquacious variants for global nontrivial comparison. In Section
5
, we describe implications (or lack thereof) between these notions.

In the brief Section
6
, we show that all notions presented in the paper collapse for nonlowness for isomorphism.

Finally, Section
7
collects additional results on structural highness notions that many colleagues have asked about but that do not seem likely to be published elsewhere in the short term.

Degrees that will never speak unless they have something to say

Definition 2.1
.

Let

3.
Degrees that always have something to say

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{d}
be a Turing degree that is uniformly high for a given notion
\mathcal{N}
as in the previous section. We say that
\mathbf{d}
is
loquaciously high for
\mathcal{N}
if
F
can be chosen such that
F(i)
is total for all
i\in\omega
.

For instance, we say that a degree
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for paths if there is a total
\mathbf{d}
-computable function
F
such that if the computable tree
T_{n}
is ill founded, then
F(n)
gives a path, and if
T_{n}
is well founded, then
F(n)
gives some element of Baire space (not necessarily with any relation to
T_{n}
).

Similarly,
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for isomorphism if there is a total
\mathbf{d}
-computable function
F
such that if
\mathcal{M}_{i},\mathcal{M}_{j}
are isomorphic computable structures, then
F(i,j)
gives an isomorphism between them, and if
\mathcal{M}_{i},\mathcal{M}_{j}
are not isomorphic, then
F(i,j)
is at least total.

Note that every PA degree is loquaciously PA. Indeed, we could computably prepare a computably given tree on
2^{<\omega}
so that the original tree is unchanged if infinite and changed to an infinite tree otherwise.

Proposition 3.2
.

A degree
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for paths if and only if it is loqaciously high for isomorphism.

Proof.

This proof follows that of Proposition 2.6 in
[
7
]
. We include it here for completeness and make careful note of the aspects of the proof that allow us to maintain loquacity.

Suppose that
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for paths. Given two isomorphic computable structures
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, we define a tree
I_{i,j}
as follows. A vertex of the tree consists of a pair
(\sigma,\tau)
such that
\sigma,\tau
are partial isomorphisms
\sigma:\mathcal{M}_{i}\to\mathcal{M}_{j}
and
\tau:\mathcal{M}_{j}\to\mathcal{M}_{i}
whose domains are finite initial segments of the universes of the respective structures (and of the same length), and
\sigma
and
\tau
are inverses where both are defined. Vertices are ordered by extension. Note that a path through
I_{i,j}
represents a pair consisting of an isomorphism and its inverse and that this tree is uniformly computable in the two structures. Now
\mathbf{d}
, via a total functional, computes a path through
I_{i,j}
, which gives an isomorphism and its inverse.

Let
\mathbf{d}
be loquaciously high for isomorphism, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a nonempty
\Pi^{0}_{1}
class of functions generated by a computable tree
T
.

Our proof is based on rank-saturated trees of infinite rank
[
6
,
8
]
; we will require the following facts:

(1)

There is a computable rank-saturated tree
S
of infinite rank that has a computable path. (This follows from the proof of Lemma 1 in
[
8
]
, beginning with a Harrison order with a computable descending sequence.)

(2)

Any two computable rank-saturated trees of infinite rank are isomorphic. (Proposition 2,
[
8
]
)

(3)

If
S
is a computable rank-saturated tree of infinite rank and
T
is any computable tree with a path, then
T\star S
is a computable rank-saturated tree of infinite rank. (Proposition 1,
[
8
]
)

Then
S
and
T\star S
are our two computable structures in some appropriate language. Let
f
be a computable path through
S
. Since
\mathbf{d}
can, via a total functional, compute an isomorphism between

and

, we have that
\mathbf{d}
can carry

over to a path in

and then project that down to a path in

Note that if

is well founded, then

will be as well, so there will be no isomorphism between

and

. In this case, our total

-computable function will give us a total function that is not an isomorphism, which we apply to the computable path through

, obtaining a function which is not a path of
T\star S
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for isomorphism.

(1)

There are
\mathbf{d}
-computable functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
such that if
\mathcal{M}_{i}\cong\mathcal{M}_{j}
are computable structures, then
\Phi(i,j):\mathcal{M}_{i}\cong\mathcal{M}_{j}
and
\Psi(i,j)=\Phi(i,j)^{-1}
.

(2)

There is a
\mathbf{d}
-computable function
\Theta
witnessing loquaciously high for isomorphism and such that for any computable
\mathcal{M}_{i}\cong\mathcal{M}_{j}
, this same
\Theta
gives the inverse as
\Theta^{-1}(j,i)=\Theta(i,j)
.

We note that this statement is not as trivial as it might first appear. As

is loquaciously high for isomorphism, we have a total
\mathbf{d}
-computable function
\Phi
giving isomorphisms, and the naive approach is to define

is producing garbage which may in particular fail to be a bijection. In that case, attempting to compute an inverse may result in something partial, which we cannot have.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for isomorphism. It follows that
\mathbf{d}
is also loquaciously high for paths. Let
\mathcal{M}_{i},\mathcal{M}_{j}
be computable structures. We construct the tree
I_{i,j}
as in the previous proof.

From
\mathbf{d}
, we can, uniformly in
i
and
j
, compute a path in
T_{i,j}
, and the two components of this path give the necessary isomorphisms. This establishes the first part.

Toward the second part, we let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be as in Part 1. Now we define
\Theta(i,j)=\Phi(i,j)
if
i<j
,
\Theta(i,j)=\Psi(i,j)
if

, and set

to be the identity if

.
∎

Indeed, the degrees loquaciously high for paths are quite powerful. We recall that there are degrees which are high for paths without further restriction which have

for their third jump. We have seen in Theorem

that the degrees reticently high for paths are exactly the ones which enumerate all

Proposition 3.4
.

If

is loquaciously high for paths, then
\mathbf{d}
enumerates all

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{d}
is loquaciously high for paths. It suffices that

can enumerate the indices of well-founded computable trees.

Let
\Phi

is loquaciously high for paths. Now, when

is the index of a computable tree

If such an

Proposition 3.5
.

There is a degree that enumerates
\mathcal{O}
but is not loquaciously high for paths.

Proof.

We carry out a forcing in which a condition is a finite
\sigma:\omega\times\omega\to\{0,1\}

At this point, it may well be asked whether the degrees loquaciously high for paths (equivalently, isomorphism) are

The following Lemma is closely related to Lemma 4.46 in

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{L}_{n}:n\in\omega)
be an standard listing of the computable linear orders. Let

be a function such that if
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is ill founded, then

is loquaciously high for descending sequences.

Proof.

Given
n

to attempt to construct a descending sequence. Define the sequence
(a_{i}:i\in\omega)
as follows:

•

Consider the first
f^{[n]}(0)

\mathcal{L}_{n}
to be enumerated, and let
a_{0}
be the rightmost of these.

•

Given
a_{i}
, consider the first

elements of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
to be enumerated, and let
a_{i+1}
be the rightmost of these which is strictly to the left of

.

Observe that this is uniform. During this construction, we may reach a point where we are unable to continue because there is no element left of

within the first

is well founded, so we simply switch to outputting 0, and loquaciousness is maintained.

Now suppose

is ill founded and

is the descending sequence majorized by

. Then, by induction, we have

. Thus,

will always be an element among the first

\mathcal{L}_{n}
and strictly to the left of

, so the construction can continue.

Theorem 3.9

There is a degree
\mathbf{d}

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\equiv_{T}\mathcal{O}

By Lemmas

Let

\mathcal{L}_{n}

majorizes a descending sequence. Note that

can compute such an

. We build a

majorizing

using the partial order of Hechler forcing. We start with the condition
(\langle\rangle,f)

Given a condition
(\sigma,h)
and an

\hat{h}
extending
\sigma
such that there is no

\sigma

with
\Phi_{e}^{\tau}(e)\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\downarrow

can answer it. If there is such an

can compute an element of it. We then make our next condition

We offer some existence results concerning reticence and loquaciousness.

Proposition 5.1

There exist degrees of the following kinds:

(1)

Both reticently high for paths and loquaciously high for paths.

(2)

Reticently high for paths but not loquaciously high for paths.

n\in\mathcal{O}\setminus X
.

The first case is that there is some
n\in X\setminus\mathcal{O}
. Then fix a witnessing
\tau
, and the condition
(\tau,\alpha,\overline{S})
ensures that
n\in W^{p}
and thus
W^{p}\neq\mathcal{O}
.

The second case is that there is some
n\in\mathcal{O}\setminus X
. Fix this
n
. Consider

\{\beta:\beta\geq\alpha\wedge\exists\tau\,[\text{$\tau$ weakly extends $(\sigma,\beta,\overline{S})$ and $n\in W^{\tau}$}]\}.

This is a
\Sigma^{1}_{1}(\overline{S})
set of ordinals. It cannot contain any pseudo-ordinals, because then the witnessing
\tau
would be a strong extension, contradicting
n\not\in X
. Since
\overline{S}
is low for
\omega_{1}^{ck}
, we may apply
\Sigma^{1}_{1}
-bounding to get some computable ordinal
\gamma
such that there is no
\tau
weakly extending
(\sigma,\gamma,\overline{S})
with
n\in W^{\tau}
. Then the condition
(\sigma,\gamma,\overline{S})
ensures that
n\not\in W^{p}
, and thus
W^{p}\neq\mathcal{O}
.

We have shown that there is a dense set of conditions ensuring
W^{p}\neq\mathcal{O}
. It follows that for a sufficiently generic filter,
\mathcal{O}\not\leq_{e}p
.
∎

Claim 5.4
.

\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}\not\leq_{e}p
.

Proof.

For
\overline{R}
a sequence of trees, we will also say
\overline{R}

extends

(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})
if
(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{R})
extends
(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})
, though we do not require that
\overline{R}
is low for
\omega_{1}^{ck}
. Note that the class of such extensions is arithmetical in
\overline{S}
.

Suppose
(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})
is a condition and
W
is an enumeration operator.
Consider

Y=\{n:(\forall\overline{R}\text{ extending $(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})$})\,\exists\tau\,[\text{$\tau$ weakly extends $(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{R})$ and $n\in W^{\tau}$}]\}.

Note that
\alpha
is a fixed computable ordinal, so weak extension is
\Delta^{1}_{1}
. Thus
Y
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}(\overline{S})
. As
\overline{S}
is low for
\omega_{1}^{ck}
, we have
Y\neq\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}
. So there is some
n\in Y\cap\mathcal{O}
or
n\not\in Y\cup\mathcal{O}
.

In the first case, fix
n
and define
\overline{R}
as follows:

•

If
S_{e}\neq T_{e}
or
T_{e}
is well-founded,
R_{e}=S_{e}
;

•

Otherwise, let
R_{e}
consist of all extendable nodes of
T_{e}
.

Note that if
T_{e}
is ill founded, then
R_{e}\subseteq T_{e}
contains no leaves, and so every string on
R_{e}
is extendable to a path through
T_{e}
. Now fix a witnessing
\tau
for this
\overline{R}
. Since every string on
R_{e}
is extendable to a path through
T_{e}
, it follows that
(\tau,\alpha,\overline{S})
is a condition, and so it is a condition extending
(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})
. Further, it ensures
n\in W^{p}
, and thus
W^{P}\neq\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

In the second case, fix
n
. We know that

\{\overline{R}:\text{$\overline{R}$ extends $(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{S})$}\wedge\neg\exists\tau\,[\text{$\tau$ weakly extends $(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{R})$ and $n\in W^{\tau}$}]\}

is nonempty, and we note that this class is hyperarithmetical in
\overline{S}
. By the Gandy Basis Theorem, there is an element
\overline{R}
which is low for
\omega_{1}^{ck}
. So
(\sigma,\alpha,\overline{R})
is a condition ensuring that
n\not\in W^{p}
, and thus
W^{p}\neq\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

We have shown that there is a dense set of conditions ensuring
W^{p}\neq\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}
. It follows that for a sufficiently generic filter,
\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}\not\leq_{e}p
.
∎

Thus there is a
p
such that
\mathcal{O}\not\leq_{e}p
,
\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}\not\leq_{e}p
, and for every ill-founded tree
T_{e}
, the function
p^{[e]}
gives a path through
T_{e}
.

By a strengthening of Selman’s Theorem (see e.g., Lemma IV.11 of
[
12
]
), there is a Turing degree
\mathbf{d}
which enumerates (the graph of)
p
, but
\mathcal{O},\omega\setminus\mathcal{O}\not\in\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathbf{d})
. From the enumeration of
p
, the degree
\mathbf{d}
must be uniformly high for paths. From Theorem
2.2
, the degree
\mathbf{d}
is not reticently high for paths. From Proposition
3.4
, the degree
\mathbf{d}
is not loquaciously high for paths. Thus,
\mathbf{d}
is our desired degree.
∎

Thus, we also have that there is a degree that is uniformly high for isomorphism that is neither loquaciously nor reticently high for isomorphism.

6.
Failure modes for non-lowness for isomorphism

There is another related notion of structural separation of a degree from
\mathbf{0}
. We say that a degree is
low for isomorphism
if, whenever it can compute an isomorphism between two computable structures, there is already a computable isomorphism between them
[
9
]
. Non-lowness for isomorphism is in this sense not precisely a “highness” notion, but still identifies a structural class of degrees that are, in a certain sense, separated from
\mathbf{0}
.

We note that the major concepts of this paper trivialize for these degrees.

Proposition 6.1
.

Every degree that is non-low for isomorphism is uniformly, reticently, and loquaciously non-low for isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{d}
be non-low for isomorphism. We pick one pair of structures
\mathcal{A}_{i}\cong\mathcal{A}_{j}
which are not computably isomorphic such that
\mathbf{d}
computes an isomorphism
f
between them. Now, given a pair of indices and an oracle for
\mathbf{d}
, we could do any of the following:

(1)

If the pair of indices given is
(i,j)
, compute
f
.

(2)

If the pair of indices given is
(i,j)
, compute
f
, otherwise do nothing.

(3)

If the pair of indices given is
(i,j)
, compute
f
, otherwise compute the constant
0
function.

The first of these procedures demonstrates uniform non-lowness for isomorphism, the second demonstrates reticent non-lowness for isomorphism, and the third demonstrates loquacious non-lowness for isomorphism.
∎

Lemma 7.1
.

If
\mathbf{d}
is high for tight descending sequences, then
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
computes

Proof.

Let

\mathbf{d}

. So
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}

and thus computes

∎

Proposition 7.2
.

There is a degree that is high for isomorphism but not high for tight descending sequences.

Proof.
