Endovascular treatment of an intraosseous dural arteriovenous fistula of the greater wing of the sphenoid: A case report and literature review
Authors: Jinlu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraosseous dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF) of the greater wing of the sphenoid is extremely rare, and its endovascular treatment faces challenges due to numerous transosseous feeders and inaccessible dural sinuses. A successful case of transarterial embolization (TAE) is reported. A 63-year-old female presented with headache but no positive neurological signs. Computed tomography and computed tomography angiography revealed left temporal lobe edema and dilated intracranial veins. Digital subtraction angiography confirmed a left intraosseous DAVF of the greater wing of the sphenoid (Cognard type IV), supplied by the dural branch of the right internal carotid artery, the lateral clival artery of the left internal carotid artery, the recurrent meningeal branch of the left ophthalmic artery, the left middle meningeal artery, and transosseous branches from the left internal maxillary artery. Venous drainage occurred via the middle cerebral vein into the veins of Trolard and Labbé and contralateral veins. The DAVF was obliterated through the internal maxillary artery terminal branch and the middle meningeal artery by casting Onyx. Post-endovascular treatment, the patient developed facial numbness. After oral administration of Methycobal 0.5 mg, she showed significant improvement at the 1-month follow-up. At the 3-month follow-up, she recovered well. A PubMed literature review identified 10 reported cases of such DAVFs. It was found that for DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid without an accessible transvenous route, TAE is required. While TAE can achieve obliteration of intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid, associated complications cannot be overlooked.Keywords: Intraosseous dural arteriovenous fistula, Greater wing of the sphenoid, Endovascular treatment, Case report, Review

Introduction

Classic dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF) is an abnormal shunt between meningeal arteries and venous sinuses and/or meningeal veins, located within the dura adjacent to a venous sinus [1,2]. Intraosseous DAVF is an uncommon and distinct entity;‌ the arteriovenous shunt is situated within the bone [[3], [4], [5]]. ‌Intraosseous DAVFs involving the greater wing of the sphenoid are exceedingly rare‌ [6,7].

The greater wing of the sphenoid contains the foramen rotundum, ovale, spinosum, and lacerum. These foramina are covered by the outer layer of the dura mater. The emissary veins pass through ‌them‌ parallel to dural arteries. ‌DAVFs may occur within these foramina and are thus classified as greater wing of the sphenoid DAVFs ‌[8]. These DAVFs may progress to an intraosseous type through bony enlargement and destruction after recruiting an increasing number of transosseous arterial branches [9].

Intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid often necessitate intervention due to cortical venous drainage involvement.‌ Endovascular treatment (EVT), including transarterial embolization (TAE) and transvenous embolization (TVE), is the primary therapeutic option [10]. However, curative EVT for intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid poses significant challenges ‌because of the presence of numerous transosseous feeders and the lack of accessible dural sinuses for TVE [11]. ‌In this report, a rare case of an intraosseous DAVF of the greater wing of the sphenoid successfully managed with TAE is presented, along with a literature review of EVT for this specific location.

Case presentation

Patient’s onset and diagnosis

A 63-year-old Chinese Han female presented with a headache lasting 20 days. On examination, she was alert and oriented, with intact limb strength (grade 5) and no pathological reflexes. Enhanced computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography (CTA) revealed left temporal lobe edema and multiple dilated intracranial veins without an apparent nidus; the greater wing of the sphenoid was destroyed; an intraosseous DAVF was considered (Fig. 1). The patient had no history of systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cerebrovascular disease, nor any prior head trauma. Her family had no history of similar cerebrovascular disease. Her laboratory and imaging findings confirmed her eligibility for EVT.

Therapeutic process

Under general anesthesia, digital subtraction angiography revealed a DAVF of the left greater wing of the sphenoid. The bilateral internal carotid arteries (ICAs) provided the minor feeding arteries, including the dural branch of the right ICA (Fig. 2A), the recurrent meningeal branch of the left ophthalmic artery (OphA), and the lateral clival artery of the left ICA (Figs. 2B and C). The major feeding arteries originated from the left external carotid artery (ECA), including terminal transosseous branches from the internal maxillary artery (IMA), the middle meningeal artery (MMA), the accessory meningeal artery (AMA), the deep temporal artery, and the ascending pharyngeal artery (Figs. 2D and E). Venous drainage of the DAVF occurred via the middle cerebral vein (MCV), which showed aneurysmal dilation. The drainage then proceeded into the veins of Trolard and Labbé, and subsequently into the superior sagittal sinus and transverse sinus, respectively. In addition, venous drainage also extended into the contralateral veins (Figs. 2D and E). The DAVF was confirmed as an intraosseous type (Fig. 2F) and was classified as Cognard grade IV.

Then, EVT was performed. Left ICA access was established using a 6F long sheath and a 5F distal access catheter (DAC). Catheterization of the left OphA was attempted with an Echelon-10 microcatheter (Medtronic, Irvine, CA) guided by a Synchro 14 microguidewire (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA); however, the attempt failed (Fig. 3A). Other ICA dural branches to the DAVF were not attempted due to their tortuosity and small caliber. Subsequently, the 6F long sheath and 5F DAC were repositioned to establish left ECA access. After the DAC was advanced into the IMA, the Echelon-10 microcatheter was advanced into the terminal branch of the IMA. Then, 3 mL of Onyx-18 liquid embolic agent (Medtronic, Irvine, CA) was injected, resulting in partial obliteration of the fistula. Next, the transosseous branch of the MMA was catheterized using a Marathon microcatheter (Medtronic, Irvine, CA) over a Synchro 10 microguidewire, allowing access to the fistula point. Following injection of 4 mL of Onyx, the DAVF was completely obliterated (Figs. 3B and C). The MMA and AMA trunks were preserved. Contrast retention was observed in the venous drainage, and Onyx deposition was noted in the sphenoid (Fig. 3D).

Postoperative and follow-up outcomes

After EVT, the patient regained consciousness and exhibited no new neurologic deficits except for left facial numbness. Subcutaneous heparin injections (5000 units twice daily) were administered for 4 days to prevent intracranial venous thrombosis. On day 1, CT revealed no hemorrhage, with stable edema (Fig. 4A). On day 2, magnetic resonance imaging showed no acute infarction and no evidence of DAVF (Figs. 4B and C). On day 3, CT demonstrated no new hemorrhage, stable temporal lobe edema, and thrombosis of the venous drainage (Fig. 4D). CTA maximum intensity projection confirmed the location of the intraosseous DAVF on the greater wing of the sphenoid (Fig. 4E) and verified DAVF obliteration (Fig. 4F). One week ‌postprocedure‌, the patient was discharged with a prescription for Methycobal (Eisai China Inc.) 0.5 mg orally 3 times daily. At the 1-month follow-up, she ‌had significant ‌improvement‌ in ‌the‌ facial numbness. At the 3-month follow-up, she recovered well.

Discussion

DAVFs exhibit various manifestations; they may be spontaneous or traumatic (Fig. 5). Among DAVFs, intraosseous types are extremely rare and unique (Fig. 6). The entity of intraosseous DAVFs was first described‌ by Malik et al. [12]. In these lesions, the feeding arteries originate from the transosseous branches of the ECA and ICA, forming a mesh-like network [3]. Although intraosseous DAVFs are confined to osseous space, embryologically, the shunts share a common involvement of the cranial dura and extradural space [13]. The etiology of intraosseous DAVFs is conjectural and may be similar to the development of classic DAVF ‌due to‌ inflammatory reaction secondary to venous thrombosis. Intraosseous DAVFs can be divided into complete and transitional types [14].

Complete types of intraosseous DAVFs are defined as shunt pouches completely surrounded by cortical bone, occurring in the skull bone and draining into intraosseous venous channels or the diploic venous system (Fig. 6A) [[15], [16], [17]]. They are often located in the dorsum sellae, clivus, and occipital condyle [17]. This is because these bones—formed by endochondral ossification—contain interconnected intraosseous venous channels due to the anatomical continuity of the dorsum sellae, clivus, and jugular tubercle. Most cavernous sinus (CS) DAVFs with posteromedial ‌shunt pouches, clival DAVFs, and anterior/posterior condylar DAVFs belong to intraosseous types, sharing similar features (Fig. 6B and C) [18].

Transitional types of intraosseous DAVFs are defined as lesions ‌that are‌ not fully surrounded by cortical bone but extend into the bony structure [18]. Transosseous feeders converge to form a shunt pouch on the bone surface and drain into the adjacent sinus, emissary veins, or cortical veins. According to this definition, ‌the present case represents‌ a transitional intraosseous DAVF. ‌Intraosseous DAVFs involving the greater wing of the sphenoid are rare. ‌The‌ greater wing of the sphenoid ‌‌ contains several foramina: the foramen rotundum (transmitting the artery of the foramen rotundum), the foramen ovale (transmitting the AMA and the artery of the foramen ovale), and the foramen spinosum (transmitting the MMA). These foramina are covered by periosteum—the outer layer of the dura mater—and are accompanied by a venous plexus [15,19]. DAVFs may develop within these foramina and are thus classified as DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid [8]. As the fistula enlarges, adjacent bony structures may undergo destruction, ‌resulting in‌ an intraosseous configuration.

Intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid can exhibit cortical venous reflux, receiving venous drainage via the paracavernous sinus (sphenobasal and sphenopetrosal sinuses) into the MCV [20,21]. Due to aneurysmal dilatation of the draining MCV, it was necessary to treat this high-grade Cognard type intraosseous DAVF. Currently, EVT is an effective method, encompassing TAE and TVE. TVE is defined as a technique that selectively occludes intraosseous shunt pouches [18,22,23]. It is appropriate for most ‌CS DAVFs‌ with posteromedial shunt pouches, ‌as well as‌ clival and anterior/posterior condylar intraosseous ‌DAVFs [24]. This is because these intraosseous DAVFs often have a relatively large and stable shunt pouch, enabling complete shunt obliteration through selective embolization ‌of‌ the pouch itself and the adjacent bony canal containing the draining veins.

However, in this case of the intraosseous DAVF of the greater wing of the sphenoid, its transitional type meant no direct intraosseous shunt pouches were accessible, and MCV could not be used as a TVE route. Therefore, TAE was necessary. Almost all branches of the IMA were involved as feeders. The terminal branch of the IMA was embolized first, followed by the transosseous branch of the MMA, resulting in complete obliteration of the DAVF. Notably, a recurrent meningeal branch of the OphA was also a feeder. ‌Extreme caution was required during TAE to avoid occlusion of the central retinal artery [25].‌ Initially, embolization of the recurrent meningeal branch of the OphA was attempted to prevent central retinal artery occlusion via its dangerous anastomoses during TAE. However, this attempt failed. Consequently, the OphA had to be monitored continuously throughout the procedure.

In this EVT procedure, embolization of the terminal branch of the IMA did not achieve complete occlusion of the DAVF. However, it significantly reduced the fistula’s hemodynamic stress, facilitating subsequent TAE via the transosseous branch of the MMA. Complete obliteration was achieved once Onyx penetrated the shunt pouch into the proximal MCV. No major complications occurred after EVT, but the patient suffered facial numbness. This was presumably due to injury to distal branches of the MMA or AMA, as the main trunks were preserved. The artery of the trigeminal ganglion from the MMA, a recurrent branch of the AMA, and the artery of the foramen rotundum from the IMA may supply the trigeminal nerve; occlusion of these branches can result in trigeminal nerve injury and facial numbness [26,27]. In addition, sphenoid wing destruction implies all foramina involvement; Onyx mass effect may compress the trigeminal nerve, causing facial numbness.

In this case report, a literature review was performed. The English literature was searched in the PubMed database (last search date: April 27, 2026) using the keywords “dural arteriovenous fistula” AND “greater wing of the sphenoid.” The reference lists of the identified articles were also screened. Only cases with complete and clear imaging and clinical data were included. Inclusion criteria required the DAVF to be located entirely or partially within the greater wing of the sphenoid. Cases located solely on the surface of the greater wing of the sphenoid without bone involvement, and those with only descriptive reports or incomplete imaging, were excluded. In total, 10 cases of intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid were collected (Table 1). These included 4 complete-type and 6 transitional-type fistulas. Three lesions presented with intracranial hemorrhage secondary to draining vein rupture. Arterial supply was extensive, involving transosseous branches of the terminal IMA, MMA, AMA, and deep temporal artery, with additional recruitment of dural branches from the ICA and OphA. Venous drainage pathways varied, encompassing intraosseous venous sinuses, the CS, laterocavernous sinus, paracavernous sinus, and the MCV. EVT was the primary therapeutic approach. TAE or TVE can be utilized: TVE in 4 DAVFs and TAE in 4 DAVFs. For 2 fistulas inaccessible via standard EVT routes, direct puncture or hybrid surgical-EVT techniques achieved success. EVT generally yielded favorable outcomes; however, complications warrant consideration. Due to dangerous anastomoses between the OphA and MMA, continuous fluoroscopic monitoring was required. ‌In 1 case, central retinal vein occlusion occurred.‌ Additionally, 1 patient experienced facial nerve palsy secondary to injury of the petrous branch of the MMA. Preservation of the skull base segment of the MMA is therefore critical to mitigate the nerve injury.

Conclusion

Intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid are rare.‌ They exhibit ‌complex angioarchitecture‌, with ‌extensive transosseous arterial feeders‌ originating from ‌branches of the ICA or the IMA‌, and drain into ‌the MCV‌, ‌resulting in an aggressive clinical course. Intervention is therefore necessary. When no accessible TVE route is available, TAE is required.‌ However, TAE ‌was challenging due to the numerous transosseous feeders from the IMA.‌ ‌While TAE can achieve obliteration of intraosseous DAVFs of the greater wing of the sphenoid, associated complications cannot be overlooked.

Limitation

As a case report, its conclusions cannot be presented in the same way as those from large real‑world samples, and they should be interpreted with caution. In addition, although the median follow‑up shows a favorable clinical outcome, there is no long‑term follow‑up angiographic confirmation of complete DAVF cure. However, if the DAVF and its proximal draining vein are penetrated by the liquid embolic agent, recurrence is uncommon. Postoperative CTA confirmed complete obliteration of the DAVF of the greater wing of the sphenoid. Therefore, the outcome of this case report can be considered good.
