Altered functional connectivity of emotional circuits and default mode network in postpartum women: a resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Kaihua Zhang, Yingli Gao, Qi Hu, Yangxue Su, Qianwen Zhang, Xiaoxia Du, Shouxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the postpartum period, postpartum women experience numerous brain structural and functional changes. Functional neuroimaging studies have focused on the responses of the maternal brain to infant stimuli ; however, the connectivity of the maternal brain remains to be explored. We aimed to examine the differences in functional connectivity patterns between postpartum women and nulliparous women.MethodsResting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data from 38 postpartum women and 25 nulliparous women were used to analyze functional connectivity. Moreover, we also collected Interpersonal Reactivity Index-C scores. The group functional connectivity differences were analyzed by a two-sample T test.ResultsCompared to nulliparous women, postpartum women exhibited increased functional connectivity in regions associated with emotional processes (insula, hippocampus, posterior cingulate gyrus, and medial frontal gyrus), while functional connectivity in the default mode network (inferior parietal lobule and precuneus) decreased significantly.ConclusionThese results suggest that the maternal brain shows altered connectivity patterns, which may reveal the neuroanatomical basis for processing emotional and cognitive information in postpartum women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04399-4.Keywords: Postpartum women, Resting-state fMRI, Functional connectivity, Default mode network, Maternal brain

Introduction

Women undergo enormous changes in their bodies and brains during the postpartum period. Previous studies have shown that the maternal brain exhibits structural and functional plasticity [1, 2]. Studies in rodents have observed structural changes in the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, hypothalamus, striatum, and medial temporal lobe of lactating rats. The affected areas are associated with motivation, maternal behavior, and emotional networks [3]. Similar brain structural changes have been observed in human mothers, with decreased gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex and medial temporal lobe, which are involved in social cognition, as well as in brain regions associated with emotional processing, such as the precuneus and insula [4, 5]. Collectively, these findings suggest that a series of adaptations take place in the female brain during the postpartum period.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies utilized emotional infant face and voice stimuli to explore the brain activity of postpartum women in response to these stimuli. Compared to nonpregnant women, postpartum women showed greater activation in the occipital lobe, fusiform gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, fusiform, insula, amygdala, middle frontal gyrus, and cingulate gyrus when they viewed infant stimuli [6–8], which overlapped with empathy, Theory of Mind (ToM) network and reward brain regions. These activations were particularly strong when presented with a negative stimulus [9]. Similar findings were found using infant sounds, in which activity in the medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate gyrus, the main areas of the female default mode network (DMN), was reduced when postpartum women heard infants crying [10, 11]. In addition, the activity of the motor area, inferior frontal gyrus, superior temporal gyrus and striatum was increased in new mothers [12, 13]. Longitudinal task-based fMRI studies also found that brain activation of mothers increased, including brain regions related to emotional processing and parenting, after a period of nursing compared to early postpartum [14], suggesting that there are persistent changes in the mother’s brain activity during the postpartum period [15], which may be related to better parenting of their offspring.

Compared to task-based fMRI, rs-fMRI can measure spontaneous activity in brain blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals without any task or stimulus input [16] and can eliminate the confounding effects of the experimental design [17]. An rs-fMRI study discovered that postpartum women show significant decreases in regional homogeneity (ReHo) and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) in the posterior cingulate gyrus and prefrontal cortex compared to nulliparous women [18]. Similarly, a dynamic study on spontaneous brain activity in healthy postpartum women found that spontaneous neural activity in the right angular gyrus and precuneus was lower than that in nulliparous women and showed sustained longitudinal changes [19]. These studies revealed that the spontaneous neural activity of the DMN may be associated with self-cognition and emotional regulation [20]. In addition, as one of the important tools for exploring the brain functional framework, resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) can measure the intrinsic functional connectivity between brain regions, which is important for studying postpartum functional plasticity [21]. In a study of postpartum rats, the functional connectivity among the anterior cingulate gyrus, limbic lobe, and sensory cortex in female rats was changed [22]. Research on human mothers has mostly focused on postpartum depression, and it has been found that women with postpartum depression have weakened functional connectivity among the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and hippocampus [23, 24]. Therefore, it is necessary to use rs-FC to study the intrinsic neural activity of healthy postpartum women to further understand the neural mechanisms of maternal behavior.

The postpartum period is marked by profound structural and functional plasticity in the maternal brain, a process crucial for a woman’s successful transition to motherhood. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating the intrinsic functional architecture of the brain during this critical phase. Recent research indicates that numerous brain networks undergo significant adaptive reorganization postpartum. For instance, neural response studies to infant emotional faces and sounds have shown that activity in the fusiform gyrus, precuneus, and parahippocampus can differentiate postpartum women from nulliparous women, suggesting these regions may underlie maternal sensitivity [8, 25]. Furthermore, new mothers exhibit significant alterations in spontaneous brain activity and grey matter volume across several areas, including the insula, striatum, superior temporal gyrus, medial orbitofrontal cortex, hippocampus, and limbic gyri, providing further evidence for extensive structural and functional remodeling of circuits involved in social cognition and emotional processing [19, 26, 27]. Of particular note, the amygdala plays a key role in emotional networks and evaluating the salience of infant cues [3, 7, 28]. Rs-fMRI studies have found that postpartum women exhibit stronger functional connectivity between the amygdala and the medial prefrontal cortex—a region involved in motivation and emotional networks—which correlates with positive maternal behaviors [25–27]. Research on pregnancy and the postpartum period has also revealed significant changes in grey matter structure and default mode network activity [4, 5]. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on specific clinical populations (e.g., women with postpartum depression), single networks (e.g., the default mode network), or specific seed regions (e.g., the amygdala). There is a lack of systematic investigation into the whole-brain functional connectivity patterns across multiple cognitive-affective circuits—involving emotion processing, empathy, reward, and self-referential processing—in healthy postpartum women.

Therefore, to systematically elucidate the network-level mechanisms underlying postpartum brain functional reorganization, this study employed rs-FC to compare functional connectivity between healthy postpartum women and nulliparous controls. Based on a synthesis of maternal neuroscience literature and the classic 7-network resting-state brain parcellation framework [28], we selected a set of regions of interest (ROIs) consistently reported to undergo plastic changes in maternal behavior research. These ROIs cover two core large-scale brain networks: (1) Core nodes of the emotional processing, salience and cognitive control networks, including the medial orbitofrontal cortex, fusiform gyrus, hippocampus, parahippocampus, insula, putamen, amygdala, superior frontal gyrus, and superior temporal gyrus, which are responsible for externally directed infant cue processing, emotion regulation and maternal behavioral execution [4, 7, 12, 29, 30]; (2) Core nodes of the default mode network (DMN), including the precuneus and inferior parietal lobule, which are responsible for internally directed self-referential processing [20, 31]. The transition to motherhood requires a shift in women’s cognitive and affective processing: from a default state dominated by internally directed self-referential processing, to a state prioritizing externally directed, infant-oriented attention, enhanced emotional processing of infant cues, and flexible emotion regulation to support adaptive maternal behavior. Based on the needs of maternal behavior and prior maternal neuroscience evidence, we propose the following two complementary hypotheses: (1) To support the enhanced demand for infant cue processing, emotion regulation and maternal behavioral execution, postpartum women will exhibit significantly increased resting-state functional connectivity within core regions of the emotional-salience-cognitive control circuit (e.g., insula, posterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala).(2) To facilitate the shift from internally directed self-processing to externally directed infant-oriented attention, postpartum women will exhibit significantly decreased functional connectivity within core nodes of the default mode network (e.g., inferior parietal lobule, precuneus). By testing these hypotheses, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive depiction of the functional reorganization patterns of the maternal brain at rest, offering new evidence for understanding postpartum neural plasticity and the neural substrates of maternal behavioral adaptation.

Methods

Participants

Forty-six postpartum women and twenty-five nulliparous women participated in the current study. Four subjects with excessive head motion and four subjects with two children were excluded, leaving thirty-eight postpartum women (age: 30.59 ± 2.95 years) and twenty-five nulliparous women (age: 26.60 ± 1.90 years) included in the analysis. The educational level was an average of 15.59 years (SD = 0.75) for postpartum women and 15.80 years (SD = 0.85) for nulliparous women. All the participants were right-handed. We also collected the information of delivery mode, feeding method, infant sex. Participants were considered eligible for participation if they (1) had no history of serious physical illness, brain trauma, or drug or alcohol dependence; (2) did not have diseases that may cause brain disorders, such as insomnia disorder or neurasthenia; (3) did not have current or historical mental or neurological disorders; and (4) did not have contraindications to MRI scanning, such as claustrophobia or the presence of ferromagnetic substances in the body.

Questionnaire analysis

All the participants completed the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) (Davis, 1983), a questionnaire that assesses empathy. The IRI contains items measuring multidimensional empathy, including personal distress (PD), empathic concern (EC), perspective taking (PT) and fantasy (FS). Participants selected one of five responses, from A (does not describe me very well) to E (describes me very well). We used Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software to assess the internal consistency of the IRI and the differences between data collected at the two scans. The internal consistency was acceptable (α = 0.88). Significant results were reported at two-tailed P < 0.05 with FDR correction.

MRI acquisition

MRI data were acquired with a Siemens 3.0 T Trio Tim MR system at the Shanghai Key Laboratory of Magnetic Resonance equipped with a 12-channel head coil (East China Normal University, Shanghai, China). To minimize subjects’ head motion during the scans, custom-fit foam pads were placed around the participants’ heads. Scanning included the following sessions: (a) rs-T2∗-weighted fMRI and (b) volumetric 3D T1-weighted MRI. High-resolution T1-weighted 3-dimensional magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient‐echo images were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time = 2530 ms, echo time = 2.34 ms, inversion time = 1100 ms, flip angle = 7°, 256 × 256 matrix, field of view = 256 × 256 mm2, total slices = 192, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, and total sequence duration = 363 s. A T2*-weighted gradient-echo echo-planar imaging pulse sequence was conducted at rest to acquire 210 whole-brain volumes with the following parameters: repetition time = 2000 ms, echo time = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, number of slices = 33, transverse orientation, field of view = 220 × 220 mm2, matrix size = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, 25% distance factor, and total sequence duration = 426 s. During the rs-fMRI scan, all the subjects were instructed to relax with their eyes closed, rest and not think of anything.

Data preprocessing

Rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-state fMRI (DPARSF, http://www.resffmri.net/forum/DPARSF) in MATLAB R2020a (The Math Works, Natick, MA, USA). The preprocessing procedure was as follows: DICOM data were converted to NIFTI images. The first 10 volumes were removed from each participant’s data to minimize the instability of the initial MRI signals, and the remaining volumes underwent slice timing and spatial realignment to correct for head motion. The high-resolution T1-weighted images were coregistered to the mean functional images and segmented. Then, the images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template (resolution of 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm). This was followed by regressing nuisance variables, including the time series of six head motion parameters, white matter signal and cerebral spinal fluid signal, using a general linear model, and the linear trends were removed from time courses. Finally, spatial smoothing with a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) was performed on the functional data.

FC analysis

The preprocessed data were subjected to rs-FC analysis. rs-FC maps for all subjects were obtained by calculating the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the regions of interest (ROI) and the rest of the brain. The ROIs were selected based on maternal neuroscience literature and the classic resting-state network parcellation [28], covering core nodes of the emotional-salience-cognitive control network and the default mode network, as detailed in the introduction section. The significant differential statistical parametric maps were generated for the above significantly activated regions [7, 8, 32], and the connection of these regions, as seeds, with the whole brain was explored using the FC method based on rs-fMRI. The delineation of ROIs was performed with image calculator in SPM12. Then, we computed the mean time series of the seeds and correlated these with the time series of other voxels in the whole brain to obtain FC maps. Finally, to improve the normality of the data distribution, FC maps were converted into z score maps. Group effects were analyzed using the two-sample t test in a second-level statistical analysis. Age and head motion parameters were used as covariates in the above statistical analyses. The surviving clusters represent those with a significant difference between the two groups at a peak-level P < 0.001 and cluster-level P < 0.05 with family-wise error rate (FWE) corrections. And false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

To control for the effect of age on functional connectivity, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by selecting age-matched subsets from both groups: postpartum women aged 26–30 years (sample size = 16) and nulliparous women aged 26–30 years (sample size = 16) (mean age difference after matching = 0.5 years, p = 0.384). Using this matched subset, we re-analyzed the intergroup differences in functional connectivity.

Correlation analysis

To further evaluate the relationship between functional connectivity (FC) patterns and Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) scores, partial correlation analysis controlling for age was performed in this study. Specifically, based on the results of intergroup difference analysis, the FC values of brain regions with significant intergroup differences were extracted from both groups (postpartum women group / nulliparous women group). With age as a covariate, Pearson’s partial correlation analysis was used to examine the correlation coefficient between FC values and IRI scores (SPSS 25.0 software). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple comparisons to control for type I errors.

To further eliminate the selection bias and circular analysis induced by the non-independent two-step analytical procedure (screening brain regions with intergroup differences in the full sample followed by correlation analysis in the same sample), and to rigorously verify the robustness and generalizability of the correlation results, we performed a Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) procedure.

The LOOCV procedure was conducted separately for the postpartum group and nulliparous group, as the correlation analyses between FC values and IRI scores were performed independently in the two cohorts. For the postpartum group (n = 38), in each iteration, one postpartum participant was left out as the completely independent test set, and the remaining 37 postpartum participants together with all 25 nulliparous participants formed the training set. For the nulliparous group (n = 25), in each iteration, one nulliparous participant was left out as the independent test set, and the remaining 24 nulliparous participants together with all 38 postpartum participants formed the training set.

We also examined whether IRI scores were associated with rs-FC in postpartum women and nulliparous women. A significant negative correlation was found between the left medial orbitofrontal–left postcentral gyrus functional connectivity and PD scores in postpartum women (r=-0.419, p = 0.036) (Table 3 and Fig. 2A). In addition, the left superior frontal gyrus–right precuneus FC was also negatively correlated with PD scores in nulliparous women (r=-0.511, p = 0.045) (Table 3 and Fig. 2B).

Discussion

The present study examined differences in rs-FC between postpartum women and nulliparous women, demonstrating that compared with nulliparous women, postpartum women exhibited altered functional connectivity in brain regions related to emotional processing. Specifically, stronger rs-FC of the posterior cingulate gyrus, medial frontal gyrus, orbitofrontal cortex, supplementary motor area, anterior cerebellar lobe, angular gyrus, insula, thalamus and hippocampus was observed in postpartum women. In addition, rs-FC was decreased in the supramarginal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule and precuneus, which are the core nodes of the DMN. These findings provide further evidence to support the plasticity of brain function in postpartum women, which is beneficial for understanding the neural mechanisms of the mother’s brain.

The rs-FC results showed increased functional connectivity of the limbic system in postpartum women, including in the thalamus and hippocampus, associated with emotional and cognitive processing. A study on postpartum sensitivity fMRI found that increased activation of the hippocampus is associated with harmonious maternal-infant relationships [29]. The hippocampus has also been shown to be involved in the regulation of emotion and stress management [33, 34]. Hence, a more harmonious mother-infant relationship can guide postpartum women to interact effectively with their infants and better regulate their stress response and emotion when exposed to the baby’s negative emotions.

In addition, this study found that postpartum women have stronger FC in the brain regions involved in empathy and ToM networks, which have been proven to be related to social cognitive processing [35, 36]. Empathy refers to the ability to share the cognitive and emotional states of others, and the insula is the core brain region of the empathy network [6]. The ToM network includes the medial frontal cortex and angular gyrus [35, 37] and is based on the ability to reflect on and attribute one’s own state of mind and accept that others have different views and intentions from one’s own [30]. Through ToM network and empathy, postpartum women can accurately understand the sound and behavior of their infants, thereby providing effective care. Studies on the changes in brain structure in postpartum women found that the gray matter volumes in the insula and posterior cingulate cortex decreased and white matter volumes increased, which significantly overlap with the empathy and ToM network [4, 38]. Research on mothers’ brain responses to emotional infant faces has also found consistent results [8]. These results indicate that the alterations in postpartum women’s brains reflect adaptive maternal reactivity to respond to infants’ needs. These alterations play a role in cultivating high-quality attachment with infants.

This study also found changed FC in the amygdala and insula, which are part of the salience network [39, 40]. The salience network is activated in response to a context-specific stimulus to improve alertness. Therefore, salience network functional connectivity greatly supports mothers’ concern for the well-being of the infant and supports postpartum women in responding accurately to stimulus cues to keep infants from being hurt. It also enhances maternal behavior and improves mother-infant attachment [25]. The stimulation of infants is complex and requires postpartum women to allocate cognitive and motivational resources to respond reasonably to infants. Thus, cognitive processing regions are also involved. Our research also found that the functional connectivity of postpartum women in the medial prefrontal lobe, superior frontal gyrus, supplementary motor area, and anterior cerebellar lobe was strengthened. The medial prefrontal cortex is implicated in higher cognitive processes, including modulating attention, decision making, and executive and emotion regulation [41, 42]. Moreover, neuroimaging studies have revealed that the cerebellum and supplementary motor area are associated with cognitive and emotional processing [32, 43]. On the basis of these results, we can presume that the maternal brain undergoes plasticity changes in the social cognitive neural network after childbirth, and similar findings have been reported in the task of recognizing emotional faces, indicating the enhanced ability of postpartum women to process social information [8]. Longitudinal morphological studies have also revealed decreased gray matter volume in the relevant brain regions in postpartum women, which may represent the specialization of brain circuits and compensatory neural mechanisms [5, 38].

The rs-FC analysis showed decreased functional connectivity in postpartum women, including the supramarginal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule and precuneus, which are the core nodes of the DMN [44, 45]. The DMN is an important rs-FC network characterized by increased activity during the resting state but decreased activity when attention is needed [46, 47]. Evidence from both animal and human studies suggests that infant stimuli deactivate the DMN in postpartum women, indicating that women shift more attention from focusing on themselves to focusing on stimuli related to their infants and the external environment after childbirth [11, 48]. In addition, studies on healthy postpartum women found that ALFF and ReHo were significantly reduced in the posterior cingulate cortex, inferior parietal lobe (including the angular gyrus and supramarginal gyrus) and precuneus [18, 19]. These results indicate that the DMN in postpartum women undergoes functional reorganization to address the various challenges of becoming a mother. We speculate that this reduced intrinsic connectivity might facilitate a shift from introspective to exteroceptive processing, enabling postpartum women to more rapidly disengage from internal mentation and reorient attention to infant cues. Therefore, the spontaneous functional connectivity changes in the DMN of postpartum women may help them better adapt to maternal identity and provide adaptive changes in maternal behavior.

Notablely, the increased functional connectivity in the emotional-salience-cognitive control network and the decreased functional connectivity in the default mode network observed in this study, reflect the antagonistic balance shift between these two large-scale brain networks in postpartum women. This balance shift from internally directed self-referential processing to externally directed infant-oriented processing, may be the core network-level neural mechanism underlying the successful transition to motherhood, which jointly supports enhanced maternal emotional responsiveness, accurate infant cue processing, and high-quality mother-infant attachment.

Parental empathy and related social behaviors are necessary for parenting; therefore, we explored the relationship between empathy-related behavioral measures (IRI scores) and functional connectivity. Consistent with previous studies demonstrating specific neural mechanisms in empathy [49], significant negative correlations between personal distress (PD) scores and rs-FC were observed in both groups, though in distinct brain regions. Briefly, rs-FC between the left medial orbitofrontal cortex and left postcentral gyrus as well as between the left superior frontal gyrus and right precuneus showed a significant negative correlation with PD subscale scores. The brain regions correlated with these associations in both groups are involved in emotion regulation and self-referential processing [50]. PD is the affective component of empathy, describing feelings of worry, nervousness and anxiety when observing the negative experiences of others [51]. The presence of negative correlations in both groups underscores the role of these networks in modulating personal distress across different life stages, while the distinct neural pathways may be modulated by maternal experience. In postpartum women, stronger connectivity between the left medial orbitofrontal cortex and left postcentral gyrus was associated with lower PD, suggesting that enhanced integration within regions involved in emotional valuation and somatosensory processing may facilitate maternal emotion regulation when confronted with infant distress. Women are the main caregivers of infants, and it is necessary for them to be more accurate in perceiving the states of infants and appeasing babies when they suffer negative emotions [52]. Thus, when the brain regions that perceive infant information have stronger functional connectivity between them, women can better distinguish and control emotions, which may lead to less PD. In nulliparous women, a similar negative correlation emerged between PD and connectivity linking the left superior frontal gyrus and right precuneus, key nodes of the default mode and cognitive control networks. This may reflect a more general mechanism of self-referential processing and cognitive reappraisal in emotion regulation. Notably, negative correlations between brain function and structure in empathy-related regions and affective empathy were also found in previous studies [53–55]. Therefore, these correlations indicated that brain functional connectivity in women may be associated with perception and response when nurturing infants.

While our research revealed altered functional connectivity in emotional circuits and the DMN in postpartum women, it had several limitations. First, given the cross-sectional comparison between postpartum women and childless women, it is difficult to make causal inferences; hence, future longitudinal follow-up studies can be carried out to explore the functional connectivity of the maternal brain. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and subsequent studies can expand the sample size. Third, the childless women were younger than the postpartum women because it was difficult to find older childless women during the recruitment period. This limitation likely has to do with women’s role in society and the reproductive risks they need to take. Thus, we controlled for age as a covariate in the analysis. In future studies, we can try to recruit older nulliparous women to participate in the experiment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study shows that there are significant differences in resting-state functional connectivity between postpartum women and nulliparous women. Alterations in FC values were located in the hippocampus, posterior cingulate gyrus, medial frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule and precuneus, which include regions corresponding to the emotional circuit and DMN. These results demonstrate that postpartum women exhibit altered functional connectivity compared to nulliparous women, suggesting that motherhood may be associated with adaptive neural reorganization in emotional and default mode networks. These alterations may facilitate maternal responsiveness, promote mother-infant attachment, and support caregiving quality.
