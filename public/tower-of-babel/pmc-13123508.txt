Tubular adenoma of the urinary bladder in a female patient: An extremely rare entity clinically mimicking urothelial malignancy
Authors: Cansu Taşpınar, Asilhan Sabuncu, Ezgi Duman Kurt, Hüsniye Esra Paşaoğlu, İsmail Engin Kandıralı, Şule Özsoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tubular adenoma of the urinary bladder is an exceptionally rare intestinal-type glandular neoplasm that may mimic urothelial malignancy. Among approximately 82 reported enteric-type bladder adenomas, only four represent pure tubular adenomas. We report a case in a 78-year-old woman presenting with gross hematuria. Cystoscopy revealed a suspicious lesion, and transurethral resection was performed. Histopathologic examination showed tubular glands with intestinal differentiation without villous architecture. Immunohistochemistry supported intestinal differentiation. To our knowledge, this represents the fifth reported case. Recognition of this entity is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and overtreatment.

Introduction

Tubular adenoma (TA) is a rare benign neoplasm of the urinary bladder that is histopathologically indistinguishable from its enteric and colorectal counterparts. TA was first described in the urinary tract by Kao et al., in 2013.1,2 Intestinal-type adenomas are extremely uncommon lesions that may arise anywhere along the urinary tract, most frequently involving the bladder dome, trigone, and urethra.3 Patients typically present with hematuria, dysuria, and other irritative lower urinary tract symptoms 4, 5, 6. These lesions show a slight male predominance and occur mainly in adults, with a mean age of 62 years (range: 23–94 years).4,7 Intestinal-type adenomas of the bladder most commonly exhibit a villous configuration. Tubular adenoma of the urinary bladder is exceedingly rare, with only four cases reported to date; here, we present the fifth case arising in the background of intestinal metaplasia.1,2

Case report

A 78-year-old woman presented to the urology outpatient clinic with a complaint of gross hematuria. Her past medical history was significant for atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, goiter, and epilepsy. She had no history of smoking and was a housewife.

Urinary tract ultrasonography performed to investigate the etiology of hematuria revealed an irregularly marginated echogenic area measuring approximately 25 mm on the left posterolateral wall of the urinary bladder. No calculi or other abnormalities explaining the hematuria were detected. The findings were primarily interpreted as being suggestive of a hematoma. Urinalysis showed no erythrocytes, and urine culture was negative for bacterial growth. Given that the patient was considered to be at high risk for urologic malignancies; cystoscopy was planned, preferably under local anesthesia in consideration of her comorbidities, to further evaluate the cause of gross hematuria.

Ureterocystoscopy was performed under local anesthesia using a Storz 19F flexible cystoscope following appropriate preparation and draping. The urethra appeared normal. The ureteral orifices were observed to be in their normal anatomical positions with a normal appearance. The lesion described on ultrasonography was not identified. However, a suspicious area on the opposite bladder wall was noted, described as slightly elevated, hyperemic, and velvety in appearance. This area was considered suspicious for malignancy, and transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TUR-T) under general anesthesia was planned for further evaluation.

Under sedoanalgesia, transurethral access was achieved using a Storz 26F resectoscope. The bladder was evaluated both under white light and using a CHROMA light filter, which is reported to improve detection of urothelial malignancies. A suspicious lesion measuring approximately 2 cm in length was identified on the opposite bladder wall, appearing elevated and concerning for malignant potential, and it was completely resected. During resection, sampling of the muscularis propria was ensured. The resected material was retrieved by irrigation and submitted to the pathology laboratory for histopathologic examination.

Microscopic examination of the TUR-T specimen revealed dysplastic epithelial cells with goblet cell differentiation lining the bladder surface, with an underlying glandular proliferation composed of similar columnar epithelium. The stroma surrounding the tubular structures showed cystitis glandularis and a mixed inflammatory reaction. A focus of intestinal metaplasia was identified adjacent to urothelial epithelium with accompanying reactive changes (Fig. 1A and B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated CDX2 and SATB2 positivity in the dysplastic tubular epithelium (Fig. 1C), while GATA3 and HMWCK were negative. Discussion

Embryologically, the distal colorectum and the urinary bladder arise from the division of the cloaca by the urorectal septum.8,9 It has been proposed that portions of cloacal remnants, with the potential to give rise to glandular neoplasms, may persist in the adult urinary bladder and urachus.8 An alternative theory suggests that glandular neoplasms may develop as a consequence of the chronic irritation–metaplasia–dysplasia–carcinoma sequence.2

Enteric- and colorectal-type adenomas are benign glandular neoplasms that may arise anywhere in the urinary tract. Histomorphologically, tubular and villous adenomas of the urinary tract are essentially identical to their colonic counterparts. In both locations, these adenomas typically have pseudostratified columnar epithelium lined by mucin-producing cells of goblet cell type, with variable degrees of nuclear atypia.8,10

In tubular and villous adenomas of the urinary tract, dysplasia is most often low grade.11 However, similar to their colorectal counterparts, high-grade dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, and invasive adenocarcinoma may also arise in association with these lesions.4

In the study by Santa et al., although the role of cystitis glandularis accompanied by intestinal metaplasia as a precursor lesion remains controversial, villous adenomas were emphasized as potential precursor lesions in the development of enteric-type adenocarcinoma of the urinary bladder.11

Intestinal metaplasia of the urothelial epithelium may occur secondary to chronic irritation and inflammation, including conditions such as indwelling catheters, calculi, neurogenic bladder, and bladder exstrophy 4, 5, 6. Although it is usually recognized microscopically, it may rarely present clinically with mucosuria due to mucin extravasation into the surrounding tissues.9 On cystoscopy, these lesions may appear flat, polypoid, or gelatinous due to mucin production.12 Villous adenomas have also been reported to form exophytic papillary projections that are difficult to distinguish cystoscopically from papillary urothelial neoplasms.7

Urologists are often confronted with cystoscopic findings that are abnormal but non-specific. The majority of such cases represent metaplastic changes of the urinary bladder epithelium, which may mimic urothelial carcinoma.12

The histopathology of tubular adenoma is highly characteristic. Since intestinal metaplasia in the surface urothelium has been reported as a potentially premalignant background for bladder adenocarcinoma, the presence of more advanced lesions should always be carefully excluded.9 Similar to gastrointestinal adenomas, the presence of a villous component and an increasing proportion of villous architecture are associated with a higher risk of progression to invasive adenocarcinoma. In our case, no villous component was identified, and there were no morphologic features of urothelial carcinoma or carcinoma in situ.

The main differential diagnoses of tubular adenoma, which can occur anywhere in the urothelial-lined urinary tract, include florid cystitis cystica and cystitis glandularis with dysplasia, urothelial carcinoma in situ with glandular differentiation, and well-differentiated adenocarcinoma.1

Tubular adenoma of the urinary bladder is an exceptionally rare lesion within the spectrum of intestinal-type glandular neoplasms. Approximately 82 cases of enteric-type adenomas have been reported in the urinary bladder; however, only a very small subset represents pure tubular adenomas. To our knowledge, only four such cases have been described in the literature,1 making the present case the fifth reported instance arising in the native urinary bladder. These previously reported cases highlight the rarity of this entity and its potential to mimic malignant lesions.1

Previously reported cases1 have demonstrated variable clinical presentations, and were often identified as polypoid or papillary lesions on cystoscopy. Histologically, these lesions are characterized by glandular structures lined by dysplastic epithelium with intestinal differentiation, frequently associated with goblet cells. Immunohistochemical findings typically support intestinal differentiation, with expression of markers such as CDX2 and SATB2.

Our patient was a 78-year-old non-smoking woman, although enteric- and colorectal-type adenomas are more commonly observed in elderly men. Most reported cases in the literature have been described as tubulovillous or villous adenomas, and Kao et al. reported a series of four cases of tubular adenoma.1

The prognosis of patients with isolated tubular/villous adenomas localized to the urinary bladder is excellent, and complete surgical resection is considered curative 8, 9, 10, 11. However, it remains uncertain whether adenocarcinoma may develop from an unresected lesion.8 Given the potential premalignant nature of these lesions, close follow-up has been recommended.8

The present case is notable for its clinical presentation as a lesion mimicking urothelial malignancy, which may lead to diagnostic confusion and potential overtreatment. In contrast to some previously reported cases, this lesion arose in a native urinary bladder without known predisposing conditions such as augmentation or chronic irritation.

From a clinical perspective, recognition of this rare entity is essential. Accurate histopathologic diagnosis allows distinction from urothelial carcinoma and other malignant lesions, thereby preventing unnecessary aggressive management. Awareness of this diagnostic pitfall is particularly important for both pathologists and urologists when evaluating glandular lesions of the bladder.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of tubular adenoma is challenging without histopathologic evaluation. As noted in the clinical history of our case, this lesion was described as flat on cystoscopy and cannot be reliably distinguished clinically from urothelial carcinoma in situ.9 Although there is no consensus in the literature regarding whether intestinal metaplasia represents a premalignant condition, the coexistence of enteric and colorectal-type adenomas with other malignant neoplasms of the urinary bladder, together with the uncertain malignant potential of these lesions, underscores the importance of awareness among clinicians and pathologists. Close follow-up of such dysplastic lesions may be beneficial until further evidence becomes available, and recognition of this exceedingly rare entity is crucial to avoid overtreatment while ensuring adequate exclusion of associated or subsequent malignancy through histopathologic evaluation and surveillance.12,13
