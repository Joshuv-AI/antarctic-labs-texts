Spatiotemporal Computational Meta‐Imager for Broadband Dual‐Layer Optical Convolution
Authors: Mengguang Wang, Chenglong Zheng, Huaping Zang, Changwei Zhang, Huai Xia, Chang Wang, Zhenrong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOptical neural networks face a tradeoff between device footprint and computational expressivity: deep systems are bulky, whereas single‐layer hardware lacks intrinsic depth and native negative‐weight support. We experimentally demonstrate a single‐layer metalens imager with “spatiotemporal intrinsic depth” for equivalent broadband dual‐layer convolution. A polarization‐multiplexed metasurface provides fixed Bessel‐vortex channels (l = 0 to 3) as the immutable first convolution layer, where the (l = 0) channel implements a hardware‐native residual connection. A metasurface‐generated 3 × 3 optical vortex matrix forms the second functional layer; time‐sequential liquid‐crystal amplitude modulation delivers tunable positive/negative weights. This temporal encoding solves the optical sign problem and converts fabrication imperfections into compensable biases via in‐situ training. Experiments verify rotational robustness for fashion‐item classification, raise full‐color CIFAR‐10 accuracy from 57.5 % to 86.6 %, and reach 99.3 % on the SIPaKMeD medical dataset, offloading 99.7 % of convolutional multiply‐accumulate operations to optics. By removing volumetric diffractive layers, this work enables low‐latency hybrid optoelectronic edge intelligent vision.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of deep neural networks has revolutionized machine vision [1], enabling unprecedented performance in image classification [2], object detection, and semantic segmentation [3, 4]. However, this progress has been accompanied by an exponential growth in model complexity and computational demand. State‐of‐the‐art vision architectures routinely require billions of multiply–accumulate operations per inference, translating to substantial energy consumption and latency that are incompatible with edge deployment scenarios [5]. The challenge is particularly acute in autonomous systems—ranging from unmanned aerial vehicles to wearable medical devices—where the imaging and processing pipeline must simultaneously satisfy stringent constraints on size, weight, power consumption, and real‐time responsiveness. These competing requirements expose a fundamental tension in conventional electronic processing: the von Neumann bottleneck between image acquisition and computation imposes irreducible data movement costs that scale poorly with increasing sensor resolution and network depth. Consequently, there is a pressing need for new computational paradigms that can shift the burden of feature extraction away from digital post‐processing and into the physical layer of image formation.

Optics has long been recognized as a promising avenue for alleviating the computational burden of convolution operations, which constitute the dominant fraction of arithmetic workload in modern vision networks [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The Fourier‐transform property of free‐space propagation enables massively parallel, passive execution of spatial filtering at the speed of light [11, 12]. Early optical correlators and more recent diffractive deep neural networks have demonstrated the viability of this approach, using cascaded phase masks to approximate multi‐layer perceptrons in the optical domain [13, 14]. Yet, these implementations typically rely on coherent illumination and require re‐imaging of digital data onto spatial light modulators, rendering them better suited as back‐end processors than as integrated front‐end imagers [15, 16]. Furthermore, the volumetric stacking inherent to multi‐layer diffractive networks directly contradicts the miniaturization imperative for edge deployment [13, 17, 18, 19, 20]. A transformative opportunity therefore lies in compressing the expressive capacity of multiple optical layers into a single, ultra‐compact optical element that operates directly on the incident scene—effectively embedding depth into the physical structure of the imager itself.

Metasurfaces [21, 22, 23, 24]—planar arrays of subwavelength scatterers that impart tailored phase, amplitude, and polarization modulation to incident light—offer a compelling platform for realizing front‐end optical computation in a form factor compatible with chip‐scale integration [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. By engineering the meta‐atom response as a function of angle, wavelength, or polarization, researchers have demonstrated compact analogues of mathematical operations such as spatial differentiation and edge detection without the need for bulky 4F Fourier optics [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Multiplexing strategies have further enabled multi‐channel operation and, through optoelectronic subtraction, the realization of negative‐valued kernels under incoherent illumination [39, 40]. Despite these advances, existing metasurface processors remain constrained in their effective architectural depth. They are typically limited to a single fixed convolution layer with positively valued weights, or to multichannel kernels whose stride equals their size—a restriction that precludes the densely overlapping receptive fields and hierarchical feature extraction that define modern convolutional neural networks [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. Bridging this gap requires an optical element that can simultaneously emulate multiple convolution layers, support arbitrary signed weights, and maintain operation across the broadband spectrum required for color imaging—all while offering a mechanism to compensate for the inevitable imperfections of nanoscale fabrication.

In this work, we introduce and experimentally validate a single metasurface aperture that computationally implements a dual‐layer convolutional kernel—a design we refer to as computational depth in the context of factorized kernel reconfigurability (Figure 1a). Unlike conventional diffractive networks that cascade discrete physical planes, our design leverages polarization‐division multiplexing to embed an immutable first convolutional layer directly into the transmission matrix of a single flat optic, while reserving a dedicated channel for hardware‐native residual connectivity. A second, reconfigurable convolutional layer is realized by an optical vortex matrix whose weight amplitudes and signs are dynamically controlled via time‐sequential liquid‐crystal modulation. This temporal encoding strategy not only circumvents the sign limitations of intensity‐based optics but uniquely enables in situ training, allowing the system to self‐calibrate against fabrication deviations (Figure 1b). We further demonstrate that the broadband dispersion of the Bessel–vortex basis sustains the convolution fidelity across the infrared spectral range, extending the operational scope to full‐color imagery (Figure 1c). The proposed architecture effectively folds two factorized convolution stages into a single optical pass, establishing a pathway toward ultra‐compact, task‐specific intelligent imagers that offload the vast majority of convolutional arithmetic to the front‐end optics. We substantiate these claims through a series of experiments spanning rotational robustness, color‐image classification, and high‐resolution medical imaging (Note S1 and Figure S1), illustrating the transformative potential of physically embedded computation for next‐generation edge vision systems (Figure 1d–f).

This operation is repeated across all positions, producing a feature map that encodes local correlations at a scale determined by the kernel size N. The computational cost scales linearly with the number of pixels and quadratically with N, quickly becoming the dominant arithmetic burden in deep vision pipelines. To alleviate this load, one may seek to transfer the convolution operation into the optical domain, where free‐space propagation inherently performs spatial averaging at the speed of light.

The bridge between digital and optical convolution is established by recognizing that an incoherent imaging system forms an image through the convolution of the object intensity with the modulus squared of its coherent point spread function (PSF). If the PSF of an optical front end can be engineered to mimic the structure of a digital kernel, then the detected image will directly represent the convolved feature map. Our approach accomplishes this by designing a metasurface that generates a discrete N × N array of diffraction‐limited focal spots in the image plane, as depicted in Figure 2b. The sign of each weight, which is inherently positive in an intensity‐only measurement, is later resolved through a temporal differential detection scheme described below.

The First Convolutional Layer: Polarization‐Multiplexed Bessel‐Vortex Priors

With the optical convolution framework established, we now detail the physical realization of a two‐layer convolutional architecture within a single metasurface. The first layer is implemented through four independent polarization channels, each producing a distinct Bessel‐vortex mode. In polar coordinates (r,ϕ) at the metasurface plane, the complex transmission coefficient of the l‐th channel is given by (3) tlr,ϕ=Jlαreilϕe−ik0r22f where l ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3} is the topological charge, Jl is the l‐th order Bessel function of the first kind, k 0 = 2π/λ is the free‐space wavenumber, f is the focal length of the metalens, and α is a scaling constant that sets the radial extent of the Bessel mode. The quadratic phase term e−ik0r22f imparts the focusing power of a conventional lens, collapsing the Bessel distribution into a sharply defined ring‐like focus at the back focal plane (Note S2 and Figure S2).

Crucially, each topological charge l corresponds to a specific spatial filtering operation (Figure 2c). The l = 0 channel produces a central bright maximum and functions as a low‐pass filter that preserves global luminance—this channel serves as a hardware‐native residual connection, providing a direct pathway for unprocessed image content to bypass the convolutional layers. The l = 1 channel generates a doughnut‐shaped focus with a central null, approximating a first‐order spatial derivative that extracts edges and gradients. The l = 2 and l = 3 channels impose higher‐order spatial differentiation, capturing curvature and fine textural variations. These four operations are fixed by the metasurface design and are applied in parallel to the incoming scene, irrespective of the downstream task. They constitute an immutable first convolutional layer that injects well‐understood physical priors into the processing pipeline, significantly reducing the learning burden on any subsequent digital classifier.

The Second Convolutional Layer and the Fusion of Two Depths

The second convolutional layer is realized through a metasurface‐generated 3 × 3 array of optical vortices, each of which acts as a discrete, reconfigurable weight element (Note S3 and Figure S3). Unlike the fixed first layer, the intensities of these nine focal spots are dynamically controlled by a liquid‐crystal (LC) amplitude modulator placed in the optical path. The LC layer is an independently addressable pixelated spatial light modulator, in which each pixel's transmission coefficient T(V(x, y))∈[0, 1] is controlled by an applied voltage. The modulator is programmed with a spatially varying transmission map that independently sets the relative intensity of each focal spot in the 3 × 3 vortex array. To incorporate both positive and negative weights—a necessity for arbitrary convolutional kernels—we employ a temporal differential encoding strategy. Each target weight wpq is decomposed as wpq=wpq+−wpq−, with wpq±≥0,. In two successive acquisition frames, the LC modulator applies transmission factors corresponding to the positive and negative matrices, respectively, and the detector records two intensity distributions I + and I −. The final feature map is obtained by digital subtraction I out = I + − I −, which not only yields signed kernel values but also suppresses common‐mode noise sources such as detector dark current and ambient background. P l is the polarization projection operator for the l‐th channel, ensuring that the channels are orthogonally separated in the polarization domain. The parameters ai and bi quantify the lateral offsets of the i‐th focal spot relative to the optical axis.

The fusion of the two convolutional layers occurs seamlessly in the optical domain. The four polarization‐multiplexed Bessel‐vortex channels from the first layer propagate through the same physical aperture and are simultaneously weighted by the LC‐modulated second‐layer kernel. Because the polarization states are orthogonal, the channels do not interfere; they are instead separated at the detection plane by a polarization‐sensitive photodetector array, where each pixel integrates an oriented grating for on‐chip polarization sorting. The result is a set of four distinct feature maps, each corresponding to a different combination of first‐layer prior and second‐layer tunable weights. This architecture effectively folds two complete convolutional layers into a single sub‐millimeter‐thick metasurface, exploiting polarization and time as orthogonal multiplexing dimensions to embed depth without volumetric stacking.

Finally, the temporal reconfigurability afforded by the liquid‐crystal amplitude modulator endows the system with a defining practical advantage: in situ error correction through online training. Nanofabrication inevitably introduces static phase deviations into the metasurface, and experimental alignment invariably involves minute mechanical misregistration. In a conventional passive metasurface, such imperfections would permanently degrade the fidelity of the optical convolution and remain uncorrectable post‐fabrication. Here, however, the hybrid optoelectronic training loop—wherein digital loss gradients are backpropagated to update the liquid‐crystal transmission map—enables the system to autonomously learn compensatory amplitude weightings during a brief calibration phase. This process effectively transforms a static, error‐prone nanostructure into an actively trainable optical layer that can self‐calibrate against both manufacturing variability and experimental setup discrepancies. The ability to absorb and neutralize these otherwise performance‐limiting deviations is the hallmark of the online training paradigm. Combined with the broadband dispersion engineering that maintains kernel geometric fidelity across the spectrum, this self‐correcting capability underpins robust, full‐color convolutional processing that offloads up to 99.7% of the multiply–accumulate operations from the digital backend to the optical front end (Note S4 and Table S1).

The hybrid optoelectronic architecture of our meta‐imager enables a unique closed‐loop training paradigm in which the optical front end, the digital back end, and an amplitude‐prediction neural network (APNN) are jointly optimized to compensate for both task‐specific feature requirements and static fabrication deviations. An Amplitude‐Prediction Neural Network

The key innovation of our training scheme lies in treating the liquid‐crystal amplitude modulator not merely as a static weight provider, but as a continuously tunable parameter plane that can be updated in response to both digital loss gradients and measured optical imperfections. To accelerate this process, we employ a lightweight APNN that learns the nonlinear mapping from a target PSF intensity distribution to the required liquid‐crystal transmission matrix. The APNN is trained offline using 4000 pairs of synthetically generated target PSFs and their corresponding optimized LC transmission profiles, obtained via conventional gradient descent. Once trained, the APNN predicts an initial LC transmission map T(V(x, y)) for any desired kernel in approximately 0.8 ms, eliminating the need for time‐consuming iterative optimization at inference time (Note S7 and Figure S8).

During online training, the system operates in a closed loop. An input batch is passed through the optical layer, where the liquid‐crystal modulator applies the current transmission map T(V(x, y)). The resulting feature maps are digitized and processed by the digital back end to compute the cross‐entropy loss L=CrossEntropy(y^,ytrue). The gradient of this loss with respect to the LC transmission values is then backpropagated through the digital layers and the optical PSF model, yielding an update direction for the amplitude plane. The transmission map is iteratively refined according to (6) TVx,yt+1=TVx,yt−η·∂CrossEntropyy^,ytrue∂TVx,y where η is the learning rate and the gradient encapsulates both the digital classification error and the physical forward model of the PSF. This update rule adjusts the relative brightness of the nine vortex spots, thereby tuning the second‐layer convolutional kernel while the first‐layer Bessel‐vortex priors remain fixed.

Figure 4e displays the normalized channel weight distribution across the four polarization‐multiplexed Bessel‐vortex modes, revealing a balanced allocation in which the l = 0 residual channel retains sufficient optical power to preserve global image content while the higher‐order channels (l = 1,2,3) distribute the remaining intensity for multi‐scale spatial differentiation (Note S10 and Figure S11). This intrinsic weight partitioning acts as a physically embedded regularize that prevents any single feature type from dominating the representation, thereby enhancing feature diversity and directly contributing to the improved classification accuracy observed across all benchmark datasets. A distinguishing theoretical attribute of the Bessel‐vortex basis is its invariance to in‐plane image rotations: a rotation of the input scene merely imparts a global phase shift to the vortex modes, leaving the intensity distribution of the convolved feature maps unchanged (Table S7). To experimentally validate this property, we constructed a rotated test set from the Fashion‐MNIST dataset by applying random in‐plane rotations uniformly sampled from 0° to 180° to each grayscale image. Representative samples are shown in Figure 4f (Notes S11 and S12, Figures S12 and S13). We evaluated classification accuracy on this rotated test set using two configurations: the complete hybrid pipeline, in which the meta‐imager performs optical convolution followed by a lightweight digital classifier, and a digital‐only double‐layer convolutional network with matched kernel dimensions and channel count. The simulated double‐layer convolutional network convolution achieved an accuracy of 94.3%, while the experimental measurement from the fabricated meta‐imager reached 93.9%, demonstrating no appreciable degradation relative to the unrotated baseline. In comparison, the digital‐only single‐layer network attained only 89.4% accuracy. The confusion matrices comparing the hybrid and digital double‐layer models are presented in Figure 4g. The substantial accuracy gap—a 4.5 percentage point advantage for the optical front end—quantifies the expressive benefit of embedding two effective convolutional layers within the single metasurface and confirms that the Bessel‐vortex basis confers inherent robustness to rotational distortions without sacrificing classification fidelity.

Having characterized the static optical performance of the meta‐imager, we next evaluated its end‐to‐end classification capabilities on a full‐color natural image benchmark. We used the CIFAR‐10 dataset, comprising ten object categories (airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, and truck) with original 32 × 32 pixel spatial resolution and three RGB color channels. Representative input images are shown in Figure 5a. For each test sample, the meta‐imager processed all three‐color channels sequentially through the same physical aperture, with the liquid‐crystal amplitude modulator applying identical kernel weights across wavelengths—a capability enabled by the broadband dispersion engineering of the Bessel‐vortex basis described previously. The resulting optically convolved feature maps (Figure 5b) exhibit pronounced edge enhancement and texture extraction that are largely invariant to the intrinsic color variations within each object category (Notes S13 and S14, Figures S14 and S15). Figure 5c presents the confusion matrices comparing the classification performance of two configurations. A purely digital single‐layer convolutional network, constrained to the same kernel size and channel count, achieves a test accuracy of only 57.5% on CIFAR‐10 (Note S15 and Figure S16). When the digital network is expanded to two convolutional layers with an equivalent number of trainable parameters, the accuracy rises substantially to 87.5%, confirming that the additional depth provides critical expressive capacity for this task. Remarkably, the full hybrid pipeline—wherein the meta‐imager replaces the digital convolutional layers and a lightweight fully connected classifier processes the resulting optical feature maps—attains 86.6% accuracy. This value approaches the performance of the two‐layer digital network while employing only a single physical metasurface aperture. The negligible 0.9 percentage‐point gap relative to the digital two‐layer baseline, contrasted with the 29.1 percentage‐point gap relative to the digital single‐layer baseline, directly quantifies the expressive power of the meta‐imager's spatiotemporal intrinsic depth: the fixed Bessel‐vortex prior layer and the tunable vortex‐matrix layer together fold two effective convolutional layers into one flat optic, delivering the accuracy of a deeper network without the corresponding digital computational burden. That classification fidelity is preserved—and indeed slightly enhanced—while offloading the dominant fraction of arithmetic to the optical domain constitutes a compelling validation of the meta‐imager's practical utility.

To rigorously assess the meta‐imager's capacity for high‐precision, domain‐specific adaptation, we turned to the SIPaKMeD dataset—a publicly available collection of high‐resolution (128 × 128 pixel) cervical cytology images annotated for five diagnostic cell categories (superficial–intermediate, parabasal, koilocytotic, dyskeratotic, and metaplastic). Accurate classification of these cellular morphologies is critical for automated Pap smear screening, yet the subtle textural and architectural cues that distinguish categories pose a formidable challenge to shallow classifiers. Representative input images and their corresponding optically convolved feature maps are displayed in Figure 5d,e, respectively. Qualitatively, the meta‐imager's Bessel‐vortex first layer amplifies diagnostically salient structures—nuclear membrane contours, chromatin granularity, and cytoplasmic boundaries—while the tunable second‐layer vortex matrix adaptively gates these features according to the learned kernel weights. Notably, the l = 2 and l = 3 channels, which implement higher‐order spatial differentiation, reveal fine textural modulations that are barely perceptible in the raw pixel data.

The classification results for SIPaKMeD are summarized in the confusion matrices of Figure 5f. A digital single‐layer convolutional network achieves a baseline accuracy of 83.6%, reflecting the inherent difficulty of the task given the constrained model capacity. Remarkably, the hybrid meta‐imager pipeline—after undergoing the closed‐loop online training procedure detailed in Figure 3—reaches a final test accuracy of 99. 3%. This 15.7 percentage‐point absolute improvement is among the highest reported for optical front‐end acceleration on a medical imaging benchmark and underscores the transformative impact of embedding two effective convolutional layers within a single physical aperture.

The convergence dynamics of online training are plotted in Figure 5g. Both simulated and experimentally measured loss trajectories exhibit rapid descent within the first five epochs, plateauing at nearly identical final cross‐entropy values. The close correspondence between simulation and experiment validates the accuracy of our optical forward model and confirms that the amplitude‐driven gradient updates, backpropagated through the APNN and applied to the LC transmission map, effectively steer the physical system toward the loss basin of the ideal digital model. Figure 5h displays the normalized 3 × 3 convolution kernel weight for all combinations of color channel (R, G, B) and polarization mode (l = 0,1,2,3). Minor channel‐dependent differences reflect residual chromatic dispersion, which is automatically compensated during online training. Finally, Figure 5i provides an intuitive visualization of the feature‐space transformation effected by the meta‐imager. t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE) projections of the raw SIPaKMeD pixel data reveal substantial inter‐class overlap, with the five diagnostic categories largely intermixed in the high‐dimensional input space (Note S16 and Figure S17). Following optical convolution, the same cell images project to well‐separated clusters with markedly improved intra‐class compactness. Collectively, these results establish the spatiotemporal intrinsic depth meta‐imager as a versatile, high‐performance front end for energy‐efficient machine vision across domains spanning natural imagery to high‐resolution medical diagnostics.

Conclusions

We have proposed and experimentally demonstrated a single‐layer metasurface imager endowed with spatiotemporal intrinsic depth—a design paradigm that collapses two complete convolutional layers into a single sub‐millimeter‐thick flat optic. By leveraging polarization‐division multiplexing, the metasurface encodes four fixed Bessel‐vortex channels (l = 0, 1, 2, 3) as an immutable first convolutional layer, with the l = 0 channel physically implementing a hardware‐native residual connection. A reconfigurable second layer is realized through a metasurface‐generated 3 × 3 optical vortex matrix, whose weights are dynamically tuned via time‐sequential liquid‐crystal amplitude modulation. This temporal differential encoding scheme resolves the long‐standing sign constraint of incoherent optical computing while uniquely enabling in situ training that compensates for nanofabrication imperfections. The broadband dispersion engineering of the Bessel‐vortex basis further sustains convolution fidelity across the broadband spectrum, extending the operational scope to full‐color imagery. Our experimental validation across three complementary benchmarks—rotation‐robust fashion‐item classification, full‐color CIFAR‐10 recognition, and high‐resolution SIPaKMeD medical cytology—demonstrates that the meta‐imager boosts single‐layer digital baseline accuracies by substantial margins (from 57.5% to 86.6% on CIFAR‐10, and from 83.6% to 99.3% on SIPaKMeD) while offloading approximately 99.7% of the multiply–accumulate operations to the optical domain. The closed‐loop online training procedure, driven by an amplitude feedback neural network, converges within a few epochs and yields learned kernels that automatically absorb static phase errors introduced during fabrication.

This work establishes a new paradigm for edge‐intelligent machine vision by demonstrating that the expressive capacity of multi‐layer diffractive networks can be folded into a monolithic metasurface through the orthogonal multiplexing dimensions of polarization and time. The decoupled amplitude–phase architecture—wherein a liquid‐crystal layer provides reconfigurable weight control and an all‐dielectric metasurface delivers high‐precision phase engineering—overcomes the efficiency–accuracy trade‐off that has constrained prior metasurface processors. More broadly, the concept of spatiotemporal intrinsic depth suggests a pathway toward ultra‐compact, task‐specific optical front ends that shift the dominant fraction of neural network inference to the passive, high‐speed physical layer. Future extensions may include scaling to larger kernel arrays, integration with advanced photodetector technologies for on‐chip polarization sorting, and co‐design with quantized digital back ends for fully integrated intelligent sensors in autonomous systems, wearable diagnostics, and augmented reality.

Metasurface Fabrication

The metasurface was fabricated on a 500‐µm‐thick fused silica (JGS2) substrate. A 750‐nm‐thick hydrogenated amorphous silicon film was deposited by plasma‐enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) at 300°C using silane and argon, achieving a deposition rate of approximately 40 nm min−1. The metasurface pattern was defined in a ZEP520A electron‐beam resist by electron‐beam lithography operating at 100 kV with an exposure dose of 270 µC/cm2. After development, a 100‐nm‐thick chromium hard mask was deposited via magnetron sputtering and subsequently patterned by lift‐off in acetone and isopropanol. The pattern was transferred into the amorphous silicon layer by inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching with a gas mixture. Finally, the residual chromium mask was removed using a commercial chromium etchant, yielding an all‐dielectric metasurface with subwavelength periodicity. Fabrication fidelity was verified by scanning electron microscopy at multiple stages.

Digital Neural Network Training

The experimental evaluation was performed on three complementary benchmarks: Fashion‐MNIST for rotation‐robustness validation, CIFAR‐10 for broadband color imaging assessment, and SIPaKMeD for online training and error‐correction verification. The meta‐imager operates with four fixed polarization‐multiplexed Bessel‐vortex channels (topological charges l = 0, 1, 2, 3), each of which is further modulated by a reconfigurable optical vortex matrix via temporally sequenced liquid‐crystal amplitude control. Positive and negative kernel weights are physically realized through differential frame acquisition, wherein two successive intensity distributions corresponding to complementary transmission maps are digitally subtracted. The hybrid optoelectronic pipeline was implemented in PyTorch with CUDA acceleration, and all digital back‐end training employed the Adam optimizer to minimize categorical cross‐entropy loss via automatic differentiation. The amplitude feedback neural network, a lightweight encoder–decoder trained offline on 4000 PSF–transmittance pairs, generated initial LC transmission maps within sub‐millisecond latency, after which online fine‐tuning proceeded for a limited number of epochs to compensate for residual fabrication errors and task‐specific feature shifts.
