Diagnostic utility of NKX2.2 and CD99 in the central nervous system to distinguish Ewing sarcoma from its mimickers
Authors: Farah Sassi, Alice Métais, Pascale Varlet, Charlotte Berthaud, Noémie Pucelle, Marion Mandoula, Zeina Dababou, Noah Carnes, Lauren Hasty, Aurélien Collard, Fabrice Chrétien, Arnault Tauziède-Espariat
Published: 2025

Ewing sarcoma (ES) is a highly aggressive bone and soft tissue tumor [1]. The latest World Health Organization's (WHO) Classification of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumors highlights the pivotal role of molecular-based tumor classification [2]. While it characterizes ES by the presence of EWSR1 rearrangements, this alteration is not included in the essential diagnostic criteria and is reserved for select cases. However, the WHO Classification of Central Nervous System (CNS) Tumors considers this marker an essential tool for identifying primary CNS ES, unlike its osseous counterparts [3]. Nevertheless, these molecular methods are expensive, time-consuming, and often restricted to specialized centers. CD99 has long been referred the diagnostic biomarker of ES [4]. However, it may lack specificity and is sometimes difficult to interpret (membranous staining only vs cytoplasmic). The transcription factor NK2 homeobox 2 (NKX2.2), a target for EWSR1 rearrangements, has recently emerged as a promising immunohistochemical (IHC) surrogate marker to distinguish ES from morphologically similar non-Ewing tumors [5, 6]. In the CNS, NKX2.2 is key in oligodendrocyte differentiation but its usefulness as a diagnostic biomarker in brain tumors has not been studied. Herein, we analyzed the sensitivity and specificity of NKX2.2 immunostaining in a large cohort of CNS tumors and assessed the additive value of CD99 co-expression.

IHC analyses were performed on 3-μm-thick formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections using anti-NKX2.2 (EP336; 1:50 dilution; Diagomics) and anti-CD99 (12E7; prediluted; Dako) antibodies. Staining was carried out using the Omnis-Automation system (Omnis). The cohort included 194 CNS tumors: 119 whole tissue sections and 75 tissue microarray (TMA) cores (with a triplicate spot for each tumor) (see Table 1). Assessment of NKX2.2 expression was based on both the intensity of nuclear staining (categorized as weak, moderate, or strong) and the percentage of positive tumor cells. NKX2.2 positivity was defined as moderate to strong nuclear staining in ≥ 5% of tumor cells [7]. Additional thresholds of ≥ 10% and ≥ 50% were also evaluated. CD99 positivity was defined by diffuse, strong membranous staining. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated for NKX2.2 alone and in combination with CD99 at each threshold. All ES cases were molecularly confirmed using RNA-sequencing analyses.

The IHC results are summarized in Table 1. Strong and diffuse nuclear staining for NKX2.2 was observed in all but one case of ES, potentially due to inadequate fixation (Fig. 1A–C). Among these, four were primary (three with EWSR1::FL1 fusion and one with EWSR1::ERG fusion) and four were metastatic (all with EWSR1::FLI1 fusion) using the three thresholds. The single NKX2.2-negative case, showing weak but diffuse staining, was a primary intracranial ES displaying strong and diffuse CD99 expression, harboring a EWSR1::FLI1 fusion. No ES case showed granular staining. Co-expression of CD99 and NKX2.2 was found in 5 out of 9 ES cases. Although NKX2.2 expression alone did not distinguish primary from metastatic ES, co-expression appeared more frequent in metastatic lesions (75% vs. 40%). As expected, NKX2.2 expression was also identified in other tumor types due to its role in glial and neuroendocrine differentiation, including metastatic neuroendocrine neoplasms, gliosarcomas, IDH-wildtype glioblastomas with primitive neuronal components (Fig. 1 D-O), tumors with BCOR alterations, and embryonal tumors with multilayered rosettes [8]. However, co-expression with CD99 in these cases was exceptional, observed in 7/194 cases (3%), one of them being a glioblastoma with a primitive neuronal component (Fig. 1 P–R). NKX2.2 alone demonstrated 89% sensitivity, whereas CD99 alone showed high specificity (92%) but lower sensitivity (67%). When both markers were combined, specificity increased to 98% but sensitivity decreased to 56% (Table 2).

This study showed, for the first time, the diagnostic relevance of NKX2.2 expression in ES within the CNS, with sensitivity and specificity in line with the soft tissue ES reported in prior studies [1, 5]. Less than 1% of ES may lack NKX2.2 expression, without any biological explanation, particularly regarding fusion type [1, 5]. Primary ES is rare in the CNS, accounting for 2% of cases of pediatric CNS series, yet constitutes a diagnostic pitfall because of several histological mimickers. Among these, embryonal tumors remain the principal differential pediatric diagnosis; especially following the recent identification of novel molecular entities such as CNS embryonal tumor with PLAGL1/2 amplification or BRD4::LEUTX fusion, CNS tumor with BCOR alterations, and CNS neuroblastoma FOXR2-activated all of which further complicate the diagnostic landscape [9]. Moreover, because samples are more frequently limited (biopsies), neuropathologists must have a robust IHC panel to better identify diagnoses. In our series, NKX2.2 and CD99 co-expression was more observed in metastatic than primary CNS ES, although the literature does not confirm a significant difference between these groups [1].

The combined use of NKX2.2 and CD99 may be relevant for the diagnosis of primary CNS ES. This combination is more relevant than CD99 alone, which may be positive in differential diagnoses (notably CIC- and BCOR-altered tumors). The NKX2.2 expression can also appear in tumors with neuronal differentiation such as glioblastomas with primitive neuronal components or neuroendocrine metastases, but these entities are not true diagnostic mimics of ES and can be excluded through clinical context, age, and additional IHC markers. In adults, CNS ES is extremely rare and typically represents metastasis from a peripheral primary lesion.

NKX2.2 specificity is imperfect, as a minority of non-Ewing small round cell neoplasms (e.g., mesenchymal chondrosarcomas or olfactory neuroblastomas) may also express NKX2.2, requiring molecular confirmation [5]. Our study found a relatively low sensitivity (56%) for combined NKX2.2 and CD99 expression compared to the 80–93% reported in larger series [6]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the small sample size of ES cases, and potential technical or biological variability in marker expression.

Accurate distinction between ES and its histologic mimics is therapeutically pivotal. ES responds to standardized multimodal regimens comprising intensive multi-agent chemotherapy followed by surgical and/or radiotherapeutic local control, whereas CIC- or BCOR-rearranged sarcomas and embryonal CNS tumors usually show poor response to these protocols thus require distinct treatment [5].

To conclude, our study reiterates that NKX2.2 is not entirely sensitive for the diagnosis of CNS ES. However, when combined with CD99, it becomes a very reliable tool for ruling out its main differential diagnoses. It should therefore be incorporated into the routine IHC panel used by neuropathologists.
