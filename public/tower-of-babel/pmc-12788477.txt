Association Between Serum Vitamin D Levels and Glycemic Status in Prediabetic Adults
Authors: Nitin Wathore, Dibyanshu, Samreen Farooqui, Tanvi Patel, Sangeeta Gupta, Mayur N. Vasava
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Vitamin D is very important for insulin secretion and sensitivity, and not getting enough of it has been related to problems with glucose metabolism. Prediabetes is a stage of glucose intolerance that can be reversed. Finding modifiable risk factors, such as a lack of vitamin D, can aid with early intervention.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional analytical investigation was done with 120 persons between the ages of 30 and 60 who had been diagnosed with prediabetes based on HbA1c readings between 5.7% and 6.4%. We used a chemiluminescent immunoassay to find out how much 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] was in the serum. We put the participants into three categories based on their vitamin D levels: deficient (<20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–29 ng/mL), and sufficient (≥30 ng/mL). We looked at glycemic indicators, such as fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance among groups.Results:Out of 120 people, 48 (40%) were low in vitamin D, 44 (36.7%) were not getting enough, and 28 (23.3%) were getting enough. The average FBG was much greater in the group that did not get enough vitamin D (112.3 ± 9.4 mg/dL) than in the group that did (102.1 ± 8.6 mg/dL) (P = 0.003). In the same way, the average HbA1c levels were 6.3% ±0.2% in the insufficient group and 5.9% ±0.3% in the sufficient group (P = 0.001). There was a strong negative connection between serum 25(OH)D and HbA1c (r = –0.42, P < 0.01).Conclusion:A lack of vitamin D is strongly linked to poor blood sugar management in persons who are prediabetic. Checking and fixing vitamin D levels may help those who are at risk of getting type 2 diabetes delay or avoid getting it.

INTRODUCTION

Prediabetes is a stage of hyperglycemia that is between normal and diabetic. It is when blood sugar levels are higher than usual but not yet in the diabetic range. This is an important time to step in since people with prediabetes are more likely to develop type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and heart problems that go along with it.[12] Finding things that can be changed at this stage can be very helpful in stopping or delaying the progression to full-blown diabetes.

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid that may change how glucose is used by the body by affecting the activity of pancreatic β-cells, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory pathways.[3] The presence of vitamin D receptors (VDRs) and the 1-α-hydroxylase enzyme in pancreatic tissues suggests that vitamin D has a role in insulin secretion.[4] Vitamin D may also help peripheral tissues respond better to insulin by controlling calcium flow and changing the inflammatory responses that cause insulin resistance.[5]

Epidemiological studies have shown that people with impaired glucose tolerance and T2DM are more likely to be deficient in vitamin D. Several reports have also shown that there is an inverse relationship between serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations and glycemic indices, like fasting glucose, HbA1c, and insulin resistance.[67]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Between January and June 2024, this cross-sectional analytical investigation took place at a tertiary care facility. The study had 120 adults between the ages of 30 and 60 who were diagnosed with prediabetes based on the American Diabetes Association’s definition of HbA1c levels between 5.7% and 6.4%. People who already had diabetes, had been using vitamin D supplements in the last three months, or had long-term problems with their kidneys, liver, or hormones were not allowed to take part. Before the study started, all participants signed a document saying they understood what was going on and gave their permission. The institutional ethics committee also gave its approval.

Blood samples from veins were taken in the morning after fasting all night. We used established enzymatic and immunoturbidimetric methods to evaluate fasting blood glucose (FBG) and HbA1c levels, respectively. A chemiluminescent immunoassay was used to look at the levels of 25(OH)D in the serum. According to the Endocrine Society’s clinical practice guidelines, vitamin D levels were grouped into three groups: deficient (<20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–29 ng/mL), and sufficient (≥30 ng/mL). We also used fasting glucose and insulin levels to figure out the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), which measures how sensitive insulin is.

IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to put together and look at the data.

RESULTS

Out of the 120 prediabetic participants included in the study, 48 individuals (40%) were found to be vitamin D deficient, 44 (36.7%) had insufficient levels, and 28 (23.3%) were categorized as vitamin D sufficient. The mean age of participants was 47.3 ± 8.6 years, with a slight female predominance (54.2%). Significant differences in glycemic parameters were observed across the three vitamin D categories.

Table 1 presents the comparison of FBG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR among the three vitamin D groups. Participants with sufficient vitamin D levels had lower mean FBG (101.8 ± 7.9 mg/dL) compared to those with insufficient (107.6 ± 8.2 mg/dL) and deficient levels (112.5 ± 9.1 mg/dL) (P < 0.01). Similarly, mean HbA1c was lowest in the sufficient group (5.9% ± 0.2%), increasing progressively in the insufficient (6.1% ± 0.3%) and deficient groups (6.3 ± 0.2%) (P < 0.001). HOMA-IR values followed a similar trend, indicating higher insulin resistance in individuals with lower vitamin D levels [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Vitamin D may impact the release of insulin by interacting with VDRs on pancreatic β-cells. This could lead to more insulin being made and less β-cell malfunction.[1] Vitamin D can also change the levels of calcium outside and within cells, which is important for processes that are controlled by insulin.[2] Vitamin D also has anti-inflammatory effects that may help with low-grade chronic inflammation that happens with insulin resistance, which may help insulin work better in peripheral tissues.[3]

The results of this study are supported by several others that came before it. Kayaniyil et al. did a cross-sectional investigation that showed that higher levels of serum 25(OH)D were linked to lower levels of insulin resistance, as evaluated by HOMA-IR, regardless of body mass index.[4567] It is crucial to remember, though, that the link between vitamin D levels and blood sugar regulation is still complicated and may be affected by other factors, like exercise, food, and sun exposure.[7] The current study took body mass index into account, although there is still a chance of residual confounding. Also, the fact that the study was cross-sectional makes it hard to prove that there is a cause-and-effect link.

CONCLUSION

Vitamin D deficiency is significantly associated with poor glycemic control and increased insulin resistance in prediabetic adults. Early identification and correction of low vitamin D levels may help prevent progression to type 2 diabetes.
