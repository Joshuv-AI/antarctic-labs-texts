SmellNet : A Large-scale Dataset for Real-world Smell Recognition
Dewei Feng, Carol Li, Wei Dai, Paul Pu Liang

Abstract
The ability of AI to sense and identify various substances based on their smell alone can have profound impacts on allergen detection (e.g., smelling gluten or peanuts in a cake), monitoring the manufacturing process, and sensing hormones that indicate emotional states, stress levels, and diseases. Despite these broad impacts, there are virtually no large-scale benchmarks, and therefore little progress, for training and evaluating AI systems’ ability to smell in the real world. In this paper, we use portable gas and chemical sensors to create SmellNet , the first large-scale database that digitizes a diverse range of smells in the natural world. SmellNet contains about 180,000 time steps of 50 substances (spanning nuts, spices, herbs, fruits, and vegetables) with 50 hours of data. Using SmellNet , we train AI models for real-time classification of substances based on their smell alone. Our best methods leverage sequence models, contrastive learning to integrate high-resolution Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry molecular data, and a new temporal difference method that identifies sharp changes in sensor readings. Our best models achieve up to 65.35% accuracy on pre-recorded data, and generalize to real-world conditions with 10.71% accuracy on nuts and 25.38% on spices in the challenging 50-way online classification task. Despite these promising results, SmellNet highlights many technical challenges in building AI for smell, including richer feature learning, on-edge smell models, and robustness to environmental changes.

Advancements in AI have revolutionized how machines perceive and interact with the world. However, almost all current progress has been limited to the text, visual, and auditory modalities
[
29
,
30
]
. The human sense of smell plays a crucial role in environmental perception, social interaction, and regulating well-being. Similarly, AI that can recognize smell can revolutionize the entertainment, e-commerce, manufacturing, and food and beverage industries
[
10
,
51
]
. More ambitiously, smell-based diagnostics can help in early disease detection, (e.g., COVID-19)
[
26
]
, and even ‘smelling’ hormones and indicators of emotional states, stress levels, and early prognosis of cancer
[
48
,
56
]
.

Of course, smell is a new data modality for AI, with little progress compared to computer vision and natural language processing. We believe that large-scale data and real-time AI models are key for learning rich feature representations of smell for accurate sensing, classification, and feature fusion between smell and other human senses.
This strategy differs from past research, which has emphasized feature engineering, small datasets, and simple classification models
[
1
,
16
,
28
]
, and processing pre-recorded smell data collected via large chemistry lab equipment
[
41
,
23
,
49
,
27
]
, which do not work in real-time.
As a step towards real-world and real-time smell sensing, we present
SmellNet
, a large-scale database of how food, beverages, and natural objects smell in the natural world.
SmellNet
is collected by applying portable sensors to 50 substances (nuts, spices, herbs, fruits, and vegetables) across 50 hours of data. With 180,000 time step readings across many environmental conditions,
SmellNet
is the largest and most diverse open-source smell dataset to date, enabling the training of AI that can classify substances based on their smell alone.

Using
SmellNet
, we investigate various preprocessing, modeling, and training methods for sensory data. Our best methods leverage the strength of long-range sequence models, contrastive learning to leverage high-resolution Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) molecular knowledge, and a new relative temporal difference method that helps identify sharp changes in sensor readings compared to the ambient environment. Our best models achieve up to 65.35% Top-1 accuracy in offline settings, and generalize to real-world conditions with 10.71% accuracy on nuts and 25.38% on spices in the challenging 50-way online classification task.
Despite these promising results, there remain key technical challenges to be tackled. Overall performance remains substantially lower than human-level accuracy, with AI systems frequently struggling in real-world conditions that deviate from controlled training environments (e.g., shifts in time, temperature, sensor location, crowd activity, and airflow).
Our analysis yields tangible directions for future work in both technical AI methods for smell sensing and tackling their real-time, robust, and efficient sensing deployment.
SmellNet
is publicly available at
https://github.com/MIT-MI/SmellNet
, which we believe can catalyze new methods for wide-ranging applications in healthcare diagnosis and interventions, the food and beverage industries, environment and manufacturing monitoring, and gaming and entertainment.

Theoretical Basis and Related Work

While large-scale AI for smell is completely unexplored, we are inspired by human smell sensing, the chemistry and biology of smell, and using AI to process small-scale smell data.

Human smell receptors
: Olfaction, the sense of smell, allows for the detection and discrimination of odors in the environment
[
44
]
. The human nose can detect and discriminate between an estimated trillion different odors, even in minute quantities
[
7
]
. This makes the human olfactory system the largest, in terms of the number and diversity of receptors, allowing for the sensing of a vast chemical landscape
[
42
]
. Human olfactory perception functions through a combinatorial code, where each odorant molecule binds to a specific set of olfactory receptors (ORs) in the nose
[
32
]
. This binding converts chemical information into electrical signals which are perceived in the brain
[
14
]
.

Smell sensors
for perceiving smell have been developed, including chemical compositions of gases based on the principles of molecular interaction and chemical potential equilibrium
[
6
]
. These sensors can employ different scientific strategies to detect and analyze gas molecules, including those based on semiconducting materials like metal oxides
[
35
]
, electrochemical sensors that generate a current proportional to the gas concentration
[
4
]
, optical sensors based on different gases absorbing different wavelengths
[
21
]
, and conductive polymers that change their conductivity when exposed to gas molecules
[
34
]
. We use gas sensors due to their portability, although all sensors can suffer challenges due to sensitivity, environmental interference, reproducibility, and device calibration
[
45
,
54
]
.

AI for smell sensing:
There has been some work in using technology to process pre-recorded smell data, but these systems are not portable or work in real-time. These include graph neural networks trained to classify smell chemical molecules (especially pre-recorded GC-MS data)
[
41
,
23
,
49
,
27
]
, but they require data collection via large chemistry lab equipment rather than portable sensors. Past research has also emphasized human domain knowledge, feature engineering, small datasets, and simple classification models rather than large-scale data-driven learning
[
1
,
16
,
28
]
.
Electronic noses have been designed to monitor pollutants and for air quality assessment
[
3
,
37
]
, but they are not generally applicable for any type of smell. Methods to classify biological olfactory data of mice and humans have also been proposed
[
13
,
52
]
, but do not enable portable real-time sensing.

Creating
SmellNet

3.1
A portable and real-time smell sensor

We use a series of portable smell sensors that detect concentrations of various gases and atmospheric factors. Specifically, a WSP2110 semiconductor flat-surfaced gas sensor detects volatile organic compounds such as benzene, acetone, and toluene. BME680 is a compact and portable gas sensor that integrates gas, pressure, humidity, and temperature sensing, using a metal-oxide (MOX) gas sensor to accurately measure air quality. MQ-X sensors offer broad sensitivity to alcohol vapor, ethanol, LPG, natural gas, and carbon monoxide by leveraging
\text{SnO}_{2}
- based chemiresistive sensing. MP503 responds to ammonia, hydrogen, and other household gases. Finally, the Grove Multichannel Gas Sensor V2 provides qualitative gas detection for
\text{NO}_{2}
, CO, and ethanol through four independent MOX sensing channels. Together, these compounds are present in many odors found in food, drinks, and other common substances. Measuring ambient factors further helps to normalize the effect of potentially confounding environmental variables. Figure
2(a)
illustrates the circuit diagram of our sensor, and all the hardware used to create the sensing devices is included in Appendix
A.1
.

3.2
Smell sensing data collection

Figure 3:
Data collection pipeline:
Each ingredient undergoes six 10-minute sensing sessions across different days, using a controlled environment to minimize external noise. During each session, 12-channel gas sensor data is recorded at 1 Hz and labeled with the ingredient identity, collection time, and associated metadata. We further pair each ingredient with high-resolution GC-MS data to enable multimodal learning. This setup enables the creation of a structured and temporally rich dataset for representation learning of smells.

We deployed these smell sensors on 50 substances divided into 5 classes: nuts, spices, herbs, fruits, and vegetables. Figure
2(b)
shows the full taxonomy of substances in each category. For each substance, we used the sensor to collect data for a 10-minute session, repeated 6 times across different days. 10 minutes is the time it takes for the gas concentration and environmental factors to reach a stable condition. Each session was done in a controlled environment to minimize environmental factors. At the end of each session, we clear out the air in the controlled environment so that the environment returns to atmospheric conditions for the next session. Figure
3
illustrates the overall data collection pipeline. This gives us 1 hour of sensor readings for each substance and 50 hours of data in total. During each session, 12-channel gas sensor data is recorded at 1 Hz, which gives us 180,000 total datapoints. Our sensing data is labeled with the name of the substance, its detailed description, and the time and date of collection. Pairing sensor data with language labels and descriptions can enable future research on cross-modal retrieval between smell and language.

3.3
Pairing with GC-MS

One potential limitation of sensor data is its low resolution, which stems from the quality of the sensors used. We therefore paired this data with preexisting open-source pre-recorded GC-MS data
[
25
]
. GC-MS devices are large, bulky, and non-portable, but can detect the exact concentration of various compounds in a substance at a high resolution. As a result, pairing gas sensing data with GC-MS readings provides a natural combination of complementary information for this task and allows for studies of multimodal fusion and cross-modal learning
[
30
]
.

4
Developing AI Models for Smell

Developing AI for smell is a challenging and unstudied problem. The AI models we develop need to satisfy the following properties:

1.

Learn rich temporal features:
The features that contribute to how substances smell are largely unknown. Therefore, instead of relying on hand-engineered features
[
11
,
31
,
8
,
33
]
, we apply learning-based architectures with non-temporal linear classifiers and neural networks, and temporal LSTMs, Transformers, and time-series analysis methods.

2.

Learn from limited data:
Due to manual collection, smell sensor data is limited and low-resolution. This calls for machine learning methods that work on small datasets or leverage larger banks of offline data (e.g., language descriptions of smells from the internet or higher resolution chemical sensors such as GC-MS). We therefore include methods based on pre-training and contrastive learning to supplement sensor data with richer offline representations.

3.

Handle noisy environmental factors:
Smell data is inherently noisy and sensitive to external environmental factors. We include methods for handling noise, including robust preprocessing methods and normalization strategies to measure relative readings to ambient environments.

4.1
Problem setup and notation

We aim to develop AI models that can classify substances based on their smell profiles, using sensor readings and optionally incorporating GC-MS supervision. Each substance is represented as a multi-channel time series
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
, where
T\approx 600
is the number of time steps and
d=12
is the number of sensor channels capturing various gas concentrations and environmental factors. The model processes the sequence
x
to produce an embedding
z\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
, where
h
is the hidden dimension of the learned representation.

The classification task maps the sequence
x
to a categorical label
y\in\{1,2,\ldots,50\}
corresponding to one of 50 substance classes. In the cross-modal setting, each substance is also associated with a GC-MS vector
g\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, where
d^{\prime}=17
denotes the dimensionality of atomic element counts derived from high-resolution chemical analysis. During training, we explore both supervised learning objectives (minimizing cross-entropy loss between the predicted class and ground truth label
y
) and contrastive learning objectives that align sensor embeddings
z
with GC-MS embeddings
g
of the same ingredient. At test time, only gas sensor readings
x
are available and used for inference, regardless of whether the model was trained using unimodal or cross-modal supervision.

Figure 6:
Overview of the models used in this study. (a) Raw multi-channel time series data is collected using a portable smell sensor as it samples an input substance (e.g., cashew). The data is optionally transformed using first-order temporal differences to emphasize signal dynamics. In parallel, high-resolution GC-MS data can be retrieved from an online database to provide chemical supervision.
(b) In supervised learning, sensor data (raw or preprocessed) is passed through classification models trained to predict the correct substance among 50 classes.
(c) In contrastive learning, paired sensor and GC-MS representations are aligned through modality-specific encoders. The resulting similarity scores rank the substance for prediction.
(d) Our framework supports multiple model types—MLPs, Transformers, and LSTMs—each capable of ingesting either raw or temporally differenced sensor inputs to perform classification or representation learning.

4.2
Sensor data preprocessing

With these desiderata in mind, we have two preprocessing methods.

Absolute sensor readings

As evidenced by the PCA projections in Figure
4
, raw sensor values alone already form distinct clusters across ingredients and categories, indicating that a meaningful signal exists even without preprocessing. This suggests that a well-trained model should be capable of distinguishing substances directly from the absolute gas concentration values at each time step.

First-order temporal difference (FOTD)

In addition to raw sensor readings, we apply a preprocessing method called
first-order temporal difference
to capture relative changes in sensors over time. For each sensor channel
x_{t}
, we compute the difference over a fixed period
p
samples as:

\Delta x_{t}=x_{t}-x_{t-p},\forall t>p
.

This approach emphasizes local dynamics, suppresses background drift, and normalizes across sessions. Inspired by classical time series and signal processing techniques, similar delta-based methods have been used in wearable and EEG sensing
[
46
,
55
]
, but this is the first application to real-time smell data.

4.3
Model architectures

We now summarize the learning architectures implemented on
SmellNet
, which span non-temporal and temporal paradigms, including time-series analysis baselines. Each model is trained to classify substances based on raw or preprocessed sensor sequences.

Non-temporal models

We begin with strong baselines that ignore temporal order and treat each time step independently. These include logistic regression and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) applied to individual frames or pooled representations of time series. Our MLP-based encoder consists of stacked linear layers with LayerNorm, SiLU activation, and dropout
[
40
]
.

Temporal models

We also implement models that explicitly capture temporal dynamics in the sensor streams. The LSTM-based model processes the time series with a unidirectional LSTM, then applies mean pooling followed by a projection head and final classifier
[
20
]
. The Transformer-based model uses a stack of Transformer encoder layers with multi-head self-attention, followed by adaptive average pooling and classification
[
50
]
. Both architectures benefit from temporal modeling and are trained on the full sequences using cross-entropy loss.

4.4
Training objectives

Based on these base architectures, we also explore several training objectives, ranging from direct supervised learning to incorporating external knowledge to supplement limited sensor data.

Supervised learning from gas sensors

Our first goal is to determine whether there is sufficient signal in the sensed smell data to support training supervised learning models for substance classification. We formulate this as a multi-class classification task with a softmax objective over 50 substance classes. Specifically, the model is trained to minimize cross-entropy loss over a 50-way softmax output layer, where each class corresponds to a unique substance in the dataset.

Robust training

Supervised training on smell data collected in controlled environments often fails to generalize when deployed in real-world settings, such as outdoor environments, across seasonal changes, or in the presence of human crowds. By including measurements of ambient environmental factors (e.g., temperature, humidity, pressure) in
SmellNet
, we can study and address this distribution shift directly. We adopt several training-time perturbations and augmentations: introducing synthetic noise to sensor channels and randomly dropping time steps to simulate sensor failure or latency. These techniques aim to force the model to learn signal patterns that generalize across nuisance factors, ultimately improving deployment-time reliability.

Cross-modal learning with gas sensors and GC-MS

Gas sensors are portable and provide real-time readings, but their resolution is limited. GC-MS devices are large, bulky, and non-portable, but can detect the exact concentration of various compounds in a substance at a high resolution, and there is rich pre-recorded data on the internet
[
15
,
24
,
53
]
. As a result, cross-modal knowledge alignment from GC-MS to gas sensors during training can help leverage complementary information to make more accurate and robust predictions
[
30
]
. One common way of performing cross-modal alignment is through contrastive learning of gas sensor representations with GC-MS representations
[
39
,
43
]
. During training, we bring together the embeddings of gas sensor and GC-MS readings for the same substance (positive pairs) and push apart the embeddings for different substances (negative pairs). During testing, only the embeddings for gas sensor readings are used, and GC-MS is no longer needed since it is not portable. Formally, given a batch of
N
gas sensor embeddings
z_{i}^{(s)}
and their corresponding GC-MS embeddings
z_{i}^{(g)}
, we minimize a symmetric contrastive loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_{i}^{(s)},z_{i}^{(g)})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_{i}^{(s)},z_{j}^{(g)})/\tau)}+\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_{i}^{(g)},z_{i}^{(s)})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_{i}^{(g)},z_{j}^{(s)})/\tau)}\right],

(1)

where
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity and
\tau
is a temperature hyperparameter.

5
Experiments

We aim to evaluate our models under both supervised and cross-modal settings using the
SmellNet
dataset. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:

RQ1:

How well can we predict substances based on gas sensor smell data alone? Which AI architectures and training objectives work best?

RQ2:

Can we enhance model performance using cross-modal learning from GC-MS? What are the limitations and tradeoffs of this approach?

RQ3:

How well do these models generalize to real-world settings with noise, environmental variation, and unseen examples?

5.1
Experimental setup

We evaluate all models on
SmellNet
across multiple substance categories and test conditions.
We report both top-1 and top-5 accuracy on the full 50-class task, as well as real-time and subset evaluations (e.g., nuts, spices).
Each model is trained using five 10-minute sequences for each substance. For evaluation, we test on held-out data stratified by substance. Architectures include non-temporal baselines (MLP), temporal models (LSTM, Transformer), and non-temporal models trained with contrastive or translation objectives using GC-MS supervision. Figure
6
shows the pipeline of model evaluation and Figure
1
shows the real-life evaluation process. Detailed hyperparameters are provided in Appendix
F
.

We also evaluate models under two primary settings: offline testing and online testing. Offline testing refers to evaluations performed on held-out data collected under the same controlled environment as training, simulating ideal deployment conditions. In contrast, online testing evaluates models on data collected in a different session or day, often under varying environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity, air composition), simulating real-world, time-shifted deployment. This distinction allows us to rigorously assess both the learning capacity and real-time generalization ability of our models.

[
12
]
. In contrast, LSTMs are more parameter-efficient and better suited for learning from small, structured sequences.

5.3
RQ2: Cross-modal learning results

We also investigate how cross-modal learning contributes to classification performance. As shown in Table
2
, the choice of learning objective plays a critical role. When using contrastive learning with GC-MS data during training, the model’s performance improves substantially across all evaluation settings. This improvement likely stems from contrastive learning’s ability to align gas sensor and GC-MS representations in a shared latent space, allowing the model to capture cross-modal similarities without requiring explicit reconstruction.

In contrast, GC-MS prediction—where the model is trained to directly predict GC-MS vectors as an auxiliary task—performs worse than even the no-GC-MS baseline. We hypothesize that this is due to the increased difficulty of the translation task: directly regressing to high-dimensional GC-MS vectors is more challenging than learning to discriminate between latent embeddings. Given the limited data available in our setting, this complexity can lead to overfitting and ineffective supervision, ultimately harming classification accuracy.

samples). Performance drops notably in this real-world setting due to distribution shifts in environmental conditions. However, applying FOTD significantly improves robustness across all models, especially in the spices category. Human performance is included as a baseline reference.

5.4
RQ3: Real-world online performance

One challenge we identify is temporal degradation in model performance. As we see in Table
3
, the online performance degrades compared to the offline experiments in Table
1
and Table
2
. When models are evaluated on data collected one week after training, accuracy drops significantly. This degradation is likely due to shifts in environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, and ambient air quality, which can affect the absolute sensor readings and lead to domain mismatch.

However, we find that applying FOTD preprocessing substantially mitigates this effect. By modeling relative changes in sensor signals rather than absolute values, the model becomes less sensitive to ambient shifts that occur at the start of sensing. These relative differences remain informative even as environmental baselines change, enabling more robust generalization over time. This explains the large performance gains observed when using FOTD preprocessing, especially in real-world test settings. Thus, we can conclude that although the performance for offline data might not be significant, FOTD is helpful for online testing.

Furthermore, we included a human baseline for evaluating our model during online testing because it provides a meaningful reference for real-world performance. Human performance was obtained by having 5 participants smell the substances in a blind test before guessing the substance out of the same 50 categories (see detailed experiment setup in Appendix
F
). This task is even difficult for humans, with only 6.67% for nuts classification and 25% for spices classification. Perhaps surprisingly, our AI models can outperform human perception for nuts, and spices when using the FOTD method.

5.5
Error analysis

As shown in Figure
7
, model accuracy varies significantly across substances, ranging from 96% for angelica to just 9% for mint. Figure
5
helps explain this disparity. Angelica exhibits a large change in
\text{NO}_{2}
concentration over time, resulting in substantial non-zero values when FOTD is applied. In contrast, mint shows minimal variation for
p=25
, likely because it emits less
\text{NO}_{2}
overall. This highlights that model performance is closely tied to the magnitude and dynamics of gas release for each substance. To address this variability, future work could explore fusion models that integrate both raw sensor readings and temporal difference features.

Another key source of errors is the lack of robustness to real-world conditions. While temporal models such as LSTMs and Transformers generally outperform non-temporal baselines, their robustness profiles differ under perturbation. The Transformer model retains more of its performance when faced with noisy or partially missing data, particularly when paired with gradient-based preprocessing. In contrast, the LSTM shows greater sensitivity to missing features, potentially due to its reliance on complete temporal continuity. We show more detailed results and analysis in Appendix
G
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
SmellNet
, the first large-scale dataset designed to enable real-time machine learning for smell recognition. By combining low-cost portable gas sensors with structured chemical data from GC-MS,
SmellNet
provides a data foundation for olfactory AI. Our experiments show the importance of FOTD to ambient conditions and that contrastive learning with GC-MS supervision boosts accuracy while retaining real-time inference. Future work can expand the dataset to cover broader chemical spaces, incorporate additional sensory modalities such as vision and language, and improve models for deployment in low-latency embedded systems for real-world environmental sensing, digital health, food safety, and multisensory perception.

A.2
Controlled environment

To ensure consistency and minimize external interference during data collection, all sensing sessions were conducted in a controlled environment. During each 10-minute recording interval, we placed both the food sample and the sensor array inside a transparent container. This enclosure prevented environmental factors such as airflow, human movement, or ambient contaminants from affecting the sensor readings. The container allowed gas emitted from the food to accumulate and diffuse evenly, while shielding the sensors from external disturbances such as changes in ambient composition caused by people walking nearby. Between sessions, we ventilated the enclosure to restore ambient conditions and eliminate residual smells from previous trials.

Despite these precautions, certain ambient factors—such as temperature, humidity, and background NO
2
levels—varied across different days and could not be entirely eliminated. These fluctuations are inherent to real-world settings and contribute to the distribution shift observed in our online testing performance. As such, they highlight the importance of building AI models that are robust to environmental variability, particularly for deployment in unconstrained conditions.

Appendix E
More Training Objectives

Translation loss

Other than cross entropy and contrastive loss, we also tried another way via translation, using smell to predict GC-MS as an auxiliary objective during training
[
38
,
47
]
while also predicting the classification label. Since this objective is not applied during testing, we do not need GC-MS during testing.

Formally, given a gas sensor input
x
with corresponding GC-MS target
y^{(g)}
and classification label
y^{(c)}
, we define the translation loss as a weighted sum of a regression loss and classification loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{translation}}=\lambda\cdot\underbrace{\|f_{\text{gcms}}(x)-y^{(g)}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{translation}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(f_{\text{class}}(x),\ y^{(c)})}_{\text{classification}}

(2)

where
f_{\text{gcms}}
is the regression head that predicts GC-MS features,
f_{\text{class}}
is the classification head, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
controls the tradeoff between the two objectives.

Appendix F
Experimental Setup

F.1
Human benchmark experiment setup

To establish a human benchmark, we recruited five undergraduate volunteers from our campus. Each participant was blindfolded and asked to smell and identify a series of food samples. For each trial, we randomly sampled 12 nut ingredients (with replacement) from a predefined pool of 10 distinct nuts. All participants received the same set of 10 samples in randomized order. For each sample, the participant selected a label from a fixed list of 50 possible ingredient names, without being told the category of any item. No feedback was given, and participants were not allowed to revisit previous choices. This setup required volunteers to perform a 50-way classification task based solely on olfactory cues, without prior training or contextual information. After the experiment on nuts, we repeat the same experiment on spices. The results are recorded in Table
3
.

F.2
Online testing limitations

We excluded herbs, fruits, and vegetables from online and human testing due to sample degradation—many of the items used in offline sessions had spoiled by the time of testing.

Table 16:
Comparison of training runtime, standard deviation, and maximum GPU memory usage across models and augmentation settings.

We compare training runtime, variability, and memory usage across model variants and augmentation strategies. As shown in Table
16
, Transformer and LSTM models exhibit the shortest training times, with Transformers completing training in under 90 seconds on average. In contrast, GC-MS contrastive learning models take significantly longer (over 600 seconds), reflecting the added complexity of chemical input processing. FOTD tends to reduce runtime across most architectures, particularly for GC-MS and translation models. Random feature drops and added noise introduce varying levels of overhead, with some configurations—such as added noise in LSTM—resulting in substantially increased variability. Despite these differences, memory usage remains constant across all models at 532 MiB, indicating efficient memory management or constraints by the hardware.
