Predicting STAS in peripheral stage I lung adenocarcinoma: the incremental value of CT-based tumor disappearance rate, with a focus on part-solid nodules
Authors: Haoran Du, Yugang Ji, Cong Liu, Dapeng Yao, Chao Jia, Lele Wang, Xiuqing Xue, Xiaofeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor spread through air spaces (STAS) is a critical prognostic factor in peripheral stage I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and substantially influences surgical decision-making. However, accurate preoperative prediction of STAS remains challenging. This study aimed to investigate the value of computed tomography (CT)-derived tumor disappearance rate (TDR) as a novel imaging biomarker for STAS prediction, and to evaluate the incremental benefit of combining TDR with consolidation tumor ratio (CTR) in part-solid nodules.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 244 patients with peripheral stage I LUAD who underwent surgical resection at Xuzhou Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University between January 2022 and December 2023. The multivariate generalized additive model (GAM) was employed to characterize the adjusted dose-response relationship between TDR and STAS, with threshold effect analysis performed via the log-likelihood ratio test. The predictive performance of TDR, CTR, and the combined TDR-CTR index (COM) was compared using ROC curves, DeLong test, and bootstrap resampling validation.ResultsSTAS was identified in 51 (20.9%) patients. The STAS-positive group exhibited a significantly lower TDR compared with the STAS-negative group (0.26 +/- 0.32 vs. 0.51 +/- 0.44, P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified TDR and spiculation sign as independent risk factors for STAS. TDR demonstrated a non-linear association with STAS (P < 0.001), with a threshold value of 0.610. In the part-solid nodule subgroup, the combined TDR-CTR index achieved an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.654–0.932), significantly outperforming CTR alone (AUC = 0.525, P = 0.040). Bootstrap validation confirmed favorable stability. While promising in this exploratory subgroup analysis, further validation in larger cohorts is warranted due to the wide confidence interval of the combined TDR-CTR index.ConclusionsCT-derived TDR is a valuable biomarker for preoperative STAS risk stratification in peripheral stage I LUAD. The combined TDR-CTR index significantly enhances STAS prediction in part-solid nodules, addressing the limitations of CTR alone and supporting individualized surgical planning.

Background

The widespread implementation of low-dose computed tomography (CT) screening has substantially increased the detection of pulmonary nodules, enabling approximately 81% of lung cancers to be diagnosed at stage I [1, 2]. Surgical resection remains the standard treatment for peripheral stage I lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [3]. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of stage I lung cancer poses a significant clinical challenge, with a 5-year recurrence rate approaching 30% after surgery and a 5-year overall survival rate of only 68.4% [4–6]. Among the factors contributing to this heterogeneity, tumor spread through air spaces (STAS) is particularly important. The presence of STAS is associated with markedly reduced 3-year recurrence-free survival (82.1% vs. 95.9%, P < 0.001) [7]. Importantly, STAS not only affects prognosis but also influences surgical approach selection, with lobectomy recommended over sublobar resection for STAS-positive cases [8]. Preoperative prediction of STAS risk can therefore guide resection planning and improve patient outcomes.

Although intraoperative frozen section analysis represents the primary modality for STAS detection, its diagnostic reliability remains controversial. For stage I tumors, frozen section exhibits a sensitivity of only 55.1% [9], rendering it unreliable for preoperative planning [10]. The consolidation tumor ratio (CTR), derived from CT imaging, has been explored as a preoperative imaging biomarker [11]. Our previous investigation demonstrated that CTR is significantly associated with STAS risk in peripheral stage IA LUAD, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.24 (P = 0.015) [12]. However, CTR is limited by poor inter-observer consistency and a lack of pathological specificity, as the radiologically defined solid component may represent non-invasive changes, while some invasive components may not appear solid on CT [13]. Identifying a supplementary CT indicator that is more objective, such as TDR which relies on a simpler binary threshold of mediastinal visibility, and reflects distinct pathological features is therefore critical for improving preoperative STAS prediction. Although the tumor disappearance rate (TDR) measured on CT has shown promise for predicting recurrence [14, 15], its predictive value for STAS in peripheral stage I LUAD remains unclear.

The present study applied the generalized additive model (GAM) to characterize the dose-response relationship between CT-derived TDR and STAS in peripheral stage I LUAD, incorporating trend testing and threshold effect analysis based on confounder-adjusted regression models. Given that CTR is widely adopted in clinical practice, we additionally constructed a combined TDR-CTR model to determine whether this combination provides incremental predictive value over CTR alone, particularly in part-solid nodules.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This retrospective study analyzed consecutive patients with malignant lung neoplasms who underwent surgical resection at Xuzhou Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University between January 2022 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) single tumor lesion; (2) peripheral lesion located in the outer one-third of the lung field on axial chest CT, with the tumor center within this region [16]; (3) maximum tumor diameter < = 4 cm on chest CT lung window, with no lymph nodes exhibiting a shortest diameter > = 10 mm on the mediastinal window; and (4) pathologically confirmed adenocarcinoma after surgery. Exclusion criteria were: (1) absence of 0.625–1 mm thin-slice imaging or incomplete imaging or clinical data on preoperative chest CT; (2) prior anti-tumor therapy (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy) before surgery; (3) history of malignant tumors within the preceding 3 years; (4) suspected tumor metastasis on preoperative examination; (5) interval exceeding one week between CT examination and surgery; or (6) removal of fewer than six lymph nodes during surgery [17, 18]. Figure 1 shows the flow chart of the study population. The primary endpoint was pathologically confirmed STAS, defined as the presence of tumor cells within air spaces in the lung parenchyma beyond the tumor edge [19].

Flow chart of the study population

Clinical data were retrieved from medical records, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease), family history, and history of pulmonary disease. Smoking status was classified as never for individuals who had never smoked or had quit for more than 10 years, and ever for all other cases [20]. TDR was designated as the exposure variable and calculated as: TDR = 1 - DM/DL, where DM represents the maximum diameter of the solid component in the mediastinal window and DL represents the maximum tumor diameter in the lung window. CT window settings were: lung window, width 1000–2000 HU, level − 500 to -700 HU; mediastinal window, width 350–600 HU, level 30–60 HU. Potential confounding covariates evaluated included sex, age, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, family history, history of pulmonary disease, lesion location, lobulation, spiculation, vacuole, pleural indentation, internal vascular sign, and bronchial abnormality, based on previously reported risk factors [12, 21].

Image evaluation and measurements

Two chest radiologists (C.J., with 12 years of experience in thoracic oncology, and L.L.W., with 6 years of experience in thoracic oncology) independently evaluated and measured CTR and TDR. Any discrepancies in qualitative assessments were resolved by consensus with the senior radiologist (C.L., 6 years of experience). For quantitative measurements (DL, DM), the mean value of the two independent measurements was used for analysis. Interobserver agreement for TDR and CTR was assessed using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). All measurements were performed on 0.625–1 mm thin-slice CT axial lung window images.

Qualitative CT features evaluated included location, lobulation, spiculation, vacuole, pleural indentation, internal vascular sign, and bronchial abnormality. Diagnostic criteria were as follows: lobulation was defined as multiple arched surfaces on the nodule resembling petals; spiculation was defined as radiating, unbranched, straight, sharp lines extending from the nodule edge; vacuole was defined as a translucent area within the lesion measuring < 5 mm; pleural indentation was defined as fibrous traction forming a triangular or trumpet-shaped water-density area on the visceral pleura without thickening or adhesion; internal vascular signs were classified into four types [21]; and bronchial abnormality was classified into three types based on the presence and morphology of bronchial branches within the nodule.

Quantitative parameters included: (a) maximum tumor diameter (DL) in the lung window; (b) maximum solid component diameter (DM) in the mediastinal window; (c) CTR, calculated as the ratio of the maximum diameter of the solid component of the tumor to the maximum diameter of the tumor in the lung window, where pure ground-glass nodules were assigned CTR = 0, solid nodules CTR = 1, and part-solid nodules CTR between 0 and 1 [22]; and (d) TDR = 1 - DM/DL (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean +/- standard deviation or median with interquartile range, based on the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and proportions. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. We also utilized the ICC to determine the agreement in feature values between the observers in 30 randomly selected patients. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Covariates were included as potential confounders if they altered the TDR estimate for STAS by more than 10% or were significantly associated with STAS. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF < = 5). The GAM smooth curve was plotted after adjusting for age, sex, smoking and size, and threshold effects were analyzed via the log-likelihood ratio test. The combined TDR-CTR index was derived from the linear predictor (log-odds) of a binary logistic regression model that included both TDR and CTR as continuous variables in the part-solid subgroup. The performance of this index was then evaluated using ROC curve analysis, with pairwise comparisons between different models performed using DeLong’s test. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (B = 1000). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the combined TDR‑CTR index. We also utilized the ICC to determine the agreement in feature values between the observers in 30 randomly selected patients. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.4.3, https://www.r-project.org; packages: glmnet, pROC, rms, dca) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 244 patients were enrolled, including 51 (20.9%) with STAS-positive and 193 with STAS-negative disease. The inter-observer ICCs and 95% CIs of the TDR and CTR were 0.903 (0.797–0.954) and 0.843 (0.674–0.925), whereas the intra-observer ICCs were 0.997(0.003–0.009) and (0.009-1.000). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the STAS-negative group, the STAS-positive group had a significantly lower TDR (0.26 +/- 0.32 vs. 0.51 +/- 0.44, P < 0.001). Other significantly different variables included smoking history, longest tumor diameter, solid component size, CTR, nodule type, and history of pulmonary disease (all P < 0.05). Among CT semantic features, lobulation, spiculation, vacuole, pleural indentation, and internal vascular sign were also significantly associated with STAS positivity (all P < 0.05).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis for STAS

Threshold effect analysis revealed a significant non-linear association between TDR and STAS after adjusting for age, sex, smoking and size, as visualized by the smooth fitting curve (Fig. 3), with a threshold value of 0.610 (Table 4, P < 0.001). Below the threshold, each unit increase in TDR was associated with an OR of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.05–2.05, P = 0.028) for STAS; above the threshold, the OR was 0.30 (95% CI: 0.21–0.49, P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis in tumors < = 3 cm further demonstrated a linear negative correlation between TDR and STAS.

Threshold effect analysis of TDR on STAS

In the part-solid nodule subgroup (n = 39), STAS was present in 13 patients (33.3%). ROC curves were plotted for TDR, CTR, and their combined index (Fig. 4). The combined TDR-CTR index achieved an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.654–0.932), which was significantly superior to CTR alone (AUC = 0.525, 95% CI: 0.314–0.736, P = 0.040). At the optimal cut-off value of 0.248, the combined index yielded a sensitivity of 1.00 and a specificity of 0.577. Decision curve analysis showed that at the threshold of 0.25, the net benefit reached 0.239, which was higher than both the “treat all” and “treat none” strategies (Fig. 5). TDR alone yielded an AUC of 0.676 (95% CI: 0.490–0.862). Bootstrap validation confirmed favorable stability and reliability of the combined indicator (Fig. 6). Discussion

This study characterizes the dose-response relationship between CT-derived TDR and STAS risk in peripheral stage I LUAD, demonstrating a significant non-linear (threshold) association. Notably, the combined TDR-CTR index substantially outperformed CTR alone in STAS prediction among part-solid nodules. We hypothesize that compared with the lung window, the solid component delineated on the mediastinal window is more consistent with the actual invasive component of lung cancer, which may account for the better predictive efficacy of the combined TDR–CTR index.

Preoperative prediction of STAS risk using CT imaging can provide thoracic surgeons with critical information for optimal surgical approach selection. Suzuki et al. [19] demonstrated that sublobar resection conferred no significant difference in survival benefit compared with lobectomy for patients with peripheral stage I non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Altorki et al. [23] similarly reported that sublobar resection was non-inferior to lobectomy in terms of disease-free survival for peripheral NSCLC (< = 2 cm) with pathologically confirmed node-negative disease. However, for STAS-positive patients, the results were less favorable. Eguchi et al. [24] found that in STAS-positive stage I lung cancer, sublobar resection tended to be associated with higher local recurrence and higher lung cancer-specific mortality compared with lobectomy. Shiono et al. [25] also identified that the inferior prognosis of sublobar resection was associated with the presence of STAS. These findings collectively suggest that sublobar resection may not be optimal for patients with STAS-positive stage I lung cancer.

Currently, most studies have identified CTR as a predictive marker for STAS. Liu et al. [26] reported that CTR is an independent risk factor for STAS in peripheral stage I LUAD (OR = 10.42, 95% CI: 3.29–33.03), while Kim et al. [27] found that CTR > = 90% exhibits high sensitivity for STAS prediction. Our study also confirmed CTR as a risk factor for STAS (OR = 9.15, 95% CI: 3.54–23.64). Moreover, we further revealed a non-linear threshold association between TDR and STAS. The threshold effect analysis indicated that the TDR threshold value for STAS was 0.610, which provides a quantitative reference for risk stratification of STAS in patients with peripheral stage IA lung adenocarcinoma. Notably, The positive association observed at TDR < 0.610 likely reflects the predominance of solid nodules in this range and the limited variability of TDR among these highly aggressive lesions, rather than a true biological effect.

For patients with peripheral stage IA LUAD with accurately assessed low preoperative STAS risk, segmentectomy or wedge resection may be selected, potentially reducing postoperative complications and intraoperative bleeding without compromising long-term prognosis compared with lobectomy. Notably, our study found a linear negative correlation between TDR and STAS in peripheral stage IA LUAD, which provides auxiliary diagnostic information for evaluating STAS risk in such patients. TDR can be used as a supplementary indicator to CTR when conducting preoperative STAS risk assessment for peripheral stage IA LUAD. The Fleischner Society recommends evaluating the solid component using narrow or mediastinal windows, or both, for part-solid nodules [28]. Johnston et al. [29] noted that pure ground-glass nodules and part-solid nodules with ground-glass predominance are typically STAS-negative and thus suitable for limited resection. In the part-solid nodule subgroup of our study, CTR alone yielded an AUC of only 0.525, which may be attributable to the small sample size and uneven value distribution (only four cases had CTR < 0.4), rendering this single indicator unable to effectively discriminate STAS risk. Accordingly, we introduced a combined index of TDR and CTR, which achieved optimal predictive performance with a statistically significant improvement over CTR alone, reaching an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.654–0.932). Internal validation confirmed good stability and reliability of the combined indicator. While the combined index improved AUC to 0.79, the associated false-positive and false-negative rates must be weighed in clinical decision-making. Using this model to select sublobar resection might risk undertreatment in a subset of patients. Therefore, the combined TDR–CTR index should be viewed as a risk stratification tool to prompt intraoperative frozen section confirmation rather than a definitive diagnostic test. Decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical utility of the combined TDR‑CTR index. Across a broad range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (0 to 0.62), the model showed a higher net benefit than both the “treat all” and “treat none” strategies. At the threshold of 0.25, the net benefit reached approximately 0.24, indicating that the combined index could effectively reduce unnecessary interventions while maintaining a high detection rate for STAS‑positive cases. These results further support the potential of the combined index to guide personalized preoperative risk assessment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, excluding patients with fewer than six lymph nodes examined may disproportionately exclude those with a better prognosis who underwent limited resections, thereby introducing bias into the study sample. Additionally, the sample size is relatively limited. Prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to verify the generalizability and predictive efficacy of the TDR threshold. Second, the sample size of the part-solid nodule subgroup will be expanded to further validate the predictive ability of the combined indicator. Third, TDR measurement still carries a degree of subjectivity, and artificial intelligence methods have not yet been incorporated to improve measurement repeatability. The application of AI-based automatic segmentation technology in the future may further enhance the accuracy and consistency of TDR measurement [30].

Conclusions

In peripheral stage I lung adenocarcinoma, CT-derived TDR holds potential as an imaging biomarker for risk stratification of STAS, and the combination of TDR and CTR significantly improves the predictive efficacy for STAS in part-solid nodules.
