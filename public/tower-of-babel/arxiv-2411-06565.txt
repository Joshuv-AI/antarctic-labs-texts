Foundation Model for Composite Microstructures: Reconstruction, Stiffness, and Nonlinear Behavior Prediction
Ting-Ju Wei, Chuin-Shan Chen

Abstract
The rapid advancement of machine learning has unlocked numerous opportunities for materials science, particularly in accelerating the design and analysis of materials. However, a significant challenge lies in the scarcity and high cost of obtaining high-quality materials datasets. While foundation models pre-trained on large datasets have excelled in fields like natural language processing by leveraging latent features through transfer learning, their application in materials science remains limited. Here, we present a foundation model specifically designed for composite materials. Pre-trained on a dataset of short-fiber composites to learn robust latent features, the model accurately predicts homogenized stiffness during transfer learning, even with limited training data. Additionally, our model effectively predicts the material’s nonlinear behavior by transferring these learned features to an Interaction-based Material Network, which is a constitutive surrogate model. These results demonstrate the potential of our foundation model to capture complex material behaviors. Our findings validate the feasibility and effectiveness of foundation models in composite materials. We anticipate extending this approach to more complex three-dimensional composite materials, polycrystalline materials, and beyond. Moreover, this framework enables high-accuracy predictions even when experimental data are scarce, paving the way for more efficient and cost-effective materials design and analysis.

Introduction

The mechanical properties of composite materials are highly dependent on their microstructure. Understanding and predicting the effective properties of composites based on their microstructural configurations is crucial for designing and optimizing advanced materials. Machine learning (ML) models have emerged as valuable tools for rapidly predicting these properties, opening new avenues in materials science research

[

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have also been integrated with deep material networks to predict the mechanical responses of composites

[
6

employed a pre-trained transformer model to predict the mechanical response of elastoplastic composite materials, illustrating the versatility of transformer architectures in materials modeling.

While these models have achieved considerable success, they predominantly rely on supervised learning, which requires large, labeled datasets for training. The necessity of extensive datasets poses a significant challenge due to the scarcity and high cost of acquiring training data, whether through experiments or simulations. Moreover, many of these models are problem-specific and may not generalize to different tasks, limiting their broader applicability
[
7
]
. In contrast, self-supervised learning approaches have revolutionized fields such as natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision by reducing the dependence on labeled data. Notably, models like BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)
[
8
,
9
]
have utilized masked language modeling to learn rich representations from unlabeled text data. The advent of foundation models has further centralized information from diverse data modalities, enabling a single model to be adapted for a wide range of downstream tasks
[

.

The concept of foundation models has also been successfully applied to computer vision. Masked autoencoders (MAEs), introduced by He et al.
[
12

, employ a strategy analogous to BERT by masking portions of the input image and training the model to reconstruct the missing parts. In these MAEs, the encoder is replaced with vision transformers (ViTs)
[
13
]
, and the decoder is a lightweight transformer designed for reconstruction. MAEs have demonstrated performance that, in some instances, outperforms state-of-the-art contrastive learning methods with ViTs, highlighting the efficacy of self-supervised learning in visual domains
[
14

.

Despite these advancements, exploring foundation models within materials science remains limited. Applying self-supervised learning to materials offers the potential to overcome data scarcity and enhance model generalizability across different tasks and material systems. Therefore, in this work, we propose a foundation model specifically designed for composite materials, termed the material masked autoencoder (MMAE), to validate the feasibility of this approach.

To validate the MMAE, we pre-trained the model on a large dataset of composite microstructures and evaluated its performance on two downstream tasks. First, we applied transfer learning to predict the homogenized stiffness components of composite materials, achieving high accuracy even with limited labeled data. Second, we extended the transfer learning framework to predict Interaction-based Material Network (IMN) parameters, enabling the extrapolation of the nonlinear behavior of composite materials
[
15

16

. These results demonstrate that the MMAE effectively adapts to various downstream tasks in materials modeling and analysis.

2
Methods

2.1
Masked autoencoders

The MMAE employed in this study was primarily based on the MAE architecture proposed by He et al.
[
12
]
. As depicted in Figure
1
, the MMAE consists of an encoder-decoder structure, where the encoder is a ViT
[
13
]
with a patch size of
16\times 16
pixels, embedding dimension of 256, 12 transformer blocks, and 4 attention heads per block. A linear layer bridges the encoder and decoder, projecting the encoder’s output embeddings to the appropriate dimension for the decoder. The decoder, tailored for reconstruction, comprises 8 transformer blocks with an embedding dimension of 128 and 16 attention heads per block.

Each composite image, originally of size
224\times 224
grayscale pixels(1 channel), was divided into 196 non-overlapping patches, each measuring
16\times 16
pixels. These patches were processed using a 2D convolutional layer (Conv2D) to generate token embeddings, which were subsequently sent to the MMAE encoder.

Figure 1:
Architecture of the MMAE during pre-training. The input microstructure image is divided into patches, with a subset masked (indicated by striped patterns). The visible patches are embedded and fed into the encoder, and the decoder reconstructs the masked patches based on the encoder’s output.

We applied random masking to a specified proportion of the image patches to introduce the self-supervised learning objective. Specifically, a random subset of patches without replacement was selected to be masked according to a predetermined masking ratio. For example, with a masking ratio of 85%, only 15% (29 out of 196) of the patches remained visible.

The tokens corresponding to the visible patches were fed into the encoder, which processes them through its transformer blocks to produce latent feature representations. Accordingly, the decoder received the encoder’s latent features and positional embeddings for visible and masked patches. It reconstructed the pixel values of the masked patches using its transformer blocks, integrating contextual information from the encoded features. By reconstructing the missing patches, the decoder helps the model learn to infer unseen parts of the microstructure from the available information.

The training objective was to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between the reconstructed and original pixel values, computed only over the masked patches. By focusing the reconstruction loss solely on the masked patches, the MMAE was encouraged to learn meaningful representations that can accurately predict missing information based on context.

Through this self-supervised approach, the MMAE leverages large amounts of unlabeled data to effectively learn the statistical correlations and spatial patterns inherent in composite microstructures. The encoder learns to generate latent features that encapsulate essential microstructural information, which can be transferred to downstream tasks through fine-tuning or other transfer learning techniques.

2.2
Transfer learning for homogenized stiffness components

Figure 2:
Illustration of the MMAE during transfer learning. The encoder processes the input microstructure image, and the [cls] token output is used as a representation. A regression head is connected to the [cls] token to predict the homogenized stiffness components.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the embeddings learned by the pre-trained MMAE for predicting homogenized material properties, we employed transfer learning techniques utilizing the [cls] token output from the encoder as a global representation of the input microstructure. We explored linear probing and fine-tuning approaches, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

2.2.1
Linear probing

We performed linear probing to assess the robustness of the latent features extracted by the MMAE. Specifically, we utilized the [cls] token output from the frozen MMAE encoder to represent each input microstructure image. The [cls] token is a unique token added to the sequence of patch embeddings during encoding, designed to capture a global representation of the entire input image. By freezing the encoder weights, we ensured that the learned features remained unchanged during this step.

We trained linear regression models using the [cls] token embeddings as inputs to predict each of the homogenized stiffness components (
\bar{C}_{1111}
,
\bar{C}_{2222}
,
\bar{C}_{1212}
). The linear regression models were optimized using the MSE loss function. This approach evaluates the effectiveness of the representations learned during pre-training for the downstream task without further modifying the encoder’s parameters.

2.2.2
Fine-tuning

We also used the [cls] token output from the MMAE encoder for fine-tuning as the input representation. However, unlike linear probing, we updated the encoder weights during training. We conducted two types of fine-tuning:

1.

End-to-end fine-tuning: In this approach, we allowed all the parameters of the MMAE encoder to be updated during training on the downstream regression tasks. This means the model could adjust its learned representations to better predict homogenized stiffness components. Again, we used the [cls] token to represent the input microstructure and trained a regression head on top of it.

2.

Partial fine-tuning: To investigate the impact of fine-tuning only a subset of the model parameters, we performed partial fine-tuning. Specifically, we fine-tuned only the last few transformer blocks of the encoder while keeping the earlier layers frozen. By doing so, we aimed to adjust task-specific representations in the fine-tuned blocks while retaining the general feature representations learned during pre-training in the frozen blocks. This approach allows us to explore a balance between computational efficiency and model performance. The [cls] token was used as the input to the regression head.

The regression head consisted of a fully connected feedforward neural network optimized using the MSE loss function for both fine-tuning methods.

2.3
Transfer learning for IMN parameters

This framework employs transfer learning by fine-tuning the pre-trained MMAE to predict the IMN parameters. The MMAE encodes microstructural information into embeddings, which are passed through a linear projection layer to generate the IMN parameters. During online prediction, the IMN utilizes these parameters to extrapolate nonlinear material behavior, enabling efficient and accurate modeling of complex materials. The schematic of the proposed FM-IMN framework is shown in Figure
3
.

2.3.1
End-to-end offline fine-tuning

The IMN is an efficient physics-based constitutive surrogate model used for multiscale modeling of materials
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. A notable advantage of the IMN is its ability to be trained using only linear elastic data while achieving accurate nonlinear extrapolation during online prediction. This capability stems from the IMN architecture, which incorporates de-homogenization and homogenization processes during online prediction, enabling it to model complex material behaviors without requiring extensive nonlinear datasets.

The IMN comprises two main components: material nodes and the material network. Specifically, an IMN with a layer depth of
N
consists of
2^{N}
material nodes and a perfect binary tree structure called the material network, which contains
2^{N}-1
tree nodes. Each material node represents a material constituent phase and is associated with a trainable parameter
w
encoding the volume fraction of that phase. Each tree node in the material network has a trainable parameter
\theta
, which represents the stress-equilibrium direction. The collection of all trainable parameters forms
\mathcal{F}
, with a total of
2^{N+1}-1
parameters.

To leverage the microstructural features extracted by the MMAE, we used the [CLS] token output from the encoder, representing a latent embedding of the input microstructure image
I_{s}
. This [CLS] token was passed through a linear projection layer to produce the flattened IMN parameters
\mathcal{F}_{s}
for each sample
s
. Subsequently, we directly used these parameters
\mathcal{F}_{s}
to parameterize the IMN, which predicted the homogenized stiffness matrix
\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{MMAE-IMN}}_{s}
, given the constituent stiffness matrices
\mathbf{C}^{p1}_{s}
and
\mathbf{C}^{p2}_{s}
.

End-to-end fine-tuning was performed to jointly optimize the MMAE encoder and the linear projection layer parameters. To quantify the error between the predicted homogenized stiffness
\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{MMAE-IMN}}_{s}
and the ground truth
\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{DNS}}_{s}
, we computed the mean squared error (MSE) using the Frobenius norm:

\text{ERR}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{dataset}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\text{dataset}}}\frac{\left\|\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{DNS}}_{s}-\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{MMAE-IMN}}_{s}(I_{s},\mathbf{C}^{p1}_{s},\mathbf{C}^{p2}_{s})\right\|_{\text{F}}}{\left\|\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{DNS}}_{s}\right\|_{\text{F}}},

(1)

where
N_{\text{dataset}}
is the total number of samples, and
\|\cdot\|_{\text{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm. We fine-tuned the MMAE encoder and the linear projection layer to accurately predict the homogenized stiffness matrices based on the microstructure images and the constituent material stiffness matrices by minimizing this error.

2.3.2
Online prediction

After training, the MMAE encoder and linear projection layer can infer the IMN parameters
\mathcal{F}
for any given RVE microstructure image
I
. The inferred IMN, leveraging its online prediction algorithm and the constitutive laws of the constituent phases, performs nonlinear extrapolation to predict the material’s response under diverse loading conditions
[
15
,
16
]
.

By integrating the MMAE’s capability to extract detailed microstructural embeddings with the IMN robust nonlinear extrapolation, this framework provides an efficient and versatile tool for nonlinear material modeling.

2.4
Dataset generation

2.4.1
Generation of microtrsturce

We generated synthetic microstructures for pre-training the MMAE and downstream tasks using the random sequential adsorption (RSA) method
[
18
]
. RSA is a stochastic process wherein particles are randomly and sequentially placed into a domain without overlapping, allowing us to create composite microstructures with inclusions of varying quantities, shapes, sizes, and orientations. This method effectively simulates realistic materials systems by capturing the randomness inherent in composite microstructures.

For the short-fiber composites, we considered representative volume element (RVE) containing elliptical inclusions (fibers) dispersed within a matrix. The microstructures were defined within a three-dimensional descriptor space characterized by:

1.

Number of Particles (
N_{p}
): The number of fibers in each RVE, ranging from 15 to 35.

2.

Aspect Ratio (
A_{r}
): The ratio of the major to minor axes of the elliptical fibers, ranging from 1 to 4.

3.

Volume Fraction (
v_{f}
): The fraction of the RVE volume occupied by the fibers, ranging from 10% to 40%.

The actual sizes of the fibers in the RVEs were determined based on
N_{p}
and
v_{f}
. The orientations of the fibers were assigned randomly during their placement within the RVEs to mimic the stochastic nature of natural materials.

We generated three datasets of RVEs using Latin hypercube sampling
[
19
]
for short-fiber composites:

1.

Pre-training dataset: A large dataset comprising 100,000 grayscale images of short-fiber composite microstructures was generated for self-supervised pre-training of the MMAE. This dataset captures a wide range of fiber arrangements, aspect ratios, and volume fractions, enabling the MMAE to learn robust and generalizable features from diverse microstructures.

2.

Homogenized stiffness dataset: A dataset of 5,000 microstructures was generated for predicting homogenized stiffness components.

3.

IMN dataset: A smaller dataset of 300 grayscale images was created for IMN transfer learning to predict nonlinear material behavior.

For the circular-inclusion composites, we generated 5,000 microstructures by uniformly sampling the volume fraction
v_{f}
within the range of 10% to 40%. The inclusions were circular, and their sizes were fixed. The number of inclusions varied according to the specified volume fractions to maintain the desired
v_{f}
. This dataset was utilized to assess the MMAE’s transfer learning performance on a distinct composite system characterized by different microstructural features. This dataset was used for the downstream task of predicting homogenized stiffness components.

2.4.2
Dataset for homogenized stiffness conponents transfer learning

To obtain the homogenized stiffness components of the generated microstructures, we performed DNS using the RVE module available in LS-DYNA
[
20
]
. The simulations were conducted under periodic boundary conditions to accurately capture the effective mechanical response of the composites.

The material properties assigned to the matrix and inclusions were as follows:

•

Matrix: Young’s modulus
E=100
, Poisson’s ratio
\nu=0.30
.

•

Short-fiber/Circular-inclusion: Young’s modulus
E=500
, Poisson’s ratio
\nu=0.19
.

Assuming linear elastic material behavior, we focused on predicting homogenized stiffness components:
\bar{C}_{1111}
,
\bar{C}_{2222}
, and
\bar{C}_{1212}
. The DNS computations provided the labels required for training and validation during transfer learning. The dataset was split 80/20 into training and validation subsets.

2.4.3
Dataset for IMN transfer learning

For IMN transfer learning, we used a dataset comprising 300 RVEs of short-fiber composite microstructures. To account for material property variability, we augmented the dataset by sampling 300 unique pairs of stiffness matrices
\mathbf{C}^{p1}_{s}
and
\mathbf{C}^{p2}_{s}
for each RVE, representing the matrix (Phase 1) and fiber (Phase 2) materials, respectively. This sampling methodology follows the approach outlined in
[
5
]
.

For each pair of material properties, DNS was performed using LS-DYNA to compute the corresponding homogenized stiffness matrix
\overline{\mathbf{C}}^{\text{DNS}}_{s}
, resulting in a total of
N_{\text{dataset}}=90{,}000
instances. The dataset was split into training and validation subsets with an 80/20 ratio, allocating 240 RVEs for training and 60 RVEs for validation.

2.5
Saliency map

We utilized saliency maps to investigate the explainability of MMAE and pinpoint the regions of the microstructure images that have the most significant influence on the model predictions. Saliency maps highlight the input areas that impact the model’s output most, offering visual insights into the model’s decision-making process. They are widely used in various fields, such as image classification and object detection, to assess the relative importance of individual input features in model predictions
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

In this study, since the task was a regression, the saliency map
M
was computed based on the gradient of the MSE loss between the predicted value
\hat{y}
and the ground truth
y
with respect to the input features. The saliency map for a specific input instance
X_{0}
is defined as:

M=\left|\frac{\partial\text{MSE}(y,\hat{y})}{\partial X}\right|_{X_{0}}

(2)

where
X
represents the input features, and
X_{0}
refers to a specific input instance. The absolute value of the gradients
M
captures the sensitivity of the MSE loss to variations in each input feature, reflecting how changes in the input influence the predicted output.

Larger values in the saliency map
M
indicate input features that influence the model’s output more. This is because higher gradient magnitudes imply that even small perturbations in these features can lead to significant changes in the MSE loss. By visualizing these saliency maps, we can pinpoint the most influential regions within the microstructure images for predicting the homogenized stiffness components.

Results and Discussion

3.1
Pre-trained MMAE reconstruction performance

To assess the pre-trained MMAE’s ability to capture latent features of composite microstructures, we evaluated its reconstruction performance using a high masking ratio of 85%, leaving only 15% (29 out of 196) of image patches visible during training. This challenging setup tests the model’s generalization capabilities on unseen data from downstream task datasets.

As shown in Figure
4
, the pre-trained MMAE accurately reconstructs composite microstructure instances, including both short-fiber and circular-inclusion composites, despite the severe masking employed during training. Notably, although the circular-inclusion composites were not included in the pre-training dataset, the MMAE could still reconstruct them effectively. These results demonstrate the MMAE’s robustness in learning representative microstructural features and its capacity to generalize to unseen microstructures, indicating its potential for effective transfer learning in downstream tasks.

3.2.1
Linear probing

To assess the utility of the latent features extracted by the MMAE, we performed linear probing by training a linear regression model on top of the fixed pre-trained embeddings to predict the homogenized stiffness components. We investigated the impact of the masking ratio used during pre-training on the linear probing performance for both the short-fiber and circular-inclusion composite datasets.

Nevertheless, the performance remains stable and satisfactory even when the masking ratio exceeds 45%. Notably, the highest validation set R2 score of 0.93 is achieved at a masking ratio of 85%, indicating that higher masking ratios can lead to more effective feature learning during pre-training. These findings highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate masking ratio to ensure the extraction of meaningful representations that generalize well to downstream tasks.

This result suggests that fine-tuning can effectively adapt the model to the downstream task, regardless of the initial quality of the pre-trained embeddings.

3.2.3
Partial fine-tuning

We conducted partial fine-tuning experiments to explore the MMAE’s behavior in the intermediate regime between linear probing and end-to-end fine-tuning. In partial fine-tuning, only the last few transformer blocks of the model are updated during training on the downstream task, while the earlier blocks remain frozen. This approach allows the model to adjust task-specific representations in the fine-tuned blocks while retaining the general feature representations learned during pre-training in the frozen blocks.

Here, tuning 0 blocks corresponds to linear probing, and tuning all 12 blocks corresponds to end-to-end fine-tuning. The results show that the accuracy improves as more blocks are fine-tuned, indicating better predictive performance. Notably, the performance increases significantly when the last two blocks are fine-tuned and then begins to plateau, suggesting that fine-tuning even a tiny portion of the model’s parameters can capture the necessary task-specific features.

Fine-tuning just a couple of transformer blocks is sufficient to adapt the model to these nonlinear aspects, achieving performance close to full fine-tuning. The substantial improvement in performance with minimal fine-tuning suggests that the earlier layers of the MMAE have learned general features that are transferable across different tasks and datasets. This property is highly desirable in foundation models, as it enables efficient adaptation to new tasks with reduced computational overhead and minimized risk of overfitting.

This shift in focus indicates that the fine-tuned model has learned to capture the nonlinear relationships and critical interactions at the interfaces that significantly influence the composite’s overall stiffness. By paying attention to these regions, the model effectively accounts for factors such as fiber orientation and aspect ratio, essential for accurately predicting the homogenized stiffness components.

The change in saliency patterns from emphasizing the matrix regions in linear probing to focusing on the matrix-fiber interfaces after fine-tuning demonstrates how fine-tuning enables the model to adapt its learned representations to capture more complex and relevant microstructural features. This adaptation improves the model’s ability to encode critical aspects of the microstructure, leading to enhanced performance in predicting the homogenized stiffness components compared to linear probing.

3.2.5
Impact of dataset size in transfer learning

To evaluate the influence of dataset size during transfer learning, we selected the MMAE pre-trained with a masking ratio of 85% and varied the number of instances in the fine-tuning dataset. As shown in Figure

However, even with a dataset containing only 1000 instances, the model achieves a validation set R2 score above 0.91, demonstrating acceptable performance. As the number of data instances increases, the performance improves, but with diminishing marginal returns. The average R2 score converges when data instances reach approximately 2,500, indicating that adding more data beyond this point yields minimal performance gains.

3.4
Summary

The downstream tasks demonstrate that MMAE’s self-supervised pre-training is effective across diverse data-scarce scenarios. For linear probing, the pre-trained embeddings facilitate the extraction of generalizable latent representations of composite microstructures, maintaining robustness even under data-scarce conditions. For more demanding tasks, such as IMN parameter calibration, end-to-end fine-tuning enhances the model’s adaptability, enabling it to meet specific task requirements with high precision.

Saliency analyses reveal that fine-tuning refines the model’s focus on critical fiber–matrix interfaces, improving predictive accuracy for homogenized stiffness components. These findings underscore the utility of self-supervised pre-training as a robust foundation for downstream tasks, while highlighting the importance of tailored adaptations to achieve accurate and efficient material property predictions, particularly in data-limited contexts.

4
Conclusion

This work proposed the MMAE, a foundation model tailored for composite materials, addressing the persistent challenges of data scarcity and transferability in materials science. Through self-supervised pre-training on a large dataset of short-fiber composite microstructures, the MMAE effectively learned robust and representative latent features, providing a solid foundation for diverse downstream applications.

In the first transfer learning scenario, we fine-tuned the pre-trained MMAE to predict the homogenized stiffness of composite materials, achieving an impressive
R^{2}
score of up to 0.959, even with limited labeled data. This result highlights the model’s effectiveness in overcoming data-scarcity challenges. In the second scenario, the MMAE was fine-tuned to infer IMN parameters, enabling precise prediction of composite material responses and demonstrating its adaptability to tasks requiring the modeling of intricate material behaviors.

These findings highlight the transformative potential of foundation models in materials science, offering scalable and efficient solutions for property prediction and microstructural analysis. Future work will explore extending the MMAE framework to more complex systems, such as three-dimensional composites and polycrystalline materials, as well as incorporating experimental datasets to enhance its applicability in real-world scenarios.
