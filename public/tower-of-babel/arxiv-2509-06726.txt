Certification of energy-restricted entanglement depth with simple measurements
Carles Roch i Carceller

Abstract
The ability to certify entanglement in multipartite scenarios is crucial for the development of quantum technologies, specially for the realization of large-scale networks. Here, we introduce a method to certify entanglement from sources with limited energy based on a state discrimination game played by multiple distant uncharacerized parties. Our approach is capable of detecting entanglement across all possible bipartitions employing a single, simple and separable measurement setting for all parties, thereby providing a robust and scalable tool for certifying the depth of multipartite entanglement. We also show that the performance and noise robustness of our method improves in the multipartite regime, growing exponentially with the number of parties.

The central idea is rooted to a simple state discrimination game in which energy-restricted entangled states exhibit strictly better performance than their separable counterparts. Two-distributed quantum state discrimination.—

We begin by analysing the simplest non-trivial instance of the distributed state discrimination task involving two receivers. The scenario is illustrated in Fig.

x_{0}

x_{1}

\displaystyle p(a,b|x_{0},x_{1})=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\Tr\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}^{(\lambda)}\bigl(A_{a}^{(\lambda)}\otimes B_{b}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right]\ .

(1)

We impose an energy restriction on the states prepared by Alice. Concretely, we take
H=\mathds{1}-\ket{00}\bra{00}

\Tr\left[H\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]\leq\omega

\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}^{(\lambda)}

\omega

\displaystyle p_{\rm sd}\leq\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\sqrt{\omega(n-1)}+\sqrt{1-\omega}\Bigr)^{2}\ .

(2)

\displaystyle p_{s}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{0},x_{1}}\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\Tr\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}^{(\lambda)}\bigl(A_{x_{0}}^{(\lambda)}\otimes B_{x_{1}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right]\ ,

Entanglement-based strategy.

We begin computing the maximum success probability attainable with entangled state preparations. Before proceeding, we make a few simplifying remarks. First, we may, without loss of generality, restrict to pure states
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=\ket{{}_{x_{0}x_{1}}}\bra{{}_{x_{0}x_{1}}}
acting on a two-qubit Hilbert space. This is the minimal dimension spanned by four pure preparations, and it is sufficient for Alice and Bob to implement projective measurements in their local laboratories. Second, since the task is symmetric under the permutation of Alice and Bob’s roles, we may assume that both parties receive identical reduced states, i.e.
\Tr_{A}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]=\Tr_{B}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]
(see App.
B
). Third, as this is a state discrimination problem under an energy constraint, the optimal preparations are those that maximize distinguishability,i.e., that saturate the vacuum component to its maximum value
1-\omega
. Altogether, these considerations leave us with the following family of two-qubit pure states parametrized by

\displaystyle\ket{{}_{x_{0}x_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\omega}\ket{00}+(-1)^{x_{1}}\sqrt{a}\ket{01}

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\Tr\left[\left(\rho^{\rm B}_{01}-\rho^{\rm B}_{11}\right)A_{0}\right]+\frac{1}{4}\Tr\left[\left(\rho^{\rm C}_{10}-\rho^{\rm C}_{11}\right)B_{0}\right]\ ,

A_{0}+A_{1}=\mathds{1}

Separable strategy.

We now analyse the maximum success probability achievable when Alice’s preparations are restricted to separable states. In this case, the relevant states are of the bi-separable form
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda){}_{x_{0}}^{\lambda}\otimes\phi_{x_{1}}^{\lambda}
,
subject to the global energy constraint
\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\bra{0}{}_{x_{0}}^{\lambda}\ket{0}\bra{0}\phi_{x_{1}}^{\lambda}\ket{0}\geq 1-\omega
. Since the set of separable density matrices is convex, and the success probability
p_{s}
is a linear functional, the maximum is attained at an extreme point of this set

;
Rockafellar 1970
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. Hence, it suffices to consider pure product preparations of the form
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}={}_{x_{0}}\otimes\phi_{x_{1}}

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\rm Sep}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Tr\left[{}_{x_{0}}A_{x_{0}}\right]\Tr\left[\phi_{x_{1}}B_{x_{1}}\right]=p_{s}^{\rm A}p_{s}^{\rm B}\ ,

(7)

where

p_{s}^{\rm B}

{}_{x_{0}}\otimes\phi_{x_{1}}

\bra{0}{}_{x_{0}}\ket{0}\geq(1-\omega)^{1/2}

). Thus, Alice’s two candidate states can be parametrized as
\ket{{}_{x_{0}}}=\sqrt{(1-\omega)^{1/2}}\ket{0}+(-1)^{x_{0}}\sqrt{1-(1-\omega)^{1/2}}\ket{1}

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\rm Sep}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(1+2\sqrt{(1-\omega)^{1/2}}\sqrt{1-(1-\omega)^{1/2}}\Big)^{2}\ .

Multipartite distributed state discrimination.—

So far, we have considered the task of distributed quantum state discrimination among two parties. We now generalise our analysis to an arbitrary number of parties
n
, each aiming to discriminate its corresponding binary message
x_{i}\in{0,1}
. Consider the generalised energy estimator
H=\mathds{1}-\ket{\vec{0}}\bra{\vec{0}}
and state preparations
\rho_{\vec{x}}
such that
\Tr\left[H\rho_{\vec{x}}\right]\leq\omega
. The overall success probability in the distributed state discrimination game is

\displaystyle p_{s}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\vec{x}}\left(\bigotimes_{i=0}^{n-1}M_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right]\ ,

(9)

where
\vec{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
labels the global message, and
M_{b_{i}}^{i}
denotes the POVM element corresponding to outcome
b_{i}
in the laboratory of the
i^{\text{th}}
party. As in the bipartite case, we will begin considering general state preparations only restricted by their energy content, which is summarized in our first result.

Result 1

Consider a device that prepares equiprobable quantum states
\rho_{\vec{x}}
with
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, such that
\Tr\left[H\rho_{\vec{x}}\right]\leq\omega
, and distributes them among
n
distant parties. The maximum probability that each party successfully discriminates their corresponding symbol

is

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\rm Ent}(\omega,n)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sqrt{(2^{n}-1)\omega}+\sqrt{1-\omega}\right)^{2}\ .

\displaystyle\ket{{}_{\vec{x}}}=\sqrt{1-\omega}\ket{\vec{0}}+\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{c_{i}}(-1)^{\vec{x}\cdot\vec{\nu}_{i}^{j}}\ket{\vec{\nu}_{i}^{j}}\ ,

(11)

where
\vec{\nu}_{i}^{j}
denotes a vector full of

n-i
zeros, and

indexes the possible different permutations without repetitions. We will take
c_{i}=\frac{\omega}{2^{n}-1}
in analogy with the bipartite case. Furthermore, consider that all parties perform the same projective measurements found to be optimal in the bipartite case, i.e.
M_{0}^{i}=\ket{+}\bra{+}
and
M_{1}^{i}=\ket{-}\bra{-}
,
\forall i
. Putting these together with the preparation
\ket{{}_{\vec{x}}}
leads to the success probability

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\rm Ent}=

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}+\frac{\omega}{2^{n}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
i\end{pmatrix}+\sqrt{\omega-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}n\\

The sums over the combinatory elements straightforwardly yield

Result 2

Consider a device that prepares equiprobable quantum states
\rho_{\vec{x}}=\overset{n-1}{\underset{i=0}{\bigotimes}}\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}

, such that
\Tr\left[H\rho_{\vec{x}}\right]\leq\omega

is

\displaystyle p_{s}^{\rm Sep}(\omega,n)=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{(1-\omega)^{1/n}}\sqrt{1-(1-\omega)^{1/n}}\right)^{n}.

(13)

Proof.
Considering state preparations which are separable across all possible bi-partitions implies that the optimal success probability reduces to the product of the individual success probabilities, i.e.
p_{s}^{\rm Sep}=\prod_{i}p_{s}^{i}
, where we denoted
p_{s}^{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x_{i}}\Tr\left[\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}M_{x_{i}}^{i}\right]
the individual success probability of the
i^{th}
party. We now invoke the symmetry between permutation of parties, i.e. the objective function

remains invariant under all permutations

, for

indexing all parties. This implies that the optimum
p_{s}^{\rm Sep}
will be achieved when all parties perform equally well in the discrimination task, i.e.
p_{s}^{i}=p_{s}^{j}
,
\forall i,j
, resulting in
p_{s}^{\rm Sep}=(p_{s}^{i})^{n}
. Also, all parties will perform equally well if the vacuum component of the global state is equally distributed among all parties. Individually therefore, each party will receive a pure state restricted by
\bra{0}\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}\ket{0}\geq(1-\omega)^{1/n}

2

\blacksquare

Certification of entanglement depth.—
Our framework permits the certification of multipartite entanglement in energy-limited state preparations with simple and separable qubit measurements. Now, we investigate whether intermediate cases entanglement across any bi-partition can also be certified. To this end, we present our final result.

Result 3

Consider an ensemble of
2^{n}
equiprobable quantum states
\rho_{\vec{x}}
such that
\Tr\left[H\rho_{\vec{x}}\right]\leq\omega
. Let
\rho_{\vec{x}}
be separable across
m
groups of
k_{j}
parties (for
j=0,\ldots,m-1
). The maximum probability that each party to successfully discriminate their corresponding symbol
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is

\displaystyle p_{s}=\max_{\{\tilde{\omega}_{j}\}}\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}p_{s}^{\rm Ent}(\tilde{\omega}_{j},k_{j})\ ,

(15)

for
\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(1-\tilde{\omega}_{j}\right)=1-\omega
and
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}k_{j}=n
.

Proof.
Let us write
\rho_{\vec{x}}=\overset{m-1}{\underset{j=0}{\bigotimes}}\sigma_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{j}
, where
\vec{x}_{j}
denotes a vector containing all symbols that belong to the parts of the entangled portion
\sigma_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{j}
. The discrimination task is then reduced to the parallel discrimination of states
\sigma_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{j}
, distributed among
k_{j}
parties. Under these conditions, we invoke Result
1
which determines that the maximal success probabilities in discriminating each group of states
\sigma_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{j}
is
p_{s}^{\rm Ent}(\tilde{\omega}_{j},k_{j})
. Then, since the maximal success probability factorizes over the different entangled portions, one ends with the product of all
p_{s}^{\rm Ent}(\tilde{\omega}_{j},k_{j})
. It remains to maximise over the possible energies
\tilde{\omega}_{j}
which must satisfy the condition
\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(1-\tilde{\omega}_{j}\right)=1-\omega
for energy conservation. Putting all together, one recovers Eq. (
15
) as the maximum success probability.
\blacksquare

Corollary 1

Let
\rho_{\vec{x}}
be entangled across groups of the exact same number of parties, i.e.
k_{i}=k_{j}:=k
,
\forall i,j
. The maximum probability that each party to successfully discriminate their corresponding symbol
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is that one from Eq. (
15
) with
\tilde{\omega}_{j}=1-\left(1-\omega\right)^{k/m}
,
\forall j
.

Proof.
It follows immediately from party-permutation invariance that all portions
\sigma_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{j}
are equivalent at the optimal point. Thus, their vacuum component must be identical as well, resulting in
\tilde{\omega}_{j}=1-\left(1-\omega\right)^{k/m}
,
\forall j
.
\blacksquare

Conclusion.—
We have introduced a semi-device-independent framework for certifying energy-restricted entanglement depth, grounded on a state-discrimination game played by distant and fully uncharacterised parties. In this game, each party attempts to simultaneously decode a party-specific symbol from a shared state. We showed that the resulting success probability surpasses any separable strategy whenever the shared state is entangled across at least one bipartition, and that this advantage strengthens as more bi-partitions become entangled, ultimately serving as an entanglement depth signature. Importantly, our method only requires simple separable measurements and exhibits robustness against white noise, making it suitable for realistic experimental scenarios. Although the analysis focused on local bit discrimination, our results can be straightforwardly generalised to higher local dimensions.

Looking ahead, several directions for future research emerge. A natural step would be to explore photonic implementations, for instance through multi-mode squeezed light sources, where energy constraints appear naturally and experimental platforms are already highly developed. Our framework also opens a path to investigating non-classical features of Gaussian states, which remain central resources in continuous-variable quantum information processing. Furthermore, exploring cases with greater number of inputs and measurement outcomes would allow a characterisation of the entanglement depth in high dimensional systems, making it possible to address other entanglement features such as the GME dimension
Cobucci and Tavakoli 2024
. Beyond foundational interest, the scheme could also inspire applications in quantum communication and quantum key distribution, where energy restrictions are not only practical but sometimes necessary.

Appendix A
The set of state discrimination success probabilities with pure states is closed under shared randomness

The full set of correlations reproducible in energy-restricted semi-device-independent prepare-and-measure frameworks with two inputs and two outputs is closed under shared randomness
Van Himbeeck et al. 2017
. This means that any observable correlation attainable with shared randomness and arbitrary quantum states can be reproduced with only pure states without shared randomness. In this part of the supplemental material, we show that this statement remains true for the set of state discrimination success probabilities with an arbitrary number of inputs and outputs. It will be conceptually important to first consider the case of pure states without shared randomness and describe the whole set of reproducible success probabilities under the energy constraint. Then, we will show that the analogous set of probabilities with shared randomness and arbitrary quantum states is indeed equivalent.

Pure state preparations without shared randomness
. Let us define the set of state discrimination success probabilities attainable with pure state preparations without shared randomness and under energetic restrictions as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\omega}=\left\{p_{s}\ \big|\ p_{s}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x}\Tr\left[\rho_{x}M_{x}\right],\Tr\left[\rho_{x}H\right]\leq\omega\right\}\ .

(16)

We know that any probability
p_{s}
that belongs to the set
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
is strictly limited by
Pauwels et al. 2025
;
Roch i Carceller and Bernal 2025

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}p_{s}&\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}+\sqrt{\omega(n-1)}\right)^{2}&\text{if}\ 0\leq\omega\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}\\
p_{s}&\leq 1&\text{if}\ 1-\frac{1}{n}\leq\omega\leq 1\end{array}\right.

Another way of writing the same restrictions is that any attainable success probability
p_{s}
must be limited by

\displaystyle g_{-}(p_{s})\leq\omega\leq g_{+}(p_{s})\quad\text{if}\ 0\leq\omega\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}\ ,

(19)

for

\displaystyle g_{\pm}(p_{s}):=\frac{1+(n-2)p_{s}\pm\sqrt{2p_{s}(1-p_{s})(n-1)}}{n}

(20)

and
0\leq p_{s}\leq 1
if
1-\frac{1}{n}\leq\omega\leq 1
. We can now use the functions
g_{\pm}(p_{s})
to define the set
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
. To do that though, we need to see that both limits Eq. (
A
) and Eq. (
19
) complement each other. In th energetic range
1-\frac{1}{n}\leq\omega\leq 1
the implication
A
\Leftrightarrow
19
is trivial. We therefore focus on the range
0\leq\omega\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}
. To see the forward direction
A
\Rightarrow
19
,

\displaystyle g_{-}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}+\sqrt{\omega(n-1)}\right)^{2}\right)=\omega\leq g_{+}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}+\sqrt{\omega(n-1)}\right)^{2}\right)\ ,

(21)

where the second inequality always holds since
g_{-}(p_{s})\leq g_{+}(p_{s})
,
\forall p_{s}
. On the other hand, the reverse direction
A
\Leftarrow
19
also holds since,

\displaystyle p_{s}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-g_{-}(p_{s})}+\sqrt{g_{-}(p_{s})(n-1)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}+\sqrt{\omega(n-1)}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sqrt{1-g_{+}(p_{s})}+\sqrt{g_{+}(p_{s})(n-1)}\right)^{2}\ .

(22)

Thus, the set
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
in the relevant range
0\leq\omega\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}
is fully described by success probabilities
p_{s}
satisfying
g_{-}(p_{s})\leq\omega\leq g_{+}(p_{s})
.

Arbitrary state preparations with shared randomness.
Let us now define the set of success probabilities attainable with arbitrary state preparations and shared randomness. This is, we consider realizations
\lambda
distributed according to
q(\lambda)
constrained by energies
\omega^{\lambda}
that reach of success probabilities
p_{s}^{\lambda}
. Then, we define the set of averaged realisations as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\omega}=\left\{\mathbf{p_{s}}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\ \big|\ p_{s}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}_{\omega^{\lambda}},\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\omega^{\lambda}=\omega\right\}\ .

(23)

We know that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\omega}\supseteq\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
. What we need to prove is that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\omega}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
, i.e. that given any mixture
\mathbf{p_{s}}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}
of pure-state success probabilities with averaged energy
\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\omega^{\lambda}=\omega
, then the exact same success probability can be obtained through a single pure-state quantum realization with energy
\omega
. This simply amounts to showing that
g_{-}(\mathbf{p_{s}})\leq\omega\leq g_{+}(\mathbf{p_{s}})
. And indeed,

\displaystyle g_{+}(\mathbf{p_{s}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(n-2)\mathbf{p_{s}}+\sqrt{2\mathbf{p_{s}}(1-\mathbf{p_{s}})(n-1)}}{n}

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(n-2)\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\right)+\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)(1-p_{s}^{\lambda})\right)}\sqrt{2(n-1)}}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\frac{1+(n-2)p_{s}^{\lambda}+\sqrt{2p_{s}^{\lambda}(1-p_{s}^{\lambda})(n-1)}}{n}=\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)g_{+}(p_{s}^{\lambda})\geq\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\omega^{\lambda}=\omega

(25)

\displaystyle g_{-}(\mathbf{p_{s}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(n-2)\mathbf{p_{s}}-\sqrt{2\mathbf{p_{s}}(1-\mathbf{p_{s}})(n-1)}}{n}

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(n-2)\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\right)-\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)(1-p_{s}^{\lambda})\right)}\sqrt{2(n-1)}}{n}

(27)

where we used the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)p_{s}^{\lambda}\right)\left(\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)(1-p_{s}^{\lambda})\right)}\geq\sum_{\lambda}q(\lambda)\sqrt{p_{s}^{\lambda}(1-p_{s}^{\lambda})}\ .

(28)

Therefore, both sets

are equivalent.

Appendix B
Proof of the symmetry argument

In this part of the supplemental material, we present in more detail the symmetry arguments used in the main text to derive the maximum success probabilities. We begin considering the bipartite case, and later extend it to multipartite state preparations looking at the genuine multipartite entangled case and the fully separable case.

B.1
Bipartite case

Consider a two-distributed state discrimination game. A device encodes the bits
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
onto the bipartite quantum state
\rho_{x_{0},x_{1}}
which has a limited energy
\Tr\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}H\right]\leq\omega
, with
H=\mathds{1}-\ket{00}\bra{00}
. This state is distributed among Alice and Bob, who perform the measurements
\{A_{a}\}
and
\{B_{b}\}
yielding outcomes
a
and
b
respectively. The success probability that both Alice and Bob simultaneously recover their respective symbols is

\displaystyle p_{s}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Tr\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\left(A_{x_{0}}\otimes B_{x_{1}}\right)\right]\ .

(29)

Our goal is to maximise
p_{s}
for any set of separable state preparations that satisfy the vacuum component bound
1-\omega
, and any implementable measurement in Alice and Bob’s devices. At this point, we make the following observation: the optimisation problem remains invariant by permuting Alice and Bob’s devices. Namely, imagine that we define the analogous task but now Bob’s device sits in Alice’s laboratory and vice versa. The success probability in this case is given by

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{s}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x_{0},x_{1}}\Tr\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\left(B_{x_{1}}\otimes A_{x_{0}}\right)\right]\ .

(30)

The probabilities
p_{s}
and
p^{\prime}_{s}
are, in general, not equivalent. Indeed, Alice and Bob could perform completely distinct measurements in non-overlapping Hilbert spaces, and their permutation would not yield the same correlations. However, here we are not interested in any set of behaviours, but only in optimal realisations such that both
p_{s}
and
p^{\prime}_{s}
are maximised. One notes then, that the optimisation of
p^{\prime}_{s}
over all measurements and states that fulfil the vacuum component restriction is exactly the same problem of optimising
p_{s}
under the same restrictions, i.e. at the optimal point,
p_{s}=p^{\prime}_{s}
. Let us define the operator
S
that permutes between laboratories, such that for any pair of operators
X
and
Y
,
S(X\otimes Y)S^{\dagger}=Y\otimes X
. Then,
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=S^{\dagger}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}S\ \Longrightarrow\ p_{s}=p^{\prime}_{s}
. The reverse direction
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=S^{\dagger}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}S\ \Longleftarrow\ p_{s}=p^{\prime}_{s}
is only true if the pair
A_{a}\otimes B_{b}
span the complete Hilbert space in which
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}
has support on. This is, however, the case, since both
A_{a}
and
B_{b}
are orthonormal projectors forming a basis which, by optimality, must have support in the Hilbert space spanned by the set of states
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}
. Thus, optimality implies

\displaystyle\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=S^{\dagger}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}S\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p_{s}=p^{\prime}_{s}\quad\ .

(31)

Now, note that
\Tr_{A}\left[SXS^{\dagger}\right]=\Tr_{B}\left[X\right]
and
\Tr_{B}\left[SXS^{\dagger}\right]=\Tr_{A}\left[X\right]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]=\Tr_{B}\left[S^{\dagger}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}S\right]=\Tr_{A}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]\ .

(32)

Therefore, the permutation symmetry present in the problem allows us to w.l.g. consider that both Alice and Bob’s shares of the global state are equivalent
\Tr_{B}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]=\Tr_{A}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]
,
\forall x_{0},x_{1}
, at the optimal point.

Let us now consider separable state preparations of the form
\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}=\phi_{x_{0}}\otimes{}_{x_{1}}
. Thanks to the permutation symmetry, one can consider that
\phi_{x}={}_{x}
,
\forall x
. With respect to the energy limitation
\bra{00}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\ket{00}=\bra{0}\phi_{x_{0}}\ket{0}\bra{0}{}_{x_{1}}\ket{0}\geq 1-\omega
, this implies that
\bra{0}\phi_{x_{0}}\ket{0}=\bra{0}{}_{x_{1}}\ket{0}\geq\sqrt{1-\omega}
.

B.2
Multipartite case

We now generalise the symmetry argument applied to the two-distributed state discrimination game to the multipartite case. Consider a device that prepares an
n
-partite quantum state
\rho_{\vec{x}}
, for
\vec{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
, with an energy limited by
\Tr\left[\rho_{\vec{x}}H\right]\leq\omega
, for
H=\mathds{1}-\ket{\vec{0}}\bra{\vec{0}}
. This state is distributed among
n
distant parties, each applying a measurement
\{M_{b_{i}}^{i}\}
and yielding an outcome
b_{i}
. The goal of each party is to recover their respective bit (party
i
aims to recover
x_{i}
). The probability that all parties simultaneously recover their correct symbol is given by the success probability

\displaystyle p_{s}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\vec{x}}\left(M_{x_{0}}^{0}\otimes M_{x_{1}}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{x_{n-1}}^{n-1}\right)\right]\ .

(33)

Let us define the cyclic permutation operator
S
such that for any set of operators
X_{i}
,
S\left(X_{0}\otimes X_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{n-1}\right)S^{\dagger}=X_{n-1}\otimes X_{0}\otimes X_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{n-2}
. The optimal success probability remains invariant under the cyclic permutation of measurement devices. That means,

\displaystyle p_{s}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\vec{x}}\left(M_{x_{0}}^{0}\otimes M_{x_{1}}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{x_{n-1}}^{n-1}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\vec{x}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\vec{x}}S\left(M_{x_{0}}^{0}\otimes M_{x_{1}}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{x_{n-1}}^{n-1}\right)S^{\dagger}\right]\ .

(34)

Since each POVM
\{M_{b_{i}}\}
is defined by orthogonal projectors that form a complete basis of the Hilbert space spanned by the states
\rho_{\vec{x}}
, the equality
p_{s}=p^{\prime}_{s}
implies that at the optimal point
\rho_{\vec{x}}=S^{\dagger}\rho_{\vec{x}}S
. Now, note that
\Tr_{B_{i}}\left[SXS^{\dagger}\right]=\Tr_{B_{i\ominus 1}}\left[X\right]
, where
B_{i}
labels the Hilbert space of laboratory
i
, which amounts to

\displaystyle\Tr_{B_{i}}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]=\Tr_{B_{i}}\left[S^{\dagger}\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}S\right]=\Tr_{B_{i\ominus 1}}\left[\rho_{x_{0}x_{1}}\right]\ \forall i\ .

(35)

Therefore, at the optimal point, all reduced state operators in each individual laboratory are equivalent.

Let us now consider completely separable state preparations of the form
\rho_{\vec{x}}=\overset{n-1}{\underset{i=0}{\bigotimes}}\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}
with
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, such that
\Tr\left[H\rho_{\vec{x}}\right]\leq\omega
. The relation in Eq. (
35
) indicates that at the optimal point,
\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}=\sigma_{x_{j}}^{j}
,
\forall i,j
. Therefore, the restriction on the vacuum component imposed by the energy limitation is translated to
\bra{\vec{0}}\rho_{\vec{x}}\ket{\vec{0}}=\left(\bra{0}\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}\ket{0}\right)^{n}\geq 1-\omega
, and thus,
\bra{0}\sigma_{x_{i}}^{i}\ket{0}\geq\left(1-\omega\right)^{1/n}
,
\forall i
.

Note.
This symmetry argument is applicable to cases with state preparations that are symmetrically entangled across the same number of groups of parties. For example, if
\rho_{\vec{x}}
is distributed among four parties and separable across the cut
AB|CD
, one can apply the symmetry argument and conclude that, at the optimal point, each group
AB
and
CD
will receive states with energy
\tilde{\omega}=1-(1-\omega)^{1/2}
. However, if
\rho_{\vec{x}}
are separable across an asymmetric cut such as
A|BCD
, the symmetry argument does no longer apply. That is why, in this and the
A|B|CD
cases, we need to use numerics to find the maximum.
