Co- and multipathologies need serious consideration for understanding Parkinson's disease pathogenesis and designing novel experimental therapeutic strategies
Authors: Ashley Boehringer, Lorraine V. Kalia, Bastiaan R. Bloem, Jeffrey H. Kordower
Published: 2026

Parkinson's disease (PD) is pathologically defined by the presence of aggregated α-synuclein in the form of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites.1–4 Considerable interest has focused on anti-α-synuclein immunotherapy as a potential disease-modifying strategy for PD, but initial phase 2a clinical trials examining this approach did not meet their primary endpoint. Most recently, the phase 2b PADOVA trial of prasinezumab, published in The Lancet Neurology, also did not meet its primary endpoint, although a non-significant delay in motor progression and prespecified exploratory findings suggested potential clinical activity.5–7

Several factors may contribute to the difficulty in translating anti-α-synuclein immunotherapy into demonstrable clinical benefit. These include pharmacological challenges, such as achieving sufficient target engagement within the central nervous system given the limited blood-brain barrier penetration of antibodies. Another possibility – and the one we wish to emphasize here – is more fundamental: anti-α-synuclein immunotherapy is inherently a single-pathology approach, targeting α-synuclein in a disease in which multiple pathologies frequently coexist. Consequently, other coexistent and equally critical pathologies are not targeted by such therapies. Indeed, it is now appreciated that multiple co-pathologies are the rule, not the exception in PD.8–13 The presence of additional pathological features such as β-amyloid, tau, cerebral vascular defects, and to a lesser extent TDP-43 has been well established in PD by numerous groups, with recent evidence exploring the relationship between these co- or multiple pathologies and clinical features. 8

What other pathologies can be found in PD brains? One important example is β-amyloid (Aβ), a pathological hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD) that has long been linked with α-synuclein, which was initially identified not in Lewy bodies but as the “non-amyloid component” of plaques in AD patients. 14 Not only is β-amyloid pathology common in PD patients, but its presence also increases with dementia. Approximately ∼10% of non-demented PD patients present at autopsy with Aβ pathology. This increases to 30–40% in PD cases with dementia (PDD), and further upwards to 80% of cases with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). 15 The level of Aβ pathology also correlates with a steeper drop in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores and with increased mortality rates, suggesting a meaningful role in disease progression.12,13

Another example is tau pathology that is seen in both AD as well as numerous other tauopathies, including frontotemporal dementia, progressive supranuclear palsy and corticobasal degeneration. 16 Recent work has suggested that tau also plays a significant role in PD as a common pathology to parkinsonian individuals with mild motor deficits, including those lacking α-synuclein pathology. 17 This result is supported by the finding of an association between tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) in the substantia nigra in non-PD aged individuals with gait impairment. 18 Tau pathology as assessed using NFT Braak staging is quite common in PD patients with approximately one third of patients classified as Braak stage I, one third as stage II, and one third as stage III. 9 This pathology is increased with age with a recent report suggesting the presence of Braak stage IV increases in abundance reaching 40%, 50%, and 60% of PD patients in their 70 s, 80 s, and 90 s respectively. 13 Furthermore, a growing body of literature demonstrates both the presence of tau in Lewy bodies and the potential role that tau plays in the aggregation and seeding of α-synuclein (and vice versa).18–23

While TDP-43 is not known as a major co-pathology of PD, far fewer studies have explored the levels of this pathology in patient tissue. Two studies have reported that 7% and 18% of the PD cases contained TDP-43 pathology, generally within the amygdala or medial temporal gyrus though case numbers were very low.24,25 Interestingly, TDP-43 was found in a high number of LRRK2 G2019S carriers (73%) including some with TDP-43 pathology in the substantia nigra. 26 Additional studies into the potential levels of TDP-43 co-pathology are needed.

Cerebrovascular defects are an additional pathological feature in PD, and are a common co-morbidity associated with aging.27,28 These features can include a wide variety of pathologies and be measured using histological analysis as well as various imaging modalities. With the wide range of defects falling into this category comes a wide range of studies reporting conflicting results, with specific changes such as small vessel disease identified in 76% of PD patients as compared to 44% of healthy controls. 29 There are more divergent results on the relationship between stroke risk and PD.30,31 Small vessel disease is also associated with cognitive impairment, anxiety, and depression in PD patients, while disease burden is linked to higher MDS-UPDRS part III motor scores, including bradykinesia, rigidity, and gait disability subscores.32,33

While each of these co-pathologies appear to have a role in PD pathobiology, the true magnitude of their importance needs to consider the additive and perhaps even synergistic role of multiple pathologies in a single individual. Multiple co-pathologies occur in most individuals suffering from neurodegenerative diseases, including PD.8,34 While individual co-pathologies carry different risk associations, the most common phenotype associated with the presence of multiple pathologies is cognitive decline, presumably followed by axial disability (changes in gait, balance or posture). Cognitive decline is seen in upwards of 80% of PD patients by end stage disease and often is a precursor to residential care and mortality.35,36 The association between AD pathology (Aβ and tau) and dementia in PD and DLB/PDD patients has been well established by numerous groups, with some even suggesting that AD pathology correlates better with dementia in PD patients than the hallmark α-synuclein pathology, though a regression including all three was most accurate. 37

Recent advances in biomarker development will pave the way for probing the pathological state of living patients, with the goal of treating the specific underlying pathobiology of that individual, rather than the disease according to their clinical diagnosis. More specifically, those with exclusively α-synuclein pathology might indeed be candidates receiving a treatment that is solely focused on targeting this type of pathology. However, those with multiple co-pathologies are more likely to benefit from treatments that tackle each of the contributing disease mechanisms. Identifying which pathologies play a role at which time point in each individual is therefore becoming increasingly important and, fortunately, also increasingly feasible. The α-synuclein seeding assay has shown great promise in identifying aggregating α-synuclein in individuals at time points prior to the manifestation of overt PD motor symptoms.38,39 Amyloid PET tracers are already in clinical use to determine eligibility for anti-amyloid antibody treatment and have shown correlations with dementia state (68% positivity in DLB, 41% in PD, and 5% in PD with mild cognitive impairment 40 ). Importantly, CSF levels of Aβ42 have been shown to be predictive of future cognitive impairment, showing the potential predictive power of these measurements. 41 Similarly, blood based phospho tau assays have shown preclinical efficacy in predicting conversion of prodromal PD syndromes to DLB rather than pure PD. 42 Further, MRI scans can readily detect the presence of comorbid cerebrovascular pathology.

The extremely high rates of co-pathologies suggest that therapeutics focused on a singular pathology are perhaps not very likely to be successful in modifying the course of PD because they are only treating a small fraction of the disease pathobiology. Recognition of this important limitation of current therapeutic approaches may help explain the difficulty in translating therapeutics that showed efficacy in animal models designed to recapitulate specific aspects of PD pathology (e.g., alpha-synuclein aggregation), rather than the full complexity of human disease. This mismatch between experimental models and the complexity of human disease may be one of several factors contributing to the difficulty in translating promising preclinical findings into disease-modifying effects in clinical trials. The development and careful characterization of animal models incorporating multiple relevant pathologies may therefore provide an important means of evaluating therapeutic effects in a disease context that more closely reflects that encountered in patients. In the interim, testing candidate therapeutics across complementary models representing different pathological processes may help determine whether their effects extend beyond a single targeted pathology. An alternative, and potentially complementary, approach is to more closely align clinical trial populations with the biological context represented by the preclinical model in which efficacy was demonstrated. For example, a therapy showing efficacy in a model driven predominantly by α-synuclein pathology might initially be evaluated in a biomarker-defined subgroup with evidence of α-synuclein pathology but without substantial concomitant amyloid-β, tau, or cerebrovascular pathology.

Cartoon illustrating the multiple co-pathologies (each represented by a single cogwheel) commonly seen in Parkinson's disease patients, including the hallmark α-synuclein, tau, beta amyloid, TDP-43 and various forms of cerebral vascular dysfunction. Together, these different types of pathologies are additive and perhaps even synergistic, jointly increasing the severity of motor and cognitive dysfunction in patients (graphically illustrated here is a forward moving conveyor belt). The order in which these pathologies are shown does not represent the temporal sequence nor the relative importance of each pathology. Uncoupling just one of the quick wheels (using a putative disease modifying therapy that tackles only one of the types of pathologies) is unlikely to slow down disease progression in all people with PD, as the conveyor belt will continue to be driven forward by the other cogwheels. 43

The ongoing phase 3 PARAISO trial of prasinezumab will provide an important test of whether targeting α-synuclein alone can produce clinically meaningful disease modification in PD. 44 Even if successful, however, the magnitude and consistency of benefit from a single-pathology intervention may be constrained by the pathological complexity and heterogeneity of the disease. This raises the possibility that more pleiotropic interventions capable of simultaneously addressing multiple disease pathologies may be needed. Alternatively, or perhaps additionally, future therapies may need to be used in combination with interventions targeting other pathologies or broader processes, such as inflammation, autophagy, or protein propagation, that contribute to multiple pathologies observed in PD.
