Conserved nucleocapsid epitopes induce cross-reactive CD4+ T-cell responses with selective protection against human coronaviruses in mice
Authors: Xiaomin Lai, Zhao Chen, Suoqun Zhao, Airu Zhu, Jian Zheng, Zhaoyong Zhang, Junxiang Wang, Yanqun Wang, Jing Sun, Fang Li, Yihao Li, Jiamin Liu, Stanley Perlman, Jingxian Zhao, Jincun Zhao, Zhen Zhuang
Published: 2026

Human coronaviruses (HCoVs) remain a continuing public health challenge because antigenically distinct viruses circulate in humans, while related animal coronaviruses retain zoonotic potential. Antibody escape and spike variation complicate durable humoral protection; attention has increasingly shifted toward conserved T-cell targets. Previous studies have reported that pre-existing HCoV-specific T cells can cross-recognize severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [[1], [2], [3]] and that airway memory CD4+ T cells can protect mice against emerging respiratory coronaviruses [4]. A central unresolved issue, however, is how to distinguish conserved epitopes that merely enable ex vivo cross-recognition from those that can be translated into protective immunity in vivo.

To address this question, we used C57BL/6J mice infected with HCoVs and immunized with Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus replicon particle (VRP) intranasally to map cross-reactive T-cell responses against structural proteins from seven HCoVs: SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), HCoV-HKU1, HCoV-OC43, HCoV-NL63, and HCoV-229E. Airway-derived lymphocytes were stimulated ex vivo with peptide pools (Figure S1), individual structural protein pools, and candidate cross-reactive epitopes (Figures S2–S6) to define the breadth and epitope repertoires of heterologous recognition. This approach enabled systematic identification of cross-reactive T-cell epitopes and provided a platform to test whether these epitopes can induce protective mucosal immunity (the SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV infection experiments were performed in the biosafety level 3 [BSL-3] facility [approval numbers IQTC202309 and IQTC2025008-01]; detailed methods in Supplementary data).

Cross-reactive recognition was not randomly distributed across the coronavirus proteome. CD4+ T-cell cross-reactivity repeatedly converged on conserved nucleocapsid (N) epitopes (Figure S7). A prominent module included MERS-CoV-N98–112, SARS-CoV-N109–123, and SARS-CoV-2-N108–122, which share a conserved core sequence (Figure S7A). Only MERS-CoV-N-specific CD4+ T cells recognized conserved N epitopes from HKU4 and HKU5 (Figure S7B–S7D). HCoV-HKU1- or HCoV-229E-N-induced CD4+ T cells recognized conserved equine coronavirus N epitopes; cross-recognition was also detected between HCoV-229E and HCoV-NL63 through conserved N epitopes (Figure S7E–S7H). These data define N protein as a recurrent reservoir of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II–restricted cross-reactive epitopes across divergent coronaviruses, suggesting that CD4+ T-cell cross-reactivity may be structured around conserved internal proteins rather than distributed uniformly.

In contrast, CD8+ T-cell cross-reactivity was less broadly organized around shared conserved regions and appeared more constrained by specific virus pairs. This is consistent with a prior report that selected SARS-CoV-2 N-specific CD8+ T cells cross-recognize homologous seasonal coronavirus epitopes [5]. We found HCoV-HKU1-N-specific CD8+ T cells cross-recognized HCoV-OC43-N98–105 and HCoV-OC43-N236–244, and reciprocal recognition was observed; however, cross-recognition targeting M protein was asymmetric (Figure S5E–S5H), and alpha-HCoV-related CD8+ responses were weaker (Figure S6). All validated cross-reactive epitopes are summarized in Table S1. These data show that conserved N epitopes are recurrent targets of coronavirus cross-reactive CD4+ T cells, while cross-reactive CD8+ T-cell responses are narrower and more dependent on the specific virus pair.

We next asked whether conserved N-directed CD4+ T cells are effective at the principal site of respiratory coronavirus replication. Intranasal VRP immunization generated cross-reactive CD4+ T cells in the airway that were Th1-polarized, antigen-sensitive, and capable of polyfunctional cytokine production while also expressing trafficking and effector-associated markers (Figures 1A–1F and S8–S9). The protective role of cross-reactive T cells was selective but measurable in defined virus-pair settings. Vaccination with the conserved N epitope accelerated viral clearance and attenuated lung pathology after the MERS-CoV challenge (Figures 1G–1H and S10). HCoV-229E N-induced CD4+ T cells conferred partial protection against HCoV-NL63 infection (Figure 1I–1J). Importantly, this protection occurred without detectable neutralizing antibodies, supporting the contribution of T cells to heterologous control (Figure 1K). This extends an earlier study using HCoV-229E and HCoV-NL63 whole-virus exposure to study heterologous coronavirus immunity [6]. Together with evidence that seasonal HCoV–induced T cells protect against SARS-CoV-2 in human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-transgenic mice [7], these findings strengthen the rationale for incorporating conserved T-cell epitopes into broadly protective coronavirus vaccine strategies.

Non-protective outcomes were equally informative. SARS-CoV-2-N108–122 was recognized ex vivo by CD4+ T cells primed with the homologous MERS-CoV-N98–112 or SARS-CoV-N109–123 epitopes, yet this peptide was not immunogenic during SARS-CoV-2 infection or VRP-SARS-CoV-2-N108 immunization, and vaccination did not enhance viral clearance after SARS-CoV-2 challenge (Figure 1L). These findings indicate that epitope conservation and recalling pre-existing T cells are not sufficient to define protective T-cell targets. Rather, protection likely depends on whether the peptide is efficiently processed, presented by MHC molecules, and positioned within the immunodominance hierarchy during infection. This interpretation aligns with concerns that some SARS-CoV-2 cross-reactive CD4+ T cells detected in unexposed individuals may have limited functional relevance [8].

A similar dissociation between cross-recognition and protection was observed for CD8+ T cells. HCoV-HKU1 N-specific CD8+ T cells cross-recognized HCoV-OC43 N epitopes and were recalled in both the respiratory tract and brain after neurotropic HCoV-OC43 challenge (Figure 1M). However, this recall response did not reduce weight loss, mortality, viral burden, or brain pathology during lethal HCoV-OC43 infection (Figure 1N‒1P). Consistently, VRP-HKU1-N vaccination did not induce neutralizing antibodies (Figure 1Q). These results do not argue against roles of CD8+ T cells in coronavirus immunity but indicate that cross-reactive CD8+ T-cell responses should be sufficiently rapid and robust to control rapid viral spread and progressive neuropathology; they also highlight the need for deeper investigation into the roles of T cells in HCoV-OC43 immunopathogenesis.

Collectively, our study refines the concept of coronavirus T-cell cross-immunity. Conserved N epitopes induce airway CD4+ T cells with antiviral effector properties and protection against selected viruses. Cross-recognition is not equivalent to cross-protection. Protective T-cell targets may satisfy conservation across viruses, efficient processing and presentation during infection, immunological dominance, and localization to the tissue in which viral replication occurs. This study has immediate implications for pan-coronavirus vaccine design. Nucleocapsid epitopes should be considered as non-spike components of broadly protective vaccines, especially for inducing CD4+ T-cell immunity. However, epitope selection should not rely on sequence alignment; candidates should be prioritized only after validation of natural presentation, tissue-localized functional quality, and protection in challenge models. Because T-cell receptor (TCR) antigen recognition is restricted by MHC, the cross-reactive T-cell epitopes identified in mice have limited clinical translatability for human vaccine antigen design. Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights. By distinguishing broadly recognized epitopes from broadly protective epitopes, our study provides a practical immunological filter for next-generation coronavirus vaccines.
