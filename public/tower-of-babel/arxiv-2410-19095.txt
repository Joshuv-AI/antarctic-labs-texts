A generalized framework for straintronics in 2D quantum materials using group theory
Rami Zemouri, A. R. Champagne, Saurabh Maiti

Abstract
In the era of 2D and quasi-2D quantum materials one needs to model strain at the level of the Hamiltonian as opposed to a semi-classical approach. Corrections to the electronic Hamiltonian due to strain arise from two sources: deformations of the lattice and changes in the hoppings. Here, we provide a general theory that takes into account the symmetry of the lattice and that of the bonds, and allows us to write down the strain corrections from all sources in any 2D lattice in terms of the band structure parameters like the velocity and the inverse mass tensor. We then use Group theory to identify when strain can be described as a scalar- and/or a vector-potential. We discuss the nature of the potentials that arise from in- and out-of-plane hoppings, allowing us to model multi-layer systems. We also show that, in general, one encounters multiple vector-potentials in different sectors of the Hilbert space, but one can derive a simpler effective low energy strained Hamiltonian via Hilbert space projections. We discuss several toy models of 2D systems and present strained bilayer graphene as a practical system that incorporates all of the above features. We identify a strain dependent energy scale in bilayer graphene above which we would no longer need to account for multiple vector-potentials. The generality of this formulation allows for a wider range of materials to be investigated for quantum transport straintronics.

Strain corrections are known to arise from two sources

[
37

We are able to identify when the potentials serve like a scalar-potential, a vector-potential, or a potential in other irreps of bond-wavevector group. In particular it is shown that a vector-potential like interpretation for in-plane deformations is only possible if the bond-wavevector group is
C_{1v}

C_{3v}

In addition to these general results, we show that the vector-potential from non-uniform deformations that are

[
38

Our theory thus suggests, quite naturally, the presence of multiple vector-potentials in different sectors of the Hilbert space that correspond to different order of hoppings. We note that this possibility was also identified earlier in the context TMDCs
[
39

We apply our results to various Bravais and non-Bravais lattices (which include square, triangular, graphene, Lieb, Kagomé and super-Honeycomb lattices) where we corroborate our results with an explicit calculation. The celebrated 2D material, graphene, in this context, is shown to be a special case which permits the interpretation of the strain correction as vector-potential. Finally, we consider the case of straining a bilayer graphene where we identify multiple vector-potentials arising from in-plane and out-of-plane hoppings. II
Review of coarse-graining of strain

The primary effect of strain on a solid is to displace its atomic sites from location
{\bf r}^{0}_{i}
(where
i
enumerates the sites) to
{\bf r}_{i}
. The standard way (see e.g. Refs.
[
41
,
42
]
) to describe this deformation is to introduce a deformation field
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}({\bf r}_{i}^{0})
which describes the deformation of the atom that was at
{\bf r}^{0}_{i}
. The new location can then be written as
{\bf r}_{i}={\bf r}_{i}^{0}+{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}({\bf r}^{0}_{i})
. Although one only needs
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
at the discrete lattice sites
{\bf r}_{i}
, the field is well defined everywhere and varies slowly over many lattice constants when an external strain is applied. This allows us to treat
{\bf r}_{i}
as a continuous variable
{\bf r}
, making
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
differentiable. Then, a vector connecting two points at
{\bf r}^{a,0}
and
{\bf r}^{b,0}
in the lattice changes as

\underbrace{{\bf r}^{a}-{\bf r}^{b}}_{\Delta{\bf l}^{ab}}=\underbrace{{\bf r}^{a,0}-{\bf r}^{b,0}}_{\Delta{\bf l}^{ab,0}}+\bm{\mathcal{D}}({\bf r}^{a,0})-\bm{\mathcal{D}}({\bf r}^{b,0}).

(1)

For small enough distances (still over a few lattice constants), this can be written in component form as

\displaystyle\Delta{l}^{ab}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta{l}^{ab,0}_{\alpha}+\underbrace{\partial_{\beta}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}_{u_{\alpha\beta}}\Delta l^{ab,0}_{\beta}+...

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta_{\alpha\beta}+u_{\alpha\beta})\Delta l_{\beta}^{ab,0}+...,

where
\alpha,\beta\in\{x,y\}
and
u_{\alpha\beta}
is the displacement-gradient tensor as introduced in the elasticity theory, which can also be expressed in terms of the symmetric linear strain tensor and the anti-symmetric rotational strain tensor
[
30
,
29
]
. The ‘
+...
’ refers to second and higher order corrections which we don’t account for in this work. Throughout the text we follow the Einstein convention where repeated pair of indices are summed. If the points
a
and
b
are in the vicinity of each other with an average location
{\bf r}
, then the above equation can be written in a position dependent fashion as (ignoring higher order corrections in the deformations)

\displaystyle\Delta{l}^{ab}_{\alpha}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\delta_{\alpha\beta}+u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r})]\Delta l_{\beta}^{ab,0}({\bf r}).

(3)

It is important to understand that the above equation presents a strain induced correction to the separation between any two points
a,b
in the vicinity of
{\bf r}
. These points could be the adjacent lattice sites, or nearest neighbor (nn) sites, or next nn site, etc. The
\delta_{\alpha\beta}+u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r})
factor provides the scaling tensor for the length correction due to strain.

An applied strain can be of three types: (i) Uniform - In this case, which is the most common scenario,
u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r})
is a constant. While this reduces the rotational symmetry of the lattice, it preserves the translational ones. In this case, if Eq. (
3
) is applied to the separation between lattice sites, then it is evident that the lattice constant is simply scaled by strain. (ii) Periodic - This type breaks the translational symmetry locally, but restores it at a larger scale leading to formation of superstructures. This type of strain is more natural in systems on a substrate or in twisted 2D structures
[
12
,
43
]
. (iii) Non-uniform - In this case (realized e.g. in Ref.
[
44
]
), the translational symmetry is broken at all scales. The type(i) strain preserves the number of periodic units in the sample but alters the lattice translation vectors. This means that the BZ has the same number of quantum states but is merely stretched/squeezed due to changes in the translation vectors. Type(ii) deformations lead to enlarging of the unit cell and leads to zone folding effects. Type(iii) strain can be intractable in general.

However, imagine a slowly varying
u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r})
such that the system could be broken down into piece-wise type(i) system, which we shall call ‘grains’. If
\eta_{\rm tol}
were to denote the tolerance below which we ignore changes in the tensor
u_{\alpha\beta}
, then a possible grain size around the grain coordinate
{\bf R}_{n}
, defined as
l_{g}\equiv|{\bf R}_{n}-{\bf r}_{\rm bound}|
, could be obtained from

\displaystyle\frac{|\partial_{\bf r}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R}_{n})|}{|u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R}_{n})|}l_{g}<\eta_{\rm tol}.

(4)

Here
{\bf r}_{\rm bound}
would be some locus of points around
{\bf R}_{n}
that would mark the boundary of the grain.
\eta_{\rm tol}
is usually set such that the resulting grain is large enough to validate the use of periodic boundary conditions leading to formation of a reasonably continuous Bloch band structure within each grain. For such slow variations, one can impose
u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r})\rightarrow u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R}_{n}),~\forall{\bf r}\in
neighbourhood of
{\bf R}_{n}
. We refer to this procedure as coarse-graining of the strain. For a large enough system the coarse-grained
{\bf R}_{n}
can also be treated continuously as
{\bf R}
. Note that if the gradient in strain is very small or absent, the grain size
l_{g}
can be as large as the sample.

Figure 2:
Coarse-graining of non uniform strain
. The shades track the strain value across the sample that has been uniaxially stretched by deformation potential
\bm{\mathcal{D}}({\bf r})\propto x^{2}\hat{x}
. The variations within a grain that are below a set tolerance (exaggerated here for visual clarity) can be ignored and assigned a single uniform strain value
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R}_{i})
. A wavepacket spanning a few grains will experience a slowly varying strain field. A grain at
{\bf R}_{i}
can be seen as having a constant lattice constant
a({\bf R}_{i})
. For large samples,
{\bf R}_{i}
is promoted to a continuous variable
{\bf R}
.

Our system can now be seen as a juxtaposition of subsystems (grains) that are parameterized by
{\bf R}
(see Fig.
2
). The grain Hamiltonian can be written in the following manner (we will ignore spin throughout this work as mechanical strain without spin-orbit coupling will not couple to the spin degree of freedom):

\hat{H}({\bf R},{\bf k})=\sum_{{\bf k}}^{({\bf R})}h_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf k})\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{a,{\bf k}}\hat{c}_{b,{\bf k}},

(5)

where
a,b
span the Hilbert space of the Hamiltonian. The sum is over the number of
{\bf k}
vectors in the first BZ, which is just the number of unit cells in the grain. This sum depends implicitly on
{\bf R}
through the modifications of the lattice vectors (and thus the BZ size) in each grain. The more explicit dependence on
{\bf R}
appears in the matrix elements
h_{ab}
that change due to the altered distances between respective atomic centres due to
u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})
. These modifications lead to grain dependent band structures
E({\bf R},{\bf k})
and wavefunctions. If
\hat{H}
is modelled within a tight-binding model, we can express the matrix element as a sum over hoppings to neighbors of different orders:

h_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf k})=\sum_{n}^{\rm orders}\left[\sum_{j_{n}}^{\rm neighbors}t^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}({\bf R})e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{a_{n}b_{n}}^{(j_{n})}({\bf R})}\right].

(6)

Here,
t^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}
is the real space hopping element between
a,b
elements of the Hilbert space, but from the
n^{\rm th}
order of the bonds. An order is identified by a particular distance between the charge centers as well as the type of charge centers. When there are different types of charge centres in the system at the same distance, there is a freedom of choice between including them in the same order or keeping them as separate. The index
j_{n}
, on the other hand, runs over the co-ordination number of the neighbors of a given order. By definition,
|\bm{\delta}^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}|
is the same for a given
n
which we may denote as
\delta_{a_{n}b_{n}}
(e.g. see grey and green bonds in Fig.
1
a). The hopping
t^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}
is usually independent of
j_{n}
, unless the hoppings are made to be phase sensitive. Both
t^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}
and
\bm{\delta}^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n}}
depend on
{\bf R}
via the displacement-gradient tensor
u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})
. This dependence for
\bm{\delta}_{a_{n}b_{n}}^{(j_{n})}({\bf R})
can be written following Eq. (
3
) as:

\delta^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n},\alpha}({\bf R})=[\delta_{\alpha\beta}+u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})]\delta^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n},\beta},

(7)

where
\delta^{(j_{n})}_{a_{n}b_{n},\beta}
is the unstrained vector. Henceforth, unless otherwise mentioned, we will work with
n=1
and also drop this subscript for brevity and with the understanding that the arguments can be readily extended to any order
n
. The deformed distance, on the other hand, is written as

\displaystyle|{\bm{\delta}}^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{2}_{ab}\left(1+\underbrace{[u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})+u_{\beta\alpha}({\bf R})+u_{\gamma\alpha}({\bf R})u_{\gamma\beta}({\bf R})]}_{2\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})}\frac{\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}}{\delta^{2}_{ab}}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|{\bm{\delta}}^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{ab}\left(1+\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\frac{\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}}{\delta^{2}_{ab}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(u^{2}_{\alpha\beta}),

(8)

where
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})
is the strain tensor. In the last line in Eq. (
8
), we have restricted ourselves to linear effects of deformation (see e.g. Refs.
[
29
,
31
]
for non-linear effects which will also include effects of out-of-plane deformations). The hopping element depends on the distance between the charge centres of the states
a,b
as
t^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})=t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-\zeta(\delta_{ab}({\bf R})/\delta_{ab}-1)}
, where
\zeta
is the electron Grüneisen parameter
[
45
]
for the bond. The parameter
\zeta
is controlled by the nature of the orbitals involved in bond formation and hence will quite generally depend on the order of the bonds considered. We can then calculate the modification in the hopping element as:

\displaystyle t^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle t^{(j)}_{ab}\left(1-\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\frac{\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}}{\delta^{2}_{ab}}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{(j)}_{ab}[1-\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})d^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}d^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}],

where we have introduced the unit vectors
d^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}\equiv\delta_{ab,\alpha}^{(j)}/\delta_{ab}
for brevity. We make an observation here that distances are modified by the symmetric strain tensor
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
, but vectors are modified by the displacement-gradient tensor
u_{\alpha\beta}
which is also sensitive to local rotations.

III
Strain corrections to the low energy Hamiltonian in a grain

Having calculated the strain corrections to the lattice parameters (the nn vectors and the hopping elements), we can now get the leading correction to the Hamiltonian at any point
{\bf K}
in the BZ as:

\displaystyle h_{ab}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}[1-iK_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}-\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})d^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}d^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}]

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}}_{h^{0}_{ab}({\bf K})}+\underbrace{K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}[-i\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}]}_{s^{D}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})}+\underbrace{\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}[-d^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}d^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}]}_{s^{H}_{ab,\parallel}({\bf R},{\bf K})}.

Here, by
h^{0}_{ab}({\bf K})
we denote the unstrained Hamiltonian. The strain correction terms to leading order are:
s^{D}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})
from the displacements (
D
) of the lattice, and
s^{H}_{ab,\parallel}({\bf R},{\bf K})
from the change in hoppings (
H
). The former involves the displacement-gradient tensor, while the latter, the strain tensor. Consider also the expansion of the unstrained
h^{0}_{ab}
in a small momentum
{\bf q}
about the same point
\bf K
in the BZ to second order:

\displaystyle h^{0}_{ab}({\bf R},{{\bf K}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{0}_{ab}({{\bf K}})+\underbrace{\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}({\bf R})}[-i\delta_{ab,\alpha}^{(j)}({\bf R})]}_{v_{ab,\alpha}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})}q_{\alpha}+\underbrace{\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}({\bf R})e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}({\bf R})}[-\delta_{ab,\alpha}^{(j)}({\bf R})\delta_{ab,\beta}^{(j)}({\bf R})]}_{I_{ab,\alpha\beta}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})}\frac{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{2},

where
v_{ab,\alpha}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})\equiv\partial_{k_{\alpha}}h^{0}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})
is the
\alpha^{\rm th}
component of velocity matrix element and
I_{ab,\alpha\beta}\equiv\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\partial_{k_{\beta}}h^{0}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})
is the
\alpha,\beta^{\rm th}
component of the inverse mass tensor matrix element between states
a,b
at point
\bf K
in the BZ in the grain at
{\bf R}
. Upon inspecting Eqs. (
10
) and (
III
) we see that

\displaystyle s^{D}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})v_{ab,\beta}({\bf R},{{\bf K}}),

\displaystyle s^{H}_{ab,\parallel}({\bf R},{\bf K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})I_{ab,\alpha\beta}({\bf R},{\bf K})/\delta_{ab}^{2},

(12)

That is, the displacement induced strain correction couples to the Hamiltonian via the velocity and the hopping induced strain correction couples via the inverse mass tensor. Thus, the strained low energy Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle h_{ab}({\bf R},{{\bf K}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{0}_{ab}({{\bf K}})+\left[q_{\beta}+K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\right]v_{ab,\beta}({{\bf K}})+\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})I_{ab,\alpha\beta}({\bf K})/\delta_{ab}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2},u_{\alpha\beta}^{2},qu_{\alpha\beta}).

(13)

The velocity and inverse mass are strain independent when considering corrections to the order stated above. This is valid for small strain and low carrier densities (leading to small Fermi momentum and hence small
q
). Velocity will acquire strain corrections from
\mathcal{O}(qu_{\alpha\beta})
terms
[
29
,
46
]
and the inverse mass will acquire strain corrections from
\mathcal{O}(u^{2}_{\alpha\beta})
terms which are not considered in this work.

Because the velocity and the inverse mass tensor only have in-plane components, the strain corrections in Eq. (
12
) will only pick up contributions from in-plane displacements. But the bonds are not restricted to be in-plane even if the lattice is 2D (e.g. the unit cell may have a 3D structure like in the TMDCs or multilayer systems). If a given bond were to have an out-of-plane component, then the hopping correction from Eq. (
10
) would split into three contributions (see also chart in Fig.
1
c): one from the in-plane projections of the bonds which couples to the inverse mass tensor; one from when one of the
\bm{\delta}^{(j)}
contributes via the out-of-plane (
z
) component and the other via the in-plane component; and the third from when both
\bm{\delta}^{(j)}
’s contribute via its
z
component. Explicitly, we can write this correction as

\displaystyle s^{H}_{ab,\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}\zeta\left\{\epsilon_{\alpha z}({\bf R})[-d^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}d^{(j)}_{ab,z}]+\epsilon_{z\beta}({\bf R})[-d^{(j)}_{ab,z}d^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}]\right\}+\sum_{j}t^{(j)}_{ab}e^{-i{{\bf K}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}\zeta\epsilon_{zz}({\bf R})[-d^{(j)}_{ab,z}d^{(j)}_{ab,z}]

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2i\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha z}({\bf R})v_{ab,\alpha}({\bf K})\delta_{ab,z}/\delta_{ab}^{2}-\zeta\epsilon_{zz}({\bf R}){h^{0}_{ab}}({\bf K})\delta^{2}_{ab,z}/\delta_{ab}^{2}.

Unlike
\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}
,
\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}
is not expressible in terms of the inverse mass, but the velocity and the scalar matrix
h^{0}_{ab}
. The linear-in-
\delta_{ab,z}
correction comes in the form
i\delta_{ab,z}
. Since
\delta_{ab,z}=-\delta_{ba,z}
, the
i
must be present to ensure the Hermitian property of the Hamiltonian. Combining all the in- and out-of-plane deformations, we can write

\displaystyle h_{ab}({\bf R},{{\bf K}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{0}_{ab}({{\bf K}})+s^{D}_{ab}({\bf R},{\bf K})+s^{H}_{ab,\parallel}({\bf R},{\bf K})+s^{H}_{ab,\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-\zeta\epsilon_{zz}({\bf R})\delta^{2}_{ab,z}/\delta_{ab}^{2}\right]h^{0}_{ab}({{\bf K}})+\left[q_{\beta}+K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})-2i\zeta\epsilon_{\beta z}({\bf R})\delta_{ab,z}/\delta_{ab}^{2}\right]v_{ab,\beta}({{\bf K}})+

\displaystyle~\zeta\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})I_{ab,\alpha\beta}({\bf K})/\delta_{ab}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2},u_{\alpha\beta}^{2},qu_{\alpha\beta}).

Equation (
15
) represents
our first general result
which expresses the leading order strain correction to Hamiltonian of any 2D system in terms of the already known band structure parameters such as the velocity and the inverse mass matrices.

III.1
Strain induced potentials

If one constructs a wavepacket out of the Fermi level states of this low energy Hamiltonian, the packet would span a few grains and would evolve according to a Schrödinger equation obtained from Eq. (
15
) by replacing
q_{\alpha}\rightarrow-i\partial_{R_{\alpha}}
. This is often referred to as the continuum limit. This continuum limit Hamiltonian then feels a perturbation from strain as various potentials. The perturbation that is
\propto\hat{h}^{0}
serves as a scalar-potential, while the ones that are
\propto\hat{v}_{\alpha}
serve as a vector-potential. From Eqs. (
12
), (
14
) and (
15
), it is evident that the
\hat{s}^{D}
and
\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}
parts of the strain corrections already have terms that serve as strain induced scalar/vector-potentials. However, the interpretation of the potential arising from
\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}
is not clear, and finding it is the purpose of this section.

First, note that the potential-like interpretation is only possible if all components of the wavepacket feel the same potential. If the Fermi surface is large, then the wavepacket that is formed out of states at the Fermi level would comprise of many
{\bf K}
components and hence many different potentials. This would lead to a convolution of the potential with the wavepacket. However, if we were to consider a lightly doped system at some singular point, like the extremum of a band or a point of degeneracy, then the wavepacket would only involve states around one
{\bf K}
point (in the form
{\bf K}+{\bf q}
, with small
{\bf q}
), allowing for a single effective potential for the wavepacket. There may be multiple singular points at the Fermi level, in which case the wavepacket may be seen as a superposition of the packets from countably finite number of singular points, each with its own potential. Once, we have identified our singular point of interest,
{\bf K}
, we can decompose the strain correction
\hat{s}^{D}+\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}+\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}
in terms of the generators of the Hilbert space. Now, a
d_{H}
dimensional Hilbert space has
d_{H}^{2}-1
generators plus the identity. However, group theoretical considerations guarantee that we only need a handful of them for the decomposition.

To see this, let us remind ourselves that any Hamiltonian
\hat{h}^{0}
, although a matrix in the Hilbert space, is a scalar (which is invariant under all actions of the point group of the lattice). Accordingly, the velocity matrix must transform as a vector, and the inverse mass matrix as a rank 2 tensor. The only way a velocity may appear in the Hamiltonian is if it is contracted with another vector (which is usually the momentum
q_{\alpha}
). Likewise, the inverse mass must contract with another tensor. However, if we consider the Hamiltonian matrix element at point
{\bf K}
from Eq. (
6
) that has the form
\sum_{j}t^{(j)}e^{-i{\bf K}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}
, it would be invariant only under the operations belonging to the wavevector group at
{\bf K}
(subgroup of the point group that leaves the
{\bf K}
point invariant). In fact, and this is often not emphasized, the symmetry group of
h_{ab}({\bf K})
that is formed out of a specific order of hoppings, is the common subgroup of the wavevector group at
{\bf K}
and the group of symmetries that leave the
ab
subspace bonds invariant. We refer to this as the
bond-wavevector group
.

The matrix structure of
h^{0}_{ab}
that is a scalar, must then belong to the fully symmetric irrep of the bond-wavevector group. For such a term to exist, we must have a scalar generator of the Hilbert space. This is guaranteed if
h^{0}_{ab}\neq 0
. The velocity matrix element from Eq. (
III
) has a linear form
\sum_{j}t^{(j)}e^{-i{\bf K}\cdot\bm{\delta}^{(j)}_{ab}}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}
characterized by the single index
\alpha
. This must transform as a vector in the bond-wavevector group and thus belongs to the irrep that contains the functions
x,y
in its character table. For such a term to exist there must be a pair of generators of Hilbert space serving as 2D vectors that can couple to these vector forms. If we cannot find such matrices, then there can be no velocity terms in the Hamiltonian. Finally, the inverse mass tensor is a quadratic form
\sum_{j}t^{(j)}e^{-i{\bf K}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}^{(j)}}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\alpha}\delta^{(j)}_{ab,\beta}
which must transform as a tensor of rank 2, i.e. the
x^{2},y^{2},xy
functions of the character table. These quadratic functions can further be decomposed into different irreps. In Table
1
, we can compare the distribution of various linear and quadratic functions in the character tables of different groups that will be described in the text. Since
\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}
is a quadratic form, it can create a potential in every irrep that contains the quadratic functions. However, the presence of a potential in a particular irrep also relies on the availability of generators of the Hilbert space that can represent that irrep. If no such generators can be found, then potentials of that irrep will not exist. These arguments apply not only to strain but also to
{\bf q}
-expansions of the unstrained Hamiltonians at any given
{\bf K}
-point.

Returning back to our problem, we now see that since the strain corrections involve scalars, linear and quadratic forms, we only need those generators that correspond to these (which are at most 5 in number in 2D:
x,y,x^{2},y^{2},xy
) and not necessarily to the full Hilbert space. If the linear and quadratic forms were to share an irrep, then the same generator can incorporate both types of corrections. This only happens in the bond-wavevector groups
C_{1}
(the trivial group),
C_{1v}
(which has identity and mirror as the only operations) and
C_{3v}
, where the quadratic functions fall under the vector irreps (see Table

There isn’t any universal scheme to classify generators into different irreps. Thus, the same matrices can serve as generators of different irreps in different groups. But once a bond-wavevector group is fixed, the generators’ association with a particular irrep is also fixed, and they can be deduced by looking at the matrix structure of the unstrained Hamiltonian and its
{\bf q}
expansions. In a Hilbert space of dimension
d_{H}
, these generators, say
\hat{M}^{r}
, have the property that Tr
[\hat{M}^{r}\hat{M}^{r^{\prime}}]=d_{H}\delta_{rr^{\prime}}
. This allows us to uniquely project the corrections onto the generators. In general, there will be a generator
\hat{M}^{0}\propto\hat{h}^{0}
projecting on to which will yield a scalar-potential. There may also exist generators
\hat{M}^{\alpha}\propto\hat{v}_{\alpha}
projecting on to which will yield a vector-potential. Similarly, one can identify potentials in any other irrep
r
of the bond-wavevector group. To this effect, observe that
\hat{s}^{D}
only has projection on to the velocity indicating that it can
only
give rise to a vector-potential as prescribed in the minimal coupling approach:

\displaystyle e\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\beta}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{D}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{v}_{\beta}({\bf K})]/v^{2}_{\beta},~\text{where }v_{\beta}^{2}\equiv{\rm Tr}[\hat{v}_{\beta}({\bf K})\hat{v}_{\beta}({\bf K})],

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R}).

Here
e
is the fundamental charge and has been introduced in the definition to make
\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\alpha}
have the same dimensions as the electromagnetic vector-potential.

Next, observe that
\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}
also has a component that is
\propto-i\delta_{ab,z}v_{ab,\alpha}
. Since
\delta_{ab,z}
switches sign between the orderings
ab
and
ba
, it will be beneficial to write it as
\delta_{ab,z}=\varepsilon_{ab}|\delta_{ab,z}|
, where
|\delta_{ab,z}|
is nothing but the inter-plane distance between the bonds and
\varepsilon_{ab}
is an anti-symmetric matrix with unit entries (not to be confused by the strain tensor
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
). We then introduce another pair of generators such that
\bar{v}_{ab,\alpha}\equiv-i\varepsilon_{ab}v_{ab,\alpha}
. Note that Tr
[\hat{v}_{\alpha}\hat{\bar{v}}_{\alpha}]=0
ensuring that
\hat{\bar{v}}_{\alpha},\hat{v}_{\alpha}
are different generators. However, the set of
\bar{v}_{ab,\alpha}
can still map back to the set of the velocity generators
v_{ab,\alpha}
with rearranged indices
\alpha
(e.g.
-i\varepsilon_{ab}\sigma_{ab,x}=\sigma_{ab,y}
and
-i\varepsilon_{ab}\sigma_{ab,y}=-\sigma_{ab,x}
). This case can arise when a third pseudo-vector (e.g.
\hat{z}
) is present in the problem that permits the scalar
\hat{\mathbf{v}}\times{\bf q}\cdot\hat{z}
to also be present. In such a case, we can extract vector-potentials simply by using the velocity generators:

\displaystyle e\mathcal{A}^{H,\perp}_{\beta}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[s^{H}_{\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{v}_{\beta}({\bf K})]/v_{\beta}^{2}.

(17)

If the new generators don’t map back to themselves, then they would represent some other irrep
r
of the system. In such cases, we can normalize the new generator
\hat{\bar{v}}_{\alpha}
to
\hat{M}^{\alpha}
such that
{\rm Tr}[\hat{M}^{\alpha}.\hat{M}^{\alpha}]=d_{H}
. We can lump these irreps together with the scalar form and label them by an index
r
. These generators can then be used to extract potentials in the system as:

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H,\perp}_{r}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{M}^{r}({\bf K})]/d_{H}.

(18)

For

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H,\perp}_{0}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K})]/d_{H}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\zeta\epsilon_{zz}({\bf R}){\rm Tr}[\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K})\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K})]\delta^{2}_{ab,z}/d_{H}\delta_{ab}^{2}.

For the other irreps with
r=x,y

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H,\perp}_{x}({\bf R},{{\bf K}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{M}^{x}({\bf K})]/d_{H}

\displaystyle 2\zeta\epsilon_{xz}({\bf R})\frac{|v_{x}||\delta_{ab,z}|}{\delta_{ab}^{2}},

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{M}^{y}({\bf K})]/d_{H}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\zeta\epsilon_{yz}({\bf R})\frac{|v_{y}||\delta_{ab,z}|}{\delta_{ab}^{2}},

where

The potentials from
\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H}_{r}({\bf R},{\bf K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}({\bf R},{\bf K})\hat{M}^{r}({\bf K})]/d_{H},

\displaystyle e\mathcal{A}^{H,\parallel}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})

\{\hat{v}_{\alpha}\}

\displaystyle=

(22a)

(22b)

III.2
Universal considerations of strain induced potentials

Time reversal symmetry:

Since we are dealing with mechanical deformations of the lattice, time reversal symmetry (TRS) must be preserved if already present in
h^{0}_{ab}
. This imposes the condition
h_{ab}({\bf R},{{\bf K}}+{\bf q})=h^{*}_{ab}({\bf R},{-{\bf K}}-{\bf q})
. Since
{\bf K}
is a singular point, we would need a pair of singular points
({\bf K},-{\bf K})
to preserve TRS, unless these points are related by translations of the reciprocal space. Since
v_{ab,\alpha}(-{{\bf K}})=-v^{*}_{ab,\alpha}(\bf K)
from Eq. (
III
), and
q_{\alpha}\rightarrow-q_{\alpha}
under TRS, the Hamiltonian pair at
({\bf K},-{\bf K})
is manifestly invariant under TRS in our expressions. Translating the TRS requirements to the potentials (which are real by construction) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}({\bf R},-{\bf K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K}),

\displaystyle\varphi_{r}({\bf R},-{\bf K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi_{r}({\bf R},{\bf K}),

(23)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
and
\varphi_{r}
represent the total vector-potentials and potentials in other irreps from all sources, respectively.

Pseudo electric and magnetic fields from strain induced potentials:

If the potentials were to vary smoothly from grain to grain, then from the vector-potential identified above, one can define a pseudo magnetic field with only one component directed out-of-plane as

\displaystyle B^{\rm s}({\bf R},{\bf K})\equiv\varepsilon_{\gamma\beta}\partial_{R_{\gamma}}[\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\beta}({\bf R},{\bf K})+\mathcal{A}^{H,\parallel}_{\beta}({\bf R},{\bf K})+\mathcal{A}^{H,\perp}_{\beta}({\bf R},{\bf K})],

(24)

where
\varepsilon_{\gamma\beta}
is the 2D Levi-Civita symbol (not to be mistaken with the similar-looking symbol of the strain tensor
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
). The pseudo magnetic field from the first term
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{D}
requires computing
\varepsilon_{\gamma\beta}\partial_{R_{\gamma}}u_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})=\varepsilon_{\gamma\beta}\partial_{R_{\gamma}}\partial_{R_{\beta}}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}({\bf R})=0
. That is, the displacement correction from strain acts as a vector-potential with no curl and hence cannot produce a pseudo magnetic field. This correction serves as a gauge transformation in the minimal coupling prescription
[
30
]
. On the other hand, the pseudo magnetic field from the hopping induced terms (both
\mathcal{A}^{H,\parallel}
and
\mathcal{A}^{H,\perp}
) require calculating
\varepsilon_{\gamma\beta}\partial_{R_{\gamma}}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}({\bf R})\neq 0
in general, leading to mimicking the effect of a magnetic field.

Further, just like in electromagnetism, we can also define a pseudo electric field from this vector-potential:

\displaystyle E^{\rm s}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})\equiv-\partial_{t}[\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})+\mathcal{A}^{H,\parallel}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})+\mathcal{A}^{H,\perp}_{\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})].

(25)

These fields switch sign between the pair of singular points at
{\bf K}
and
-{\bf K}
(an hence the prefix of pseudo). In addition to these, we can also define fields from
\varphi_{r}
in different irreps as

E^{\rm s}_{r,\alpha}({\bf R},{\bf K})\equiv-\partial_{R_{\alpha}}\varphi_{r}({\bf R},{\bf K}).

The
r=0
field would correspond to an electric field that doesn’t actually flip sign between the singular point pairs and can serve as a real electric field.

Strain induced shift of high-symmetry points and singular points:

Strain induced vector-potentials couple to the Hamiltonian in the form
\hat{v}_{\alpha}(q_{\alpha}+e\mathcal{A}_{\alpha})
. Such a coupling shifts the singular point from
{\bf K}
point to
{{\bf K}}-e\bm{\mathcal{A}}
. This shift comes from three sources: the lattice displacement (
\mathcal{A}^{D}
), changes to the in-plane, and that to out-of-plane hoppings(
\mathcal{A}^{H,\parallel}
and
\mathcal{A}^{H,\perp}
). Each has a different consequence. The displacement shift is associated with the deformation of the BZ vectors from the unstrained case:
-e\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\beta}=-K_{\alpha}u_{\alpha\beta}
(see the introduction to the Appendix). This is the shift that is not associated with the pseudo magnetic field and is also independent of the details of the unit cell. The other two shifts which arise from hopping corrections additionally move the singular point. As a consequence, if a singular point in the unstrained case were also a high-symmetry point of the BZ, then in the strained case, the shift due to the displacement part would not decouple the singular point from the high-symmetry point. The hopping induced shifts, on the other hand, would. In the context of graphene, this has been discussed in the literature under the label of ‘frame-effects’
[
30
]
.

IV
Applications and extensions

We now apply our results to commonly used toy models of square and triangular lattices with 1, 2 and 3 atoms per unit cell. These models are not restricted to materials and are also useful for photonic lattices, systems of coupled resonators, or any system that could be mapped to a tunable tight-binding model. The second part of this section will deal with an example of modelling strain in a system with a high dimensional Hilbert space and reducing it to smaller dimensional subsystems. Henceforth, we will be suppressing the
\mathbf{R}
-dependence of the
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
terms.

IV.1
Strain induced potentials in 2D Bravais and non-Bravais systems

In this subsection we consider strictly 2D models and write down the strain corrections and the corresponding potentials. Since there will be no out-of-plane bonds, the potentials
\varphi^{H,\perp},\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{H,\perp}
would be absent. The supporting explicit calculations are presented in the Appendices. In all the cases we discuss below,
a
will denote the lattice constant.

IV.1.1
Lattices with 1 atom per unit cell

Let us start with the very common system of a square lattice with nn hopping. This system has two relevant singular points,
\Gamma
-point (0,0) and the
M
-points
\{(\pm\pi,\pm\pi)/a\}
, where we can apply our results. Our first result allows us to write the strain corrected Hamiltonian in terms of the inverse mass tensors at those points, which are
\hat{I}_{xx}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/M})=\hat{I}_{yy}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/M})=\pm 2a^{2}t
. This leads to [up to
\mathcal{O}(q^{2})
]

\displaystyle h^{\rm Sq}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/M}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mp 4t\underbrace{\pm 2t\zeta\left(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}\right)}_{s^{H}_{\parallel}}.

(26)

Here, we have used the fact that nn distance
\delta_{ab}
is just the lattice constant
a
. These forms are corroborated by an explicit calculation in Appendix
A
. Our second result allows us to interpret this correction as a potential in the irreps of the bond-wavevector group of the singular points. The bonds connecting a given atom to its nn have a
C_{4v}
symmetry (see Fig.
3
) and both the singular points in the BZ are also
C_{4v}
, making all the bond-wavevector groups also
C_{4v}
. The quadratic forms of this group are such that
x^{2}+y^{2}
belongs to the scalar irrep
A_{1}
(see Table
1
), while
x^{2}-y^{2},xy
belong to irreps
B_{1},B_{2}
. Since this is a 1 atom per unit cell system, the Hilbert space matrix structure is trivial with
\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/M})=\mathds{1}_{1\times 1}
being the only generator of the Hilbert space. As this is already being used to represent the scalar irrep, we cannot represent any other irreps. This is also why there are no velocity (vector) terms at these singular points as they would have needed generators of the
E
-irrep to couple with. Thus, we should expect the quadratic form to only show up as
x^{2}+y^{2}
which leads to the form
\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}
. Indeed, that is what is seen in Eq. (
26
). Next, following Eq. (
22e
) with
r=0
, we find the scalar-potential to be

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H}_{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm 2t\zeta\left(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}\right).

(27)

Another 1 atom per unit cell system would be the triangular lattice. Here there are 3 singular points: the
\Gamma
and the
K,K^{\prime}
points of the hexagonal BZ (the coordinates of these in our frame are listed in the Appendix). The
K,K^{\prime}
points form a
{\bf K},-{\bf K}
pair. The inverse masses are
\hat{I}_{xx}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})=\hat{I}_{yy}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})=3a^{2}t\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})
and
\hat{I}_{xx}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})=\hat{I}_{yy}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})=-(3a^{2}t/2)\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})
, where
\hat{M}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma/K/K^{\prime}})=\mathds{1}_{1\times 1}
. Our first result allows us to write:

\displaystyle h^{\rm Tr}({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-6t\underbrace{+3t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy})}_{s^{H}_{\parallel}},

\displaystyle h^{\rm Tr}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3t\underbrace{-\frac{3}{2}t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy})}_{s^{H}_{\parallel}}.

(28)

We have used the fact that the nn distance is
a
. These are corroborated again with an explicit calculation in Appendix
A
. To interpret these corrections as potentials, observe that the bonds here have a
C_{6v}
symmetry (see Fig.
3
), and the
\Gamma
and the
K,K^{\prime}
points have
C_{6v}
and
C_{3v}
symmetry, respectively, making the bond-wavevector group to be
C_{6v}
at the
\Gamma
point and
C_{3v}
at the
K,K^{\prime}
points. Once again, because the Hilbert space structure is trivial, we can only have scalar generators. This means that no velocity (vector) terms can appear and only the quadratic form
x^{2}+y^{2}
should show up (Table
1
). Following Eq. (
22e
) with
r=0
, we can identify the scalar-potentials at these points as

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H}_{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}),

\displaystyle-e\varphi^{H}_{0}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}).

(29)

We see that for systems with just one atom per unit cell, strain only acts as a grain dependent scalar correction which can also be seen as the modulation of the chemical potential.

[

54

V.1
Multiple vector-potentials

Having established that every order of bonds can induce its own set of scalar- and vector-potentials, it is now pertinent to ask what impact it has in the analysis of the electronic structure. By extension, this will affect processes involving scattering, quantum tunnelling, etc., but this will not be addressed here. The first point to note would be that for lightly doped/gated systems, not all of 4 bands are relevant for low energy physics. Performing Löwdin’s projection on to the subspace of
(A_{1},B_{2})

[
61
]
we get the following low-energy effective Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H^{\rm Low}_{\rm BLG}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{\sqrt{3}at_{3}}{2}(q_{+}+e\mathcal{A}^{t_{3}}_{+})-\frac{1}{2m}(q_{+}+e\mathcal{A}^{t_{0}}_{+})^{2}\\
-\frac{\sqrt{3}at_{3}}{2}(q_{-}+e\mathcal{A}^{t_{3}}_{-})-\frac{1}{2m}(q_{-}+e\mathcal{A}^{t_{0}}_{-})^{2}&0\end{pmatrix},

(62)

with
m\equiv(1-\zeta\epsilon_{zz})\frac{2t_{1}}{3a^{2}t_{0}^{2}}
being the effective mass of the band, and
\mathcal{A}^{p}_{\pm}=\mathcal{A}^{p}_{x}\pm i\mathcal{A}^{p}_{y}
, for
p\in\{t_{0},t_{3}\}
. Observe also that if
t_{3}\rightarrow 0
, then the linear term would be absent. Had we started with the low energy model for the unstrained system, we would not have gotten the vector-potential coupling as the bands would have no velocity. This demonstrates the need of modelling strain in the appropriate Hilbert space and then projecting out the higher energy degrees of freedom.

The matrix elements are of the form
v(q+A^{t_{3}})+(q+A^{t_{0}})^{2}/2m=(q+A^{t_{0}}+mv)^{2}/2m+v(A^{t_{3}}-A^{t_{0}})-mv^{2}/2
. This form makes it clear that at high energies (large
q
) the system essentially acts like a parabolic system subject to the potential
A^{t_{0}}
, while at low energies (small q) the system is affected both by
A^{t_{0}}
and the constant shift from
v(A^{t_{3}}-A^{t_{0}})
. This would notably affect the position of the Dirac points. Indeed this is what we find in Fig.
5
b,c where we show the strain modification to the spectrum of bilayer graphene. The shift of the Dirac points at low energies is some non-linear function of both in- and out-of-plane vector-potentials
f(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{t_{0}},\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{t_{3}})
(here
\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{t_{3}}=\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{t_{4}}
as
\epsilon_{\alpha z}=0
). These are described in more detail in Refs.
[
63
,
64
]
. However, the higher energies are only affected by a constant shift of
\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{t_{0}}
. This means that, for a given strain, there is a threshold energy (indicated by the dashed line in the Figure
5
c inset) below which the system can be accurately modeled as behaving under the effects of a single vector-potential. This is particularly useful for analyzing experimental data (of gate dependent transport measurements, for example) where a sample would be appropriately doped and strain could be accounted for without the added complexity of multiple vector-potentials. Provided the strain and energy ranges are experimentally feasible like in bilayer graphene, this insight opens the door to a systematic treatment of strain in many other 2D materials, such as TMDCs.

VI
Conclusions

While it is known that strain corrections arise from two sources (displacements and change in hoppings as a result of displacements), in this work we have shown that the corrections from these two sources could actually be broken down into three parts and can be written directly in terms of the band structure parameters of the system. The displacement induced correction (
\hat{s}^{D}
) is the first part and couples to the velocity [Eq. (
12
)]. The hopping induced term provides the other two parts as in-plane (
\hat{s}^{H}_{\parallel}
) and out-of-plane(
\hat{s}^{H}_{\perp}
) contributions. The former is expressed in terms of the inverse mass tensor [Eq. (
12
)] and the latter in terms of the velocity and the unstrained (scalar) form of the Hamiltonian [Eq. (
14
)]. In addition, we introduced the notion of orders of bonds that is classified based on distance and the type of bonds and showed that strain corrections can be written down for each order of the bonds.

Next, we introduced the notion of bond-wavevector group that takes into account the local symmetry of a given bond and allows us to regroup the inverse mass tensor into scalar and vector terms (in general, into all irreps of the bond-wavevector group). We identified Hilbert space generators that represent various scalars and vectors and prescribed a projection technique using these generators to extract scalar- and vector-potentials from the strain correction terms identified above [set of Eqs. (
22
)]. We showed that a vector-potential like interpretation of in-plane strain is only possible for a system where the bond-wavevector group is
C_{1v}
and
C_{3v}
. Since there are multiple orders of bonds, it is natural in our theory to expect multiple scalar- and vector-potentials.

We then demonstrated the applicability of our prescription in several toy models involving square and triangular lattices with multiple atoms per unit cell and showed that the popular system of graphene is a special case that respects all the conditions necessary to let strain serve as a vector-potential. We also demonstrated how one can start from a high dimensional strained Hilbert space and arrive at effective low energy models for strained Hamiltonians. We showed that the Hilbert space projection preserves the bond-wavevector based classification in the projected system. Finally, we applied our result to strained bilayer graphene and showed the existence of multiple vector-potentials, presented a Hilbert space projected low-energy model for strained bilayer graphene and identified energy regimes where one vector-potential would be sufficient and where one would need all the potentials to correctly capture the electronic structure.

We believe that this general formulation opens up the door to many interesting investigations. It is easy to see that the approach is not limited to materials, but these principles extend to other engineered 2D systems. For example, strained carbon nanotubes of different chiralities can be modeled readily from this procedure. The effect of strain involving out-of-plane
\varepsilon_{\alpha z}
components becomes relevant in the case of hetero-strain
[
38
]
where multiple layers are deformed differently. Any system that can be mapped to a tight-binding model, such as strained photonic systems, can also benefit from the principles outlined in this work. Time-varying strain and strain induced electric fields can be studied in systems beyond graphene for nano electro-mechanical systems based applications
[
65
,
66
,
67
]
. The most immediate benefit in all of the above cases is that the theory readily provides a parametric model Hamiltonian that allows access to strain corrected wavefunctions which in turn allows us to model strain tuned ballistic quantum transport phenomena.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}).

(69)

Appendix B
{\bf q}
and strain expansions for 2 atom per unit cell structures

Square Lattice:

We consider the interesting case of
\pi
-flux square lattice whose Hamiltonian is:

h^{\pi}_{ab}({\bf k})=\begin{pmatrix}0&f({\bf k})\\
f^{*}({\bf k})&0\end{pmatrix},

(70)

where

f({\bf k})=-2t\left(\cos\frac{k_{x}+k_{y}}{2}-i\sin\frac{k_{x}-k_{y}}{2}\right).

At the
\Gamma
,
M
, Dirac (D) [at
\pm\frac{\pi}{2}(1,1)
], and Parabolic (P) points [at
\pm\frac{\pi}{2}(1,-1)
], the
{\bf q}
-expansion of
f
yields:

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\left[2-i(q_{x}-q_{y})-\frac{1}{4}(q_{x}+q_{y})^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{M}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\left[-2-i(q_{x}-q_{y})+\frac{1}{4}(q_{x}+q_{y})^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{D_{\pm}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm t\left[q_{x}+q_{y}\pm i(q_{x}-q_{y})\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm\sqrt{2}te^{\pm i\pi/4}(q_{x}\mp iq_{y})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{P_{\pm}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\left[\left\{2-\frac{1}{4}(q_{x}+q_{y})^{2}\right\}\mp i\left\{2-\frac{1}{4}(q_{x}-q_{y})^{2}\right\}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}),

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t}{2\sqrt{2}}e^{\mp i\pi/4}\left[-8+q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}\pm 2iq_{x}q_{y}\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}).

This example is non-standard as it involves the presence of a magnetic flux. The symmetry group would involve magnetic translations and systems with
2\pi n/m
flux linked with the original square plaquette, with
m
being even, would have
m
Dirac points. This enforcement arises from special gauge symmetries
[
49
]
addressing which are beyond the scope of this work. Nevertheless, we can model the system in a particular gauge as a 2 atom per unit cell structure with only mirror as the symmetry. This structure belongs to the
C_{1v}
group which allows for linear and quadratic terms to belong to the same scalar irrep
A_{1}
. The presence of the Dirac and parabolic points cannot be foreseen from spatial symmetries due to the presence of the magnetic flux, and arise from different considerations. But they are such that at the Dirac points the quadratic forms are absent while at the Parabolic points the linear terms are absent.

The explicit calculation of the leading order strain corrections to these terms [leading to
f({\bf k})+s({\bf k})
in the Hamiltonian] leads to

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\zeta\left[\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\epsilon_{xy}\right],

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle it\left[e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{x}-e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{y}\right]-t\zeta\left[\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\epsilon_{xy}\right],

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{D_{\pm}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pm\sqrt{2}te^{\pm i\pi/4}(e\mathcal{A}^{D_{\pm}}_{x}\mp ie\mathcal{A}^{D_{\pm}}_{y}),

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{P_{\pm}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}t\zeta e^{\mp i\pi/4}\left[\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}\pm 2i\epsilon_{xy}\right],

(72)

where
e\mathcal{A}^{X}_{\alpha}=K_{X,\beta}u_{\beta\alpha}
, with
X\in\{M,D_{+},D_{-}\}
.

Triangular Lattice:

We consider the case of nn Honeycomb lattice whose Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle h^{\rm Gr}_{ab}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&f({\bf k})\\
f^{*}({\bf k})&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\text{where, }f({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-te^{-i\frac{k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}}[1+2e^{i\frac{\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{2}}\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}].

(73)

The
\Gamma
- and
K
-point
{\bf q}
expansions are given by:

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-3t+\frac{t}{4}(q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle f({\bf K}_{K}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{3}t}{2}\left[q_{x}-iq_{y}\right]-\frac{t}{8}[q^{2}_{x}-q_{y}^{2}+2iq_{x}q_{y}]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}).

(74)

The bond-wavevector group at
\Gamma
is
C_{3v}
. The scalars can be constructed from the
A_{1}
irrep with the form
1,x^{2}+y^{2},x(x^{2}-3y^{2})
, or from other irreps if we can find generators to support those irreps. This also explains why the next leading order is
q^{3}
. Since at
\Gamma
-point we already have
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})=-3t\hat{\sigma}_{x}
(with the
x
Pauli matrix),
\hat{\sigma}_{x}
has to belong to the
A_{1}
irrep at
\Gamma
-point. We can also think of creating scalars using
x,y
(to have finite velocity terms) that belong to
E
irrep, but we would have to find a pair of generators that could belong to
E
irrep. Since graphene is a
2\times 2
bipartite system, we can only have off-diagonal entries in the generator. This can only be possible by the pair
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}\}
, but
\sigma_{x}
has already been used to represent
A_{1}
. Thus, at
\Gamma
we cannot have any Hilbert space generators for the vector irrep and hence there cannot be a velocity term.

The bond-wavevector group at
K,K^{\prime}
is
C_{3v}
as well. The scalars can again be from the
A_{1}
irrep with the form
1,x^{2}+y^{2},x(x^{2}-3y^{2})
, but also from
E
with form
\{x,y\},\{x^{2}-y^{2},xy\},(x^{2}+y^{2})\{x,y\},
etc., if we can find generators in
E
. At this
{\bf K}
point, since
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})=0
, we have the pair
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}\}
available and hence can represent the
E
irrep. And precisely because of the bipartite nature and that
\hat{\sigma}_{x},\hat{\sigma}_{y}
have been used up for the E irrep, we don’t have any generators left for the
A_{1}
irrep, and thus there can be no
x^{2}+y^{2}
term in the
{\bf q}
expansion.

The explicit calculations of the leading order strain corrections to these terms [leading to
f({\bf k})+s({\bf k})
in the Hamiltonian] are

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}t\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}),

\displaystyle s({\bf K}_{K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{3}t}{2}\left[e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}-ie\mathcal{A}^{K}_{y}\right]-\frac{3}{4}t\zeta[\epsilon_{xx}-\epsilon_{yy}+2i\epsilon_{xy}],

(75)

where
e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{\alpha}=K_{K,\beta}u_{\beta\alpha}
. The result at the
K^{\prime}
point is obtained by setting
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{K^{\prime}}=-\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{K^{\prime}}
and
s^{H}(-{\bf K})=s^{H*}({\bf K})
. The latter results in
i\rightarrow-i
in the
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
terms. If we were to include the nnn hopping (
t^{\prime}
) of the honeycomb lattice, this would add a diagonal term to the Hamiltonian
h^{\rm Gr}_{ab}
of the form
\delta_{ab}\bar{f}({\bf k})
where

\bar{f}({\bf k})=2t^{\prime}-4t^{\prime}\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\left(\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}+\cos\frac{\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{2}\right).

The
\Gamma
- and
K/K^{\prime}
-point
{\bf q}
expansions of this term are given by:

\displaystyle\bar{f}({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-6t^{\prime}+\frac{3}{2}t^{\prime}(q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}),

\displaystyle\bar{f}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~~3t^{\prime}-\frac{3}{4}t^{\prime}(q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}).

(76)

This just acts like two copies of the 1 atom per unit cell triangular lattice case and the same results follow. The leading order strain corrections to this term [leading to
\bar{f}({\bf k})+\bar{s}({\bf k})
in the Hamiltonian] are

\displaystyle\bar{s}^{\rm Gr}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~~~3t^{\prime}\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}),

\displaystyle\bar{s}^{\rm Gr}({\bf K}_{K/K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}t^{\prime}\zeta(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}).

(77)

Since
t^{\prime}
is in the diagonal, the scalar-potential in this case can be seen as a correction to the local chemical potential. But since the
s^{H}
correction is entirely real, the correction is the same at the
K,K^{\prime}
points. This allows for the scalar-potential correction from nnn to be interpreted as a true chemical potential correction.

Appendix C
{\bf q}
and strain expansions for 3 atom per unit cell structures

Square Lattice:

Consider the Lieb lattice whose Hamiltonian is:

h^{\rm Li}_{ab}({\bf k})=-2t\begin{pmatrix}0&\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}&\cos\frac{k_{y}}{2}\\
\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}&0&0\\
\cos\frac{k_{y}}{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(78)

The
\Gamma
- and
M
-point
{\bf q}
expansions are given by:

\displaystyle h^{\rm Li}_{ab}({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2t\begin{pmatrix}0&1-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}&1-\frac{q_{y}^{2}}{8}\\
1-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}&0&0\\
1-\frac{q_{y}^{2}}{8}&0&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}),

\displaystyle h^{\rm Li}_{ab}({\bf K}_{M}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\begin{pmatrix}0&q_{x}&q_{y}\\
q_{x}&0&0\\
q_{y}&0&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}).

(79)

Here, the
\Gamma
and
M
points have
C_{4v}
symmetry, but the bonds are
C_{2v}
. This leads to the bond-wavevector group to be
C_{2v}
. Each bond will form its own bond-wavevector group. Thus,
h_{12}({\bf K})
and
h_{13}({\bf K})
form two independent
C_{2v}
bond-wavevector groups. Note that in the previous examples, the same bond contributed to all entries in the Hilbert space. But in systems with 3 or more atoms per unit cell, this is not always the case. Once again, the scalars can be formed from
A_{1}
which contains
1,x^{2},y^{2}
functions. Since
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})\neq 0
, the matrix structure at this point must belong to
A_{1}
. This matrix covers the entries corresponding to both bonds and hence no other entries can exist. This means that the matrix form of
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})
used up the generator space to represent scalars leaving none to represent the vector irreps (which belong to
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
). Thus, we cannot have any velocity elements at the
\Gamma
-point. The prevention of the mixing of the linear terms
x,y
with the quadratic ones is the reason why the next contribution is
\mathcal{O}(q^{4})
.

At the
M
-point, where the bond-wavevector group is still
C_{2v}
,
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{M})=0
. This means that we can form matrices in the
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
irreps to contract with the vector forms
x,y
in this group. However, in this case then, we cannot have any scalar forms
1,x^{2},y^{2}
. This is why the next order contribution here is
\mathcal{O}(q^{3})
.

Further, because of how the linear and quadratic forms are split, it is clear that
\Gamma
-point only has
s^{H}
type corrections, whereas
M
-point has only
s^{D}
type corrections. Indeed, an explicit calculation gives:

\displaystyle s^{\rm Li}_{ab}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2t\zeta\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon_{xx}&\epsilon_{yy}\\
\epsilon_{xx}&0&0\\
\epsilon_{yy}&0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle s^{\rm Li}_{ab}({\bf K}_{M})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\begin{pmatrix}0&e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{x}&e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{y}\\
e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{x}&0&0\\
e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{y}&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(80)

where
e\mathcal{A}^{M}_{\alpha}=K_{M,\beta}u_{\beta\alpha}
.

Triangular Lattice:

Consider the Kagomé lattice whose Hamiltonian is:

h^{\rm Kag}_{ab}({\bf k})=-2t\begin{pmatrix}0&\cos\frac{k_{x}+\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{4}&\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\\
\cos\frac{k_{x}+\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{4}&0&\cos\frac{k_{x}-\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{4}\\
\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}&\cos\frac{k_{x}-\sqrt{3}k_{y}}{4}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(81)

The
\Gamma
- and
K
-point
{\bf q}
expansions are given by:

\displaystyle h^{\rm Kag}_{ab}({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2t\begin{pmatrix}0&1-\frac{(q_{x}+\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&1-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}\\
1-\frac{(q_{x}+\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&0&1-\frac{(q_{x}-\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}\\
1-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}&1-\frac{(q_{x}-\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}),

\displaystyle h^{\rm Kag}_{ab}({\bf K}_{K}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\begin{pmatrix}0&-1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}+3q_{y}}{4}+\frac{(q_{x}+\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}}{2}-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}\\
-1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}+3q_{y}}{4}+\frac{(q_{x}+\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&0&-1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}-3q_{y}}{4}+\frac{(q_{x}-\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}\\
1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}}{2}-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{8}&-1-\frac{\sqrt{3}q_{x}-3q_{y}}{4}+\frac{(q_{x}-\sqrt{3}q_{y})^{2}}{32}&0\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}).

Here the bonds are also
C_{2v}
and bond-wavevector group is
C_{2v}
at the
\Gamma
-point, while at
K,K^{\prime}
they are
C_{1v}
(the common subgroup of
C_{3v}
and
C_{2v}
). At the
\Gamma
-point the analysis is the same as above and we cannot have the linear functions
x,y
due to
\hat{h}^{0}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})\neq 0
. The next order also starts from
q^{4}
. Since the group is
C_{2v}
, the
x,y
or
x^{2},y^{2}
forms correspond to the distances along the direction of the bond in whatever co-ordinate system. This is the reason for the grouping of
q_{x},q_{y}
in Eq. (
C
): those correspond to
{\bf q}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{ab}
.

At the
K,K^{\prime}
points however, both the linear and quadratic functions are allowed as the group is now
C_{1v}
. Once again the
x,x^{2}
are to be replaced by the distances along the bonds. The leading order strain corrections also follow this picture leading to:

\displaystyle s^{\rm Kag}_{ab}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\zeta\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{2}&2\epsilon_{xx}\\
\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{2}&0&\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{2}\\
2\epsilon_{xx}&\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{2}&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle s^{\rm Kag}_{ab}({\bf K}_{K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}+3e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{y}}{4}+\zeta\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{4}&-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}}{2}-\zeta\epsilon_{xx}\\
-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}+3e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{y}}{4}+\zeta\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{4}&0&-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}-3e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{y}}{4}+\zeta\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{4}\\
-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}}{2}-\zeta\epsilon_{xx}&-\frac{\sqrt{3}e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{x}-3e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{y}}{4}+\zeta\frac{\epsilon_{xx}+3\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}}{4}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{\alpha}=K_{K,\beta}u_{\beta\alpha}
. The Hamiltonian at
K^{\prime}
point is obtained in the same way as was done in the triangular lattice section in Appendix
B
.

Appendix D
{\bf q}
and strain expansions for a large Hilbert space

Consider the super-Honeycomb lattice with 5 atoms per unit cell. The Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle h_{ab}^{\rm sH}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0_{2\times 2}&\hat{b}_{2\times 3}({\bf k})\\
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{3\times 2}({\bf k})&0_{3\times 3}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\text{where, }\hat{b}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\frac{k_{y}+\sqrt{3}k_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}}}&e^{i\frac{k_{y}}{2\sqrt{3}}}&e^{-i\frac{k_{y}-\sqrt{3}k_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}}}\\
e^{i\frac{k_{y}+\sqrt{3}k_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}}}&e^{-i\frac{k_{y}}{2\sqrt{3}}}&e^{i\frac{k_{y}-\sqrt{3}k_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(84)

The
\Gamma
- and
K
-point
{\bf q}
expansions are given by:

\displaystyle\hat{b}({\bf K}_{\Gamma}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\begin{pmatrix}1-ir_{1}-r_{1}^{2}/2&1+ir_{2}-r_{2}^{2}/2&1-ir_{3}-r_{3}^{2}/2\\
1+ir_{1}-r_{1}^{2}/2&1-ir_{2}-r_{2}^{2}/2&1+ir_{3}-r_{3}^{2}/2\\
\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

\displaystyle\hat{b}({\bf K}_{K}+{\bf q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\pi/3}(1-ir_{1}-r_{1}^{2}/2)&1+ir_{2}-r_{2}^{2}/2&e^{-i\pi/3}(1-ir_{3}-r_{3}^{2}/2)\\
e^{-i\pi/3}(1+ir_{1}-r_{1}^{2}/2)&1-ir_{2}-r_{2}^{2}/2&e^{i\pi/3}(1+ir_{3}-r_{3}^{2}/2)\\
\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}).

(85)

where

r_{1}\equiv\frac{q_{y}+\sqrt{3}q_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}};~r_{2}\equiv\frac{q_{y}}{2\sqrt{3}};~r_{3}\equiv\frac{q_{y}-\sqrt{3}q_{x}}{4\sqrt{3}}.

In this case the groups everywhere are
C_{1v}
and thus fall under the same category as the Kagomé lattice. The leading order strain corrections (which result in
\hat{b}+\hat{s}
in the Hamiltonian) are

\displaystyle\hat{s}({\bf K}_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}t\zeta\begin{pmatrix}3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}&4\epsilon_{yy}&3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}\\
3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}&4\epsilon_{yy}&3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\hat{s}({\bf K}_{K})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}t\zeta\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\pi/3}[3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}]&4\epsilon_{yy}&e^{-i\pi/3}[3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}]\\
e^{-i\pi/3}[3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}]&4\epsilon_{yy}&e^{i\pi/3}[3\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}-2\sqrt{3}\epsilon_{xy}]\end{pmatrix}

(86)

\displaystyle-t\begin{pmatrix}-ie^{i\pi/3}A_{1}&iA_{2}&-ie^{-i\pi/3}A_{3}\\
ie^{-i\pi/3}A_{1}&-iA_{2}&ie^{i\pi/3}A_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
A_{i}
is the same as
r_{i}
with
q_{\alpha}\rightarrow e\mathcal{A}^{K}_{\alpha}\equiv K_{K,\beta}u_{\beta\alpha}
.
