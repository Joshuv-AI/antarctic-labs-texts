Antiviral Resistance Associated With Multiple Mutations Occurring in Seasonal Influenza A(H3N2) Virus in a Patient Treated With Oseltamivir
Authors: Saira Hussain, Christina Guo, Yi‐Mo Deng, Nikita Deshpande, Presa Chanthalavanh, Natalie Spirason, Lauren Burmas, Heidi Peck, Bradley J. Gardiner, Ian G. Barr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOur case highlights the importance of early diagnosis in respiratory illness and timely detection of drug resistance so that antiviral treatment can be initiated and modified, particularly for high‐risk groups. Vaccination and early, carefully dosed antiviral treatment are key to mitigating severe influenza cases, especially in immunocompromised patients.

Introduction

Influenza is a global health problem. The World Health Organization estimates there are 3–5 million cases of severe illness and 290,000–650,000 associated deaths annually. Current guidelines recommend that patients with influenza‐related illness who require hospitalization or are at risk of developing complications should receive antiviral treatment, ideally within 48 h of the onset of illness [1]. The main antiviral medications used for influenza worldwide are neuraminidase inhibitors (NAIs) targeting the viral NA glycoprotein, with oseltamivir being the most widely used. A single cap‐dependent endonuclease inhibitor (CENI), oral baloxavir marboxil (baloxavir) has been used since 2018, but can be significantly more expensive than NAIs, limiting use in Australia and elsewhere. Frequencies of seasonal influenza viruses showing reduced or highly reduced inhibition (RI/HRI) from NAIs have remained low (< 0.7%) in recent years, especially in A(H3N2) subtype viruses, with the most frequent HRI associated substitution being NA‐H275Y in A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses [2]. Immunocompromised patients experience significant morbidity associated with influenza infection and are often prescribed NAIs at diagnosis [3]. Viral mutations that reduce susceptibility to oseltamivir occur more often in this vulnerable patient population [4]. We report a case of a kidney transplant recipient infected with A(H3N2) influenza virus, who subsequently acquired multiple neuraminidase mutations, detected in serial samples, causing highly reduced inhibition by oseltamivir.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 30‐year‐old man with cystic fibrosis and end stage renal failure received a living‐related renal transplant, with induction immunosuppression including basiliximab, prednisolone, tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil. Transplant was complicated by delayed graft function from acute tubular necrosis and early rejection requiring pulse methylprednisolone and hemodialysis. Other comorbidities included Type 2 diabetes and recurrent bacterial pneumonia. He was vaccinated for seasonal influenza 5 months prior, at the beginning of the local influenza season (Afluria quadrivalent influenza vaccine).

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

An overview of patient clinical signs and symptoms is provided in Figure 1. Eleven days post‐transplant, while hospitalized, he developed respiratory symptoms including a productive cough, dyspnea, and bibasal crackles on physical examination (Figure 1). Symptoms worsened over three days, with new fevers, sore throat, and headache. Laboratory investigations demonstrated lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count 0.3 × 109/L), a chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed patchy infiltrates in the lower lobes bilaterally, and influenza A was subsequently detected on quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

The patient was treated with a one‐week course of renally adjusted oseltamivir, 30 mg daily after dialysis plus intravenous ceftazidime 1 g daily and inhaled tobramycin 160 mg twice daily. He was discharged after partial clinical improvement, then readmitted three days later with fever and productive cough and retreated with several weeks of ceftazidime.

There was no significant improvement, with ongoing productive cough, night sweats, and nausea. Sputum cultured Aspergillus spp.; CT chest showed new diffuse centrilobular ground‐glass nodules (Figure 2), and he was commenced on posaconazole.

Nine and a half weeks posttransplant, the patient continued to test positive for influenza A and was given a second course of oseltamivir for 20 days (30 mg twice daily for 4 days then 75 mg BD for 16 days). Hypogammaglobulinemia was identified (IgG 5.7 g/L) and repleted with intravenous immunoglobulin. Cough, nausea, and night sweats persisted and qPCR testing for influenza remained positive, concerning for treatment failure. Given a high clinical suspicion of resistance, samples were referred for further testing. Whole genome sequencing using Oxford Nanopore Technologies or Illumina platforms was performed and A(H3N2) virus isolates were generated which were tested for susceptibility to NAIs oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir, and laninamivir by a phenotypic assay using the 2′‐(4‐Methylumbelliferyl)‐α‐D‐N‐acetylneuraminic acid (MUNANA) substrate method [2].

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Influenza, diagnosis, treatment, and detection of antiviral resistance is provided in Figure 1. Samples showed variable cycle threshold values (Ct 10–32) by qPCR for influenza A over the 14‐week disease course (Figure 1). IC50 values determined by phenotypic assay were compared to a reference IC50 value for influenza A(H3N2) viruses susceptible to each NAI and fold changes calculated to determine normal inhibition (NI), or RI/HRI phenotypes (Table 1). A week after treatment, known NAI resistance mutations NA‐R292K and NA‐E119V were detected transiently (Figure 1). Highly reduced inhibition to oseltamivir was confirmed phenotypically, with IC50 values 103‐ to 166‐fold higher than the seasonal median IC50 value for NI of A(H3N2) viruses. The patient continued to test influenza A(H3N2) positive for 6 weeks after oseltamivir treatment (Ct 23–27) with no NAI resistance subsequently detected. During the second course of oseltamivir treatment, he developed HRI to oseltamivir, low level RI to zanamivir (6–13 IC50‐fold change; associated with a known resistance marker deletion 245–248 in NA) but remained NI to peramivir and laninamivir. During week 15, inhaled zanamivir 10 mg twice for two weeks was commenced, which finally cleared infection and resolved clinical symptoms.

Discussion

We report a complex case of nosocomially acquired influenza A(H3N2) virus shortly after living‐related renal transplant. Infection occurred during a period of intensive immunosuppression marked by lymphopenia and hypogammaglobulinemia, resulting in insufficient immunity to clear the virus. Treatment was complicated by renal impairment leading to likely inadvertent underdosing of oseltamivir and inadequate duration with subsequent viral rebound. These factors contributed to the development of a variety of NA mutations causing antiviral failure.

Reduced inhibition to NAIs is less likely to occur in influenza A(H3N2) and influenza B viruses compared with A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses. Due to the differences in NA structure, resistance mutations in N2 and B NAs cause a significant loss of enzymatic function, resulting in reduced in vitro and in vivo viral fitness and less transmissibility [6, 7]. In cases of emergence of A(H3N2) and B viruses with reduced inhibition to NAIs in the clinic, this has generally been reported after prolonged antiviral treatment (> 10 days but often over 1 month) [5, 8]. Influenza infection in immunocompromised hosts is often associated with severe disease, higher mortality, persistent virus shedding, and prolonged infection which can require extended treatment courses, increasing the likelihood of drug resistance [3, 4, 9]. In our case, several markers associated with RI/HRI to NAIs occurred with oseltamivir treatment, transient NA‐E119V, and NA‐R292K substitutions arose as quasi‐species and were lost, but then an NA‐∆245–248 deletion emerged later and was sustained. These NA mutations have been previously reported in patients treated with oseltamivir and zanamivir [5, 8, 10]. An immunocompromised patient infected with A(H3N2), subjected to repeated dosing with oseltamivir and/or zanamivir, acquired antiviral resistance markers at NA‐E119V, NA‐R292K, NA‐∆245–248 deletion and an additional marker NA‐Q136K which confers RI/HRI to zanamivir [8]. An A(H3N2)‐infected patient who received a combination of oseltamivir and peramivir also developed NA‐R292K [5].

Dosing of antivirals such as oseltamivir in patients with renal impairment can be challenging. Accurately measuring renal function, especially when fluctuating, is difficult. While there are several published pharmacokinetic studies that have led to the current dosing recommendations, we suspect that in our case despite following these, the dose was likely insufficient, contributing to resistance development [11, 12, 13]. Given the relatively favorable side effect profile, absence of clinically significant drug interactions and the lack of routinely available therapeutic drug monitoring, clinicians could consider erring on the side of higher dosing especially early in the course of the infection to optimize efficacy and minimize the risk resistance and treatment failure occurring in the future [12, 14].

Vaccination remains a key strategy for reducing the morbidity and mortality of seasonal influenza, especially in high‐risk groups. Our patient received the Afluria quadrivalent influenza vaccine prior to transplant but still developed severe influenza. Transplant recipients often mount an inferior immune response to standard vaccination compared to the general population, and clinical trials have shown increased immunogenicity with the high‐dose quadrivalent vaccine or the adjuvanted quadrivalent vaccine, while maintaining good safety profiles and not appearing to increase the risk of allograft rejection [15].

Antivirals approved in Australia for influenza treatment are three NAIs, oseltamivir (oral) which is first‐line, zanamivir (inhaled, or intravenous only for critically ill patients), peramivir (intravenous), and one CENI, baloxavir (oral), which has not been easily accessible until recently. Treatment with inhaled zanamivir [16] or intravenous peramivir [17] has shown similar efficacy to oral oseltamivir in hospitalized patients. The genetic barrier for resistance to zanamivir is lower than oseltamivir and peramivir, and the most frequent resistance mutation NA‐H275Y, occurring in A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses, confers HRI to oseltamivir and peramivir. Baloxavir has been shown to be safer and significantly shorten hospitalization compared to oral oseltamivir [18], however, a retrospective study of immunocompromised patients showed baloxavir to be equally effective to oseltamivir [19]. Moreover, in clinical trials resistance rates for baloxavir were up to 10.9% in adults and 19.5% in children in A(H3N2) infections [20]. Given that immunocompromised patients can develop multiple NAIs resistance mutations, combination therapy with the two different drug classes could prove more effective than monotherapy. Clinical trials with combination therapy of oseltamivir and baloxavir for patients hospitalized with severe influenza have shown no significant benefit compared to monotherapy overall, although a post hoc analysis of immunocompromised patients suggests improved outcomes in this subgroup [21, 22]. In our case, zanamivir was chosen given its ease of administration and availability.

Influenza infection in solid organ transplant recipients can be associated with more severe disease, such as in our case, and is associated with higher rates of hospitalization and lower respiratory tract infection. Complications of influenza including bacterial and fungal superinfections contribute to excess morbidity and mortality. Invasive aspergillosis, such as seen in our case, typically occurs in severely immunocompromised hosts [23]. Oseltamivir dose reduction is recommended in patients with renal impairment [1], but as demonstrated in our case, this can inadvertently lead to underdosing and resistance. Our case highlights the importance of early diagnosis in respiratory illness and timely detection of drug resistance so that antiviral treatment can be initiated and modified, particularly for high‐risk groups. Vaccination and early, carefully dosed antiviral treatment are key to mitigating severe influenza cases, especially in immunocompromised patients.
