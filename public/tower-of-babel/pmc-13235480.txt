Lumbar discectomy patients with a criminal incarceration history are at risk of higher postoperative opioid utilization, emergency department visits, and adverse events
Authors: Anthony E Seddio, Michael J Gouzoulis, Wesley Day, Julian Smith-Voudouris, Joshua G Sanchez, Sahir S Jabbouri, Daniel R Rubio, Jonathan N Grauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNearly 700,000 individuals are released from US correctional facilities annually. Those with a history of criminal incarceration (HCI) experience inferior health outcomes related to limited healthcare access, chronic disease burden, and economic marginalization. These inequities may increase complications following spine surgery, such as lumbar discectomy (LD); however, postoperative outcomes in this population remain unknown.MethodsPatients undergoing single-level LD between 2016 and 2023 were identified from a large, national, multi-insurance claims database. Exclusion criteria included: age <18, concurrent lumbar procedures, multilevel surgery, <90-day follow-up, or trauma, neoplasm, or infection within 90 days preoperatively. Patients were stratified by documented prior incarceration, imprisonment, or problems related to prison release. (+)HCI patients were matched 1:4 to (−)HCI controls by age, sex, and comorbidity burden. Multivariable logistic regression assessed associations between HCI and social determinants of health, as well as 90-day postoperative outcomes. Five-year reoperation was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsHCI was identified in 146 LD patients; after matching, 141 (+)HCI and 551 (−)HCI patients remained. Relative to matched controls, (+)HCI patients demonstrated higher odds of housing insecurity (odds ratio [OR] 4.62, p<.001), economic inequality (OR 4.25, p<.001), and unemployment (OR 3.82, p=.001), with no differences in Medicaid (p=.260) or Medicare coverage (p=.288), relative to commercial insurance. Postoperatively, (+)HCI patients were prescribed higher opioid morphine milligram equivalents (+361.8, p=.022) and had greater odds of emergency department visits (OR 2.83, p<.001) and minor adverse events (OR 2.46, p=.006). No differences were observed in readmission (p=.082) or 5-year reoperation (4.1% vs. 2.5%, p=.100).ConclusionsAmong the largest spine surgery cohort to date with a criminal incarceration history, LD patients with HCI were associated with substantial social determinants of health barriers, increased postoperative opioid exposure, emergency department utilization, and complications. These inequities warrant attention within postoperative care pathways and underscore the need for further investigation to optimize support strategies for this vulnerable and growing population.Keywords: Healthcare equity, Social determinants of health, SDOH, Lumbar discectomy, Outcomes, Criminal incarceration, Healthcare access, Emergency department utilization

Introduction

The United States incarcerates the highest number and proportion of its citizens compared to the rest of the world [1,2]. As of 2022, 1.19 million people are under correctional supervision in the nations prisons and jails, which represents a 500% increase over the last four decades [3]. Of those incarcerated, an estimated 95% are eventually released from correctional facilities, with nearly 700,000 returning to the community setting annually [4,5]. While currently incarcerated individuals are guaranteed healthcare under the eight amendment of the United States Constitution [6], this does not apply following release. Literature has reported that, despite an increased prevalence of chronic diseases [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]] and substance use disorders [12,13] compared to the general population, patients with history of criminal incarceration (HCI) have inferior access to medical care, even when controlling for insurance status [[8], [9], [10], [11],14].

In a general surgery cohort, Mao et al. retrospectively evaluated 6,675 currently incarcerated patients who underwent various abdominal and vascular surgeries. These authors found significantly higher risk of 30-day morbidity related to surgical site infection, unplanned intubation, and pneumonia, but comparable readmission and mortality [15]. In the orthopaedics literature, a single-institution cohort study by Wood et al. [16] of 20 currently incarcerated patients versus 171 controls who underwent primary total joint arthroplasty found these patients were nearly five-times more likely to have postoperative infection and over three-times more likely to require revision arthroplasty. In a spine population, a single-institution cohort study by Findlay et al. [17] of 27 currently incarcerated patients versus 81 controls found incarcerated patients at nine times greater odds of nonunion following spinal fusion.

While the aforementioned single-institution cohort studies of surgical outcomes elucidate potential postoperative outcome disparities faced by those currently incarcerated, an even larger population exists with HCI in the community setting following release [4,5]. In the spine literature, while the association of various social determinant of health (SDOH) factors such as race, income inequality, and educational attainment on perioperative outcomes have been increasingly investigated [[18], [19], [20]], to our knowledge, no study has assessed orthopaedic surgery outcomes of previously incarcerated patients once they leave correctional facilities.

Thus, the current study leveraged a large, national, multi-insurance dataset to evaluate underlying SDOH barriers, postdischarge opioid prescriptions, 90-day complications, postoperative healthcare utilization, and 5-year reoperation rates following single-level lumbar discectomy (LD) among the vulnerable patient population in the community setting with HCI.

Methods

Data source and study population

Data was abstracted from 2016 to Q1 2023 from the PearlDiver Mariner 170 database (PearlDiver Technologies). This is a large, national, multi-insurance administrative claims database that contains nearly 170 million patient records. The use of this database has been well established in the spine literature [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. As data from this database are deidentified, our Institutional Review Board has determined studies using this dataset exempt from review.

Adult patients undergoing single-level lumbar laminotomy/discectomy were identified by Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 63030. Exclusion criteria included: patients less than 18 years of age, those undergoing other concurrent lumbar spinal procedures, multilevel laminotomy/discectomy, those with fewer than 90 days of follow-up, and those with trauma, neoplasm, or infection diagnosed within 3 months prior to surgery.

Those with HCI were then identified based on International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes ICD-10-D-Z651 and ICD-10-D-Z652 documented prior to surgery. Patients with (+)HCI and without (−)HCI were matched 1:4 using an exact match methodology within the PearlDiver Bellwether software by age, sex, and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI) to account for potential differences in underlying clinical demographics and comorbidity burden.

Clinical demographic data tabulated before and after matching included age, sex, and ECI, which represents a quantified measure of comorbidity burden[26] for the presence of the following: congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, valvular disease, pulmonary circulation disorders, peripheral vascular disorders, hypertension, paralysis, other neurological disorders, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, hypothyroidism, renal failure, liver disease, peptic ulcer disease, AIDS/HIV, lymphoma, solid tumor without metastasis, rheumatoid arthritis/collagen vascular diseases, coagulopathy, obesity, weight loss, fluid/electrolyte disorders, blood loss anemia, deficiency anemia, alcohol abuse, drug abuse, psychoses, and depression.

Insurance status and SDOH barriers

Nonclinical demographic factors assessed for their association with HCI included insurance status (commercial, Medicare, and Medicaid) and SDOH barriers, including housing insecurity (inadequate/lack of housing or homelessness), economic inequality (poverty, low income, or financial hardship), and unemployment status.

Insurance status was determined by the billing claim for the index procedure, while SDOH barriers were determined by ICD codes for the defined SDOH domains (Appendix 1). Demographic factors identified in <11 patients were reported as such to protect patient privacy.

Postdischarge opioid utilization

Opioid utilization was identified by the prescription billing records using Uniform System of Classification (USC) codes for opioids: 02211, 02212, 02214, 02221, 02222, and 02232. The current study captured all opioid prescriptions within 90 days following discharge.

Opioid utilization was quantified by morphine milligram equivalents (MME) by the formula: strength per unit×opioid conversion factor×unit count using the PearlDiver “MME” function. Prescription length was quantified by the mean number of days of prescribed opioid supply to be used as needed for pain management (standing order). History of opioid use disorder (opioid abuse/dependence) was identified for patients with and without HCI using relevant ICD codes (Appendix 1).

Postoperative adverse events

Postoperative adverse events occurring within 90 days were identified using associated ICD codes. Severe adverse events were noted by the occurrence of sepsis, surgical site infection, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, or cardiac arrest. Minor adverse events were noted by the occurrence of pneumonia, urinary tract infection, transfusion, wound dehiscence, hematoma, or acute kidney injury. Any adverse event was noted by the occurrence of any severe or minor adverse events.

Postoperative healthcare utilization metrics were then assessed. These included occurrences of emergency department (ED) visits and hospital readmissions within 90 days following LD. These were identified using ED-specific evaluation and management ICD codes and the PearlDiver “admission” function, respectively. The occurrence of ED visits for each postoperative week for patients with versus without HCI was then assessed. Additionally, the occurrence of an established patient visit with a primary care provider (PCP) was determined by CPT codes (99395, 99396, 99397). The presence of an established PCP visit within 12 months prior to LD was controlled for in posthoc analyses of ED utilization between cohorts.

To determine 5-year all-cause reoperation-free survival, the occurrence of subsequent lumbar reoperation within 5 years of the index procedure was determined based on CPT codes.

Data analyses

Clinical demographic characteristics, including mean age, sex, and ECI, were tabulated for each patient with and without HCI and compared by univariable analysis using a Student t test and Pearson χ²-test where appropriate. These comparisons were repeated after matching.

Nonclinical demographic characteristics were assessed with univariable analysis between matched cohorts using a Pearson χ²-test. Multivariable logistic regression controlling for age, sex, and ECI was performed for patients with, relative to without HCI, to determine the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of association with each nonclinical factor.

Opioid utilization, including mean MME and mean length of prescription were compared between matched cohorts using a Student t test. Multivariable linear regression controlling for age, sex, and ECI was performed to determine the MME excess or deficit and prescription length for patients with, relative to without HCI. Additionally, preoperative opioid use was controlled for in posthoc analyses of opioid MME prescription patterns between cohorts.

The occurrence of 90-day adverse events was then compared with univariable analysis between matched cohorts using a Pearson χ²-test. Multivariable logistic regression controlling for age, sex, and ECI of all adverse events was performed for patients with relative to without HCI to determine the OR and 95% CI of the association with postoperative adverse events.

The 5-year reoperation-free survival of the matched cohorts was assessed with Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. A log-rank test (Mantel–Cox) was then used to statistically analyze the rates of reoperation between (+)HCI patients versus (−)HCI controls.

All statistical tests were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) embedded within the R statistical package of the PearlDiver Bellwether software. Statistical significance for all analyses was defined as p<.05. Tables were constructed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation), and figures were created with Prism 10 (GraphPad Software).

Multivariable analysis of the association between HCI and postdischarge opioid utilization are shown (Table 3). Discussion

There is increasing awareness that SDOH factors need to be considered in regard to healthcare delivery [[18], [19], [20]], and growing impetus to improve equity in orthopaedic care [[27], [28], [29]]. To that end, those with HCI have been shown to have inferior access to healthcare despite their greater mental and physical health issue burden [7,8,11,30]. Since nearly 700,000 incarcerated individuals are released from correctional facilities annually [4], and incarceration rates continue to rise each year in the United States [2], this vulnerable patient population living in the community setting undergoing orthopaedic surgery should not be ignored. While few studies have investigated orthopaedic care of currently incarcerated patients [16,17,[31], [32], [33]], to our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate spine surgery outcomes for patients once they leave correctional facilities and return to the community setting.

Patients undergoing single-level LD with HCI were, on average, younger, more frequently male, and had higher comorbidity burden than those without HCI, which is consistent with the distinct demographics of this vulnerable population [[34], [35], [36]]. Based on these differences, matching was performed to improve clinical comparability of the studied populations, and these variables were further controlled for in multivariable analyses.

The current study further explored nonclinical demographic factors of HCI patients. Patients undergoing single-level LD with and without HCI had similar odds of insurance coverage; however, HCI patients were associated with significantly greater odds of facing SDOH barriers on multivariable analysis. Specifically, patients with HCI demonstrated heightened odds of housing insecurity, economic inequality, and unemployment. These nonnegligible inequities, which have been linked to inferior health outcomes [8,11,30,37], should be considered within perioperative care pathway refinement of this vulnerable patient population, as they may have amplified the inferior postoperative outcomes and heightened emergency healthcare service utilization seen in the current study.

With clinicians and policymakers recognizing the importance of addressing healthcare inequities, the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services and Department of Health and Human Services have recently mandated (as of Q1 2024) that hospitals screen for SDOH barriers and report these data to the Inpatient Quality Reporting program [38]. This national initiative, which aims to close the disparity gap in healthcare equity should motivate providers to elucidate these important SDOH barriers within preoperative screening of all patients, especially those with HCI, and refine perioperative care pathways accordingly.

The current study found patients with HCI who underwent single-level LD were prescribed significantly greater opioid MME following discharge. The reason for this is likely multifactorial. Patients with HCI were found to be at nearly four-times greater odds of prior opioid use disorder in the current study, which is consistent with literature noting that 25% of all incarcerated individuals in the US have opioid use disorder [12,13]. Although higher opioid tolerance in those with history of preoperative opioid use may play a role [39], even when controlling for this, postoperative opioid MME remained significantly elevated in LD patients with HCI.

Additionally, patients with HCI were found to be at nearly three-times greater odds of 90-day postoperative ED utilization and remained significantly elevated even when controlling for the presence of an established PCP. Notably, the observed elevated rates of ED visits were found to be isolated to the first 3 weeks postoperatively, then statistically comparable thereafter. Providers should consider the potential learned patterns of healthcare delivery among patients living in the community setting with HCI. During the period of time in which these patients were incarcerated, those seeking healthcare beyond what could be provided on-site at correctional facilities are often initially brought to an ED and then transferred to the appropriate facility [40]. Therefore, this unique healthcare utilization pattern while incarcerated may reinforce this approach even after release. It therefore remains critically important for providers to refine preoperative counseling to encourage non-ED follow-up care.

Patients with HCI encouragingly demonstrated similar 5-year reoperation rates compared to those without HCI, which differs from prior literature of currently incarcerated patients who have been noted to be at greater odds of revision following total joint arthroplasty [16]. This incongruity in superior long-term outcomes of orthopaedic surgery for patients with HCI, while likely multifactorial and procedure specific, is nonetheless encouraging. It may reflect that, despite the significant SDOH barriers faced by this patient population, the community healthcare setting may provide a more equitable environment with regard to long-term reoperation in those undergoing LD.

Limitations

As with any administrative database study, the current analysis is limited by reliance on coded data, which precludes assessment of patient-reported functional outcomes. Additionally, opioid utilization was determined by prescription data, which represents a proxy for consumption and may not fully reflect patient use patterns. The identification of HCI based on ICD-10 coding likely improved specificity; however, this approach remains susceptible to underreporting, as some patients may be reluctant to disclose a history of incarceration, potentially reducing sensitivity. Furthermore, the database does not provide information on the timing or duration of incarceration relative to surgery. Thus, we are unable to assess whether outcomes differ based on recency or length of incarceration, which may influence both clinical and socioeconomic characteristics. These limitations are inherent to the study design and highlight opportunities for future investigations to incorporate more granular incarceration history variables and gather patient-reported functional outcomes data to further contextualize postoperative outcomes among this vulnerable patient population living in the community setting with HCI.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study found significantly elevated opioid MME prescriptions, greater odds of minor adverse events, and ED visits following single-level LD in patients with HCI. However, 5-year reoperation rates for patients with and without HCI were found to be encouragingly similar. The heightened odds of ED utilization within the postoperative period, which is thought to be a marker of issues with healthcare access, highlights ongoing healthcare inequities faced by this population. As the number of criminally incarcerated individuals continues to rise each year in the United States, the population in the community healthcare setting with HCI setting seeking spine surgery is expected to parallel in growth and should therefore be considered in the context of care pathway refinement to improve healthcare equity of the studied patient population.
