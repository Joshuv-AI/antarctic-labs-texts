Generalized Quantum Signal Processing and Non-Linear Fourier Transform are equivalent
Lorenzo Laneve

Abstract
Quantum signal processing (QSP) and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) are powerful techniques for the development of quantum procedures. They allow to derive circuits preparing desired polynomial transformations. Recent research [Alexis et al. 2024] showed that Non-Linear Fourier Analysis (NLFA) can be employed to numerically compute a QSP protocol, with provable stability. In this work we extend their result, showing that GQSP and the Non-Linear Fourier Transform over S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) are the same object. This statement — proven by a simple argument — has a bunch of consequences: first, the Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm can be turned, with little modifications and no penalty in complexity, into a unified, provably stable algorithm for the computation of phase factors in any QSP variant, including GQSP. Secondly, we derive a uniqueness result for the existence of GQSP phase factors based on the bijectivity of the Non-Linear Fourier Transform. Furthermore, NLFA provides a complete theory of infinite generalized quantum signal processing , which characterizes the class of functions approximable by GQSP protocols.

Introduction

Quantum signal processing (QSP) is a technique that draw significant attention in the past years

The rich class of polynomials provided by QSP, along with the fact that the ansatz is generally carried out on a single qubit, makes QET and QSVT flexible and generally applicable tools in quantum algorithmic design: they were shown to unify — and sometimes even improve — many known quantum algorithms, including Hamiltonian simulation
[

,

,

, amplitude amplification

More recently, the traditional QSP theory was enlarged with what is called
generalized QSP
(or GQSP)
[
17
]
: roughly speaking, while traditional QSP only allowed for determined subsets of
SU(2)
as valid processing operators, GQSP allows any single-qubit operation, which corresponds to an enlarged set of achievable polynomials. This not only gave further results and improvements
[
18
,
19
]
, but gives a more complete treatment of single-qubit quantum signal processing, as it shows that the full class of polynomials admits a GQSP decomposition.

Usually, QSP applications consists in three steps: first one has to design a polynomial
P(z)
approximating some desired target function. The second step is
completion
, where one computes a polynomial
Q(z)
satisfying
|P|^{2}+|Q|^{2}=1
, a condition necessary for
P
to be embedded in a quantum state. The last step is to compute numerically the processing operators
A_{k}
constituting the protocol for
(P,Q)
. While the original QSP theorems are proven using an induction argument, the corresponding procedure — the so-called
layer stripping
method —, does not guarantee numerical stability, and cannot provide reliable results for polynomials of too large degrees. An important line of research is devoted to this task, and many results were found using several strategies, such as root finding
[
20
,
21
]
, Prony’s method
[
22
]
, and iterative methods
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. Iterative methods are generally faster and more stable than algebraic methods, but they seem to fail in the
fully-coherent regime
, i.e., when
\norm{P}_{\infty}\simeq 1
. Moreover, all these methods are developed for the traditional QSP, while numerically stable algorithms for GQSP are not known.

More recently, QSP was shown to have connections with the
Non-Linear Fourier Transform
(or NLFT)
[
27
,
28
]
. In particular it was shown that the inverse NLFT can be used to stably compute a QSP protocol for a desired target function, using the
Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm

[
29
]
. Moreover, NLFT provided an answer to what was called
infinite quantum signal processing

[
26
]
, where the QSP protocols have infinite length, producing non-polynomial target functions.

In this work we show that the NLFT not only is useful to compute the processing operators for QSP protocols, but that GQSP and NLFT are indeed the same theory. More precisely, while traditional QSP protocols (where the
A_{k}
are
X
-rotations) correspond to imaginary sequences in the pre-image of the NLFT, protocols with
Y
-rotations correspond to real sequences, and generally complex sequences yield GQSP protocols. This simple statement has many consequences: first, this gives a complete theory of
infinite generalized quantum signal processing
, which can be further investigated by directly working on the NLFT. Moreover this confirms that, given a target function, computing the QSP processing operators is computationally equivalent to the computation of the inverse NLFT, regardless of the variant of QSP taken into consideration, i.e., also the latter problem is (partially) solved by the aforementioned algorithms, which can then be employed to invert the NLFT in other contexts. The third is that the Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm (presented in
[
29
]
and later improved in
[
30
]
) can be turned — with little modification and without any penalty in complexity — into a unified algorithm for QSP factorization in any variant and, in particular, gives the first stable algorithm for GQSP, even in the fully-coherent regime.

This work starts with Section
2
, which gives a comprehensive treatment of the QSP literature, showing how the QSP zoo — including QSVT and QET-U — can all be reduced to a convenient version of GQSP. Section
3
will then give a brief and informal introduction to the NLFT over
SU(2)
, along with a bunch of important properties. Section
4

5
is devoted to review the Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm, as well as the Half Cholesky method of
[
30
]
, showing how to extend them for arbitrary complex polynomials. A Python implementation of the unified Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm can be found in
[
31
]
.

1.1
Preliminaries and notation

For a complex matrix
A
, we use
A^{*}
to denote entrywise conjugation, and
A^{\dagger}
for the conjugate transpose. Given a function of complex variable
f(z)
, its conjugate function is defined as
f^{*}(z):=\overline{f(1/\overline{z})}
, where
\overline{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of the number
z
. We use
\mathbb{D}
to denote the unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
, while
\mathbb{T}
is used to denote the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
. Similarly,
\mathbb{D}^{*}:=\{1/\overline{z}:z\in\mathbb{D}\}
is the (closed) region of the complex plane outside of the unit circle. The Hardy spaces
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
are the spaces of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
, whose norm

\displaystyle\norm{f}_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}:=\sup_{0\leq r<1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f(rz)|^{2}

is bounded. Roughly speaking,
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
can be represented by a formal power series with non-negative frequencies. Similarly, one defines the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{*})
for the holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}^{*}
, whose formal power series have only non-positive frequencies. We define the Pauli matrices

\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \ Y=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \ Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},

while
H
is the single-qubit Hadamard gate satisfying
HZH=X,HXH=Z
.

2
General theory of single-qubit quantum signal processing

We give an introduction to the theory of quantum signal processing (QSP). In particular we reduce all the variants of QSP known across the literature to a slightly modified version of Generalized QSP
[
17
]
, which we will then relate to the NLFT. In this setting, we have access to a single-qubit operator parameterized by a variable
z\in\mathbb{T}
(the
signal
operator). For example, we can take the following operator

\displaystyle\tilde{w}=\operatorname{diag}(z,1)\ .

We then choose a sequence
A_{k}\in SU(2)
of single-qubit operations independent of
z
(the
processing
operators), and we construct a protocol as follows

\displaystyle A_{0}\tilde{w}A_{1}\tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}A_{n}\ .

(1)

If we start, say, in the
\ket{0}
state, then by matrix multiplication one can see that the final state will be of the form

\displaystyle P(z)\ket{0}+Q(z)\ket{1}

where
P(z),Q(z)
are polynomials in
z
of degree
n
, satisfying
|P(z)|^{2}+|Q(z)|^{2}\equiv 1
on
\mathbb{T}
. It is then natural to ask whether
any
such polynomial pair
(P,Q)
admits a decomposition of the form of (
1
), and the answer was proven to be positive
[
17
]
.

A second signal operator found in the literature
[
20
]
is the following

\displaystyle\tilde{v}=\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1})\ .

Because of the presence of
z^{-1}
, the polynomials produced by a protocol using
\tilde{v}
will be Laurent polynomials in general, and since
z
and
z^{-1}
have two degrees of distance between each other, such Laurent polynomials will have definite parity, i.e., will be either even or odd in
z
. That said, similarly to the first case, any pair of normalized, definite-parity Laurent polynomials can be decomposed if one replaces
\tilde{w}
with
\tilde{v}
in (
1
).

From now on we will call the first variant
analytic
QSP, while we use
Laurent
QSP to denote the second form. While Laurent QSP is the first one considered in the literature, analytic QSP is easier to visualize and has a much cleaner relationship with the NLFT, thus we will mainly focus on it, and the rest of this section is devoted to showing that every single-qubit QSP variant encountered in the literature can be reduced to the analytic case. We first show that analytic and Laurent QSP are perfectly equivalent, in the following sense:

Lemma 1

(
[
32
]
)
.

If the pair
(P,Q)
is constructed by a sequence of processing operators
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n}
using analytic QSP, then the pair of Laurent polynomials

\displaystyle(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}):=(z^{-n}P(z^{2}),z^{-n}Q(z^{2}))

is constructed by Laurent QSP using the same processing operators.

Proof.

If
A_{0}\tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}A_{n}\ket{0}=(P,Q)
, then by replacing
\tilde{w}
with
\tilde{w}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(z^{2},1)
, we construct
(P(z^{2}),Q(z^{2}))
. The claim holds by simply noticing that
\tilde{v}=z^{-1}\tilde{w}^{\prime}
, and collecting the
z^{-1}
.
∎

Hence, if one wants to construct some definite-parity Laurent polynomial
(P,Q)
using Laurent QSP, this can always be converted to an analytic version
(z^{n/2}P(z^{1/2}),z^{n/2}Q(z^{1/2}))
, essentially squeezing the support from
\{-n,\ldots,n\}
to
\{0,\ldots,n\}
, and then deriving the protocol in the analytic picture. The corresponding processing operators will be valid also for the original polynomials, by replacing back
\tilde{w}
with
\tilde{v}
.

Usually, either because constructions require it or for ease of implementations, the
A_{k}
’s are not allowed to be general
SU(2)
unitaries. The usual choice is to consider only operators of the form

\displaystyle A_{k}=e^{i\phi_{k}X}

where the
\phi_{k}
are usually called
phase factors
. In both Laurent and analytic pictures, constraining the processing operators to be
X
-rotations gives the additional condition that
P
must have real coefficients, and
Q
must have purely imaginary coefficients. In this work we will refer to the case where the
A_{k}
’s are constrained to be
X/Y/Z
-rotations as
X/Y/Z
-constrained QSP, respectively. The following theorem sums up what said so far:

Theorem 2

(Generalized QSP, adapted from
[
17
]
)
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z]
of degree
n
. There exist phase factors
\lambda,\phi_{k},\theta_{k}\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
such that:

\displaystyle e^{i\lambda Z}e^{i\phi_{0}X}e^{i\theta_{0}Z}\ \tilde{w}\ e^{i\phi_{1}X}e^{i\theta_{1}Z}\ \tilde{w}\cdots\tilde{w}\ e^{i\phi_{n}X}e^{i\theta_{n}Z}\ket{0}=(P,Q)

if and only if
|P(z)|^{2}+|Q(z)|^{2}\equiv 1
on
\mathbb{T}

This is slightly different from the original GQSP theorem of
[
17

SU(2)
rotations, while they use asymmetric rotations and a reflection. This version — equivalent up to global phases —, however, is a direct generalization of the traditional QSP ansätze.

We remark that GQSP can be equivalently expressed in the Laurent picture, with a clear characterization given by Lemma
1

2.1
Chebyshev and reflection QSP

The reader more familiar with QSVT than QSP might find the theory above slightly different. Originally, QSP was formalized as a protocol that constructs a polynomial
P(x)
, for a real variable
x\in[-1,1]
given by the signal operator
[
33
,
3
]

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x&i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&x\end{bmatrix}

and constraining the signal operators to be
Z
-rotations
A_{k}=e^{i\phi_{k}Z}
. In order to obtain Chebyshev QSP from Laurent QSP (argument also shown in
[
20
,
11
]
), we simply do a change of variable
x=(z+z^{-1})/2
in a
X
-constrained Laurent QSP.

\displaystyle e^{i\phi_{0}X}\tilde{v}\ e^{i\phi_{1}X}\tilde{v}\cdots\tilde{v}\ e^{i\phi_{n}X}=\begin{bmatrix}P(z)&Q(z)\\
-Q^{*}(z)&P^{*}(z)\end{bmatrix}\ .

Since
\tilde{x}=H\tilde{v}H
, the above protocol is equal to the following

\displaystyle He^{i\phi_{0}Z}\tilde{x}e^{i\phi_{1}Z}\tilde{x}\cdots\tilde{x}e^{i\phi_{n}Z}H=H\begin{bmatrix}P^{\prime}(z)&Q^{\prime}(z)\\
-Q^{\prime*}(z)&P^{\prime*}(z)\end{bmatrix}H\ .

By the Hadamard transformation, we obtain:

\displaystyle P^{\prime}=\frac{P+P^{*}}{2}+\frac{Q-Q^{*}}{2},\,\,\,Q^{\prime}=\frac{P-P^{*}}{2}-\frac{Q+Q^{*}}{2}\ .

Since we have an
X
-constrained QSP protocol, then
P\in\mathbb{R}[z,z^{-1}],Q\in i\mathbb{R}[z,z^{-1}]
, which implies that
P^{\prime}(z^{-1})=P^{\prime}(z),Q^{\prime}(z)=-Q^{\prime}(z^{-1})
. By exchanging the variables we obtain:

\displaystyle P^{\prime\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p^{\prime}_{k}T_{k}(x),\,\,\,iQ^{\prime\prime}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\prime}_{k}U_{k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

where
T_{k},U_{k}
are the Chebyshev polynomials of first and second kind, respectively, which satisfy

\displaystyle T_{k}(x)=\frac{z^{k}+z^{-k}}{2}=\cos(k\theta),\,\,\,U_{k-1}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=\frac{z^{k}-z^{-k}}{2i}=\sin(k\theta)

with
z=e^{i\theta}
and
x=\cos\theta
. To summarize, Chebyshev QSP is of the form

\displaystyle e^{i\phi_{0}Z}\tilde{x}e^{i\phi_{1}Z}\tilde{x}\cdots\tilde{x}e^{i\phi_{n}Z}=\begin{bmatrix}P^{\prime\prime}(x)&iQ^{\prime\prime}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
-iQ^{\prime\prime*}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&P^{\prime\prime*}(x)\end{bmatrix}

where any definite-parity
P^{\prime\prime},Q^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
can be constructed
[
11
]
. The reflection QSP is similar to the Chebyshev variant, except that the signal operator is

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=\begin{bmatrix}x&\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&-x\end{bmatrix}\ .

The reflection variant is the heart of the quantum singular value transformation
[
5
]
, and leverages the fact that the signal operator is Hermitian (the QSVT theorem can be proven by showing that
\tilde{r}
appears in the Cosine-Sine decomposition of the block-encoding unitary
[
6
]
). The reduction to Chebyshev QSP is straightforward by replacing

\tilde{r}=-ie^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}Z}\tilde{x}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}Z}\ .

3
The Non-Linear Fourier transform over
SU(2)

The Non-Linear Fourier transform is a mathematical tool with its own history and line of research
[
28
,
34
]
. We define it briefly and state some useful properties, inviting the interested reader to check
[
27
]
for a more comprehensive overview.

Definition 3
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a complex sequence in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The
Non-Linear Fourier Transform
over
SU(2)
is defined informally as the infinite product

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{F}(z)=\prod_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|F_{n}|^{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}1&F_{n}z^{n}\\
-F_{n}^{*}z^{-n}&1\end{bmatrix}

Notice that
{\mathcal{G}}_{F}
produces a function from
\mathbb{T}
to
SU(2)
. We use
(a,b)
as a shortcut to denote the unique
SU(2)
element whose first row is
(a,b)
, so that
{\mathcal{G}}_{F}(z)=(a(z),b(z))
. We introduce some important definitions: a function
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is called
inner
if
|f|=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. Two functions
a,b
are said to share a (non-trivial) common inner factor
f
if
a=fa^{\prime},b=fb^{\prime}
for some
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
.

Definition 4

(
[
34
,
27
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{H}_{\geq k}
be the space of measurable functions
(a,b)
such that:

•

aa^{*}+bb^{*}=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
;

•

a\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{*})
with
a(\infty)>0
, and
b\in z^{k}H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
;

•

a^{*},b
share no common inner factor.

Moreover, we denote with
\mathbf{H}=\bigcup_{k}\mathbf{H}_{\geq k}
.

The second condition implies the Laurent series of
a
has degrees in
(-\infty,0]
(with zero-frequency coefficient being positive), while the series of
b
has degrees in
[k,+\infty)
.

Theorem 5

(
[
34
,
27
]
)
.

The NLFT is a bijection between the space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq k})
of one-sided square-summable sequences starting from
k
and
\mathbf{H}_{\geq k}
.

For completeness we also report some properties of the NLFT.

Theorem 6

(
[
27
]
)
.

Suppose we have two sequences
F,G
, and
{\mathcal{G}}_{F}=(a,b)
.

(i)

Shift property:
if
G_{n}=F_{n-k}
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{G}=(a,z^{k}b)
;

(ii)

Composition property:
if the support of
F
is entirely on the left of the support of
G
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{F+G}={\mathcal{G}}_{F}\cdot{\mathcal{G}}_{G}
;

(iii)

Phase property:
if
|c|=1
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{cF}=(a,cb)
;

(iv)

Reflection property:
if
G_{n}=F_{-n}
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{G}=(a^{*}(z^{-1}),b(z^{-1}))
;

(v)

Conjugation property:
if
G_{n}=F_{n}^{*}
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{G}=(a^{*}(z^{-1}),b^{*}(z^{-1}))
.

Another important property is that the support of
b
follows the one of
F
: more precisely, if
F_{n}
is supported on
\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{2}\}
, then
b(z)
will be a polynomial of the form

b(z)=\sum_{k=n_{1}}^{n_{2}}b_{k}z^{k}\ .

a
will thus be a polynomial with the same support, but shifted so that its last non-zero coefficient is positive and on the zero frequency (see Figure
2
).

Theorem 7

For completeness, we also briefly discuss how to obtain a NLFT sequence from a GQSP protocol. Considering a protocol of the form as in Theorem

4.3
Switching the polynomials

While the convention with the NLFT is to construct
(a,b)
, for a desired
b
, the current convention in QSP the target polynomial is actually the left polynomial
P
in
(P,Q)
. Once we found the inverse NLFT, this section showed so far how to find phase factors for
(Q,P)
. Finding the phase factors for
(P,Q)
is straightforward by multiplying on the right by

\displaystyle(Q,P)\cdot iX=(iP,iQ)

In order to obtain the desired

(or equivalently, we multiply the obtain inverse NLFT by

). The

can be easily incorporated in the last GQSP processing operator using the properties of Pauli matrices:

\displaystyle e^{i\phi_{n}X}e^{i\theta_{n}Z}\cdot iX=e^{i(\phi_{n}+\frac{\pi}{2})X}e^{-i\theta_{n}Z}\ .

Thus, it is sufficient to transform
\phi_{n}\leftarrow\phi_{n}+\frac{\pi}{2},\theta_{n}\leftarrow-\theta_{n}
to obtain
(P,iQ)
. We multiply by
iX
instead of appearently more convenient

, because

preserves the subalgebra of

-constrained QSP. Indeed, if

turns out to be real, then

will be purely imaginary, and the complementary

will be real. Therefore, the phase factors for
(P,iQ)
will be
X
-constrained. Similarly, one can swap the two polynomials in the

-constrained subalgebra by multiplying by

on the right.

The first procedure — which we recall in Algorithm

6
Discussion

In this work we consider Non-Linear Fourier Analysis and the
SU(2)
-Fourier transform for different variants of quantum signal processing, extending the results of
[
27
,
29
]
. In particular, we show that different variants of QSP (that correspond to subalgebras of the
SU(2)
matrix polynomials
[
21
]
), correspond to constraints on the NLFT sequence. In particular, a generally complex sequence can always be translated to a GQSP protocol.

We highlight that, in light of this work, a proof of Ya-Ju Tsai from 2005
[
34
]
characterizing the image of compactly supported sequences through the
SU(2)
-NLFT can be seen as an alternative proof for GQSP. The same statement gave a uniqueness result for the phase factors in GQSP, as shown in Theorem
10
. Moreover,
\mathbf{H}
gives the image of a possible extension of GQSP to one-sided infinite sequences of phase factors. By the relation
F_{k}=i\tan(\phi_k)e^{2i\psi_{k}}
found in (
3
), we can conclude
\phi_{k}\rightarrow 0
(like in
[
26
]
), but the prefactors
\psi_{k}
need not converge, as they only control the phase of
F_{k}
. For the same reason, however, they do not influence the convergence of the processing operators, since

\norm{A_k - \id}=\norm{e^{i\psi_k Z} e^{i\phi_k X} e^{-i\psi_k Z} - \id}=2\sin(\frac{\abs{\phi_k}}{2})

which converges to
0
regardless of the behaviour of
\psi_{k}
. In other words, the effects of
\theta_{k}
in an infinite GQSP protocol become negligible as
k\rightarrow\infty
, as expected.

We also showed how the Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm in
[
29
,
30
]
can be easily extended to generally complex sequences, giving a provably stable algorithm for the phase factors computation of GQSP, with the notable property that the produced protocol will be in a minimal subalgebra, i.e., whenever the desired polynomial pair
(P,Q)
can be constructed with only
X/Y
rotations, then the double GQSP rotations will automatically reduce to a single
X/Y
rotation.

NLFA is a promising tool: first, it is interesting to understand whether the Riemann-Hilbert-Weiss algorithm can be further optimized, by improving or even removing the dependence on
\eta
, and/or further bringing down the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(n\operatorname{poly}\log n)
. Moreover, NLFA might be a crucial tool in understanding the properties of M-QSP: in particular, the rigidity of the layer stripping argument — which is not always possible in the multivariate case
[
38
,
39
,
32
]
— makes it hard to deduce characterizations of the M-QSP polynomials, and a treatment based on Riemann-Hilbert factorization is subject to future work.
