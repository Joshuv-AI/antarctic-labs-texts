The in vivo CAR-T paradigm shift—Reflections from Boston and the rise of programmable immunity
Authors: Paloma H Giangrande
Published: 2026

Main text

The message from Boston during the mid-May 2026 conference week was unmistakable: cell therapy is moving beyond centralized manufacturing and into the era of in vivo reprogramming. What had long seemed like a distant goal is now emerging as a practical and disruptive reality.

That shift was amplified by an unusual convergence of scientific events across the city. The 29th American Society of Gene & Cell Therapy (ASGCT) Annual Meeting, the 6th mRNA-Based Therapeutics Summit, TIDES USA, and PEGS Boston together turned Boston into a single, interconnected forum for gene, cell, RNA, oligonucleotide, peptide, and antibody innovation. Across these meetings, the same themes kept surfacing: advances in nucleic acid design, more precise targeting strategies, and increasingly programmable delivery systems.

For years, the nucleic acid and cell therapy communities viewed in vivo chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell engineering as an elegant but remote ambition. Today, the conversation has changed. By avoiding the clinical, logistical, and financial burdens of ex vivo leukapheresis, genetic modification, and reinfusion, in vivo platforms can turn the body into an autologous bioreactor. The central question is no longer whether immune cells can be engineered safely in situ, but how quickly these approaches can be scaled, refined, and expanded beyond oncology into autoimmune disease.

A front-line view of RNA innovation

I have had the rare opportunity to watch this transition firsthand. As Senior Vice President of Research and Development at Orbital Therapeutics, I have seen programmable, transient immunity move from theory to compelling non-human primate data—including complete B cell depletion in the blood, spleen, and lymph nodes without toxic chemotherapy preconditioning. That experience has only deepened my confidence in the promise of next-generation RNA technologies.

The field’s momentum is also visible in a powerful wave of consolidation. Beyond early moves such as Eli Lilly’s acquisitions of Orna and Kelonia, 2025 brought a rapid succession of major transactions that signaled growing confidence in in vivo cell engineering.

Taken together, these deals suggest that large pharmaceutical companies are no longer simply hedging. Leading innovators and key advances

While large pharmaceutical companies are consolidating platforms through acquisition, emerging biotechs are driving the pace of clinical translation while bench work is focused on exploring novel modalities beyond RNA and delivery platforms characterized by innate tropism with tissue to cell specificity without the need of a surface ligand. Their progress was a central theme throughout Boston’s conference week.

CREATE Medicines: Validation at clinical scale

CREATE Medicines drew significant attention in Boston with the announcement of a $122 million Series B financing round co-led by Newpath Partners and ARCH Venture Partners. At the mRNA-Based Therapeutics Summit, the company also reported that more than 50 patients have been dosed across its in vivo CAR clinical programs, giving it the largest translational dataset publicly described in the field to date.

A major contributor to the success of CREATE’s platform is its linear mRNA engineering approach, in which sequence and structural optimization have enabled several key advances. These include (1) durable expression in T cells exceeding 7 days, (2) sustained expression in monocyte-based programs lasting 12–14 days, and (3) the ability to administer repeat doses in humans without clinically meaningful reactogenicity, including cumulative dosing up to 10,000 μg of mRNA (approximately 333-fold higher than that used in the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine) without observable reactogenicity. These findings have been presented at major scientific conferences, including ASCO Annual Meeting, AACR Annual Meeting, SITC Annual Meeting, among others.

CREATE’s proprietary mRNA-LNP platform goes beyond a single-cell strategy through what it describes as “multi-immune programming.” Rather than targeting only T cells, the platform is designed to program T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and myeloid cells in parallel in vivo.

Just as important, CREATE’s ownership of its manufacturing infrastructure helps address a translation-to-scale bottleneck that has long constrained nucleic acid startups.

ParcelBio: Modular targeting with built-in flexibility

At ASGCT 2026, ParcelBio (Parcel Biosciences) offered a complementary model of programmable delivery. The company recently emerged from stealth with a $13.2 million seed round and its proprietary “STAmP” (Stabilized mRNA and Targeting Platform) technology. Its lock-and-key approach allows researchers to stabilize an mRNA payload while swapping surface ligands to direct that payload to a broad range of nontraditional cell types. It is exactly the kind of modular nucleic acid platform that MTNA has long championed.

From science fiction to strategic conviction

The energy in Boston reflected a broader industry shift—some observers have described as an advanced-therapy “gold rush.” The field is moving away from labor-intensive, bespoke models and toward more scalable, structured development strategies. That transition is being enabled by increasingly sophisticated engineering, especially non-viral LNPs and mRNA systems equipped with precise surface ligands that can target specific cells directly in circulation.

Editorial outlook: What comes next

For Molecular Therapy Nucleic Acids, the implications of this in vivo surge are profound. Data from ASGCT, CREATE’s expanding clinical footprint, and multibillion-dollar commitments from large pharmaceutical companies are together reshaping the translational playbook.

Over the next several years, the field may reach a major regulatory and clinical inflection point. Multicenter phase 1 trials are likely to expand as preclinical leaders—including Orbital’s OTX-201 program under the BMS banner—move into human dosing. As first-in-human results emerge, attention will shift from animal proof-of-concept to clinical safety, long-term tolerability, and the durability of B cell “immune resets” in patients.

At the same time, competition in delivery technology is intensifying. The next 24 months may determine whether the field ultimately favors permanent, single-infusion genomic integration through engineered viral vectors or the transient, tunable, and redosable kinetics enabled by circular RNA, long-lived linear RNA, and targeted LNPs. Emerging from this clash of modalities is a third distinct front: the rise of specialized platforms like Kano Therapeutics, and 4basebio, which are actively pioneering circular single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) delivery for in vivo cell engineering. By leveraging the inherent stability and high fidelity of circular ssDNA, these innovators aim to bypass the immunogenicity of viral vectors and the transient limitations of RNA, potentially offering a highly precise, durable, yet non-integrating sweet spot for genetic reprogramming directly inside the patient.

Even so, editorial enthusiasm must be matched by scientific discipline. As these platforms advance into pivotal clinical studies, three priorities should remain central.

1.Tissue-specific homing kinetics and the prevention of off-target payload delivery.
