Tertiary lymphoid structures in cancer: spatiotemporal heterogeneity, immune orchestration, and translational opportunities​​
Authors: Shuxuan Deng, Yanjie Chen, Bin Song, Heng Wang, Shanshan Huang, Kongming Wu, Qian Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid aggregates that form in or in close proximity to tumors and other chronically inflamed tissues, where they serve as crucial sites for local antigen presentation, lymphocyte priming, and adaptive immune coordination. Increasing evidence across diverse cancers supports TLSs as key modulators of antitumor immunity, correlating with improved patient prognosis and response to immune checkpoint blockade. Yet, TLSs exhibit striking spatiotemporal and functional heterogeneity—ranging from immune-stimulatory to immune-suppressive—which complicates their clinical interpretation and therapeutic targeting. Recent studies have focused on elucidating the molecular cues driving TLS induction, the mechanisms regulating their maturation, and their dynamic interactions with the tumor microenvironment. In this review, we provide an integrated overview of these advances and discuss the clinical and translational implications of TLSs as prognostic biomarkers and immunotherapeutic targets.Keywords: Tertiary lymphoid structures, Tumor microenvironment, Immunotherapy, Spatial heterogeneity, Prognostic biomarkers, TLS-targeted strategies

Background

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly PD-1/PD-L1 blockades, has revolutionized cancer treatment by reactivating tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells to eliminate malignant cells [1–3]. However, the limited objective response rates—ranging from 20% to 40% [4–6]—and the frequent emergence of therapeutic resistance underscore our incomplete understanding of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and highlight the need to move beyond T cell-centric paradigms [7–10]. Recent studies have identified tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs)—ectopically formed lymphoid aggregates in non-lymphoid tissues—as pivotal coordinators of adaptive immunity with the potential to overcome immunotherapy resistance [11].

Histologically, TLSs are characterized by spatially segregated B cell follicles, T cell zones, and specialized high endothelial venules (HEVs), resembling secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) such as lymph nodes [12–14]. Unlike SLOs, which form during embryogenesis, TLSs arise de novo under pathological conditions including chronic inflammation and cancer [15]. Clinically, TLSs that display germinal center (GC) features—terms many studies label as “mature” (see Sect. 3 for staging definitions and caveats)—have been strongly associated with prolonged survival and enhanced ICI efficacy in patients with melanoma [1], non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [2, 16], and breast cancer [17]. These structures promote antitumor immunity by supporting B cell activation, antibody production, and CD8⁺ T cell priming, thus bridging humoral and cellular immune responses [11, 18]. Notably, there is currently no field-wide consensus on the nomenclature and criteria for TLS “stages,” which complicates cross-study comparisons; throughout this section we therefore retain study-specific terminology and refer readers to Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS" for a harmonized framework.

Nonetheless, TLSs exhibit dual functional roles. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and NSCLC, TLS enrichment has been associated with the accumulation of immunosuppressive regulatory B cell (Breg) subsets that facilitate tumor progression [19, 20]. In prostate and esophageal cancers, plasma cells contribute to immunosuppression through IgA [21] and IgG4 [22] production. Moreover, TLSs found in gastric cancer peritoneal metastases and early-stage HCC lesions—defined by the respective study authors as “immature” TLSs—reveal structural deficiencies that may contribute directly to immune evasion, a concept elaborated in Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS" [23, 24]. These observations underscore the importance of TLS structural maturity, spatial localization, and cellular composition in determining their immunological function.

Single-cell and spatial omics studies have recently revealed TLS heterogeneity [25, 26]. For example, TLSs can be classified into “mature,” “conforming,” and “deviating” types based on B-cell differentiation trajectories (see Sect."Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS"). While conforming TLSs resemble mature TLSs in immunological function and correlate with anti-PD-1 responsiveness, deviating TLSs—driven by tumor-derived metabolic disruptions such as TDO2-mediated tryptophan catabolism—accumulate kynurenine, impair B cell differentiation, and hinder TLS maturation [27].

These insights challenge the T cell-centric paradigm of antitumor immunity and call for a new integrative framework that incorporates TLS biology into diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. However, clinical translation of TLS-based approaches faces several key challenges. First, current TLS detection methods lack standardized scoring systems [28]. Second, the structural and functional heterogeneity of TLSs remains insufficiently understood, limiting their application as prognostic or predictive biomarkers [29]. Third, emerging strategies aimed at modulating TLSs—such as cytokine agonists and biomaterial-guided lymphoid neogenesis—are still in experimental stages with limited clinical validation [30]. Addressing these gaps will require synergistic integration of mechanistic insights, technological advances, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

This review synthesizes recent progress in TLS biology and its translational implications from three interconnected perspectives. First, we dissect the molecular drivers of TLS formation, drawing parallels with lymphoid organogenesis while highlighting tumor-specific regulatory mechanisms. Second, we deconstruct TLS heterogeneity through cutting-edge spatial and single-cell profiling, aiming to establish a conceptual framework that reconciles their dual roles in immune activation and suppression. Finally, we evaluate the clinical potential of TLSs as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, proposing standardized analytical pipelines and individualized immunotherapy strategies. By bridging mechanistic discovery with translational challenges, this review aims to reshape our understanding of TLSs as dynamic hubs orchestrating tumor immunity and resistance.

Induction and developmental mechanisms of TLS

TLS formation reflects the reactivation of SLO-like developmental programs in pathological contexts. Rather than a linear process, it is a dynamic continuum shaped by inflammatory cytokines, immune–stromal cross-talk, and tumor-specific environmental signals. Three broad stages can be distinguished: initiation, structural organization, and functional maturation.

TLS induction and development​​

Stage I - Initiation. TLS initiation is triggered by inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α released by tumor and stromal cells [31–34]. These activate NF-κB and STAT3 pathways and induce chemokines (CXCL13, CCL19, CCL21), which recruit lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells, naïve lymphocytes, and dendritic cells (DCs) [35, 36]. In tumors, classical LTi functions can also be performed by diverse subsets, including ILC2, Th17, Tfh, NK, CD8⁺ T cells, and fibroblast-like stromal cells [37–41]. Pioneer cells expressing LTα1β2 engage LTβR on stromal and endothelial cells [42, 43], sustaining chemokine and adhesion molecule expression and promoting HEV differentiation [44, 45]. HEVs display peripheral node addressin (PNAd) and present CCR7 ligands (e.g., CCL21) to facilitate lymphocyte entry, whereas stromal/follicular dendritic cell (FDC) networks produce CXCL13 and CCL19, together enabling the continuous recruitment of T cells, B cells, and DCs [46, 47]. In endothelial Notch loss-of-function models, loss of signaling de-repressed HEV programs (PNAd, MAdCAM-1) and induced spontaneous TLS-like structures in kidney, liver, and lung, highlighting endothelial Notch as a brake on TLS initiation in inflammatory contexts [48].

Stage II - Structural organization. CXCL13 and CCL19/21 orchestrate the spatial segregation of B and T cells via CXCR5 and CCR7, respectively [49, 50], while LTα1β2 further promotes adhesion molecule expression to stabilize follicle-like clustering. Vascular and stromal activation, exemplified by LIGHT-VTP–induced CCL21 and macrophage-derived IL-1β/LTα, yields TLSs with MECA-79⁺ HEVs and distinct B/T compartments in preclinical pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor and lung carcinoma models [51], with related vascular–stromal programs also observed in human lung cancer and experimental TLS models [52, 53]. Tumor-associated fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs), derived from mesenchymal or pericyte precursors, phenotypically resemble reticular cells in lymphoid organs and form CCL19-expressing scaffolds that interconnect TLSs and support T-cell migration [54].

Stage III - Functional maturation. Within these organized structures, antigen-loaded DCs prime CD4⁺ T cells, which differentiate into Tfh cells under IL-6/IL-12 and TGF-β–SATB1 signaling [41]. Tfh cells (BCL6⁺CXCR5⁺ICOS⁺) provide CD40L–CD40 stimulation to B cells [55–57], while macrophage-derived BAFF/APRIL and antigen-presenting CAFs further reinforce activation [58, 59]. With sufficient co-stimulation, B cells initiate GC reactions: IL-21 induces activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) and drives somatic hypermutation (SHM) [60, 61], while CXCR4⁺ centroblasts and centrocytes interact with FDCs and Tfh in polarized dark/light zones [62, 63]. Spatial transcriptomics in renal cell carcinoma confirmed local GC activity [64]. Mature TLS thereby generate long-lived plasma cells producing high-affinity IgG antibodies and memory B cells, which strengthen antitumor immunity across multiple human cancers [64, 65] (Fig. 1) (Table 1).

The generation and maturation of TLS in TME. TLS induction begins with proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) released by tumor or stromal cells, which activate chemokine expression (CXCL13, CCL19, CCL21) via NF-κB and STAT3 signaling. These chemokines recruit lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells or functionally analogous subsets (Th17, ILC2s, NK cells, CD8⁺ T cells), along with B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and dendritic cells. LTi-expressed LTα1β2 activates LTβR on stromal and endothelial cells, driving HEV formation and upregulation of adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1), enabling lymphocyte infiltration and aggregation. Early TLS exhibit unstructured clusters, which progressively organize into distinct B and T cell zones under chemokine-mediated guidance. Further maturation involves FDC network development and germinal center (GC) formation, supporting somatic hypermutation and B cell differentiation into plasma cells and memory cells, aided by T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and myeloid-derived cytokines (e.g., BAFF, IL-21). Microenvironmental regulation of TLS

TME exerts profound influence on TLS homeostasis, fine-tuning whether these aggregates progress toward functional maturation or collapse into suppressive states. In a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) mouse model, mechanistic work demonstrated that IL-33–activated ILC2 amplify LTβR signaling and induce CCL19 and CXCL13 [37], while in HCC, studies have shown that macrophage-derived CCL3-CCR5 signaling enhances antigen uptake and CD4⁺ T-cell priming, thereby favoring TLS induction [103]. Studies in cGasKI and Trex1⁻/⁻ murine models, as well as tumor-bearing lung cancer mice, showed that endothelial cGAS activation induces CCL5 and upregulates CCR5 in T cells, creating an IRF1-dependent feedback loop that amplifies CD8⁺ infiltration and promotes TLS maturation; these findings were further supported by human cancer datasets [104]. In the liver, Kupffer cells sensing tumor-derived extracellular vesicle DNA activate ATM/ATR pathways, release TNF and CXCL10, and orchestrate the assembly of antimetastatic TLS [105]. Genetic lesions may also contribute, as VHL mutations in clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC) may potentiate NF-κB–linked inflammatory programs that are permissive for TLS formation [106].

Conversely, immunosuppressive and metabolic cues can derail TLS development. In ovarian cancer, evidence from ID8 murine models and spatial genomics analyses demonstrated that tumor-derived IL-4 signals through macrophage IL-4R to impair effector recruitment [107]. This finding contrasts with in vitro studies showing that IL-4 can promote GC B-cell differentiation [108], thereby highlighting the strong context dependence of IL-4 signaling in TLS regulation. In HCC, tumor-derived tryptophan catabolism via TDO2 generates kynurenine, which blocks B-cell differentiation and TLS maturation [27], while in NSCLC, ACAT1-driven mitochondrial reprogramming was found to elevate ROS, suppressing B-cell activation—a defect reversible by NAC or ACAT1 deletion [109]. Finally, in HCC and related tumor settings, integrated single-cell and spatial analyses revealed that excess glutamine reprograms atypical memory B cells through α-KG–BATF/T-bet–mTORC1 signaling, reinforcing immature-like TLS phenotypes [110] (see Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS" for staging nuances).

Taken together, these findings illustrate that TLS induction is rooted in conserved lymphoid-organizing pathways but is extensively reshaped by tumor- and context-specific signals. Canonical developmental steps alone cannot capture the broad diversity of TLS phenotypes; the structural and functional heterogeneity of TLS—including distinct maturation states and noncanonical variants—is discussed in detail in Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS".

Spatiotemporal heterogeneity of TLS

TLS are not uniform entities but represent highly dynamic and context-dependent lymphoid structures whose functions range from potent tumor rejection to active immune suppression [11, 111, 112]. While Sect. "Induction and Developmental Mechanisms of TLS" outlined the canonical steps of TLS induction and maturation, these processes alone cannot fully explain the diverse phenotypes observed across tumor types and treatment contexts [19, 110, 113]. Here, we conceptualize TLS heterogeneity as emerging from four interrelated axes: (i) developmental maturation, reflecting the degree of lymphoid organization from early aggregates (E-TLS) to primary follicle-like TLS (PFL-TLS) and secondary follicle-like TLS (SFL-TLS); (ii) functional polarization, shaped by the balance between effector and regulatory immune subsets; (iii) fate trajectory, capturing structural remodeling or involution under therapeutic pressure; and (iv) spatial localization, recognizing that tumor topography profoundly influences TLS identity and immune function.

For clarity and consistency throughout this review, when referring to studies that mention lymphoid aggregates (LA), immature TLS (iTLS), or mature TLS (mTLS), we instead use the unified terms E-TLS, PFL-TLS, and SFL-TLS. Beyond these canonical maturation stages, recent reports have described atypical phenotypes—such as suppressive [114, 115], dysfunctional [27, 116], or involuted TLS [117, 118]—defined by distinctive structural, cellular, or functional features across tumor contexts. These classifications are not yet standardized but are used here as descriptive operational terms. Their characteristics are discussed within each subsequent subsection (3.1–3.4). By integrating these dimensions, this framework delineates how TLS composition and organization evolve across space and time, ultimately shaping their prognostic and therapeutic impact in cancer (Fig. 2) (Table 2).

Classification and key features of Tumor-Associated TLS subtypes

​​TLS maturation heterogeneity​​

The degree of structural and functional maturation is the most established dimension of TLS heterogeneity. Based on progressive lymphoid organization, tumor-associated TLS are commonly classified into E-TLS, PFL-TLS, and SFL-TLS [120, 121], each reflecting distinct immune capabilities.

E-TLS are early-stage lymphoid collections composed of intermingled CD20⁺ B cells and CD3⁺ T cells, lacking T/B cell segregation, FDC networks, and HEVs [24]. They are frequently detected in early tumorigenesis, such as hepatocellular dysplastic nodules, and display transcriptional signatures enriched in both inflammatory mediators (e.g., CCL17, LTA) and inhibitory molecules (e.g., PD-L2, Tim-3) [122]. In addition, studies across cancer types indicate that E-TLS are accompanied by regulatory T cells and immunosuppressive macrophages, fostering a tolerogenic microenvironment that correlates with poor prognosis and reduced responsiveness to ICIs [119, 123]. E-TLS therefore act as a transitional niche with ambiguous roles in antitumor immunity.

PFL-TLS exhibit rudimentary structural features such as partial T/B cell zonation and the presence of sparse CD21⁺/CD35⁺ FDC networks. CXCL13⁺ stromal cells are often detected, but GC markers are absent, and HEVs may be irregular or underdeveloped [120, 124]. PFL-TLS provide only limited support for B-cell class switching (IgG/IgA) and do not drive high-affinity antibody maturation. Clinically, tumors enriched in PFL-TLS often exhibit transient and suboptimal antitumor responses [64]. For example, gastric primary tumors harbor a predominance of SFL-TLS, whereas peritoneal metastases are dominated by E-/PFL-TLS, accompanied by immunoregulatory macrophages and Tregs that promote IL-6/TGF-β-mediated tumor progression. Notably, the enrichment of E-/PFL-TLS in peritoneal metastases has been linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment and may contribute to poorer prognosis and reduced responsiveness to ICI therapy [23].

SFL-TLS represent the fully mature stage of tumor-associated lymphoid neogenesis, defined by compartmentalized architecture with CXCR5⁺ B-cell follicles, CCR7⁺ T-cell zones, GCs containing FDCs and HEVs [125]. SFL-TLS resemble SLOs in sustaining GC-dependent B-cell activation, SHM, and the generation of high-affinity tumor-reactive antibodies [110]. In addition to enhancing humoral immunity, SFL-TLS also sustain effector T-cell responses, including CD103⁺CD8⁺ Trm and NK cells with robust cytotoxic activity [126]. Across multiple tumor types, the presence of SFL-TLS has been consistently associated with favorable prognosis and improved responses to ICIs, underscoring their role as the most functionally competent TLS subtype and a promising biomarker for immunotherapy stratification [125].

The continuum from E-TLS to SFL-TLS reflects increasing immune competence. Yet, TLS that fail to complete this trajectory may instead acquire suppressive or dysfunctional features in hostile tumor microenvironments, linking maturation status to broader functional polarization (see Sect. "TLS Functional Polarization​​").

​​TLS functional polarization​​

While TLS maturation enhances the potential for adaptive immune activation, their cellular composition and microenvironmental context can redirect their function toward immunosuppression or dysfunction. Two noncanonical TLS phenotypes have emerged across multiple tumor types: suppressive TLS, which actively inhibit antitumor immunity despite partial structural organization, and dysfunctional TLS, which are structurally impaired and immunologically inert.

Suppressive TLS are enriched in Tregs, M2-like macrophages, and Bregs, shifting niches from activation to immune evasion. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), spatial multi-omics revealed TLS within solid nodules accumulate exhausted CD8⁺/CD4⁺ subsets and lose the B cell–endothelium–T cell triad near HEVs [114]. Complementary NSCLC analyses defined “declining/late” TLS characterized by CXCL13 down-regulation, increased Treg/TGF-β signaling, and hypoxia/stress programs, in contrast to “activated” TLS enriched for GC B cells and Tfh cells, which correlated with improved outcomes [115]. TLS enriched in CD21⁺CD20⁺ Bregs have been observed in melanoma, predicting worse survival, whereas AID⁺ activated B cells indicated benefit [127]. In Kras/p53-driven LUAD, activated Tregs restrained DC–T cell interactions, whereas Treg depletion restored proliferation and induced regression [128].

Dysfunctional TLS are defined by architectural disarray and impaired B-cell maturation. In high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), CAF-rich CXCL12 niches segregated from CCL19/CCL21 and plasma-cell encapsulation correlated with GC deficiency and early relapse [116]. In HCC, Stereo-seq, single-cell, and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) resolve a deviating TLS trajectory with disrupted organization and impaired B-cell differentiation driven by tumor-cell TDO2→kynurenine metabolism; metabolic restriction—low tryptophan or the TDO2 inhibitor LM10—rescues TLS maturation and synergizes with PD-1 blockade [27].

Notably, suppressive and dysfunctional TLS may arise independently of maturation status often and coexist within the same tumor, particularly in advanced or immune-excluded contexts. Their presence is associated with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy, underscoring the need to distinguish TLS functionality beyond histologic maturation alone.

​​TLS fate trajectory​​

In response to therapeutic intervention, TLS can undergo dynamic structural remodeling that reflects a transition from immune activation to contraction or immune memory maintenance. Currently, the only fate trajectory with direct evidence is the emergence of involuted TLS, reported in HCC as a therapy-induced end-stage morphology [117, 118].

Involuted TLS represent a terminal stage of structural regression often seen in post-treatment or chronically inflamed tumor environments. These structures lack GC markers entirely and instead display concentric architectures: CD20⁺ B cell halos surrounding a CD8⁺ T cell-rich core, enriched in TCF1⁺ stem-like and tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) subsets. Functional assays and transcriptomic analyses suggest that involuted TLS may serve as long-lived immunological niches capable of supporting memory recall upon re-challenge [117, 118].

These findings indicate that TLS are not static structures but can dynamically adapt to therapeutic and microenvironmental pressures. Involuted TLS may constitute an endpoint of functional remodeling, capable of sustaining antitumor immunity in a GC-independent manner. Whether other trajectories, such as senescent phenotypes, exist remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

​​Spatial modulation of TLS identity and function

TLS exhibit non-random spatial organization across cancers, most often located in the stroma and concentrated at invasive margins, whereas intratumoral TLS are relatively rare [125]. Importantly, their anatomical positioning is tightly coupled with both functional polarization and maturational status, such that tumors may harbor effector-prone intratumoral TLS, immunosuppressive peritumoral TLS, or site-specific variations in TLS developmental stage. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) and colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM), intratumoral TLS were enriched in Tfh and CD8⁺ T cells, which reflected effector activity and favorable outcomes. In contrast, peritumoral TLS accumulated Tregs and M2 macrophages and were associated with immunosuppression and poor prognosis. Most peritumoral TLS were E-/PFL-TLS; however, the presence of SFL-TLS predicted better survival, with CCL19 linked to early stages and CXCL13 to maturation [129–131]. In ccRCC, proximal TLS adjacent to tumors were more frequently SFL-TLS, while distal TLS remained in early forms, linking location with developmental stage [123]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer (CRC), three-dimensional spatial mapping revealed that TLS-adjacent infiltrative margins could evolve into suppressive niches enriched with Tregs, PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, and PD-L1⁺ tumor cells [132]. In gastric cancer, core-localized TLS function as immune-activating hubs enriched in CD8⁺PD-1⁺ and CD4⁺FoxP3⁻ T cells, whereas peritumoral TLS frequently co-localize with M2 macrophages and exhausted T cells, indicating location-linked functional divergence [133]. These findings emphasize that TLS spatial distribution is tightly linked to maturation and immune function, shaping effector activity intratumorally, suppression peritumorally, and immaturity-driven escape in metastatic sites.

These observations underscore that TLS heterogeneity cannot be reduced to a simple mature–immature dichotomy. The most pressing unresolved questions concern the developmental trajectory of TLS, the true nature of suppressive and dysfunctional subsets, and the reversibility of fate states under therapeutic pressure. It remains unclear whether E-/PFL-TLS invariably progress toward functional maturity or instead stall and convert into tolerogenic aggregates, and why suppressive TLS emerge in some tumors as stable lineages while in others they appear as maladaptive derivatives. Addressing these controversies will require longitudinal, multi-omic profiling and functional interrogation in clinically relevant models. Ultimately, resolving these questions is crucial not only for refining TLS classification but also for deciding whether therapies should aim to broadly induce TLS formation, selectively promote their maturation, or actively reprogram suppressive niches into effective immune hubs.

Functional roles of TLS in tumor immunity

As outlined in Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS", TLS can adopt diverse states that range from stimulatory to suppressive. In the present section, the focus shifts from descriptive heterogeneity to mechanistic underpinnings of TLS function. Specifically, we examine how TLS regulate tumor immunity through molecular and cellular pathways: by enabling antibody production and sustaining cytotoxic and memory T-cell responses, but also by fostering immunosuppressive circuits, pathogenic antibody production, or structural immaturity. These complementary perspectives highlight TLS as both facilitators and barriers of antitumor immunity (Fig. 3).

Humoral immunity: local B-cell activation and antibody production

A central antitumor function of SFL-TLS is to support B-cell maturation and antibody diversification within ectopic GCs. In SFL-TLS, B cells undergo GC reactions with the help of T cells, including SHM, class-switch recombination (CSR), and differentiation into antibody-secreting plasma cells [64, 134]. TLS primarily generate IgG and IgA, whereas IgM is less abundant, indicating that most B cells within TLS have completed maturation [127, 135]. Among IgG subclasses, IgG1 and IgG3 mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), playing direct roles in tumor clearance [136]. Notably, subclass switching from IgG3 to IgG1 (IgG3-1 sCSR) is enriched in various cancers and positively correlates with the expression of the NK cell effector marker FCGR3A [137–139]; high SHM rates combined with IgG3-1 switching have been associated with improved survival in melanoma and ovarian cancer [138, 140, 141].

However, TLS-derived antibody responses are not uniformly protective. IgG4, due to its low affinity for FcγR, has been implicated in immune evasion in pancreatic, colorectal, esophageal and melanoma tumors [22, 142–144]. IgA is prominently observed in ovarian [145], colorectal [146], gastric [147], and liver cancers [148]. Its functions are dual: for instance, in ovarian cancer, IgA is transcytosed via binding to polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) on tumor cells, enabling both antigen-dependent and -independent functions that synergistically inhibit tumor progression [145, 149, 150]. In fibrotic liver tissue, aberrant accumulation of IgA within tumors suppresses IFNγ production and cytotoxic activity of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells via a PD-L1–dependent mechanism [151]. Moreover, spatial localization shapes plasma cell function: in HGSOC, plasma cells within TLS correlate with favorable prognosis, whereas stromal plasma cells outside TLS are linked to immunosuppression and early recurrence [116]. These findings highlight that the subclass and anatomical context of antibodies determine whether TLS humoral responses act as allies or antagonists in antitumor immunity. This raises the possibility that antibody subclass profiles could serve as clinically actionable biomarkers or companion diagnostics to stratify patients.

A striking feature of tumor-associated humoral responses is their frequent bias toward tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), whereas bona fide neoantigens appear to be less commonly targeted. Antibodies identified in cancer often recognize aberrantly expressed or structurally altered self-proteins such as Her2, MUC1, MMP14, and mutant p53 [12, 111, 152, 153]. This bias likely reflects the limited accessibility and weak immunogenicity of intracellular or poorly secreted neoantigenic proteins. In PDAC, HGSOC, and NSCLC, tumor-infiltrating B cells have been shown to produce IgG antibodies targeting intracellular structural proteins such as F-actin, HSPD1, and MMP14, implying a breakdown of tolerance [154–156]. Germline reversion analyses have demonstrated that tumor-reactive antibodies originate from two distinct progenitor types: one acquires antigen specificity through SHM within TLS, while the other derives from germline-encoded autoreactive B cells [140, 154]. In NSCLC, some tumor-associated antibodies even exhibit cross-reactivity with viral epitopes, supporting the hypothesis of molecular mimicry as a driver of antitumor humoral responses [155]. Beyond tumor and viral antigens, recent evidence shows that cancer patients frequently harbor autoantibodies against immunoregulatory cytokines—including IL-6, IL-17, IFN-I and TNFSF members—which correlate with improved responses to ICIs; functional studies further confirmed that anti–IFN-I and anti–TL1A (TNFSF15) antibodies can causally enhance ICIs efficacy in preclinical models [157].

These findings illustrate that tumor-associated antibodies span a wide repertoire of antigenic targets. A key unresolved issue is how such responses are generated and regulated: while TLS provide a supportive niche for B-cell activation and diversification, other compartments may also contribute. Equally important is the question of tolerance—how do these autoreactive antibodies, whether initiated in TLS or elsewhere, avoid provoking systemic autoimmunity? One possibility is that local regulatory checkpoints, such as PD-1/PD-L1 interactions or immunosuppressive cytokines like IL-10, constrain autoreactive B-cell activity within a controlled range. Disentangling the interplay between antibody origin and tolerance will be essential to define how protective versus pathogenic humoral immunity is orchestrated in the tumor context.

The cellular origin of tumor-associated plasma cells remains a key unresolved issue, with evidence pointing to both TLS and peripheral lymphoid organs [64, 112]. In HPV⁺ HNSCC, single-cell BCR sequencing revealed GC-like TLS structures populated with clonally expanded B cells producing high-SHM HPV-specific IgG antibodies, directly implicating TLS as local antibody factories [158]. In contrast, glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), which rarely harbors TLS, recruits plasma cells to glioblastoma stem cell (GSC) niches via CCR2 chemotaxis. These plasma cells produce IgG that activates the FcyRIIA-AKT-mTOR axis on GSCs, promoting tumor proliferation—highlighting a non–TLS-dependent mode of B cell activation [159]. These findings suggest that TLS and draining lymph nodes (dLNs) provide complementary, and sometimes divergent, sources of antibodies. Whether TLS-derived antibodies differ systematically from dLN-derived antibodies in affinity maturation, glycosylation patterns, or Fc effector functions remains unknown, but resolving this issue could have major implications for predicting therapeutic efficacy.

In summary, TLS-mediated humoral immunity represents a double-edged sword. On the one hand, GC-driven IgG1/IgG3 production provides powerful antitumor effector functions; on the other hand, IgA, IgG4, and mislocalized plasma cells may facilitate immune evasion. Moreover, antibody specificity is often biased toward TAAs, raising questions about local autoreactivity and tolerance. By shaping both protective and potentially pathogenic antibody responses, TLS act as context-dependent regulators of humoral immunity. Understanding the determinants of antibody subclass distribution, autoreactivity control, and the relative contributions of TLS versus dLNs will be critical for harnessing humoral immunity in TLS as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer.

Cytotoxic immune responses: activation and maintenance of CD8+ T cells and memory T cells

A major cellular function of TLS is to serve as intratumoral niches for CD8⁺ T cell priming and maintenance. Within TLS, HEVs and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) provide continuous access and co-stimulatory cues, enabling recruitment, clonal expansion, and effector differentiation of tumor-reactive T cells [160]. In CRC, CODEX imaging revealed that TLSs (cellular neighborhood CN-5) form cross-compartmental communication networks with adjacent T cell-enriched regions and vascularized stromal zones, thereby promoting local T cell activation and antigen presentation [161]. Similarly, in LUAD, TLSs colocalize with CD141⁺ DCs, which express lymphotoxin-β (LTβ) and facilitate the formation of HEVs, enhancing CD8⁺ T cell recruitment and activation [46, 162]. Moreover, a cooperative B–Tfh–CD8 axis operates within TLS: antigen uptake by B cells promotes the differentiation of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, which secrete IL-21 to boost CD8⁺ cytotoxicity, including the expression of granzyme B and IFN-γ [163, 164]. Additionally, in HCC and gastric cancer models, B cells were found to promote the expression of CXCL13 and perforin in Trm cells via LTα/TNFR2 signaling or metabolic activation, thereby augmenting their tumor-killing capacity [165, 166]. These observations establish TLS as local priming centers that recapitulate the functional hallmarks of SLOs in situ.

A hallmark of TLS function is their ability to sustain progenitor-like or precursor exhausted (Tpex) CD8⁺ T cells, thereby maintaining a reservoir for long-term antitumor responses [167–169]. These cells, typically defined by a PD-1⁺TCF1⁺ phenotype, are maintained by cytokines such as IL-7, IL-15, and Tfh-derived IL-21 [170–175]. In HCC, lineage tracing demonstrated that most clonally expanded effector T cells in responders originated from TLS-proximal progenitor pools [176, 177]. In glioblastoma, AAV-LIGHT–induced TLSs enriched for TCF1⁺ progenitor cells, delaying terminal exhaustion [178]. Spatial multi-omics further identified TLSs as niches where progenitor populations continuously differentiate into effector subsets under persistent antigen stimulation [176, 179, 180]. Nevertheless, TLSs are not uniformly protective: in lung, liver, and esophageal cancers, TLSs also harbor terminally exhausted TIM-3⁺PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, correlating with poor prognosis [181–183]. Moreover, tumor-driven metabolic cues can exacerbate this dysfunction—for instance, α-KG–mediated epigenetic remodeling promotes the differentiation of atypical myeloid populations within TLSs, which secrete immunosuppressive cytokines and further hinder T cell reinvigoration [108, 110]. Taken together, current evidence indicates that the functional trajectory of TLS is dynamic: under supportive cytokine and antigen conditions they preserve stem-like/Tpex reservoirs that sustain antitumor immunity, whereas high antigen load, suppressive cytokines, and tumor-driven metabolic reprogramming drive their evolution into niches dominated by terminal exhaustion.

The contribution of TLS to CD8⁺ T cell immunity is further modulated by their spatial positioning and structural integrity. In renal, prostate, and bladder cancers, marginal TLSs were not significantly associated with CD8⁺ infiltration, whereas APC-rich niches within the tumor parenchyma more closely resembled SLO T-cell zones and preferentially supported stem-like T cells [184]. Similarly, PFL-TLSs lacking GCs or APCs frequently fail to sustain productive cytotoxic immunity and instead accumulate suppressive or exhausted lymphocytes [23, 185, 186]. These observations emphasize that TLS are functionally heterogeneous and that only well-structured TLSs with intact HEVs and antigen-presenting capacity reliably foster effective CD8⁺ responses.

In summary, TLSs exert multifaceted influences on cellular immunity. They serve as local sites for priming and expansion of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells, foster B–Tfh–CD8 interactions, and maintain progenitor-like T cell reservoirs that underpin durable responses to ICIs. Yet in certain contexts, TLSs also accumulate Bregs, Tregs, M2 macrophages, and terminally exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, or are compromised by metabolic reprogramming and structural immaturity [19, 20, 23, 128, 131, 187]. The overall outcome is thus not uniform but shaped by TLS maturation state, spatial positioning, and microenvironmental signals. Clarifying the determinants that drive TLS toward sustaining functional progenitor pools versus dysfunctional exhaustion niches will be critical for reprogramming these structures into reliable engines of cytotoxic immunity.

Detection and analytical approaches for TLS

Accurate detection of TLS is critical for defining their biological roles and clinical significance. Current approaches can be broadly divided into three levels: histological methods, which remain the clinical gold standard; molecular approaches, which provide quantitative signatures; and spatial technologies, which capture TLS organization and heterogeneity. Each method offers unique strengths but also faces challenges of standardization and clinical translation.

​​Histological methods​​: H&E staining, IHC

Histology remains the cornerstone of TLS detection, offering direct visualization of their architecture and maturation. H&E staining highlights dense lymphocytic aggregates, but morphology alone is insufficient, making immunohistochemistry (IHC) essential. A widely adopted criterion is the identification of CD20⁺ B-cell aggregates larger than 150 μm in diameter, which serves as a minimal structural unit for TLS [118, 122]. To distinguish TLS from non-organized lymphocyte infiltration, many studies have incorporated co-staining with T-cell markers such as CD3, CD4, or CD8 to verify the spatial organization of adjacent B-cell and T-cell zones [188–191]. FDCs (CD21, CD23, CD35) and HEVs (PNAd/MECA-79) further confirm organized structure (37, 155–157). A widely referenced definition by Petitprez et al. specifies TLS as structures containing CD20⁺ B-cell follicles adjacent to CD3⁺ T-cell areas and at least one DC-LAMP⁺ mature DC [192]. Moreover, GC markers such as BCL6, AID, Ki67, or SEMA4A indicate functional maturation, typically correlating with B-cell activation and antibody production [117, 190].

Beyond basic morphology, multimodal strategies have been applied to comprehensively assess TLS. In NSCLC [193], cervical cancer [194], PDAC [37], HCC [122], and ovarian cancer [195], mIHC panels (CD3, CD20, CD21, DC-LAMP, CD23) combined with digital pathology and spatial analyses (e.g., HALO, Delaunay triangulation) have been used to quantify TLS density, composition, and topology. These approaches are further integrated with molecular tools such as laser capture microdissection with TCR/BCR sequencing [117], imaging mass cytometry (IMC) [122], flow cytometry, and Luminex profiling [194] to characterize immune cell phenotypes, chemokine expression, and clonal responses within TLS (Table 3).

​​Transcriptomic signatures

While histological and immunohistochemical methods remain the gold standard for TLS identification, they are largely qualitative, subject to inter-observer variability, and limited in capturing functional diversity. To address these limitations, transcriptomic approaches have been developed to provide quantitative and reproducible molecular readouts, offering higher-dimensional insights into TLS composition, maturation, and heterogeneity across cancer types. By leveraging bulk RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and single-cell sequencing, researchers have established molecular signatures that complement histological assessment and enable in situ mapping of TLS.

Common TLS-associated genes include CXCL13, CCL19, LTB, MS4A1, and CD79A. Among these, CXCL13—primarily secreted by PD-1hiCXCR5⁺CD4⁺ Tfh cells in tumors—has been identified across multiple transcriptomic studies as a pivotal initiator and spatial anchor of TLS formation [75, 192]. In some studies, CXCL13 alone is used as a simplified surrogate marker of TLS presence [197]. More robust approaches employ composite gene sets: the 12-chemokine signature for broad TLS identification [198], the 29-gene panel for SFL-TLS [192, 197], and cancer-specific tools such as a four-gene classifier (PAX5, TCL1A, TNFRSF13C, CD79A) in cholangiocarcinoma [199].

The advent of spatial omics has enabled high-resolution characterization of TLS in situ. Reported signatures include a 30-gene panel in TNBC [200], a 20-gene GeoMX DSP signature in HGSOC [113], and a 50-gene set in oral squamous carcinoma [191]. Integrative frameworks further refined these findings: SpaTopic identified a TLS-25 core gene set in HCC and cHCC [201], while the RMYY-TLS signature combined single-cell, spatial, and survival data to provide a broadly applicable classifier [202].

Transcriptome-based scoring systems have also been developed. In CRC, the CRC-associated 9-gene TLSscore (e.g., CCR6, CD79B, CD1D, LAT) stratified patients by TLS activity [203]. In pancreatic cancer, GC-related genes such as AICDA, IL21R, and CD79A were detected via laser capture microdissection and NanoString [31]. A 10-gene classifier proposed by Tang et al. (CXCL9, CXCL13, PAX5, IRF8, TNFRSF14, BTLA, among others) further distinguishes TLS subtypes and captures functional heterogeneity [27]. While these TLS-related scores facilitate molecular detection and quantification of TLS, their prognostic and predictive implications will be discussed in detail in Sect. "Clinical Significance of TLS as Prognostic/Predictive Biomarkers". These findings emphasize that transcriptomic signatures highlight the potential of molecular tools for TLS classification, but variability across panels and lack of standardization remain major obstacles to clinical translation (Table 4).

While transcriptomic signatures provide quantitative molecular features of TLS, they lack spatial resolution. Automated TLS detection from histology. Advances in digital pathology and artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled automated TLS identification from conventional sections. A deep learning framework integrating ResNet18 for tumor segmentation and Mask R-CNN for lymphocyte recognition achieved TLS detection and maturity classification (E-TLS, PFL-TLS, SFL-TLS), and further proposed a TLS-to-tumor area ratio as a standardized quantitative metric [208]. These methods provide objective, high-throughput analysis but still require pathologist validation and standardized protocols.

Radiomics-based TLS prediction. Radiomics extracts high-dimensional features from medical imaging to infer TLS distribution non-invasively. In HCC, transfer learning-based MRI models integrating ResNet50-derived deep learning features with conventional texture descriptors achieved stable predictive performance (AUC = 0.85) [205], while another MRI study incorporating peri-tumoral features predicted TLS density with high accuracy (AUC = 0.91) [206]. In LUAD, CT radiomics combined with deep learning-based segmentation identified 18 TLS-associated imaging features (e.g., margin sharpness, heterogeneity), and an SVM classifier achieved robust performance across multicenter cohorts (AUC = 0.804 internal, 0.747 external) [207]. These observations point to the emerging role of imaging-derived biomarkers in TLS detection, though their clinical translation will require improved interpretability and validation across diverse cohorts.

Three-dimensional imaging and reconstruction. High-resolution 3D approaches have revealed TLS organization beyond two-dimensional sections. In CRC, cyclic immunofluorescence (CyCIF) combined with H&E and cross-sectional reconstruction showed that TLS appearing isolated in 2D often formed interconnected 3D networks, with clustering and PCA analyses further stratifying TLS by cellular composition and spatial heterogeneity [132]. In a Kras/Trp53-driven LUAD mouse model, IHC/immunofluorescence (IF) with CLARITY-based tissue clearing confirmed tumor-associated TLS composed of B cells, T cells, DCs, and HEVs, and characterized their immune features [210]. Beyond organ-specific reconstructions, the wildDISCO platform combined whole-body immunolabeling (e.g., CD23, CD3), light-sheet fluorescence microscopy, and deep learning to map TLS across the mouse body. In the 4T1 breast cancer metastasis model, TLS were mainly located near pulmonary (62%) and intestinal (19%) metastases, with volumes markedly smaller than metastatic foci (0.0021 vs. 0.066 mm³) and no fixed spatial distance pattern [209]. By integrating optimized antibody perfusion and tissue clearing, wildDISCO generated a neuro-lymphatic-vascular atlas, providing a systemic perspective on TLS in immune surveillance and tumor progression.

Spatial technologies—from AI-based digital pathology to radiomics and 3D reconstruction—offer unprecedented insights into TLS distribution and organization. While these approaches expand the scale of TLS analysis, issues of cost, standardization, and clinical applicability remain. Future integration of AI pathology, imaging biomarkers, and spatial multi-omics may yield robust frameworks for TLS spatial evaluation.

Clinical significance of TLS as prognostic/predictive biomarkers

As immunological hubs within or in close proximity to tumors, TLS have emerged as important determinants of clinical outcomes. Across multiple malignancies, their presence has been linked to favorable prognosis and enhanced responses to immunotherapy. However, the predictive value of TLS is not uniform and depends on a spectrum of factors, including their abundance, degree of maturation, anatomical location, and integration into the surrounding tumor microenvironment. Understanding these dimensions is critical for refining TLS into reliable clinical biomarkers and for translating them into tools that guide therapeutic decision making.

Quantitative indicators: presence and density of TLS

Large-scale studies consistently demonstrate that TLS abundance correlates with improved survival and therapeutic response. In NSCLC, analyses from the phase III OAK and phase II POPLAR trials revealed that TLS presence, often accompanied by plasma cell signatures, was associated with improved survival with atezolizumab (HR = 0.60), but not with chemotherapy [2]. In HCC, Calderaro et al. examined 273 surgical specimens and reported that the presence of intratumoral TLS was associated with a significantly reduced risk of early recurrence; this finding was validated in an independent cohort of 225 patients [122]. In a separate large-scale study of 660 HCC patients, integration of spatial transcriptomics and pathology demonstrated that high-density peritumoral TLS enriched with CXCL9⁺/CXCL10⁺ plasma cells and mature B cells were prognosticated to prolonged overall survival and improved immunotherapy response [206]. Similarly, in advanced ccRCC, analyses from the CheckMate 214 and JAVELIN Renal 101 trials, together with real-world International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) cohorts, revealed that durable responses to ICIs were characterized by upregulation of TLS-related gene signatures and plasma cell markers [211].

Beyond these cancers, increased TLS density has also been reported as a favorable prognostic feature in soft tissue sarcoma (STS) [192], triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [200], pancreatic cancer [196], cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) [199, 212], and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [191], although most of these findings derive from smaller single-institution cohorts (Table 6). Collectively, these studies highlight TLS presence and density as convenient indicators of antitumor immunity. Yet, their predictive power remains context-dependent, as not all TLS exert equivalent immunological functions. This underscores the need to integrate quantitative measures with qualitative features such as maturation and spatial distribution in order to achieve greater clinical precision.

Qualitative indicators: maturation and spatial localization

The prognostic and predictive impact of TLS cannot be explained by density alone. Clinical outcomes differ even in patients with similar TLS abundance, and increasing evidence indicates that structural maturation and anatomical localization are the critical determinants of TLS function.

Structural maturation strongly influences the ability of TLS to support effective immune responses. As outlined in Sect. "Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity of TLS", only fully developed SFL-TLS containing GCs and FDC networks can sustain robust B–T cell interactions. In a cohort of 509 NSCLC patients treated with ICIs, SFL-TLS were associated with markedly higher objective response rates, prolonged progression-free survival, and extended overall survival compared to TLS-negative patients [215]. In CRLM (n = 178), integrated spatial transcriptomics and single-cell analysis demonstrated that SFL-TLS regions were enriched in IgG⁺ plasma cells mediating antitumor antibody responses, whereas TLS-deficient tumors were dominated by IgA⁺ plasma cells [130]. In breast cancer, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) showed that SFL-TLS were enriched in CD103⁺ Trm cells and NK cells, and TLS maturation independently predicted improved survival [126]. A multicenter study in ccRCC (n = 754) further demonstrated that only high-density TLS containing CD21⁺ FDCs and vascular structures were independently associated with favorable prognosis [217]. Similar results were observed in STS and iCCA, where only SFL-TLS—neither E-TLS nor PFL-TLS—correlated with improved outcomes [123, 129].

Spatial localization within the tumor also modifies the clinical impact of TLS. In NSCLC, analysis of 536 tumor samples revealed that only peritumoral TLS correlated with longer overall survival, whereas intratumoral TLS showed no prognostic significance [216]. By contrast, in ccRCC, intratumoral TLS were predominantly SFL-TLS and associated with higher immunotherapy response rates, while peritumoral TLS were mostly E-TLS co-localizing with immunosuppressive myeloid subsets [119]. Primary gastric tumors frequently harbor SFL-TLS, whereas peritoneal metastases are enriched for intratumoral E-/PFL-TLS together with regulatory macrophages/Tregs; this pattern aligns with inferior prognosis and attenuated benefit from ICIs in the metastatic setting [23]. In HGSOC, TLS were prevalent in omental metastases but largely absent in primary ovarian lesions, where B cell activity was suppressed [113]. In papillary thyroid carcinoma, TLS were mainly detected in indolent tumors and restricted to adjacent nonmalignant tissue in aggressive cases [224].

These findings indicate that maturation and spatial positioning together determine whether TLS act as effective immune hubs or immunosuppressive reservoirs. Prognostic and predictive associations are strongest when TLS are structurally mature and embedded in permissive niches. Conversely, immature or peripherally located TLS may harbor suppressive subsets and even associate with poor outcomes. These observations provide a plausible explanation for inconsistent findings across tumor types and highlight the need to integrate qualitative features into TLS-based biomarkers.

TLS-Based scoring models: toward clinical translation

Composite models translate TLS biology into clinically actionable predictors by encoding abundance, maturation, and spatial context and reporting effects on survival and treatment response; construction details are introduced earlier (see Sect. "Detection and Analytical Approaches for TLS"). Here we evaluate clinical endpoints and deployability rather than model construction.

Transcriptomic signatures stratify prognosis and immunotherapy benefit across independent cohorts. The CRC TLSscore and a related nine-gene panel—introduced earlier—predict longer survival and higher ICI response in training and validation sets [203]. An AI-assisted TLS signature (AITS) in breast cancer separates immune-evasive from immune-sensitive groups across 13 cohorts with external validation and associations with OS/PFS and ICI response [220]. Additional tumor-specific signatures (e.g., TNBC 30-gene, HGSOC 20-gene, OSCC 50-gene, SpaTopic TLS-25, RMYY-TLS) show concordant trends but generally require broader external validation before clinical adoption [113, 191, 200–202]. In HCC, a TLS subtype classifier linking a higher “deviated” fraction to reduced ICI efficacy and worse survival captures morpho-functional risk beyond density alone [27].

Spatial and pathology-based models operationalize maturation, location, and cellular topology without requiring genomics. In NSCLC, an immune-related pathological response classifier (irPRC) that integrates TLS presence with lymphocytic infiltration and neovascular features improves consistency of major pathological response (MPR) assessment after neoadjuvant therapy [226]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), a random-forest model built on TLS-centric spatial variables—such as within TLS CD8⁺ T/B-cell relationships—achieves high discrimination for ICI response (AUC ≈ 0.97) [222]. In biliary tract cancer and CRLM, T-score (intratumoral TLS) and P-score (peritumoral TLS) define four immune classes, with high T-score/low P-score cases showing the most active immunity and best outcomes [129, 131]. In HCC, a spatial transcriptomic risk score distinguishing “deviated” from “conforming” TLS quantifies aberrancy rather than density and associates with inferior survival and ICI response [27].

Models that encode SFL-TLS features and spatial context can complement—or, in some settings, outperform—PD-L1 IHC and TMB [27]. Clinical utility depends on reproducible implementation. Pathology-anchored scores (H&E/IHC or limited multiplex) are faster and less expensive but may undercapture functional maturity. Bulk or spatial transcriptomic models better represent maturation and cellular topology, but they add assay complexity and are vulnerable to batch effects. Regardless of platform, cutoffs require calibration, performance must be verified across laboratories and instruments, and black-box learners need interpretable features and a clear regulatory path [70, 76]. Priority should go to models that jointly capture SFL-TLS maturation, spatial positioning, and within TLS CD8⁺ T–B-cell co-localization, with transparent cutoffs and independent external validation.

Limitations and inconsistencies in the predictive value of TLS

Evidence of benefit is not universal. In the Stereotactic Ablative Radiotherapy (SABR)-PD-L1 trial for advanced soft tissue sarcoma, TLS were infrequent and, when present, showed no predictive association with clinical benefit, whereas monocyte-lineage dysregulation emerged as the dominant correlate of resistance [213]. In HNSCC, TLS density tracked with B-cell infiltration yet was not an independent predictor, whereas tumor and peripheral B-cell levels were more informative [225]. In renal and prostate cancers, intratumoral MHC-II⁺ APC-rich niches resembling lymphoid microenvironments—rather than TLS per se—were identified as the critical sites sustaining TCF1⁺ stem-like CD8 T cells and durable responses, while tumors lacking such niches showed poor CD8 infiltration [184]. In HGSOC, TLS were frequent in omental metastases but underdeveloped at primary ovarian sites, indicating site-dependent function [227].

Divergent findings arise from both biology and methods. Biologically, TLS scarcity in immune-cold tumors, immature/peritumoral E-TLS that co-localize with suppressive myeloid cells, and morpho-functional “deviated” TLS in HCC can attenuate or invert associations [27]. Methodologically, non-uniform nomenclature (i/mTLS vs. E-/PFL-/SFL-TLS; LA vs. E-TLS), heterogeneous sampling (intratumoral vs. peritumoral; hotspot selection), 2D underrepresentation of 3D networks, platform-specific thresholds and batch effects, and collinearity with inflamed-TME signals (e.g., IFN-γ, plasma-cell markers, PD-L1, TMB) reduce comparability.

Clinical deployment will hinge on standardization rather than additional signatures. Studies should adopt a unified stage nomenclature (E-/PFL-/SFL-TLS), distinguish intratumoral from peritumoral locations, and report simple spatial topology (e.g., CD8⁺ T–B-cell co-localization) alongside SFL-TLS quality. Prospective, multi-center designs with pre-registered analysis plans and harmonized assays are needed to establish generalizability, and spatially resolved, multi-omic frameworks should be leveraged to integrate maturation, position, and neighborhood features into a reproducible reporting set. These steps will convert TLS from descriptive correlates into dependable clinical tools.

TLS as therapeutic targets

​​Inducing TLS: innovative strategies

Induction strategies generally follow two complementary paradigms: a ligand-first route that re-engages lymphoid organogenesis signals (e.g., LTβR/LIGHT and CXCL13, CCL19/21) and a niche-first route that establishes permissive local microenvironments via scaffolds or nanoplatforms with controlled release. Both aim to gently, yet persistently, transition immune-cold tumors toward niches enriched for E-, PFL-, and ultimately SFL-TLS, thereby amplifying endogenous and therapy-evoked immunity (Fig. 4) (Table 7).

Therapeutic strategies promoting TLS formation in the TME

Chemokine and cytokine delivery

Cytokine/chemokine delivery is most effective when it licenses stromal organizers and remodels HEVs, rather than simply increasing lymphocyte influx. Within this framework, a biomimetic probe (FITC-HFe₃O₄@Gd ~ CC) that co-delivers CXCL13 and CCL21 promoted B/T-cell congregation and TLS neogenesis in glioma while enabling concurrent imaging, underscoring the value of coupling localization with signaling [228]. A vascular-targeted LIGHT-VTP fusion activated a herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM/TNFRSF14)-CCL21-LTα cascade, driving HEV remodeling and TLS construction in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and Lewis lung carcinoma, with favorable synergy when combined with PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade [51]. In PD-1–refractory gliomas, AAV-mediated LIGHT expression targeted to the brain vasculature enriched TCF1⁺ “stem-like” CD8⁺ pools and reinforced immune memory, highlighting the importance of organ-specific vascular addressability [178]. Moreover, concurrent local activation of LTβR and LTα increased TLS incidence from ~ 10% to near-universal levels with mature FDC/FRC networks and lymphatics, suggesting that concurrency of organizer and chemokine signals can facilitate progression toward functional maturation [46]. In some settings, brief innate “priming” appears to precede chemokine/LTβR-driven organization; representative TLR-based examples are discussed in Sect. "Innate Immune Agonists Driving TLS Formation" . From a translational standpoint, ligand-first approaches are attractive because they are mechanism-directed and naturally synergize with HEV remodeling, yet they carry risks of off-tumor inflammation and difficult control over chemokine gradients. Locoregional, image-guided, and controlled-release formats therefore appear most promising clinically, although standardization of formulations and real-time safety monitoring remain practical bottlenecks.

Inducing TLS via nanomaterials and tissue engineering scaffolds

Biomaterials and tissue engineering provide a spatial governance and controlled-release solution that helps E-TLS transition to PFL- and SFL-TLS within a supportive niche. The microporous annealed particle (MAP) scaffold (gelatin methacryloyl, GelMA) implanted into post-resection cavities—with sustained antigen and CpG delivery—promoted local HEVs and B/T-cell clustering and complemented PD-1 blockade by reducing recurrence and metastasis [229]. Ferritin–resilin hydrogels bearing CP05 peptides enabled efficient delivery of exosomes enriched for CXCL13/CCL21, generating SFL-TLS at subcutaneous sites and increasing complete responses to PD-1 blockade in CRC models [230]. Photothermally triggered aggregation-induced-emission covalent organic frameworks (AIE-COFs) (e.g., TPDA-ViBT-COF) induced CXCL10/CCL19, fostered TLS formation, and synergized with anti-CTLA-4 in MC38 and 4MOSC1 tumors [231]; COF-306@FM, which mimics bacterial membranes, augmented immune-homing signals and TLS density and amplified systemic antitumor immunity with PD-L1 blockade [232]. Material-based strategies offer spatial governance and tunable release with inherently low systemic exposure, positioning them well for post-resection beds and other accessible lesions. Their path to the clinic will depend on rigorous demonstration of biocompatibility and biodegradation profiles, along with scalable manufacturing and clear regulatory classification, which remain the main hurdles.

Engineered cells and gene delivery platforms

Engineered cellular and gene-delivery systems introduce programmability, enabling “sense-and-release” control over the timing and localization of TLS-inducing cues. The E@L-P/ICG platform couples LIGHT-expressing engineered E. coli with photothermal poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)–indocyanine green (PLGA-ICG) nanoparticles; near-infrared–triggered bacterial lysis co-releases tumor antigens and LIGHT, remodels HEVs, and organizes functional TLS, thereby extending survival in murine models [233]. Complementarily, ADU-S@M1—M1 macrophage-derived exosomes encapsulating the STING agonist ADU-S100—homes to tumor sites, activates STAT1/STING signaling, and induces stromal CCL19/CXCL13, culminating in TLS assembly [234]. At the same time, immunogenicity, biodistribution containment, and the need for kill-switch safeguards and good manufacturing practice (GMP)-grade production set a high bar for clinical deployment.

Innate immune agonists driving TLS formation

Innate agonists—particularly STING- and TLR-based platforms—offer a rapid “priming” route to re-shape local inflammation in favor of TLS neogenesis and maturation. The ZCCG hydrogel (Zn²⁺/chitosan/CpG) co-activated cGAS–STING and TLR9, upregulated CXCL10/CCL19/CCL21, promoted HEV formation, and paired well with PD-1 blockade in breast cancer and melanoma [235]. A radiotherapy-derived exosomal system, BafA1@Rexo-SC, prolonged STING activation and VEGFA-mediated HEV remodeling, increasing TLS density and improving survival and complete responses in glioma models [236]. In parallel, TLR agonist OK432 combined with tumor antigens engaged LTβR signaling along microglia–Th17 axes to support TLS development [40]. Innate agonists can rapidly “prime” cold microenvironments toward TLS organization, but cytokine-driven toxicities and narrow therapeutic windows limit systemic use. Local depots that provide mild, sustained, multi-axis activation look most translatable, pending robust biomarkers of on-target engagement and tolerability.

​​Therapy-driven TLS modulation​​

Existing anticancer therapies can modulate TLS by altering antigen availability, vascular and stromal states, and inflammatory programs, with effects that are highly context- and timing-dependent. In this section we synthesize how ICIs, radiotherapy/chemotherapy, and oncolytic platforms shape the emergence, maturation, or maintenance of E-, PFL-, and SFL-TLS, and we outline design principles for rational combinations (Fig. 4).

Synergistic TLS induction by ICIs

Across tumor types, PD-1/PD-L1 (± CTLA-4) blockade is associated with increased TLS density and maturation. In bladder cancer, ICIs expand B cells and Tfh cells and initiate nascent TLS, establishing the link between checkpoint blockade and TLS induction [237]. Mechanistic work in RIP1-Tag5 and MC38 models shows that combined PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade upregulates CXCL13/CCL19, driving lymphocyte clustering and TLS assembly [51], and findings in PDAC extend this paradigm, where KRAS inhibition cooperates with CTLA-4 blockade to remodel the microenvironment and promote TLS maturation [51]. Clinically, in HCC, neoadjuvant ICIs markedly increase intratumoral TLS density and associate with higher pathologic response rates and prolonged recurrence-free survival [117, 118]. Similarly, in neoadjuvant settings, ICI-containing regimens in gastric adenocarcinoma [238] and NSCLC promoted TLS formation and B-cell infiltration [239]. Together, these studies demonstrate that ICI regimens not only relieve suppression but also coincide with TLS neogenesis and maturation, yet whether TLS are causal drivers of efficacy or concomitant markers remains unresolved. Clinically, integrating ICIs with organizer signals or priming modalities is appealing, provided trials include TLS-quality readouts—such as SFL-TLS features, HEV density, and TCF1⁺ CD8⁺ reservoirs—to guide timing and patient selection.

Chemotherapy-induced TLS formation and maturation

A growing body of studies has explored how chemotherapy may induce or mature TLS. In HGSOC, Neoadjuvant platinum-taxane chemotherapy (NACT) upregulates CHOP/CALR, augments antigen release, and generates GC-like TLS at metastatic sites, with signals of improved immunotherapy response [240]. Complementing this antigen-release axis, in breast cancer, chemotherapy-derived C3 fragments activate ICOSL⁺ B cells via CR2, expanding them within TLS and reinforcing B-Tfh-CD8 crosstalk [241]. Similarly, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gemcitabine-cisplatin expands CD27⁺IgD⁺IgM⁺ innate-like B cells and tunes Tfh differentiation through the ICOSL-ICOS pathway, yielding TLS-like structures that track objective response [242]. Notably in bladder cancer, the same regimen (gemcitabine-cisplatin) increases intratumoral CD20⁺ B cells and reduces FOXP3⁺ Tregs, indicating local immune activation [237]. Chemotherapy can favor TLS assembly through ICD-mediated antigen release and chemokine licensing, but benefits are schedule- and drug-class-dependent and may be offset by lymphodepletion. Neoadjuvant windows and ICD-biased regimens appear most suitable clinically, with prospective studies needed to optimize sequencing with ICIs and to validate predictive biomarkers.

Radiotherapy-driven TLS induction and immune cooperation

Radiotherapy (RT) has been shown to promote E-TLS formation and to enhance TLS maturation in murine LUAD models [243]. Compared with chemotherapy, RT more typically acts through local DNA damage and danger signals—dsDNA and DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1, ATP)—that activate cGAS-STING and support chemokine and vascular programs. In a complementary engineered approach, an irradiated-tumor–derived exosomal system (BafA1@Rexo-SC) prolonged STING activation and VEGFA-mediated HEV remodeling, increasing TLS density and recruiting T and B cells [236]. These data are consistent with a priming role for low-dose or spatially fractionated RT in enabling TLS-supportive chemokine/vascular programs [91, 98]. Clinically, dose, fractionation, and field design likely determine whether immunity is fostered or lymphopenia ensues, underscoring the need for standardized schemas and TLS-focused endpoints in combination trials.

TLS induction and synergy by oncolytic virus therapy

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) selectively lyse tumor cells and release immunostimulatory factors, creating a TLS-permissive milieu. In various murine models, oncolytic herpesvirus (oHSV) upregulated the CXCL10-CXCR3 axis, recruited TCF1⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, and induced TLS with B-cell clustering—effects potentiated by PD-1 blockade [244]. Early clinical data echo these patterns: phase Ib regimens in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (intravesical CG0070 + nivolumab) and acral melanoma (orienX010 + toripalimab) increased TLS features—most notably SFL-TLS with plasma cells/class switching—and biomarker readouts (urinary CXCL13) in responders [245, 246]. Thus, OVs appear to synergize with PD-1, especially via intratumoral delivery; translation will hinge on mitigating pre-existing immunity/neutralization, managing biodistribution, and ensuring manufacturing and shedding control for durable, safe benefit.

Altogether, existing therapeutic modalities—through direct or indirect mechanisms—can modulate the initiation, maturation, and immunological functionality of TLS. A nuanced understanding of these interactions facilitates the strategic integration of TLS-targeted or TLS-enhancing approaches into personalized regimens and informs the development of TLS-based biomarkers for outcome prediction. Building on this synthesis, we outline a practical, testable blueprint—prime → organize → mature: use radiotherapy, ICD-favoring chemotherapy, or oncolytic platforms to prime antigen release and innate licensing; apply organizers (LTβR/LIGHT, CXCL13/CCL19/21, or biomaterial scaffolds) to structure and stabilize PFL-/SFL-TLS; and then consolidate with ICIs. Anti-VEGF–mediated vascular normalization can be interleaved to improve access. We recommend pre-specifying TLS stage (E/PFL/SFL), HEV density, GC/Tfh programs, and TCF1⁺ CD8⁺ reservoirs as companion readouts to guide timing and de-risk trials.

Conclusions

TLSs, as dynamic immune aggregates formed in non-lymphoid tissues, have emerged as pivotal orchestrators of antitumor immunity. Through promoting local antigen presentation, supporting B cell maturation, and sustaining effector and memory T cell responses, TLSs provide an essential spatial and functional hub that integrates both humoral and cellular immune arms. Compelling evidence from a broad range of solid tumors has established the prognostic and predictive value of TLSs—particularly their density, maturity, and spatial location—as crucial determinants of immunotherapeutic efficacy and long-term clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, TLSs are not uniformly beneficial. Their structural heterogeneity and context-dependent cellular composition may lead to immunosuppressive niches that promote immune evasion, especially in the presence of Tregs, AtM, or inhibitory cytokines. These divergent roles underscore the importance of precise TLS characterization at the phenotypic, spatial, and molecular levels. Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have begun to deconvolute TLS development trajectories, unveiling novel regulatory networks, stromal interactions, and metabolic influences that govern TLS function. Emerging therapeutic strategies—ranging from cytokine delivery and HEV induction to nanoparticle scaffolds and engineered stromal cell programming—have shown promise in TLS modulation, although their clinical translation remains nascent.

To harness the full potential of TLSs in cancer immunotherapy, future research should focus on several key directions: First, refining unified TLS classification systems based on maturation status and spatial distribution; Second, integrating multi-omic datasets to decode TLS-related immunological circuits; Third, developing non-invasive imaging and AI-assisted histological tools for TLS detection; and Finally, designing combination strategies that promote TLS maturation and synergize with ICIs. Moreover, the development of TLS-based predictive models that outperform conventional biomarkers such as PD-L1 or TMB holds great promise for guiding personalized immunotherapeutic decisions. In conclusion, TLSs represent not only a biological phenomenon of ectopic lymphoid neogenesis but also a central platform for coordinating immune activation, therapeutic responsiveness, and potentially immune memory. Bridging mechanistic insights with translational innovation, TLS-centered approaches may redefine the landscape of precision cancer immunotherapy in the coming years.
