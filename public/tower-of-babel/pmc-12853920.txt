Satisfactory perioperative management for hip arthroscopy with small local pericapsular nerve group block: a single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chong Zhang, Yixin Fan, Peiyu Li, Mi Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent analgesic options for hip arthroscopy include fascia iliaca block and peripheral nerve block of the hip. Although the latter provides effective analgesia with reduced motor impairment compared with the fascia iliaca approach, motor blockade still occurs in ≥ 25% of patients. This study aimed to investigate whether administering low-volume local anesthetic for peripheral nerve blockade in the hip reduces the incidence of postoperative quadriceps weakness while maintaining adequate pain control.MethodsA total of 90 patients who underwent hip arthroscopy were randomly assigned to one of three groups. The first group was administered a fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB), the second group was administered a pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block with 20 ml of local anesthetic, and the third group was administered a PENG block with 10 ml of local anesthetic. The primary outcome measure was postoperative quadriceps muscle strength, whereas the secondary outcome measures were the postoperative 2-h Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score, intraoperative changes in blood pressure and heart rate, NRS score at one day after surgery, intraoperative and postoperative opioid consumption, postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, patient satisfaction, pain experience, and quality of recovery.ResultsNo evidence of a difference were observed in the basic statistical parameters among the three patient groups. Moreover, no significant discrepancies were identified in the NRS scores at the 2-h or 1-day post-operative intervals. However, a marked difference in quadriceps muscle strength was evident among the three groups at the 2-h post-operative mark (PENG 10 ml = 3.43 ± 0.45, PENG 20 ml = 2.43 ± 1.81, FICB = 0.57 ± 0.82; p < 0.001). There was a difference in the quality of postoperative recovery (PENG 10 ml = 125.57 ± 4.70, PENG 20 ml = 123.53 ± 5.45, FICB = 120.80 ± 3.34; p < 0.01), with no significant difference in any other outcome.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing hip arthroscopy, preoperative FICB, PENG (10 mL), and PENG (20 mL) blocks resulted in comparable intraoperative stress responses and provided comparable, satisfactory postoperative analgesia.However, patients who received low-volume PENG blocks demonstrated superior postoperative preservation of quadriceps muscle strength and a statistically significant improvement in the quality of recovery. Notably, the magnitude of this improvement in recovery quality did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400091099.Date: October 21, 2024; retrospectively registered.URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09407-3.

Introduction

During the past two decades, the prevalence of hip joint diseases, particularly hip impingement syndrome, has notably increased. This condition has been identified as a primary contributing factor to hip pain, impaired mobility, and the onset of early osteoarthritis. The condition is typified by acute pain in the hip joint and a rattling sensation. As the disease progresses, patients may experience limitations in their activities and mobility. In the context of this disease, early intervention and treatment are imperative. The utilization of hip arthroscopic joint debridement as a primary treatment modality has markedly increased, with a 600% increase from 2003 to 2010 [1, 2], and 53,103 hip arthroscopic surgeries were completed across the United States from 2010 to 2017 [3].

As demonstrated in the relevant literature [4–8], effective postoperative pain management is a crucial component of joint recovery and other surgical procedures. Adequate analgesia has been shown to reduce postoperative opioid use and allow patients to get up and out of bed early enough to avoid venous thrombosis in the lower extremities and to promote joint rehabilitation. A common analgesic technique used today is iliofascial compartment block (FICB), and a novel regional anesthetic approach for pain management in patients with hip fractures, described by Girón [9] et al. (2018), is pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block. However, the former can result in postoperative weakness of the lower extremity muscles in patients, whereas the latter has been demonstrated to provide superior analgesia in patients undergoing hip arthroplasty or arthroscopy [9–12] without affecting muscle strength in the lower extremities. Nevertheless, recent documentation has revealed that PENG block can cause lower limb muscle weakness in up to 25% of patients. Furthermore, no discernible difference in lower limb muscle strength has been observed between patients who have undergone a PENG block and those who have undergone a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block [13–15].

A recent cadaveric study [16] demonstrated that in the PENG block procedure, both 10 ml and 20 ml of the drug could diffuse into the gap between the iliopsoas muscle and the anterior capsule of the hip joint. Furthermore, that study showed that 10 ml of dye did not diffuse into the main trunk of the femoral nerve, thus preventing motor block. In light of these findings, we propose a new approach to achieve optimal pain relief and minimal motor block while reducing the volume of the drug needed. This approach maintains the efficacy of PENG block in hip arthroscopy while preventing diffusion of the drug to the femoral nerve trunk. Given that there are currently no clinical trials in this area, we have adopted a double-blind randomized comparison approach for our study.

Materials and methods

This study was carried out on the China National Medical Research Registration System and was simultaneously registered with the China Clinical Trials Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2400091099). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Orthopedic Hospital, and all patients were included only after ethical approval was obtained.

All patients were required to sign an informed consent form, and a total of 90 patients were enrolled between February 1, 2024, and September 10, 2024 (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: adults aged 18–80 years with a diagnosis of acetabular impingement syndrome who underwent hip arthroscopy for the first time, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade 1–3, no contraindications to regional block, signed informed consent and a body mass index (BMI) in the range of 18–35. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with cognitive impairment or language disorders who were unable to communicate, had contraindications to regional anesthesia, failed to sign the informed consent form, had allergies to local anesthetics, had hepatic or renal failure, had previous surgery in the inguinal area, were pregnant, had previous long-term use of opioids, had an age outside the range of 18–80 years, or had neurological or anatomical defects in the lower limbs. Withdrawal criteria: those who did not meet the inclusion criteria or met the exclusion criteria, had incomplete data collection, had data that could not be analyzed, had serious postoperative complications, could not communicated with, or had poor adherence to the protocol during the trial.Fig. 1CONSORT diagram

Randomization and blinding

This was a double-blind study. Patients, surgeons, outcome assessors, and data analysts were all blinded to group allocation.

On the day of surgery, a researcher not involved in patient care or outcome assessment allocated patients to groups using a computer-generated, simple randomization sequence with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio. The allocation results were placed in opaque envelopes and handed to the operating anesthesiologist. Notably, the designated anesthesiologist was not involved in the study, and all the medications were prepared by this anesthesiologist.

All study medications were prepared by an independent anesthesiologist who was not involved in patient recruitment, intervention, or data collection. The medications were drawn into identical syringes equal in model and volume and were visually indistinguishable in color. Each syringe was labeled only with the patient’s study identification number. Patients remained in a supine position throughout the procedure, preventing them from observing the anesthesiologist’s actions or the dosage being administered. Nerve blocks were performed in the inguinal region for all three groups.

The doctor responsible for the management of anesthesia was unaware of the grouping and did not participate in this study. The independent researchers and surgeons responsible for collecting intraoperative and postoperative data were also unaware of the grouping information. All participants, including both medical staff and patients, were instructed to abstain from deliberations pertaining to grouping conjectures. Furthermore, all parties were aware of the emergency unblinding procedure.

Prior to surgery, patients received a nerve block injection according to their assigned group: 0.33% ropivacaine PENG block (10 mL), 0.33% ropivacaine PENG block (20 mL), or 0.33% ropivacaine FICB block (30 mL). All blocks were performed using a 23G, 50 mm nerve block needle.

Following block administration, general anesthesia was induced with propofol (2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg), and rocuronium bromide (0.5 mg/kg). A laryngeal mask was then inserted on the basis of the patient’s weight. Anesthesia was maintained intraoperatively with sevoflurane, propofol, and remifentanil. All surgical procedures were performed by a team of qualified attending physicians.

In this study, the surgeons did not administer any additional types of local anesthetics during the operation.

Nerve block manipulation

PENG block is a regional anesthetic technique involving the injection of a local anesthetic into the iliac fossa, which is the area between the hip and the pubic bone. In accordance with the methodology delineated by Giron-Arango [9], the patient was positioned in the supine posture. Under aseptic conditions, a high-frequency wire-array probe was subsequently placed parallel to the patient’s transverse axis in the region of the anterior inferior iliac spine. Thereafter, the probe was rotated parallel to the pubic ramus. To obtain short-axis images of the iliopsoas muscle and the conjoint tendon, which were located in the pubic ramus in the vicinity of the ilio-pubic augmentation (Fig. 2), the nerve block needle was punctured from outside to inside, with the tip placed on the myofascial plane between the conjoint tendon and the pubic ramus. A solution of 0.33% ropivacaine was administered slowly after retraction without blood.Fig. 2PENG block. Postoperative analgesic regimen

Postsurgical patients were transferred to the postanesthesia care unit (PACU). In instances where a patient’s pain rating exceeded 4 on a scale of 0 to 10 (where 0 denotes no pain and 10 denotes the most intense pain imaginable), the analgesic drug sufentanil (0.1 mcg/kg) was administered as a rescue medication. All patients received oral celecoxib (200 mg) and intravenous parecoxib sodium (40 mg) every 12 h. In cases where the patient’s persistent pain score exceeded 4, 100 mg tramadol was administered intravenously. If the patient developed postoperative complications, they were managed symptomatically.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was quadriceps muscle strength 2 h after surgery.

Secondary outcome indicators: The Maximum Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) was used to record pain intensity at 2 h and 1 d after surgery, both at rest and during dynamic movements (passive straight leg elevation at 15°). Additionally, the muscle strength of the quadriceps femoris was assessed 24 h after surgery. Furthermore, changes in patients’ blood pressure and heart rate were monitored at four distinct time points: at admission to the operating theatre (T1), prior to skin incision (T2), one minute following incision (T3), and at the conclusion of the operation (T4). The duration of the operation, the maximal NRS scores at rest and during dynamic movements (passive straight leg elevation at 15°) in the 1 d post-operative period, the intra- and postoperative opioid consumption, the average NRS in the 3 d postoperative period, and the maximal NRS in the 3 d postoperative period were also recorded. Postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, patient satisfaction, and quality of recovery were assessed.

The assessment of quadriceps muscle strength was conducted through the implementation of the knee extension test and the Oxford muscle strength grading scale, and opioids were administered mainly for intraoperative use, followed by additional daily tramadol use for two days post-operatively. Tramadol use was converted to oral morphine equivalents.

The Quality of Recovery 15 (QoR-15) questionnaire was used to evaluate patients' quality of recovery on the day following surgery, and complications were documented according to the Clavien–Dindo classification.

Sample size calculation and statistical analyses

A series of sample calculations were conducted utilizing sample size software (Kaysville, Utah, USA). In the preliminary experiment, the mean muscle strength at 2 h after surgery was 4.5 in the PENG (10 ml) cohort, 2.5 in the PENG (20 ml) cohort, and 0.5 in the FICB cohort, with a standard deviation of 2, an alpha of 0.05, a beta of 0.2, and a power of 0.95, plus a 15% dropout rate. The total required sample size was 30 patients in each group, totaling 90 patients.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V.21 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, New York). For continuous variables, the Shapiro‒Wilk test was employed to evaluate the adherence of these variables to a normal distribution. This was followed by an examination of homogeneity of variance, and ultimately, a subsequent analysis of variance was performed. For categorical data, the chi-square test was used for analysis; for nonnormally distributed data, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for analysis; for variables that did not meet the homogeneity of variance requirement, Welch's ANOVA was used for analysis. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

The primary outcome (quadriceps muscle strength at 2 h postoperatively) was analyzed with a significance level set at P < 0.05. For the secondary outcomes, to control the family-wise error rate due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. The significance level for the secondary outcomes was adjusted to P < 0.004, calculated as 0.05 divided by the number of key secondary outcome comparisons (n = 13). Therefore, the results of the secondary outcomes should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Results

A total of 92 patients satisfied the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in the study throughout the trial period. Two patients opted against participating in the trial and the rest signed the informed consent form. This resulted in a total of 90 patients signing the informed consent form and being randomly assigned to three groups (Fig. 1). All 90 patients completed the study, and complete data were collected for all 90.

Secondary outcomes

NRS scores were comparable among the three groups at two hours postoperatively. either for static (p = 0.063) or dynamic (passive straight leg elevation at 15°) (p = 0.326) assessments. Moreover, no substantial disparities were observed in the NRS scores for static (p = 1) and dynamic (passive straight leg elevation at 15°, p = 0.957) assessments among the three groups on postoperative day 1. Additionally, no significant difference in quadriceps muscle strength was detected 24 h after the surgery (p = 0.646) (Table 2).

An analysis of the data revealed no statistically significant differences in the changes in blood pressure (Fig. 4) or heart rate (Fig. 5) at any time point during surgery among the three groups.Fig. 4Intraoperative blood pressureFig. 5Intraoperative heart rate

The present study revealed no significant differences in the duration of surgery among the three groups (p = 0.780). Furthermore, no evidence of a difference was observed between the mean NRS score at three days post-operatively and the maximum NRS score within three days post-operatively. In terms of the Clavien–Dindo score and comorbidity classification (grades 0–5), the three groups demonstrated analogous patterns of complication grading. During the course of hospitalization, four patients (13%) in the FICB group and one patient (3%) in the 20 ml group experienced a fall, whereas no patients in the 10 ml group experienced a fall (p = 0.124). In terms of inpatient satisfaction, two patients (7%) in the FICB group expressed dissatisfaction, while the remaining patients in all three groups indicated satisfaction (p = 0.324). A significant difference in the quality of postoperative recovery was observed among all three groups when the QoR-15 questionnaire was used (PENG 10 ml = 125.57 ± 4.70, PENG 20 ml = 123.53 ± 5.45, FICB = 120.80 ± 3.34; P < 0.001).This difference remained statistically significant even after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted P threshold < 0.004). This finding suggests that, in comparison to the other two groups, patients in the PENG (10 ml) group exhibited a significantly superior postoperative recovery quality. No significant difference was observed in the length of hospitalization among the three groups (p = 0.121) (Table 3). Discussion

In this randomized trial, none of the patients with PENG block with low-volume local anesthetics developed quadriceps paralysis or significantly decreased muscle strength, and no patients fell after surgery. Moreover, compared with the other two groups, the PENG (10 ml) group provided comparable intraoperative auxiliary anesthesia and postoperative analgesia.

In the present study, the majority of patients in the FICB group exhibited quadriceps paralysis two hours post-surgery, with muscle strength ratings of approximately grade 1. None of the patients demonstrated the capacity to elevate their lower limbs in response to gravitational forces. In the 20 ml PENG group, 30–40% of patients exhibited quadriceps paralysis and reduced muscle strength, which is consistent with prior research findings [17–19]. However, no patients in the 10 ml group exhibited symptoms of quadriceps paralysis, which may be attributable to the anatomical configuration of the nerves surrounding the hip joint. The musculature of the hip and lower limb is primarily innervated by the femoral, sciatic and obturator nerves, which innervate the anterior, posterior and medial muscles, respectively, of the thigh. In the FICB cohort, the vast majority of patients exhibited femoral nerve blocking, which is known to innervate the quadriceps musculature. This resulted in postoperative quadriceps paralysis in the patients. However, PENG block affects mainly the joint capsule branches of the three nerves, namely, the femoral nerve, obturator nerve, and collateral obturator nerve. Therefore, there was no quadriceps femoris paralysis.

The occurrence of quadriceps femoris paralysis in the PENG (20 ml) group is currently considered to be caused by two factors [16, 17]: 1. The path and position of the needle tip during the PENG block are major influencing factors. When PENG block is performed, the iliopsoas tendon may be destroyed, resulting in the injection of some drugs through the ilioinguinal sac, breaking through the medial boundary of the iliopsoas fascia and spreading along the surface of the psoas major muscle to the anteromedial side, involving the femoral nerve and causing quadriceps femoris paralysis. 2. An excessive volume of the drug leads to high pressure load, causing rupture of the iliopectineal bursa and excessive drug diffusion. Currently, the optimal needle tip position recommended for PENG block is between the iliopsoas tendon and the iliopsoas bone and as laterally as possible [17], which reduces the excessive pressure that can destroy the iliopectineal bursa when the drug is injected. We avoided the iliopsoas tendon by maneuvering and placing the needle tip in the optimal position in this study, and no patient in the PENG (10 ml) block group developed quadriceps paralysis; thus, the quadriceps paralysis in the PENG (20 ml) group in the present study may have been due to the diffusion of the drug into the main trunk of the femoral nerve as a result of an excessive volume of local anesthetic. The results of the present study also indirectly support the findings of Tran et al. [16], who reported that PENG block with 10 ml of local anesthetic does not spread to the main trunk of the femoral nerve. On the first postoperative day, muscle strength returned to normal in all the patients in our study.Therefore, in this study, it was observed that PENG block may not consistently preserve motor function completely. Factors such as the volume of the injectate and the technical performance of the block could contribute to the impairment of motor function.

Falls occurred in four patients in the FICB group and one in the PENG (20 ml) group, all within 24 h of surgery, without serious consequences such as refracture, and none in the PENG (10 ml) group. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 30 studies revealed [20] that lower extremity muscle weakness was the primary factor contributing to falls, as indicated by another study that revealed [21] a significant negative correlation between quadriceps muscle strength and falling. As quadriceps weakness is a primary risk factor for falls [20, 21], this motor impairment may have heightened the perceived risk of falling and contributed to functional limitations, potentially explaining why two patients in the FICB group expressed dissatisfaction with their hospitalization. However, Falls were recorded in 4 patients in the FICB group and 1 patient in the PENG (20 mL) group; no formal statistical comparison was performed due to the low event rate.

In the present study, no statistically significant differences were observed in the NRS scores of patients at 2 h and 1 d after surgery, particularly in the PENG (20 ml) and FICB groups. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies [13]. In the neuroanatomy of the hip joint [22, 23], the anterior hip capsule is the region with the highest degree of innervation. This region is primarily innervated by the articular capsule branch of three nerves: the femoral nerve, the obturator nerve, and the accessory obturator nerve. The PENG (10 ml) group also demonstrated satisfactory perioperative analgesia, indicating that 10 ml of local anesthetic was sufficient to block the joint capsule branches of the three nerves, namely, the femoral, obturator and paracapillary obturator nerves. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Tran [16]. However, this study did not perform formal equivalence or noninferiority testing; therefore, the observed analgesic effects should be interpreted in the context of this methodological limitation.

During intraoperative skin incision, compared with the FICB group, the other two groups tended to have elevated blood pressure. During postoperative follow-up, most patients who received PENG block reported pain concentrated around the incision area. The underlying reason may be that the PENG block primarily targets the deep sensory nerves innervating the hip joint capsule, aiming to alleviate joint-derived pain. However, pain at the incision site of hip arthroscopy involves the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN), which innervates the skin in this region. Standard PENG block techniques typically fail to effectively block the LFCN, which represents a direction for our future research. However, the pain points in the FICB group were more often located on the inner thigh, which may be related to traction during surgery and incomplete obturator nerve block.

No patients in any of the three groups experienced postoperative complications of a severity greater than grade 2 according to the Clavien–Dindo classification. This outcome is likely attributable to the superior preoperative condition of the patients in this study and the less invasive nature of the procedure, which was more homogeneous. The difference in QoR-15 scores among the groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and remained so after correction for multiple comparisons. It should be noted that the observed mean improvement (e.g., 4.77 points between PENG 10 mL and FICB groups) did not exceed the established MCID of 6–8 points for this scale [24]. Thus, the clinical relevance of this difference at the individual patient level requires further investigation. It represents a consistent and complementary benefit that aligns with the primary motor-sparing advantage of the technique, suggesting that the low-volume PENG block enhances recovery quality without compromising its key objective. This compelling trend justifies further investigation to identify patient subgroups that may derive clinically important benefits.

Opioid consumption did not differ significantly among the three groups,which is consistent with the results of previous studies, and there was no evidence of a difference in either the maximum or mean NRS score among the three groups at three days after surgery. However, the NRS scores were lower than those reported in previous studies, which may be attributed to the administration of oral celecoxib (200 mg) and intravenous parecoxib sodium (40 mg) every 12 h post-operatively to all patients. A significant difference was observed in the postoperative quality of recovery scale scores among the three groups, with the PENG (10 ml) group exhibiting the highest scores and the FICB group demonstrating the lowest scores. This was deemed to be associated with the inability of the patients to ambulate because of postoperative quadriceps paralysis. However, this did not impact the duration of hospitalization.

The generalizability of our findings is anchored in our study cohort—largely healthy ASA I-II patients with normal BMI. While this promotes internal validity, it demands caution in extending results to older, multimorbid, or obese populations. The core mechanism, however—that reduced injectate volume minimizes femoral nerve exposure—is anatomical and likely generalizable. This motor-sparing effect could be particularly impactful in frail patients, where quadriceps weakness poses a major fall risk, and in athletic or ambulatory cases, where early mobilization is crucial.

Conversely, technical challenges in obesity or altered anatomy from severe joint disease may limit applicability. Thus, the low-volume PENG block is not a universal solution, but a versatile tool for stratified analgesia.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we did not include a control group without nerve block. Therefore, the absolute benefit of the blocks versus no block cannot be quantified. However, since the efficacy of FICB and PENG blocks for analgesia in hip arthroscopy is well-established, we believed that including a control group was unnecessary for the purpose of our study, which focused on comparing motor outcomes between techniques, and this omission likely has little effect on our primary results. Second, numerous factors influencing postoperative pain, such as the duration of thigh traction and intraoperative water flow, were not controlled for, potentially introducing confounding. Third, the integrity of blinding was not formally evaluated; despite our strict measures, we cannot rule out its potential influence. Furthermore, while the well-balanced baseline characteristics across groups strengthen the internal validity of our comparisons, it is important to acknowledge that in randomized trials with a limited sample size, such balance can occur by chance.Finally, the Oxford scale used may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle strength changes; future studies employing quantitative tools like handheld dynamometry could provide more sensitive assessments.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates that a low-volume (10 mL) PENG block provides a favorable balance between analgesia and functional preservation. It achieved a level of postoperative pain control comparable to that of a conventional-volume PENG block or an FICB, while uniquely preserving quadriceps muscle strength. Therefore, the low-volume PENG block represents a suitable and advantageous option for perioperative analgesia in hip arthroscopy.
