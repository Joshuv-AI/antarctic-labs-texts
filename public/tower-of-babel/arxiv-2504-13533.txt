Spectral Gap for the Stochastic Exchange Model
Eric A. Carlen, Gustavo Posta, Imre Péter Tóth 1 . 1. Department of Mathematics, Hill Center, Rutgers University 110 Frelinghuysen Road Piscataway NJ 08854 USA 2 . 2. Dipartimento di Matematica G. Castelnuovo, Sapienza Università di Roma,Piazzale Aldo Moro, 5, I-00185 Roma, Italy 3 . 3. Department of Stochastics, TU Budapest,Egry József u. 1, H-1111, Budapest, Hungary

Abstract
We prove a spectral gap inequality for the stochastic exchange model studied by Gaspard and Gilbert and by Grigo, Khanin and Szász in connection with understanding heat conduction in a deterministic billiards model. The bound on the spectral gap that we prove is uniform in the number of particles, as had been conjectured. We adapt techniques that were originally developed to prove spectral gap bounds for the Kac model with hard sphere collisions, which, like the stochastic exchange model, has degenerate jump rates.



Mathematics subject classification numbers: 60J25, 60J46, 45C05

Key Words: spectral gap, Markov jump process, degenerate rates

Introduction

1.1
The stochastic exchange model

The stochastic exchange model was studied in
[
10
,
11
]
and in
[
13
]
in order to understand how a deterministic billiards model can give rise to a diffusive law for heat conduction. It was also later studied by Sasada
[
18
]
. In this earlier work, the problem of proving the relevant spectral gap bound for a stochastic exchange model with nearest-neighbor interactions was successfully reduced to the problem of proving a spectral gap bound for a mean-field model that is uniform in
N
, the number of particles
[
18
]
. However, a complete proof of this last bound had been missing and it is provided here.

For
N\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
and
E>0
, let
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
be the set
consisting of all vectors
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N})\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
with

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}=E\\
.

A point
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
specifies the energies of a collection of
N
particles, and

{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
is the
state space
for
N
particles with mean energy
E
per particle. The stochastic exchange model that we consider is a continuous time random walk on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}

in which each step of the walk corresponds to a binary collision of a single pair of particles.
With each collision,
the state of the process “jumps” from
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N})
to

(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{i}^{*},\dots,\eta_{j}^{*},\dots,\eta_{N})\ .

In the stochastic exchange model, the two active particles repartition their total energy
\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}
into two new energies
\eta_{i}^{*}
and
\eta_{j}^{*}
, dividing the total energy uniformly at random – the stochastic exchange. Then of course

\eta_{i}^{*}+\eta_{j}^{*}=\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}\ .

(1.1)

The configuration of the energies after the stochastic exchange of energy between particles
i
and
j
will be
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}_{i,j,\alpha}
, for some randomly chosen
\alpha\in[0,1]
where

({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}_{i,j,\alpha})_{k}=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)\eta_{i}+\alpha\eta_{j}&k=i\\
\alpha\eta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\eta_{j}&k=j\\
\eta_{k}&k\neq i,j\ .\end{cases}\

(1.2)

The jump process is then fully specified by giving the rate at which these pairwise stochastic exchanges occur.
Associate to each pair
(i,j)
,
i<j
, an exponential random variable
T_{i,j}
with parameter

\lambda_{i,j}=N\left(\begin{matrix}N\cr 2\cr\end{matrix}\right)^{-1}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j})^{\gamma}\ ,

(1.3)

where
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
. In more detail, the
T_{i,j}
are independent, and

\mathbb{P}\{T_{i,j}>t\}=e^{-t\lambda_{i,j}}
.

T_{i,j}
is the waiting time for particles
i
and
j
to collide.
The first collision occurs at time

T=\min_{i<j}\{T_{i,j}\}\ .

(1.4)

At the time
T
, the pair
(i,j)
furnishing the minimum exchange energy in the prescribed way: With
\alpha\in[0,1]
selected uniformly at random, the process jumps from

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
to

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}_{i,j,\alpha}
. After each collision, the process starts afresh.
Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(t)
denote the random state of the process at time
t
.

The object of our investigation is the spectral gap for the generator of the Markov semigroup associated to the stochastic exchange model described above.
For any continuous function
f
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
,
define the Kac walk generator
L_{\gamma,N,E}
by

L_{\gamma,N,E}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\frac{1}{h}\lim_{h\downarrow 0}{\mathbb{E}}\{f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}(h))-f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\ |\ {\boldsymbol{\eta}}(0)={\boldsymbol{\eta}}\ \}\ ,

where the conditional expectation is taken with respect to the law of the process.
The case
\gamma=1/2
models the case of main physical interest, but the analysis of this case draws on the analysis for
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
, and in the approach taken here,
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
are relatively simple, but for the rest, there is nothing to be gained by focusing on any one value of
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

One readily computes that

L_{\gamma,N,E}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=-{N}{\left(\begin{matrix}N\cr 2\cr\end{matrix}\right)}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j})^{\gamma}\ \int_{0}^{1}[f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})-f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}_{i,j,\alpha}))]{\rm d}\alpha\ .

(1.5)

Introducing the notation

{\displaystyle[f]^{(i,j)}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}):=\int_{0}^{1}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}_{i,j,\alpha}){\rm d}\alpha\ ,}

the generator can be written as

L_{\gamma,N,E}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=-{N}{\left(\begin{matrix}N\cr 2\cr\end{matrix}\right)}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j})^{\gamma}\left[f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})-[f]^{(i,j)}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\right]\ .

(1.6)

There is a somewhat more probabilistic way to write this: Let
\sigma_{N,E}
denote the uniform probability measure on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
.
For
i<j
, let

{\mathcal{F}}_{i,j}
be the
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}_{i,j}:=\sigma\{\eta_{k}\>\ k\neq i,j\}
.
Then

[f]^{(i,j)}={\mathbb{E}}\{f|{\mathcal{F}}_{i,j}\}
where the conditional expectation is taken with respect to
\sigma_{N,E}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{N,E}
denote the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}_{N,E},\sigma_{N,E})
. One readily checks that
L_{\gamma,N,E}
is self-adjoint and bounded on
\mathcal{H}_{N,E}
. For
f,g\in\mathcal{H}_{N,E}
, we denote the inner product of
f
and
g
by
\langle f,g\rangle
and the
norm of
f
by
{\|}f{\|}_{2}
.
More generally, for a measurable function
f
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
{\|}f{\|}_{p}
denote the norm of
f
in
L^{p}({\mathcal{S}}_{N,E},\sigma_{N,E})
.

Let
{\cal E}_{\gamma,N,E}
be the Dirichlet form on
\mathcal{H}_{N,E}
,

{\cal E}_{\gamma,N,E}(f,f)=-\langle f,L_{\gamma,N,E}f\rangle\ .

(1.7)

Evidently, the uniform distribution
\sigma_{N,E}
is the unique equilibrium state for this process.

The object of our investigation, the
spectral gap
, is the quantity

\Delta_{\gamma,N,E}:=\inf\{{\cal E}_{\gamma,N,E}(f,f)\ :\ f\in\mathcal{H}_{N,E}\ ,\ {\|}f{\|}_{2}=1\ ,\ \langle f,1\rangle=0\ \}\ .

(1.8)

Because the jump rates defined in (
1.3
) are homogeneous in
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
, the dependence of
\Delta_{\gamma,N,E}
on
E
is a simple matter of scaling:

1.1 LEMMA
.

For all
N>0
,
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and
E,E^{\prime}>0
,

\Delta_{\gamma,N,E}=\left(\frac{E}{E^{\prime}}\right)^{\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma,N,E^{\prime}}\ .

(1.9)

Proof.

Suppose
f
is any measurable function on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E^{\prime}}
. Define
Uf
by

{\displaystyle Uf({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=f\left(\frac{E^{\prime}}{E}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)}
.
Then,
U
is unitary from
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,E^{\prime}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,E}
because
\sigma_{N,E^{\prime}}
is the push-forward of
\sigma_{N,E}
under the map
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mapsto\frac{E^{\prime}}{E}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
For the same reason,

N{\left(\begin{matrix}N\cr 2\cr\end{matrix}\right)}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E^{\prime}}}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j})^{\gamma}(f-[f]^{(i,j)})^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N,E^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{E^{\prime}}{E}\right)^{\gamma}{\cal E}_{\gamma,N,E}(Uf,Uf)\ .

(1.10)

Then (
1.9
) follows from (
1.10
) and the definition (
1.8
).
∎

This scaling relation will be important in the inductive estimation of
\Delta_{\gamma,N,1}
that follows.
Note that in the case of uniform jump rates; that is,
\gamma=0
, the value of
E
is immaterial.
On account of Lemma
1.1
, we simplify our notation: We need only determine the spectral gap for unit energy per particle, and then we know it in general.
Therefore, we define

\mathcal{S}_{N}=\mathcal{S}_{N,1},\quad\sigma_{N}:=\sigma_{N,1},\quad L_{\gamma,N}:=L_{\gamma,N,1},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}:=\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N,1},\quad\Delta_{\gamma,N}:=\Delta_{\gamma,N,1},\quad\mathcal{H}_{N}:=\mathcal{H}_{N,1}\ .

Our goal is then to determine
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
. Our approach will be inductive on
N
. For
\gamma>0
, it is difficult to compute, or even closely estimate,
\Delta_{\gamma,3}
. However, for
N=2
, it is a simple matter to exactly compute the spectral gap for all
\gamma
.
When
N=2
, there is only one pair
i<j
and
\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}=2
for all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
Then from (
1.6
)

L_{\gamma,2}f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=-2^{1+\gamma}\left[f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})-{\mathbb{E}}(f)\right]\ .

(1.11)

If follows that the eigenvalues of
L_{\gamma,2}
are
0
and
-2^{1+\gamma}
and the
0
eigenspace is spanned by the constant function
1
, and the

-2^{1+\gamma}
eigenspace is spanned by the functions orthogonal to
1
. We record this as a lemma:

1.2 LEMMA
.

\Delta_{\gamma,2}=2^{\gamma+1}
for all
\gamma\geq 0
.

Our main result is:

1.3 THEOREM
.

For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
and all
N\geq 2
there is a constant
C>0
depending only on
\gamma
such that

\Delta_{\gamma,N}\geq C\ .

(1.12)

The simplest case is
\gamma=0
, which was already treated in
[
3
,
12
]
. The next simplest case is
\gamma=1
, and this was treated in
[
18
]
, which also deals with
\gamma>1
. However, dealing with
0<\gamma<1
is more involved and was open until now. As discussed in the next subsection, the case of greatest physical interest is
\gamma=1/2
.

In view of Lemma
1.2
, even where it is not explicitly stated, we tacitly assume
N\geq 3
.
Furthermore in the sequel “a constant” will be a number which may depend on
\gamma
but not on
N
, and the value of the constant
C
may change from line to line.

1.2
Physical context and previous results

We close the introduction with a brief discussion of the physical context of this problem and the history of the progress made on it. Its origins lie in ground-breaking work of Gaspard and Gilbert
[
10
,
11
]
on the long-standing problem of deriving Fourier’s law of heat transfer in insulating solid materials. In this work, as further developed by Grigo, Khanin, and Szász
[
13
]
, the stochastic exchange process described here arises for the specific value
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}
. The physically relevant problem was to show that
\Delta_{\frac{1}{2},N}
is bounded below by a strictly positive constant
C
, independent of
N
.

The spectral gap problem for the stochastic exchange
model is closely related to the spectral gap problem for the
Kac model
[
15
]
. In the simplest version of the latter, the simplex is replaced by the
sphere representing the velocities of
N
particles with given total kinetic energy, and the dynamics consists of binary “collisions” that conserve the kinetic energy, or for velocities in
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
,
d>2
, the kinetic energy and momentum.
In 1956, Kac conjectured a uniform lower bound on the gap for the simplest version of the model with one-dimensional velocities and
and uniform “collision rates”, so that all kinematically possible collisions are equally likely. This conjecture was first proved by Janvresse
[
14
]
, and then the exact value of the gap was determined in
[
4
,
5
]
, using an induction on the number of particles with controlled bounds on correlations. For further background on the Kac model, see
[
6
]
.

Another method for bounding the spectral gap was developed by Caputo
[
3
]
that does not use induction on the number of particles. He showed that when the spectral gap for 3 particles is sufficiently large, then this directly implies a lower bound on the gap for all
N
; see

[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
. He applied this not only to the original Kac model, but what he calls the “flat Kac model”, which is the stochastic exchange model for
\gamma=0
. He notes that while his method is quite simple, it is somewhat less general. The Kac model spectral gap in
3
dimensions with energy and momentum conserving collisions, but still with uniform collision rates, was computed in
[
9
]
, and as Caputo notes, the gap for
N=3
is too small for his method to apply. About the same time, Geroux and Ferland treated a class of models including the stochastic exchange model for
\gamma=0
, using the methods of
[
4
,
5
]
, and they too obtained the exact value of the spectral gap in this case.

In the more physically meaningful versions of the Kac model and the stochastic exchange model, not all collisions occur with the same rates. Instead, the rates depend on the energy of the pair of particles, or other conserved quantities depending on the model, and the rates are generally degenerate. That is, the rate can be zero for some pairs. This leads to considerable mathematical difficulty, but for the Kac model with physically realistic “hard-sphere collisions” the problem of bounding the spectral gap from below uniformly in
N
was solved in
[
7
]
in one dimension, and in
[
8
]
in three dimensions, using induction with controlled correlation bounds.

Around the same time, Sasada was working on a class of stochastic exchange models, including the one discussed here. She adapted Caputo’s method from
[
3
]
to non-uniform rates
[
18
]
; see also
[
17
]
. She proved a uniform lower bound on the spectral gap for
\gamma\geq 1
, but her work for
\gamma<1
depended on the calculation in
an appendix that was intended to show that also for
\gamma<1
, the gap for
N=3
is sufficiently large to imply a uniform lower bound for all
N
.
Unfortunately, it was discovered that these computations were not correct.

Here we apply the methods developed for the Kac model to the stochastic exchange model, and prove in Theorem
1.3
a uniform lower bound on the spectral gap in the specific case that is relevant to the physics discussed in
[
10
,
11
]
and
[
13
]
. A broader class of models can be handled by the same method, and we briefly discuss this in the final section.

An advantage of the inductive method that we develop here is that it allows one to make use of simplifications that emerge for large
N
. In particular, for large
N
, any pair of coordinate functions
(\eta_{j},\eta_{k})
are approximately independent with respect to the uniform probability measure on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
. If the correlations are already sufficiently small at
N=3
, then Caputo’s method is very efficient. But verifying this, even for
N=3
can be quite difficult. The method used here avoids such difficult calculations; one need only do careful calculations for large
N
, and then take advantage of simplifications due the the approximate independence.
In fact, in other work, Caputo
[
2
]
has made use of the inductive approach to treat uniform spectral gaps for diffusive Ginzburg-Landau model on the simplex, and he used Local Central Limit Theorem estimates, among other tools, to take advantage of the simplicity that emerges for large
N
.

We now explain the induction with controlled correlations.

2
The induction

The inductive step consists of writing the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)
in terms of an average over Dirichlet forms in which the
k^{{\rm th}}
particle is “frozen” and not allowed to exchange energy with the others. What remains is the interaction between
N-1
particles. Then the total energy possessed by these
N-1
particles is
N-\eta_{k}
, and hence the energy per particle is
{\displaystyle\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
. Thus, the remaining
N-1
particles carry out the same sort of walk on

\mathcal{S}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
.
These considerations lead to the definition of the

conditional Dirichlet form

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k})
:

{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k}):={(N-1)}{\left(\begin{matrix}N-1\cr 2\cr\end{matrix}\right)}^{-1}\sum_{i<j;i,j\neq k}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}}(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j})^{\gamma}(f-[f]^{(i,j)})^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}\ ,

(2.1)

noting that with the value of
\eta_{k}
fixed, the remaining
N-1
variables range over a copy of
\mathcal{S}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
which is the sense in which the integral is to be taken.

Let
{\rm d}\nu_{N}
be the marginal distribution induced on
[0,N]
by the map

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mapsto\eta_{k}
and the uniform probability measure
{\rm d}\sigma_{N}
on

{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
, which is independent of
k
by symmetry. Since
\sigma_{N}
is a flat Dirichlet measure, it is well-known (and easy to see) that

{\rm d}\nu_{N}(\eta)=\frac{N-1}{N^{N-1}}(N-\eta)^{N-2}{\rm d}\eta=\frac{N-1}{N}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{N}\right)^{N-2}{\rm d}\eta\ .

(2.2)

which is a rescaled
\beta
distribution. Therefore, in the limit
N\to\infty
,
{\rm d}\nu_{N}
converges to the exponential distribution

e^{-\eta}{\rm d}\eta
on
[0,\infty)
. A simple computation shows that all moments of
{\rm d}\nu_{N}
are bounded by those of the exponential distribution.

One easily checks that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)=\frac{N}{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{N}{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k}){\rm d}\nu_{N}(\eta_{k})\right)\ .

(2.3)

Furthermore,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k})={\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}(g_{\eta_{k}},g_{\eta_{k}})

(2.4)

where
g_{\eta_{k}}
is the restriction of
f
to the
slice of

\mathcal{S}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
at constant
\eta_{k}
.
If
g_{\eta_{k}}
were orthogonal to the constants in
{{\mathcal{H}}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}}
, we could estimate the right hand side of (
2.4
)
in terms of
\Delta_{\gamma,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
. By Lemma
1.1
,

{\Delta_{\gamma,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}=\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}}
.
Combining this with (
2.4
) would yield (assuming
g_{\eta_{k}}
is orthogonal to the constants)

{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k})\geq\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}{\|}g_{\eta_{k}}{\|}_{2}^{2}\ .

(2.5)

By the Fubini-Tonelli theorem,
{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}=\int_{[0,N]}{\|}g_{\eta_{k}}{\|}_{2}^{2}{\rm d}\nu_{N}(\eta_{k})
for any
k
.
For
\gamma=0
, this together with (
2.3
) would give us

{\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0,N}(f,f)=\frac{N}{N-1}\Delta_{0,N-1}{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}}
,
which is too good to be true for all
f
, since in that case is would yield, by a simple induction starting from Lemma
1.2
, that
\Delta_{0,N}\geq N
. This is demonstrably false.

We must take into account that even if

f
is orthogonal to the constants in

\mathcal{H}_{N}
, it need not be the case that
g_{\eta_{k}}

is orthogonal to the constants on its slice.

2.1 DEFINITION
.

Define the operator
P_{k}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}

to be the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of functions depending only on
\eta_{k}
. In probabilistic terms,
P_{k}
is the operator of conditional expectation given
\eta_{k}
.

Then the restriction of
f-P_{k}f
to any slice determined by fixing a value for
\eta_{k}
is orthogonal to the constants on the slice. Therefore,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f|\eta_{k})={\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma,N}(f-P_{k}f,f-P_{k}f|\eta_{k})\ .

Denoting with
{\|}\cdot{\|}_{2,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
the norm on
\mathcal{H}_{N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}
, by the definition of the spectral gap and scaling relation (
1.9
),

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}(f-P_{k}f,f-P_{k}f|\eta_{k})\geq\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}\|f-P_{k}f\|^{2}_{2,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}\ .

Finally, we have the obvious identity

\displaystyle\|f-P_{k}f\|^{2}_{2,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}=\|f\|^{2}_{2,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}-\|P_{k}f\|^{2}_{2,N-1,\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}}\ .

Altogether, going back to (
2.3
), one has

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\geq\frac{N}{N-1}\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\left(f^{2}-|P_{k}f|^{2}\right){\rm d}\sigma_{N}\right)\ .

(2.6)

To summarize our conclusions, define the quadratic form
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}

as follows:

2.2 DEFINITION
.

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\int_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}w^{(\gamma)}_{N}(\eta_{k})(f-P_{k}f)^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}\right]\

(2.7)

where

w^{(\gamma)}_{N}(\eta):=\left(\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\ .

(2.8)

We have proved:

2.3 THEOREM
.

For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
and
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
with
{\|}f{\|}^{2}_{2}=1
and
\langle 1,f\rangle=0
,

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\geq\left[\frac{N}{N-1}\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}\right]\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\ .

(2.9)

We therefore define

2.4 DEFINITION
.

\Gamma_{\gamma,N}:=\inf\{\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\ :\ f\in\mathcal{H}_{N}\ ,\ {\|}f{\|}_{2}=1\ ,\ \langle f,1\rangle=0\}\ .

(2.10)

Combining this definition with (
1.8
), Theorem
2.3
yields

\Delta_{\gamma,N}\geq\Delta_{\gamma,N-1}\frac{N}{N-1}\Gamma_{\gamma,N}\ .

(2.11)

For (
2.11
) to provide a lower bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
that is uniform in
N
, we need a lower bound on
\Gamma_{\gamma,N}
at least as strong as

\Gamma_{\gamma,N}\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}-A_{N}

(2.12)

where

0\leq A_{N}<\frac{N-1}{N}\quad{\rm and}\quad\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}A_{N}<\infty\ .

(2.13)

This is because

{\displaystyle\frac{N}{N-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}-A_{N}\right)=1-\frac{N}{N-1}A_{N}\ .}

Therefore, with
0\leq A_{N}<\frac{N-1}{N}
for all
N\geq 3
, and for any
N_{0}\geq 3
,

\prod_{N=N_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{N}{N-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}-A_{N}\right)>0

if and only if the summability condition in (
2.13
) is satisfied. In particular, if one replaces
the second term on the right in (
2.12
) by
-\frac{C}{N}
for any
C>1
, the infinite product would converge to zero, yielding a trivial bound. Likewise, were it the case that
C<1
, one would obtain a spectral gap that diverges to infinity with
N
, and simple trial functional calculations show that this is impossible. Therefore, if the strategy is to work,
\Gamma_{\gamma,N}
must satisfy
\Gamma_{\gamma,N}=1-\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
, and one must calculate the first two terms
exactly

and then show that the remainder is summable.

As we shall see, this is easily done for
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
. However, the Dirichlet forms
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}
are not monotone in
\gamma
. Indeed, for all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
except
(1,\dots,1)
, there exists at least one index
k
such that
\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}<1
,
and at least one index
k
such that
\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}>1
. Consequently, the rates in the Dirichlet forms
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)
are not monotone in
\gamma
. In more probabilistic terms, it is not the case that any one of the corresponding processes simply runs faster than another. Therefore, there is no trivial monotonicity argument for passing estimates for
\gamma\in\{0,1\}
to
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Most of our work goes into proving, in
Section
6
, the following theorem:

2.5 THEOREM
.

For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
and all
N\geq 3
there is a constant
C>0
depending only on
\gamma
such that

\Gamma_{\gamma,N}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{N}-\frac{C}{N^{3/2}}\right)\ .

(2.14)

For small values of
N
, the lower bound provided by Theorem
2.5
may be trivial; the right side of (
2.14
) may be negative. Define
N_{0}
to be the least value of
N
such that the right side of (
2.14
) is strictly positive. Then, provided we know that
\Delta_{\gamma,N}>0
for all
N\leq N_{0}
, we may begin the induction at
N_{0}
to obtain the lower bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
claimed in our main result, Theorem
1.3
.

It is relatively simple to prove that
\Delta_{\gamma,N}>0
for all
N\leq N_{0}
. A uniform lower bound on
\Delta_{1,N}
has already been proved by Sasada
[
18
, Theorem 4]
using her refinement of Caputo’s method
[
3
]
. As we show in
Section
4
, a simple comparison argument leads from this to
a lower bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
that is not uniform, but does give us what we need to begin the induction at
N=N_{0}
, and to then apply Theorem
2.5
. The next lemma is proved in
Section
4
.

2.6 LEMMA
.

For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
and all
N\geq 3
there is a constant
C>0
depending only on
\gamma
such that

\Delta_{\gamma,N}\geq CN^{\gamma-1}\ .

(2.15)

Assuming for now the validity of Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
, we now prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Choose
N_{0}
sufficiently large that
1-N_{0}^{-1}-CN_{0}^{-3/2}>0
where
C
is the constant in (
2.14
).
By Lemma
2.6
,
\Delta_{\gamma,N_{0}}>0
. By (
2.11
) and Theorem
2.5
, for all
N>N_{0}
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma,N}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma,N_{0}}\prod_{j=N_{0}+1}^{N}\frac{j}{j-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{j}-\frac{C}{j^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma,N_{0}}\prod_{j=N_{0}+1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{C}{j^{3/2}}\right)\ ,

where
C
has changed in the second line. The infinite product is strictly positive since
\sum_{j=N_{0}}^{\infty}j^{-3/2}<\infty
. The lower bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
for
N<N_{0}
is taken care of by Lemma
2.6
.
∎

From this point on, our focus is on proving Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
. This comes down to a careful analysis of the
Dirichlet form
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}
and its spectral gap
\Gamma_{\gamma,N}
. Note that the corresponding process is much simpler than the original stochastic exchange process. At each jump, one energy
\eta_{k}
is fixed and all of the other particles “jump to uniform” on the slice given by the fixed value of
\eta_{k}
. The rate for such a jump is degenerate, but mildly so: it vanishes only if the
k^{{\rm th}}
particle has all of the energy.

In proving Theorem
2.5
, we make essential use of the fact that for large
N
, the random variables are almost independent. A number of precise expressions of this fact that we shall need are given in Section
3
. We now explain the importance of this asymptotic independence, and at the same time, why the cases
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
are so much simpler.

For this purpose, it is useful to rewrite the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}
in another way. Define

P^{(\gamma)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}P_{k}\ .

(2.16)

For each
k
, both
P_{k}
and the multiplication operator

{\displaystyle\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}}
are commuting and self adjoint, so that

P^{(\gamma)}
itself is self-adjoint, and even non-negative.
Since each
P_{k}
is a projection,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}|P_{k}f|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}=\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}\ .

(2.17)

Define the function
W^{(\gamma)}
by

W^{(\gamma)}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w^{(\gamma)}_{N}(\eta_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\ .

(2.18)

We may now rewrite
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}
as

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}W^{(\gamma)}_{N}f^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}-\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle\ .

(2.19)

From an upper bound of
P^{(\gamma)}
, and a lower bound on
w^{(\gamma)}_{N}
, we can deduce a lower bound on
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}
.

Note that
W^{(0)}=W^{(1)}=1
. Therefore, denoting with
{\mathds{1}}
the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
,

\mathcal{G}_{0,N}(f,f)=\langle f,({\mathds{1}}-P^{(0)})f\rangle\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{1,N}(f,f)=\langle f,({\mathds{1}}-P^{(1)})f\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}\ .

(2.20)

Hence for these two cases, we only need information on the spectrum of
P^{(0)}
and
P^{(1)}
. One can see from (
2.18
) and the Law of Large Numbers that if the random variables
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
were exactly independent, then for large
N
,

W^{(\gamma)}
would be very close to the constant
{\mathbb{E}}[W^{(\gamma)}]
. However this alone is not enough. The proof of Theorem
2.5
is accomplished by showing that trial functions
f
for which
\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}

is large,
f^{2}
cannot concentrate too much mass in places where

W^{(\gamma)}
is significantly below
1
.

3
Chaoticity bounds

The operator
P^{(0)}
is an average over
N
orthogonal projections. If
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
were independent random variables on

{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
, these projections would commute, and the intersection of their ranges would be spanned by the constant function
1
. In this case,
the spectrum of
P^{(0)}
would be the set
\{1,\frac{1}{N},0\}
, with the constant function
1
spanning the eigenspace with eigenvalue
1
, and the
eigenspace for
\frac{1}{N}
being spanned by functions of the form
f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\varphi(\eta_{k})
for some
k
, with
\varphi
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(\eta_{k})]=0
.

Because of the constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}=N
,
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
are not independent, but for large
N
we expect the dependence to be weak.
In this section we prove several quantitative bounds on the dependence of these variables that are crucial for what follows.

3.1 DEFINITION
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
denote the Hilbert space

L^{2}([0,N],\nu_{N})

where
\nu_{N}
is the probability measure defined in (
2.2
).

Define the map
T_{N}:[0,N]\times{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}\to\mathcal{S}_{N}
by

T_{N}(\eta,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left(\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\xi_{1},\dots\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\xi_{N-1},\eta\right)\ .

(3.1)

It is easy to check that the push-forward under
T_{N}
of
\nu_{N}\otimes\sigma_{N-1}
is
\sigma_{N}
.

3.2 DEFINITION
.

The
correlation operator

K
on
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
is defined by

K\varphi(\eta)=\mathbb{E}\{\varphi\left(\eta_{1}\right)|\eta_{N}=\eta\}\ .

Defining
\pi_{k}:\mathcal{S}_{N}\to[0,N]
by
\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\eta_{k}
, we may relate
K
to the operator
P_{N}
specified in Definition
2.1
:

K\varphi(\eta_{N})=P_{N}(\varphi\circ\pi_{1})(\eta_{N})\ .

(3.2)

Note that by symmetry, in (
3.2
) the indices
1
and
N
can be replaced by any pair of distinct indices, and in particular, the roles of
1
and
N
can be interchanged. Then by (
3.2
),
for
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
,

\langle\psi,K\varphi\rangle={\mathbb{E}}\left((\psi\circ\pi_{N})(\varphi\circ\pi_{1})\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left((\psi\circ\pi_{1})(\varphi\circ\pi_{N})\right)=\langle K\psi,\varphi\rangle\ .

Thus,
K
is self-adjoint, and we now determine its spectrum.

By (
3.1
),

K\varphi(\eta)=\int_{0}^{N}\varphi\left(\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}x\right){\rm d}\nu_{N-1}(x)\ .

(3.3)

From this explicit expression, we conclude that for any positive integer
m
, the space of polynomials of degree at most
m
is invariant under
K
.
To see this, let
p_{n}(x):=x^{n}
,
n
a non-negative integer. Then,
using the map
T_{N}
defined in (
3.1
)

Kp_{n}(\eta)=\left(\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\right)^{n}\int_{0}^{N-1}x^{n}{\rm d}\nu_{N-1}(x)=(N-\eta)^{n}\left((N-2)\int_{0}^{1}y^{n}(1-y)^{N-3}{\rm d}y\right)\ .

(3.4)

The integral on the right is a
\beta
function integral, and in any case, integration by parts yields

(N-2)\int_{0}^{1}y^{n}(1-y)^{N-3}{\rm d}y=\frac{n!(N-2)!}{(n+N-2)!}.

(3.5)

Thus the subspace
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
of
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
consisting of polynomials of degree
n
or less is invariant under
K
. Since
K
is self adjoint,

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\perp}
is also invariant, and then so is the one dimensional space
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{n-1}^{\perp}
.
This is precisely the space spanned by the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
polynomial in the sequence
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of orthogonal polynomials obtained by applying the
Gram-Schmidt procedure to the sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and using the inner product in
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
. Hence for each
n
,
\phi_{n}
is an eigenvector of
K
. Let
\kappa_{n}
denote the associated eigenvalue.

Then expanding the monomial
p_{n}
in this basis,

{\displaystyle p_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}\phi_{j}}
where
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
, which we may write as
p_{n}=\alpha_{n}\phi_{n}+q

where
q
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
. Then

\kappa_{n}\phi_{n}=K\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}K\left(p_{n}-q\right)=(N-\eta)^{n}\frac{n!(N-2)!}{(n+N-2)!}-Kq\ .

Therefore,

\kappa_{n}\phi_{n}-(-1)^{n}\eta^{n}\frac{n!(N-2)!}{(n+N-2)!}

is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
. The left hand side of (
3.5
) clearly decreases as
n
increases, showing that
|\kappa_{n}|
is a decreasing function of
n
. This proves:

3.3 THEOREM
.

Let
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of orthogonal polynomials obtained by applying the
Gram-Schmidt procedure to the sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Then
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
consisting of eigenvectors of
K
wth
K\phi_{n}=\kappa_{n}\phi_{n}
where

\kappa_{n}=(-1)^{n}\frac{n!(N-2)!}{(n+N-2)!}\ .

(3.6)

For all
n
,
|\kappa_{n+1}|\leq|\kappa_{n}|
.

The first three eigenvalues are

\kappa_{0}=1\ ,\quad\kappa_{1}=-\frac{1}{N-1}\quad{\rm and}\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\ ,

(3.7)

and the first two normalized eigenfunctions are

\phi_{0}=1\quad{\rm and}\quad\phi_{1}(\eta)=\sqrt{\frac{N+1}{N-1}}(\eta-1)\ .\

(3.8)

The same method for determining the spectrum of the analogous
K
operator for the Kac model was used in
[
4
,
5
]
, and has been applied to the

K
operator for a class of models including the Stochastic Exchange model in
[
12
]
. An analysis of the spectrum of the
K
operator has been made by
Caputo
[
2
]
in a setting in which the uniform probability measure on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
is replaced by a fairly general conditioned product measure,
such as those discussed in the final section of this paper. While Caputo’s result,
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
, covers a broad range of
reference measures, the results are less precise than the specific bounds provided by
Theorem
3.3
, and these bounds are needed in our application.

3.4 DEFINITION
.

Let
{\mathord{\cal A}}_{N}

denote the subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
consisting of functions
f
of the form

f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\ .

(3.9)

with the further condition that
{\mathbb{E}}[f]=0
.

Note that
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})]={\mathbb{E}}[f]=0}
. Therefore subtracting
{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})]
from

\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})
for each
j
does not change the sum in (
3.9
), and we may freely suppose that the functions
\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})
in
(
3.9
) are such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})]=0
.

Because
\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\eta_{j}-1)=0
, for any
\alpha\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
, we may also subtract
\alpha(\eta_{j}-1)
from each
\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})

without changing the sum in (
3.9
). We shall choose
\alpha
to make
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}}
as small as possible as
\alpha
is varied.

Expand each
\varphi_{j}
in the orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

{\displaystyle\varphi_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j,k}\phi_{k}}

where the sum starts from
k=1
since
\alpha_{j,0}=\langle\phi_{0},\varphi_{j}\rangle={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})]=0
.
Then

\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left((\alpha_{j,1}-\alpha)^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}|\alpha_{j,k}|^{2}\right)\ .

This is minimized by choosing
\alpha:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j,1}
. We make this choice, and note that consequently

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j,1}=0\ .

(3.10)

We have proved the following lemma:

3.5 LEMMA
.

Every function
f\in{\mathord{\cal A}}_{N}
has a canonical representation
{\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})}
where, for
j=1,\dots,N
,
\varphi_{j}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\varphi(\eta_{j})\in\mathcal{H}_{N}

with

{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})]=0
and

\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})(\eta_{j}-1)]=\sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\phi_{1}(\eta_{j})]=0\ .

(3.11)

3.6 THEOREM
.

For
f\in{\mathord{\cal A}}_{N}
, let
{\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})}
be its canonical representation as provided by Lemma
3.5
.
Then for all
N\geq 2
,

\left(1-\frac{2}{N}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}\leq{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{N}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}

(3.12)

Proof.

We compute
{\displaystyle{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq k}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})]}
. Define

\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}:=\varphi_{j}-\alpha_{j,1}\phi_{1}
.
Then since by (
3.10
),

\sum_{k\colon k\neq j}\alpha_{k,1}=-\alpha_{j,1}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq k}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})]=\sum_{j\neq k}\langle\varphi_{j},K\varphi_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\neq k}\alpha_{j,1}\alpha_{k,1}\frac{1}{N-1}\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{1}\rangle+\sum_{j\neq k}\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},K\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha^{2}_{j,1}+\sum_{j\neq k}\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},K\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}\rangle.

Next, for
j\neq k
,

|\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},K\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}\rangle|\leq\kappa_{2}{\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}{\|}_{2}{\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}{\|}_{2}=\frac{2}{N(N-1)}{\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}{\|}_{2}{\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}{\|}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{N(N-1)}({\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}+{\|}\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}{\|}_{2}^{2})\ .

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq k}|\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},K\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}\rangle|\leq\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}|\alpha_{j,k}|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}^{2}

,

and (
3.12

∎

Let
\bigoplus^{N}{\mathcal{K}}_{N}

We now determine the spectrum of

. An equivalent result has been obtained in
[

]
by different method based on the original approach for the Kac model in

.

3.8 LEMMA
.

For all
N\geq 3
, the spectrum of
P^{(0)}
consists entirely of eigenvalues.
Let
\mu_{N}
denote the second largest eigenvalue of
P^{(0)}
after the eigenvalue

. Then for
N\geq 3
,

\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\ .

(3.13)

Proof.

The range of
P^{(0)}
is
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
, and it suffices to determine the spectrum of
P^{(0)}
as an operator on
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
. For

{\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{N}}
, we compute, using (
3.2
) and symmetry in the coordinates,

P^{(0)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})+\sum_{k\neq j}K\varphi_{k}(\eta_{j})\right)\ .

Let
{\mathcal{T}}:{\mathcal{B}}_{N}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
be defined as in Remark
3.7
. Then by this computation,

{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}P^{(0)}{\mathcal{T}}

can be written as an
N\times N
block matrix operator
whose entries

are given by

M_{i,j}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{N}I\quad{\rm if}\ i=j\quad{\rm and}\quad M_{i,j}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{N}K\quad{\rm if}\ i\neq j\ ,

where

is the identity operator on
\mathcal{K}_{N}
.

This is unitarily equivalent to the block matrix operator

\frac{1}{N}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}I+(N-1)K&0&\cdots&0\\
0&I-K&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&I-K\end{array}\right]\ .

By Theorem
3.3

K
is compact, and hence the spectrum of this operator consists entirely of eigenvalues. If
\lambda
is any eigenvalue, it follows that either
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of

or else

is an eigenvalue of

. Thus, the second largest eigenvalue of

either
\frac{1+(N-1)\kappa_{2}}{N}
, where

is the second largest eigenvalue of

, or else

\frac{1-\kappa_{1}}{N}
where
\kappa_{1}
is the least eigenvalue of

, namely
-\frac{1}{N-1}
. The two alternatives are

\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\ .

The first is larger for all

, and hence this gives the second largest eigenvalue.
∎

4
Bounds for small
N

We begin by proving a uniform lower bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
for
\gamma=0
and then

. For
\gamma=0

\Delta_{0,N}
in terms of

may be applied, and he has found

, and hence the sharp uniform bound

We present a short proof here as the simplest introduction to the use of the estimates obtained in the previous section. This result covers the
\gamma=0

. It is worth noting that the inductive method also yields the sharp value for the gap for
\gamma=0
in the stochastic exchange model, as it did for the Kac model
[

.

4.1 THEOREM
.

The spectral gap
\Gamma_{0,N}
is strictly positive for all
N\geq 3
, and satisfies

\Gamma_{0,N}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{N}-\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right)\ .

(4.1)

The spectral gap
\Delta_{0,N}
is bounded below uniformly in
N\geq 3
. Specifically,

\Delta_{0,N}\geq\frac{2}{3}\frac{N+1}{N-1}\ .

(4.2)

Proof.

First, for
\gamma=0
, we have from
(
2.20
) and Lemma

that for
f
orthogonal to the constants

{\mathcal{G}}_{0,N}(f,f)=\langle f,({\mathds{1}}-P^{(0)})f\rangle\geq\left(1-\tfrac{1}{N}-\tfrac{2}{N^{2}}\right){\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}\ .

This shows that
\Gamma_{0,N}\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}-\tfrac{2}{N^{2}}>0

for all
N\geq 3
.
Next,

\displaystyle\frac{N}{N-1}\Gamma_{0,N}\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{N-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{N}-\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}=\left(\frac{N+1}{N-1}\right)\left(\frac{N}{N-2}\right)^{-1}\ .

Then by (
2.11
),

\displaystyle\Delta_{0,N}\geq\Delta_{0,2}\prod_{j=3}^{N}\left(\frac{j+1}{j-1}\right)\left(\frac{j}{j-2}\right)^{-1}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{N+1}{N-1}\ ,

which is the exact lower bound found in
[
3
,
12
]
. This lower bound can be shown to be sharp by displaying the corresponding eigenfunction; see
[
3
]
.
∎

We next illustrate the inductive method by giving a short proof of Sasada’s result
[
18
]
that
\Delta_{1,N}
is bounded below, uniformly in
N
, by a strictly positive constant.
This simple proof keeps our paper self-contained, and we can proceed almost exactly as in the proof of Theorem

;
we only need an upper bound on
\langle f,P^{(1)}f\rangle
for
f
orthogonal to the constants.

4.2 LEMMA
.

For all
N
, all
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
, and all
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
orthogonal to the constants,

\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle\leq\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\langle f,P^{(0)}f\rangle\ .

(4.3)

Proof.

Using the point-wise upper bound

{\displaystyle\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)\leq\frac{N}{N-1}}
, we have

\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}|P_{k}f|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}\leq\\
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}|P_{k}f|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}=\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\langle f,P^{(0)}f\rangle\ .

(4.4)

∎

4.3 THEOREM
.

The spectral gap
\Gamma_{1,N}
is strictly positive for all
N\geq 3
, and satisfies

\Gamma_{1,N}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\right)\ .

(4.5)

The spectral gap
\Delta_{1,N}
is uniformly bounded below in
N\geq 3
. Specifically,

\Delta_{1,N}\geq 4\prod_{j=3}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{3}{(j-1)^{2}}\right)>0\ .

(4.6)

Proof.

First, for
\gamma=1
, we have from
(
2.20
) and Lemma
4.2
that for
f
orthogonal to the constants

{\mathcal{G}}_{1,N}(f,f)=\langle f,({\mathds{1}}-P^{(1)})f\rangle\geq\left\langle f,\left(1-\frac{N}{N-1}P^{(0)}\right)f\right\rangle\ .

By Lemma
3.8
,

\left\langle f,\left(1-\frac{N}{N-1}P^{(0)}\right)f\right\rangle\geq 1-\frac{N}{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\ .

This shows that
\Gamma_{1,N}\geq 1-\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}
which is strictly positive for all
N\geq 3
.

Next,

\frac{N}{N-1}\Gamma_{1,N}\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{N-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\right)=1-\frac{3}{(N-1)^{2}}\ .

Since
\sum_{N=3}^{\infty}\tfrac{3}{(N-1)^{2}}<\infty
,
\prod_{j=3}^{\infty}\left(1-\tfrac{3}{(j-1)^{2}}\right)>0
, and by Lemma
1.2
,
\Delta_{1,2}=4
.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.3
, we can give a simple proof that for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
and all
{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}\geq 3
,
\Delta_{\gamma,N}>0

which is all we really need from Lemma

.
Since

\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\leq\frac{N}{N-1}
,

w^{(\gamma)}_{N}(\eta)=\left(\frac{N-\eta}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\geq\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{1-\gamma}w^{(1)}_{N}(\eta),

it follows from the definition (

) that for all trial functions
f
,

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\geq\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{1-\gamma}\mathcal{G}_{1,N}(f,f)\geq\left(1-(1-\gamma)\frac{1}{N-1}\right)\mathcal{G}_{1,N}(f,f)\

where the final inequality is a simple convexity estimate that gives up nothing of consequence.

Combining this with (
4.1
) then yields that for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
N\geq 3
.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma,N}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-(1-\gamma)\frac{1}{N-1})\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2-\gamma}{N-1}-\frac{(1+\gamma)}{(N-1)^{2}}=1-\frac{(2-\gamma)N-3}{(N-1)^{2}}>0\ .

In fact, this argument almost gives a proof of Lemma
2.6
. It shows that for any
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
, and all sufficiently large
N
(depending on the choice of
\gamma^{\prime}
),
\Gamma_{\gamma,N}\geq\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{\gamma^{\prime}}
.
Therefore, a simple induction using (
2.11
) yields
\Delta_{\gamma,N}\geq CN^{\gamma^{\prime}-1}
for a constant
C
depending only on
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
.

The previous argument is simple but somewhat crude, and one may hope that a more direct application of the inductive method
using a sharp lower bound on
W^{(\gamma)}
would yield a significantly better bound on
\Delta_{\gamma,N}
. The next lemma provides the lower bound on
W^{(\gamma)}
.

4.4 LEMMA
.

For all
N
, all
0<\gamma\leq 1
, and for all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
,

\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{1-\gamma}\ \leq\ W^{(\gamma)}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\ \leq\ 1\ .

(4.7)

Proof.

Since

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}=1
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)=1
,
Jensen’s inequality yields

{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}=1\ .}

For the lower bound, since the function
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}
is concave, it is minimized at an extreme point of
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
.
The extreme points
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
are those for which
\eta_{k}=N
for some
k
with all other entries being zero. By symmetry, the minimum occurs at
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(N,0,\dots,0)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

For all
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
,
f
orthogonal to the constants, by (
2.19
)

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}W^{(\gamma)}f^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}-\langle f,P^{(\gamma)}f\rangle\ .

(4.8)

Applying Lemma
4.2
, Lemma
4.4

and Lemma
3.8
we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{1-\gamma}-\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right)\right]{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma-2}\left[\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)-\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right)\right]{\|}f{\|}_{2}^{2}\ .

This shows that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma,N}\geq\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma-2}\left[\left(\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)-\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N}\right)-\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\frac{2}{N^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma-2}\left[1-\frac{3}{(N-1)^{2}}\right]\ ,

which yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma,N}\geq\Delta_{\gamma,2}\prod_{j=3}^{N}\left(\frac{j}{j-1}\right)^{\gamma-1}\left(1-\frac{3}{(j-1)^{2}}\right)\geq\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^{1-\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma,2}\prod_{j=3}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{3}{(j-1)^{2}}\right)\ .

This proves (
2.15
) because
\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{3}{(j-1)^{2}}<\infty
.
∎

Clearly, the weak point in the argument leading to Lemma
2.6
is the use of the uniform lower bound on
W^{(\gamma)}
provided by Lemma
4.4
. The remedy to this is to show that at least for large
N
, reasonable trial functions
cannot concentrate much mass near the minimum of
W^{(\gamma)}
.

This is facilitated by modifying the jump-rate function
w^{(\gamma)}_{N}
, replacing it with a with a polynomial
m_{N}^{(\gamma)}
such that
w^{(\gamma)}_{N}\geq m_{N}^{(\gamma)}
everywhere. As we shall see, we get a sufficiently close fit using a quadratic polynomial.
We then employ a trial function decomposition to show, using the precise calculations that we can make for the polynomial rates, that reasonable trial functions
cannot concentrate much mass near the minimum of
W^{(\gamma)}
.
The following simple lemma, repeated from
[
7
]
, is the starting point.

4.5 LEMMA
.

For all
0<\gamma<1
and all
x>-1
,

(1+x)^{\gamma}\geq 1+\gamma x-(1-\gamma)x^{2}\ .

(4.9)

Proof.

Let
\eta:[-1,\infty)\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
be defined by

\eta(x):=(1+x)^{\gamma}-(1+\gamma x-(1-\gamma)x^{2})
.
Note that

\eta^{\prime\prime}(x)=(1-\gamma)(2-\gamma(1+x)^{\gamma-2})\ .

Thus,
\eta^{\prime\prime}(x)=0
has the single solution
x=x_{*}
where

x_{*}:=(\gamma/2)^{1/(2-\gamma)}-1
.
Note that
\eta
is convex on
(x_{*},\infty)
, and concave on
(-1,x_{*}]
, and also that

-1<x_{*}<0
.

Since
\eta
is concave on
[-1,x_{*}]
,

\min\{\eta(x)\ :\-1\leq x\leq x_{*}\ \}=\min\{\eta(-1),\eta(x_{*})\}=\eta(-1)=0\ .

Since
\eta
is convex on
(x_{*},\infty)
, and this interval contains a point, namely
0
,
at which
\eta^{\prime}
vanishes, the minimum of
\eta
over this interval is attained at
x=0
, and
thus
\eta
is non-negative on
(x_{*},\infty)
as well as on
[-1,x_{*}]
.
∎

Lemma
4.5
gives us the lower bound

w^{(\gamma)}_{N}(\eta)\geq m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta):=1+\gamma\left(\frac{1-\eta}{N-1}\right)-(1-\gamma)\left(\frac{1-\eta}{N-1}\right)^{2}\ .

(4.10)

Note that

m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta)=\left[1-(1-\gamma)\left(\frac{1-\eta}{N-1}\right)\right]\left[\left(\frac{1-\eta}{N-1}\right)+1\right]>0\ .

Then

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})[f-P_{k}f]^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}\right]:=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)\ .

(4.11)

The following notation will be convenient.

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{({\gamma})}(\boldsymbol{\eta}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})=1-\frac{1-\gamma}{(N-1)^{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\eta_{k}^{2}-1)\geq 1-\frac{1-\gamma}{N-1}\ ,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})P_{k}\ ,

(4.13)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f):=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\widetilde{W}^{({\gamma})}f^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}-\langle f,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}f\rangle\ .

(4.14)

4.6 Remark
.

Because of (
4.11
), any spectral gap bound for the Dirichlet form
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma,N}
serves as a spectral gap bound for the
Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma,N}
.

5
The trial function decomposition

We begin by specifying a trial function decomposition that we shall use. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
be the subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
specified in Definition
3.4
.
For any
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
orthogonal to the constants, define
p
and
h
to be the orthogonal projections of
f
onto
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{\perp}
respectively.
Then since
1\in{\mathcal{A}}_{N}^{\perp}
,
p
is orthogonal to the constants, and then
h=f-p
is orthogonal to the constants. The following lemma motivates this decomposition. Throughout this section we work with
\widetilde{W}^{({\gamma})}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
and
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
as defined in (
4.12
) and (
4.13
).

5.1 LEMMA
.

For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
the null space of
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
is the intersection of the null spaces of
P_{k}
for
k=1,\dots N
.
For all
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the closure of the range of
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
is the subspace

{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}

given in Definition
3.4
.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}\geq 0
,
f
belongs to the null space of
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
if and only if

\langle f,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}f\rangle=0
.
Because
m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})>0
almost everywhere

{0=\langle f,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}f\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})|P_{k}f|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}}\ ,

it must be the case that
|P_{k}f|^{2}
vanishes identically. Thus, if
f
is in the null space of
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
,
then
P_{k}f=0
for each
k
, and
f
is in the null space of

P^{(0)}
. Conversely if
f
is in the null space of
P^{(0)}
, then
P_{k}f=0
for each
k
, and then clearly
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}f=0
.

Since
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}\geq 0
, the closure of its range is the orthogonal complement of its null space. Thus the null space does not depend on
\gamma
, neither does the range. Evidently,
{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
is the closure of the range of
P^{(0)}
.
∎

Let
f=p+h
be the trial function decomposition described at the beginning of this section. By Lemma
5.1
,

h
is the component of
f
in the null space of
\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}
for each
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and hence

\langle f,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}f\rangle=\langle p,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}p\rangle\ .

(5.1)

Using this in (
4.14
) yields

\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\gamma,N}(f,f)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\widetilde{W}^{({\gamma})}f^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}-\langle p,\widetilde{P}^{({\gamma})}p\rangle\ .

(5.2)

Since
p\in{\mathcal{A}}_{N}
, it has a canonical decomposition of Lemma
3.5

p({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{j}(\eta_{j})\

(5.3)

where

\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}[\rho_{j}(\eta_{j})(1-\eta_{j})]=0\quad{\rm and\ for\ each}\ j,\quad{\mathbb{E}}[\rho_{j}(\eta_{j})]=0\ .

(5.4)

We make a further decomposition of
\rho_{j}(\eta_{j})
as follows:

5.2 DEFINITION
.

Let
p
be a function given by a sum of the form (
5.3
) subject to the constraints (
5.4
).
Expand each
\rho_{j}
in the orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of eigenfunctions of
K
.
Define

s:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\phi_{1},\rho_{j}\rangle\phi_{1}(\eta_{j})\quad{\rm and\ then}\quad g:=p-s=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\langle\phi_{n},\rho_{j}\rangle\phi_{n}(\eta_{j})\ .

(5.5)

To simplify notation we define

\alpha_{j}:=\langle\phi_{1},\rho_{j}\rangle\ ,\quad\psi_{j}(\eta_{j}):=\alpha_{j}\phi_{1}(\eta_{j})\quad{\rm and}\quad\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j}):=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\langle\phi_{n},\rho_{j}\rangle\phi_{n}(\eta_{j})\ .

(5.6)

The
trial function decomposition
of any
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
that is orthogonal to the constants is given by

f=p+h=s+g+h=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\phi_{1}(\eta_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\langle\phi_{n},\rho_{j}\rangle\phi_{n}(\eta_{j})+h=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}+h

(5.7)

where
p
is the component of
f
in the closure of the range of
P^{(\gamma)}
,
h
is the component of
f
in the null space of
P^{(0)}
, and

p=s+g
is the decomposition of
p
defined in (
5.5
).

The next lemma gives a crucial upper bound on
\langle g,P^{(\gamma)}g\rangle
. In proving it, we make use of Theorem
3.3

which has the consequence that for each
j
,

\displaystyle{\|}K\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}\leq\kappa_{2}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}=\frac{2}{N(N+1)}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}\ .

(5.8)

Had we not split off the
\phi_{1}
component of
\rho_{j}
, we would have had to use
\kappa_{1}
in place of
\kappa_{2}
in this estimate, which would degrade it by a factor of
N
. This is the reason for splitting
p=s+g
. Since
s
is affine, we can treat it separately by other means, as we shall see.

At this point is worth remarking that if the trial function
f
is symmetric under coordinate permutations, then
s=0
because
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}=0

and
\alpha_{j}
is independent of
j
. This was taken advantage of in
[

]
to “skip over” all of the estimates on
s
, and produce a sharper bound on the spectral gap for symmetric

, the case relevant in kinetic theory. Here we do not keep track of constants, but we do estimate the contribution form
s
so that there is no symmetry restriction in our spectral gap bound.

5.3 LEMMA
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
be orthogonal to the constants and let
f=s+g+h
be its trial function decomposition (
5.7
),
so that
g
has the form

g=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(\eta_{j})
. Then there is a constant
C
independent of
N
and

such that

\langle g,\widetilde{P}^{(\gamma)}g\rangle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})|\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}+\frac{C}{N^{2}}{\|}g{\|}_{2}^{2}\ .

(5.9)

Proof.

Since
P_{k}g=\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})+\sum_{j\colon j\neq k}K\varphi_{j}(\eta_{k})
,

\displaystyle\langle g,P^{(\gamma)}g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})\left(\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})+\sum_{j\colon j\neq k}K\varphi_{j}(\eta_{k})\right)^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}

(5.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})|\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})|^{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{N}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\colon j\neq k}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})K\varphi_{j}(\eta_{k}){\rm d}\sigma_{N}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\colon j\neq k}\sum_{\ell\colon\ell\neq k}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})K\varphi_{j}(\eta_{k})K\varphi_{\ell}(\eta_{k}){\rm d}\sigma_{N}

(5.11)

Using the pointwise bound
m_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})\leq w_{N}^{(\gamma)}(\eta_{k})\leq\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}
and (
5.8
), for
j\neq k
,

\left|\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\left(\frac{N-\eta_{k}}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\varphi_{k}(\eta_{k})K\varphi_{j}(\eta_{k}){\rm d}\sigma_{N}\right|\leq\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma}\kappa_{2}{\|}\varphi_{j}{\|}_{2}{\|}\varphi_{k}{\|}_{2}\ .

Therefore, the expression in line (

) is bounded above by
\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{\gamma-1}\kappa_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\|}\varphi_{k}{\|}_{2}^{2}
, and then by (
3.7
) and Theorem

, this is bounded above by
\frac{C}{N^{2}}{\|}g{\|}_{2}^{2}
. In the same way, one shows that the sum of the integrals
in (
5.11
) is bounded above by
\frac{C}{N^{3}}{\|}g{\|}_{2}^{2}
.
∎

The components of
s
, which lie in the eigenspace of
K
with eigenvalue
\kappa_{1}=-\frac{1}{N-1}
, are more correlated with one another than the components of
g
, but

has other nice properties given in the next two lemmas.

5.4 LEMMA
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
be orthogonal to the constant and let
f=s+g+h
be its trial function decomposition (
5.7
), so that
s
has the form

s=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\phi_{1}(\eta_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}(\eta_{j})
with
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}=0
.
Then for each
k

P_{k}s=\frac{N}{N-1}\alpha_{k}\phi_{1}(\eta_{k})=\frac{N}{N-1}\psi_{k}(\eta_{k})\ .

(5.12)

Proof.

Recall from Definition
3.2

that for
1\leq k\leq N
,
\pi_{k}:\mathcal{S}_{N}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
is defined by

\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\eta_{k}
. For
j\neq k
,
P_{k}(\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{j})=-\frac{1}{N-1}\phi_{1}\circ\pi_{k}
. Therefore,

P_{k}s=\alpha_{k}\phi_{1}(\eta_{k})-\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{N-1}\alpha_{j}\phi_{1}(\eta_{k})=\frac{N}{N-1}\alpha_{k}\phi_{1}(\eta_{k})\

where in the last equality we have used
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}=0
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
5.4
, we obtain the analog of (
5.9
), as an identity instead of as an inequality:

7
A broader class of models

The fact that with
T_{N}
given by (
3.1
), the push-forward of
\nu_{N}\otimes\sigma_{N-1}
under
T_{N}
, is
\sigma_{N}
underlies all of the
chaoticity bounds derived in Section
3
. This is true not only for the flat Dirichlet measure, but for a broader class of probability measures on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}

that we now determine.

The flat Dirichlet measure on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
arises by equipping
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
with the probability measure
\prod_{j=1}^{N}f(\eta_{j}){\rm d\eta_{j}}
and then conditioning on

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}=NE
for the choice
f(\eta)=e^{-\eta}
.

Now, let
f
be any continuously differentiable and strictly positive probability density on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}
. For
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
, define
F({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}f(\eta_{j})
which is a
probability density on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
. Let
\nu_{f,N}
denote the corresponding probability measure on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
.

For
E>0
, define

\sigma_{f,N,E}
to be the conditional distribution of
\nu_{f,N}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
. That is, for continuous
\varphi
on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{f,N}}\left\{\varphi({\boldsymbol{\eta}})\ \Bigg|\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}=E\right\}=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{\eta}}){\rm d}\sigma_{f,N,E}\ .

If
E=\int_{{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}}\eta f(\eta){\rm d}\eta
, the mean of
f
, then the probability measure is very concentrated near
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
; various precise formulations are known under the rubric of “equivalence of ensembles”. Because of this,
\sigma_{f,N,E}
is approximately a product distribution, and the various coordinate functions
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
are approximately independent random variables under
\sigma_{f,N,E}
.

For the symmetric Dirichlet distributions, and in particular, the flat Dirichlet distribution, this near independence is valid for all values of
E
, not only the mean of
f
, because of a scaling property that characterizes the Gamma distributions, from which they arise.

The different “slices”
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
of
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
, all simplexes, are related by scaling: For
t>0
define
S_{t}:{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{N}
by

S_{t}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})=t^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
Then
S_{t}
is invertible from
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
onto
\mathcal{S}_{N,tE}
. Let
S_{t}\#\sigma_{N,E}
denote the push-forward of
\sigma_{N,E}
under
S_{t}
. That is, for
\varphi
on
\mathcal{S}_{N,tE}
.

\int_{\mathcal{S}_{N,tE}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{\eta}}){\rm d}S_{t}\#\sigma_{f,N,E}=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}}\varphi(S_{t}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})){\rm d}\sigma_{f,N,E}=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}}\varphi(t^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}){\rm d}\sigma_{f,N,E}\ .

Recall that the Gamma distribution with parameters
\alpha,\lambda>0
is the probability on
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}
with density
f_{\alpha,\lambda}(x):=\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x}
. The following theorem gives a characterization of the Gamma distribution that is relevant to the considerations of this paper.

7.1 THEOREM
.

For all
t>0
,
S_{t}\#\sigma_{f,N,E}=\sigma_{f,N,tE}
if and only if
f
is a Gamma density.

Proof.

It is clear that
S_{t}\#\sigma_{f,N,E}=\sigma_{f,N,tE}
for all
t>0
if and only if for all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
,

\frac{F(t{\boldsymbol{\eta}})}{F({t\boldsymbol{\xi}})}=\frac{F({\boldsymbol{\eta}})}{F({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}

for all
t>0
. Define
h=\log f
. Then we can rewrite this as

{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}h(t\eta_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}h(t\xi_{j})=C}

for some constant
C
. Differentiating in
t
, and then setting
t=1
,

{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\eta_{j}h^{\prime}(\eta_{j})-\xi_{j}h^{\prime}(\xi_{j}))=0}
.
Define
\varphi(\eta)=\eta h^{\prime}(\eta)
. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varphi(\eta_{j})
is constant on
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,E}
. By the Lemma
7.2
, this means that for some constant
a,b
,
\varphi(\eta)=a\eta+b
.

Therefore, for some constants
a,b
,
h^{\prime}(\eta)=a+\frac{b}{\eta}
. It follows that for some
c\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
,

h(\eta)=a\eta+b\log\eta+c\ .

This is the general form of the logarithm of a Gamma density, and hence
f
is a Gamma density.
∎
