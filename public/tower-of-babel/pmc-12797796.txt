Intercellular communication from myotubes to fibroblasts is differentially impacted by mineralocorticoid receptor signaling and muscular dystrophy
Authors: Swathy Krishna, Chetan K. Gomatam, Reva Reddy, Jeovanna Lowe, Christoph Lepper, Jill A. Rafael-Fortney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReplacement of striated muscle with fibrosis leads to the symptoms and fatality in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). Transient local production of the mineralocorticoid aldosterone in the skeletal muscle microenvironment enhances wound healing. However, persistent aldosterone signaling through the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) in multiple cell types within skeletal muscles exacerbates damage and fibrosis in DMD. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists demonstrate therapeutic benefits by preventing aldosterone binding to MR and downstream transcriptional activation. Therefore, defining aldosterone-mediated intercellular crosstalk in health and disease is highly relevant.MethodsTo delineate MR-regulated signaling mechanisms from myotubes to fibroblasts in health and disease, we treated cultured wild-type and dystrophin-deficient mdx (DMD model) myotubes with aldosterone or vehicle and used myotube conditioned media to treat neonatal fibroblasts isolated from the corresponding skeletal muscles. We performed: RNA sequencing of myotubes and conditioned media-treated fibroblasts, ligand-receptor predictions, unbiased cytokine profiling and fibroblast functional assays including proliferation, migration and matrix production to gain a systems-level understanding of intercellular communication from myotubes to fibroblasts.ResultsConditioned media from aldosterone-treated wild-type myotubes induced numerous transcriptional changes in wild-type fibroblasts, supporting MR-regulated cell communication from myotubes to fibroblasts. Mdx myotubes and conditioned media-treated fibroblasts showed an altered transcriptional profile compared to wild-type, supporting differences in both cell types due to intrinsic dystrophin deficiency even prior to the onset of pathology. Local aldosterone synthase prior to disease onset led to reduced differential expression in mdx fibroblasts from aldosterone-treated myotube signaling. Distinct ligand/receptor combinations influenced by MR-signaling and dystrophin deficiency in myotube to fibroblast signaling were identified. While signaling from aldosterone-treated wild-type myotubes enhanced fibroblast functional properties including proliferation and matrix production, signaling from dystrophic myotubes resulted in reduced migration and matrix production in dystrophic fibroblasts.ConclusionsThis study provides new mechanistic insight along the cell communication axis demonstrating that myotubes regulate fibroblast properties and this intercellular signaling is modified differentially by aldosterone and disease. These findings have implications for understanding skeletal muscle health and disease and improving wound healing and chronic fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02515-2.

Introduction

Fibrosis is the aberrant, excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components in response to injury or pathology [1–3]. In skeletal muscle, fibrosis is associated with reduced function, impaired regeneration, and eventually, increased cell death. Fibrosis is an underlying complication in myopathies, severe muscle injuries, and age-related loss of muscle tissue. In chronic striated muscle pathologies like Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), repetitive degeneration and regeneration lead to further exacerbation of the chronic fibrosis and overall pathology [4]. The primary cause of DMD, a progressive striated muscle degenerative disease, is the absence of a functional subsarcolemmal dystrophin protein that results in the death of affected individuals due to severe cardio-respiratory complications [4]. Dystrophin is part of the dystrophin glycoprotein complex that connects the basal lamina to the cytoskeleton, and its absence causes contraction-induced muscle damage. The result of the damage is loss of muscle tissue, persistent inflammation, and fibrosis, which eventually affects muscle function.

Although glucocorticoids, the most prevalent therapeutic option for DMD, prolong ambulation, they are associated with a wide range of side effects, including obesity and osteoporosis [5]. Despite the current promise of gene therapy for treating DMD by delivering a short version of dystrophin, the long-term consequences and many mechanistic questions still need to be evaluated [6]. Alternative therapeutic targets that reduce skeletal muscle fibrosis will likely be required for an optimal therapeutic strategy to treat DMD.

We have previously established the therapeutic efficacy of mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) antagonists (MRAs) on reducing fibrosis and improving functional outcomes in dystrophic cardiomyopathy as well as skeletal muscle pathology in DMD mouse models [7–10]. The cardiac efficacy has already been translated to DMD patients in 2 clinical trials and MRAs have become a recommended treatment for DMD cardiomyopathy [11–14]. MRAs prevent the binding of the endogenous mineralocorticoid aldosterone (Aldo) to MR, thus preventing the nuclear translocation of MR and transcription of pro-fibrotic genes. Previous investigations into the role of Aldo in wound healing versus DMD models identified that the transient presence of Aldo assists wound healing, but prolonged exposure exacerbates pathology in dystrophic muscles [15, 16]. Additionally, the cell-specific ablation of MR in myofibers and myeloid immune cells demonstrated contrasting physiological outcomes of reduced fibrosis versus increased fibrosis, respectively, suggesting intercellular signaling between the various cell types present in the dystrophic skeletal muscle microenvironment [17–19].

Maintenance of structural integrity and tissue architecture in skeletal muscle is complemented by fibroblasts through the secretion of ECM components. However, the potential of fibroblasts for ECM remodeling is influenced by the intercellular signaling in the tissue microenvironment [2]. This complex and intricate cellular crosstalk between various cell types, such as myofibers, immune cells, and fibroblasts, contributes to cellular homeostasis in muscle growth and repair. The complementary nature of crosstalk between the muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) and fibroblasts in repair and regeneration has been well explored [20, 21]. Fibroblasts were found to rapidly expand proximal to MuSCs during tissue regeneration post-injury, and their depletion impaired MuSC expansion [20]. There have also been reports of how damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from myofibers following muscle injury initiate the recruitment of immune cells [22, 23]. However, how signaling from myofibers influences fibroblast function in wound healing and disease has not been explored. Considering that myofibers are the most prominent cell type in muscle tissue and the initial responders following an injury, understanding how myofibers influence fibroblast gene expression and function is highly therapeutically relevant. Modulation of signaling to fibroblasts could influence their activity and lead to scarless wound healing in chronic muscle pathologies.

The presence of MR in various cell types present in the muscle microenvironment, such as fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and myofibers, is well established [24–26]. Thus, the presence of Aldo in the muscle microenvironment can have a direct as well as indirect effect on fibroblast gene expression and function. The effect of direct Aldo on skeletal muscle fibroblast function has recently been explored (Gomatam, Rafael-Fortney, unpublished observations).

The objective of this study is to dissect how Aldo-mediated signaling from myofibers impacts fibroblast gene expression and function to begin to understand how these cells communicate differentially in wound healing versus dystrophic skeletal muscle microenvironments. To delineate this intercellular communication, we utilized cultured multinucleated myotubes differentiated from isolated primary mouse myogenic progenitor cells, and fibroblasts isolated from skeletal muscle following adherence purification. We hypothesized that Aldo would induce distinct signaling between wild-type and dystrophin-deficient mdx differentiated myotubes, and the resulting secreted factors would contribute to modified fibroblast gene expression and function.

Materials and methods

Isolation of primary myogenic progenitor cells and fibroblasts

Myogenic progenitor cells and fibroblasts were isolated from less than 5-day-old C57BL/10 (wild-type) and mdx (DMD model) pups. Mice were maintained following the guidelines of IACUC at the Ohio State University. The hindlimb skeletal muscles collected were minced and digested using buffer containing 0.1% collagenase type II (C6885-500MG, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1% BSA (BP1605-100, Fisher Scientific), and 0.1% DNase I (RQ1, Promega) by incubating at 37 °C for 30 min and triturating every 10 min. After the incubation, the cell suspension was filtered using a 70 µm filter (22–363–548, Fisher Scientific) followed by 40 µm filter (22–363–547, Fisher Scientific). To separate the fibroblasts from the cell suspension, the cells were pre-plated for 2 h, and the fibroblasts adhered to the plate [20]. The fibroblast-deficient supernatant from pre-plating was collected and plated on 1% gelatin-coated (G1393-100ML, Sigma-Aldrich) plates to select for myoblasts. Fibroblasts were maintained in media containing DMEM (11,995–065, ThermoFisher Scientific), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, S11150, R&D Systems), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Pen/Strep, 15,140–122, Gibco™). Myoblasts were grown and expanded in the plates for 5 days post-isolation and were grown in F10 Ham media (N6013-500ML, Sigma-Aldrich) with 15% horse serum (26,050–088, Gibco™), 1% Pen/Strep, 1.2 mM CaCl2, and 1 mM L-glutamine (25,030, Gibco™) supplemented with 6 ng/mL bFGF (F0291-25UG, Sigma-Aldrich) for the first week and then with 2 ng/mL bFGF to maintain a proliferative state. To initiate differentiation of the myoblasts and fusion into multinucleated myotubes, cells were incubated in media containing F10 Ham with 5% horse serum, 1% Pen/Strep, 1.2 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM glutamine (no bFGF) for 24 h. The differentiating myotubes were maintained in differentiation media, F10 Ham with 10% horse serum, 1% Pen/Strep, 1.2 mM CaCl2, and 1 mM L-glutamine. Before treating the cells with aldosterone (A9477-5MG, Sigma-Aldrich) or CM, the cells were incubated in media containing charcoal-stripped horse serum, so that endogenous steroidal compounds would not interfere with the treatment.

For the CM treatment, 10 days after initiation of differentiation, the differentiated myotubes were treated with either aldosterone (Aldo, 1 μM) or DMSO to a final concentration of 0.001% v/v (vehicle/Veh, D2650-100ML, Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h. The plates were rinsed with dPBS without calcium and magnesium (14,190–144, Gibco™) after 48 h, and the myotubes were incubated in fresh media for 15 h. This media (conditioned media, CM) was then used for treating the fibroblasts. The collected CM was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, followed by a filtration using a 0.22 μm filter to ensure no cells are transferred along with the media. The fibroblasts were also acclimated in media containing charcoal-stripped horse serum and were then treated with the CM from myotubes. Since we used primary cell cultures for our experiments, all the cells in this study were used at passage two, to minimize the possible presence of other cell types that would not survive through passaging and to balance this goal with also retaining the most in vivo-like phenotypes. Representative images of the myotubes and fibroblasts used for the CM experiments are in Additional file 1. Additionally, the expression levels of common fibroblast markers [2, 20] such as Col1a1, Pdgfra, Hic1, Cd90/Thy1, Tcf4, and Fsp1/S100A4 (represented as transcripts per million, tpm) are shown in Additional file 1.

RNA sequencing

Aldo and Veh-treated myotubes and CM-treated fibroblasts from n = 3 biological replicates of each C57 and mdx neonatal mice were used for RNA sequencing. The myotubes (following the collection of CM) and fibroblasts (following 24 h CM treatment) were directly lysed by adding 100 μL TRIzol (15,596,026, Invitrogen) for each cm2 of surface area of the culture plate. RNA was isolated using the Directzol™ RNA microprep kit (R2060, Zymo Research) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an equal volume of 100% ethanol was added to the lysed samples and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was transferred to a collection tube containing a Zymo-Spin column and centrifuged at 13000G for 30 s. The flow-through was discarded, columns washed and then treated with DNase I for 15 min at room temperature. RNA was eluted from the column in DNase/RNase-free water by centrifuging at 13000G.

mRNA library preparation (polyA enrichment) and sequencing were performed by Novogene. Alignment was performed using HISAT2 and the reference genome used was mm9 (NCBI RefSeq assembly: GCF_000001635.18). Differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using DESeq2 in NovoMagic with significance established at |fold change|> 1.5 and padj < 0.1. Gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) using the counts was performed on GSEA 4.3.2 using the default parameters [27, 28]. normalized enrichment score, NES|> 1.6 and FDR q-val < 0.1 was used as the significant enrichment cutoff for GSEA. To identify the dystrophin transcripts, transcript analysis on the fibroblast sequencing data was performed using Kallisto. The RNA sequencing data generated and used in this study are deposited in the NIH NCBI GEO GSE305235.

For the cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots, log2 fold changes from the DE analyses were used. The matrisome genes and categorization were obtained from MatrisomeDB 2.0 [29]. Matrisome genes were subsetted from the RNA sequencing results, and the CDF plots were prepared in R.

Ligand-receptor prediction based on the gene expression from myotubes (sender/central cell) and fibroblasts (receiver/partner) was performed using an R package, ICELLNET [30], which generated the predictions using bulk RNA sequencing data. A chord diagram, created using the R package circlize [31] and representing all the identified interactions is shown in Additional file 3. A threshold of communication score = 40 was set based on the score density plot (Additional file 3).

Proteome profiler

Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array (ARY028, R&D Systems) was used for the detection of secreted factors from the myotubes present in the CM based on the manufacturer’s instructions. The CM collected following 15 h of incubation after 48 h of Aldo or Veh treatment of myotubes to allow translation and secretion from upregulated genes was stored at −80 °C, then was thawed, and 1X Halt™ protease phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78,440, Thermo Scientific™) was added before pooling replicates for each condition. Briefly, the membranes were blocked and incubated overnight with CM (n = 4 biological replicates combined for each treatment group). Following overnight incubation, membranes were washed in the array buffer and incubated with the detection antibody cocktail. The membranes were then incubated in streptavidin-HRP followed by Chemi-Reagent mix incubation for detection with ECL. The membranes were imaged using the Chemidoc MP Imaging system (Bio-Rad) at exposures ranging from low to high. Representative images at multiple exposures to visualize the changes in intensity are demonstrated in Additional file 3. The dots in the images were quantified using Image Lab 6.1 (Bio-Rad). All the values were selected with an intensity higher than 9000 to ensure that the spots are visible. All proteins with more than 10% intensity differences were considered as differentially expressed proteins.

For the heatmaps, plotting of normalized pixel intensity of spots with more than 10% change was performed using the R [32] package pheatmap [33]. Normalization was performed across rows by calculating the z-score by subtracting the mean and then dividing by the standard deviation. UpsetR was used for creating the upset plot [34]. Additional R packages used for analysis include tidyverse [35] and ggplot2 [36].

Western blotting

For protein extraction, cells were collected following trypsinization of the plates (2 mL of Trypsin/EDTA (5428C-500ML, Sigma-Aldrich) for 100 mm plates) by centrifuging at 1000 rpm for 10 min and discarding the media. Newcastle buffer (75 mM Tris pH 6.8, 3.8% SDS, 4 M urea, 20% glycerol with protease inhibitors 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM benzamidine, 0.5 μg leupeptin/mL, 0.2 units aprotinin/mL) was added to the cells. The cells with buffer were vortexed, and the suspension was passed through 21G needles to shear the DNA, followed by centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein was quantified using the DC protein assay (Biorad). For western blotting, 15 μg of protein was loaded in 15-well 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN precast polyacrylamide gels (456–1096, Bio-Rad). For separation, gels were run at 80 V for 30 min, then 120 V for 90 min at room temp. Nitrocellulose membranes were used for transfer at 100 V for 40 min. Ponceau S staining was performed and quantified to confirm equal loading of protein. Blocking with 5% milk in tris buffered saline with tween 20 (TBST) for 30 min (if monoclonal antibodies) or 1 h (if polyclonal antibodies) was performed. Primary antibodies were incubated for 2 h (unless mentioned otherwise), followed by TBST washes. For secondary incubation (1 h at room temp), horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated goat anti-rabbit (111–035–144, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs) against rabbit primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (115–035–146, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs) against mouse primary antibodies were used. Detection was performed with the ECL2 kit (80,196, Pierce Biotechnology) and imaged using the Chemidoc MP Imaging system (Bio-Rad). The blots were quantified using the Bio-Rad ImageLab software. EdU proliferation assay

Fibroblasts were isolated from three individual mice per genotype, and three replicates were plated from each biological replicate for each condition. Fibroblasts (approximately 10,000 cells per well) were seeded in 4-well chamber slides and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h in media containing myotube differentiation media with charcoal-stripped horse serum. CM collected following 15 h of fresh media incubation of the myotubes (C57 and mdx) after 48 h of Veh or Aldo treatment, was used for 24 h of incubation of the fibroblasts following which EdU assay was performed. Fibroblasts were incubated in media containing 10 μM EdU (Click-iT™ cell proliferation kit for imaging, C10340, Invitrogen) for 2 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (P6148-500G, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by 2X washes with dPBS (5 min each).

For staining of α-smooth muscle actin and detection of incorporated EdU, fibroblasts were permeabilized at room temperature with 0.5% TritonX (1,610,407, BioRad) in dPBS. The slides were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (KPBS) and blocked with 1% normal goat serum (NGS, S-1000–20, VectorLabs). The slides were then incubated in primary antibody (rabbit monoclonal α-smooth muscle actin, 1:500, Abcam, ab124964) for 2 h at room temperature in a humid chamber. The slides were then incubated with secondary antibody, goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (1:200, A-21429, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temp in the dark. EdU detection followed the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously described (Gomatam, Rafael-Fortney, unpublished observations). Briefly, sulfo-cyanine 5 azide, autoclaved Millipore water, 10X buffer, copper sulphate, and sodium ascorbate were sequentially added, and the slides were incubated for 30 min in the dark. The slides were cover-slipped with Vectashield (H-1000, Vector Laboratories) containing DAPI (1 μg/mL, D9564-10MG, Sigma-Aldrich) and stored at −20 °C until imaged.

The slides were imaged with a Nikon NiE microscope (Tokyo, Japan) with a Teledyne Moment CMOS camera using NiS Elements software. For quantification of EdU, more than 150 nuclei per sample were considered, and imaging was done using a 20X objective. The average of the three replicates per mouse was used for statistical analysis. Quantification of EdU and DAPI was performed using NiS Elements by setting up a threshold and performing the automated measurement. After obtaining the numbers of total nuclei and EdU-positive nuclei, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.4.0 software.

Migration assay

Fibroblasts were seeded in 12-well plates and grown to approximately 80% confluency. These cells were incubated in myotube differentiation media with charcoal-stripped horse serum for 48 h before the CM treatment. In order to restrict the interference of cell proliferation on the assessment of cell migration, the cells were maintained in charcoal-stripped differentiation media with Aphidicolin (178,273, Sigma-Aldrich) at 0.2 ng/mL [37–39]. In vitro scratch assay [40] was performed by making a scratch with a 200 μL pipette tip on the wells at 15 h of CM pre-treatment. The scratch was imaged using a BioTek Cytation1 Imaging Reader at 15 h (scratch), 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 84 h following CM-treatment. Quantification for the whole scratch at various time points was performed in ImageJ using the stitched image created in BioTek Cytation1 Imaging Reader. Immunohistochemistry

Whole hindlimbs from 2-day-old C57 (n = 7) and mdx (n = 7) mice pups were dissected, and following removal of the skin, were embedded in optimal cutting compound, frozen, and sectioned at 8 μm thickness. Immunohistochemistry of Cyp11b2 was performed as described previously [15]. Briefly, sections were incubated in Cyp11b2 (1:250, AS2084, rabbit anti-mouse) overnight at 4 °C, and then in HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:200, 111–035–144, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by detection with ImmPACT DAB (SK-4105, Vector). Slides were cover slipped with Vectamount (H-5501, Vector Laboratories) and imaged using a Nikon NiE microscope (Tokyo, Japan) with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera using the NiS Elements software. Quantification was performed using NiS Elements software on the imaged whole hindlimb sections.

Statistics

Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test in GraphPad Prism, and the outliers identified were excluded before performing the appropriate statistical analyses. For the functional assays, two-way ANOVA and repeated measures ANOVA were performed in GraphPad Prism based on the experimental design, and significance was established at p < 0.05. For the CDF plots, the W-statistic and p-values were calculated using the wilcox.test function in R.

Results

Conditioned media from aldosterone-treated myotubes induced transcriptomic profiles in fibroblasts that differ with treatment and dystrophin deficiency

To determine whether MR signaling in differentiated muscle cells indirectly affects skeletal muscle fibroblasts, we performed a conditioned media (CM) transfer experiment from cultured myotubes to fibroblasts (Fig. 1A). Myoblasts and fibroblasts were isolated from limb skeletal muscles from neonatal C57BL/10 wild-type (C57) mice and from the dystrophin-deficient mdx genotypic mouse model of DMD (n = 3 biological replicates each). Fibroblasts were isolated by substrate adherence and have been previously shown to be equivalent to the cell type also called fibroadipogenic progenitors (FAPs) from skeletal muscle [41]. C57 and mdx myoblasts were differentiated into multinucleated myotubes (Fig. 1 A, Additional file 1) for 10 days and then treated with Aldo (1 μM) or vehicle (Veh) for 48 h in media containing charcoal-stripped horse serum to remove endogenous steroids. Aldo- or Veh-containing media was removed from the myotubes to avoid direct transfer of Aldo and replaced with differentiation media with charcoal-stripped horse serum that was collected after 15 h. This CM, containing secretions from C57 or mdx myotubes was then transferred to neonatal skeletal muscle fibroblasts isolated from the same mouse genotype (Additional file 1), and fibroblasts were collected for RNA isolation after 24 h of incubation. Myotubes were also collected for RNA isolation after removal of CM. RNA sequencing was then performed on myotubes and CM-treated fibroblasts. The purity of fibroblast cultures was also established by confirming the gene expression levels (transcripts per million (tpm)) of common fibroblast markers such as Col1a1, Pdgfra, Hic1, Cd90/Thy1, Tcf4, and Fsp1/S100A4 (Additional file 1) [2, 20]. Direct Aldo treatment for 48 h induced changes in transcriptomic profiles of both wild-type (C57) and mdx myotubes, and treatment with CM from these myotubes induced corresponding effects in fibroblasts (Fig. 1 B). Differential expression (DE) analyses revealed 95 genes (50 downregulated, 45 upregulated) with significantly changed expression levels (padj < 0.1, |fold change|> 1.5) in C57 myotubes with direct Aldo treatment (C57 Aldo versus C57 Veh) (Fig. 1 B). In response to CM-treatment from Aldo-treated C57 myotubes, 74 genes were significantly downregulated, and 61 genes were significantly upregulated (padj < 0.1, |fold change|> 1.5) in C57 fibroblasts (Fig. 1 B).

Dystrophin deficiency itself in mdx myotubes caused downregulation of 212 genes and upregulation of 302 genes (padj < 0.1, |fold change|> 1.5) compared to C57 myotubes. Corresponding to these changes, 166 genes were downregulated and 91 genes were upregulated (padj < 0.1, |fold change|> 1.5) in Veh CM-treated mdx compared to Veh CM-treated C57 fibroblasts, suggesting a substantial effect of dystrophin deficiency on myotube-fibroblast communication (Fig. 1 B).

Surprisingly, direct treatment with Aldo compared to Veh caused DE of very few genes (4 downregulated and 9 upregulated) in mdx myotubes (Fig. 1 B). Subsequently, mdx myotube CM from Aldo- compared to Veh-treatment led to no DE genes (DEGs) in the mdx fibroblasts (Fig. 1 B). Since it has been repeatedly demonstrated that muscle damage is not present in mdx mice at the neonatal stage from which the myoblasts and fibroblasts were isolated and does not start until approximately 3 weeks-of-age, any damage-associated priming of cells was not anticipated [42–44]. The observation of 9 Aldo-driven DEGs in mdx compared to 95 in C57 myotubes suggested the presence of Aldo signaling in the mdx muscle microenvironment even prior to the initiation of overt muscle damage. To test the activation of early signaling, immunohistochemistry of aldosterone synthase (Cyp11b2), previously shown to correlate with the presence of Aldo in muscle, [16] was performed on neonatal hindlimbs (Fig. 1 C). Increased Cyp11b2 in neonatal (2-day-old) mdx hindlimb (n = 7 each, C57 and mdx, p < 0.05) suggested early priming of myotubes and fibroblasts in the dystrophic muscle microenvironment. Based on DE, more transcriptional changes were observed in genotypic comparisons (mdx versus C57) compared to those of Aldo versus Veh treatment.

Aldh1a1, Gpx3, Dlk1, Nr1d1, Fos, Map2, Cdh13, Egr1, Gpx3, S100a8, Lcn2, and Map2 are among the upregulated DE genes in Aldo versus Veh-treated C57 normal mouse myotubes (Fig. 2 A, padj < 0.1, |fold change|> 1.5), of which Aldh1a1, Gpx3, and Map2 were also upregulated in Aldo-treated normal human myotubes as observed previously [25]. Dystrophin deficiency and aldosterone signaling induce unique ligand/receptor combinations in myotube to fibroblast signaling

The DE in myotubes and the corresponding gene expression changes with CM-treatment in fibroblasts suggested intercellular communication. Since ligands, including cytokines and chemokines, released in the microenvironment influence gene expression by binding to receptors on other cell types, we performed a ligand-receptor prediction using ICELLNET. ICELLNET was developed to predict intercellular communication from bulk RNA sequencing data, in addition to single-cell transcriptomic data [30]. Additionally, ICELLNET uses an expert-curated ligand-receptor database with 1669 interactions that are grouped into various biologically relevant signaling families [30, 50]. For this analysis, myotubes were designated as sender cells and fibroblasts as receiver cells, to predict combinations of plausible ligand-receptor interactions based on their gene expression levels. The interactions are predicted based on the comprehensive ligand-receptor interactions database by calculating a communication score that quantifies potential signaling between the sender and receiver.

A chord diagram representing all the predicted ligand-receptor interactions is in Additional file 3. Among the various overall interactions in all the treatment groups considered, WNT9A, WNT7B, BMP8A, BMP2, and ITGB1 + ITGA2, ITGB1 + ITGA11, ITGB1 + ITGA10 have higher communication scores, suggesting these to be the major communicators from myotubes to fibroblasts. When discerning the treatment effects, in C57 Veh treatment, the interactions with the highest communication scores were GDF11/TGFBR1 + ACVR2A (score = 90.99), GDF11/TGFBR1 + ACVR2B (score = 90.99), JAG2/NOTCH2 (score = 89.70), PDGFA/PDGFRA (score = 89.31) and SOST/LRP5 (score = 87.76). In C57 Aldo treatment, the most prominent interactions were VWF/TNFRSF11B (score = 90.42), VEGFB/NRP1 (score = 88.48), CADM1/NECTIN3 (score = 87.69), IGF2/IGF1R (score = 83.89) and LAMA2/ADGRG6 (score = 83.65). The interactions with the highest communication scores in mdx Veh are PDGFA/PDGFRA (score = 72.29), RELN/LRP8 (score = 72.04), PDGFD/PDGFRB (score = 71.78), COL7A1/ITGB1 + ITGA10 (score = 66.06), and COL7A1/ITGB1 + ITGA11 (score = 66.06). DSCAM/PTPRM (score = 76.93), PDGFD/PDGFRB (score = 75.03), WNT9A/FZD5 + LRP5 (score = 64.92), WNT9A/FZD4 + LRP5 (score = 63.31), and PDGFA/PDGFRA (score = 60.94) are the predicted interactions with the highest communication scores in mdx Aldo treatment group. Most of these top ligands and receptors, such as GDF11 (BMP11), PDGFRA, VEGF, WNT, NOTCH2, and collagens, have well-established roles in the regulation of fibroblast function [2].

Distinct overall effects on matrisome gene expression are present in myotubes compared to fibroblasts

Since relying on only gene ontology (GO) categories may not allow comprehensive analysis of ECM components, we used MatrisomeDB for a more in-depth examination of the contributions of myotubes and fibroblasts specifically on ECM and ECM-associated genes [29, 55]. MatrisomeDB is an expert-curated proteomic resource based on 17 studies (ECM data of healthy and diseased tissues) with ECM proteins categorized as core matrisome and matrisome-associated proteins, which contribute to the deposition and remodeling of ECM [29]. The core matrisome had categories such as collagens, ECM glycoproteins, and proteoglycans, which assemble into the ECM, while the matrisome-associated proteins constitute categories such as secreted proteins, ECM regulators, and ECM-affiliated categories that are related to more functional aspects of ECM remodeling.

To decipher the overall effect of fibroblasts and myotubes on matrisome gene expression, the mouse matrisome gene list obtained from MatrisomeDB [29] was subsetted from the RNA sequencing datasets from myotubes and CM-treated fibroblasts and categorized. For a protein level validation, we then performed western blots of some of the matrisome proteins which were also considered to be highly expressed by myofibroblasts [2, 56] including fibronectin (Fn1), lysyl oxidase like 1 (Loxl1), collagen 1 (Col1), lysyl oxidase (Lox), and periostin (Postn), and to validate the matrisome gene expression in myotubes and fibroblasts (Fig. 7 D). Among the tested proteins, Col1 was decreased in CM-treated mdx fibroblasts (p < 0.05) compared to CM-treated wild-type, and Loxl1 (glycosylated, 50 kDa) had a significant disease main effect (p < 0.05) showing decreased expression with mdx genotype compared to C57, further validating the reduction of ECM production from mdx fibroblasts. Postn demonstrated differences between C57 and mdx only in myotubes, with increased levels in mdx compared to C57 (p < 0.05), corroborating the MatrisomeDB gene expression analysis.

Discussion

The process of tissue repair following an injury is marked by a synchronized sequence of events involving various cell types present in the tissue microenvironment. In normal tissue repair, following an injury, fibroblasts are activated, leading to increased proliferation, migration, ECM production, and eventually apoptotic clearance [2, 57]. However, in chronic myopathies like DMD, ECM components accumulate, damaging tissue function and eventually leading to loss of tissue, further exacerbating the pathology. In chronic fibrosis, the synchronized events of wound healing are likely disrupted at multiple points, but understanding disruptions due to intercellular signaling within the muscle microenvironment remains underexplored.

We have previously shown that the MR agonist hormone Aldo is produced locally during muscle injury for a short period of time during normal wound healing and chronically during muscular dystrophy [15, 16]. In the current study, we identified that Aldo treatment of C57 myotubes led to secreted factors that altered C57 fibroblast gene expression, leading to activation of processes related to fibroblast proliferation. In contrast, dystrophin deficiency in myotubes led to a dramatically suppressed response to Aldo and fewer alterations in signaling to fibroblasts.

These gene expression changes translated into functional changes, demonstrating that signaling from myotubes following Aldo treatment increased proliferation in C57 but not in mdx. Migration was also the highest in C57 fibroblasts treated with Aldo-CM and was suppressed in CM-treated mdx fibroblasts compared to C57. There were no differences in proliferation or migration between Aldo and Veh CM-treated mdx fibroblasts, further supporting the lack of Aldo-regulated myotube signaling of gene expression changes in these fibroblasts. Interestingly, we observed the presence of the myofibroblast marker α-SMA in all the fibroblasts regardless of treatment with Aldo or Veh CM from myotubes and despite their proliferation status. However, increased α-SMA protein levels in Aldo CM-treated mdx fibroblasts suggest that these cells are already primed to be myofibroblasts with stress fibers and require only minimal signaling from the myotubes. This may also be justified by the decreased overall expression of matrisome genes as observed in the CDF plots for mdx Veh versus C57 Veh (disease comparison) as opposed to C57 Aldo vs C57 Veh.

Persistence of myofibroblasts in many diseases is also associated with low ECM production. The overall production of core matrisome and matrisome-associated components, as represented by CDF plots, suggests the mdx versus C57 comparison to have a lower overall effect and more downregulated matrisome genes compared to that of the C57 Aldo versus C57 Veh comparison. Additionally, a weaker communication score for the mdx groups further corroborates the finding that mdx fibroblasts may be dysfunctional. It was previously identified that myofibroblasts evade apoptosis in chronic fibrosis [58]. It was also demonstrated that the infiltrating macrophages in mdx mice produced high levels of Tgfb, preventing apoptosis of fibroblasts but transforming them to matrix-producing states, which, when blocked with nilotinib, reduced fibrosis [59]. In this current study, these findings are further supported by GSEA analysis of gene expression suggesting an evasion of apoptosis by mdx fibroblasts compared to C57, even when isolated from neonatal muscle before the observable onset of disease pathology (Additional file 5). Thus, the evasion of apoptosis could be an inherent property of mdx fibroblasts and, combined with a relatively reduced overall matrisome gene expression, suggests entering a senescent phenotypic state, which needs to be further investigated. Overall, when we consider the findings based on gene expression, GSEA, and functional analysis, it suggests that the CM-treated dystrophic fibroblasts possibly retained the persistent myofibroblast stage. Interestingly, these differences in fibroblast gene expression and function could also be modified by the intrinsic nature of fibroblasts, especially since we found no correlation between the gene expression changes in direct Aldo treatment versus indirect Aldo treatment (Additional file 5) in C57. Thus, specificity and heterogeneity of these fibroblasts need to be further explored.

MPO and Csf1 expression are increased in mdx but not in C57 Aldo myotube secretions compared to C57 Veh and could be of therapeutic interest. MPO was previously described to be released by monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages. Here, we show that MPO is also released by dystrophin-deficient mdx myotubes. The implications of higher amounts of MPO and Csf1 in the muscle microenvironment from all these cell types would translate into increased oxidative stress and exacerbation of damage. Interestingly, many studies on cardiovascular diseases report plasma levels of MPO to be both a diagnostic and prognostic marker [60–62]. Notably, MPO, secreted by myotubes and increased in mdx Aldo and Veh CM-treated fibroblasts and reduced in C57 Aldo CM fibroblasts, could lead to the production of HOCl, similar to that previously reported in smooth muscle cells that was found to reduce adhesion and modify phenotype and proliferation [63]. This finding, along with the GSEA results suggesting reduced cell adhesion and actomyosin filaments, suggests reduced migration in mdx fibroblasts. Functional assessment of migration through in vitro scratch assays further confirms reduced fibroblast migration in disease compared to Aldo treatment when influenced by myotube secretions.

Additionally, increased Csf1 suggests secreted factors from mdx myotubes may be recruiting more immune cells [64] compared to the wild-type myotubes treated with Aldo, which needs to be further investigated. However, this possibility may account for the chronic inflammation that exacerbates damage and fibrosis in dystrophic muscles in contrast to the brief presence of intramuscular immune cells in resolving fibrosis during normal wound healing.

Spp1, encoding osteopontin, was previously identified to be a major communicator in the muscle microenvironment based on a spatial transcriptomics analysis [65] and was increased in the secretome of myofibers isolated from mice with sciatic nerve transection [66]. However, despite the increased gene expression of Spp1 in mdx myotubes compared to C57, it was decreased in secreted myotube proteins with both disease and Aldo-treatment. Interestingly, Tlr4, a pattern recognition receptor which could be activated by DAMPS, was increased in both mdx myotubes and CM-treated mdx fibroblasts compared to C57. These findings further substantiate the role of Tlr4 in the pathogenesis of DMD. Ablation of Tlr4 in mdx mice resulted in increased muscle force, decreased fibrosis, and shifted the muscle macrophages to a more anti-inflammatory type [67]. Tlr4 was also recently discovered as a regulator of trained immunity in DMD [68].

The finding that neonatal mdx skeletal muscle fibroblasts were less responsive to Aldo was likely due to the activation of MR as early as 2-days after the mice were born. Although the source for the production of Aldo was not explored in this study, we have previously reported the localized production of Cyp11b2 from myeloid cells in older dystrophic muscles [16]. Of note, the DE in mdx compared to C57 fibroblasts could be a result of intrinsic dystrophin deficiency itself, and an exploration into the presence of dystrophin isoforms in fibroblasts identified the presence of Dp427 and Dp71 mRNAs, but only Dp71 protein in fibroblasts, consistent with previous reports [4, 48, 49, 69].

In conclusion, this study showed, for the first time to our knowledge, the presence of Aldo- and disease-regulated secreted factors from myotubes that communicate with fibroblasts to alter their gene expression and function. Future studies on the influence of fibroblast population heterogeneity and the contributions of additional paracrine versus autocrine factors on fibroblasts will provide additional insight into new treatment targets to prevent or reverse chronic fibrosis and promote a wound healing phenotype even in disease conditions.
