Comparative Evaluation of Pain Assessment Using Star Pen and Conventional Injection Technique during Inferior Alveolar Nerve Block Injection in Children Aged between 6 and 10 Years: A Randomized Controlled Trial

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Dental caries remain one of the most common chronic diseases affecting children worldwide. Despite being largely preventable, untreated caries can cause pain, infection, impaired function, and reduced quality of life. Early diagnosis and intervention are essential to prevent disease progression. However, many dental procedures cause discomfort, making effective pain management crucial in pediatric dentistry.

Pediatric dentistry addresses the unique physiological and psychological needs of children, with managing dental anxiety and ensuring cooperation being key challenges. Minimizing procedural pain fosters trust, reduces fear, and promotes positive dental experiences, thereby promoting future compliance. Local anesthesia (LA) is the cornerstone of pain control, but needle fear is a significant barrier, often resulting in uncooperative behavior and treatment avoidance.1,2

Strategies to reduce injection pain include topical anesthetics, buffering agents, distraction techniques, and controlling injection speed.3 Slower, steady anesthetic delivery significantly decreases discomfort, yet manual control is challenging.4 Computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery (CCLAD) systems regulate flow and pressure, providing consistent slow injections that reduce tissue trauma and pain. Their pen-like design also lessens anxiety and improves cooperation.5,6

The Woodpecker Star Pen is a wireless CCLAD device that delivers anesthetic at controlled speeds using an algorithm, depositing solution ahead of the needle to reduce pain and enhance comfort.7,8 Its ergonomic design allows precise injections, making it suitable for pediatric patients.

This randomized split-mouth trial compares pain perception between the conventional syringe and the Star Pen during inferior alveolar nerve blocks in children, aiming to provide evidence on their relative efficacy in pediatric pain management.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study employed a randomized, split-mouth crossover design involving 50 healthy pediatric patients aged 6–10 years, each requiring bilateral inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) for dental treatment in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Bharati Vidyapeeth Dental College and Hospital, Pune. Each participant received both local anesthesia (LA) techniques: Star Pen and conventional cartridge syringe in two separate sessions, spaced 2 weeks apart to eliminate carryover effects. The sequence of technique administration was determined by simple randomization using a coin toss by the principal investigator. The study enrolled participants between June 1st, 2023, and January 1st 2024. Final follow-up assessments were completed by February 1st 2024.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was explained to both the parents/guardians and the participants. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents, and verbal or written assent was secured from the children prior to enrollment. All participants received an information sheet outlining the study procedures. The trial is registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI) under registration number CTRI/2025/05/087907, ensuring transparency and adherence to national research guidelines.

Intervention Protocol

At each session, topical anesthesia (5% lignocaine gel) was applied to the injection site for 1–2 minutes using a cotton roll. Local anesthetic administration involved 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine, delivered via a 30-gauge, 1-inch needle in both techniques.

Computer-controlled delivery (Star Pen): Anesthesia was delivered using the Woodpecker Star Pen, a computerized local anesthetic device set to hybrid mode, enabling steady and controlled injection. Aspiration was performed by engaging the device's aspiration function prior to deposition.

Conventional syringe technique: A self-aspirating conventional cartridge syringe was used to administer the anesthetic over a period of 1–1.5 minutes. Standard aspiration was confirmed prior to injection (Fig. 1).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23, with the level of significance set at 5%. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the two groups with respect to the Modified Wong–Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale, SEM scale scores, duration of anesthesia administration, and heart rate between groups, as the data were non-parametric. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare pre- and post-intervention heart rate within each group. These analyses were conducted to evaluate differences in pain perception, behavioral responses, physiological parameters, and procedural efficiency between the two anesthesia techniques (Fig. 3).

Results

Demographic Data

A total of 50 pediatric patients aged between 6 and 10 years were recruited, with a mean age of 7.41 ± 1.53 years. Of these, 27 were males, and 24 were females. One patient dropped out during the course of the study and was excluded from further analysis.

Pain Perception (Modified Wong–Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale)

Pain perception was evaluated using the Modified Wong–Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale. The mean pain score in the conventional syringe group was 3.24 ± 1.94, while the Star Pen group demonstrated a significantly lower mean score of 1.44 ± 1.82. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test), indicating that the Star Pen technique was associated with reduced pain perception during local anesthesia administration (Fig. 4).

Comparison of the Doremon Faces Pain Rating Scale between the two groups

Behavioral responses to local anesthesia administration were evaluated using the SEM scale. The mean scores across all three subcomponents—Sound, Eye, and Motor—as well as the total SEM score, were significantly lower in the Star Pen group compared to the conventional syringe group, indicating reduced observable distress and enhanced patient comfort.

The mean Sound score was 2.04 ± 0.93 in the conventional group and 1.47 ± 0.54 in the Star Pen group. Eye scores were 2.02 ± 0.91 for the conventional group and 1.37 ± 0.53 for the Star Pen group. Motor scores were 1.64 ± 0.72 and 1.18 ± 0.44 for the conventional and Star Pen groups, respectively. The total SEM score was significantly higher in the conventional group (5.59 ± 2.18) compared to the Star Pen group (4.02 ± 1.23).

All intergroup differences were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test), highlighting that the computerized delivery system resulted in a more favorable behavioral response during injection (Tables 1 and 2, and Fig. 5).

The mean duration for administration of local anesthesia was significantly longer in the Star Pen group (1.51 ± 0.37 minutes) compared to the conventional syringe group (1.03 ± 0.24 minutes) (p < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test) (Table 3 and Fig. 6). The increased duration reflects the controlled, slow delivery mechanism of the computer-assisted system.

Heart Rate Analysis

Heart rate was measured pre- and postintervention to assess physiological responses to injection. In the conventional syringe group, the mean heart rate increased significantly from 97.72 ± 9.72 bpm (preintervention) to 102.12 ± 8.22 bpm (postintervention) (p = 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). In contrast, the Star Pen group showed no significant change, with heart rates of 98.55 ± 16.03 bpm preintervention and 99.00 ± 17.29 bpm postintervention (p = 0.527) (Table 4 and Fig. 7).

Discussion

Dental fear and anxiety remain prevalent concerns in pediatric dentistry, affecting approximately 9% of children.9 One of the most significant contributors to this anxiety is the administration of local anesthetic (LA) injections, which are often perceived as painful and distressing by children.2 This fear frequently leads to avoidance of dental treatment, resulting in poorer oral health outcomes and a heightened risk of long-term dental phobia.10 The origins of dental anxiety in children are multifactorial, involving personal traits (such as temperament and coping skills), previous negative experiences, and environmental factors.11

Needle-related anxiety, in particular, plays a central role.12 Injections are not only associated with actual pain but also evoke anticipatory anxiety, especially in younger children.2 Around 19% of children aged 4–6 years experience needle phobia, which gradually declines with age as coping mechanisms mature.2 Nevertheless, a notable percentage of adolescents still report significant anxiety related to dental injections. Moreover, children with generalized needle phobia often display higher levels of dental fear due to the overlap with blood-injury-injection phobia.13

Although local anesthesia is critical for achieving pain-free dental treatment, its administration can paradoxically become one of the most challenging and distressing steps for pediatric patients.2 Multiple factors influence discomfort during injection, including the speed of administration, injection site, needle gauge, anesthetic volume, and operator technique.14 Importantly, anxiety and pain are bidirectionally linked—children with higher preprocedural anxiety often report greater pain perception.15 Early negative experiences, especially during injections, may impair the effectiveness of pain control in subsequent visits and reduce children's compliance with future dental care.2,16

Traditional metallic syringes often intensify this distress due to their sharp, clinical appearance, which may trigger fear even before the injection begins.17 In response, various innovations have emerged to improve the pediatric injection experience. Camouflaged syringes help by masking the visual cues that provoke anxiety.18 More recently, CCLAD systems have shown promise in optimizing pain control by regulating flow rate and pressure during injection.19 Unlike manual syringes, which rely heavily on the operator's skill, CCLAD systems deliver anesthetic at a slow, consistent rate, minimizing tissue distension and associated pain.

In the current study, the Star Pen—a CCLAD device demonstrated clear advantages over conventional syringes in managing injection-related pain and distress. Subjective pain, as assessed using the Doremon Faces Pain Rating Scale, was significantly lower in the Star Pen group (mean 1.44) compared to the conventional group (mean 3.24). This aligns with prior findings by Lenka et al.20 and San Martin-Lopez et al.,21 who observed higher pain scores with traditional inferior alveolar nerve block techniques.

Objective behavioral responses, evaluated using the SEM scale, also favored the Star Pen group, which exhibited a lower mean score (4.0) than the conventional group (5.59). These results corroborate findings by Helmy et al.,22 who reported significantly reduced distress during both injection and extraction when using CC-ILA, another computer-assisted system. Lower SEM scores reflect not just reduced pain but improved behavioral cooperation, which is a critical determinant of treatment success in pediatric dentistry. Physiological stress responses, such as changes in heart rate, provide an additional layer of insight. In our study, the conventional group exhibited a significant increase in postinjection heart rate, whereas the Star Pen group showed minimal change. These results mirror those of Thoppe-Dhamodhara et al.,23 Garrett-Bernardin et al.,4 and Patini et al.,24 who reported significantly lower cardiovascular stress in children treated with devices such as the Wand®. Reduced heart rate variation may indicate not only decreased pain perception but also a calmer, more tolerable experience for the child. However, other studies, such as Helmy et al.22 and Chittora et al.,25 found no significant differences in physiological parameters, suggesting individual variability and procedural context may influence outcomes.

The prolonged injection time associated with Star Pen (mean 1.51 min vs 1.03 min for conventional syringes) likely contributed to its superior performance. Slower anesthetic delivery reduces intratissue pressure, which in turn minimizes mechanical distension and associated discomfort. Primosch and Brooks26 demonstrated that children receiving slow palatal injections via the Wand® system experienced significantly less discomfort. Similarly, Heise et al.,27 Kudo,28 and Zijlstra et al.29 all confirmed that injection speed plays a key role in pain modulation, with slower speeds consistently linked to lower pain levels. Although Aggarwal et al. did not find a statistically significant difference in pain scores between varying injection speeds, patient preference strongly favored slower, more controlled administration.30

The role of device appearance and design should not be underestimated. Rajan et al.17 emphasized the negative impact of the metallic syringe's intimidating appearance on child behavior. Conversely, Bagher et al.18 found that child-friendly, camouflaged, or digital devices improved both cooperation and comfort. By reducing visual threat, these devices help mitigate anticipatory anxiety, an important trigger for pain amplification.12

Additionally, our findings are consistent with several other investigations into CCLAD. Aggarwal et al.30 reported reduced anxiety levels with CCLAD use in children, while Patel et al.31 observed lower postoperative pain using INJEX, a needle-free anesthetic system. Langthasa et al.32 and San Martin-Lopez et al.21 also found significantly better pain control with computerized injection methods.

It is worth noting that not all studies favor CCLAD unequivocally. Research by Versloot et al.,2 Allen et al.,33 Ram and Peretz12 found no substantial difference in pain or anxiety levels between computerized and conventional systems. These findings underscore the importance of patient-specific variables such as baseline anxiety, pain threshold, and behavioral disposition, as well as operator-related factors like experience and technique.34

Limitations

This study's limitations include a relatively small sample size, which may affect generalizability. The crossover design, while robust, may have introduced expectation bias despite the washout period. Blinding was not feasible due to the distinct appearance and function of the devices, potentially influencing subjective and evaluator responses. Pain assessment tools, though validated, are prone to individual variability. Lastly, operator experience—especially with newer devices such as the Star Pen—was not controlled for, which may have affected outcomes.

Conclusion

The findings of this randomized controlled trial demonstrate that the Woodpecker Star Pen, a CCLAD system, provides superior outcomes compared to the conventional cartridge syringe for inferior alveolar nerve block administration in pediatric patients. The Star Pen significantly reduced pain perception as measured by validated subjective and objective scales, and elicited a lower physiological stress response, indicated by minimal changes in heart rate. Although the administration time was longer with the Star Pen, the controlled, gradual delivery of anesthetic likely contributed to reduced tissue trauma and improved patient comfort. These results suggest that CCLAD devices enhance the efficacy and tolerability of local anesthesia in children, supporting their integration into pediatric dental practice to optimize patient cooperation and clinical outcomes.
