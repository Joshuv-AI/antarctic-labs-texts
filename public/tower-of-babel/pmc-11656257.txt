Origin, Distribution, and Influential Factors of Organic Acids in Deep and Ultradeep Clastic Reservoirs within the Fukang Sag of the Junggar Basin
Authors: Wenjun Pang, Jing Li, Shixin Zhou, Liangliang Liu, Yaoyu Li, Hao Wang, Gengrong Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In deep and ultradeep clastic reservoirs, secondary porosity functions as the primary space for hydrocarbon storage, intricately associated with the dissolution processes of water-soluble organic acids (WSOAs). However, conventional theories concerning secondary porosity predominantly emphasize medium-depth or shallow reservoirs, lacking a thorough investigation into how WSOA-driven mechanisms affect deeper strata formations. To bridge this gap, our research involved selecting 36 samples from Mesozoic Permian clastic rock formations situated in western China’s Fukang Sag within the Junggar Basin region. We performed comprehensive analyses utilizing the Soxhlet extraction method combined with qualitative and quantitative assessments via 940 ion chromatography (Metrohm AG). These findings were integrated with oilfield production data to investigate the sources, composition, distribution characteristics, and influencing factors associated with organic acids in deep and ultradeep clastic reservoirs. Our investigation revealed that WSOAs persist even within ultradeep reservoirs; increased buried depths initially lead to a rise in WSOA concentrations followed by a subsequent decline. Similarly, effective porosities closely mirrored these trends alongside variations observed across WSOA concentrations while permeability remained consistently low yet stable throughout these transitions. This indicated significant involvement of WSOAs during dissolution processes contributing to the creation and maintenance of secondary pore spaces. Furthermore, notable positive correlations have emerged establishing a direct relationship between WSOA generation concentrations and corresponding shifts in formation pressures and temperatures. In deep and ultradeep reservoirs, the concentration of organic acids exhibits an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease in response to escalating formation temperature and pressure. These findings underscore the critical roles played by key influential factors associated with WSOAs in these geological settings.

Introduction

In recent years, the recoverable resources of shallow and medium-depth hydrocarbon in China have been nearly depleted, necessitating urgent exploration for hydrocarbon resources in deep and ultradeep reservoirs. The unique geological conditions of high temperature and high pressure at depths exceeding 3000–4500 m (deep layers) and 4500 m (ultradeep layers) often result in the development of dense reservoirs. Within these density reservoirs, certain areas exhibit abnormally high permeability, creating favorable conditions for hydrocarbon enrichment. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the formation mechanism of abnormal high permeability in deep and ultradeep reservoirs.1−3 Due to substantial mechanical compaction resulting from deep burial, the conventional primary pores in deep and ultradeep reservoirs is significantly diminished. Consequently, secondary pores emerges as the predominant pore storage system for the formation of clastic rock reservoirs and the retention of oil and gas. Thus, deep and ultradeep reservoir rocks are distinguished by abundant fractures and secondary pores, alongside exhibiting ultralow porosity, ultralow permeability, source-reservoir integration, and a high degree of thermal evolution.4 Since Surdam investigated the diagenetic processes and secondary pore formation and evolution of reservoirs from the perspective of organic–inorganic interactions, significant advancements have been made in the theory of secondary pores, which has greatly contributed to oil and gas exploration and development.5−7 It has been established that at least one-third of sandstone hydrocarbon reservoir storage space is formed during diagenetic process,8 with high-quality reservoirs often demonstrating extensive development of secondary dissolution pores.9 In deep and ultradeep high-temperature, high-pressure environments, the interactions among hydrocarbons, water, and rocks continuously influence reservoir quality as well as hydrocarbon accumulation while elucidating the mechanisms underlying secondary pore development in clastic reservoirs.10−12 Domestic and international scholars have put forward multiple dissolution pore-forming mechanisms for minerals in deep and ultradeep oil and gas basins, such as acid dissolution mechanisms (the atmospheric freshwater leaching mechanism,13 the organic acid dissolution mechanism resulting from the thermal evolution of organic matter,14 the dissolution mechanism of generating CO2 through the thermal decarboxylation of organic matter,15 the reaction dissolution mechanism of clay-carbonate minerals,16 and the deep-seated hydrothermal dissolution mechanism17) and alkaline dissolution.18 Moreover, the dissolution effect of organic acids has the capacity to enlarge pores during the diagenesis of clastic rocks. Therefore, the presence of extensive intergranular dissolved pores and intragranular dissolved pores serve as compelling evidence for secondary porosity arising from mineral dissolution by organic acids.19,20 Despite existing advancements, a substantial gap persists concerning the genesis, composition, distribution, and influencing factors of organic acids in deep and ultradeep oil and gas reservoir spaces. This deficiency impedes research into the mechanisms that govern secondary pore formation and hydrocarbon storage dynamics. This study has selected clastic reservoir rock samples from critical exploration and development strata within the Fukang Sag of the western Junggar Basin in China. The objective is to analyze the genesis, composition, distribution, and influencing factors of organic acids present in these samples while also investigating their role in secondary pore dissolution mechanisms. Ultimately, this research aspires to provide scientific evidence for advancing exploration efforts targeting deep and ultradeep hydrocarbon resources.

Geological Overview of the Study Area

The Junggar Basin, situated in western China, is a significant hydrocarbon-bearing basin. The Fukang Sag, situated at the northern foothills of the Bogda Mountains on the southeastern margin of the Junggar Basin, represents a secondary sag within the eastern part of the basin.21 It serves as a vital hydrocarbon generation sag and an essential conduit for oil and gas migration.22 The Fukang Sag is located in the southeastern part of the central depression of Junggar Basin, as indicated by the geological profile and drilling data. It is encompassed by three uplifts: Baijiahai, Shaqi, and Beisantai. It serves as a significant source of hydrocarbons for the Middle Permian Lucaogou Formation (P2l) and the Lower Jurassic Badaowan Formation (J1b). The study area covers Carboniferous, Permian, Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous, Paleogene, Neogene, and Quaternary systems with specific emphasis on the Permian system. Notably, the Baijiahai Bulge, Shaqi bulge, and Beisantai Bulge exhibit varying degrees of development; particularly significant uplift has been observed in the Beisantai bulge over an extended period with multiple sets of strata denudated until some strata peaked out.23 Tectonic movements in this region have undergone Hercynian, Indosinian, and Yanshan stages,24,25 with substantial influence exerted by Yanshan movement on structure and hydrocarbon accumulation in eastern Jungdong area leading to a complex tectonic pattern within this region. Due to its relatively closed nature within a confined area during geological periods characterized by continuous deposition without significant impact from multistage tectonic movements, all Permian and above stratigraphic sequences in Fukang Sag are comprehensively developed, encompassing source rock, reservoir rock, and cap rock.26,27 In this study, drilling core samples from Jurassic Badaowan Formation, Permian Wuerhe Formation and Lucaogou Formation were selected for analysis to determine organic acid composition distribution as well as influencing factors using mineralogy, petrology, geochemistry, and organic acid characterization.

Samples Collection and Analysis

Sample Collection

Based on the lithological characteristics, stratigraphic setting, and buried depth of Mesozoic reservoir formations in Fukang Sag, Junggar Basin, a total of 36 representative samples of reservoir rocks encompassing diverse lithologies, predominantly sandstones, were chosen for analysis (Figure 1). The samples are predominantly concentrated within the Jurassic and Permian formations, characterized by lithologies primarily comprising gray gravelly coarse sandstone, gray-green sand and gravel, gray medium to fine sandstone, and gray fine sandstone (Table 1, Figure 2). The drilling core of the Upper Wuerhe Formation in the middle Permian reservoir exhibits well-developed fine-grained lithic sandstone and organic-rich black shale. These formations are characterized by interbedded layers of sandstone and mudstone, with clearly visible particles of feldspar, calcite, turbidiolite, pyroclastic material, and quartz. Notably present within this formation are horizontal strata with closely spaced intervals often occurring in concentrated belt-like distributions. The Lucaogou Formation showcases a diverse range of lithologies including sandstone, gravel, and mudstone interspersed with alternating layers of gray-black shale and mudstones. Both the sandstones and mudstones display layered distributions; furthermore, the organic content within the mudstones is relatively high at an average total organic carbon content reaching 5.74%. Additionally, the maturity level ranges from mature to highly mature stages. The lower section primarily comprises well-sorted sandstones and conglomerates composed of sands and gravels.

Sample Analysis

To prevent contamination of organic acids in the sample by drilling fluid, the surface material of the core sample should be removed prior to organic acid extraction. The sample was then mechanically ground to 200 mesh and dried for 48 h using a Scientz-10N freeze-drying instrument for future use. To minimize the impact of filter study on organic acids, the filter study was initially rinsed with deionized water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm for 72 h, followed by a 48 h freeze-drying process. Organic acids in the sample were extracted using the Soxhlet extraction method. A total of 15 g powdered sample was wrapped in prefreeze-dried filter study, loaded into a Soxhlet extractor, and subjected to a 72 h extraction with 250 mL deionized water. Following extraction, the resulting liquid product was filtered through a 0.45 μm needle filter membrane.

The porosity and permeability of core samples were measured using the PoroPDP-200 automatic overburden porosity and permeability measuring instrument manufactured by American Core Company. Initially, the core sample was prepared into a cylinder with a diameter of 1.5 cm and a height of 2–3 cm, then dried in a 50 °C oven for more than 12 h to remove water and residual hydrocarbons, followed by placement in a 22 °C dryer for 5 h. The volume Vo of the core at 200 psi is determined by injecting helium gas into the core sample in a precompression chamber with known volume based on the principle of helium expansion. Permeability determination for low-permeability cores is conducted using the pressure pulse attenuation method.

Results and Discussion

Petrological Features of Clastic Reservoirs and the Properties of Organic Acids

Petrological Characteristics of Clastic Reservoirs

The Permian strata in the periphery of the Fukang Sag primarily consist of near-source fan delta deposits formed within a semiclosed environment, transitioning from a semiclosed saline lake basin to an open freshwater setting. In terms of rock and ore composition, the lithology of the Lucaogou Formation is predominantly lithic sandstone, followed by feldspar lithic sandstone (Figure 3A). The primary lithic components are volcanic rock fragments such as tuff, andesite, and felsite. The main interstitial materials in the reservoir include albite, calcite, argillite, and turbidites; specifically, the average contents are 5.4% for albite, 4.82% for calcite, 3.02% for argillite, and 2.65% for turbidites. Within the Permian Wuerhe Formation in Fukang Sag, clastic rock reservoirs are mainly composed of coarse sandstone, medium sandstone, and fine sandstone—primarily classified as lithic sandstones based on petrological analysis (Figure 3B). The quartz content within these rocks ranges from 1–18%, with an average value of 6.9%. Feldspar content varies between 1% and 22%, averaging at approximately 7.6%. Rock debris constitutes between 68% to nearly all (100%) of the composition with an average proportion of about 85.5%. This debris is predominantly comprised of tuffaceous volcanic rocks that account for over 70%, followed by smaller amounts of andesite, felsite, and granite fragments. Reservoir interstitials mainly comprise clay minerals along with carbonate minerals and siliceous materials including turbidites; among these constituents clay minerals serve as primary cementing agents ranging from approximately 2–12%, primarily consisting of chlorite and Illite alongside kaolinite due to aluminosilicate mineral dissolution coupled with volcanic ash alteration processes. Carbonate minerals function as secondary cementing substances with contents varying between roughly 1–9%, chiefly comprising calcite; while turbidites range from 0–3%, resulting from hydrolytic alterations involving volcanic ash deposition. The abundance of debris and feldspar particles within the reservoir provides the essential material for the dissolution of organic acids.

Content Characteristics of Organic Acids in Clastic Reservoirs

The overall concentration of organic acids in the clastic reservoir cores within the study area fluctuates within the range of 2.47 to 12.44 mg/L, featuring an average magnitude of 5.54 mg/L and a median value of 5.19 mg/L (Table 1 and Figure 4). The cumulative concentration of organic acids throughout all core samples within the study area totals 199.47 mg/L, maintaining an average of 5.54 mg/L. Concurrently, the total concentration of organic acids in ten deep reservoir rock samples amounts to 63.96 mg/L, attaining an average value of 6.4 mg/L; in contrast, for twenty-six ultradeep reservoir rock samples, it stands at 135.51 mg/L, with an average value of 5.21 mg/L. Notably, the concentration of organic acids in deep clastic reservoir rock samples exceeds that in ultradeep counterparts, suggesting that with increasing buried depth, the concentrations of organic acids in both deep and ultradeep reservoirs initially rise and then fall.

Composition Characteristics of Organic Acids in Clastic Reservoirs

The predominant organic acids identified in the clastic reservoirs of the study area include formic, acetic, propionic, butyric, oxalic, malonic, and succinic (Table 1 and Figure 5). Figure 4 illustrates the relative distribution of various organic acids within the clastic reservoir rock cores from the study area. The concentration of formic ranges from 0.196 to 3.42 mg/L with an average value of 1.45 mg/L; acetic concentration range from 0.301 to 5.203 mg/L with an average of 1.7 mg/L; malonic varies between 0.096 and 3.497 mg/L with an average content of approximately 0.67 mg/L; while succinic levels range from 0.151 to 4.078 mg/L with an average close to 1.39 mg/L. Monocarboxylic acids constitute the highest proportion among these compounds, ranging from 32–95%, averaging at 68%. In contrast, dicarboxylic acids exhibit lower concentrations ranging from 5–68%, with an average of 32%. In the deep and ultradeep reservoir rocks of Fukang Sag, monocarboxylic acids are predominantly represented by formic and acetic, whereas dicarboxylic acids primarily consist of succinic and butanedioic. This composition aligns well with previously documented organic acid profiles in formation water studies. This composition is consistent with the organic acid profile previously documented in formation water studies. Monocarboxylic acids dominate formation waters, with acetic accounting for 50–100% of monocarboxylic acids.28−30 When comparing the organic acidic components in reservoir rocks, the average ratio of monocarboxylic acids to dicarboxylic acids is 2.59, indicating that the majority of organic acids in deep and ultradeep reservoir rocks consist of monocarboxylic acids while dicarboxylic acids remain as a secondary component.

Relative composition of organic acids in deep and ultradeep reservoir rocks in Fukang Sag.

This study, in conjunction with previous research, demonstrates that organic acids exhibit limited thermal stability and are prone to degradation even under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. Nevertheless, a certain quantity of monocarboxylic acids and dicarboxylic acids remains stable within the ultradeep reservoir rocks. The TOC-normalized concentration of water-soluble organic acids (WSOAs) expressed in mg/gTOC represents the ratio of total WSOA concentrations to total organic carbon (TOC), which can be utilized to characterize the WSOA-generating capacity per gram of organic matter. This metric serves as an indicator of each gram of organic matter’s potential to generate water-soluble organic acids. To quantitatively assess the correlation between different types of WSOAs, this study employs the Pearson correlation coefficient. Table 2 reveals that, in most instances, the Pearson correlation coefficient between concentrations of various types of organic acids in core samples from the study area approaches 1. This finding suggests a strong correlation among different types of WSOAs, indicating that their formation processes and states may be analogous.31

Influence of Organic Acid Components and Concentrations on Clastic Reservoir Dissolution

Fukang Sag is located in the central region of the Junggar Basin, which features a relatively enclosed diagenetic environment characterized by limited large-scale disintegration and fault development. A comparison of rock slices and organic acid concentrations from reservoirs at various well locations (Figures 6 and 7) reveals that intergranular dissolved pores, intragranular dissolved pores, and cement-dissolved pores serve as spatial types and migration pathways for deep and ultradeep hydrocarbon reservoirs. While the development degree of secondary dissolved pores show regional differences, a positive correlation between high organic acid concentration and solubility porosity is observed, as illustrate in Figure 6. Furthermore, although regional differences are noted in the extent of secondary solution pore development, a clear positive correlation between organic acid concentrations and dissolution porosity is evident (Figure 6). As buried depth increases, the concentration of total organic acids in core samples gradually declines. This phenomenon is attributed to the significant release of organic acid fluids during the dissolution of reservoir rocks, including feldspar, calcite, laumonite aluminum silicate minerals, and certain carbonate minerals and debris, which leads to the formation of secondary dissolution pores. For example, at a depth of 3135.9 m in Fu43 (Figure 7A), four groups of clastic core samples exhibit the highest concentration of organic acids (12.44 mg/L). The secondary pores are primarily intergranular dissolved pores resulting from intense dissolution of turbidized zeolite cements; other intergranular dissolved pores arise from minor volcanic rock debris following weak dissolution processes. Monoacid and dio-acid are identified as the predominant soluble organic acids. In Figure 7B, at a depth of 4601.6 m in KT2, both intergranular and intragranular solution pores with moderate degrees of dissolution predominate; these are mainly formed by organic acid-mediated dissolution of turbidite cement and tuffaceous interstitial material. Moving to Figure 7C at a depth of 5298.2 m in KT1, predominantly intergranular dissolved pores emerge due to medium-to-strong dissolution processes involving calcite and turbidite cement; additionally, there exist secondary pores resulting from mineral dissolution induced by organic acids. However, in Figure 7D at a depth of 6257 m in KT5, despite low concentrations of organic acids (2.47 mg/L), the presence of dissolution pores within particles indicates that organic acids continue to play a significant role in the dissolution process.

Relationship Analysis Between Effective Porosity, Permeability, and Organic Acids in Clastic Reservoirs

Porosity is a critical index for assessing the pore space size of reservoir rocks. In medium and low permeability reservoirs, porosity and permeability are essential parameters for evaluating the physical properties of reservoirs and calculating hydrocarbon reserves.32,33 The effective porosity and permeability test results for deep and ultradeep reservoir rocks in Fukang Sag are presented in Table 1. The effective porosity of samples from the study area ranges from 2.3–12.2%, with an average value of 5.16%. Permeability varies from 0.001 to 0.968 mD, averaging at 0.11 mD, categorizing it as a densified reservoir with extremely low porosity and permeability characteristics. However, within this densified reservoir, the presence of secondary pores resulting from intergranular and intragranular dissolution indicates that organic acids may have transformed the densified reservoir into a typical high-quality clastic rock deep reservoir space. The Wuerhe Formation and Lucaogou Formation in Fukang Sag represent part of the middle diagenetic process where organic acid fluids infiltrate through microfractures, dissolving soluble mineral particles on either side of these fractures as well as cementing materials filled with turbidite and calcite between them; this process creates migration pathways for hydrocarbon. The concentration of organic acids and effective porosity in the reservoir both exhibit a declining trend with increasing buried depth, as illustrated in Figure 8. Furthermore, Figure 9A indicates a weak correlation coefficient between effective porosity and permeability, while Figure 9B reveals an exponential positive correlation between effective porosity and organic acid concentration, with a correlation coefficient (R2) reaching 0.54. These findings suggest that the dissolution of organic acids may have a pore-expanding effect on the effective pore space within ultradeep reservoirs. In homogeneous high-porosity and high-permeability reservoirs, there is a strong positive correlation between porosity and permeability; higher porosity correlates with superior permeability. Conversely, in densified reservoirs characterized by extremely low porosity and permeability, an increase in organic acid concentration leads to enhanced effective porosity but reduced permeability within specific zones. This phenomenon can be attributed to the dissolution of silicate minerals by organic acids, resulting in secondary pore formation. However, fine particles that fill these pores due to dissolution contribute to decreased seepage capacity within the reservoir’s pore structure, ultimately leading to reduced permeability.

Influencing Factors and Sources of Organic Acids in Deep and Ultradeep Clastic Reservoirs

Influence of Formation Pressure on Organic Acid Generation in Clastic Reservoirs

Formation pressure and temperature in sedimentary basins are fundamental physical parameters that govern the formation and evolution of hydrocarbon reservoirs.34,35 Although the mechanism of organic acid generation from organic matter under high temperature and pressure has been simulated, there remains a paucity of studies examining the relationship between the temperature and pressure of ultradeep reservoirs and the composition and concentration of organic acids in actual basins.3Figure 10 illustrates that as reservoir pressure increases, the concentration of organic acids in clastic rock reservoirs initially rises before subsequently declining, suggesting that elevated-pressure geological environments promote organic acid generation. Li3 observed that the decomposition reaction rates of oxalic and acetic respectively exhibit a marked increase at temperatures of 180 and 230 °C. They also noted that the decomposition reaction rates of organic acids in reservoir rocks are relatively slow at temperatures below 180 °C. Given that the formation temperature in the study area does not exceed 180 °C, it can be inferred that organic acids may remain fully preserved within the reservoir. Previous research has indicated that water-soluble organic acids produced by kerogen pyrolysis and thermal oxidation of crude oil contribute to both formation water and organic acid sources in reservoirs. This suggests that when hydrocarbons are generated from source rock organic matter, thermal alteration occurs within the geological matrix, leading to substantial production of organic acids and CO2.36 Furthermore, it is acknowledged that formation pressure serves as a critical driving force for hydrocarbon flow within the reservoir.37,38 In low-permeability reservoirs, high hydrocarbon-producing zones not only exist but also serve as significant indicators of the energy contained within hydrocarbon reservoirs.39 The pressure coefficient is a critical parameter that characterizes both the nature and magnitude of fluid pressure; a value exceeding 1 indicates the presence of elevated abnormal fluid pressure.40 High pressure coefficients are typically associated with substantial formation pressures. In Schedule 1 of Fukang Sag, the formation pressure coefficient ranges from 1.45 to 1.79, surpassing 1.45 and corresponding to formation pressures between 60.5 and 100.7 MPa. As illustrated in Figure 10, when the formation pressure approaches approximately 80 MPa, the concentration of organic acid in the reservoir reaches its peak; furthermore, as local pressures exceed this threshold, there is a gradual decline in organic acid content within the reservoir.

Influence of Buried Depth and Formation Temperature on Organic Acids in Clastic Reservoirs

The thermal stability of organic acids decreases with increasing buried depth in reservoirs.3,43 The thermal stability simulation experiment demonstrates that at temperatures below 230 °C (acetic) and 180 °C (oxalic), the decomposition reaction rates of these organic acids are relatively slow. Once temperatures exceed 230 °C, both acetic acid and oxalic become prone to decarboxylation, resulting in an increased decomposition reaction rate. However, within the clastic rock reservoir studied at a depth of 6257 m, the formation temperature is a modest 164 °C and does not surpass 180 °C. Consequently, the decomposition reaction rate remains minimal, ensuring the complete preservation of organic acids in ultradeep reservoir rocks. Figure 11 illustrates that the concentration of organic acids in clastic reservoirs initially increases before declining with buried depth. The high-pressure, low-geothermal-gradient area in Fukang Sag, located in southeastern Junggar Basin, is particularly conducive to hydrocarbon reservoir formation and preservation.41 As Surdam’s research,6 temperature significantly affects the concentration of organic acids in formation water; specifically, a temperature range of 80–160 °C represents an optimal preservation zone for organic acids where concentrations can reach up to 10,000 mg/L. Below 80 °C, bacterial decomposition results in decreased concentrations of organic acids while above 160 °C decarboxylation similarly leads to a decline. The Mesozoic Permian Lucaogou Formation mud/shale in Fukang Sag has attained maturity (Ro = 1.1%) at a buried depth of 5000 m, with a maximum formation temperature of 120 °C.21 Through TTI calculations and verification of vitrinite reflectance (Ro), Wang Yutao42 confirmed the low geothermal gradient of the Mesozoic Permian Lucaogou Formation in Fukang Sag: Paleozoic at 5.3 °C/100 m, Mesozoic at 3.5 °C/100 m, and Cenozoic at 2.42 °C/100 m. Based on the geothermal gradient calculations presented in Figure 11 and Table 3, it is evident that when the formation temperature falls below 80 °C, thermal oxidation of immature organic matter begins, leading to increased bacterial and microbial activity. The organic acids produced are rapidly consumed through bacterial and microbial oxidation, resulting in only a small fraction entering the reservoir rock and consequently yielding relatively low concentrations of organic acids. In contrast, within reservoirs located at depths between 4000 and 6000 m where organic matter ranges from low to mature stages and formation temperatures lie between 80 and 160 °C, substantial hydrocarbon generation occurs alongside significant production of organic acids and CO2. Consequently, there is an increase in organic acid concentrations corresponding to higher formation temperatures. Within this temperature range, the dissolution of minerals in the clastic reservoir results in the development of abundant secondary pores, thereby facilitating hydrocarbon migration and storage. When the reservoir exceeds a depth of 6000 m and the formation temperature surpasses 160 °C, organic matter transitions into a stage of high to over maturity. This leads to a gradual shift from oil generation to gas generation, resulting in saturation of liquid hydrocarbons within the reservoir and aqueous solutions. Furthermore, under relatively stable fluid conditions, organic acid concentrations decrease due to these factors. Therefore, the elevated geological temperatures present in Fukang Sag are conducive to the formation of organic acids that dissolve reservoir materials and create secondary pores.

Origins of Organic Acids in Deep and Ultradeep Clastic Reservoirs

Despite the observed variation trend of organic acid content with buried depth in the clastic reservoirs, as illustrated in Figure 12, the complex nature of different strata within Fukang Sag necessitates a comprehensive classification and detailed investigation into the sources and generation mechanisms of organic acids for each stratum. Researcher44,45 indicates that during the thermal evolution process of source rocks, fluids accompanying hydrocarbon charging often exhibit elevated concentrations of organic acids. These acidic fluids release H+ ions, which effectively dissolve feldspar and carbonate minerals in dense sandstones, thereby creating favorable conditions for significant secondary porosity development in deep-seated reservoirs. It is noteworthy that due to their proximity to organic matter, aluminosilicate and carbonate minerals present in source rocks react with generated organic acids during the early stages of formation, leading to partial consumption of these acids and thus regulating their concentration within the reservoir.46,47 Furthermore, owing to their hydrophilic nature, organic acids preferentially dissolve in formation water and effectively enter the reservoir as mud/shale is expelled. However, as organic acids migrate from source rocks to reservoirs and are consumed over time, both their generation scale and concentration decrease accordingly.

Variation characteristics of organic acids with buried depth in clastic reservoir within Fukang Sag.

The Upper Jurassic and Lower Permian strata in Fukang Sag have accumulated substantial hydrocarbon resources. The Badaowan Formation of the Jurassic, the Lucaogou Formation of the Permian, and the Upper Wuerhe Formation all demonstrate industrial-scale hydrocarbon production. The Triassic series is primarily composed of mud-rich lacustrine deposits that are deeply buried and undergo late-stage diagenesis, resulting in high-quality regional cap rocks that effectively seal off Permian hydrocarbons. The Permian series constitutes a comprehensive petroleum system encompassing source, reservoir, and cap rock components. Hydrocarbons generated from the Lucaogou Formation preferentially accumulate within its own reservoir-cap rock assemblage. Furthermore, the sandstone-gravel bodies of the Upper Wuerhe Formation are encased by two sets of regional cap rocks, providing favorable sealing conditions for near-source accumulation and efficient hydrocarbon entrapment (Figure 2).48

Figure 12A through C illustrate the variations in organic acid concentrations relative to buried depth across Jurassic reservoirs, as well as within the Permian Wuerhe Formation and Lucaogou Formation clastic reservoir rocks located in Fukang Sag. These Jurassic reservoirs comprise a combination of sandstone, shale, and coal formations. The Badaowan Formation serves as a primary hydrocarbon source rock at an early stage of maturation characterized by high levels of organic material; under thermal stress conditions, it yields liquid hydrocarbons alongside significant production of organic acids, resulting in an initial peak depicted in Figure 12A. In the Permian source rock within these reservoirs, substantial quantities of organic acids are generated during thermal cracking and oxidative decomposition processes. These acids can be effectively preserved within P3w sand-gravel reservoirs, leading to a “single peak” trend of increasing then decreasing organic acid concentrations with depth, as shown in Figure 12B. The Lucaogou Formation (P2l) exhibits an integrated source-reservoir structure where mud/shale interbeds contain high levels of mature to highly mature stages of organic matter. Its notable hydrocarbon generation efficiency demonstrates a clear positive correlation between organic acid production and maturity level (Figure 13). In ultradeep geological environments, thermal maturation processes generate significant amounts of organic acids that dissolve into formation water due to their strong hydrophilic nature. They subsequently undergo dissolution–chemical reactions with adjacent clastic reservoir rocks, consuming large quantities and leading to a gradual decrease in their concentration with depth (Figure 12C). In conclusion, various geological factors including source rock thermal evolution, chemical properties of reservoir rocks and mechanisms for migration and consumption contribute collectively to determine distribution characteristics and storage performance of organic acids within deep or ultradeep clastic reservoirs.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively investigates the origins, compositions, distribution characteristics, and effects of organic acids on pore evolution in the deep and ultradeep clastic reservoirs of Fukang Sag. It emphasizes the critical role of organic acids in the formation and development of deep and ultradeep hydrocarbon resources.1The organic acids present in these reservoir rocks primarily consist of monocarboxylic acids (formate and acetate) and dicarboxylic acids (oxalate and succinate), with significantly higher concentrations of monocarboxylic acids compared to dicarboxylic acids. This finding enhances our fundamental understanding of the chemical characteristics of organic acids in tight reservoirs and has important implications for subsequent research on hydrocarbon accumulation.2The development of secondary dissolution pores in tight clastic reservoir rocks within Fukang Sag exhibits regional variability and is positively correlated with the composition and concentration of organic acids. As buried depth increases, the effective porosity demonstrates a trend of initially increasing and subsequently gradually decreasing in conjunction with organic acids, thereby further substantiating the significant influence of organic acid dissolution on the evolution of reservoir porosity.This trend not only reflects the generation and preservation of organic acids in clastic reservoir rocks but also indicates that a geological environment characterized by high pressure and high temperature is most conducive to organic acid generation.3Multiple factors, including buried depth, formation pressure, and formation temperature, collectively influence the generation, migration, and preservation of organic acids in deep and ultradeep tight clastic reservoir rocks. These combined effects determine the porosity and permeability of tight clastic reservoirs, ultimately affecting hydrocarbon accumulation and flow. Taking these geological influences into account during the assessment and development of tight hydrocarbon reservoirs can yield more accurate predictions of reservoir quality while offering a novel perspective for the efficient exploration and development of ultradeep hydrocarbon resources.
