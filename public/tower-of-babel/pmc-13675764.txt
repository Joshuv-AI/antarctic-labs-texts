Comparative multicenter evaluation of thalamic neuromodulation for treatment‐resistant epilepsy in children

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUse of neuromodulation strategies targeting thalamic nuclei, including deep brain stimulation (DBS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS), for treatment of pediatric drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) is increasing, despite limited evidence for efficacy and safety. We present the initial results from the Comparative Multicenter Evaluation of Thalamic Neuromodulation for Treatment‐Resistant Epilepsy in Children consortium, which was created to study thalamic modulation in children with DRE.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of children who underwent thalamic DBS or RNS for DRE between January 2015 and December 2024 across 12 centers. Primary outcomes were percent seizure reduction, Engel class, and responder rates (>50% seizure reduction) at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included antiseizure medications and surgical complications. Differences were quantified using ordinal logistic regression, generalized estimating equations with center clustering, and mixed‐effects models. Stratified analyses were performed by thalamic target (centromedian vs. anterior nucleus) and epilepsy diagnosis (Lennox–Gastaut syndrome, generalized, focal, and multifocal).ResultsAmong the 221 included patients (111 DBS, 110 RNS) included, DBS and RNS achieved comparable Engel scores and 12‐month responder rates, but different temporal trajectories were observed. There was a significant device × time interaction (odds ratio = 1.10/month, 95% confidence interval = 1.01–1.20, p = .04), with RNS response rates increasing from 26.9% at 3 months to 55.6% at 12 months (paired p < .001) but with DBS response rates reaching 41.7% at 3 months and remaining comparatively stable over time. DBS achieved significantly higher 12‐month seizure freedom (9.5% vs. 1.2%, p = .03). Neither thalamic target selection nor diagnosis predicted seizure outcome. Safety profiles were equivalent.SignificanceThis multicenter analysis provides pediatric‐specific data to inform surgical indications, device selection, and preoperative counseling. DBS and RNS were effective for treatment of pediatric DRE, with equivalent 12‐month seizure outcomes but different temporal trajectories. DBS provided earlier seizure control and higher rates of overall seizure freedom.Keywords: deep brain stimulation, drug‐resistant epilepsy, neuromodulation, pediatric, responsive neurostimulation

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is the most common serious neurological disorder of childhood, with approximately 25% of individuals progressing to drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined as failure of at least two appropriately selected antiseizure medications. 1 , 2 , 3 For children with DRE, resective surgery remains the gold standard because it may be curative; however, it is often not feasible in children with eloquent cortex involvement or multifocal/generalized onset. 4 , 5 Consequently, neuromodulation strategies, including deep brain stimulation (DBS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS), have emerged as important therapeutic alternatives, presenting evolving treatment options for pediatric patients with DRE. Thalamic neuromodulation is increasingly used for generalized and multifocal seizure disorders, although data remain limited regarding the safety and efficacy of this approach in children. 6

The thalamus is a critical integrative hub that mediates reciprocal corticosubcortical connections and facilitates both seizure propagation and termination. 7 , 8 The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) and centromedian nucleus (CM) have become principal targets for neuromodulation in epilepsy. 9 The ANT modulates limbic and temporal lobe seizure networks, and the efficacy of ANT neurostimulation for DRE is supported by the landmark SANTE trial, which demonstrated significant seizure reduction with open‐loop DBS in adults with focal epilepsy. 10 , 11 Stimulation of the CM, an intralaminar nucleus with broad cortical and subcortical projections, has shown particular promise for generalized epilepsy syndromes, as demonstrated by the ESTEL trial for Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS). 9 , 12 DBS delivers continuous, open‐loop stimulation to modulate network excitability, whereas RNS employs a closed‐loop system that is thought to detect aberrant electrographic activity and deliver targeted stimulation in real time. 13 , 14 Although randomized trials have focused on adult populations, their results have supported off‐label applications in children with DRE, 15 , 16 and a single partially randomized trial showed that the addition of DBS to optimally managed patients with vagus nerve stimulation results in better seizure control in pediatric populations. 17

Although several systematic reviews have supported the safety and feasibility of thalamic neuromodulation in children, the existing literature comprises small, retrospective cohorts with heterogeneous targets, devices, and outcome measures. 15 , 16 , 18 There is a lack of published data comparing DBS and RNS for pediatric thalamic neuromodulation, and no multicenter dataset has been assembled to evaluate these therapies across diverse institutional practices and patient populations. 19 As a result, surgeon experience, institutional practice patterns, and patient preference determine device selection rather than comparative evidence. To address this gap, we present a multicenter comparison of thalamic DBS and RNS for pediatric DRE from the Comparative Evaluation of Neuromodulation for Treatment‐Resistant Epilepsy (CENTRE) consortium. Our aim was to provide a systematic multicenter comparison of thalamic DBS and RNS in pediatric DRE, characterizing seizure outcomes, temporal trajectories of response, and adverse events across the two modalities, to inform shared decision‐making and establish equipoise for prospective comparative trials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This multicenter retrospective cohort study included patients from 12 pediatric epilepsy centers across North America. Specific inclusion criteria were age younger than 21 years at the time of device implantation, a diagnosis of DRE or refractory focal epilepsy as defined by International League Against Epilepsy criteria, 1 and surgical thalamic DBS or RNS device implantation for epilepsy between January 2015 and December 2024. Any patients who had a lead explanted during the follow‐up period were excluded. Institutional review board approval was obtained independently at each participating site with a waiver of consent. This paper was prepared in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. 20

Data collection

Clinical data from electronic health records were collected retrospectively using the ADAPT (A Database of Pediatric Thalamic Neuromodulation) REDCap database, which was managed at the University of Utah and had an established data use agreement at each participating site. As a retrospective cohort study, target and device selection were at the discretion of multidisciplinary teams at each individual center. Variables included patient demographics (age at epilepsy onset, age at implantation, sex), epilepsy characteristics (diagnosis, seizure semiology, 21 preoperative electroencephalographic findings, number of preoperative antiseizure medications), surgical history (prior intracranial monitoring, prior epilepsy surgery), and surgical details (device type, thalamic target, implantation technique, laterality, number of leads). Epilepsy diagnoses were classified as LGS/LGS‐like, focal, multifocal, or other generalized epilepsy. Thalamic targets included the CM, ANT, and pulvinar. Seizure outcomes and antiseizure medication changes were recorded at 3, 6, and 12 months after implantation. Safety data, including intraoperative complications, hospital length of stay, and unplanned return to the operating room, were collected for all patients.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were (1) seizure reduction category, measured on a four‐level ordinal scale: <50% reduction, 50%–75% reduction, >75%–99% reduction, and seizure freedom; (2) Engel classification; and (3) responder rates at 3, 6, and 12 months after implantation. Seizure reduction categories were determined by the treating clinician at each follow‐up visit based on caregiver‐reported seizure frequency relative to the preimplantation baseline, consistent with outcome reporting in prior neuromodulation trials. 10 , 12 Responder rate was defined as the proportion of patients achieving at least a 50% reduction in seizures to index clinically meaningful responses. 22 Seizure freedom (0 seizures since previous time point), change in antiseizure medication burden, and the occurrence of surgical complications, including intraoperative adverse events and return to the operating room, were assessed as secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were compared between device groups using the Mann–Whitney U‐test and reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher exact test (n < 5), depending on the sample size. The primary analysis compared seizure‐reduction categories between DBS and RNS at each postimplantation time point using an ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds model), with device type as the primary predictor. The proportional odds assumption was verified using the Brant test and was satisfied across all time points and model specifications. Adjusted models included thalamic target, epilepsy diagnosis, age at modulation, number of antiseizure medications, baseline seizure frequency, prior vagus nerve stimulation, prior resective surgery, prior intracranial electroencephalographic (EEG) monitoring, baseline focal motor clonic seizures, and treating site as covariates.

To account for within‐center correlations arising from the multicenter design, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure were used for the binary responder outcome, with center as the clustering variable. 10 , 23 The exchangeable structure was selected based on the assumption that outcomes within a given center are equally correlated regardless of the temporal ordering of implantations. Mixed‐effects logistic models with random intercept for center were also fitted, with both approaches yielding concordant results, and GEE reported as the primary clustered analysis. The longitudinal trajectory of treatment response was evaluated using a repeated‐measures GEE model with observations at 3, 6, and 12 months, with patients as the clustering unit and an exchangeable correlation structure. A device × time interaction term was included to test whether the temporal trajectory of response differed between DBS and RNS. Within‐device temporal changes were assessed using McNemar test for the binary responder outcome and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for ordinal seizure reduction category in patients with data available at both 3 and 12 months.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed stratified by thalamic target (CM, ANT) and epilepsy diagnosis (LGS/LGS‐like, focal, multifocal, or other generalized). An exploratory subgroup analysis stratified by seizure onset location on preoperative EEG (focal, multifocal, or generalized) was performed in a sensitivity manner. A device × target interaction term was tested in the ordinal logistic model to evaluate whether device effects varied by thalamic target. All subgroup analyses were considered exploratory. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each subgroup family (thalamic target or epilepsy diagnosis) to account for multiple comparisons. Both nominal and FDR‐corrected p‐values are reported for subgroup analyses. Complete‐case analysis was used at each time point, with the pattern and extent of missing data reported. Notably, differential missingness was observed at the 3‐month time point, with a higher proportion of missing data in the DBS group compared with the RNS group. Differential missingness may reflect expected differences in postoperative follow‐up cadence between devices, with RNS patients typically returning earlier for initial device interrogation. As a sensitivity analysis for missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20, proportional‐odds methods for the ordinal seizure reduction outcome) was performed, conditioning on baseline covariates and outcomes at adjacent time points. All tests were two‐sided with a significance threshold of p < .05. Analyses were performed using Python 3.12 and R 4.3.

RESULTS

Patient demographics, baseline characteristics, and safety

A total of 221 patients (111 DBS, 110 RNS) from 12 centers met the inclusion criteria. The mean age at device implantation was 14.7 ± 5.7 years (range = 2.3–20.6) in the DBS group and 15.3 ± 4.2 years (range = 3.2–20.7) in the RNS group (p = .68; Table 1). There were 45 (40.5%) female patients in the DBS cohort and 45 (40.9%) in the RNS cohort (p > .99). Epilepsy diagnosis distribution was comparable between groups (p = .16), with LGS/LGS‐like representing the largest category (35.7%), followed by other generalized epilepsy (21.7%), multifocal (19.5%), and focal (20.4%). There were significantly more focal motor clonic seizures in the DBS group (52.3% vs. 30.0%, p < .001), whereas the rates of other seizure types were similar between the two groups.

Overall seizure outcomes

Availability of outcome data varied by time point (Table S1). At 3 months, data were available for 165 patients (72/111 DBS [64.9%] and 93/110 RNS [84.5%]), reflecting differential missingness in the DBS group. Data were available for 165 patients at 6 months (82/111 DBS [73.9%] and 83/110 RNS [75.5%]) and for 165 patients at 12 months (84/111 DBS [75.7%] and 81/110 RNS [73.6%]).

At 12 months, responder rates were similar between devices (DBS 50.0% vs. RNS 55.6%, p = .39). Engel class was comparable between groups (DBS median 3 [IQR = 3–4] vs. RNS 3 [IQR = 3–4], p = .69; Table 2). The numbers of patients achieving <50%, 50%–75%, >75%–99%, or 100% seizure reduction with DBS and RNS are shown in Table S2. Ordinal logistic regression demonstrated no significant difference in seizure reduction category at 12 months (odds ratio [OR] = .71, 95% confidence interval [CI] = .35–2.43, p = .49; Table 3).

Temporal trajectories

Despite equivalent 12‐month outcomes, DBS and RNS exhibited significantly different temporal trajectories of treatment response (Figure 1A). DBS demonstrated proportionally earlier seizure reduction, with a 3‐month responder rate of 41.7% compared with 26.9% for RNS (Table 2). Although the DBS responder rate remained relatively stable across follow‐up (Table 2; 41.7% to 50.0%, paired McNemar p = .13, Wilcoxon signed‐rank p = .15), RNS was slower to achieve a responder status, with responder rates increasing from 26.9% at 3 months to 55.6% at 12 months. Paired analysis among 73 RNS patients with data at both time points confirmed the trajectory; 26 patients gained responder status, whereas only one responder became a nonresponder (McNemar p < .001), with a significant shift in response category (Wilcoxon p < .001; Table 2). The longitudinal GEE model with a device × time interaction confirmed the rate of improvement differed significantly between devices (OR = 1.10 per month, 95% CI = 1.01–1.20, p = .04; Table S3). These findings were supported by multiple imputation under the missing at random method (pooled OR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.05–1.25, p = .002; Table S4). Seizure freedom at 12 months (0 seizures since 6‐month follow‐up appointment) was achieved in 9.5% of DBS patients compared with 1.2% of RNS patients (p = .03; Figure 1B). In the adjusted longitudinal model, ANT targeting was associated with lower odds of responder status compared with CM targeting (OR = .43, 95% CI = .19–.97, p = .04; Table 3).

Outcomes by thalamic target

In the CM subgroup (75 DBS, 77 RNS; with outcome data available for 40–59 DBS and 56–63 RNS patients across time points), responder rates were 46.0% for DBS and 25.4% for RNS at 3 months (p = .02), with rates converging by 12 months (47.5% vs. 56.9%, p = .31; Table S5). Seizure freedom was 8.5% at 12 months after CM‐targeted DBS and 1.7% after CM‐targeted RNS (p = .21). The device × target interaction was not significant (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = .25–4.93, p = .88; Table S3). In the ANT subgroup (34 DBS, 17 RNS; with outcome data available for 22–24 DBS and 11–16 RNS patients across time points), responder rates were equivalent between DBS and RNS across all time points (3 months 31.8% vs. 31.2%, 6 months 34.8% vs. 35.7%, 12 months 54.2% vs. 54.5%, all p > .99), although interpretation is limited by the small number of ANT‐targeted RNS patients with outcome data (Table S4). The pulvinar was targeted exclusively with RNS (n = 10, from three centers), predominantly for multifocal epilepsy. Given the small sample size, outcomes are reported descriptively; responder rates were 25.0% at 3 months (2/8), 66.7% at 6 months (4/6), and 40.0% at 12 months (2/5). No patients who underwent pulvinar‐targeted RNS achieved seizure freedom. No target subgroup comparison remained significant after FDR correction (Table S6).

Outcomes by epilepsy diagnosis

Exploratory subgroup analyses of responder rates stratified by epilepsy diagnosis revealed no significant difference between devices for LGS/LGS‐like (DBS 53.1% vs. RNS 50.0%, p > .99), multifocal epilepsy (DBS 52.9% vs. RNS 53.8%, p > .99), focal epilepsy (DBS 64.3% vs. RNS 55.6%, p = .72), or other generalized epilepsy (DBS 33.3% vs. 68.4%, p = .06) at 12 months (Figure 2, Table S6). Among patients with other generalized epilepsy, RNS demonstrated a nominally higher 12‐month responder rate than DBS; however, this did not survive correction for multiple comparisons (Benjamini–Hochberg q = .22).

Outcomes by seizure onset location

In an exploratory analysis prompted by the distinct connectivity profiles of the anterior and centromedian thalamic nuclei, we stratified patients by predominant seizure onset location on preoperative EEG (focal, multifocal, or generalized) and restricted the analysis to 150 patients with a pure category designation. No statistically significant onset × cohort interactions were detected. Relative to focal onset, the RNS versus DBS effect did not differ significantly for multifocal onset (OR = 1.52, 95% CI = .26–8.99, p = .65) or generalized onset (OR = 2.73, 95% CI = .44–17.05, p = .28; Table S7).

Model concordance

All modeling approaches (ordinal logistic regression with site as a fixed effect, GEE clustered by center, and mixed‐effects logistic regression with random intercept for site) yielded concordant conclusions, with all point estimates near 1 and no statistically significant 12‐month differences between devices (Table 3, Figure 3, Table S3).

DISCUSSION

This study from the CENTRE consortium provides a large multicenter comparison of DBS and RNS for pediatric DRE and refractory focal epilepsy, encompassing 221 patients across 12 centers. Within this cohort, three principal findings emerged. First, both devices demonstrated favorable safety profiles, with similarly low rates of intraoperative complications, return to the operating room, and short hospital length of stay. Second, DBS and RNS achieved similar overall seizure reduction at 12 months, with responder rates of 50.0% and 56.6%, respectively, but exhibited distinct temporal trajectories; DBS provided earlier seizure reduction that plateaued over time, whereas RNS showed slower reduction rates between 3 and 12 months (device × time interaction, p = .04). Third, DBS was associated with a higher rate of seizure freedom at 12 months (9.5% vs. 1.2%, p = .03). Taken together, these findings support the safety and efficacy of RNS and DBS thalamic neuromodulation for DRE in this patient population, while highlighting temporal and seizure freedom differences that may inform device selection and family counseling.

Temporal divergence

The underlying mechanisms of DBS and RNS may explain the divergent temporal trajectories we observed between these devices. DBS uses open‐loop stimulation to modulate thalamic relay circuits. 14 This seemingly instantaneous “on” state may explain the earlier onset of seizure reduction observed in our cohort. Although DBS programming parameters, including frequency, pulse width, and voltage, are iteratively adjusted over the initial weeks to months after implantation, this optimization period is considerably shorter than the RNS titration cycle, which involves both detection signal refinement and stimulation parameter adjustment, allowing it to operate as a closed‐loop system that relies on detecting abnormal electrocorticographic activity and requires titration of target doses. 13 , 14 Consistent with this mechanistic framework, we observed that RNS activation occurred a median of 9 days later than DBS activation (35 vs. 26 days, p = .01), reflecting the additional time required for detection algorithm optimization. However, this 9‐day offset is modest relative to the 12‐month follow‐up window and may not fully account for the sustained divergence in trajectory. Continued improvement in postoperative seizure outcomes with RNS has previously been reported by Nair et al., 24 who described median seizure reduction rates increasing from 44% to 75% over 9 years of follow‐up in adults with focal epilepsy treated with cortical RNS, with responder rates continuing to improve beyond 5 years. Chronic neurostimulation with RNS (and likely also DBS) may induce corticothalamocortical plasticity, progressively remodeling the epileptic network. 25 , 26 Repeated interruption of seizure propagation is also thought to produce an antikindling effect, which gradually raises the seizure threshold over time. 27 The relative plateau in the DBS response rate may indicate that continuous stimulation achieves maximal network modulation earlier after device implantation. Whether DBS or RNS outcomes continue to improve beyond 12 months in children, as adult data suggest, 24 is the basis of ongoing study in the CENTRE consortium.

Seizure freedom

In comparison with RNS, DBS was associated with greater seizure freedom at 12 months; however, in view of the relatively small subset of patients who achieved seizure freedom (eight DBS vs. one RNS patient), this finding should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Our 12‐month DBS seizure freedom rate (9.5%) is consistent with that observed in the systematic review by Yan et al. (12.5%), 15 is slightly lower than that seen in the SANTE long‐term data (16% at 5 years), 11 and exceeds the pooled estimates from the recent 2025 meta‐analysis by Dhaliwal et al. (ANT 3.57%, CM 1.32%). 28 Although these comparisons are limited by differences in follow‐up duration, outcome definitions, and population characteristics, several mechanistic considerations may explain the differential seizure freedom rates between devices. The higher rate of seizure freedom with DBS may reflect fundamental differences between the neuromodulation techniques. Continuous thalamic stimulation produces sustained disruption of corticothalamic relay circuits, potentially achieving a threshold of network inhibition sufficient to fully suppress seizure generation. Bernstein et al. 29 recently described the long‐term effects of continuous ANT stimulation, demonstrating that seizure freedom may be maintained for years in generalized epilepsy, perhaps suggesting that tonic modulation of thalamic nodes may produce a durable suppressive effect on seizure networks. Further, as Samanta et al. 30 discussed in their review of neuromodulation for developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, CM‐targeted DBS exerts broad modulatory effects across multiple seizure types by continuously engaging thalamocortical circuits. In contrast, responsive event‐triggered stimulation may not replicate this effect in the interictal period. Whether longer follow‐up narrows this gap remains an open question.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses by thalamic target and epilepsy diagnosis, although exploratory, yielded several findings that may direct future device selection and investigation. Within the CM subgroup, which comprised the majority of patients, no statistically significant difference in responder rate was observed between devices at any time point, although a numerically higher early response was noted for DBS at 3 months (46.0% vs. 25.4%, p = .02). This descriptive pattern converged by 12 months, and the device × target interaction was not significant (p = .88), confirming that the overall temporal trajectory finding was likely not driven by differences in target distribution between devices. In the adjusted longitudinal model, ANT targeting was associated with lower odds of responder status compared with CM targeting; however, crude responder rates were similar across targets, and this adjusted signal was most likely driven by within‐site differences likely reflecting residual confounding by indication. Yet, this possible early CM‐targeted DBS effect is consistent with the established efficacy of CM stimulation for generalized seizure types and with short‐term improvements reported in the ESTEL trial. 12 , 31 This pattern reproduces the overall trajectory finding at the target level, indicating that the divergent temporal profiles are likely not caused by differences in target distribution between devices. In the ANT subgroup, device outcomes were equivalent across all time points, although the small number of ANT‐targeted RNS patients (n = 11–16 with outcome data) limits the reliability of this comparison. Nevertheless, this finding aligns with the wider body of research. Talbot et al. 32 reported no significant differences in seizure reduction between ANT‐targeted DBS and RNS in a recent meta‐analysis. Similarly, in a meta‐analysis of pediatric thalamic neuromodulation involving 88 patients, Sharma et al. 33 found no significant association between target or modality and treatment response.

Among the exploratory subgroup analyses, a numerically higher RNS responder rate was observed in other generalized epilepsy (68.4% vs. 33.3%, p = .06, FDR‐corrected q = .22), which did not reach statistical significance. Prior single‐center comparisons have generally found no difference in neuromodulation effectiveness between focal and generalized epilepsy or between open‐loop and closed‐loop paradigms. 34 Although the present study was underpowered to detect subgroup differences, this relationship should be examined further in larger prospective cohorts. The hypothesis aligns with emerging evidence that CM‐targeted RNS may be particularly effective for generalized and multifocal epilepsy syndromes. Nanda et al. 35 reported an average seizure reduction of 82.6% and an 18% Engel class I rate with CM‐targeted RNS in patients with idiopathic generalized and rapidly generalizing epilepsy, and pediatric‐specific series from Bercu et al. 36 and Speakes et al. 37 have reported responder rates of 83% and 71%, respectively, in children with generalized DRE treated with CM‐targeted RNS. However, in a recent review, Hsu et al. 38 noted that no modality has demonstrated clear superiority, and our subgroup findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating. Our exploratory analysis, stratified by EEG‐defined seizure onset location, likewise did not detect significant onset × cohort interactions, although cell sizes were modest and CIs were wide. Whether anterior versus centromedial thalamic targeting confers differential benefit by seizure onset location merits dedicated prospective evaluation. The pulvinar, targeted exclusively with RNS in our cohort (n = 10), demonstrated variable responder rates (25%–67% across time points) without achieving seizure freedom, resulting, most likely, from the small sample size, which precluded meaningful comparison. Emerging interest in pulvinar stimulation for posterior quadrant epilepsy 39 warrants further dedicated study.

Device selection: What is the answer?

Device selection across the CENTRE consortium was influenced by a range of factors beyond individual patient characteristics, including local device availability, surgeon training and familiarity, programming infrastructure, and payer coverage. Although we adjusted for treating site as a fixed effect, we cannot fully rule out time‐varying site‐level effects or device × site interactions that are not captured by mean site differences. However, it is plausible that DBS may be advantageous when rapid response is clinically important, such as in the setting of a high seizure burden, in status‐prone phenotypes, or when minimizing programming visits is a priority. RNS, with its closed‐loop responsive paradigm and on‐device electrocorticography, may offer particular value when chronic electrocorticographic data are clinically useful or when localized seizure onset is well defined but unresectable. Yet, because subgroup analysis did not survive FDR correction, prospective data are needed before more specific device recommendations can be made.

Limitations

Our study was retrospective and nonrandomized, and factors such as surgeon preference, institutional experience, and unmeasured patient characteristics may have affected device choice. Seizure outcomes were assessed using clinician‐determined reduction categories based on caregiver report rather than prospective seizure diaries, introducing potential recall and classification bias. Differential missingness was observed at 3 months (DBS 35.1% vs. RNS 15.5%), which may bias early time point comparisons. This asymmetry resolved by 12 months, and sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation under the missing at random method supported the primary device × time interaction. However, severely informative missingness that depends on unobserved outcome values cannot be fully ruled out. Follow‐up was limited to 12 months, which may have been insufficient to capture the full trajectory of either device, given that the adult data demonstrate continued improvement over years to decades. 24 Patients who had their device explanted before enrollment were explicitly excluded, and this group warrants further evaluation. We were unable to account for differences in surgical targeting, programming strategies, stimulation parameters, or detection algorithm optimization across the 12 participating centers, all of which may influence outcomes independently of device type. Stimulation‐related and behavioral side effects, including mood changes, were not systematically captured in the retrospective dataset and may be underreported. Prospective registries with standardized outcome capture, including patient‐reported and neuropsychological measures, will be necessary to address these limitations and establish definitive comparative guidance.

Future directions

These findings underscore the need for prospective, comparative studies of thalamic neuromodulation in pediatric patients with DRE. The CENTRE consortium is currently collecting 2‐year follow‐up data from the ADAPT database to further characterize seizure responses between DBS and RNS. Several other critical clinical questions remain. Prospective registries with standardized outcome measures and seizure diaries would address the limitations inherent in retrospective caregiver‐reported data. We will also continue to collect patient data through the ADAPT database to achieve more detailed subgroup analyses. Finally, integrating electrophysiologic biomarkers into prospective trial designs may enable early prediction of treatment response and individualized device selection, moving the field beyond empiric choice toward precision neuromodulation.

CONCLUSIONS

This large multicenter study from the CENTRE consortium indicated that thalamic DBS and RNS may offer similar seizure reduction in children with DRE, although their effects appear to unfold differently over time. DBS tended to produce more rapid benefits and a higher rate of seizure freedom, whereas RNS showed notable improvements gradually over the first year. These results support choosing the device based on individual clinical needs and epilepsy features, and they emphasize the importance of prospective multicenter registries with standardized outcomes and long‐term follow‐up to inform treatment for pediatric patients with DRE.
