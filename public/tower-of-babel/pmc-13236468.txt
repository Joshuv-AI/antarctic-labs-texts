Basic fibroblast growth factor preserves rabbit adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells in serum-free culture
Authors: Suryo Kuncorojakti, Ahmad Aswin, Diyantoro Diyantoro, Helen Susilowati, Ira Sari Yudaniayanti, Lina Susanti, Watchareewan Rodprasert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceFetal bovine serum (FBS) is widely used for mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) expansion but carries risks of contamination, batch variability, and xenogeneic exposure that can compromise reproducibility and downstream therapeutic use.ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate a rabbit adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cell (Rab-ADMSC) serum-free medium (SFM) supplemented with growth factors that maintains key MSC characteristics.MethodsRab-ADMSCs were isolated from 3 male New Zealand White rabbits. Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), transforming growth factor-β1, and insulin-like growth factor-1 were screened for support of cell viability in SFM. Cells were then cultured in α-Minimum Essential Medium with 10% FBS or in SFM supplemented with bFGF and assessed for morphology, proliferation, expression of stemness- and senescence-associated transcripts, surface marker expression, and tri-lineage differentiation potential.ResultsSFM supplemented with bFGF preserved fibroblast-like morphology and supported Rab-ADMSC expansion. Transcript levels of stemness-associated markers and senescence-associated markers were comparable between culture conditions. Rab-ADMSCs retained osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation potential. Flow cytometry showed higher expression of CD81, CD49f, and CD29 in the SFM plus bFGF condition, whereas CD34 and CD45 remained low in both conditions.Conclusions and RelevanceA tailored SFM supplemented with bFGF supports Rab-ADMSC growth while maintaining core MSC phenotypic and functional characteristics, supporting its potential use for more standardized MSC manufacturing in veterinary regenerative applications.Keywords: Animals, adipose tissue, cell culture techniques, serum-free media, fibroblast growth factors

INTRODUCTION

Stem cell-based therapy plays an important role in regenerative medicine as a new therapeutic modality due to its potential to treat intractable diseases. The goal of this approach is to repair damaged tissues or cells by maintaining the cell pool and/or stimulating, regulating, and modulating endogenous stem cell populations in order to promote tissue homeostasis and regeneration [1]. The use of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) has garnered substantial attention over the past 30 years because of their remarkable cellular biology, extensive therapeutic potential, and foundational role in the advancement of new regenerative-based therapeutic modalities [2]. MSC-based therapies have been widely investigated in numerous studies to treat various diseases, such as cardiovascular [3], renal [4], and metabolic diseases [5,6], as well as immunological disorders [7] and wound healing [8,9]. Despite the growing body of research on MSCs, most studies have focused on human-derived MSCs. In contrast, investigations into the animal-derived MSCs remain limited, especially the information regarding the availability of specialized serum-free media (SFMs) for animal-derived MSCs. Many studies on stem cell developments involving animal-derived MSCs still rely on serum supplementation. The use of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in traditional MSC culture techniques has been standard practice because it promotes cell attachment and provides vital nutrients and growth factors [10]. However, the use of FBS presents several concerns, including contamination by bacteria, viruses, and prions, as well as batch-to-batch variability and xeno-contamination [11]. In response to these challenges, there is growing consensus, particularly in the biopharmaceutical industries, on the need to implement serum-free (SF) culture systems with the objective of reducing variability, improving traceability, and eventually establishing a controlled pipeline as a vital requirement for regulatory approval [12].

Rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) are frequently used as animal models in both human and veterinary research due to their size, ease of breeding, and inexpensive cost of maintenance and feed. In addition, rabbits are phylogenetically closer to primates than rodents, and despite being larger than mice, they have a shorter gestation period, allowing for their utilization in studies requiring a large number of animals [13]. As animal models, rabbits are the third most popular mammal model in animal experiments (3.12%) in the European Union, following mice (60.9%) and rats (13.9%). However, in comparison to other widely used models such mice, key characteristics of rabbits, such as their immunogenetics and MSC characterization, remain underexplored [14]. Previous studies have documented species-related variability in the phenotype, surface marker, and gene expression of MSCs [15,16]. It has been proven that donor characteristics and anatomical sources affect the characteristics and properties of MSCs [17]. According to Calle et al. [14], rabbit MSCs derived from various tissues did not vary in their morphological characteristics, differentiation potential, and MSC surface marker expression. Nonetheless, other studies have reported different phenotypes among rabbit MSCs [18].

Despite the fact that MSCs can be isolated from any postnatal tissues, they are usually obtained from bone marrow or adipose tissue because of their relative ease of isolation. Because the quantity of MSCs is limited in adult tissues, they are usually cultured to obtain an adequate quantity [19]. MSC heterogeneity is significantly influenced by differences in isolation or culture techniques, including culture reagents, containers, and environmental conditions. Studies have reported a number of media that may be used to isolate and cultivate human or animal MSCs from bone marrow and adipose tissue. These commonly include Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium and Minimum Essential Medium, which are enhanced with 10%–20% (v/v) FBS. FBS provides a variety of nutrients, including growth and attachment factors. However, even when FBS are sourced from the same supplier, the concentrations of these nutrients and components might differ significantly between batches. Therefore, using FBS with unidentified components from different batches may contribute to the variability of MSCs both in quantity and quality [20]. Although this might not be an issue from an academic perspective, batch-to-batch uniformity in the quality of MSCs is crucial for regulatory purposes during the manufacturing process. Additionally, several growth factors present in FBS can induce stem cell proliferation and differentiation [12,20]. In response to these concerns, the development of SFMs is essential to address some of the drawbacks related to the use of FBS in MSC culture. Although the concept of SF, animal component-free media for human and animal MSCs to eliminate the variability associated with FBS is not news, the effectiveness of such formulations is influenced by their composition [21]. Similarly, SFMs developed for human or rodent MSCs to cultivate canine MSCs frequently yield inconsistent outcomes [20,22]. These discrepancies further indicate that animal-derived MSCs may require species-specific nutrients or growth stimulants to support optimal growth. To date, studies on the development of SFMs for rabbit MSCs remain limited. Therefore, this study is the first attempt to formulate a customized SFM for rabbit MSCs. The findings of this study are expected to support future studies utilizing rabbit MSCs as an animal model, thereby accelerating the translation of MSC-based therapies to clinical application in both veterinary and human medicine.

METHODS

Animals and cell culture

Male New Zealand White rabbits (n = 3) aged two months and weighing approximately 2 kg were used as donors. Before tissue collection, the rabbits were anesthetized with a combination of 25 mg/kg of body weight (BW) of ketamin (Ketamil-Ilium; Troy Laboratories, Australia) and 3 mg/kg BW of xylazine (Interchemie, The Netherlands) intramuscularly. Subcutaneous adipose tissues were collected aseptically from the interscapular region. Rabbit adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (Rab-ADMSCs) were then isolated following previously established protocols [23,24]. Briefly, the adipose tissue was dissociated both mechanically and enzymatically using TrypLE Express (Gibco, UK) under constant stirring at 37oC for 1 h. The resulting single cell suspension was seeded into T25 culture flasks (Nest, China) and incubated at 37oC in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. For conventional culture, the α-Minimum Essential Medium (αMEM) (Gibco) was used, supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% amphotericin B (all from Gibco). The culture medium was replaced every 48 h, and cells were sub-cultured upon reaching 80% confluency. Cells at passages 4 to 6 were used for subsequent experiments. The SFMs consisted of αMEM (Gibco). supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), 1% amphotericin B (Gibco), 4 g/L bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 0.5 g/L fetuin (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 1x GlutaMAX (Gibco), 1x insulin-transferrin-sodium selenite (ITS) (Gibco), 200 mM mg/mL L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) and growth factors (type and concentration) were added as indicated below. Cell morphology was evaluated microscopically using an inverted microscope (Invitrogen EVOS XL Core Configured Cell Imager; Invitrogen, USA).

Growth factor screening study

Three different growth factors were screen in this study to determine the most suitable growth factor for Rab-ADMSC cultivation medium. Briefly, SFM as formulated previously was supplemented with 2 ng/mL of human basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (Gibco), 1 ng/mL of human transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) (BioLegend, USA), and 10 ng/mL human insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) (BioLegend). The growth factor supplementation was both individually and in various combinations (double and triple). To evaluate the effects of these growth factors in Rab-ADMSC, the cells at passage 4 were seeded into 96-well plates (Nest) at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well using conventional medium (FBS-supplemented). After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with SFM supplemented with growth factors (single, double, and triple combinations). The evaluation was conducted after 48 h of incubation by assessing the cell mitochondrial activity using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The MTT assay was performed following previously established protocols [23].

Phenotypic evaluation of mesenchymal stem cell surface markers

The phenotypic characterization of Rab-ADMSCs at passage 6 (after two consecutive passage was maintained in SFM/bFGF) was conducted using an Attune CytPix Flow Cytometer (Invitrogen) to evaluate the expression of MSC surface markers against specific antibodies: CD81, CD9, CD49f, CD29, CD34 and CD45. Detailed information on the antibodies used in this study is provided in Table 1. The isotype antibodies were included as negative controls. All procedures were performed in accordance with previously established protocols [23,24].

Tri-lineage differentiation study

To evaluate the differentiation potential of Rab-ADMSC into osteogenic, adipogenic and chondrogenic lineages, the cells at passage 6 (after two consecutive passage was maintained in SFM/bFGF) were seeded and cultured in two different media, namely αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (αMEM/10%FBS) and SFM supplemented with growth factor (SFM/bFGF), followed by customized induction media. The osteogenic medium consisted of the basal medium supplemented with 50 mg/mL of L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 10 mM of β-glycerophosphate (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), and 100 nM of dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The adipogenic induction medium was prepared with 10 mM of isobutyl-methylxanthine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 100 mM of indomethacin (Sigma Aldrich, USA), 1 mM of dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), and 10 μg/mL of insulin (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). Finally, the chondrogenic induction medium was supplemented with 1x ITS (Gibco), 40 mg/mL of L-proline (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 50 mg/mL of L-ascorbic acid-2-phospate (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), 1 ng/mL of TGF-β3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 100 nM of dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The differentiation protocols in this study were adopted from previously established methods [23,24]. The phenotypic evaluation was performed using alizarin red staining for osteogenic induction, oil red O staining for adipogenic induction, and alcian blue staining chondrogenic induction. The gene (messenger RNA [mRNA]) expression was also quantified using reverse transcriptase quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).

RT-qPCR

In this study, the gene expression was quantified using RT-qPCR in accordance with previously established protocols [24]. Rab-ADMSCs were cultured using two different media, namely conventional medium containing αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (αMEM/10%FBS) and SFM supplemented with growth factor (SFM/bFGF). The gene expression was evaluated using a set of primers to detect the stemness-associated markers and senescence-related markers, as well as tri-lineage differentiation markers as previously described [23]. The total RNA was extracted using the RNAsimple Total RNAKit (Tiangen, China), followed by quantification using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA was synthesized by using the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RTSuperMix (Tiangen). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) reactions were carried out using the Forget-Me-NotTM EvaGreen qPCR Master Mix (Biotium, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mRNA expression was determined according to the 2−(∆∆Ct) formula, with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase serving as the housekeeping gene for normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. For growth-factor screening experiments involving > 2 groups, differences were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn multiple-comparisons test. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The p values were two-sided and considered significant at p < 0.05. Different letters above bars indicate statistically significant differences after multiple-comparisons adjustment.

RESULTS

Effect of bFGF on the expansion of Rab-ADMSC

Growth factors play a pivotal role in MSC proliferation, several growth factors were screened to obtained the most suitable growth factor for rabbit ADMSC. This study was initially conducted to evaluate three different growth factors, namely bFGF, TGF-β1 and IGF-1, on the expansion of Rab-ADMSC. The growth factors were supplemented to the SFM either individually or in combination. The results of this study demonstrated that at 48 h post-cultivation, Rab-ADMSC cultured in SMF/bFGF exhibited a higher expansion rate compared to those supplemented with other growth factor combinations (Fig. 1). However, based on the MTT assay evaluation, the expansion rate of the SFM/bFGF group was significantly lower compared to that of the serum-supplemented medium (αMEM/10%FBS). The supplementation with TGF-β1 and IGF-1 in SFM either individually or in combination failed to support the proliferation of Rab-ADMSC. Therefore, SFM supplemented with bFGF alone was selected for further evaluation in this study.

Effect of supplementation of bFGF on morphology, pluripotency of gene expression, and senescence-related markers under serum-free conditions

According to the International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT), microscopic evaluation should be performed to evaluate the characteristic of MSC. The objective of this study is to evaluate the morphology of MSC microscopically. Descriptive microscopic evaluation to asses cell morphology revealed that Rab-ADMSCs cultured in both αMEM/10%FBS and SFM/bFGF exhibited similar characteristics, namely adherent growth with a fibroblastic morphology (Fig. 2A). Gene expression evaluation of stemness-associated markers (NANOG, SOX2, REX1, and NES) between the two groups also showed similar results (Fig. 2B). In addition, mRNA expression levels of senescence-related markers CDKN1A and TP53 were not significantly different between the two groups (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that the supplementation of bFGF alone in SFM could maintain the morphology of Rab-ADMSC as well as the expression of mRNA encoding pluripotency and senescence-related markers.

Effect of supplementation of bFGF on the expression of mesenchymal surface markers under serum-free conditions

Immunophenotyping to assess specific MSC surface markers were performed by using flow cytometry as a gold standard method. The evaluation of the effect of customized SFM (SFM/bFGF) on the expression of MSC surface markers yielded notable results. High levels of CD81 and CD9 protein expression were observed in both groups. However, CD81 expression was significantly higher in SFM/bFGF group compared to the αMEM/10%FBS. Differences in the expression of CD49f and CD29 were also noted between the two groups, with higher levels of expression in the SFM/bFGF group than in the serum-supplemented medium. Both hematopoietic stem cell markers, CD34 and CD45, were not expressed in either group (Fig. 3).

Effect of supplementation of bFGF on the differentiation potential of Rab-ADMSC

To assess the multipotentiality of Rab-ADMSC according to ISCT requirement, tri-lineage differentiation was performed. The cells were cultured in both serum-containing medium and SFM/bFGF, followed by osteogenic, adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation media. Phenotypic evaluation revealed comparable results between the two groups. Following 21 days of osteogenic induction, phenotypic changes were observed, including extensive network with intense red staining indicative of calcium nodule deposits. After 14 days of adipogenic induction, red-stained lipid droplets were also observed, while glycosaminoglycan deposits appeared blue following 21 days of chondrogenic induction (Fig. 4). The gene expression analysis using RT-qPCR demonstrated similar patterns in both αMEM/10%FBS and SFM/bFGF groups. The mRNA expression levels of RUNX2, BMP2, and OPN as osteogenic-related markers were not significantly different (Fig. 5A). Similar results were observed in adipogenic and chondogenic differentiation potential. In particular, the mRNA expression of PPARγ and LPL as well as SOX9 and COL2A1 as adipogenic- and chondrogenic-related markers were not significantly different (Fig. 5B and C). These findings indicate that Rab-ADMSCs that were cultivated in SFMs containing bFGF maintained their multi-lineage differentiation potential.

DISCUSSION

MSCs, a type of adult stem cell, have gained increasing popularity from the scientific community due to their multipotent somatic stem/progenitor cells that may be obtained from a variety of tissues. This is because MSCs have shown considerable promise in treating previously incurable diseases [25]. Traditionally, MSCs are cultured in nutrient-rich media supplemented with biological fluids such as serum or plasma along with salts, carbohydrates, minerals, and vitamins. FBS is the most frequently utilized supplement due to its widespread availability. FBS also provides essential nutrients, growth factors, hormones, and carrier proteins, which collectively promote cell growth by facilitating the uptake and transport of vitamins, lipids, metals, hormones, attachment and spreading factors, protease inhibitors, and buffering agents. However, standardizing the production of an MSC-based therapy is difficult because FBS composition varies significantly between batches [20]. In addition, the supplementation of FBS in conventional MSC cultures can increase the risk of exposure to animal-derived pathogens [25]. To address these limitations, SFMs have been developed to meet all of the requirements for clinical applications. However, due to issues with the availability and expense of specified medium that can maintain cells of different species, the use of SFMs for the production of animal MSCs remains limited. For instance, one study demonstrated that SFM formulations developed for human MSCs yielded inconsistent outcomes when used for canine MSCs [20]. This study indicated that animal MSCs have their own particular nutritional and growth requirements. As a result, there are currently no standardized SFMs formulations specifically designed for animal MSCs.

A significant challenge in stem cell cultivation is to maintain high growth rates without losing cell phenotype and characteristics. The results of this study demonstrated that the cultivation of Rab-ADMSC in SFM could maintain their morphology and proliferation. The elimination of serum from the culture system poses serious consequences for cell growth due to the lack of survival supporting factors needed by the cells. The addition of BSA and fetuin serves as a carrier protein [26] and an attachment factor [27], while the inclusion of glutamine, insulin, transferrin, and selenium promotes cellular growth by providing essential nutrients such as vitamins, hormones, and trace elements. Transferrin is essential as a cofactor for enzymes involved in DNA synthesis and metabolism. Insulin is the hormone that is most commonly added to SFM because it facilitates the synthesis of proteins and nucleic acids, lipogenesis, intracellular transport, and the uptake of glucose and amino acids. In addition, ascorbic acid promotes the growth of human MSCs while maintaining their differentiation potential and cell phenotype [20]. Growth factors play pivotal role for cell proliferation including MSC. The challenge in SFM development for animal MSC is due to the species-specific difference in growth requirements. The approach of this study in mainly focused on the combination of growth factors to support Rab-ADMSC growth. According previous study, bFGF and TGF-β1 can support canine MSC [20], while IGF promote rat MSC [28]. However, the result of this study revealed that supplementation bFGF alone possess a promising result. In this study, the supplementation of bFGF in SFM may serve as a mitogen that can stimulate cell division [29] and maintain the differentiation potential while keeping the MSCs in the undifferentiation stage. Additionally, bFGF can prevent premature differentiation through specific differentiation pathways [30]. Although this study did not use rabbit specific growth factors, our result prove that the human recombinant growth factors can act as specific ligand for rabbit growth factor receptors.

Regarding the mRNA expression of stemness-associated and senescence markers, this study found no statistically significant differences between cells cultured in serum-supplemented media and those in SFM. This may be attributed to the bFGF supplementation in SFM. Notably, in the early stages, bFGF can support the expression of genes associated with stem cell markers, contributing to the maintenance of stemness property. Additionally, bFGF partially inhibits apoptosis by regulating Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 signals through the ERK pathway. This indicates that the vitality and stemness of stem cells are all significantly influenced by bFGF [31]. Although it can increase cell viability, bFGF does not offer complete protection against senescence. Rather, it may delay or mitigate the cellular aging process in MSCs by affecting the expression of genes and proteins associated with senescence. Recent studies have shown that fibroblast growth factors and their receptors are important regulators of both senescence and self-renewal in a variety of stem cell types [32]. Possible mechanisms underlying the protective effect of bFGF against MSC senescence involve its ability to modulate signaling pathways such as p53 and p16 [33,34].

Despite the successful development of customized SFM for Rab-ADMSCs in this study, which can promote cell growth a maintain stemness property as well as inhibiting cellular senescence, important aspects of phenotypic characterizations such MSC surface markers and differentiation potential were also investigated. The ISCT states that the expression of surface markers is one of the essential criteria for characterizing MSCs. Less than 2% of MSC population should express CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a, or CD19, and HLA class II, while more than 95% of MSC population should express CD73, CD90, and CD105 [25]. Unfortunately, standardized MSC surface markers in rabbits remain poorly established. Common human MSC surface markers are expressed inconsistently in rabbit-derived MSCs [14,17,23]. Generally, the expression of surface markers in MSCs grown in SFMs does not significantly differ from those grown in serum-supplemented media. Previous studies have also reported no significant differences in the surface markers of human and canine ADMSCs [20,35]. In contrast, this study demonstrated that Rab-ADMSC cultured in SFM exhibited higher expression of MSC surface markers CD81, CD49f, and CD29. However, the difference in expression of CD9 between the groups was not significantly different. A previous study on human BM-MSCs yielded similar outcomes, indicating that SFM-expanded MSCs exhibited variations in morphology, surface markers, senescence status, and differentiation potential. The mechanisms underlying the varying expression of MSC surface markers in rabbit-derived cells remain unclear, but the presence of adhesion factors in the culture system may contribute to the expression of surface markers and downstream pathways especially in extended culture [12]. During exosome biogenesis, CD81 contributes to exosome budding and release by influencing membrane curvature and the expression CD81 is higher under SFM environment [36]. Under SF condition MSC undergo for compensation mechanism by producing more adhesion factors which may facilitated by CD49f and CD29 [37,38]. Furthermore, this study did not observe variations in the differentiation potential of Rab-ADMSC, indicating that the utilization of rabbit-specialized SFM did not alter tri-lineage differentiation. On the contrary, previous studies have shown that after extended in vitro culture, human and murine MSCs exhibited reduced differentiation potential in SFM, which positively correlated with the senescence status [39,40,41]. Other studies have reported that SF conditions can alter tri-lineage differentiation of MSC by affecting proliferation rate and senescence status [35,42]. Since no significant differences in proliferation capacity and senescence status were observed in this study, this may explain the ability of SFM to maintain tri-lineage differentiation in Rab-ADMSC. To maximize the therapeutic benefits of MSCs, these media formulations may need to be adjusted according to the donor MSCs and their tissue origins, as MSCs respond differently to various SFM formulations. Therefore, determining the most effective SFM for clinical-scale MSC expansion is crucial [43]. However, the long-term perspective to elucidate how many passages the cells can be maintained under SFM was absent in this study, that might become the limitation of this study. The goal setting of this study is focused on developing the specific SFM for rabbit ADMSC for clinical setting which might not require long-term passage under SFM conditions.

This study demonstrates that customized SFM for Rab-ADMSCs can maintain cellular growth, proliferation rate, stemness property, senescence status, and tri-lineage differentiation capacity. In addition, SFM-cultivated cells show higher expression levels of MSC surface markers CD81, CD49f and CD29 compared to those cultivated in serum-supplemented media. These findings highlight the significance of utilizing SFM in cell-based therapies and offer novel standards for MSC characteristics cultivated in SFM. In addition, the development of a SFMs could be advantageous for clinical and scientific purposes. According to our findings, rabbit MSCs at around passage 4 expanded in any individual manner can be expected to benefit from culturing with SFM/bFGF for clinical applications
