Use and Dynamic Adjustment of Self-Control Strategies in Physical Activity Engagement Among Young and Middle-Aged Stroke Patients: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Xingru Li, Chao Zhang, Wenguang Xie, Wenhao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung and middle-aged stroke patients often have poor self-control due to brain injury and limited experience in disease management, leading to conflicts between intentions to engage in physical activity (PA) and temptations. However, little is known about PA participation from a self-control perspective. This study therefore investigated how these patients use self-control to manage such conflicts.MethodsThis descriptive qualitative study employed purposive sampling to recruit 16 stroke patients (10 ischemic, 6 hemorrhagic) aged 18–59 years with relatively mild disability, all of whom were living independently in the community. The majority were married and resided in urban areas. Thematic analysis was employed for data analysis. This report follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist.ResultsThree themes were identified: (1) conflict between PA intentions and temptations, (2) adaptive self-control strategies in PA contexts, and (3) temporal and contextual dynamics of self-control motivation in PA. Participants’ PA intentions were driven by health cognitions and identity roles. However, knowledge gaps and symptom interpretation biases led them to rationalize temptations. Participants adopted various adaptive self-control strategies (eg, self-motivation, establishing external supervision, and daily habits). Furthermore, self-control motivation varied across different stages and contexts of PA, and was primarily influenced by anticipated and actual fatigue, as well as body image concerns.ConclusionThis study offers insight into how young and middle-aged stroke patients navigate the conflict between PA intentions and temptations from a self-control perspective. Healthcare professionals may consider offering motivational interviewing and guidance on PA safety to reduce stroke patients’ tendency to rationalize temptations. Furthermore, attention should be paid to patients’ psychological resources and external environmental support, in order to deliver individualized self-control strategies that promote PA participation and long-term adherence.Keywords: stroke, self-control, physical activity, young and middle-aged adults, qualitative research

Introduction

Stroke is a major global health burden and the third leading cause of disability.1 It not only places considerable strain on healthcare systems but also prevents individuals from fully regaining their previous level of daily functioning.2 Stroke is traditionally considered a disease of the elderly; however, it affects more than 15 million people annually, including at least 1.5 million young adults.3 Physical activity (PA) is an important modifiable risk factor for stroke and is also essential for reducing disability and improving physical function, walking ability, and balance.4 For young and middle-aged stroke patients, PA facilitates return to work and resumption of family roles.5 Nevertheless, initiating and maintaining PA remains challenging for stroke patients. Moore et al6 reported that less than 30% of stroke patients meet the recommended PA levels. This underscores the need for a better understanding of the behavioral mechanisms underlying PA participation in this population.

Self-control refers to the ability to consciously resist temptations, manage impulsive behaviors, and pursue goal-directed activities that yield long-term benefits,7 and it plays a key role in health-related behaviors that require sustained regulation, such as PA. It demands that individuals maintain a dynamic balance between the pursuit of immediate rewards and deliberate self-regulation.8,9 Disruption of this balance has been associated with various risk behaviors, including physical inactivity. Typically, the rewards of PA are not immediate but delayed. In such contexts, individuals with lower self-control are more susceptible to impulses and have greater difficulty resisting the temptation of comfort behaviors, such as sedentary behavior.

Previous studies10,11 have linked brain injury to impaired self-control and heightened impulsive decision-making. For young stroke patients, the abrupt disruption of their normal life trajectory caused by the disease may further increases their inherent vulnerability, predisposing them to shift toward a decision-making pattern that favors immediate temptations.12,13 Notably, in the context of PA after stroke, the temptation to rest extends beyond the conventional notion of impulsivity; it may also manifest as a rationalized choice to avoid PA, driven by considerations of safety, fatigue, and uncertain symptoms. This rationalizing tendency potentially undermines their capacity to discern intention-behavior conflicts, thereby making self-control more challenging.14 Currently, a growing number of studies have shown that self-control is associated with PA participation. For example, a meta-analysis revealed that individuals with stronger self-control are more likely to maintain higher levels of PA.15

However, existing research on the facilitators and barriers to PA participation in stroke patients has primarily focused on factors within the COM-B framework, including capability (eg, dysfunction and fatigue), opportunity (eg, lack of social support and environmental constraints),16,17 and motivation (eg, low self-efficacy).18 Although this framework provides valuable insights into the factors influencing PA participation, it offers a limited understanding of how stroke patients experience and cope with PA intentions-temptation conflicts. Therefore, this study aimed to use qualitative research to explore the strategies employed by young and middle‑aged stroke patients to cope with such conflicts from the perspective of self‑control, and to examine how these strategies change across time and physical activity contexts. The findings are expected to provide insights into behavior change intervention strategies to facilitate the adoption and maintenance of PA in this population.

Methods

Study Design

A descriptive qualitative research approach was employed to capture the self-control experiences of PA among young and middle-aged stroke patients. The descriptive qualitative design was selected due to its methodological flexibility, which is particularly suitable for obtaining rich first-hand descriptions, especially when existing knowledge on the topic is limited.19 The study report adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).20

Participants and Settings

Using a purposive sampling method, patients were recruited from the nursing clinic of the Stroke Health Management Center in a tertiary hospital between February and April 2026 to participate in interviews. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) aged between 18 and 59 years; (2) had a history of stroke lasting more than six months (i.e, had passed the acute phase) and were living at home; (3) had a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of ≤2, indicating sufficient independent mobility and freedom of activity choice;21 and (4) had no cognitive or communication impairments, as assessed jointly by experienced researchers and stroke clinic managers during recruitment through medical record review and clinical judgment. Patients with severe organic or mental disorders were excluded.

Data Collection

The primary researcher approached patients during outpatient follow-up visits, established rapport, and explained the study’s purpose and procedures. After obtaining informed consent, the researcher collected patients’ contact information and scheduled a time for a telephone interview. Prior to the interviews, the research team collectively discussed the interview guide and conducted a pilot test with two participants. During this process, the interview guide was revised by adding prompts such as “Could you give an example?” and “Could you provide more details?” The pilot interviews were not included in the final data analysis. The final interview guide is presented in Supplementary file 1 Interview outline. The primary interviewer was researcher LXR, a doctoral student in nursing with extensive experience in qualitative research. Another researcher, XWG, was also present and was primarily responsible for recording participants’ paralinguistic features, such as pauses and tone of voice. All interviews were audio‑recorded with the patients’ consent, and each interview lasted approximately 30–40 minutes. The sample size was determined by data saturation, which was defined as the point at which no new themes emerged from consecutive interviews.22 After the 14th interview, the preliminary analysis conducted by two researchers indicated that no new themes or sub‑themes had emerged. To further validate the findings, two additional interviews were conducted. Overall, apart from the two participants involved in the pilot interviews, a total of 17 eligible participants were contacted, among whom one refused to participate because of scheduling conflicts.

Data Analysis

The interview data were manually transcribed within 24 hours, and each participant was assigned an identification number (eg, P1). The data analysis process employed an inductive thematic analysis method23 and was conducted in Microsoft Excel. Notably, during the analysis, the researchers did not apply self‑control theory a priori as a coding framework. Instead, the coding and theme generation process was grounded in patients’ original expressions. The theory served merely as a sensitizing concept, helping the researchers to attend to phenomena related to self‑control.24 First, two researchers (LXR and XWG) immersed themselves in the transcripts to become familiar with the data through repeated reading. At the same time, they recorded their first impressions of the transcripts. Subsequently, the two researchers independently identified meaningful text segments related to PA self-control and performed line-by-line coding. After every five interview transcripts were coded, the two researchers met to discuss similarities and differences. Any disagreements that could be resolved through a third researcher (ZC, a doctoral supervisor). Next, based on code similarity, the codes were clustered into potential subthemes. The two researchers met regularly to review the subthemes against the data, ensuring internal consistency and clear distinctions between them. Subsequently, the subthemes were further organized into themes, which were finalized by the team. The final results, including themes, sub‑themes, and representative quotations, were returned to the 16 participants for their comments on the accuracy of the findings. Of these, 13 participants confirmed that the results accurately reflected their experiences, while the remaining 3 did not respond, despite two follow‑up reminders sent by the researchers.

Rigor and Reflexivity

This study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s standards.25 The researchers enhanced credibility by repeatedly immersing themselves in the transcripts, conducting independent double coding, and reaching a consensus. Dependability was strengthened through peer debriefing and conference discussions. Confirmability was ensured by documenting all research processes and recording patients’ verbal and paralinguistic features. Transferability was facilitated by providing a detailed description of participants’ demographic characteristics and maximizing the use of participants’ original language in the research findings.

The first and third authors are doctoral students in nursing. The second author is a doctoral supervisor in nursing and also a manager. The first and second authors both have many years of work experience in neurosurgery and rehabilitation. The fourth author is a graduate student in nursing with rotation experience in neurosurgery. None of the authors had prior acquaintance with the participants. Although the researchers’ clinical experience helped in understanding the participants’ experiences, it also potentially introduced an over-interpretation bias. Therefore, reflective memos were written, and regular meetings were held to ensure that the descriptions of participants’ experiences remained as close as possible to their original expressions.

Theme and Subtheme

Theme 1: Conflict Between PA Intentions and Temptations

Subtheme 1: High PA Intentions Driven by Health and Role Demands

The intention underlying participation in PA extended beyond the conventional notions of rehabilitation and prevention. For some participants, their PA intentions were intertwined with social or familial roles and life pursuits. For instance, among several middle‑aged men serving as family breadwinners, the intention to engage in PA was often associated with the preservation of functional capacity and the fulfillment of familial and social obligations. Similarly, among some young women, the intention to participate in PA was linked to shedding the stigmatizing label of “disabled” and pursuing normative romantic relationships.

My foot hasn’t fully recovered yet, if you look closely, you can still see a difference. At least exercise won’t make my lower limb function any worse. (P1)

At our age, with parents to support and children to raise, we’re in an awkward spot. I can’t afford not to exercise. If my physical functions decline and I start walking with a limp, what company would ever hire someone like us? (P5)

I am currently a person with a disability. For example, when it comes to finding a partner, others will definitely mind. However, I believe that as long as I persist in exercising, I can be just like them. (P12)

Subtheme 2: Rationalizing Temptation

Although some participants reported high intentions to engage in PA, factors such as lack of knowledge, attributional barriers, and symptom interpretation biases may lead some participants to perceive rest or PA avoidance as justified in certain situations, even when such justifications run counter to their long-term PA goals. For instance, when some participants experienced stress or fatigue, their behavioral motivation may be altered. Moreover, owing to insufficient information, participants may have concerns about the safety of PA programs and consequently choose to withdraw.

I had my onset when I was under a lot of stress. If I’m feeling very stressed at work, I won’t go running, because running also makes my heart beat faster and makes my nerves more tense. (P2)

We usually get tired very easily, and if we exercise, it adds to our physical burden. Because once we feel exhausted, it takes us several days to recover. (P6)

It seems I can’t do some types of aerobic exercise, though I can’t quite remember which ones. As a result, I don’t really exercise much now, because I don’t know which activities might worsen my condition. (P10)

Theme 2: Adaptive Self-Control Strategies in PA Contexts

Subtheme 1: Intrinsic Drive Strategies Based on Self-Motivation

In the face of competitive temptations, some participants employed self-motivation as a strategy to enhance their engagement in PA. The implementation of immediate rewards and the establishment of short-term, specific goals may help participants reduce the discounting of future rewards associated with PA, thereby counteracting their preference for immediate gratification. Furthermore, evoking positive past memories and envisioning a beneficial future can increase participants’ awareness of the necessity of PA, thus translating abstract visions into concrete actions.

When I don’t feel like exercising, I give myself some rewards, even if it’s just a very small reward, like buying a bouquet of flowers today. I know that as long as I come back from exercising, I can get it. (P12)

I don’t set long-term goals for myself, for example, I want to. in a few years. That’s too far away and not realistic. I only make short-term plans, just for this month. (P4)

I also want to be lazy sometimes, so I imagine myself running a marathon in every city. And if I have that wish, I have to take action. (P6)

Subtheme 2: Autonomous Construction of External Supervision

Some participants informed their family members and partners of their PA plans in advance, and external supervision and reminders helped them overcome immediate temptations. Moreover, tracking PA progress and assuming the roles of “helper” and “role model” in a virtual community not only helped participants cultivate a sense of achievement but also imposed potential supervision on their PA behaviors.

I tell my boyfriend about my plans for the current stage. When I don’t feel like exercising, he pulls me up and takes me running with him. (P2)

We have an online WeChat group, all members are patients around my age. Every morning, we check in by posting things like photos of our breakfast and our workouts. If everyone else is checking in and you don’t, you feel guilty. (P15)

I created a Little Red Book account specifically to record videos of my daily runs. Now I have many followers, and I explain exercise-related knowledge to them. If I do not run today, I will not have any material to post. Gradually, others will not trust you either. (P14)

Subtheme 3: Facilitation of PA Habits Based on Daily Routines

In addition to internal and external strategies, the development of PA habits enabled some participants to achieve effortless and successful self-control, as PA was integrated into their daily life and work routines and was regarded as a basic physiological need.

I don’t deliberately try to remember this thing, because I usually go grocery shopping in the morning. I don’t say I’ll go by car; I always walk. (P16)

My job is quite special. I often travel on business. But no matter where I go, even when I’m waiting at the airport, I can take ten minutes to get myself moving. (P13)

Theme 3: Temporal and Contextual Dynamics of Self-Control Motivation in PA

Subtheme 1: Temporal Dynamics of Self-Control Motivation

In the initial stage of PA, some participants tended to anticipate the substantial effort required, rather than experiencing actual fatigue. This psychological barrier led to lower motivation for self-control. Therefore, at this stage, positive mental rehearsal was used to enhance motivation for engaging in PA. Based on the accumulation of physical fatigue, self-control was further depleted during the middle stage of PA. However, some participants automatically lowered their execution threshold for PA rather than completely discontinuing the activity. Another alternative perspective suggested that pleasant and enjoyable bodily sensations during the middle stage of PA might, in turn, enhance participants’ motivation for self-control.

The hardest part when you start running is the decision-making stage, because running for 30 minutes is not easy; but I will then think that once you run for a while, you feel the comfortable sensation of sweating. (P3)

I often feel tired in the middle of running or even walking. At such times, I take the initiative to adjust my level of exercise and don’t force myself to push through, because I know that wouldn’t do me any good. I believe that as long as I find the right amount of exercise for myself and stick with it, that’s enough. (P5)

Once you’ve passed the 20-minute mark, you’ll start to enjoy that pleasurable feeling of sweating, and your steps will become lighter. (P11)

Subtheme 2: Contextual Sensitivity of Self-Control Motivation

Self-control motivation varied not only with fluctuations in the duration of PA but also with the context in which the PA took place. Due to their limb disabilities, some participants experienced body image concerns. When the context of PA participation shifted from home to the community, their self-control motivation was weakened, and PA was prone to interruption.

I don’t like crowded places. When I pass by a place where people are chatting, I want to stop, because right now, when I run, my form is still twisted. (P12)

When I lift dumbbells at home, I feel fine. But as soon as I go out, I feel really uncomfortable I don’t know if they might be talking about me. (P4)

To address this issue, participants developed two context-based adjustment strategies, namely temporal avoidance and social restructuring. Some participants chose to engage in PA at night to avoid others’ strange looks or comments. Other participants, by exercising together with fellow patients, perceived a return to being “normal people” within a peer-supported environment, which facilitated the continuation of PA in public spaces.

It’s not true that I don’t care about others. In the early morning, when there are hardly any people outside, I like to walk outside at that time. (P4)

I got to know a few fellow patients. When I run with them, I feel like I’ve become a normal person again. We are all the same. In that situation, I don’t care about what others think anymore. (P1)

Discussion

This study investigated how young and middle-aged stroke patients manage the conflict between PA intentions and competing temptations from a self-control perspective. The findings revealed that although health cognitions and identity roles drove participants to form PA intentions, knowledge gaps and symptom interpretation biases confer rationality to competing temptations. Patients developed multiple self-control strategies, including self-motivation, the establishment of external supervision, and daily habits. Notably, self-control motivation was not static but fluctuated with changes in PA stages and contexts, prompting patients to make dynamic adjustments. These findings help healthcare providers offer personalized self-control strategies to facilitate PA change and maintenance in stroke patients.

Conflict Between PA Intentions and Temptations

Most participants reported that the pursuit of health drove their PA intentions, which is consistent with the findings of Liu et al14 This study further revealed distinct characteristics of young and middle-aged adults, a population currently navigating the development of romantic relationships and careers, while also being shaped by the role expectations of family support imposed by Chinese collectivist culture.26 Therefore, participants may place greater emphasis on high PA intentions driven by family and social roles. In contrast, studies conducted in individualistic cultures have often highlighted self-identity‑driven PA behavior.27,28 However, because cross‑cultural differences were not directly examined in this study, this comparison remains speculative and needs further investigation in multicenter or cross‑cultural research.

Although participants had intentions for PA, they were still affected by competing temptations. The two-stage model of self-control29 suggests that individuals employ self-control strategies only when they identify a conflict between temptations and their goals. However, in this study, participants’ rationalization of temptation impaired this recognition process, which is consistent with a previous study.14 This study further identifies symptom interpretation biases as important contributors to the failure of conflict recognition. A possible reason for this cognitive bias is that participants lacked knowledge about the relationship between fatigue, stress, and appropriate PA. Moreover, inadequate communication between medical staff and stroke patients at home leaves participants without professional PA guidance, forcing them to rely on subjective judgments. This finding indicates that healthcare professionals should provide patients with individualized PA safety guidance, specifying appropriate PA types and intensity levels. Additionally, timely follow-up and motivational interviewing could be employed to help patients mitigate misconceptions about PA, thereby reducing their propensity to rationalize temptation.

Adaptive Self-Control Strategies in PA Contexts

In this study, participants countered temptations by setting immediate rewards or short-term goals, which is consistent with the findings of Berardi et al.30 They suggested that proximal incentives can prompt individuals to forgo the short-term rewards of unhealthy behaviors, which may further enhance executive function and reduce discounting.30 In addition, participants exercised self-control by imagining a positive future, which is consistent with the findings of Lempert et al31 Imagining future events using the episodic memory system can enhance patience, which in turn leads to choices that benefit future outcomes, such as PA behavior.31 Although participants reported using the recall of positive past events to resist temptations, the effectiveness of recalling past events in inhibiting immediate temptations and facilitating future-beneficial choices remains controversial.31,32 Future empirical research could further investigate the specific mechanisms through which recalling past events contributes to self-control.

Consistent with previous research,14 seeking social support is an important component of self-control strategies in individuals’ health behaviors. The availability of external resources (eg, social support) helps replenish and maintain internal resources (eg, self-control), thereby preventing resource depletion.33 In this study, participants not only passively received social support but also, by assuming the roles of role models and helpers, exerted a reverse influence on the monitoring of PA behaviors. When participants acted as role models and helpers, their perceived sense of competence and the value of social relationships enhanced their autonomous motivation for self-control. This can be explained by self-determination theory,34 in which the satisfaction of competence and relatedness needs facilitates the internalization of external monitoring into self-regulated behavior. Furthermore, positive social signals released during social interactions, such as encouragement and recognition, may promote the integration of cognition and emotion and helping individuals better maintain their self-regulatory ability.35 In future research, healthcare professionals could guide stroke patients to consolidate their self-control ability by helping others and serving as role models.

Participants also reported that integrating PA into their work and daily routines facilitated habit formation, which in turn reduced the need for effortful self-control, thereby making PA engagement easier.36,37 According to dual-process theory,38 habitual behavior is triggered by situational cues and occurs automatically without conscious awareness, resulting in reduced demand for self-control resources. In future research, healthcare professionals could develop personalized habit formation-based intervention programs for stroke patients, for example, using contextual cues (such as after meals or during commuting) to automatically initiate PA behaviors, ultimately helping patients achieve long-term, effortless PA maintenance in naturalistic settings.

Temporal and Contextual Dynamics of Self-Control Motivation in PA

Self-depletion theory proposes that self-control is a limited resource,7 which provides an explanatory framework for understanding participants’ self-control in the context of PA. From the perspective of this theory, participants in this study anticipated greater effort during the early stage of PA, potentially representing their evaluation of future resource depletion and consequently reducing their motivation to initiate the activity. Similarly, mid-stage fatigue may have directly consumed self-control resources, impairing individuals’ ability to resist temptation. Under fatigued conditions, some participants flexibly reduced PA intensity, a finding consistent with the extended self-control model proposed by Werner et al39 which emphasizes the monitoring process of self-control that determines whether to maintain or switch goals. Future interventions should distinguish between these time points: in the early stage, positive mental rehearsal can be used to reduce the anticipated burden, while in the mid-stage, fatigue self-management skills need to be taught. Notably, the positive experiences perceived during PA can enhance participants’ self-control motivation. One hypothetical explanation is that PA may improve mood states and enhance self‑control through neuroendocrine pathways, such as dopamine and endorphin release.40 However, such a biological interpretation is not directly supported by qualitative data. Future research could not only examine the effectiveness of interventions designed to promote positive PA experiences in enhancing self‑control, but also further explore the biological mechanisms that may account for these effects.

In this study, PA contexts also influenced participants’ self-control motivation, which was primarily associated with perceived body image concerns. This finding is consistent with Heatherton et al41 who demonstrated that negative emotions impair self-control. It is noteworthy that patients feel “being normal” when exercising with peers, suggesting that homogeneous group environments may protect self-control resources by reducing social evaluation threats. Future interventions may recommend that patients join PA groups with peers to mitigate the erosion of self-control motivation by body image concerns.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single hospital with a limited sample size. As recruitment took place in a tertiary hospital located in a provincial capital, this potentially restricted the inclusion of rural participants. During recruitment, although participants with cognitive or communication impairments were excluded based on medical records and clinical judgment, the absence of standardized screening tools may have introduced selection bias. Future studies could incorporate standardized assessment instruments to reduce such bias. Furthermore, the findings only represent patients with relatively good function who are capable of independent living, which may have made self-control in PA more feasible for them. In addition, the study did not record the location of brain lesions, which could have an impact on self-control. Future research should examine the dynamic process of self-control in PA contexts using more diverse samples. Second, this study used telephone interviews, which potentially overlooked the expression of non-verbal cues from patients. To compensate for this limitation, the researchers recorded participants’ tone and intonation, which to some extent reflected their attitudes. In future studies, face-to-face interviews could be employed to obtain additional nonverbal information. Third, due to the inherent constraints of qualitative research, this study did not quantify the effectiveness of self-control strategies. Future research employing a mixed-methods design could address this gap. Finally, this study may not have captured the broader range of dynamic adjustment strategies used by stroke patients. A longitudinal approach could be adopted in the future to explore this process.

Conclusion

Young and middle-aged stroke patients experience a conflict between PA intentions and temptations. Internal, external, and automatic self-control strategies are employed to cope with these temptations. Moreover, self-control motivation varies across different stages and contexts of PA and is influenced by factors such as fatigue and body image concerns. Healthcare professionals may consider identifying the reasons for the presence of temptations and develop personalized self-control strategies based on patient characteristics, so as to reduce reliance on effortful self-control and support sustained physical activity participation. Given that these findings are most relevant to community-dwelling stroke patients with mild functional limitations and no substantial cognitive or communication deficits, their generalizability to those with more severe disabilities needs further investigation.
