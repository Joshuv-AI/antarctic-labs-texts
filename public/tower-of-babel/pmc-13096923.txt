Bone turnover in postmenopausal women during denosumab treatment: Biomarker analysis in naive and discontinuing patients
Authors: Mikolaj Bartosik, Esther Wolf, Alexander Simon, Felix N von Brackel, Oskar Windels, Ulrich C Klostermeier, Florian Barvencik, Michael Amling, Ralf Oheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOsteoporosis is characterized by an imbalance in bone turnover, with increased resorption over formation. Antiresorptive treatment, such as denosumab (Dmab), aims to restore this balance, making monitoring of bone turnover markers useful. Key bone resorption parameters are serum C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) and urinary deoxypyridinoline/creatinine (DPD).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the bone turnover in a total of 96 postmenopausal women, 59 were treatment-naive before starting with Dmab, and 37 had discontinued prior Dmab therapy for ≥9 months before resumption. Bone turnover markers were measured every 6 months, and DXA scans were performed annually over 36 months. Comparative analysis included only treatment-naive patients with elevated baseline resorption markers (CTX or DPD above reference). Additionally, a large laboratory database (n = 21,802) was used to examine the correlation between CTX and DPD.ResultsBoth CTX and DPD decreased significantly within 6 months of Dmab initiation. Notably, while CTX was increased only in 5.1%, DPD was elevated in 32.2% in the treatment-naive group. At baseline, 26.3% of patients in the discontinued group showed elevated CTX levels, while DPD was elevated in all patients (100%). After resuming Dmab treatment following a treatment discontinuation, DPD remained elevated in 51.8% of patients despite normal CTX. Correlation analysis revealed a moderate association between CTX and DPD (rs = 0.431).ConclusionsOur data suggest that urinary DPD may provide additional biochemical information in certain clinical contexts, particularly after denosumab discontinuation. Thus, simultaneous assessment of CTX and DPD may enhance diagnostic accuracy and may guide individualized osteoporosis management; however, prospective validation is needed.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Denosumab, Bone turnover markers, Serum C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX), Deoxypyridinoline (DPD)

Introduction

The balance between bone formation and bone resorption is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the skeleton, and monitoring respective bone turnover markers that reflect both processes is important for effective management of osteoporosis treatment. As the balance between bone formation and bone resorption is disturbed in osteoporosis, monitoring both aspects of bone metabolism can be used to guide therapy (Vasikaran et al., 2011; Bhattoa et al., 2025). Biochemical parameters such as osteocalcin, procollagen type 1 N-terminal peptide, and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase reflect the activation of osteoblasts and are therefore considered indicators of bone formation (Vasikaran et al., 2011; Delmas et al., 2000). Laboratory markers for bone resorption include the C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) in serum and urinary deoxypyridinoline/creatinine ratio (DPD) (Vasikaran et al., 2011; Coen et al., 2000; Westhoff et al., 2006; Delmas et al., 1991).

CTX is a fragment of the C-terminal telopeptide of collagen type I, which is released during the degradation of type I collagen, which is mainly found in bone tissue (Wichers et al., 1999). The serum level of CTX reflects relatively rapid enzyme-mediated cleavage of type I collagen and therefore is an indicator of osteoclastic resorption activity and can be determined from a blood sample (Okabe et al., 2004). DPD represents a pyridinium cross-linking component of mature type I collagen that is released during bone resorption and is excreted unchanged in the urine (Westhoff et al., 2006). Early kinetic studies have shown that urinary DPD correlate with radiotracer-based estimates of bone resorption, supporting their biological relevance as indicators of skeletal matrix degradation (Eastell et al., 1997). However, compared with serum CTX, which is widely endorsed by international societies for monitoring antiresorptive therapy, the clinical use of DPD has been less extensively standardized, and its role in specific settings such as denosumab treatment and discontinuation remains understudied. Previous studies have shown that although CTX and DPD are correlated, they are not interchangeable, suggesting that they may provide complementary information on bone resorption dynamics (Ju et al., 1997). In clinical practice, both parameters can be used to monitor osteoporosis treatment or for diagnostic purposes of metabolic bone diseases (Westhoff et al., 2006; Kaji et al., 2010; Fassbender et al., 2009).

Denosumab (Dmab), as a potent antiresorptive drug, is a human monoclonal antibody that prevents interaction with the RANK receptor on osteoclast precursors by binding to the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL). This reduces the formation, function, and survival of osteoclasts, the cells responsible for bone resorption (Hanley et al., 2012; McClung et al., 2006). When starting Dmab treatment, a distinct decrease in bone resorption markers can be observed. However, discontinuation of Dmab has been associated with a subsequent increase in bone turnover with a simultaneous decrease in bone mineral density (Tsourdi et al., 2020; Everts-Graber et al., 2021). The Dmab rebound effect describes the rapid escalation of bone turnover that occurs once treatment is stopped, potentially leading to osteoporotic fractures due to accelerated bone loss (Tsourdi et al., 2020). This phenomenon typically begins to manifest within 7–9 months after the last Dmab injection, coinciding with the waning pharmacological effect of the drug (Degboé and Couture, 2026). Without timely antiresorptive follow-up therapy, bone resorption markers can rise markedly above pretreatment levels, and bone mineral density may decline, leading to a high risk of vertebral fractures (Burckhardt et al., 2021). Recognizing and managing this rebound is crucial for osteoporosis treatment. Therefore, monitoring biochemical markers of bone turnover after discontinuation of Dmab can help to identify patients at risk early and to guide timely clinical interventions to protect bone health, as elevated bone resorption markers may serve as early indicators of an increased rebound risk (Liebich et al., 2023).

The aim of our study was to investigate bone turnover marker dynamics, particularly the bone resorption markers serum CTX and urinary DPD, under denosumab therapy in two clinically distinct patient populations. In a treatment-naive postmenopausal women cohort initiating Dmab for the first time, and in patients resuming Dmab after a period of discontinuation. The study specifically compared these groups to assess potential differences in marker responses and to evaluate the utility of CTX and DPD for monitoring bone resorption during and after Dmab therapy.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the local ethics committee (TEMP504246-WF) and the Declaration of Helsinki. Of 1060 screened patients, a total of 96 postmenopausal women were included who presented to our specialized outpatient clinic between February 2021 and April 2024. Among them, 59 patients were treatment-naive before initiating Dmab therapy (treatment-naive group). In this group, no patients had previously received treatment for osteoporosis (but one patient received 3 doses of denosumab more than 5 years ago). The remaining 37 patients in the discontinuation group had previously received Dmab for a mean duration of 48.2 ± 23.5 months. Treatment was interrupted for at least 9 months, primarily due to compliance-related factors, before Dmab therapy was resumed at baseline (T0) (discontinuation group) (Fig. 1a). The inclusion criteria were postmenopausal osteoporosis with an indication for bone-specific treatment following national guidelines, treated with Dmab. Patients with active tumor disease, steroid-induced osteoporosis, rheumatic disease, or other specific treatments with potential bone metabolic effects (e.g. antihormonal treatment) were excluded. Patients' demographics, DXA, and biochemical measurements were collected during routine clinical follow-up. Bone turnover markers were assessed at 6-month intervals (baseline: T0, 6Mo, 12Mo, 18Mo, 24Mo, 30Mo, and 36Mo), whereas DXA scans were performed at 12-month intervals (T0, 12Mo, 24Mo, and 36Mo) (Fig. 1b). Baseline was defined as the time of first presentation and treatment decision before initiation of the current Dmab treatment course. In the discontinuation group, baseline referred to the time point of re-presentation and re-initiation of denosumab after treatment interruption. Subsequently, a comparison was conducted between the treatment-naive and discontinuation groups. To ensure comparability, the comparative analysis included only treatment-naïve patients with elevated baseline bone resorption parameters (exceeding the upper limit of the local laboratory reference range for CTX and/or DPD) since high resorption at baseline is a risk factor for the rebound phenomenon. After applying these criteria, 39 treatment-naive patients were included, compared with 35 patients in the discontinuation group.

Study schema. Postmenopausal women were screened for Dmab treatment. A total of 432 postmenopausal patients receiving Dmab were identified. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 96 patients were selected and classified into two groups: 59 treatment-naive patients initiating Dmab 60 mg s.c. every 6 months, and 37 patients who had discontinued Dmab treatment for ≥9 months (due to compliance-related factors) before resumption with Dmab (A). In both groups, bone turnover markers were assessed at 6-month intervals (baseline, 6Mo, 12Mo, 18Mo, 24Mo, 30Mo, and 36Mo), while DXA scans were performed at 12-month intervals (baseline, 12Mo, 24Mo, and 36Mo) (B). In addition, CTX and DPD values from a laboratory database were used to examine the overall association between both resorption markers. These 21,802 measurements were derived from a heterogeneous real-world osteological patient population seen in our specialized bone outpatient clinic and were not restricted to denosumab-treated patients or to the 96-patient study cohort. Because this dataset was based on routine clinical laboratory records, no predefined study-specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied for this correlation analysis.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)

The areal bone mineral density (T-score with corresponding aBMD) was measured by DXA (Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA). The total lumbar spine (L1-L4) and each side (left/right) of the hip (total hip) were evaluated. The T-score of L1-L4 with corresponding areal BMD were used for further analysis. In accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, daily calibration scans for DXA quality control were performed using a special phantom. Precision testing included least significant change calculations according to the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) guidelines (Jankowski et al., 2019).

Biochemical analysis

As part of routine clinical practice, analyses of serum and spot urine calcium homeostasis and bone turnover were performed in our on-site laboratory. The serum levels of calcium, phosphate, vitamin D (25-OH-D), parathyroid hormone (PTH), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were measured with the Atellica Solution from Siemens. Bone formation parameters such as bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (bALP) and osteocalcin were measured using the Liaison XL from Diasorin. Procollagen type 1 N-terminal peptide (P1NP) and the bone resorption marker C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) were measured using the Cobas E411 from Roche Diagnostics. Levels of deoxypyridinoline/creatinine in urine (DPD) were measured using the Siemens Immulite XP. Reference ranges were age- and sex-specific and established by our in-house laboratory based on standardized procedures and historical data, together with the manufacturer's guidelines and recommendations. The following age- and gender-adjusted local laboratory reference ranges were applied: Serum calcium 2.18–2.60 mmol/L, Serum phosphate 0.77–1.65 mmol/L, ALP 46–116 U/L, osteocalcin 5.4–59.1 μg/L, 25(OH)D > 30 μg/L, bALP 5.5–26.6 μg/L, P1NP 24.0–134.0 μg/L, CTX 0.183–1.060 μg/L, PTH 17.4–80.1 ng/L, and DPD 3–7 nmol/mmol.

Statistical analysis

JASP 0.19.1 (University of Amsterdam, Netherlands) and GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) were used for statistical analysis. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For between-group comparisons at single time points, the unpaired two-tailed t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for nonparametric data. For within-group comparisons over time, repeated measurements were analyzed using a mixed-effects model, which accounts for within-patient correlations across time points and accommodates missing data by including all available observations. The Geisser-Greenhouse correction was applied when sphericity assumptions were not met. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Šídák's multiple comparisons test. Differences in the distribution of subgroups were tested with the chi-square test. Correlation analyses were performed in order to assess the relationship between CTX and DPD variables from the large laboratory database. Since these variables were not normally distributed, the Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships. In addition, a simple linear regression model was applied to evaluate the independent predictive value. Parameters that showed relevance in previous analyses were included. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Both groups showed a high frequency of major osteoporotic fractures, with about two-thirds of the respective group. Both the naive group and the discontinuation group showed a sufficient vitamin D level (40.7 ± 13.2 μg/L and 43.5 ± 12.2 μg/L, respectively) at baseline. Bone turnover parameters and bone density in the treatment-naive group under Dmab therapy

In the patients who were treatment-naive before Dmab application, the bone resorption parameters CTX and DPD, as well as bone formation parameters osteocalcin, P1NP, and bone ALP decreased significantly at 6 months after the first application (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a and b). For most patients, CTX levels were already within the normal range before the first Dmab administration. Only 5.1% of patients showed increased CTX levels; in contrast, the majority (67.8%) showed increased DPD levels (p < 0.001). At the end of the first treatment interval (6 months, T1), all patients showed normalized CTX levels; however, 29.6% still showed elevated DPD levels (p < 0.001). The mean CTX values were mostly in the lower reference range, while the DPD levels were in the upper reference range over the entire time period and increased in the majority of patients (Fig. 2c).

Dmab treatment led to a significant increase in bone mineral density (aBMD, T-score) at the lumbar spine in each year over the 3 years. In both hips, a significant increase in T-score was observed after the first year, with stable values in the following years (Fig. 2d).

Bone turnover parameters and bone mineral density in the discontinuation group

At the initial presentation and before resuming Dmab treatment, CTX values were already elevated in 26.3% of patients, while elevated DPD values were present in 100% of patients (p < 0.001). Six months after resuming Dmab, all patients showed CTX values in the reference range, while 51.8% of the patients still had elevated DPD values (p < 0.001). Patients with Dmab discontinuation exhibited a significant decrease in the bone resorption parameters CTX and DPD, as well as the bone formation parameters osteocalcin, P1NP, and bone ALP 6 months after the first administration (Fig. 3a and b). These real-world data show that a reduction in bone resorption measured by DPD was achieved in approximately 50% of patients (Fig. 3c). Following the resumed Dmab treatment, the mean CTX values were in the lower reference range, whereas DPD levels remained in the upper reference range throughout the entire period. The bone mineral density (T-score and aBMD) of the discontinuation group did not increase noticeably under reinitiated Dmab therapy (Fig. 3d).

Association between CTX level and DPD level in a large laboratory database collective

To analyze associations between serum CTX and urinary DPD, data were extracted from the institutional laboratory database. The dataset represents patients from the osteoporosis cohort of our outpatient bone clinic. Urine and blood samples were collected during routine clinical care between March 2021 and November 2023. As this is a real-world cohort, no formal control group was included. Only paired measurements, i.e., those in which both CTX and DPD were collected on the same day, were included in the analysis (n = 21,802).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a moderate but statistically significant positive correlation between CTX and DPD values (rs = 0.431, 95% CI: 0.420 to 0.442, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5). However, simple linear regression analysis demonstrated a weak correlation with a R2 of 0.286 (Slope: 6.85, 95% CI: 6.71 to 7.00 p < 0.001), suggesting that only 28.6% of the variance in DPD values can be explained by CTX values. It was found that in total 49.2% of our patients showed elevated DPD levels, whereas only 2.2% showed elevated CTX. A simultaneous increase in both markers was found in only 2.1% of all cases.

Discussion

Laboratory parameters reflecting bone turnover are crucial in clinical practice as they provide valuable insights into the dynamic processes of bone formation and resorption. Bone turnover markers are commonly used to evaluate the response to osteoporosis therapy by determining whether changes in marker levels exceed the minimum significant change, the smallest variation that can be reliably distinguished from biological and analytical variability. However, in the ambulatory care setting, follow-up measurements are often unavailable or obtained from different laboratories, using heterogeneous assays and reference ranges. In such cases, markers with well-defined cut-off values may provide additional, complementary insight to support clinical decision-making. CTX is a C-terminal telopeptide fragment that is released during the destruction of type I collagen (Delmas et al., 2000), and DPD is a collagen cross-link molecule excreted in the urine during bone resorption (Delmas et al., 2000; Delmas et al., 1991). These markers are used in clinical practice to evaluate bone metabolic activity and to monitor the effectiveness of bone-specific therapies (Okabe et al., 2004; Kaji et al., 2010; Bone et al., 2011).

Among bone turnover markers, serum CTX and P1NP have been proposed by The European Society for Clinical and Economic Aspects of Osteoporosis, Osteoarthritis and Musculoskeletal Diseases (ESCEO), International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), and International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) as reference markers for osteoporosis studies and treatment monitoring, whereas urinary markers such as DPD are less commonly used in contemporary routine monitoring (Bhattoa et al., 2025). In the present study, we investigated bone turnover markers, emphasizing bone resorption markers, using laboratory parameters in treatment-naive patients receiving long-term therapy with Dmab. In addition, we aimed to examine the effects of discontinuing Dmab on bone turnover.

In the majority of treatment-naive patients (94.9%), CTX values were within the reference range before Dmab treatment initiation, while 67.8% in the same group showed elevated DPD levels. Furthermore, after 6 months of Dmab treatment, approximately 30% of patients still showed elevated DPD levels despite complete normalization of CTX. While many studies use CTX to reflect bone resorption, there are currently only few studies using DPD despite its potential significance. Our study indicates that while both CTX and DPD reflect bone resorption, urinary DPD levels were more frequently elevated above the local reference range at the end of the denosumab dosing interval than serum CTX in this cohort. Thus, additional urinary DPD measurements may provide complementary biochemical information when CTX values are still suppressed or within the (lower) reference range. In addition to the resorption markers CTX and DPD, bone formation markers, including bALP, osteocalcin, and P1NP, were measured. These parameters demonstrated a comparable decrease following denosumab administration, consistent with existing literature and with the known coupling between bone resorption and formation (Bone et al., 2011; Cummings et al., 2009). This suppression across both resorption and formation markers supports the expected pharmacodynamic effect of denosumab on bone turnover. Simultaneously, bone mineral density increased significantly in both the lumbar region and the hip after 12 months of treatment.

In osteoporosis, both parameters, alongside bone formation markers, play a relevant role in evaluating bone health and monitoring the effectiveness of treatment (Melton III et al., 1997; Akesson et al., 1993). DPD is released during the degradation of mature collagen in bone, making it a highly specific marker for bone resorption processes (Vasikaran et al., 2011; Hlaing and Compston, 2014). CTX is also reflect bone turnover with high specificity, as it is primarily derived from the breakdown of type I collagen, which is the main structural protein in bone. CTX has been well validated in numerous studies and is commonly used in osteoporosis management to assess treatment response and disease activity (Everts-Graber et al., 2021; Eastell et al., 2011; Miller et al., 2008). The two markers are biologically related but not interchangeable. Notably, during denosumab treatment, CTX rapidly decreases to a very low level, whereas DPD may remain detectable, capturing residual or slow collagen turnover, thereby providing complementary information on ongoing bone resorption (Fassbender et al., 2009). Earlier comparative work has also suggested differences in biological variability between resorption markers, with CTX showing pronounced diurnal variation and urinary DPD showing least variability with distinct analytical and biological characteristics (Ju et al., 1997). Furthermore, the present findings indicate that serum CTX and urinary DPD do not always show parallel changes under denosumab therapy, suggesting that these markers may capture different aspects of bone resorption dynamics. In our cohort, DPD levels remained elevated in a subset of patients despite normalization of CTX, which also may reflect biological variability or methodological differences between serum and urine-based measurements rather than a true difference in bone turnover activity. These observations highlight that no single biochemical marker fully represents bone resorption processes and support the concept that CTX and DPD may provide partly complementary information on skeletal metabolism. Nevertheless, interpretation of these parameters should always consider their physiological variability and the clinical context rather than implying a direct relationship with disease activity or therapeutic response.

In patients who discontinued Dmab, we found significantly higher DPD levels compared to the treatment-naive group with elevated bone resorption at baseline. This is consistent with previous studies showing an increase in bone turnover markers after a discontinuation of the treatment (Tsourdi et al., 2017; Zanchetta et al., 2018). In the discontinued Dmab group, initial laboratory assessment revealed elevated CTX in 26.3% and elevated DPD in all patients (100%). Importantly, all patients showed normal CTX levels after resumption of treatment, while DPD remained elevated in over 50% of cases. Therefore, urinary DPD and serum CTX may show differential responses after denosumab discontinuation. While CTX is measured in serum, whereas DPD is measured in urine. Therefore, differences in half-life, diurnal variation, and analytical variability should be considered when interpreting results, particularly in patients with renal impairment or during antiresorptive therapy. Although direct comparisons between urinary DPD and serum CTX are limited, the available studies show a moderate correlation and largely comparable performance characteristics. DPD serves as a direct measure of the release of mature collagen cross-links during bone matrix degradation and are excreted unchanged in the urine, thereby providing a cumulative and stable measure of bone resorption (Gerrits et al., 1995). In contrast, CTX reflects the cathepsin K-mediated cleavage of the C-telopeptide of type I collagen during osteoclast-driven bone resorption, making it a specific marker for canonical osteoclastic activity in bone remodeling (Garnero et al., 2003). DPD may additionally detect subtle residual or non-canonical collagen degradation processes, thereby potentially providing complementary information. However, comparative mechanistic data remain limited. Given that an increase in bone resorption is known to occur following Dmab withdrawal, monitoring more than one resorption markers could potentially help to capture early biochemical changes associated with this phenomenon. Although our study was not designed to establish predictive value, the combined assessment of CTX and DPD may offer complementary information on bone metabolism, particularly during treatment transitions or discontinuation.

Despite CTX and DPD significantly correlated (rs = 0.431) in a large data set (n = 21,802), the regression analysis showed only a weak association (R2 = 0.286) with elevated DPD levels in 49.2% and elevated CTX values in only 2.2% of all patients. In addition, only 2.1% of patients showed simultaneous pathologic elevations in both markers. This low agreement again suggests that CTX and DPD, although reflecting similar biological processes, capture different aspects or time periods of bone resorption. While CTX is a widely used marker of bone resorption, DPD is less frequently applied, potentially due to the need for urine sampling rather than serum collection (Seibel, 2005; Szulc and Delmas, 2001). The modest correlation should therefore not be interpreted as contradictory to their shared link with bone resorption, but rather as evidence that the markers are biologically and analytically distinct. Clinically, patients with elevated DPD but normal CTX might warrant closer or more frequent monitoring, and DPD measurement could be considered when feasible, particularly in the context of denosumab discontinuation. Overall, combined CTX and DPD measurement may be potentially useful in specific clinical scenarios, such as treatment initiation, monitoring, or discontinuation, but its added clinical value remains to be established prospectively.

We are aware of the strengths and weaknesses of our study. The present study primarily describes the biochemical dynamics of bone resorption markers under denosumab therapy. While our findings suggest that urinary DPD may remain elevated in a subset of patients despite normalization of serum CTX, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. The study design was retrospective and did not include clinical outcome measures such as fracture incidence. Therefore, no causal inference can be made regarding the relationship between persistently elevated DPD levels and adverse clinical outcomes or therapeutic response. Some timepoints were missing for certain patients, which could introduce bias despite the use of mixed-effects models that accommodate incomplete data. In addition, the comparative subgroup analysis was restricted to treatment-naive patients with elevated baseline resorption markers, which reduced sample size and may limit generalizability. However, we also enabled an analysis of a large patient group, reflecting real-world clinical practice. The large CTX and DPD correlation dataset was derived from a broader osteological patient population and should therefore be interpreted separately from the denosumab-treated cohort.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings suggest that the combined evaluation of serum CTX and urinary DPD may provide complementary biochemical information on bone resorption under denosumab therapy. While urine sampling requires additional effort, the inclusion of DPD assessment could enhance the understanding of individual variability in treatment response, particularly in complex clinical settings such as therapy initiation or discontinuation. However, these observations are based on biochemical data and do not establish a direct link to clinical outcomes. Future prospective studies are needed to define optimal timing, thresholds, and the combined use of DPD and CTX for monitoring osteoporosis treatment and guiding clinical decision-making during and after denosumab therapy.
