Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome in adolescents
Authors: Allison Furness, Sarah McQuillan
Published: 2026

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) affects approximately 8% of adolescents1

Previously called polycystic ovarian syndrome, PMOS is characterized by the presence of both menstrual irregularities and clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism. Adolescents who meet only 1 of these criteria should be considered at risk of PMOS and reassessed longitudinally.1 Irregular menstrual cycles are common in this group because of hypothalamic–pituitary axis immaturity, complicating diagnosis.1,2 Menstrual irregularities are defined as amenorrhea greater than 90 days in the first year after menarche, greater than 45 days from 1 to 3 years postmenarche, or greater than 35 days after 3 years.1 Hyperandrogenism may manifest as hirsutism, severe acne, or elevated serum androgens.1,2

Pelvic ultrasonography is not recommended for the diagnosis of PMOS in adolescents

Multifollicular ovaries are common in adolescents, as peak ovarian maturity has not yet been reached.2 No definitive ultrasonography criteria exist to identify polycystic ovary morphology in adolescents.3 Ultrasonography should be reserved for patients at least 8 years postmenarche or for those with other indications.1

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion

Investigations for other causes of menstrual irregularities should be undertaken to rule out pregnancy, premature ovarian insufficiency, thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, and non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Initial investigations include testing β-human chorionic gonadotropin, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, estradiol, thyroid-stimulating hormone, prolactin, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone levels, as well as total and free testosterone.4 These should be completed on day 2 to 4 of the patient’s menstrual cycle, or randomly if the patient is amenorrheic. Marked or rapidly progressive hyperandrogenism raises concern for androgen-secreting tumours and warrants urgent evaluation.2,5

Adolescents with PMOS require counselling regarding contraception

Misconceptions about PMOS being associated with infertility can lead to unintended pregnancies. The combined oral contraceptive pill is an effective first-line option for PMOS, improving menstrual regularity and hyperandrogenism when used for at least 6 months.1

The condition is associated with metabolic and psychological comorbidities

Adolescents with PMOS have increased risks of insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, anxiety, depression, and body-image concerns.1,5 Longitudinal management includes screening for and managing metabolic and mental health concerns and promoting healthy lifestyle interventions.5
