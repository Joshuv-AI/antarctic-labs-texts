On Complementation of Nondeterministic Finite Automata without Full Determinization (Technical Report)
Lukáš Holík, Ondřej Lengál, Juraj Major, Adéla Štěpková, Jan Strejček

Abstract
Complementation of finite automata is a basic operation used in numerous applications. The standard way to complement a nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA) is to transform it into an equivalent deterministic finite automaton (DFA) and complement the DFA. The DFA can, however, be exponentially larger than the corresponding NFA. In this paper, we study several alternative approaches to complementation, which are based either on reverse powerset construction or on two novel constructions that exploit a commonly occurring structure of NFAs. Our experiment on a large data set shows that using a different than the classical approach can, in many cases, yield significantly smaller complements.

Introduction

Complementation of
finite automata
is an operation with many applications
in formal methods.
It is used, e.g.,
in regular model checking
[
KestenMMPS01
,
BouajjaniJNT00
,
BouajjaniHRV12
]
,
representing extended regular expressions
[

]

to implement negation in automata-based decision procedures for logics such as

or monadic

Complementing
deterministic finite automata

transitions to this state, and swap accepting and non-accepting states.

In practice,
nondeterministic finite automata
(NFAs) are often favored over DFAs due to their potentially much
(up to exponentially) smaller size.
The classical approach to complementing NFAs goes through determinization of
the input NFA using the

into a DFA and then using DFA complementation.
While easy to implement, this approach is prone to cause a blow-up in the number
of states, as determinizing an NFA with
n
states may, in the worst case,
result in a DFA with
2^{n}
states
[
RabinS59
]
and the size of the
complement would then also be exponential.
Some automata-based algorithms are highly sensitive to the sizes
of complement automata, such as decision procedures of certain
logics
[

,
HabermehlHHHL24
,
Buchi60
,
ElgaardKM98
,
GlennG96
,
KelbMMG97
,
FiedorHLV19
,
FiedorHJLV17
,
AyariB00
]

where the output of the complement may be the basic structure over which another
complementation is performed (usually after projection, which can turn
a potentially deterministic automaton into a nondeterministic one);
for some of the logics, the increase in the size of the complement is
the underlying cause of their non-elementary complexity
[
Meyer72
]
.
Due to this, some of the applications tried to avoid complementation altogether,
for instance using symbolic
techniques
[
VarataluVE25
,
FiedorHLV19
,
FiedorHJLV17
]
.
This is, however, not always possible or feasible, as symbolic techniques often

Since the lower bounds on the worst-case sizes of deterministic and complement

Preliminaries

An
alphabet

\Sigma
is a finite nonempty set of

symbols
.
A
word
over
\Sigma
is a sequence
u=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}
where
u_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
with its

length

n
denoted by
|u|
.
The
empty word
is denoted by
\varepsilon
.
The set of all words over
\Sigma
is denoted by
\Sigma^{*}
, and
its subsets are
languages
over
\Sigma
.
The
concatenation
of
u
with a word
v=u_{n+1}\ldots u_{m}
is the word

uv=u_{1}\ldots u_{m}
.
The
reverse
of
u
is the word
\mathit{rev}(u)=u_{n}u_{n-1}\ldots u_{1}
.
The
concatenation
of languages
L,L^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is the
language
L.L^{\prime}=\{uv\mid u\in L,v\in L^{\prime}\}
.
The
reverse
of
L
is the language

\mathit{rev}(L)=\{\mathit{rev}(w)\mid w\in L\}
and its

complement
is the language

\mathit{co}(L)=\Sigma^{*}\smallsetminus L
.

Finite Automata.

A
nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA)
is defined as a tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,I,F)
, where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
\Sigma
is an alphabet,

\delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
is a
transition
relation
,
I\subseteq Q
is a set of
initial states
, and

F\subseteq Q
is a set of
accepting states
(also called

final states
). We write
q\xrightarrow{a}r\in\delta

instead of
(q,a,r)\in\delta
. If
\delta
is clear from the context,
we write just
q\xrightarrow{a}r
.
The
size
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as
|\mathcal{A}|=|Q|
.
We abuse the notation and use
\delta
also as the function

\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow 2^{Q}
defined as

\delta(q,a)=\{r\in Q\mid q\xrightarrow{a}r\}
.

We also extend the
function
\delta
to sets of states
P\subseteq Q
as

\delta(P,a)=\bigcup_{q\in P}\delta(q,a)
.
A
deterministic finite automaton (DFA)
is an NFA
with
|I|=1
and

|\delta(q,a)|\leq 1
for all
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
. A DFA is

complete
if
|\delta(q,a)|=1
for all
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
.
A
run
of an NFA
\mathcal{A}
over a word
v=v_{1}v_{2}\ldots v_{n}
is a
sequence of transitions
q_{0}\xrightarrow{v_{1}}q_{1},q_{1}\xrightarrow{v_{2}}q_{2},\ldots,q_{n-1}\xrightarrow{v_{n}}q_{n}
with
q_{0}\in I
.
It is
accepting
if
q_{n}\in F
.
A state that appears in a run over some word
v\in\Sigma^{*}
is

reachable
, else it is
unreachable
.
A state is
reachable from a state

q
if it is reachable in the NFA
(Q,\Sigma,\delta,\{q\},F)
.

\mathcal{A}

accepts
the
language
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
of all words over
\Sigma
for which it has an accepting run.
Automata
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are

equivalent
iff
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
.

The
reverse
of
\mathcal{A}
is the NFA

\mathit{rev}(\mathcal{A})=(Q,\Sigma,\mathit{rev}(\delta),F,I)
, where

\mathit{rev}(\delta)=\{r\xrightarrow{a}q\mid q\xrightarrow{a}r\in\delta\}
.
\mathcal{A}
is called
reverse-deterministic
iff
\mathit{rev}(\mathcal{A})
is
a DFA.
An NFA
\mathcal{C}
is called a
complement
of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to an alphabet
\Lambda
if

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{C}\right)=\Lambda^{*}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
If
\Lambda
is not specified, we assume that
\Lambda=\Sigma
.
Given two NFAs
\mathcal{A}_{1}=(Q_{1},\Sigma,\delta_{1},I_{1},F_{1})
and

\mathcal{A}_{2}=(Q_{2},\Sigma,\delta_{2},I_{2},F_{2})
where

Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}=\emptyset
, their
union
is the NFA

\mathcal{A}_{1}\Cup\mathcal{A}_{2}=(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2},\Sigma,\delta_{1}\cup\delta_{2},I_{1}\cup I_{2},F_{1}\cup F_{2})
.
A
strongly connected component (SCC)
of
\mathcal{A}
is a maximal subset

C\subseteq Q
in which every state is reachable from every state.
Note that the term
component
will be used in a more general
sense later.

Forward Powerset Complementation.

The standard complementation first uses the
powerset
construction
to transform a given NFA

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,I,F)
into an equivalent complete DFA

\mathit{det}(\mathcal{A})=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},I^{\prime},F^{\prime})
, where
Q^{\prime}=2^{Q}
,

\delta^{\prime}=\{P\xrightarrow{a}\delta(P,a)\mid P\in Q^{\prime},a\in\Sigma\}
,

I^{\prime}=\{I\}
, and
F^{\prime}=\{P\in Q^{\prime}\mid P\cap F\neq\emptyset\}
. In
the following, we assume that
\mathit{det}(\mathcal{A})
does not contain unreachable
states (only the reachable part of
Q^{\prime}
is constructed). This does not
improve the upper bound on the size of the DFA, which is still

2^{|Q|}
.
Given a complete DFA
\mathcal{D}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,I,F)
, its complement can be constructed as

\mathit{co}(\mathcal{D})=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,I,Q\smallsetminus F)
.
A complement of an NFA
\mathcal{A}
can thus be constructed as
\mathit{co}(\mathit{det}(\mathcal{A}))
.
We call this construction
forward powerset complementation
.

Heuristic for Forward vs. Reverse Powerset.

Naturally, the powerset construction can be efficient
in one direction (forward or reverse) while causing a blow-up in the
other. To address this, we designed a cheap heuristic to choose the more
favorable direction for a given NFA, for cases when running
a portfolio is too expensive.

The heuristic sums the sizes of all powerset successors of each state
in a given NFA. Formally, for an NFA

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,I,F)
, we define

\mathit{sc}(q)=\{\delta(q,a)\mid a\in\Sigma\}
for each state

q\in Q
and compute
\mathit{powSC}(\mathcal{A})=|I|+\sum_{q\in Q}\sum_{S\in\mathit{sc}(q)}|S|
.
We emphasize that if
q
has the same successors under two different
symbols
a,b\in\Sigma
, then the set
\delta(q,a)=\delta(q,b)

contributes to the sum only once.

Intuitively, a higher value of
\mathit{powSC}(\mathcal{A})
should indicate a
higher number of states produced by the powerset construction applied
to
\mathcal{A}
. Hence, comparing
\mathit{powSC}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathit{powSC}(\mathit{rev}(\mathcal{A}))

gives a hint which of the powerset
constructions has a greater risk of blow-up. The heuristic’s performance is
experimentally evaluated in

4.1
Sequential Complementation

Sequential complementation
builds a potentially nondeterministic
complement
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{A}
from a complete DFA
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathit{det}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and an NFA complement
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. The
technique is called sequential complementation because we first
complement
\mathcal{A}_{2}
using an arbitrary complementation approach,

\mathcal{A}
.

The construction is based on the following observation. The language

\mathit{co}(L_{1}.\{c\}.L_{2})
consists of words
w
such that, for all pairs of
words
u,v
satisfying
ucv=w
, if
u\in L_{1}
then

v\in\mathit{co}(L_{2})
.
Note that if
w
does not contain any
c
, the

Therefore,
\mathcal{C}
will simulate
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
and whenever a final state of

\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
is visited, that is,
\mathcal{C}

.

Formally, let
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime}_{1},\Sigma,\delta^{\prime}_{1},\{q^{\prime}_{0}\},F^{\prime}_{1})

We construct an NFA

representing

as follows.

•

\mathcal{C}
’s states are pairs composed of the current state of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1}
and states of instances of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
,
\widetilde{Q}=Q^{\prime}_{1}\times 2^{\widetilde{Q}_{2}}
.

•

For each
a\in\Sigma
, the transition relation
\widetilde{\delta}

simulates the corresponding transition of
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
and arbitrary

\mathcal{C}_{2}

\mathcal{C}_{2}

\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\prime}
