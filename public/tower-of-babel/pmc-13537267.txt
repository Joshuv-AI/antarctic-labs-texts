Mechanical cues of an interpenetrating polysaccharide matrix regulate self-assembly of collagen fibers
Authors: Fatemeh Tavakoli Joorabi, Nicholas J. Derr, Zecheng Li, Bryan A. Nerger, Chris H. Rycroft, David J. Mooney, Kyle H. Vining
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collagen molecules self-assemble into supramolecular fibers within a molecularly crowded, polysaccharide-rich extracellular matrix (ECM) that has fluid-like, viscoelastic properties. Here, we determine that the viscoelasticity of alginate networks regulates the assembly of type I collagen fibers. The viscoelasticity and shear moduli of the alginate network were tuned by the polymer weight percentage and degree of cooperative ionic and covalent norbornene-tetrazine cross-linking. Stepwise shear strain applied to covalently cross-linked hydrogels generated higher stress than in ionic hydrogels. Hydrogels with reduced viscoelasticity also showed reduced water permeability. Second-harmonic generation confocal imaging revealed that decreasing viscoelasticity significantly suppressed collagen fiber self-assembly. Simulations demonstrated mechanical coupling between the hydrogel network and the aggregate size of collagen molecules, which was consistent with experimental results showing impaired rate and magnitude of self-assembly in covalently cross-linked networks. These results provide a framework for understanding how ECM mechanical properties can influence the assembly and organization of fibrillar macromolecules.

INTRODUCTION

Type I collagen is the most abundant structural protein in the human body, representing nearly 30% of the total protein mass and forming the primary fibrous scaffold of the extracellular matrix (ECM) (comprising up to 90% of the total ECM protein content) (1, 2). Abnormal regulation of type I collagen deposition and organization is implicated in a wide range of pathologies, including fibrosis, cardiovascular disease, and cancer (3). Excessive deposition and cross-linking of collagen type I (Col I) stiffen tissues in fibrotic disease (4), while tumor-associated collagen remodeling promotes invasion and metastasis (5). These observations highlight that understanding how type I collagen assembles within the ECM is not only fundamental to tissue biology but also crucial to uncovering disease mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets.

The biosynthesis of Col I assembly begins with the synthesis of individual pro-α peptide chains, which are the fundamental building blocks of procollagen. These pro-α chains assemble into procollagen molecules that fold into a triple-helical conformation with the assistance of C-terminal propeptides by zipping-up mechanism (6). Once secreted into the ECM, the loose terminal propeptides are cleaved by metalloproteinases, producing tropocollagen monomers of ≈300 nm length that are competent for fibril formation (7). Tropocollagen molecules align in a quarter-staggered configuration to form microfibrils, the basic repeating units of fibrils, characterized by the 67-nm D-period. This arrangement is stabilized through intermolecular cross-links at the N- and C-terminal telopeptides (8–11). Under physiological conditions (including appropriate temperature, pH, and molecular crowding) tropocollagen spontaneously self-assembles into collagen fibrils, which further organize into mature collagen fibers (12, 13). Although the biochemical pathways that regulate collagen self-assembly have been extensively studied (7, 14–16), it is less understood how the physical properties of the surrounding ECM regulate fibrillogenesis.

The ECM can be conceptualized as a fibrous polymer network whose stiffness is determined by fiber rigidity, cross-link density, network connectivity, alignment, and compaction (17, 18). However, tissues are not purely elastic: They are viscoelastic, which means that they dissipate stress over time, and they can accumulate residual stress under sustained deformation (19, 20). Meanwhile, the ECM also exhibits finite permeability as a porous network, with pore size, tortuosity, connectivity, compaction, and cross-link density controlling how freely water and mobile molecules move through the matrix (18, 21, 22). In polymer networks, modest changes in cross-linking or compressive deformation can change permeability by orders of magnitude, thus altering the solute diffusivity (23). Accumulated stress, resulting from constrained matrix remodeling, can distort network geometry and densify local pores, further restricting both the translational and rotational mobility of tropocollagen monomers (17, 20).

From a physical standpoint, collagen fibrillogenesis is a hierarchical self-assembly of biopolymers characterized by thermally activated nucleation and growth (16, 24, 25). Collagen networks fall within the class of semiflexible polymer system, whose emergent mechanical behavior arises from collective network connectivity (26–28). These fibrous matrices exhibit nonlinear elasticity, strain-stiffening behavior, and architecture-dependent stress transmission characteristic of fibrous polymer gels (26, 28–31). In hydrated environments, poroelastic coupling between the polymer network and interstitial fluid further links structural reorganization to stress redistribution (32, 33). Although many studies have examined individual mechanical properties of extracellular matrices, such as stiffness or viscoelasticity (21, 34), it remains unclear how the coupled influence of permeability, stress relaxation, and stiffness regulates the growth of collagen fibrils within a confined 3D environment.

To address this, we developed an artificial ECM hydrogel that incorporates both ionic and covalent cross-linking. Alginate is a polysaccharide that forms ionic bonds with calcium ions (Ca2+), which mimics the ionic interactions found in native ECM (Fig. 1, A and B). Alginate was modified with norbornene (Nb) and tetrazine (Tz) functional groups to facilitate covalent cross-linking using click chemistry (Fig. 1, A and C). This dual cross-linking strategy enables the precise tuning of the mechanical properties of the matrix. To isolate the influence of matrix mechanics on collagen fibrillogenesis, we controlled the sequence of network formation. Alginate hydrogels were first fully cross-linked at 4°C, a temperature at which collagen remains soluble and does not assemble. This step establishes a preexisting polymer network with defined mechanical properties. After complete gelation (3.5 hours), the temperature was raised to physiological conditions (37°C) to initiate collagen self-assembly within the already formed polymer matrix (Fig. 1D). Because fibrillogenesis occurs after the polymer network is established, collagen fibers assemble under the geometric and mechanical constraints imposed by the surrounding hydrogel.

This sequential assembly strategy mimics key aspects of in vivo collagen deposition, where newly secreted tropocollagen assembles within an existing ECM that already has defined mechanical properties. In contrast to many collagen-alginate systems where both networks form simultaneously (35–39), our approach decouples matrix formation from collagen fibrillogenesis. This design allows us to systematically interrogate how independently controlled mechanical parameters (including stiffness, viscoelastic stress relaxation, and permeability) regulate the hierarchical assembly of collagen fibers within interpenetrating polysaccharide matrices. Combined experimental measurements and computational modeling provide a framework for quantifying how these mechanical properties influence fibril growth, organization, and overall ECM architecture.

RESULTS

Fabrication and mechanical properties of the hydrogel system

Alginate G-blocks (glucuronic acid-rich segments) cooperatively coordinate Ca2+ ions through their carboxyl groups (Fig. 1B), forming ionically cross-linked networks with viscoelastic, time-dependent behavior (40, 41). To further tune the viscoelastic response, alginate chains were covalently functionalized with norbornene (Nb) and tetrazine (Tz) groups via carbodiimide coupling [N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC)/N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)], enabling subsequent Nb-Tz inverse-electron-demand Diels-Alder click reactions that introduce permanent covalent cross-links within the polysaccharide backbone (Fig. 1C) (35, 42). This dual-cross-linking strategy reinforces the ionic network without reacting with proteins or cells, allowing orthogonal control over stiffness and relaxation behavior.

C-Alg [collagen-alginate interpenetrating network (IPN)] and C-NbTz (collagen-alginate modified with NbTz IPN) hydrogels enable tunable control over the mechanical microenvironment in which collagen self-assembly occurs. Because collagen remains soluble and unassembled at 4°C but undergoes supramolecular fibrillogenesis upon warming to 37°C (Fig. 1D), rheological measurements performed at 4°C characterize the mechanical properties of the polymer network before collagen fiber formation. This approach isolates the viscoelastic properties of the alginate-based matrix that defines the mechanical environment experienced by collagen monomers during subsequent fibrillogenesis.

To quantify these preassembly mechanical properties, we performed oscillatory shear rheology to characterize gelation kinetics and the frequency-dependent viscoelastic behavior of collagen-alginate hydrogels (C-Alg and C-NbTz). Time sweep measurements were conducted at 4°C (0.1 Hz, 2% strain) to monitor the evolution of the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) as the hydrogel networks formed. For consistency across conditions, the time-dependent evolution of G′ and G′′ is reported over the first 4 hours of gelation (14,400 s), enabling comparison of the mechanical properties of ionically cross-linked (C-Alg) and covalently reinforced (C-NbTz) networks before collagen assembly.

For ionically cross-linked alginate hydrogels (C-Alg), increasing Ca2+ concentration resulted in progressive stiffening of the network. The plateau storage modulus increased from approximately ∼10 Pa at 0.1% Ca2+ to ∼703 Pa at 0.2% Ca2+ and ∼2642 Pa at 0.3% Ca2+, while the loss modulus increased from ∼1.4 to ∼54 Pa and ∼209 Pa across the same Ca2+ concentrations. Therefore, the loss tangent (tanδ) decreased from ∼0.143 at 0.1% Ca2+ to ∼0.077 at 0.2% Ca2+ and ∼0.079 at 0.3% Ca2+ (Fig. 2A). Because tanδ=G′′/G′, this reduction reflects the increasing dominance of elastic energy storage over viscous energy dissipation as the density of ionic cross-links increases. A similar trend was observed for covalently reinforced hydrogels (C-NbTz) prepared with constant Ca2+ concentration (0.3% w/v) but varying Nb:Tz cross-link ratios. Increasing covalent cross-link density led to progressively higher stiffness. The plateau G′ increased from ∼2442 Pa for Nb:Tz = 2:1 to ∼3091 Pa for 1:2 and ∼3974 Pa for 1:1, while G′′ increased from ∼92 to ∼116 Pa and ∼131 Pa, respectively. In contrast, tanδ remained low (∼0.033 to 0.038), indicating highly elastic network behavior (Fig. 2B). Compared with C-Alg gels, C-NbTz hydrogels reached substantially higher storage moduli and exhibited lower loss tangents, consistent with the introduction of permanent covalent cross-links that stabilize the network and suppress viscous relaxation. Frequency sweep measurements (0.01 to 25 Hz, 2% strain) further revealed that C-Alg hydrogels exhibited modest increases in both G′ and G′′ with frequency, along with relatively higher and weakly frequency-dependent tanδ, indicative of a softer, more dissipative network. In contrast, C-NbTz hydrogels maintained substantially higher G′ with minimal frequency dependence and consistently low tanδ, confirming a more elastic, covalently reinforced network behavior (fig. Shear strain–dependent rheology and permeability of the alginate network

We hypothesized that hierarchical collagen fibrillogenesis generates local volumetric reorganization and internal stress within the surrounding hydrogel network as aggregate size increases several orders of magnitude during assembly. In our precross-linked alginate matrix, individual tropocollagen molecules (length ∼300 nm, D ∼1.5 nm) have a cross-sectional dimension smaller than the characteristic alginate pore size (5 to 10 nm). Their elongated molecular length greatly exceeds the mesh size, meaning that transport requires threading through multiple pores and is therefore sterically constrained. As monomers laterally assemble into fibrils (D ∼100 nm) and subsequently bundle into micrometer-scale fibers (D ∼10 μm), the growing collagen structures rapidly exceed the native mesh size of the hydrogel. This geometric mismatch necessitates deformation of the surrounding alginate chains (D ∼1 nm), leading to localized volumetric expansion, chain stretching, and the development of tensile and compressive stress fields within the network. Thus, collagen assembly in this system does not occur in a mechanically neutral environment; rather, fibril growth progressively perturbs the preexisting polymer network, creating dynamic mechanical feedback that can regulate fibril thickening, bundle formation, and overall matrix architecture (Fig. 3A) (43, 44). We propose that in NbTz hydrogels, covalent cross-links restrict polymer chain mobility and diminish stress relaxation, leading to higher accumulated stress within the network (Fig. 3A). We measured the nonlinear shear response of Alg and NbTz hydrogels up to 100% strain (fig. S3) to evaluate the rheology of the gels under large shear deformations (Fig. 3, B and C). Pregeled hydrogel discs were preloaded with 5% axial compression to ensure full plate contact and to allow compressive stress to relax before testing (fig. S4). Both continuous and stepwise shear tests were performed to evaluate stress accumulation and relaxation behavior (Fig. 3D). Shear stress fully relaxed in Alg hydrogels after each shear step, whereas NbTz hydrogels exhibited more linear stress-strain behavior and accumulated stress over successive cycles (Fig. 3E). During stress relaxation, NbTz hydrogels retained approximately 80% of the applied stress, compared to 60% in Alg hydrogels (Fig. 3, F and G, 5% strain). This trend was consistent across a broader strain range (5 to 40%), where NbTz hydrogels continued to exhibit slower stress relaxation and greater stress retention than Alg hydrogels (fig. S5). We hypothesized that these differences in stress relaxation will affect collagen fibril alignment and fiber growth.

The observed differences in storage modulus (Fig. 2) and stress relaxation (Fig. 3, D and F) indicate underlying changes in network architecture and cross-link density. Because these structural features govern both effective diffusivity and mechanical resistance within the hydrogel mesh, we next evaluated permeability to determine whether intrinsic nanoscale transport constraints differ between gel systems. Given that collagen fibrillogenesis occurs within a precross-linked porous network under static conditions, assembly kinetics are expected to depend on diffusion and confinement within the matrix rather than on bulk convective mixing (45).

To quantify permeability, we measured fluid transport through the hydrogel network during controlled axial compression using a rheometer equipped with optical imaging. The volumetric flow rate, Q, of fluid exiting the gel was recorded during compression (Fig. 4A). Flux, q, was calculated using q=Q/A, where A denotes the effective cross-sectional area for fluid outflow. Permeability, k, was determined using Darcy’s law, q=(−kΔP)/(μl), where μ represents the dynamic viscosity of the fluid, ΔP is the pressure difference across the sample, and L is the effective distance for fluid flow corresponding to the hydrogel radius (Fig. 4B). These measurements revealed that Alg hydrogels exhibit significantly higher permeability than NbTz hydrogels (Fig. 4C).

These permeability differences have important implications for collagen fibrillogenesis within the network. Continued fibril growth requires deformation of alginate chains and local volumetric reorganization of the matrix, a process that necessarily involves displacement of interstitial solvent and stretching of the surrounding polymer network. The ability of the matrix to accommodate this reorganization is governed in part by its hydraulic permeability, which determines how easily solvent can flow through the porous polymer network. In more highly cross-linked matrices, permeability is reduced, limiting the rate at which interstitial fluid can redistribute during fibril growth. Because fibril thickening and bundle formation increase the local collagen volume fraction, solvent must be displaced from the surrounding matrix to accommodate this structural expansion. When permeability is low and stress relaxation is limited, the network resists this solvent redistribution and polymer deformation, effectively increasing the energetic barrier to continued fibril thickening and bundle formation. Consistent with this model, the reduced permeability of NbTz hydrogels likely promotes the accumulation of mechanical stress on alginate chains surrounding assembling collagen fibrils, thereby restricting fibril elongation and limiting the formation of larger fibrillar structures (Fig. 4D).

Collagen fiber self-assembly in collagen-alginate hydrogels

We next investigated the impact of hydrogel composition and mechanical properties on collagen organization within the hydrogel matrix. We used second-harmonic generation (SHG) imaging to quantify collagen self-assembly in 1.5% (w/v) hydrogels with varying cross-linking chemistries and calcium ion (Ca2+) concentrations (Fig. 5, A and B) to achieve a specific range of stiffness (G′), energy dissipation (tanδ), and permeability. Results revealed that collagen assembly was impaired in C-NbTz hydrogels compared to C-Alg hydrogels, as evidenced by decreased SHG signal intensity and increased fibril dispersion (Fig. 5, A and B). A similar trend was observed in 1% (w/v) hydrogels, indicating that reducing the overall polymer content (Alg/NbTz chains) did not alter the general trend from C-Alg to C-NbTz (fig. S6).

Quantitative image analysis using ImageJ and CT-FIRE revealed pronounced differences in collagen fiber organization between C-Alg and C-NbTz hydrogels. The overall collagen area fraction was significantly lower in C-NbTz hydrogels compared to C-Alg hydrogels, indicating that fewer or smaller collagen networks formed under the more restrictive mechanical environment. Collagen fibers in C-NbTz hydrogels were markedly shorter, narrower, and straighter than those in C-Alg hydrogels, reflecting the influence of matrix mechanics on fiber morphology (Fig. 5C and figs. S12 to S14). At 1.5% (w/v) alginate, the matrix is denser and stiffer compared to 1% (w/v) (fig. S6), which led to shorter fibers and a reduced area fraction of collagen with an increase in fiber width. These findings highlight how matrix density and stiffness modulate collagen fiber morphology and organization, providing critical insights into the mechanics of collagen assembly. Collagen gels formed in alginate matrices lacking Ca2+ cross-linking (C-Alg, C-Nb, and C-Tz) exhibit comparable fiber area fraction and mean fiber thickness despite backbone modification with norbornene or tetrazine. In contrast, only the dual-modified C-NbTz hydrogel suppresses fibrillogenesis, indicating that baseline changes in alginate chemistry or hydrophobicity alone do not account for altered collagen nanoscale transport or assembly in the absence of network cross-linking (fig. S16). Furthermore, in control experiments where alginate and collagen were allowed to gel simultaneously at 37°C, collagen fiber width, length, and overall morphology remained comparable across gel formulations (fig. S17, A and B). Together, these results demonstrate that backbone modification or baseline hydration differences are insufficient to disrupt collagen assembly, and that sequential network formation, where collagen assembles within a preformed matrix, is required to reveal mechanics-dependent modulation of fibrillogenesis.

Confocal and SHG imaging further confirmed differences in collagen assembly across gel conditions (fig. S18). Representative images show labeled collagen (detecting both soluble tropocollagen and fibrillar collagen) and SHG signal (detecting only structured collagen fibrils) for C-Alg and C-NbTz hydrogels at varying Nb:Tz ratios (fig. S18A). Quantification of labeled collagen revealed similar total collagen area fractions across all conditions (fig. S18B), consistent with the constant collagen concentration (4 mg/ml). In contrast, SHG imaging showed a significant reduction in fibrillar collagen area fraction in C-NbTz hydrogels compared to C-Alg gels, with the lowest fibrillar signal at Nb:Tz = 1:1 (fig. S18B), indicating reduced fibrillogenesis. Colocalization analysis further supported this observation: Both the fraction of labeled collagen monomers colocalized with fibrillar collagen and the fraction of structured fibrils colocalized with labeled collagen decreased with increasing Nb:Tz cross-link density (fig. S18, C and D). Notably, the percentage of fibrils colocalized with labeled collagen was consistently higher than the fraction of labeled collagen colocalized with fibrils because SHG detects only fibrils, whereas the fluorescence signal includes both fibrillar and soluble collagen pools. Together, these results indicate that while total collagen distribution remains constant, increasing matrix cross-link density suppresses collagen fibrillogenesis rather than simply altering collagen spatial distribution.

We performed multiple linear regression analyses on 24 hydrogel formulations [1 and 1.5% (w/v) alginate matrices, including C-Alg and C-NbTz gels prepared with Ca2+ concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3% (w/v) and Nb:Tz ratios of 2:1, 1:2, and 1:1] to identify which mechanical and transport parameters most strongly regulate collagen fiber architecture, using storage modulus (G′), viscoelasticity (tanδ), and permeability as predictors (Fig. 5D). The models revealed distinct but complementary relationships across collagen structural features, explaining most of the variance in assembly behavior. For collagen area fraction, the overall model was highly significant (R2 = 0.72 and P < 0.0001), with permeability emerging as the dominant positive predictor (β = +1.45 × 1016 and P = 0.0048), showing that higher matrix permeability facilitates fibril diffusion and lateral aggregation, which results in more extensive network coverage. In contrast, G′ (β = −1.028 and P = 0.1518) and tanδ (β = −37.78 and P = 0.5557) showed negative but nonsignificant trends, suggesting that increased stiffness and reduced stress relaxation or viscoelasticity limit overall collagen fiber formation. For fiber length, the regression model (R2 = 0.73 and P < 0.0001) revealed that higher G′ was associated with shorter fibers (β = −0.9063 and P = 0.0606), while greater tanδ correlated positively with fiber elongation (β = 76.50 and P = 0.0812). Thus, both matrix stiffness and viscoelastic relaxation jointly modulate fibril elongation. Permeability also displayed a positive but nonsignificant effect (β = +2.089 × 1015 and P = 0.4972). Fiber width was similarly governed by mechanical effects (R2 = 0.59 and P = 0.0004), where stiffness showed a near-significant negative correlation (β = −0.1298 and P = 0.0581), and tanδ a weaker positive trend (β = 8.344 and P = 0.1729). Permeability exhibited a negligible and nonsignificant effect (β = 1.019 × 1014 and P = 0.8143). Last, fiber straightness (R2 = 0.33 and P = 0.0429) displayed a significant dependence on tanδ (β = −0.2614 and P = 0.0427), with lower viscoelasticity yielding straighter, more aligned fibers and higher viscoelasticity producing wavier morphologies. Stiffness (G′) showed a weak positive but nonsignificant trend (β = +0.001577 and P = 0.2465), suggesting that increased rigidity may modestly enhance fiber alignment, while permeability also exhibited a nonsignificant positive effect (β = +1.3324 × 1013 and P = 0.1437) (fig. S15).

Because multiple matrix properties (e.g., storage modulus, stress relaxation, and permeability) change simultaneously across gel formulations, we next applied principal components analysis (PCA) to identify which physical parameters most strongly correlate with collagen fiber architecture. We performed PCA on the 24 hydrogel formulations to identify the dominant variables governing collagen fiber organization and to visualize how mechanical and compositional features contribute to these differences (Fig. 5, E and F). The first two components accounted for 87.23% of the total variance (PC1 = 73.2% and PC2 = 14.03%), satisfying the Kaiser criterion (λ > 1). PC1 represented the primary mechanical axis, capturing the contrast between stiffness and stress accumulation versus viscoelastic relaxation and permeability. The loading plot (Fig. 5E) showed that G′ (0.611), shear stress (0.928), and collagen fibers’ straightness (0.583) loaded positively along PC1, whereas permeability (−0.928), relaxation percentage (−0.928), tanδ (−0.918), and collagen morphological parameters, such as area% (−0.901), length (−0.939), and width (−0.868) loaded negatively. Thus, stiffer, more elastic hydrogels promoted straighter but shorter and thinner collagen fibers, while more permeable and viscoelastic matrices supported larger, longer, and thicker networks. PC2 (14.03%) captured secondary variations related to transport and local relaxation. Fiber straightness (0.716), permeability (0.361), relaxation percentage (0.361), and stiffness (G′, 0.339) contributed positively along PC2, while fiber width (−0.39), length (−0.25), and shear stress (−0.361) loaded negatively, and tanδ (0.175) and collagen area% (0.029) showed weak contributions. These results indicate that PC2 defines a relaxation-permeability-viscoelasticity axis. Matrices with greater permeability and faster stress relaxation, along with moderately higher tanδ, promote more extensive collagen assembly. This relationship is reflected in higher fiber area% and the formation of straighter, more uniformly distributed collagen networks. The PCA score plot (Fig. 5F) showed a clear separation by cross-linking chemistry along PC1: C-NbTz hydrogels clustered at positive values, reflecting higher stiffness and stress retention, while C-Alg hydrogels clustered at negative values, corresponding to greater relaxation, permeability, collagen area%, width, and length. Overall, PCA revealed that permeability and viscoelastic relaxation cluster most strongly with collagen fiber morphology metrics, indicating that transport and stress-relaxation behavior are dominant predictors of fibril formation, whereas storage modulus alone explains less of the observed variation.

Modeling and time-course analysis of collagen assembly in alginate hydrogels

We hypothesized that the differences in collagen self-assembly observed between C-Alg and C-NbTz hydrogels arise from changes in the effective pore size of the surrounding hydrogel network. Increased cross-linking in C-NbTz is expected to reduce pore size, which restricts the nanoscale transport of tropocollagen monomers and small assembling clusters required for fibrillogenesis. In the model, these assembling clusters are referred to as collagen aggregates, representing groups of collagen molecules that progressively associate during fibril formation. To describe this process, we developed a simplified kinetic model that tracks how the population of aggregates evolves over time. Rather than treating each discrete aggregate size separately, aggregate size is approximated as a continuous variable, which allows the assembly process to be described by a smooth distribution of aggregate size or, equivalently, mass. This approximation simplifies the mathematical description while still capturing the gradual growth of aggregates during fibrillogenesis. In addition, we neglect spatial dependence, instead focusing on time evolution of the distribution in mass space. This reduction focuses the model on the key process controlling fibril formation (the transport-limited growth of collagen aggregates) while avoiding unnecessary spatial complexity. Although simplified, the model captures the essential physics governing collagen assembly under confinement. In particular, it reproduces the qualitative trends observed experimentally, where increasing covalent cross-link density reduces effective pore size and suppresses fibrillogenesis. The model further predicts that when covalent cross-links strongly restrict nanoscale transport, assembly can drop below measurable levels, while intermediate cross-linking produces a threshold regime in which reduced but still detectable fibril formation may occur.

DISCUSSION

Collagen assembly in nonfibrillar matrices was modeled with an IPN of Col I and chemically modified alginate polysaccharides. The alginate network provided a tunable concentration of covalent cross-linking, allowing one to vary the viscoelasticity and shear moduli of the network, independent of the concentration of collagen proteins. Alginate is a model polysaccharide that is similar in composition to the native hyaluronic acid ECM. Nonfibrillar material affects the structural properties of artificial and native tissues (47). For example, hyaluronic-binding proteins are reduced in aged skin, which can lead to increased plasticity (48). In our system, the alginate network is first established, and collagen assembly is subsequently initiated by neutralization and incubation at physiological temperature (37°C) and pH (7.5), allowing thermally driven fibrillogenesis to occur within a mechanically defined three-dimensional matrix. This platform therefore enables systematic examination of how the viscoelasticity and permeability of nonfibrillar polysaccharide networks regulate the hierarchical self-assembly of collagen.

Incremental stepwise shear rheology experiments in Fig. 3 revealed that NbTz hydrogels exhibit slower relaxation and higher accumulated stress compared to ionic Alg hydrogels. In Fig. 4, the lower permeability of NbTz compared to Alg hydrogels suggests that NbTz-cross-linked polysaccharides may restrict collagen diffusion, resulting in different collagen fiber morphologies. We propose that a reduction in permeability in NbTz hydrogels may concentrate collagen molecules in confined regions, resulting in shorter, denser fibers, while more permeable Alg hydrogels may facilitate more uniform diffusion and the growth of longer fibers. SHG confocal imaging was used to evaluate the functional consequences of hydrogel modifications on collagen structure, which has broader implications for designing ECM-mimicking materials in tissue engineering. Results in Fig. 5 revealed that decreasing viscoelasticity significantly suppressed the collagen fiber signal. The findings align with the other results of the study, highlighting the important role of matrix structure and mechanics in regulating collagen fiber self-assembly.

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that stiffness, viscoelasticity, and permeability together explained the majority of variation in collagen fiber area fraction, length, and width. Permeability was the strongest positive predictor of collagen deposition, whereas stiffness showed a negative correlation with fibril elongation and thickening. A model of collagen self-assembly demonstrated a coupling between the covalently cross-linked hydrogel network and the aggregate size of collagen molecules, which impaired the rate and magnitude of self-assembly. This finding was consistent with experimental time-course results, demonstrating that higher covalent cross-linking (Nb-Tz ratio) delays collagen fiber nucleation and limits growth.

Pockets of heterogeneous collagen fiber self-assembly persisted in C-NbTz hydrogels Fig. 6C, suggesting discontinuous spatial segregation of collagen fibers. We expect that generalizing our model to account for a spatial variable could yield similar results. In particular, when Aˆ is very near the transition point Aˆc, we would expect that variations in material properties. In those regions where assembly occurs, a local reduction in ϕ will induce mass transfer of monomers from nonassembly regions to assembling regions, increasing the existing spatial differences between the (respectively) positive and negative mass space velocities q. This inhomogeneity in the aggregate field ψ depends on reduced transport and diffusion of aggregates as opposed to monomers, which is exactly the behavior we associate with the effectively reduced pore size of the covalently cross-linked C-NbTz hydrogels.

Overall, these data present new insights into how the properties of tissue regulate the assembly of macromolecules into larger-scale, hierarchical structures within interpenetrating viscoelastic, nanoporous, three-dimensional ECM networks. The data were consistent with previous reports using poly(ethylene glycol) in an interpenetrating nonfibrillar matrix with collagen. Although viscoelasticity was not evaluated, molecular crowding by poly(ethylene glycol) produced matrices with tighter fibril networks that were less susceptible to proteinase-mediated degradation; however, it did not significantly alter matrix stiffness (49). Our study lays the groundwork for building designer hydrogel systems with a programmable structural hierarchy. For example, spatially controlled covalent reinforcement of the alginate polysaccharide network could provide spatial control of hierarchical assembly (50, 51). Our computational model can be used to predict composition-structural relationships of de novo polymeric systems. Ultimately, these systems will lead to rationally designed ECM-mimicking biomaterials with tunable spatial and hierarchical structures.

A limitation of the present study is that the proposed relationship between hydrogel mechanics, local stress accumulation, and collagen self-assembly could not be directly validated through spatially resolved measurements of stress within the bulk hydrogel network. Although our interpretations are supported by rheological characterization, permeability measurements, collagen structural analyses, and computational modeling, direct quantification of heterogeneous microscale or nanoscale stress distributions remains technically challenging in opaque three-dimensional hydrogels. Consequently, the mechanistic links between local stress fields and collagen assembly are inferred from complementary experimental observations rather than directly observed. Future advances in stress-mapping methodologies for three-dimensional biomaterials may enable direct validation of these relationships.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Alginate functionalization

Ultrapure sodium alginate with very low viscosity (ProNova UP VLVG, NovaMatrix), here referred to as Alg, was used, having an approximate molecular weight of 32 kDa and a ratio of guluronate to mannuronic monomer of ≥1.5, as specified by the manufacturer. Covalent modification was achieved by conjugating either 5-norbornene-2-methylamine (Nb; TCI America) or (4-(6-methyl-1,2,4,5-tetrazin-3-yl)phenyl)methanamine hydrochloride (Tz; Karebay Biochem) to Alg, producing Alg-Nb and Alg-Tz, respectively, following previously established protocols (35, 42, 52). The procedure targeted a theoretical degree of substitution of 25%. Initially, alginate was dissolved at a 1% (w/v) concentration in a 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid buffer with 0.3 M NaCl at pH 6.5, adjusted using NaOH. Next, EDC and NHS were added at a 2.5-fold molar excess relative to alginate carboxylic groups, with continuous stirring at 4°C. Then, Nb or Tz was incrementally added dropwise at a concentration of 4.7 mmol per gram of alginate, with both EDC, NHS, Nb, and Tz divided into four aliquots and introduced in four intervals over 2 hours each. The reaction proceeded for an additional 18 hours at 4°C with stirring. After reaction completion, the solution underwent four rounds of ultracentrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min each, followed by filtration through a 0.22-μm membrane. Subsequently, the product was dialyzed using a 3.5-kDa–molecular–weight cutoff membrane (Spectra/Por 3; Spectrum Labs) against a stepwise decreasing NaCl gradient of 150, 100, 50, and 0 mM in Milli-Q water over 4 days, with two buffer changes per concentration per day. The final product was lyophilized (Labconco, 710202000) and stored at −80°C. Unless otherwise specified, chemicals were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich.

Fabrication of interpenetrating collagen-alginate network hydrogels

Hydrogels of interpenetrating Col I and alginate were prepared as previously described (35). A buffered salt solution was prepared by adding 20 mM 50× N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES; Gibco) into 1× Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Gibco) and adjusting the pH to 7.5 with 10 M NaOH. Stock solutions of either unmodified alginate (Alg hydrogel) or amine-functionalized alginates (NbTz hydrogel; Alg-Nb and Alg-Tz) were dissolved at 5% (w/v) in the HBSS/HEPES buffer. Precipitated calcium carbonate (CaCO3; Specialty Minerals) was dispersed in Milli-Q water at a 10% (w/v) concentration and ultrasonicated (Thermo Fisher Scientific, FB505A110 equipped with a 418-A probe) at 50% amplitude for 15 s to create a slurry. Freshly prepared d-(+)-gluconic acid δ-lactone (GDL; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved at 0.4 g/ml in the HBSS/HEPES buffer immediately before use. To make the working collagen solution, rat tail telo–Col I (8 to 11 mg/ml, Corning) was mixed on ice with 10% (w/v) of 10× HBSS (without calcium and magnesium, with phenol red, Sigma-Aldrich), and 10% (w/v) of 1 M N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (20 mM final concentration, HEPES, Gibco). For purely ionically cross-linked hydrogels, final concentration of 4 mg/ml of working collagen solution, HBSS-HEPES buffer, 1.2% (w/v) of 1 M NaOH to neutralize the pH to 7.5, CaCO3 with varying concentration of 0.1% (w/v), 0.2% (w/v), and 0.3% (w/v), Alg with concentration of either 1% (w/v) or 1.5% (w/v), and GDL solution (5× molar excess of Ca2+) were sequentially added and fully stirred on ice. For click-modified alginates (Alg-Nb and Alg-Tz), the HBSS/HEPES buffer, CaCO3, Alg and Alg-Nb stock solutions, and GDL solution with same concentrations as unmodified alginate (Alg), were added sequentially with thorough mixing before introducing the Alg-Tz stock solution. The ratio of Alg-Nb to Alg-Tz (Nb:Tz ratio) groups was adjusted to 2:1, 1:2, and 1:1 (table S1). The prepared solution was promptly transferred via micropipette either onto a rheometer plate set to 4°C to analyze the gel’s viscoelastic properties or into the microwells of 35 mm glass-bottom dish (No. 0 coverslip, MatTek) for examining collagen assembly through SHG imaging. The experimental formulations for the cross-linked hydrogels are detailed in table S1, where ionic cross-linking was achieved with Ca2+, and covalent cross-links were established via Nb-Tz click chemistry.

Throughout the manuscript, we use a consistent naming convention to distinguish between alginate and collagen-alginate hydrogel systems with different cross-linking chemistries. “Alg” refers to unmodified alginate hydrogels that are ionically cross-linked with Ca2+ (collagen is not present). “NbTz” denotes modified alginate hydrogels in which alginate functionalized with norbornene (Nb) and tetrazine (Tz) undergoes both ionic Ca2+ cross-linking and covalent Nb-Tz click cross-linking (collagen is not present). “C-Alg” refers to IPN collagen-alginate hydrogels formed with unmodified alginate that is ionically cross-linked. “C-NbTz” denotes IPN collagen-alginate hydrogels based on NbTz-modified alginate, which incorporate both ionic Ca2+ cross-linking and covalent Nb-Tz click cross-linking. These abbreviations are used consistently throughout the manuscript to clearly differentiate the hydrogel compositions and cross-linking mechanisms.

SHG confocal imaging of collagen fiber assembly and fibrosis

Initial gelation was conducted at 4°C on ice for 3.5 hours. Following gelation, samples were moved to an incubator at 37°C. A calcium chloride solution (1 M in H2O, Sigma-Aldrich) was incorporated into the previously described HBSS/HEPES buffer formulation to achieve a final concentration of 5 mM. Following a 1-hour incubation period, 2 ml of the prepared media was added into each petri dish to prevent dehydration and preserve gel structure. After 2 hours, the media were replaced, and the dishes were maintained in the incubator overnight. The samples were subsequently imaged using SHG microscopy, a nonlinear optical process wherein two photons at frequency ω are annihilated in a medium, and a new photon with doubled frequency/energy 2ω is generated (53). For Fig. 5A, collagen fibers were imaged using a Leica SP8 multiphoton confocal microscope with a laser excitation wavelength of 910 nm and capturing the SHG signal using a nondescanned detector set to detect wavelengths at 455 nm, with a 20× (1.0 NA) water immersion objective.

Fibrillar collagen analysis

Different regions of hydrogels were imaged and used as technical replicates. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ). For collagen area fraction analysis, z-stack SHG images were acquired with a step size of 1 μm, and the central 15 μm of each stack (15 optical sections) was selected for analysis. These sections were thresholded in Fiji using the same thresholding settings across all images to ensure consistency and accuracy, and the collagen fiber area fraction was calculated for each layer. The values were then averaged to obtain a single representative value per imaged region. Five distinct regions per hydrogel were analyzed to generate five technical replicates. Example thresholded SHG images used for area fraction quantification are shown in figs. S7 to S11. For analysis of collagen fiber morphology, including fiber thickness, length, and straightness, z-stack images were first converted into background-subtracted maximum intensity projections. These projections were then analyzed using the CT-FIRE program (version 1.3, beta 267; http://loci.wisc.edu/software/ctfire) to extract parameters of individual collagen fibers. CT-FIRE reports morphological properties for each detected fiber, including length, width, and straightness. The values from all detected fibers within an image were averaged to generate a single technical replicate corresponding to that imaging region.

Oscillatory rheological characterizations of collagen-alginate hydrogels

Rheological characterizations of purely ionically cross-linked and dual-cross-linked (ionically and covalently cross-linked) hydrogels were conducted on a TA Instruments HR-30 Rheometer at 4°C, with a gap setting of 350 μm. A 20-mm-diameter parallel-plate geometry, equipped with a solvent trap to prevent sample dehydration, was used for oscillatory shear rheology measurements. The hydrogel mixture (150 μl) was promptly transferred from the reaction vial onto the Peltier plate. Oscillatory time sweep tests (0.1 Hz, 2% strain) were conducted to record the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G′′), and loss tangent (tanδ) until a plateau was reached, followed by frequency sweep tests (2% strain, 0.01 to 25 Hz).

Shear flow tests on alginate hydrogels

A 1.5 weight % solution of unmodified alginate or click-modified alginate containing 0.3% (w/v) calcium ions was prepared as previously described. A total of 102 μl of hydrogel solution was loaded into a homemade acrylic mold and allowed to polymerize for 5 hours at room temperature to form a hydrogel disc (D×H=8 mm×2 mm). The hydrogel disc was preconditioned for shear testing by soaking in ultrapure Milli-Q water for 24 hours. The disc was then transferred to a stress-controlled rheometer (DHR-30, TA Instruments) and confined between two impermeable plates (top: 20 mm sandblasted geometry; bottom: immersion cup). After the geometry was brought into contact with the top surface of the gel, a 5% compressive preload was applied at a strain rate of 0.025% s−1 to ensure full contact and prevent slippage between the disc and plates. The preload was fully relaxed before shear testing (Fig. 3C). Sandpaper was used to further minimize slippage. For the continuous shear test, 100% shear strain was applied at a strain rate of 1% s−1. For the stepwise shear relaxation test, the total 50% shear strain was divided into 10 steps, where each step consisted of 5% strain applied at 1% s−1 followed by 1500 s of stress relaxation.

Compression test for permeability measurement

The preconditioned hydrogel disk was placed on a stress-controlled rheometer (DHR-20, TA Instruments) and secured between an impermeable 20-mm stainless-steel upper geometry and a glass lower plate positioned above a high-resolution camera. After confirming complete contact with the gel surface, the sample was compressed to 10% strain at a constant rate of 0.025% s−1. The deformation process and the subsequent 2500 s relaxation period were continuously recorded to capture changes in the lateral profile of the gel. Video frames were analyzed in ImageJ to extract the time-dependent gel radius, which was combined with the rheometer-reported height to compute the instantaneous volume. Differentiation of the volume-time curve provided the volumetric flow rate, and the flux was obtained by normalizing this value by the effective fluid outflow area, modeled as the lateral surface of a deforming cylindrical gel. A custom Python script synchronized the rheometer stress data with the video-derived flux measurements to generate flux-stress relationships for each compression step. Permeability was determined by applying Darcy’s law. Assuming that the primary chemical potential gradient was oriented radially, we approximated this gradient as σ(t)/r(t), where σ is the measured Cauchy normal stress and r(t) is the instantaneous radius. Linear fitting of the low-flux regime yielded the term k/(ηL), allowing calculation of the intrinsic permeability k by taking L≈r(t). This analytical approach, adapted from prior models of fluid transport in compressed porous media, provided a robust estimate of gel permeability under confined compression (30).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism GraphPad software (version 10.3.1). Experimental data are shown as mean values ± SD. For multiple comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test was applied. P values less than 0.05 were considered to be significant in all analyses. PCA was performed to identify the dominant factors governing collagen fiber organization and to visualize relationships between hydrogel mechanical and transport properties. The analysis included nine variables: storage modulus (G′), viscoelasticity (tanδ), permeability, shear stress, relaxation percentage, fiber area fraction, length, width, and straightness. All variables were mean-centered and variance-scaled before analysis. Principal components were selected on the basis of the Kaiser criterion, retaining components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. Variable loadings were used to assess each parameter’s contribution to the principal components, and score plots were used to visualize sample clustering by hydrogel chemistry and composition. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to quantify the relationship between hydrogel mechanical parameters and collagen fiber morphology. Storage modulus (G′), viscoelasticity (tanδ), and permeability were used as independent variables, while collagen fiber area fraction, length, width, and straightness were treated as dependent variables. Regression models were evaluated using least-squares fitting, and coefficients of determination (R2). P values were reported to assess model significance and the relative contribution of each parameter (as shown in the plots and in tables S5 and S9).
