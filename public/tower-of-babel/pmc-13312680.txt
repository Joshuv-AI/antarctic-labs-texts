Punishment sensitivity and life satisfaction: the mediating role of self-concealment and secrecy-induced cognitive preoccupation
Authors: Jiaxin Chen, QingLei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLife satisfaction is central to well-being, yet how punishment sensitivity is associated with life satisfaction remains insufficiently understood. Across two studies, we examined concealment-related processes that may help explain this association at different levels of analysis. In Study 1 (N = 284), punishment sensitivity was positively associated with self-concealment and negatively associated with life satisfaction; self-concealment was also negatively associated with life satisfaction. A mediation model indicated a significant indirect association linking punishment sensitivity, self-concealment, and life satisfaction, even after controlling for negative affect, age, and gender. In Study 2 (N = 249), participants recalled either a high-burden or low-burden secret. Participants in the high-burden secret recall condition reported greater cognitive preoccupation and lower state life satisfaction than those in the low-burden condition. Cognitive preoccupation partially explained the association between the high-burden secret recall condition and lower state life satisfaction. Moderated mediation analyses further showed that punishment sensitivity amplified the association between the high-burden secret recall condition and cognitive preoccupation, with conditional indirect effects emerging at mean and high levels of punishment sensitivity. Together, the findings are consistent with an RST-informed account in which punishment sensitivity is associated with both enduring self-concealment tendencies and immediate cognitive costs in secrecy-related contexts.Keywords: Punishment sensitivity, Reinforcement sensitivity theory, Self-concealment, Secrecy burden, Cognitive preoccupation, Life satisfaction

Introduction

Life satisfaction is a central marker of subjective well-being and is increasingly used to complement objective indicators when evaluating quality of life and societal progress [1]. Higher life satisfaction is consistently associated with better health and longevity across adulthood and ageing [2, 3]. Accordingly, identifying the processes that sustain—or erode—life satisfaction is an important goal. Research has documented multiple personality-based pathways to life satisfaction, showing that stable trait differences systematically shape how satisfied people feel with their lives [4, 5]. However, broad trait taxonomies do not fully clarify who is most vulnerable to lower life satisfaction when situations involve punishment cues, social-evaluative costs, or approach–avoidance conflict. In this research, we draw on Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory [6] together with the self-concealment and secrecy literatures [7, 8] to examine how punishment sensitivity may be associated with life satisfaction via self-concealment and heightened secrecy related cognitive burden.

The relation between punishment sensitivity and life satisfaction

Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory provides a useful framework for understanding why individual differences in responsiveness to aversive and conflict-relevant cues may be associated with life satisfaction. RST proposes that personality differences reflect variation in sensitivity to appetitive and aversive environmental signals, which in turn shapes motivational orientation and self-regulatory responses [9, 10]. Within this framework, BIS-related functioning is especially relevant to punishment- and conflict-relevant situations, including contexts in which potential gains and potential costs co-occur. When approach–avoidance conflict is activated, BIS-related processes are expected to inhibit direct approach and promote cautious observation, risk assessment, and monitoring [6, 9].

In the present research, we focus on punishment sensitivity as an RST-informed disposition indexed by the BIS scale. We use this term to refer to relatively stable individual differences in sensitivity to punishment and conflict-relevant cues. From this perspective, individuals higher in punishment sensitivity may experience potential losses, criticism, rejection, or other social-evaluative costs as more salient. Such heightened sensitivity may promote behavioral inhibition, caution, and avoidance-oriented regulation in situations where negative consequences are anticipated [11].

These tendencies may be relevant to life satisfaction because global life evaluations are shaped not only by objective circumstances, but also by how individuals appraise, regulate, and respond to recurring interpersonal and self-relevant challenges. Individuals who are chronically attuned to possible punishment or social-evaluative costs may be more likely to experience daily situations as constrained, risky, or conflict laden, which may contribute to less favorable evaluations of their lives. Consistent with this possibility, prior work has linked higher punishment sensitivity with lower life satisfaction across adult samples [12]. However, the psychological processes that may help explain this association remain insufficiently specified.

Self-concealment as a mediating mechanism

Self-concealment is typically conceptualized as a trait-like individual difference—“the predisposition to actively conceal from others personal information that one perceives as distressing or negative” [7, 13]. In their integrative review, Larson and colleagues situate self-concealment within the broader psychology of secrets, emphasizing that it reflects a motivated pattern of managing threatening self-relevant information rather than merely low disclosure [14, 15]. Consistent with this distinction, self-concealment is empirically separable from self-disclosure (even when disclosure is restricted to distressing content) and is associated with greater secret keeping and reduced authenticity or openness [7, 14, 16, 17]. In the present research, we treat self-concealment as a relatively stable tendency to manage distressing self-relevant information by keeping it from others.

From the perspective of Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST), individuals higher in punishment sensitivity should be more attuned to punishment- and conflict-relevant cues and thus more likely to engage in inhibition, risk assessment, and avoidance-oriented regulation [6, 18, 19, 20]. Importantly, this does not imply that punishment sensitivity should lead to self-concealment rather than any other form of avoidance in all contexts. Avoidance can take multiple forms, including social withdrawal, experiential avoidance, and the regulation or suppression of self-relevant information [21]. From this perspective, self-concealment represents one specific form of avoidance that operates at the level of interpersonal information management rather than behavioral disengagement.

Self-concealment is especially relevant in social-evaluative contexts, where individuals must balance the desire to avoid negative interpersonal outcomes with the need to remain socially engaged. In such contexts, withholding distressing or potentially discrediting information can serve as a strategy to reduce perceived disclosure risks while maintaining participation in ongoing interactions [7, 22]. Rather than reflecting simple withdrawal, self-concealment thus represents an active form of regulation aimed at managing how one is perceived by others. In this sense, self-concealment can be understood as involving both BIS-related inhibition of potentially threatening self-information and active management of what is disclosed to others.

This mapping is consistent with the self-concealment working model, which identifies social-evaluative concerns as a key antecedent domain and explicitly notes that those sensitive to rejection or inhibited socially and psychologically should be more inclined to self-conceal [14]. Emerging evidence also links BIS-related punishment sensitivity to higher self-concealment in nonclinical adults: punishment sensitivity shows robust positive associations with self-concealment [23], and related work similarly frames self-concealment as heightened responsiveness to punishment cues and avoidance-oriented regulation [24]. When punishment-relevant interpersonal costs are anticipated, withholding distressing self-information can be experienced as a low-risk option in the short run, particularly in contexts where perceived disclosure costs, such as criticism or rejection, are salient [15, 25]. Moreover, empirical work has connected higher self-concealment to lower life satisfaction and subjective well-being [26, 27], and related recent evidence on concealment more broadly points to reduced belonging and social support as plausible mechanisms linking concealment to poorer well-being [28]. Together, these findings identify self-concealment as one plausible pathway through which punishment sensitivity may be associated with lower life satisfaction.

Punishment sensitivity in a secrecy context

Concealment related costs may also emerge in specific situations in which a particular secret becomes salient. Such situations may be especially consequential for punishment sensitive individuals, because possible criticism, rejection, or other negative consequences are likely to feel more salient under conditions of concealment. Examining punishment sensitivity in a secrecy context therefore helps clarify how a relatively stable disposition may be associated with more immediate cognitive and evaluative costs [29, 30]. This distinction is especially relevant for punishment sensitivity, because RST characterizes BIS-related functioning as involving heightened sensitivity to punishment and goal conflict, accompanied by behavioral inhibition, risk assessment, and cautious monitoring when conflict-relevant cues are activated [6, 9, 18]. Accordingly, examining punishment sensitivity in a context that makes concealment and conflict management situationally salient provides a useful context for examining its possible downstream consequences.

A secrecy context provides a useful setting for examining how concealed content can become mentally absorbing. Contemporary secrecy research indicates that the burden of secrecy is often expressed less as frequent “in-the-moment” concealment and more as recurrent thought and preoccupation—cognitive occupancy—reflecting the extent to which the secret consumes mental space [8, 22]. This proximal mechanism closely aligns with the RST informed account: when concealment involves anticipated interpersonal costs, punishment sensitive individuals may be especially prone to sustained monitoring and difficulty disengaging from conflict relevant information once it becomes activated [6]. Thus, when secrecy is activated, it should increase cognitive preoccupation, and this increase may be especially pronounced among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity.

Secrecy burden is expected to increase cognitive preoccupation because burdensome secrets often involve unresolved concerns, anticipated interpersonal costs, and repeated consideration of possible disclosure [16, 22]. Importantly, punishment sensitivity is theorized to shape how strongly such concerns are processed. Within Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory, individuals higher in punishment sensitivity are more responsive to punishment-related and socially evaluative cues, which increases the salience of potential negative outcomes when such information becomes activated [31, 32]. As a result, they are more likely to experience secrecy burden as cognitively intrusive and difficult to disengage from, consistent with accounts linking threat sensitivity to sustained attention to negative or conflict-relevant information [33].

More broadly, the present framework focuses on this processing stage because both secrecy research and Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory converge at this level of explanation. Prior work on secrecy identifies cognitive preoccupation as one important proximal process through which burdensome secrets may affect well-being, whereas RST characterizes punishment sensitivity in terms of heightened responsiveness to threat-relevant information and sustained cognitive engagement with such cues. Accordingly, the overlap between these two perspectives lies in how strongly secrecy-related concerns become cognitively engaging.

By comparison, although punishment sensitivity may be broadly associated with more negative evaluative responses, the present framework provides a more specific basis for expecting it to influence the translation of secrecy burden into cognitive preoccupation than for expecting it to alter the downstream association between cognitive preoccupation and state life satisfaction or the overall impact of secrecy burden on life satisfaction. Accordingly, punishment sensitivity is expected to moderate the association between secrecy burden and cognitive preoccupation.

In Study 2, we further examine the immediate evaluative consequence of this process using state life satisfaction, that is, satisfaction judgments anchored to a discrete time window rather than one’s life “as a whole” [34]. Because such state satisfaction judgments can be operationalized by time-framing established life-satisfaction item content [34, 35], they are well suited for capturing momentary shifts following a secrecy induction. Given that secrecy related cognitive occupancy reflects one plausible proximal burden associated with secrecy, we expect secrecy activation to be accompanied by lower state life satisfaction, particularly among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity.

The present research

The present research examines how punishment sensitivity may be linked to life satisfaction through two complementary concealment related pathways. In Study 1, we test whether self-concealment helps explain the association between punishment sensitivity and lower life satisfaction (H1 to H4). In Study 2, we examine whether secrecy burden increases cognitive preoccupation and lowers state life satisfaction in a specific secrecy context, particularly among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity (H5 to H6).

H1. Punishment sensitivity is negatively associated with life satisfaction.

H2. Punishment sensitivity is positively associated with self-concealment.

H3. Self-concealment is negatively associated with life satisfaction.

H4. Self-concealment mediates the association between punishment sensitivity.

and life satisfaction.

H5. Recalling a high-burden (vs. low-burden) secret will predict lower state life satisfaction indirectly via increased cognitive preoccupation.

H6. Punishment sensitivity will strengthen the effect of secrecy burden on cognitive preoccupation, yielding a stronger indirect effect on state life satisfaction at higher punishment sensitivity.

Study 1

Method

Participants and procedure

To determine the requisite sample size for our mediation model, we conducted a Monte Carlo power analysis for indirect effects [36]. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 223 participants was necessary to achieve a statistical power of 0.90, assuming intercorrelations of r = .30 and a standard deviation of 0.10. Accordingly, we recruited a cohort of 290 Chinese adults via Credamo, an online survey platform functionally similar to Amazon Mechanical Turk. Six participants were excluded from the analysis due to failure to pass the attention check questions, resulting in a final sample of 284 participants (Mage= 25.79, SD = 6.44; 53% female). To ensure data quality, participant eligibility was restricted to individuals with a minimum task approval rate of 90% on the Credamo platform. The full dataset and associated materials have been made publicly available on the Credamo platform and can be accessed via the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/pdhuq/overview? view_only=d012bf583ac64bc4b9f8c04010279f7c. All study materials and procedures were approved by the Ethics in Human Research Committee at the first Author’s Institution.

Measures

Self-Concealment:

Self-concealment was assessed using the 10-item Self-Concealment Scale (SCS) [7]. Participants rated their agreement with statements reflecting a tendency to hide personal information (e.g., “I have a secret so private I would lie if anybody asked me about it”) on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate a greater propensity for self-concealment. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.89.

Punishment sensitivity:

Punishment sensitivity was assessed using the 7-item Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) scale [37]. This scale measures individual differences in sensitivity to potential punishment. Participants rated items such as “I worry about making mistakes” on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate heightened punishment sensitivity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.85.

Life satisfaction:

Life satisfaction was measured using the 5-item Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) [2]. Participants rated their global cognitive judgments of satisfaction with their lives (e.g., “In most ways my life is close to my ideal”) on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate higher levels of life satisfaction. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.87.

Because negative affect may overlap with punishment sensitivity, self-concealment, and life satisfaction, we included negative affect as a control variable, along with age and gender.

Negative affect:

Negative affect was measured using the Negative Affect subscale of the PANAS [38] which consists of 10 negative emotions (e.g., nervous, upset). Participants rated the extent to which they had experienced each emotion over the past week on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.93.

Results

Means, standard deviations, and correlations among the study variables are presented in (Table 1). As hypothesized, punishment sensitivity was positively correlated with self-concealment (r = .54, p < .001) and negatively correlated with life satisfaction (r = − .33, p < .001). Furthermore, self-concealment was negatively correlated with life satisfaction (r = − .26, p < .001). The zero-order associations were small to moderate in magnitude, with the strongest association observed between punishment sensitivity and self-concealment (r = .54).

To test our hypothesis regarding an indirect association between punishment sensitivity and life satisfaction via self-concealment, we conducted a mediation analysis using PROCESS Model 4 [39], implemented in R. Age and gender were controlled for in the model. We employed bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples to assess the significance of the indirect effect.

The results are presented in (Fig. 1). Controlling for negative affect, gender and age, punishment sensitivity was positively associated with self-concealment (b = 0.56, SE = 0.05, p < .001, 95% CI [0.46, 0.65]). Self-concealment was negatively associated with life satisfaction (b = − 0.20, SE = 0.07, p < .05, 95% CI [− 0.33, − 0.06]). The direct effect of punishment sensitivity on life satisfaction remained significant (b = − 0.16, SE = 0.07, p < .01, 95% CI [− 0.29, − 0.03]). Bootstrap analyses with 5,000 samples further indicated a significant indirect association between punishment sensitivity and life satisfaction via self-concealment (indirect effect = − 0.11, BootSE = 0.04, 95% BootCI [− 0.20, − 0.03]). The total effect of punishment sensitivity on life satisfaction was b = − 0.27, SE = 0.06, p < .001, 95% CI [− 0.39, − 0.17]. Negative affect was not a significant predictor in either the mediator model, b = -0.05, SE = 0.058, p = .357, 95% CI [-0.14, 0.05], or the outcome model, b = 0.01, SE = 0.06, p = .80, 95% CI [-0.10, 0.13]. These results indicate a statistically significant indirect association linking punishment sensitivity, self-concealment, and life satisfaction after controlling for negative affect, age, and gender, consistent with the proposed account.

Study 2

Method

Participants and procedure

Following the protocol from Study 1, we conducted a Monte Carlo power analysis for indirect effects [36] to determine the requisite sample size. We initially recruited 261 participants via Credamo. Twelve participants were excluded for failing the attention check (which explicitly instructed: “Please choose ‘agree with it at all’ as your answer”), resulting in a final sample of 249 participants (162 males; Mage = 30.55, SD = 7.54). Participants were randomly assigned to a high-burden secrecy condition (n = 122) or a low-burden secrecy condition (n = 127). After the manipulation, participants completed measures assessing cognitive preoccupation, punishment sensitivity, state life satisfaction, and relevant control variables. A manipulation check was also included.

Manipulation

We adopted the secrecy manipulation established by Slepian et al. (2015) [8]. Participants in the high-burden secrecy condition were instructed to recall a personal secret that met three criteria: (a) they thought about it frequently, (b) it had a significant emotional impact, and (c) it caused substantial distress. They were then asked to write four sentences describing this secret. Conversely, participants in the low-burden secrecy condition were instructed to recall a secret that (a) they rarely thought about, (b) had minimal emotional impact, and (c) generally elicited a sense of ease. They also wrote four sentences regarding their chosen secret.

Measures

Manipulation check

To verify the distinct nature of the secrets, we measured using a single-item manipulation check asked, “How much effort does it take for you to keep your secret?” rated from 1 (none at all) to 7(a lot), following Slepian et al. (2015) [8].

Cognitive preoccupation

Cognitive preoccupation was measured with four items. Two items were adapted from the preoccupation scale developed by Slepian et al. (2015) [8] (e.g., “How much do you think about your secret?”; “How much does your secret affect you?”), and two additional items were drawn from prior work [40]assessing the extent to which thoughts about the secret are intrusive (e.g., distracting from task completion or persisting longer than desired). Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale. An exploratory factor analysis (principal component method, eigenvalue > 1 criterion) revealed that a single factor accounted for 65.96% of the total variance (KMO = 0.73, Bartlett’s test of sphericity p < .001). Factor loadings for all items ranged from 0.78 to 0.86, providing preliminary evidence for the unidimensional structure of the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.82.

Punishment sensitivity

Consistent with Study 1, punishment sensitivity was measured using the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) scale [37]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.79.

State life satisfaction

State life satisfaction was assessed using the 5-item Satisfaction with Life Scale [34], revised to reflect the particular focus of this study (e.g., ‘Right now, I am satisfied with my life’). Participants rated items on a 7-point Likert scale. An exploratory factor analysis (principal component method, eigenvalue > 1 criterion) showed that a single factor accounted for 73.42% of the total variance (KMO = 0.88, Bartlett’s test of sphericity p < .001). Factor loadings for all items ranged from 0.77 to 0.91, providing preliminary evidence for the unidimensional structure of the scale. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present study was 0.91.

Control variables

State negative affect

State negative affect was measured using the same Negative Affect subscale of the PANAS [38], adapted to the experimental context. Participants rated the extent to which they felt each emotion when thinking about their recalled secret on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). The Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.87.

We also controlled demographic variables including age and gender (1 = male, 2 = female).

Results

Manipulation check

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to verify the effectiveness of the experimental manipulation. As expected, participants in the high-burden secrecy condition reported significantly higher effort to keep the secret (M = 5.09, SD = 1.34) compared to the low-burden secrecy condition (M = 2.33, SD = 0.93), t (247) = 18.94, p < .001. Cohen’s d = 2.39.

Main effects

We examined the direct impact of the experimental condition on the cognitive preoccupation and state life satisfaction (Table 2). Participants in the high-burden secrecy condition reported significantly higher levels of cognitive preoccupation (M = 3.33, SD = 0.92) than those in the low-burden secrecy condition (M = 2.82, SD = 0.82), t (247) = 4.69, p < .001. Cohen’s d = 0.59. Participants in the high-burden secrecy condition reported significantly lower state life satisfaction (M = 2.83, SD = 0.96) compared to the low-burden secrecy condition(M = 3.33, SD = 0.81), t (247) = -4.22, p < .001. Cohen’s d = 0.57.

Although the interaction effect was modest, the significant index of moderated mediation indicates that the conditional indirect effect varied reliably across levels of punishment sensitivity.

Discussion

The present studies examined how punishment sensitivity may be associated with life satisfaction through two complementary concealment related pathways within an RST informed framework. In Study 1, punishment sensitivity was associated with greater self concealment, which was in turn associated with lower life satisfaction. In Study 2, greater secrecy burden was associated with higher cognitive preoccupation and lower state life satisfaction, with these effects being stronger among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity. Taken together, the findings are consistent with an RST-informed account in which punishment sensitivity may be linked to lower life satisfaction through both a more enduring tendency toward concealment and more immediate secrecy related cognitive costs that arise in secrecy contexts.

Concealment-related pathways linking punishment sensitivity to life satisfaction

The association involving self-concealment is informative because it identifies a behaviorally meaningful correlate of the link between punishment sensitivity and global life satisfaction. At the trait level, self-concealment reflects a relatively stable tendency to manage distressing self-relevant content by keeping it from others [7, 14]. What matters for life satisfaction is not simply “hiding” per se, but the self-regulatory and interpersonal costs that may accompany the long-term maintenance of concealment. For example, self-determination accounts suggest that concealment undermines well-being by frustrating autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs; in this view, concealment involves ongoing monitoring and suppression of thoughts and impulses, which is detrimental to need satisfaction and, in turn, to well-being [17].

This account aligns with an RST-informed view of punishment sensitivity. Revised RST characterizes the BIS as a conflict system that inhibits ongoing behavior and increases risk assessment and attention during conflict [9, 41]. If punishment sensitive individuals are more likely to enter a vigilant and risk-assessing mode in social-evaluative contexts, self-concealment may become a stable regulatory strategy for managing anticipated criticism, rejection, or other interpersonal costs. Such a strategy may reduce immediate exposure to negative evaluation, but it may also involve sustained monitoring, reduced openness, and constrained access to social support [14]. These costs may plausibly be associated with less favorable global life evaluations, consistent with prior evidence linking self-concealment to poorer well-being and need frustration [14, 17]. Importantly, the Study 1 pattern remained significant after controlling for negative affect, suggesting that the observed indirect association was not fully reducible to broader negative emotionality alone. At the same time, the remaining direct association between punishment sensitivity and life satisfaction indicates that self-concealment provides only a partial account of this association. Other unmeasured processes, such as threat appraisal, social withdrawal, experiential avoidance, emotion regulation difficulties, reduced perceived social support, or other distress-related interpersonal processes, may also be involved.

Study 2 adds a complementary situational perspective by examining what happens when a burdensome secret becomes salient. The secrecy burden induction was associated with greater cognitive preoccupation and lower state life satisfaction, and cognitive preoccupation partially explained the association between secrecy burden and state life satisfaction. This situational process illustrates one way in which concealment-related content may become cognitively and evaluatively costly in the short run. Specifically, burdensome secrets may capture attention and generate recurrent thought, a process closely related to what secrecy research describes as cognitive occupancy [8, 22]. Moreover, punishment sensitivity amplified secrecy-related preoccupation, suggesting that punishment-sensitive individuals may be especially likely to experience burdensome concealed content as cognitively absorbing.

This pattern clarifies where punishment sensitivity appears to exert its strongest role in Study 2: it shapes the extent to which secrecy burden is translated into cognitive preoccupation. This is consistent with the RST-informed idea that punishment sensitivity involves heightened responsiveness to punishment-relevant and conflict-relevant cues. However, Study 2 should be understood as offering a complementary situational account rather than as directly validating the trait-level mediation observed in Study 1. In other words, Study 1 speaks to a more enduring tendency toward self-concealment, whereas Study 2 shows how secrecy-related content can become cognitively and evaluatively costly in a specific context.

Even after controlling for state negative affect, secrecy burden continued to predict greater cognitive preoccupation, and this association remained stronger among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity. These findings suggest that high-burden secrecy contexts carry a meaningful cognitive cost that is not reducible to general negative affect alone. At the same time, the manipulation likely varied not only in secrecy burden but also in emotional intensity, intrusiveness, and subjective distress. Thus, the Study 2 findings are best understood as evidence that secrecy related high-burden contexts are associated with cognitive preoccupation and lower momentary life evaluation, rather than as evidence that secrecy burden has been fully isolated from broader emotional burden.

Using state life satisfaction in Study 2 strengthens the interpretation that secrecy burden can acutely shift evaluative life satisfaction. Although life satisfaction is often conceptualized as a broad judgment, evidence supports the validity of brief and even single-item life satisfaction assessments when the goal is to capture evaluative standing at a given moment or within a short time window [42]. In the present context, the secrecy induction was designed to create a transient psychological state; thus, a state-anchored satisfaction judgment is an appropriate outcome for detecting immediate evaluative consequences. This choice also aligns with the broader message of the paper: punishment sensitivity is associated not only with stable well-being correlates, but also with momentary evaluative costs that may arise when concealment-related concerns become salient.

Taken together, the two studies suggest that punishment sensitivity may be linked to lower life satisfaction through two complementary concealment-related routes: a more enduring tendency toward self-concealment and a more immediate secrecy-related cognitive burden. Conceptually, the first route helps explain why some individuals may live with concealment as a recurring interpersonal pattern, whereas the second helps explain how concealment can become costly in the short run through recurrent thought and cognitive absorption. Because the effect of secrecy burden on state life satisfaction was only partially mediated by cognitive preoccupation, other proximal processes may operate in parallel, including affective arousal, anticipatory anxiety, behavioral inhibition, or perceived lack of relational safety. These processes are unlikely to be shaped by punishment sensitivity alone. Broader social psychological work suggests that openness and withdrawal are shaped not only by dispositional sensitivity, but also by how interpersonal contexts are construed in affective and relational terms [43, 44]. Concealment-related distress may therefore reflect not only individual differences in punishment sensitivity, but also whether disclosure is experienced as evaluatively threatening, unsupportive, or relationally unsafe [45].

The present findings are also compatible with a broader account in which punishment-sensitive individuals are generally more emotionally reactive and more prone to experiencing distressing autobiographical material as cognitively intrusive. From this perspective, recalling a high-burden secret may function as a particularly potent instance of engaging with negative and personally significant content, thereby eliciting sustained cognitive preoccupation and lower momentary well-being. The current results help specify one pathway within this broader framework: secrecy-related contexts may serve as a meaningful domain in which emotional reactivity and cognitive engagement become especially consequential. Future research should more clearly disentangle secrecy-specific effects from broader emotional burden, for example by holding emotional intensity constant while varying concealment demands.

Limitations and implications

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, Study 1 relied on cross-sectional self-report data. Therefore, the mediation results should be interpreted as evidence of an indirect association consistent with the proposed process rather than as evidence of causal mediation. Although punishment sensitivity was associated with greater self-concealment, which in turn was associated with lower life satisfaction, the present design cannot establish temporal ordering. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to test whether self-concealment prospectively helps explain the association between punishment sensitivity and later life satisfaction.

Second, Study 2 used a high-burden versus low-burden secret recall manipulation. Although this manipulation was adapted from established secrecy research, the two conditions likely differed not only in concealment demand but also in emotional intensity, intrusiveness, and perceived seriousness. The revised analyses showed that the key effects remained after controlling for state negative affect, which suggests that the observed pattern is not reducible to negative affect alone. However, this control does not fully separate secrecy burden from other closely related affective and cognitive features of the recalled content. Future work could strengthen causal inference by holding event valence and intensity constant across conditions, independently manipulating concealment demand, or using paradigms that vary disclosure constraints without confounding secrecy burden with event distress.

Third, punishment sensitivity was operationalized using the BIS scale. Although this is a common RST-informed approach, BIS-based measures may overlap empirically with broader distress-related constructs, including trait anxiety, neuroticism, and general negative emotionality. The revised analyses controlled for negative affect, and the key patterns remained significant, suggesting that the findings are not fully reducible to negative affect alone. Nevertheless, the present data do not establish complete construct specificity. Future research should further clarify this issue by incorporating alternative RST-focused measures and broader trait-level measures of anxiety, neuroticism, and general distress.

Finally, punishment sensitivity in Study 2 was measured in the same session after the secrecy recall induction. Although the BIS scale is intended to assess relatively stable individual differences, assessing it before the induction would have provided a cleaner test of its moderating role. The present timing leaves open the possibility that the induced state influenced response style or momentary self-perception. Future studies should measure dispositional punishment sensitivity before any experimental manipulation or in a separate session.

Conclusions

In sum, the present findings suggest that punishment sensitivity may be associated with lower life satisfaction through two complementary concealment-related routes: a more enduring tendency toward self-concealment and a more immediate secrecy-related cognitive burden. Study 1 showed that punishment sensitivity was associated with greater self-concealment, which was in turn associated with lower global life satisfaction. Study 2 showed that secrecy burden was associated with greater cognitive preoccupation and lower state life satisfaction, and that the association between secrecy burden and cognitive preoccupation was stronger among individuals higher in punishment sensitivity. Together, these findings suggest that concealment-related processes may help explain why punishment sensitivity is linked to lower life satisfaction, while also highlighting the need for future research to disentangle secrecy-specific processes from broader emotional burden and to test these associations longitudinally.
