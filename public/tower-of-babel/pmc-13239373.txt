Role of family medicine on health systems in the Americas: scoping review
Authors: Erwin Hernando Hernandez Rincon, July Andrea Hernandez Rincon, Laura Daniela Saza Salas, Mauricio Fernando Ulloa Roddriguez, Claudia Liliana Jaimes Peñuela, Diana Marcela Diaz Quijano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesise the available evidence on the reported role of family physicians in health systems in the Americas.DesignA scoping review was conducted following the methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute. The results were grouped into thematic categories and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.Eligibility criteriaPeer-reviewed articles and relevant grey literature published between 2010 and 2025 examining the role or impact of family physicians within health systems in the Americas. Studies that did not explicitly address family physicians or lacked accessible full text were excluded.Information sourcesA comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, LILACS and the Virtual Health Library (BIREME). Grey literature was identified through institutional repositories, policy reports and targeted searches of official websites.ResultsOf 1397 records identified, 34 studies met the inclusion criteria. Most publications were produced between 2013 and 2024 and represented diverse health system contexts across the region. The attributes most frequently associated with family physicians were comprehensiveness, accessibility, continuity of care, coordination and family and community orientation. Across multiple national settings, the included studies consistently reported that stronger integration of family physicians within primary healthcare systems was associated with improved access to services, greater continuity of care, more efficient coordination across levels of care and a more rational use of diagnostic and therapeutic resources.ConclusionsFamily physicians appear to represent an important component of effective primary healthcare systems in the Americas. Their integration within health services is associated with improved service accessibility, greater care continuity and more efficient resource use. Strengthening family medicine workforce capacity and its institutional role may contribute to more equitable, resilient and sustainable health systems across the region.Trial registration numberProtocol registered and available on this OSF project https://osf.io/pnf2b/.Keywords: Public Health Systems Research, Family Medicine, Primary Health Care, Family Practice

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This scoping review, conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, identified 34 studies reporting associations between family physicians and key primary healthcare attributes, including accessibility, continuity, coordination and comprehensiveness of care. Across the included literature, stronger integration of family physicians was frequently linked to more appropriate use of diagnostic resources, lower emergency department utilisation, fewer hospitalisations and improved patient-reported care experiences.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings highlight the strategic value of family physicians in strengthening health systems, particularly in regions with fragmented care and inequities. The study underscores the need for public policies that support their training, recognition and equitable distribution. Future research should explore the implementation of family medicine in under-represented areas and its long-term effects on health equity and system sustainability.

Introduction

The Alma Ata declaration in 1978 established a healthcare model centred on primary healthcare (PHC), emphasising the need for holistic care, with a focus on the individual, the family and the community.1 2 Within this framework, four main characteristics of PHC are highlighted: accessibility, comprehensiveness, longitudinally and coordination of care, which are elements that improve health outcomes, reduce inequalities and strengthen the sustainability of health systems.3,5

In the context of the Americas, family medicine is understood as a medical specialty oriented towards comprehensive, continuous, person-centred and community-based care, integrating biological, psychological, social and cultural dimensions of health, and functioning as the cornerstone of PHC systems.3

In this sense, several studies have shown that health systems supported by PHC achieve better health indicators, increased access to healthcare and lower costs. In Latin America, the world’s most inequitable region, these benefits are significant, given the severe social inequalities and structural challenges that health systems face, such as the division of services, lack of qualified human resources, and unbalanced coverage.6 7

On the other hand, family medicine is the medical specialty that most fully incorporates the attributes of PHC within the principles postulated as pillars by Ian McWhinney (table 1). Its ability to establish relationships of trust and continuity contributes to more effective and satisfactory care. However, despite its importance, the role of the family physician is not always adequately appreciated or understood in health systems, such as in the region of the Americas. Similarly, in several countries, the specialty still faces obstacles such as limited institutional recognition, insufficient allocation of resources and poor articulation between levels of care. In addition, there are still gaps in the understanding of how this figure is truly being incorporated into health systems, what its impact is, and what strategies could improve its participation in public policymaking.3,58

Despite the growing body of international literature on PHC, few studies have comprehensively synthesised how family physicians concretely influence health system performance across the diverse political, economic and social contexts of the Americas. This review addresses this gap by providing a regionally focused, system-level analysis of family medicine’s contributions.

It is therefore crucial to generate evidence to evaluate the scope and benefits of the role of family doctors in health systems. For this reason, this research was conducted to synthesise the available evidence on the reported role of family physicians in health systems in the Americas.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted following the methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute. Thus, the central question was as follows: what is the role of family doctors in the health systems of the Americas?

The literature search was conducted between June 2024 and April 2025 using PubMed, Scopus, Embase, LILACS and the Virtual Health Library (VHL/BIREME) platform. Search strategies were developed a priori and adapted for each database using controlled vocabulary (MeSH and DeCS) and free-text terms. Language (English, Spanish, Portuguese) and date (2010–2025) limits were applied at the search stage. Grey literature was identified through official institutional websites, policy repositories and targeted web searches. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined to ensure the relevance and quality of the selected studies (table 2). Articles obtained from the search were registered in Rayyan and Microsoft Excel, and duplicates were removed. Papers were then screened by title and abstract independently by two researchers to determine relevance, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher. The selected full texts were grouped into thematic categories according to commonalities between family physicians and PHCs (table 1). The search and study selection process was documented following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) methodology (figure 1). For this review, the ‘role of the family physician’ was operationalised a priori across the following domains: (1) scope of clinical practice, (2) contribution to PHC functions (accessibility, continuity, comprehensiveness, coordination, first contact, community orientation), (3) health system performance outcomes (costs, hospitalisations, emergency visits, quality of care) and (4) policy and governance positioning. Studies were included only if they addressed at least one of these domains explicitly. This operational definition is conceptually aligned with the international ‘Storylines of Family Medicine’ framework, particularly the foundational building blocks of context, community and health and the core principles of primary care, systems and family, as described in the BMJ Family Medicine and Community Health special issue (2024). This framework provides an established international reference for structuring the thematic synthesis and interpreting the role of family medicine within complex health systems.12 13

Results

After searching, 1397 articles were collected, 34 of which were excluded for duplication, leaving 1363 records for screening by title and abstract. A total of 1312 articles were discarded because they were not relevant to the research question, leaving 51 articles for full reading.

Following full-text retrieval, one article (n=1) was excluded because the full text was unavailable, leaving 50 articles for eligibility assessment. Of these, 16 articles were excluded after full-text review for the following reasons: seven (n=7) addressed the importance of PHC in health systems without specifically mentioning family physicians; four (n=4) focused exclusively on barriers faced by family physicians in health systems; three (n=3) described training requirements for family physicians and two (n=2) did not correspond to the scope of this review, as one explored physicians’ employment preferences and the other focused on the role of family physicians in Spain.

Finally, 34 articles met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review. These articles were downloaded and stored in PDF format. The included studies were organised according to the pre-established thematic categories defined by the researchers. The articles included in the review were organised according to the categories preestablished by the researchers, of which 12 were review studies, 7 descriptive, 5 observational, 4 qualitative, 2 cohort, 1 expert consensus, 1 formal, 1 editorial and 1 quantitative. The publication period ranged from 2013 to 2024. Canada (n=12), Brazil (n=10) and the USA (n=9) were the most mentioned countries (table 3).

Among the 34 included studies, the most frequently reported category was comprehensiveness (18 studies, 52.9%), followed by accessibility and continuity (13 studies each, 38.2%), coordination, family and community orientation (12 studies each, 35.3%).

Discussion

The findings of this review suggest an important role of family physicians in the health systems of the Americas. Interpreted through the Storylines of Family Medicine framework,12 13 these contributions reflect interconnected system-level functions grounded in core primary care principles rather than isolated clinical activities. As shown in table 4, family physicians are associated with multiple reported benefits through their fundamental principles. These effects are mediated through several mechanisms, including strengthened continuity of care, improved coordination across service levels, enhanced patient–provider trust, rationalisation of diagnostic and therapeutic decisions, and increased capacity for community-based prevention and health promotion.3 5 14

Accessibility

Family physicians guarantee universal access, providing care to people of all ages and conditions, including the underserved population. Their work overcomes geographical, financial and sociocultural barriers, and their plasticity allows them to adapt their practices to the needs of their community.35 14,20

In the USA, the role of family physicians in promoting health equity by identifying institutional barriers and integrating equity strategies into primary care is highlighted. In addition, family physicians participate in the identification and mitigation of social determinants of health, such as racism, poverty and educational inequality, through collaborative multisectoral actions.21

In Brazil, Canada and Cuba, family physicians have contributed to strengthening health systems by increasing coverage, improving health outcomes and reducing inequalities.17 Moreover, in Canada, the formal assignment of patients to family physicians has increased the number of consultations, an effective strategy for improving access to care, as patients without a regular provider often avoid medical care, have unmet needs, face difficulties accessing preventive care and accumulate health problems.17 22 23

As another strategy to improve access, family physicians in countries such as Cuba, Colombia and the USA have implemented extended hours, adapting their hours of care to facilitate access for those whose responsibilities prevent them from attending during regular hours.3 22 24

These findings underline the relevance of family medicine in the organisation and efficiency of health systems, highlighting the importance of strengthening it to ensure equitable and continuous access to health services.35 14,20 22

First contact

In several countries of the Americas, the family physician acts as the gateway to the health system, providing comprehensive, continuous and decisive care from the first contact.3 5 14 26 27 This role is particularly evident in countries such as Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Paraguay and Peru, where their work is concentrated at the first level of care.26

In Mexico, family doctors represent the gateway to the entire health system of the Mexican Social Security Institute.27 Similarly, in the USA, family medicine is distinguished as a reliable first contact that directly attends to most medical needs without requiring referrals and, in many cases, is the only health professional with whom the patient interacts.14

In Colombia, family doctors play a crucial role in guaranteeing effective access to the health system, resolving approximately 80% of health problems without the need for referrals to other levels. This demonstrates the high-resolution capacity and relevance of family medicine in the care of the population.3 5

Comprehensiveness

In most of the countries of the Americas, family physicians provide continuous, coordinated and person-centred care, including the family, and consider biological, psychological, social, religious and cultural aspects in a variety of settings. This type of care is characterised by interventions based on a risk-based approach and comprehensive and continuous care, including rehabilitation activities and epidemiological surveillance with appropriate use of resources. In addition, family physicians manage the challenges associated with caring for populations with high needs and low health literacy through their ability to perform diagnosis and clinical management in resource-limited settings, the establishment of trusting relationships, health education and the management of barriers related to time and cost.35 14,17 20 22 28

In the USA, it was reported that the most comprehensive family physicians were found in community, educational and nationally qualified health centres, where they provided a broader range of services.31 It was also shown that physicians who offered a greater diversity of services had a reduction in total costs for their patients and a decrease in hospitalisations compared with those with more limited practices.32

In Brazil, the family physician most frequently diagnoses chronic and mental illnesses, including conditions that require an active approach to be suspected, investigated and diagnosed, which are often neglected in the community.33

Additionally, in Canada, 50% of family medicine consultations address more than one reason, with older adults being the group with the most concerns. This shows how the family physician’s comprehensive approach is beneficial both for patients, who have the possibility of addressing all their health concerns in a single visit, as well as for health systems, optimising the use of resources and improving the quality of clinical care.34

The literature highlights the versatility of family physicians, who can manage more than 85% of health problems and improve the health system’s capacity to resolve them.3 15 19 25 35

Continuity

Continuity implies offering medical care over time and in different life cycles, maintaining a stable and meaningful clinical relationship with the patient and family.3 14 36 In countries such as Argentina, this role of the family doctor has shown that greater continuity is associated with fewer visits to the emergency department.36

This added value is especially reinforced in vulnerable groups such as older adults, who reported having relationships of up to 20 years with their family doctor, resisting a change of doctor except for unavoidable circumstances, such as the retirement of the doctor or the patient moving home.37 These long-term relationships strengthen patient autonomy, improve the detection of chronic diseases and facilitate access to the health system.3 14 15 33 35 38

In Canada, an assessment of the impact of family physician continuity revealed that patients with high continuity had lower emergency department use than did those with moderate or low continuity. In terms of hospitalisations, the average number of patients was similar between groups.39

The literature agrees that continuity strengthens the therapeutic link, improves adherence, increases the quality of care, promotes greater preventive activities, generates greater patient satisfaction and allows for safer and more personalised care. In addition, it has a beneficial health impact on health systems by reducing emergency room visits, hospitalisations and mortality rates.5 14 23 28 36 38 39

When continuity is not achieved, patients experience dissatisfaction with care, decreasing their level of trust in the system, which may delay seeking care, self-medicating and even discontinuing treatment. Among the elements limiting continuity are certain organisational characteristics of health services and the low availability of appointments on weekends. These findings indicate that the organisation of health services plays a crucial role in the experience of continuity.20 38

Family and community orientation

This principle promotes comprehensive medical care, oriented not only to the care of the individual, his or her family and the community to which he or she belongs but also towards social transformation.20 40 41

In Latin America and the Caribbean, family medicine is positioned as an essential model for caring for oppressed and marginalised populations. Its ability to act in community settings and build multisectoral relationships allows it to play a role as an advocate, educator and collaborator in reducing health disparities and promoting a more just and equitable system.5 21 32 42 Within this broad range of family physicians’ care tasks, notable strengths were found in Paraguay, Mexico and Peru, countries with a high impact on community activities.29

A representative example is the Cuban model, where Basic Health Teams (BHT, Equipos Básicos de Salud-EBS) were created, which are composed of doctors and nurses in community clinics, who focus on health situation analysis, home care and care for terminally ill patients.22

In Canada, the role of the family physician is very broad and is oriented mainly towards universal clinical skills, integrated healthcare and community health. In rural areas, they provide more consultations per patient than their urban counterparts do, reflecting their importance in areas with less access to specialists.17 20 In the USA, however, family physicians tailor their care to the needs of their patients and communities. However, although the role in the community is fundamental, it is not always well defined, which is why there is a need to strengthen this dimension within professional practice.14

Appropriate care

Family physicians make rational and efficient use of resources by prioritising interventions based on the best scientific evidence.5 14 15 22 24

Studies have shown that patients seen by family physicians are less likely to undergo commonly requested laboratory tests during a consultation than those seen by general practitioners are, without compromising the timely diagnosis of different pathologies. This practice is based on clinical criteria and adequate patient continuity. Furthermore, family doctors minimise the exposure of patients to invasive tests or tests of low clinical value without compromising the quality of care. These findings suggest that family physicians may contribute to greater health system efficiency through more appropriate use of diagnostic resources based on clinical judgement and available evidence.3 22 24 33 35

Coordination

One of the essential principles of the family physician is the coordination of care, which is understood as the ability to articulate health services at different levels of care according to the needs of the patient. To this end, the available resources must be integrated, always offering quality care without fragmenting patient care. In this framework, family doctors play a key role in integrated service networks, acting as leaders and active members of multidisciplinary teams.3 5 14 17 20 22 24 43

Several experiences in the region demonstrate the strategic value of this role. In Mexico, for example, the family doctor is a professional who acts as a gateway to the health system, coordinating patient care and follow-up.27 Similarly, in Canada, they act as gatekeepers in referrals to higher levels, favouring the rational use of resources.19

In 2021, a study on referral and counter-referral systems in 16 Latin American countries revealed that in 9 of them, it was mandatory to go through family doctors and/or general practitioners to access specialist care and other hospital services. In other countries (Argentina, Colombia, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Peru, Paraguay and Venezuela), intermediation is not required.26

In Brazil, when a family doctor leads a consultation, after applying clinical reasoning, referrals to specialties such as otorhinolaryngology, orthopaedics, general surgery, gynaecology, vascular surgery and urology are reduced by more than 50%. Moreover, those areas requiring specialised equipment or skills such as physiotherapy, ophthalmology and surgery increased, thus allowing the efficient use of resources by managing resolvable cases in primary care.35

Health promotion and disease prevention

Family doctors play important roles in health promotion and disease prevention, intervening in the health-disease process at an early stage, which has a significant effect on health indicators.519 27,30 42 44

In Cuba, these actions have led to improvements in health indicators, including increased vaccination coverage in children under 1 year of age, early prenatal care (<12 weeks), increased exclusive breastfeeding up to 6 months and greater coverage in sex education and family planning.22

In addition, family physicians design individualised screening and preventive treatment plans for older adults, considering life expectancy, frailty, comorbidities and functionality, especially in the absence of specific guidelines for those over 74 years of age.5 44

Resource manager

The PHC model guarantees access to and effectiveness in health through strategies with quality standards. In this context, the family doctor optimises care by promoting a rational use of diagnostic tests, minimises the number of consultations required to resolve a health problem, reduces the number of emergency consultations and hospitalisations, efficiently coordinates multidisciplinary teams, resolves approximately 80% of health problems without the need for referrals, and has an impact on preventable immune diseases. All of the above factors translate into lower costs for patients and the health system.3 5 14 17 24 32 34 35 40 45

In Cuba, with the implementation of family medicine, improvements in public health have been observed, reflected in a decrease in infant and under-five mortality, a reduction in the incidence of low birth weight, a decrease in hospital admissions and stays and an increase in life expectancy. These results are in line with findings in other Latin American countries, where a greater presence of family doctors has been associated with better economic and health indicators, including greater investment in health, reduced inequality, lower rates of childhood anaemia and decreased maternal and infant mortality without increasing out-of-pocket spending.22 45

All these data show that a greater presence of family doctors is associated with better public health indicators.3 5 14 17 24 32 34 35 40 45

Taken together, these findings illustrate that the impact of family medicine extends beyond individual clinical encounters and operates at the structural level of health systems, influencing efficiency, equity and sustainability—dimensions that are central to contemporary health policy debates in the Americas.

The family doctor beyond the Americas

This section is presented to contrast international experiences with those of the Americas and to identify transferable strategies that may inform regional policy development, workforce planning and health system reform.

Comparing experiences across regions is essential to identify similarities, differences and lessons learnt that can strengthen PHC at a global level. A study in 14 European countries identified continuity, comprehensiveness, orientation, coordination, community cooperation, teamwork, accessibility, prevention of overdiagnosis and overtreatment, family orientation and leadership, all of which focused on comprehensive patient care, as core values of family doctors.5 46

Spain and Portugal stand out for having the highest rates of family physicians (74.8 and 53.6 per 100 000 inhabitants, respectively), in contrast to most countries in the Americas, where availability is considerably lower. In countries such as Mexico, Uruguay and Argentina, the figures range from 14 to 34, whereas in others such as Colombia, Peru and Brazil, they are less than 10 per 100 000. A higher density of family doctors in Europe was associated with better gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, lower mortality and higher life expectancy (up to 83 years in Spain vs 68.7 in Bolivia).45

In England, greater availability and access to family doctors are associated with better quality of care and better health outcomes in individuals with cardiovascular disease, osteoarthritis, diabetes, incontinence and hearing problems.45 47

In addition, multidisciplinary teamwork, which is present in countries such as England and Spain, is associated with lower health expenditures. In Denmark, Italy, England and Spain, family doctors act as a gateway to the system, whereas in France and Germany, patients directly access the desired specialty, including family medicine.48,50

Future development of family medicine

Future strengthening of family medicine in the Americas requires integrated workforce planning, stable financing mechanisms, expanded academic capacity and explicit incorporation of family medicine into national health system governance. Strategic investments in training, retention and equitable distribution of family physicians will be central to advancing universal health coverage and health equity.3 5 16 17 20 21 24 27 30 35 39 40 43 45

Strengths and implications of the study

This review provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of family medicine’s system-level impact in the Americas. Its findings offer actionable insights for policymakers, educators and health system planners seeking to strengthen PHC and advance more equitable and resilient health systems.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of this research include the possible exclusion of important literature due to language barriers, lack of access to full text, selected databases, search range or indexing criteria, which could have excluded relevant studies from the analysis. In addition, the diversity across countries and models of care makes direct comparisons of results difficult.

Additional limitations include heterogeneity in definitions of family medicine across countries, variability in outcome measures and potential publication bias within the included literature.

Conclusions

Family physicians are consistently associated with improving accessibility and quality of care and reducing health inequities in the health systems of the Americas. Their presence is associated with improved public health indicators, greater efficiency in the use of resources and reduced inequities.

In countries such as Canada and the USA, family physicians play a central role as care managers, with autonomy and recognition. In other countries, such as Brazil, Paraguay and Chile, it has gained ground as a pillar of PHC strategies. These initiatives have shown significant progress in terms of coverage, community orientation and articulation with service networks, although there are still challenges related to financing, training and coverage. In contexts such as Mexico, Colombia or Peru, the implementation of the family medicine model has been more heterogeneous, depending largely on national policies and local efforts to consolidate multidisciplinary teams and effective lines of care.

Overall, this review reinforces family physicians as cornerstone actors in strengthening health systems across the Americas. Sustained policy commitment, academic investment, and system-level integration of family medicine are essential for achieving equitable, efficient, and people-centred health systems in the region.
