Surveillance of Pharmaceutical Risk‐Mitigation Behavior: Applying and Comparing Statistical Process Control Methods Using Real World Data
Authors: Harris Butler, John D. Rice, Nichole E. Carlson, Elaine H. Morrato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionActive post‐marketing surveillance of prescribing behavior of high‐risk drugs may provide early warning of unforeseen issues in a population, yet analysis approaches for surveillance using real‐world data are underdeveloped. This paper evaluates a modified statistical process control (SPC) method for surveillance of risk minimization measures derived from administrative claims data. The approach detects changes in population‐level prescribing behaviors and informs investigators of the timing and nature of any detected changes.MethodsWe investigated prescription drug claims for tapentadol extended release (ER) from the Colorado All‐Payers Claims Database (2012–2019). The cohort was 2702 unique patients receiving their first prescriptions of tapentadol ER (an opiate with an FDA Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy). The risk minimization measures were the prescribing rate and the proportion of prescriptions with appropriate dosing. A statistical model was fitted to data from January–December 2012 to establish a stable baseline and then updated biweekly through June 2019. We conducted surveillance with our modified SPC method and a classical SPC method, controlling the false alarm rate to 0.005 for each, and compared how detections aligned with external policy actions.ResultsOur method detected two periods of unusual prescribing behavior beginning in March 2015 (p < 0.005) and April 2016 (p < 0.005). The classical method detected a single period in October 2016 (p < 0.005). Our detections aligned with risk minimization activities in Colorado; the classical method aligned with only the second activity, 6 months later.ConclusionOur modified SPC method, which monitors model misspecification rather than raw prescribing data, detects more periods of changing behavior that better temporally align with risk minimization actions. This tool may be useful for regulatory agencies to independently monitor for emerging risks and prescribing trends to complement REMS assessments.

Background

Digital infrastructure and reporting are foundational for learning health systems (LHS). This paper evaluates a case‐application of a modified continuous monitoring method, i.e., statistical process control, for active postmarket surveillance of prescribing and REMS risk mitigating behaviors using real‐world administrative claims data [1]. SPC is a method for monitoring a process using control charts to distinguish between common‐cause variation (stable or in‐control) and special‐cause variation (signals or out‐of‐control). SPC has been implemented in healthcare applications for several years [2], but not in a postmarket surveillance application. We base our method on an SPC method because SPC is suited to large electronic claims data and the way SPC methods track a process enables surveillance and rapid detection of changes in risk management behavior at a population level; our proposed method takes advantage of these characteristics while correcting performance issues with an application of classical SPC methods [3]. Classical SPC approaches assume data from a typically steady process over time and signal when the data moves outside of pre‐specified bounds, which will be violated in typical surveillance applications. More specifically, our approach is based on detecting model misspecification using a two‐phase modified statistical process control (SPC) approach, which is designed to more quickly detect changing prescribing behavior while avoiding false alarms from exogenous covariates such as changing population demographics. The case‐application investigates safe‐use prescribing behaviors associated with an extended‐release opiate in the state of Colorado over an 8‐year period in which the context of national‐ and state‐level actions affecting opioid prescribing were numerous (Table 1). We compare the approach with a classical method to demonstrate greater signal sensitivity with our modified method.

Motivation

The Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act of 2007 gave the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) authority to require Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS) for certain medications with serious safety concerns. The goal of a REMS program is to help ensure a drug's benefits outweigh its risks when used in clinical practice. REMS are specialized drug safety programs implemented in the health system and can require special prescriber, patient, pharmacy certification, clinical and/or laboratory requirements before dispensing, and long‐term monitoring. As of February 18, 2026, there are 73 active REMS [4], which also require periodic assessment of the REMS, provided by the pharmaceutical company, to determine if the policies are achieving the desired effect.

However, as noted by Banerjee et al. [21] REMS assessments are sometimes late or missing required information. Concerns have been raised that the FDA lacks the necessary data to determine whether REMS are effective [22]. Additionally, even when complete REMS assessments are provided to the FDA on time, they are retrospective in nature and only required annually, and sometimes less frequently [4]. As a result, assessment of compliance with REMS risk mitigation requirements and their impact on healthcare behavior is not available in near real‐time for quality improvement purposes and ensuring public health.

Two of FDA's regulatory science priorities are advancing product safety surveillance and use of real‐world evidence to support regulatory decision making [23]. In 2024, FDA issued new guidance to industry—REMS Logic Model: A Framework to Link Program Design with Assessment [24]. The guidance introduces the concept of key performance indicators as measures for providing insight into whether a REMS is having its intended effect. For example, indicators can include observable safe use behaviors affecting prescribing, testing, and treatment patterns. FDA's approach is analogous to HEDIS (The Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set) measures established by NCQA (National Committee for Quality Assurance) to audit health care performance across important clinical areas and across health systems. Similar to clinical quality improvement norms within a learning health system, modifications to REMS programs are common as new scientific evidence emerges and the healthcare system in which they operate evolves [25].

The ability for FDA to conduct its own independent surveillance of key REMS performance indicators using its real‐world data resources, e.g., the Sentinel Initiative [26], would also advance postmarket safety surveillance and encourage a learning regulatory health system. To promote transparency, active REMS surveillance would ideally be carried out using publicly available data in a timely manner to determine if a shift in risk‐mitigation practices or prescribing behavior occurs. Moreover, this approach would be responsive to FDA's desire for using real‐world data (RWD), i.e., routinely collected electronic health data on patient health status and/or the delivery of health care, to support its regulatory decision making [27]. However, methods for this type of active REMS key performance surveillance are under‐developed. There is potential to realize surveillance goals by using statistical process control methods on electronic health data, resulting in a system that enables rapid detection of behavioral changes to enable policy feedback and improvement. Administrative claims data is well suited to this task. While not fully public, it is available to the FDA and routinely generated for billing, making it available in a timely fashion and less likely to fall victim to the many reasons why a formal REMS assessment might be missing or delayed.

REMS Case Example: Tapentadol Extended Release (Nucynta ER)

Tapentadol ER is an extended‐release opioid indicated for patients for whom other pain management therapies are ineffective [28]. Immediate‐release tapentadol received FDA approval in 2008, while the extended‐release dose form was approved for the management of moderate to severe chronic pain on August 26, 2011, and for management of pain associated with diabetic peripheral neuropathy on August 29, 2012 [5]. Tapentadol ER has been subject to the extended‐release/long‐acting (ER/LA) Opioids REMS requirements since July 9, 2012 [4]. The goal of the ER/LA REMS program was to educate prescribers on the safe use of opioids and to inform patients on appropriate use through patient counseling with their provider and through a patient Medication Guide dispensed with the drug [8]. Given the sustained national opioid epidemic, significant federal and state policy action occurred throughout the past decade at various times and political levels, affecting prescribing and safe use of these medicines [29, 30, 31, 32]; making it worthwhile to monitor prescribing behavior. Tapentadol ER was selected for this case study because it is an opioid subject to REMS requirements and regulatory changes that may have driven changes in prescribing behavior observable in administrative claims data.

Methods

Data Source

We evaluate tapentadol ER prescribing behaviors in the state of Colorado. In 2012, Colorado ranked in the top quarter of US states with the highest rates of self‐reported nonmedical use of opioids [33]. The state took significant legislative and regulatory actions to address misuse and diversion (Table 1).

Study data comes from the Colorado All Payer Claims Database (CO APCD), administered by the Center for Improving Value in Healthcare [34, 35]. The CO APCD includes insurance claims for Coloradans covered by an insurance company that reports their information to the Center for Improving Value in Healthcare (CIVHC) [36]. APCDs were established by states to monitor population health and promote cost transparency, and they have been used to inform and evaluate policymaking affecting healthcare delivery [37]. The CO APCD includes 68% of all Coloradans and 75% of insured Coloradans [36]. Payers include Medicare, Medicaid, and major commercial insurers. Although our modified method can accommodate covariates, we used a population‐level model (described later) for this case example and did not adjust for covariates since our demographics were relatively stable during the time period of our data.

Study Population

This analysis includes all medical and pharmacy claims from a payer participating in the CO APCD for any patient who was prescribed any drug with a REMS requirement between 1 January 2012 and 31 July 2019 [4]. We selected all patients who had been prescribed tapentadol ER. The study was conducted under a data use agreement with CIVHC and received approval from the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (protocol #18‐1871). We identified the first (index) prescription of tapentadol ER for every patient that filled at least one prescription during the period covered by the data (N = 3119). We excluded all patients who did not have 6 months of continuous insurance eligibility prior to their first tapentadol ER prescription claim, as we could not reasonably determine whether the first prescription we observed was their first prescription filled (N = 2702). From the record of the first tapentadol ER claim we used quantity, dosage, and days supplied to determine the daily dosage of the first prescription. We also looked back 6 months to find prescriptions of other opioids, which, if present, changed our determination of the patient's recommended dose according to the tapentadol ER labeling.

Determination of First Dose Appropriateness

We identified opioids using FDA listed NDC codes [38] and used the FDA definition of opioid naive, i.e., that the patient has not taken any opioid for at least a week. We used the FDA definition of opioid tolerant, i.e., that the patient has taken at least 60 mg daily morphine or equivalent in the past week [39], using the CDC's Oral MME conversion table [38], which was current at the time of this research and included conversion factors for oral and non‐oral routes. A patient classed as not opioid tolerant but also not opioid naive is one that has taken opioids in the past week but is below the 60 mg daily morphine or equivalent threshold to be considered tolerant.

By examining each patient's prior prescription history, we determined whether they were opioid naive, not opioid tolerant, or opioid tolerant. This analysis was patient‐centric, meaning that all of a patient's claims were considered, even if a patient saw multiple providers or claims for multiple opioids. According to the tapentadol ER drug labeling [28], patients that are opioid naive or not opioid tolerant should start tapentadol ER with a daily dose of 100 mg or less. Patients that have a prior prescription for tapentadol (immediate release) can be converted to an equivalent daily dose of tapentadol ER. Patients that are opioid tolerant but receiving an opioid other than tapentadol are recommended to start tapentadol ER with a daily dose of 100 mg.

To evaluate safe use, appropriate first dose is defined as naive and non‐tolerant patients with a daily dose per a drug's labeling. Opioid‐tolerant patients were assumed to be at an appropriate first dose unless they exceed the maximum recommended dose. No patient, regardless of their tolerance, should have a daily dose greater than 500 mg according to the labeling [28].

Modified Statistical Process Control Model

Part 1 of our statistical process control approach is to define a statistical model of the outcomes of interest for monitoring/surveillance. Based on our prior work [1], the primary outcomes of interest were the biweekly count of prescriptions for tapentadol ER and the number of people starting their index prescription at an appropriate dose as determined by the labeling and a patient's past 6 months of opioid prescriptions. These outcomes are relevant to the risk in a population because prescription counts provide information on the opportunity for misuse within a population, and the proportion of prescriptions with appropriate dosing provides information on the risk associated with each prescription.

Using statistical notation, let X be the count of new prescriptions in 2 weeks, which follows a Poisson distribution with a rate parameter λ: X~Poissonλ=abeθ1

The rate parameter λ is determined by the Colorado population in millions (a) and an exposure time of 14 days (b), times the daily per capita prescribing rate per million people (eθ1). All of these values (λ,a,b,eθ1) should be strictly positive. Let Y∣X be the number of prescriptions that use an appropriate first dose out of the total X, which follows a binomial distribution conditional on X: Y∣X~Binomialn=X,p=11+e−θ2.

The probability of a prescription having an appropriate first dose (p) is determined by a logistic transformation on the parameter θ2. We fit this model using the method of maximum likelihood. Covariates could be incorporated into θ1 and θ2 as common linear predictors [40].

Surveillance as Detection of Model Misspecification

Our goal is to repeatedly assess data accumulating over time for changes in prescribing behavior. One way to identify such changes is to look for departures from a particular statistical model, which can be cast as a model misspecification problem. White proposed the Information Matrix (IM) test for model misspecification [41], which has components with a similar mathematical form to Pignatiello and Runger's MC1 method of statistical process control (SPC) [42]. We create an SPC method for monitoring model misspecification by replacing components of the MC1 method with components of White's IM test. A more technical treatment of our approach can be found in [3].

In the method described below, surveillance proceeds in two phases. In Phase I, an initial period of data is used to fit the statistical model and establish a stable baseline of prescribing behavior. Control parameters are selected to achieve the desired balance between sensitivity to change and false alarm rate.

In Phase II, new data are incorporated sequentially in regular batches. For each batch, model misspecification is calculated by comparing observed outcomes to those expected under the previously fitted model. This misspecification measure is standardized using its estimated covariance structure, yielding a single scalar quantity that accumulates evidence of deviation from the baseline model over time. When the cumulative statistic exceeds the pre‐specified control limit, the process is flagged as out‐of‐control and an investigation is warranted. If the statistic returns to zero, the chart resets and the model is re‐fit using all available data.

The terms in‐control and out‐of‐control are not meant to imply good and bad behavior. In this application context, more appropriate terms might be “stable” and “departure from stability.” As an extreme example, if in the first 6 months, every physician always prescribed 1000 mg doses of tapentadol ER to every patient and this behavior continued after phase I, a SPC method would perceive that as stable (or in‐control) behavior. However, the behavior would be considered undesirable from a risk management perspective. Now consider the situation where a policy was implemented and every physician immediately begins prescribing a much smaller dose, following safe prescribing practices. An SPC method would detect that change and signal an out‐of‐control period. Investigation of the out‐of‐control signal would show a desirable change in the prescribing process. Thus, we recommend that practitioners ensure that their initial stable behavior in phase I is acceptable, and to investigate any out‐of‐control signals during phase II to determine if changing prescribing behavior is in a desirable or undesirable direction.

Determining Statistical Process Control Parameters

The surveillance procedure requires specification of a decay parameter and a control limit. In prior work, we describe a simulation‐based approach for selecting these parameters by resampling Phase I data to achieve a desired false alarm rate [3]. In this application, we used that approach to calibrate the monitoring procedure. For evaluation, we selected a target false alarm rate of 0.005, corresponding to approximately one false signal in 200 monitoring batches under stable conditions. This value reflects a balance between early detection of meaningful changes and limiting unnecessary investigations. Additional technical details regarding parameter selection are provided in [3].

Comparison Application: Standard Statistical Process Control Method

To compare the performance of our modified surveillance method to a commonly used SPC approach, we applied Crosier's multivariate cumulative sum (MCUSUM) method to the same tapentadol ER prescribing data using the qcr package in R [43, 44]. We used the same Phase I baseline period and Phase II biweekly monitoring batches as in the proposed method. For both methods, control parameters were selected using the Phase I data to target a false alarm rate of no more than 1 in 200 monitoring batches (α = 0.005). Under biweekly monitoring, this corresponds to an expected false alarm approximately once every 7.7 years, which is appropriate for public health surveillance of multiple drugs. For this application, the selected parameters were s = 1.8 and h = 3.6.

This indicates that the out‐of‐control behavior only slightly departed from the stable model, and small changes like this would likely not be detected using less sensitive methods. We investigated the regulatory and policy landscape around these two time periods to understand why a departure from stability might be plausible. During the out‐of‐control periods the batches of data typically had higher than usual prescribing rates, while the prescribing rates for the in‐control periods are usually centered around the estimated parameter θ1. These results also highlight a strength of fitting a joint model on multiple outcomes‐ changes from stability may appear in either outcome, and our method can detect when one or multiple outcomes change.

Discussion

This paper evaluated a modified statistical process control method for active postmarket surveillance of risk minimization behaviors as a novel approach for evaluating the effects of regulatory actions in near real time. The modified model's utility was demonstrated using real‐world publicly available electronic health data for a case example involving safe drug initiation of an ER/LA opioid medication. While the standard statistical process control method worked in detecting state‐level regulatory actions affecting opioid prescribing, using our modified method of monitoring appears to outperform the standard method (Crosier's MCUSUM) at detecting changes in prescribing behavior sooner. We hypothesize this is for two reasons. First, the standard method's control parameters were adjusted to obtain the desired false alarm rate without the data being normally distributed; while CUSUM parameters can be adjusted for non‐normal data, they perform best with normally distributed data. Second, the standard method does not have the benefit of a model with normalized parameters, which again reduces sensitivity because the CUSUM parameters have to account for variation in underlying demographics. In contrast, our method could obtain the same false alarm rate at a higher sensitivity because the SPC method was not sensitive to fluctuating population sizes and overall prescription counts, as well as the misspecification statistics of each batch more closely following a normal distribution. In other words, even a simple statistical model has the potential to greatly improve the sensitivity of a non‐model‐based SPC surveillance method.

There are three distinct advantages of this model compared to a more naive approach. First, this model can track normalized parameters, such as the prescribing rate per million people as opposed to raw prescribing counts, which we expect to change with population. Second, it is a joint model in which parameters associated with two distinct outcomes are estimated simultaneously and in which unified model misspecification measures are dependent on both outcomes, as opposed to fitting separate models in which each outcome is independent of the other and there are two sets of model misspecification measures. Third, fitting a model with maximum likelihood results in our model misspecification measures being asymptotically normally distributed, which is desirable for SPC methods to perform well. While there are SPC methods that track joint outcomes, ours is the first to make use of a generalized framework allowing any statistical model fit with maximum likelihood, which enables surveillance practitioners to use one method against nearly any set of outcomes observable in claims data [3].

Learning Health Systems

As stated earlier, digital infrastructure and reporting are foundational for learning health systems. To achieve the greatest gains in continuous process improvement in health and healthcare, immediate feedback of performance results is necessary [45]. Statistical process control methods have been applied for improvement of public health and healthcare processes of care and service delivery [2, 46, 47]. The FDA Sentinel System, a distributed electronic data network involving healthcare and administrative claims data, was designed to support a national real‐world data infrastructure for a learning health system and the monitoring of risks associated with drug and biologic products to inform regulatory decisions [26]. The modified statistical process control methods can be readily applied to the Sentinel System and other real‐world data for prospective risk management assessment to inform future REMS modifications and evaluate their effectiveness [48]. We distinguish the statistical method validated here from the broader system required for active surveillance. Operationalizing this approach within a Learning Health System necessitates infrastructure beyond the analytic engine, including automated pipelines for ingesting real‐world data and visualization dashboards for stakeholders. Ultimately, transforming these signals into care improvements requires communicating statistical alerts directly to regulatory or clinical decision‐makers, thereby closing the loop between data analysis and policy intervention.

Interpretation

When interpreting the findings and potential causal factors triggering signal detection, we must also consider context, i.e., not only changes in FDA REMS policies but also other state and national actions affecting opioid prescribing during the study time period. The tapentadol ER case example indicates that state‐level action designed to specifically affect prescribing behaviors, i.e., PDMP and daily morphine equivalent mandates, was temporally associated with signal detection whereas more diffuse national educational actions, i.e., ER/LA REMS and other continuing medical education efforts, were not. However, the current findings indicate that state‐level prescriber and payer policies had an immediate and detectable impact and underscore the importance of understanding state‐level factors when evaluating REMS key performance. This is particularly true given the variety of state‐level actions taken to curb the opioid epidemic [29] and broad societal changes in opioid prescribing that were occurring during the time period of the study [49].

The literature indicates a wide range of effectiveness for different interventions targeting opioid safe‐use prescribing behaviors. Opioid dosing guidelines and/or physician training have been shown to impact prescribing practices [50]. Studies examining Colorado policies have shown mixed results. Financially incentivizing physicians to adhere to chronic pain management and opioid prescribing guidelines, versus the use of mandates, only temporarily improved prescribing practices among Colorado workers' compensation providers [50]. Similarly, statutory limits on days' supply among opioid naive patients (i.e., ≤ 7‐day supply) had little impact on opioid prescribing in Colorado [51], consistent with our finding of no detectable shift in 2018.

The example presented in this paper is reassuring as it provides evidence that prescribing behavior of tapentadol ER varied over time in terms of prescribing rate per capita and the proportion of appropriate dosing. In addition, those changes in prescribing behavior correlated temporally with policy changes. While we believe that these observed changes are plausibly associated with policy changes, this analysis is not causal.

Intended Application

We demonstrated the method with a retrospective study, but it is intended for prospective scenarios such as surveillance of prescribing patterns. A notional use case is a subscription to a query from a claims data provider, and the method can be updated when new data are available from the provider. An investigation would be conducted the first time the method signals a departure from stable prescribing behavior, and a regulatory agency may choose to make policy changes.

Limitations

This method is subject to the limitations of administrative claims data. First, claims data do not capture prescriptions paid for with cash, nor can it account for opioid exposure via illicit sources or diversion. Second, claims data lack clinical indications, preventing verification of the medical intent behind a prescription or justification of doses above FDA labeling. Finally, the SPC framework presented here is optimized for detecting shifts in population‐level patterns; it is not suitable for monitoring rare adverse events, which lack the frequency necessary to establish stable process baselines or reflect broader behavioral changes.
