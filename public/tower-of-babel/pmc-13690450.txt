Decline in dementia patients during the COVID-19 pandemic: A cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Meng-Ju Tsai, Yu-Ruei Lin, Yung-Shuan Lin, Chun-Yu Chen, Jong-Ling Fuh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The cognitive consequences of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in patients with dementia remain uncertain. This study examined whether COVID-19 infection was associated with cognitive decline.Methods:From October 2020 to October 2022, COVID-19-naive patients with dementia were recruited from a hospital-based memory clinic in Taipei. During follow-up, patients who developed COVID-19 were classified as the infected group, while others served as controls. For controls, baseline and follow-up were defined as two consecutive annual cognitive assessments. For infected patients, baseline and follow-up were the nearest assessments before and after COVID-19 infection, respectively. The mean assessment interval was approximately 1 year in both groups. Outcomes included the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), and Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI). Linear mixed-effects models assessed whether cognitive trajectories differed by COVID-19 status.Results:After excluding patients without eligible follow-up data, infected patients were matched with controls by age and sex. Overall, 44 patients with dementia who developed COVID-19 and 22 who remained uninfected were included in the study. Baseline demographic characteristics and cognitive scores were comparable. The mean age was 84.1 years, with 36% (24/66) male. At follow-up, cognitive performance did not differ significantly between infected and control groups. Within-group analyses showed significant declines in MMSE, CDR-SB, and CASI in the infected group, whereas controls declined significantly only in CASI. In adjusted mixed-effects models, the COVID-19 × time interaction was not significant (β = –0.427; p = 0.676), suggesting no significant difference in MMSE decline trajectory between groups. Higher education was associated with higher MMSE scores (β = 0.553; p = 0.002). Sensitivity analyses supported these findings.Conclusion:After accounting for age, sex, education, and assessment interval, COVID-19 infection was not associated with faster cognitive decline. Participants with more education generally had higher MMSE scores, but education did not significantly alter the rate of decline.

1. INTRODUCTION

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), quickly spread worldwide after its emergence.1,2 Taiwan exhibited a unique infection pattern: a contained community outbreak in May 2021 was followed by a much larger wave beginning mid-2022 that infected over half the population by March 2023.3 This staggered timeline left a proportion of the population uninfected for an extended period, providing a useful context for examining the impact of COVID-19.

Although initially regarded as a respiratory disease, COVID-19 is now recognized as a systemic disease. Neurological symptoms were present in one-third of infected individuals in the early stages,4 and accumulating evidence indicates that COVID-19 may lead to long-term cognitive and neurobehavioral consequences.5–7

Patients with dementia are more susceptible to contracting COVID-19 due to increased age, comorbidities, difficulties adhering to self-protection measures, and a higher likelihood of institutional living.8–10 Once infected, they experienced more severe illness and higher mortality. Large cohort studies from the UK Biobank and Korea have identified preexisting dementia as a major independent risk factor for hospitalization and death.11,12

Several studies have suggested that COVID-19 infection may accelerate cognitive decline in individuals with dementia. For example, an Italian cohort reported a faster annual Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) decline among infected patients,13 whereas an Indian study found poorer post-infection cognitive performance in all survivors.14 However, prior studies often involved small or highly vulnerable cohorts and lacked methods to account for unequal follow-up intervals, leaving unclear whether COVID-19 independently accelerates cognitive decline in typical outpatient dementia populations.

Despite this accumulating evidence, whether COVID-19 infection accelerates cognitive decline in individuals with dementia remains inconclusive. We hypothesized that (1) COVID-19 infection would be associated with an accelerated trajectory of cognitive deterioration in patients with dementia and (2) this association would persist as an independent predictor even after adjusting for demographic factors such as age and education. To examine these hypotheses, we conducted a longitudinal cohort study comparing MMSE trajectories between patients with dementia with and without COVID-19 infection.

2. METHODS

2.1. Participants

This prospective observational study was conducted at the neurology memory clinic of Taipei Veterans General Hospital. From October 2020 to October 2022, we recruited patients diagnosed with major neurocognitive disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, criteria.15 All participants were COVID-19-naive at enrollment and underwent regular outpatient follow-up.

COVID-19 infection status during follow-up was determined by patient or caregiver report and verified through medical record review whenever available. Participants who developed COVID-19 during follow-up were assigned to the infected group, whereas those who remained uninfected throughout the observation period served as controls. To reduce confounding by age and sex, each control participant was matched by sex and age, within ±5 years, with up to two infected participants.

Participants were excluded if they missed follow-up visits, had an interval of more than two years between cognitive assessments, or had cognition-interfering comorbidities before inclusion. These conditions included acute changes in consciousness due to systemic illness, drug intoxication, central nervous system infection, or head trauma causing neurological deficits. Such comorbidities were evaluated through history taking, physical examination, medical record review, and inspection of laboratory and imaging reports.

The infected group was further categorized according to whether patients received COVID-19-specific treatments, including nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, molnupiravir, or remdesivir. Patients who did not receive these agents were classified as receiving symptomatic treatment only.

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taipei Veterans General Hospital approved this study. Written informed consent was obtained from the caregivers of all participants with dementia. All identifying information was encrypted before data release for research purposes. The IRB number was 2021-09-006A.

2.2. Clinical evaluation and procedures

Upon recruitment, participants underwent standardized clinical evaluation and testing, which included interviewing, physical examination, and global neurocognitive screening. The global neurocognitive screening included the MMSE, Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI), and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), all of which were arranged once per year for each patient.16–18 The time-consuming CASI (30-40 minutes) was occasionally omitted due to outpatient logistical constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic, with clinicians relying on the MMSE and CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) instead. Before each assessment, patients were screened for delirium by healthcare professionals. If delirium was detected, treatment was initiated, and the cognitive assessment was postponed to a later date. MMSE and CASI were used to assess global cognitive function, while the CDR assessed the capability of daily living. Additionally, the CDR-SB was calculated. Dementia severity was stratified according to baseline CDR scores: CDR = 0.5 indicated very mild dementia, CDR = 1 indicated mild dementia, and CDR = 2 or 3 indicated moderate to severe dementia.

For the control group, the first available annual cognitive assessment was defined as baseline, and the subsequent annual assessment was defined as follow-up. For the infected group, the cognitive assessment closest before COVID-19 infection was defined as baseline, and the cognitive assessment closest after infection was defined as follow-up. Thus, both groups were evaluated over an approximately 1-year interval, while the infected group was specifically analyzed using pre- and post-infection cognitive assessments.

2.3. Data analysis

Due to the relatively small sample size and non-normal distribution in our dataset, nonparametric tests were utilized. Differences in demographic profiles and cognitive capacity between the control and infected groups, and between infected subgroups receiving symptomatic vs COVID-19-specific treatments, were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test and chi-square test (with Fisher exact test applied when approximation was not applicable). Cliff’s delta was used to assess effect size and ensure baseline comparability.19 The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare baseline and 1-year follow-up cognitive capacity within each group.

To examine whether COVID-19 infection was independently associated with cognitive decline, we employed linear mixed-effects models (LMMs), which account for the correlated, unbalanced, and repeated-measures nature of the data. The MMSE score was specified as the dependent variable. Fixed effects included age, sex, years of education, COVID-19 status, the interval between assessments in years, and the COVID-19 diagnosis × assessment interval, the latter of which was of primary interest for evaluating group differences in cognitive trajectory. A random intercept was included for each subject to account for individual variability in baseline MMSE scores, with a variance components (VCs) covariance structure specified for the random effect. The same approach was applied to CDR-SB and CASI scores. Statistical significance of fixed effects was assessed using restricted maximum likelihood (REML)-based t tests, with denominator degrees of freedom calculated via the Satterthwaite approximation and type III sums of squares. Analyses were conducted using complete cases for each outcome; therefore, participants with missing CASI data were excluded only from the CASI model, while their available MMSE and CDR-SB data were retained.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26 (IBM, Inc., Armonk, NY), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

2.4. Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the main findings. First, to evaluate potential attrition bias, baseline characteristics were compared between participants who completed follow-up and those who dropped out. Because non-Alzheimer’s disease (AD) participants showed a higher dropout rate, the primary analysis was repeated after excluding non-AD cases.

Second, to reduce the potential influence of acute or subacute post-infection effects, we repeated the analysis after excluding infected participants whose follow-up cognitive assessment was performed within three months after COVID-19 infection. Third, because nursing home residents may have poorer baseline health status and different environmental exposures from home-dwelling participants, we repeated the analysis after excluding nursing home residents.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Study participants

Of the 93 enrolled participants, 19 did not complete the second assessment, and one completed follow-up beyond the prespecified 2-year interval. Thus, 73 participants were eligible for the longitudinal analysis. Compared with eligible participants (n = 73), excluded participants (n = 20) were significantly younger (78.5 ± 8.3 vs 83.1 ± 8.5 years; p = 0.025) and had a higher proportion of non-AD etiology (40% vs 12.3%; p = 0.009; Table S1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A425). No significant differences were found in sex, education, or baseline cognitive scores. All excluded participants were COVID-19 positive; three were asymptomatic, while the rest experienced mild symptoms (eg, cough or fever). None required hospitalization due to COVID-19. Of the eligible 73 participants, 51 had a history of COVID-19 infection during the follow-up period, while 22 had not been infected. Each control participant was matched by sex and age (within ±5 years) with up to two infected participants, resulting in a matched cohort of 22 controls and 44 infected participants. Seven infected patients were excluded from the final analysis due to the lack of age-matched controls within the specified range (±5 years). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups showed no significant differences (Table 1; all p > 0.05). Most participants were community-dwelling and attended the outpatient clinic directly from home. The mean interval between two evaluations was 371.8 ± 69.6 days. The whole cohort had a mean age of 84.1 years, 36.4% being men, and an average education level of 8.9 years. The baseline MMSE score was 16.6, and the CDR-SB score was 7.2. Eighteen participants lacked CASI scores. While younger and having more non-AD etiology (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A426), their baseline MMSE (p = 0.613), CDR-SB (p = 0.778), and COVID-19 incidence (p = 0.241) were comparable to those with CASI, indicating missingness was independent of disease severity and infection status. In the infected group, the majority (88.6%, n = 39) were asymptomatic or experienced mild symptoms, while 11.4% (n = 5) had moderate to severe symptoms. The average interval between the infection date and the follow-up tests was 157.8 days (range, 5-365 days). The effect sizes were –0.04 (95% CI, –0.32 to 0.24) for age, 0.12 (–0.17 to 0.39) for education, –0.01 (–0.28 to 0.26) for baseline MMSE, –0.10 (–0.36 to 0.18) for baseline CDR-SB, and –0.01 (–0.33 to 0.30) for baseline CASI, indicating negligible effect sizes for all parameters.

The analysis results of cognitive function tests are summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 1. In the intergroup comparison, no significant differences were found between the control and infected groups in the 1-year follow-up MMSE (p = 0.924), CDR-SB scores (p = 0.946), and CASI (p = 0.901). 3.3. Linear mixed-effects models analysis

Table 3 summarizes the results of the LMM investigating the determinants of cognitive decline. For the primary outcome, higher education was associated with higher MMSE scores, with each additional year of education associated with an increase of over 0.5 points in MMSE scores (β = 0.553 [0.207-0.898]; p = 0.002). Regarding the secondary outcomes, older age was significantly associated with greater cognitive and functional deterioration, as reflected by higher CDR-SB scores (β = 0.156 [0.017-0.295]; p = 0.029) and lower CASI scores (β = –0.991 [–1.860 to –0.123]; p = 0.026). A significant negative association between follow-up time and cognitive function was observed exclusively in CASI (β = –7.928 [–14.573 to –1.283]; p = 0.02). Follow-up time was significantly associated with CASI scores, suggesting that CASI captured measurable cognitive change over the follow-up interval.

3.4. Exploratory descriptive analysis according to receipt of COVID-19-specific treatment

Because treatment receipt was strongly confounded by disease severity and hospitalization, this analysis was descriptive and was not intended to estimate treatment efficacy. Among the 44 participants infected with COVID-19, more than half (n = 24) received COVID-19-specific treatments, whereas the others received only symptomatic treatments. Table S3 (https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A427) summarizes the characteristics of these two groups. No significant difference was found in age, comorbidities, education level, type of dementia, baseline MMSE, CDR-SB and CASI scores, or CDR severity stratification. The specific treatment group included a higher proportion of men, a trend toward having more moderate to severe symptoms (20.8% vs 0%; p = 0.053), and more hospitalizations (33.3% vs 5.0%; p = 0.027). Although a longer duration of admission was observed in the specific treatment group (15.4 ± 22.0 vs 9.0 days), statistical analysis was not conducted because only one patient was hospitalized in the symptomatic treatment group.

Table 4 presents the results of statistical analysis of cognitive capacity between the two groups. In the intergroup comparison, no significant difference was found between the symptomatic and specific treatment groups in the 1-year follow-up MMSE (p = 0.732), CDR-SB scores (p = 0.415), and CASI (p = 0.885). In the intragroup comparison of baseline and 1-year follow-up MMSE scores, no significant difference was observed in either group (p = 0.296 and 0.072, respectively). However, when comparing the baseline and 1-year follow-up CDR-SB and CASI scores, significant differences were found in the specific treatment groups (CDR-SB: 7.7 ± 4.2 to 9.1 ± 4.2; p = 0.001 and CASI: 55.6 ± 18.5 to 47.6 ± 20.9; p = 0.015), while only a trend was observed in the symptomatic treatment group (CDR-SB: 6.6 ± 4.8 to 8.2 ± 6.6; p = 0.226 and CASI: 56.7 ± 24.0 to 48.9 ± 31.4; p = 0.065).

3.5. Sensitivity analyses

To determine whether the unbalanced distribution of dementia types among dropouts biased our main findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding non-AD participants. The results were consistent with the primary analysis. The interaction effect of COVID-19 diagnosis and time remained nonsignificant (p = 0.825), and the association between higher education and higher MMSE scores was retained (p = 0.001). Interestingly, age emerged as a significant predictor of cognitive decline in this restricted model (β = –0.239 [–0.424 to –0.054]; p = 0.012). This is likely due to the reduced heterogeneity in dementia etiology, as non-AD pathologies may exhibit weaker linear associations with age compared with AD (Table S4, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A428).

The results remained similar to the primary findings when patients evaluated within the 3-month acute post-infection period were excluded. The interaction effect of COVID-19 diagnosis and time was again nonsignificant (p = 0.468), while education remained significantly associated with higher MMSE scores (β = 0.465 [0.079-0.851]; p = 0.019; Table S5, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A429).

The interaction effect of COVID-19 diagnosis and time (p = 0.865) and the main effect of education (β = 0.499 [0.129-0.870]; p = 0.009) remained the same after excluding nursing home residents (Table S6, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A430).

In summary, these sensitivity analyses demonstrated that our primary conclusions are robust and were not driven by differential attrition, the effects of acute or subacute infection, or the inclusion of nursing home residents.

4. DISCUSSION

In this prospective, observational study of 66 outpatients with dementia, we investigated whether COVID-19 infection was associated with accelerated cognitive decline. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, COVID-19 infection was not associated with a significantly different rate of cognitive decline over time. Higher educational attainment was associated with better MMSE performance across assessments; however, the nonsignificant education × time interaction suggests that education did not modify the rate of cognitive decline. These findings suggest that the cognitive impact of post-acute, nonsevere COVID-19 infection in individuals with dementia may be limited.

Previous studies have reported accelerated cognitive and functional decline in patients with dementia during the COVID-19 pandemic, attributed to both infection-related factors and indirect consequences such as reduced social engagement and physical activity.13,20–25 Other work suggested that post-infection cognitive impairment may relate to white-matter changes or frontal-subcortical network disruption.14 These findings indicate that multiple mechanisms may contribute to cognitive worsening in vulnerable populations.

Although individual variability exists, age serves as a baseline risk factor reflecting cumulative physical, environmental, and social influences.26 However, in our study, older age was associated with higher CDR-SB scores and lower CASI scores across assessments, but not MMSE. This discrepancy likely reflects MMSE’s lower sensitivity to subtle age-related changes.

In contrast to some prior studies, our study did not find a significant association between COVID-19 infection and cognitive decline. This aligns with a study that examined older adults across the spectrum of AD, where no differences were observed in cognitive trajectories before and after COVID-19 infection.27 However, other studies have reported that COVID-19 infection accelerated cognitive decline in patients with preexisting dementia.13 The discrepancy may stem from differences in clinical conditions between study populations. In our cohort, 95.5% of our participants lived at home, whereas in prior studies, up to 27% of patients were institutionalized post-infection, suggesting that disease severity and living environment may moderate the impact of COVID-19 on cognition.

Severe COVID-19 has been consistently associated with greater and more persistent cognitive decline, whereas mild or nonsevere infections often lead to transient impairment. Large cohort studies of older adults have shown that cognitive deficits following nonsevere infection typically resolve within several months, while severe cases may exhibit deterioration lasting up to a year.28–30 Neuroimaging findings support this pattern: frontoparietal and temporal hypometabolism observed after acute infection tends to improve over time, paralleling recovery in cognitive scores.31 In our cohort, patients who required COVID-19-specific treatment, with a trend toward having greater symptom severity, demonstrated more pronounced declines in CDR-SB and CASI, whereas those receiving only symptomatic treatment exhibited no significant decline. These findings, in combination with prior evidence, suggest that illness severity may play a more important role than infection status alone.7,32

Education also emerged as an important factor in our cohort, with higher educational attainment associated with higher MMSE scores but not with a significantly different rate of cognitive decline over time. (An education × time interaction was tested but was not significant (p = 0.613) and was therefore excluded from the final model.) Although one study of hospitalized COVID-19 patients reported that lower education was linked to poorer cognitive outcomes at 6 months,33 the broader literature offers mixed conclusions regarding whether education modifies the trajectory of dementia progression. Some studies suggest that higher education accelerates decline due to greater cognitive reserve masking earlier disease,34–36 whereas others argue that education influences baseline performance rather than the rate of deterioration.37,38 Our findings align more with the latter interpretation, but further studies are needed to clarify how education interacts with post-COVID cognitive trajectories in dementia.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, regarding the study design, its observational nature precludes causal inference, and reverse causality cannot be entirely excluded. Despite comparable baseline characteristics and rigorous sensitivity analyses, residual confounding remains a possibility. Second, the relatively small sample size limited our statistical power. A post hoc power calculation yielded a value of 0.47, suggesting that findings regarding COVID-19 status should be viewed as exploratory. Nevertheless, our LMM retained sufficient sensitivity to detect established predictors of cognitive decline, such as education. Third, potential biases must be acknowledged. Attrition bias is a concern, as all participants lost to follow-up belonged to the COVID-19 positive group. If accelerated cognitive or physical decline post-infection contributed to their dropout, our results might underestimate the true negative impact of COVID-19 on dementia progression. Additionally, the lack of mandatory universal testing may have led to undetected asymptomatic cases in the control group; such potential nondifferential misclassification could blur the distinction between groups and bias the results toward the null. Finally, limitations regarding the temporal scope and generalizability must be addressed. Although the mean interval between infection and cognitive assessment extended beyond the acute post-infection period, the 1-year follow-up may have been insufficient to capture delayed neurodegenerative effects. Furthermore, the single-center and outpatient origin of the cohort limits patient diversity. However, this design effectively minimized assessment heterogeneity, which is crucial for detecting subtle clinical associations. We therefore consider our findings to be a robust preliminary step. Future multicenter studies utilizing larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods, repeated cognitive assessments, and neuroimaging biomarkers are warranted to elucidate the long-term impact of COVID-19 fully.

In conclusion, COVID-19 infection was not associated with accelerated cognitive decline over approximately 1 year. Higher education was associated with higher MMSE scores rather than a slower rate of decline. To overcome the current methodological constraints, future large-scale, longitudinal studies are warranted to fully delineate the long-term neurocognitive sequelae of COVID-19 and the impact of infection severity in this vulnerable population.
