A Randomised Approach to Distributed Sorting
Sam Olesker-Taylor

Abstract
We introduce and analyse a new, extremely simple, randomised sorting algorithm: ⋅ \bm{\cdot} choose a pair of indices { i , j } \{i,j\} according to some distribution q q ; ⋅ \bm{\cdot} sort the elements in positions i i and j j of the array in ascending order. We prove that taking q { i , j } ∝ 1 / | j − i | q_{\{i,j\}}\propto 1/|j-i| leads to an order- n ​ ( log ⁡ n ) 2 n(\log n)^{2} sorting time. The sorter trivially parallelises in the asynchronous setting, yielding a linear speed-up. We also exhibit a low-communication, synchronous version with a linear speed-up. We compare and contrast with other sorters, and discuss some of its benefits, particularly its robustness and amenability to parallelisation and distributed computing.

1  Introduction

1.1  Overview

This paper investigates
randomised sorting algorithms
based on weighted graphs.

Sorting algorithms lie and the core of computer science, underpinning a vast range of applications, from database management to scientific computing.
Classical sorting methods are less suitable in the era of ‘big data’, even if they have optimal sequential complexity.
They need to be robust, scalable and amenable to both parallelisation and distribution.

This paper introduces a novel class of randomised sorting algorithms, leveraging graph-based probabilistic mechanisms to achieve efficient sorting with these properties.

The study of sorting networks provides a foundational perspective on parallel and distributed sorting, with origins tracing back to Batcher’s bitonic sorting network
[
Bat68
]
.
Sorters such as bitonic
[
Bat68
]
or parallel MergeSort
[
Col88
]
require
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
parallel steps for merging, leading to a total of
\mathcal{O}((\log n)^{2})
steps.
A theoretical breakthrough came when
[
AKS83
,
AKS83a
]
gave a depth-
\Theta(\log n)
sorting network—itself a randomised construction.
Practically,
n\geq 2^{75}
is needed for AKS to beat bitonic, though.

Deterministic sorting networks like bitonic and odd–even MergeSort have been widely implemented in hardware due to their predictable structure.
However, they require precise coordination across nodes, and are susceptible to worst-case inputs, limiting their utility in larger-scale or adversarial environments.

Randomisation can be added to address some of these challenges. A prominent example is randomised QuickSort: it achieves
\Theta(n\log n)
complexity for all inputs, avoiding the worst-case
\Theta(n^{2})
for deterministic QuickSort.
Another example is random sampling in MergeSort.

The introduction of
randomised
sorting networks significantly broadened the scope of parallel sorting techniques.

[
Ang+07
]
analysed the probabilistic behaviour of uniformly random sorting networks.
More recently, work has focused on extending these principles to distributed and fault-tolerating settings—settings in which our particular proposal excels.

[
GSS23
]
achieved robust sorting in dynamic, multi-agent systems with the use of randomised algorithms by introducing introduced self-replicating population protocols.

[
Ajt+16
,
Ajt+09
]
address sorting with adversarial comparisons; in particular, they show a lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1+\varepsilon})
for deterministic algorithms.

[
Tre24
]
builds on this work, deriving a randomised sorting algorithm needing
\mathcal{O}(n(\log n)^{2})
comparisons, thus outperforming deterministic counterparts.

Randomisation has been key in addressing scalability in distributed settings.
Eg, parallel MergeSort
[
Col88
]
uses random sampling to optimise performance in shared-memory architectures,
and randomised load-balancing techniques have been applied
[
AHS94
]
.

Building on this body of work, the algorithm we present in this paper selects a pair
\{i,j\}
(with
i\neq j
) according to some distribution
q
and sorts the entries in positions
i
and
j
.
The key is to choose
q
, and then runtime
T
, smartly:
we simply require

1/q_{i,j}=\mathcal{O}(|i-j|)\mathchoice{\quad\text{and}\quad}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}T=\Theta\bigl(n(\log n)^{2}\bigr).

We also provide two parallel implementations, yielding a linear speed-up for up to
n/4
cores.

The approach has several key benefits, which we outline very briefly here.

Simplicity.

The algorithm is extremely easy to implement, including in distributed settings.

Robustness and Fault Tolerance.

Randomisation makes it naturally resilient to adversarial inputs.
It is also highly tolerant to faults, unlikely highly structured approaches, in which errors propagate through recursive stages.

Scalability and Parellisation.

Each comparison operates independently.
This makes it highly compatible with parallel and distributed systems,
whether asynchronous or not.

Load Balancing.

Each element in the array is inspected roughly the same number of times.

A more detailed comparison of sorting algorithms is given in §
1.3
.

1.2  Set-Up and Main Results

For clarity, when we talk about the complexity of a
randomised
algorithm, we mean that the bound holds both in expectation and with polynomially high probability in the length
n
.

We start by defining the framework of
graph-based randomised sorters
.

Definition 1.1.

Let
V
be a finite set with an implicit total ordering.
Let
G=(V,w)
be a weighted graph; here,
w:\{\{i,j\}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}i,j\in V,i\neq j\}\to[0,\infty)
.
We write
E\coloneqq\{e\mid w(e)\neq 0\}
for the set of (non-null) edges, and

when

.

A single step of the
randomised sorter
chooses an edge
e\in E
according to
w

e\in E
is chosen with probability
w(e)/\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E}\displaystyle}{\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E}}{\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E}}{\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E}}w(e^{\prime})

More precisely, suppose that

with
i<j
is chosen in a given step:

\bm{\cdot}

swap the cards if the label of the card in position

is larger than that in position
j
;

\bm{\cdot}

otherwise, do nothing, leaving the cards in ascending order.

Remark 1.2.

This graph-based randomised sorter is robust to mistakes.
Suppose that the sorting mechanism may be faulty:
it correctly sorts pair
(i,j)
with probability
p_{i,j}\geq p
;
otherwise, it does nothing—it never ‘unsorts’ a pair.
All our proofs apply to this model, and the same bounds hold except with the runtimes multiplied by
1/p
.
∎

We start by analysing the most basic procedure: choose a pair uniformly at random.

Theorem 1.3.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A single step of the
uniform sorter
chooses
i,j\in[n]
uniformly at random without replacement and sorts the cards at
\{i,j\}
.

The uniform sorter
requires order
n^{2}\log n
comparisons to sort a length-
n
list in the worst case,
both in expectation and with polynomially small-failure probability.

This corresponds to the complete graph,
which is one extreme.
The other is the path.

Theorem 1.4
(
[
GSS23
, Theorem 12]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A single step of the
adjacent sorter
chooses
k\in[n-1]
uniformly at random and sorts the cards at
\{k,k+1\}
.

The adjacent sorter
requires order
n^{2}
comparisons to sort a length-
n

both in expectation and with polynomially small-failure probability.

Both the complete graph and path are very slow,
but for different reasons.

Path.

Each edge is chosen at rate
1/(n-1)\approx 1/n
; this is as fast as possible since the graph must be connected, so
|E|\geq n-1
.
However, a single card is only ever moved distance
1
, and thus may need to be touched
n-1\approx n
times to reach its target position.

Complete Graph.

On the other hand, in the complete graph, regardless of a card’s current position, there is an edge connecting it to its target position.
However, getting closer to the target does not help: the required edges is always chosen at rate
1/\binom{n}{2}=\Theta(1/n^{2})
.

In short, the complete graph has long-range sorting, whilst the path only does short-range.

We desire a graph that handles
all
scales simultaneously to get
n\poly\log n
comparisons.

Theorem 1.5.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The
harmonic sorter
is the randomised sorter
with

V\coloneqq[n]\mathchoice{\quad\text{and}\quad}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}w(\{i,j\})\coloneqq 1/|j-i|\mathchoice{\quad\text{for}\quad}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}i,j\in[n]\text{ with }i\neq j.

The harmonic sorter
requires order
n(\log n)^{2}
comparisons to sort a length-
n
list in the worst case,
both in expectation and with polynomially small failure probability.

Remark 1.6.

Detailed intuition for this construction is given in §
2
.
In short,

\log 2\leq w\bigl(0,[2^{k-1},2^{k})\bigr)\leq 1\mathchoice{\quad\text{for all}\quad}{\text{ for all }}{\text{ for all }}{\text{ for all }}k\in\{1,...,\lfloor\lg n\rfloor\},\mathchoice{\quad\text{where}\quad}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}\lg n\coloneqq\log_{2}n.

So, if the scales grow exponentially, it is equally fast to compare
0
with any scale.
Roughly, this allows the sorter to
approximately halve

get most of the cards with label
\ell<n/2
to a position
i<n/2
and those with label
k\geq n/2
to a position
j\geq n/2
, in order
w(E)=\Theta(n\log n)
comparisons.
If the halving were
perfect
, then we would simply iterate

It is not perfect, but this gives some intuition for the order
n(\log n)^{2}
sorting time.
∎

We now explain how to implement these algorithms in a computational environment.

Remark 1.10.

Suppose there are
p=n/4

Sampling the matching in

requires a centralised controller to sample

,

This sampling requires order

With just this information, and its own label, each node can easily determine which comparison it must undertake.
∎

Remark 1.11.

Suppose there are
p\leq n/4
computational nodes.
For
Theorem

1.9
,
each node samples an edge according
w
—ie,
\{i,j\}
is chosen with probability proportional to

—which requires order
\log n

The actual update takes place in two rounds.

1.

In the first round, each node places a mark on each endpoint of the edge it has selected.

2.

After this has been done, each node checks if was the only node to mark either endpoint of its edge.
If this is the case, then it sorts that pair; otherwise, it does nothing.

The key point is that, conditional on one node choosing
\{i,j\}

the chance that either

or
j
are chosen by other nodes is at most

In fact, our proof is more flexible than this, provided

when

is sorted, both locations
i
and
j
are locked until the sort action is complete;
any other computational node requesting the value at these locations must wait until the sort is complete.
This way, all the
p
pairs can be sorted—even those intersecting each other.
This (stochastically) reduces the number of rounds required, but atomic actions are slower.
∎

As explained in §

This is still straightforward to sample from, and yields a simple parallelisation. But, it would be nice to be able to analyse the hypercube sorter too.
It parallelises particularly easily, too:

1.3  Comparison of Sorting Algorithms

There is more literature on sorting than can be repeated here, or even in a dedicated survey.
We focus on benefits of randomisation in general, then on those particular to
our
algorithm.

Randomised vs Deterministic

We highlight several advantages of randomised sorters.

Robustness Against Adversaries.

Randomised algorithms ensure robustness by relying on unpredictable choices which an adversary cannot anticipate or exploit.

Resource Optimisation.

Randomness is are often amenable to distributed computing set-ups,
and can help balance load across computation nodes in such cases; eg,
choosing pivot/division points in QuickSort/MergeSort
or
randomising the gap in ShellSort.

Practical Performance.

Randomised algorithms often outperform deterministic ones in real-world scenarios due to their average performance typically being sufficient.
Eg, randomised QuickSort typically outperforms deterministic MergeSort
[
Ski20
]
.

Benefits of Our Randomised Sorting Algorithm

See
Table

1.1
for a summary.

Simplicity of Implementation.

The sorting procedure is extremely simple.
No recursive structure, or maintaining of auxiliary data structures (eg, heaps or merge trees), is needed.

No Synchronisation.

Many algorithms, such as QuickSort or MergeSort, and particularly bitonic sort,
require explicit synchronisation or coordination;
eg, “Sort these subarrays, then merge.”
Contrastingly, every step of our algorithm has the same description.

Distributed-System Compatibility.

Structured algorithms often require frequent exchange of data, possibly with auxiliary data structures, which is expensive in a distributed system; eg, merging subarrays.
In our algorithm, nodes operate almost independently.

Natural Load Balancing.

If the weighted graph is close to regular, then every element is inspected roughly the same number of times. So, the load is automatically balanced.

Fault Tolerance.

The algorithm does not need to know if a given sorting step was successful or not, only the probability of being successful; see
Remark

1.2
.
Contrast this with MergeSort or bitonic, in which errors in sorting subarrays propagate.

2  Outline

We outline the key steps in the proof.
First, we reduce to a simpler problem; see §
3
.

Reduction.

The 0–1 principle, adjusted to randomised sorting networks, can be used to reduce to handling only 0–1-valued sequences.
By padding the start/end of the sequence with 0-s/1-s, respectively, we may assume that the 0–1 sequence has length a power of
2
and is
balanced
—ie, has the same number of 0-s as 1-s.
We assume this from now on.
Sorting such a sequence requires moving all the 0-s to the back half and the 0-s to the front half.
∎

We now turn to the task of moving the 0-s and 1-s to their respective target halves.

Uniform Sorter.

For the uniform sorter, we do this directly, in essence in a single argument.
For every 0 in the back half—ie, with position
i\geq n/2
—there is 1 in the front half—ie, with position
j<n/2
.
Let
M_{t}
denote the number of these
misplaced
0-s (or 1-s) at time
t
.

For every misplaced 0, it needs to be swapped with a misplaced 1.
Hence, there are precisely
M_{t}^{2}
pairs that need swapping.
So, in a single step,
M_{t}\to M_{t}-1
with probability
M_{t}^{2}/\binom{n}{2}
, remaining unchanged otherwise.
The sorting time is precisely
\inf\{t\geq 0\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}M_{t}=0\}
.

By the summability of
\sum_{m\geq 1}1/m^{2}
, this has expectation order
n^{2}
.
However, we need a polynomially small failure probability, which requires an extra
\log n
factor.
∎

The harmonic sorter is significantly more challenging to handle.
First, we give intuition.

Intuition.

We have seen that both the path and complete graphs give rise to slow sorting. However, this is for fundamentally different reasons:
the complete graph performs long-range, approximate sorting well,
but its short-range sorting is slow;
the path performs short-range sorting well,
but has no long-range edges.
We desire a graph which sorts well at all scales.

A candidate is the hypercube
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
, with vertices enumerated by Gray codes, rather than the usual binary (ie, lexicographic) ordering.
Briefly, the
N
-th Gray code
\mathsf{Gray}_{N}
is defined by concatenating two copies of the (
N-1
)-th Gray code:
the first is prepended with a
0

and
the second with a
1
, and also reversed.
Concretely,

\mathsf{Gray}_{0}
is the empty sequence
\emptyset
,

\mathsf{Gray}_{1}=(0,1)
,

\mathsf{Gray}_{2}=(00,01,11,10)

and

\mathsf{Gray}_{3}=(000,001,011,010,110,111,1001,100)
.

In particular, the first vertex
0...0
connects to the
last
vertex of every sub-dimension: eg, for
N=4
, the 0-th vertex
0=0000
connects to
1=0001
,
3=0010
,
7=0100
and
15=1000
.
The long-range edges allow the sorter to get the card into approximately the right location, before the short-range edges take over to do the final local corrections.

Each vertex has degree
N=\lg n
, so the total number of edges is
\tfrac{1}{2}n\lg n
. In the unweighted case (ie,
w(e)=1
for all edges
e
), this means each edge is picked at rate
2/(n\log n)
.

Altogether, this makes it a good candidate for
n\poly n
sorting.
However, the rigid structure of the hypercube can make it hard to work with: a vertex in the first half (of the form
0\star
) connects to a
specific
vertex in the second half.
The harmonic sorter is a relaxation of this, in some sense:
vertex
0...0
connects to
all
vertices in the second half,
spreading its weight roughly equally amongst all
n/2
,
and similarly for the other sub-cubes.
∎

Next, we explain how to use this mixture of long- and short-range edges to sort.
It is convenient to rescale the labels and positions by
n
, so that both lie in
[0,1)
rather than
[n]
.

Using Long- and Short-Range Edges Iteratively.

The strategy is not to move a misplaced 0—ie, one in
[\tfrac{1}{2},1)
—
directly
into
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.
Indeed, the long-range edges have low weight, so this swap may have probability order
1/(n^{2}\log n)
, eg if the current state is
10...01...10
.

Instead, the misplaced 0-s/1-s are iteratively moved closer and closer to
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
/
[\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, respectively,
roughly
having their distance away in each iteration.
Eg, for
10...01...10
, it is easy to move the misplaced 0 (at the end)
close
to the front half.
As the misplaced labels get closer to their target half, the number of labels they can swap with gets smaller, but this is balanced (perfectly) by the increasing of the weight of the shortening edges.
∎

Next, we describe a slightly naive approach, yielding an order
n(\log n)^{3}
bound;
see §
5.2
.

Naive: Order
n(\log n)^{3}
.

We first ‘clean’ the
entirety
of
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})
/
[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
of 1-s/0-s, respectively.
Once
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})
/
[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
consists of no 1-s/0-s, respectively, no comparison involving
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
can ever cause a swap.
So, it remains to sort the ‘inner’ half of the sequence.
Sorting this is done analogously:
first, clean
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{8})
and
[\tfrac{5}{8},\tfrac{3}{4})
,
then the inner quarter remains.
This must be iterated
\lg n
times, after which point the full sequence is sorted.
The ‘scale-invariance’ highlighted in
Remark

1.6
means each iteration takes roughly the same length of time.

In a single step, a given misplaced 0 is moved out of
[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
with probability order
1/(n\log n)
.
This
looks like
it leads to an order
n\log n
‘cleaning’ time for
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
; this would lead to an order
n(\log n)^{2}
sorting time.
However, a coupon-collector argument means an extra
\log n
factor is needed to
completely
clean
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
.
∎

Finally, we refine this argument to get the true order
n(\log n)^{2}
bound;
see §
5.3
.

Refined: Order
n(\log n)^{2}
.

The previous approach was an iterative halver.
However, do we really need the
entirety
of
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
to be clean before we start cleaning
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{8})\cup[\tfrac{5}{8},\tfrac{3}{4})
?

The answer is clearly “no”.
Instead, we wait until
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
is 99% clean—which does not require an extra
\log n
factor—before starting on
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{8})\cup[\tfrac{5}{8},\tfrac{3}{4})
.
The key challenge now is to argue that this remaining ‘dirty’ 1%
does not overly impact the future cleaning.
Arguing this carefully is technically challenging.
This gives the desired order
n(\log n)^{2}
bound.
∎

It remains to discuss the parallel implementations.

Parallel Implementations.

Given a collection of
vertex-disjoint
edges (ie, a matching) to be applied, the order in which the edge-sortings are applied is irrelevant.
So, a (parallel) matching-based implementation can still be viewed as an ordered sequence of comparisons.

The difficulty in viewing such ‘parallel’ Markov chains in this ‘sequential’ manner is that the ‘sequential chain’ is no longer Markovian: the choice of edge in each step is not independent.
Nevertheless, our proof is highly robust to these changes.
All that really matters is that the probability that pair
\{i,j\}
is chosen as part of a matching behaves like
1/|j-i|
.

The reason for this is the way the technical aspect of the ‘sequential’ proof works.
Very roughly, there arise certain sums of random variables which are independent in the sequential case, but not in the parallel framework.
However, the proof only relies on their expectation (and tail via Markov’s inequality).
So, the lack of independence does not matter.
∎

We make two final comments on the nature of the proofs, before proceeding formally.

Continuous-Time.

The ‘sequential’ proofs use the analogous
continuous-time
process:

\bm{\cdot}

compare, and sort, the endpoints of each edge
e\in E
at rate
w(e)
;

\bm{\cdot}

in particular,
some
comparison is undertaken at rate
w(E)
.

It is straightforward to convert a continuous-time result into an analogous discrete-time one,
using Poisson concentration,
since the claimed sorting times are of the form
\Theta(w(E)\log n)
.

For clarity, we exclusively use
time
to refer to units of continuous time; eg, “in time
T
” means
T
units of continuous time, in which (roughly)
Tw(E)
comparisons are made.

Using continuous time makes the robustness in
Remark

1.2
clear:
proposing
\{i,j\}
at rate
w(\{i,j\})
but only accepting with probability

is
identical
to
proposing at rate
p_{i,j}w(\{i,j\})
and always accepting.
Moreover, our proofs only rely on
lower
bounds on the rates.
∎

Space Rescaling.

For simplicity, we rescale the labels and locations by a factor
n
,

\text{from}\quad[n]=\{0,...,n-1\}\mathchoice{\quad\text{to}\quad}{\text{ to }}{\text{ to }}{\text{ to }}[0,1)_{n}\coloneqq\{0,1/n,...,1-1/n\}\subseteq[0,1).\qed

A reader who is comfortable with the set-up may safely skip to the harmonic sorter in §
5
:

\bm{\cdot}

the only result used from §

is that only balanced, 0–1 strings need be considered;

\bm{\cdot}

the analysis of the uniform sorter in §

is not referenced, serving more as a warm-up.

This completes the outline.
The remainder of the paper consists of the formal proofs.

3  Reduction

The purpose of this section is to prove the following simplification.
Once we get to the proofs of the main theorems (in §
4
and §
5
), we assume this simplification, without comment.

Reduction 3.1.

To establish the claimed bounds on the random sorting times,
we may assume the following,
provided the failure probability established is at most
1/n^{2}
.

\bm{\cdot}

The sequences have length which is a power of
2
.

\bm{\cdot}

The sequences are 0–1 valued, with half the entries 0 and half 1.

The 0–1 principle
states that a sorting network correctly sorts all lists of a given length if and only if it sorts all such 0–1-valued lists.
Our sorters are
probabilistic
, though: there is no grand coupling such that
all
length-
n
lists are sorted by
the same
sequence of comparisons.
The 0–1 principle extends to this set-up, at the cost of a factor
n
to the failure probability.

Proposition 3.2.

Let

and

\bm{\cdot}

Proof.

Each

The converse is a simple inductive argument.
We prove it in the case that all entries of

Let

Regarding the probabilistic aspect, if

We now need only sort 0–1 sequences.

Lemma 3.4.

Let

We prove the theorems by showing that any 0–1 length-

\bm{\cdot}

\bm{\cdot}

Since

For the remainder of the paper, we assume that the sequence

4  Uniform Sorter

In this section, we analyse the
uniform sorter
which chooses a pair of distinct locations

and
j
uniformly at random and relatively sorts the cards at these positions.
This corresponds to letting

be the unweighted (ie,

) complete graph in

.
In particular, note that
w(E)=\binom{n}{2}\asymp n^{2}
.
So,
n^{2}\log n

comparisons

.

This section should be seen as a warm-up for the main event (namely, the harmonic sorter), helping the reader get familiar with the type of continuous-time arguments used.
It is safe for a confident reader to skip the section entirely, if desired.

Theorem 4.1
(
Theorem

1.3
)
.

The uniform sorter
requires order
n^{2}\log n
comparisons to sort any length-
n
list,
both in expectation and with polynomially high probability.

The lower bound is short and simple, so we give it straight away.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
: Lower Bound.

Start from permutation
x=(2,1,4,3,6,5,...)
.
Then, the only pairs
(i,j)
which have any effect are of the form
(2k-1,2k)
. Once each such pair has been applied (at least) once, the deck is sorted.
Each is selected at rate
1
, so it takes time order
\log n
to select all
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
such pairs, by the standard coupon-collector argument.
∎

We use continuous time to prove the upper bound:
each pair
(i,j)\in[n]^{2}
with
i<j
is selected at rate
1
(simultaneously).
In this scaling, we must show an order-

Recall also that we are dealing only with 0–1 sequences with an equal number of 0-s and 1-s.

Definition 4.2.

For
t\geq 0
,
let

be the number of misplaced 0-s (or 1-s):

M_{t}\coloneqq\bigl|X_{t}^{0}\setminus[n/2]\bigr|=\bigl|X_{t}^{1}\setminus([n]\setminus[n/2])\bigr|

Observe that

is (deterministically) non-increasing under the dynamics: a 0 can only move to the left (or stay in place), so cannot move out of its target set
[n/2]
.
The strategy behind the proof is always to bound the rate at which the number
M_{t}
decreases.
The sorting time, which we denote
\tau_{\star}
, is precisely the first time at which

.

Proposition 4.3.

Let
E_{m}\sim\Exp(m^{2})
independently for

.
Then,

satisfies

\tau_{\star}\lesssim T_{\star}\coloneqq\mathchoice{\textstyle\sum_{m\geq 1}\displaystyle}{\sum_{m\geq 1}}{\sum_{m\geq 1}}{\sum_{m\geq 1}}E_{m}.

Proof.

Rather than drawing

(i,j)
, with

, we equivalently draw

(k,\ell)
, with
k<\ell
.
We assume that their positions satisfy
X_{t}(k)>X_{t}(\ell)
,
else no swap happens.

\bm{\cdot}

If

or

,
then the count is unaffected.

\bm{\cdot}

If
\ell\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
,
then both cards have labels not in
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

\bm{\cdot}

If
X_{t}(\ell)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
,
then both cards have positions not in
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

\bm{\cdot}

Else,
if
\ell\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
or

,
then the count is still unaffected.

\bm{\cdot}

If
X_{t}(k)<\tfrac{1}{2}
,
then both cards labelled
k
and

[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

\bm{\cdot}

If

and

,
and also

by the ‘else’,
then card

was not; the swap moves

\bm{\cdot}

Else,

, in which case the count decreases by

.

We now determine the rate at which the count

decreases by

Suppose that

\bm{\cdot}

\bm{\cdot}

.
The claimed stochastic domination follows easily from this and the non-increasing property of

∎

This dominates the sorting time of a
given

that is independent of

.
However, in order to sort

The reader is encouraged to recall the intuition for the harmonic sorter given in §

Definition 5.1.

Remark 5.2.

Let us compare the harmonic sorter with the Gray hypercube, described in §

\bm{\cdot}

5.1  Graph Preliminaries and Lower Bound

Proof of

: Lower Bound.

Consider sorting the ‘alternating’ sequence

The only comparison that can ever do anything is of the form

Moreover, the sequence is not sorted until

Each happens at rate

Recall that we only consider 0–1 sequences whose length is a power of

5.2  Approach 1: Time Order
(\lg n)^{2}

We first establish a bound on the sorting time of order
N^{2}=(\lg n)^{2}
.

Theorem 5.5.

The continuous-time harmonic sorter
requires time order
N^{2}
to sort any balanced, 0–1-valued list of length
2^{N}
,
both in expectation and with high probability.

Strategy.

The strategy is not to move a misplaced 0
directly
into
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.
Indeed, the long-range edges have low weight, so if there are only, say, 3 misplaced 0-s, then such a move would take
time
order
n
to happen, as the edge can have weight at most
8/n
.

Instead, the misplaced 0 should be moved closer and closer to
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
,
roughly
halving its distance in every step.
As it gets closer to
[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, the number of 1-s with which it can swap gets smaller, but this is balanced (perfectly) by the increased weight of the short-range edges.

More concretely, we first ‘clean’ the entirety of
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})
/
[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
of 1-s/0-s, respectively.
Once this is the case, no comparisons with one endpoint in
[0,\tfrac{1}{4})\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1)
ever leads to a swap, so it is just like sorting a half-length sequence.
We iterate, ‘cleaning’
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{8})
and
[\tfrac{5}{8},\tfrac{3}{4})

\bm{\cdot}

Now, we ask ourselves:

Recall the intervals defined in

Definition 5.11.

Let

Proposition 5.12.

5.4  Parallel Comparisons

We now consider a discrete-time, synchronised, parallel approach:
in each step, a disjoint collection
\{(i_{\ell},j_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
of pairs is chosen;
the labels at positions
i_{\ell}
and
j_{\ell}
are sorted, for each
k\in[L]

The number

Sorting a collection of pairs simultaneously breaks the independence between different pairs provided by the continuous-time nature of the previous proof.
We show below that the proof is actually very robust to this, and only minor adjustments are required to sort multiple pairs simultaneously, in discrete time.
All that really matters is that the probability that a particular pair
\{i,j\}
is chosen is lower bounded by a constant times

∎

Proposition 5.16.

and let

.

Proof.

A.2  Inversion-Based Proof

A simple argument, based on the number of inversions, was outlined by
[
Mur19
]
.
Whilst this does not give such intuition for how the sorter operates, the proof is short.

We fill in the details here, for completeness, but claim no originality for the idea.

Definition A.7.

Let
x
be a permutation on
[n]
.
Then, an
inversion
is a pair
(i,j)
with
i<j
and
x(i)>x(j)
.
Write
\inv x
for the number of inversions in
x
:

\inv x\coloneqq|\{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x(i)>x(j)\}|.

Trivially,
\inv x\leq n^{2}
.
The key is that a sorting step decreases the number of inversions.

Lemma A.8.

Let
x
be a permutation and
(i,j)
be an inversion for
x
:

i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
and
x(i)>x(j)
.
Let
x^{\prime}
denote the permutation resulting from sorting
i
and
j
:

x^{\prime}(i)\coloneqq x(i)\wedge x(j),\quad x^{\prime}(j)\coloneqq x(i)\vee x(j)\mathchoice{\quad\text{and}\quad}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}x^{\prime}(k)\coloneqq x(k)\mathchoice{\quad\text{for}\quad}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}{\text{ for }}k\notin\{i,j\}.

Then,

\inv x^{\prime}\leq\inv x-1.

Proof.

No new inversions are created, after relabelling,
and
(i,j)
is no longer one.
∎

It is simple to estimate how long it takes to sort, establishing
Theorem

1.3
.

Proposition A.9.

The process
(\inv X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of inversion counts is stochastically dominated by a process
(I_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
I_{0}\coloneqq\inv X_{0}
and
I_{t}\to I_{t}-1
at rate
I_{t}
.
In particular,

\mathbb{P}_{#1}[\tau_{\star}>n^{2}\log n+\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha n^{2}]\leq e^{-\alpha}\mathchoice{\quad\text{and}\quad}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}{\text{ and }}\mathbb{E}_{#1}[\tau_{\star}]=n^{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})\asymp n^{2}\log n,

where
\tau_{\star}
is the sorting time, as before.

Proof.

If the pair chosen does not form an inversion, then the comparison has no effect on the process; in particular, the number of inversions does not change.
If the pair does form an inversion, then the number of inversions decreases by at least
1
.
Each pair is chosen at rate
1
, hence the claimed stochastic domination holds.

We know by the coupon-collector problem that

\tau_{\star}\lesssim\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{n}{2}\displaystyle}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}\max\{E_{1},...,E_{n^{2}}\}\quad\text{in distribution}\mathchoice{\quad\text{where}\quad}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}{\text{ where }}E_{1},E_{2},...\sim^{\textsf{iid}}\Exp(1).

In particular,

\mathbb{P}_{#1}[\tau_{\star}/\mathchoice{\textstyle\binom{n}{2}\displaystyle}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}}>\log(n^{2})+\alpha]\leq n^{2}e^{-\log(n^{2})-\alpha}=e^{-\alpha}.

But, observe that
\binom{n}{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n^{2}
and
\log(n^{2})=2\log n
.
This completes the proof.
∎
