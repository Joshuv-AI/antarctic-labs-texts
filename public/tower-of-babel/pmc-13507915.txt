Self-Pay Prices and Financial Assistance Programs for Urology Appointments
Authors: Walter Hsiang, Darren Chau, Joy Barrett, Lindsay Van Winkle, Arnav Surpur, Angela Kim, Bao Nguyen, Patrick Hoang, Marshall Stoller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the self-pay appointment prices and availability of financial assistance programs at urology clinics nationwide?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 1150 urology clinics in the US, 75.2% provided a self-pay appointment price, with a national median price of $265. Price transparency was significantly lower in hospital-based and academic clinics than private practices, although hospital-based and academic clinics were significantly more likely to offer financial assistance.MeaningThese findings suggest that gaps in price transparency create financial uncertainty that may hinder access to urology care for self-pay patients.

Introduction

Ambulatory visits represent a frequent entry point for accessing urology care. For uninsured, underinsured, or out-of-network patients who must self-pay for outpatient visits, uncertainty regarding the expected charge for an appointment may present a meaningful barrier to timely evaluation and treatment.1 Although high price variation and limited price transparency for hospital-based services are well documented,2,3,4,5,6,7 no studies to our knowledge have characterized self-pay appointment prices in the ambulatory urologic setting.

Evidence from primary care illustrates the feasibility of measuring and characterizing self-pay prices at the time of appointment scheduling. In a multistate audit study,8 89.2% of primary care clinics provided self-pay appointment prices for uninsured adults, with an average quoted price of $157 for a new patient visit. A separate audit study similarly reported a mean price of $160 for a new patient visit.9 Notably, clinics that accepted Medicaid or were located in lower-income areas were associated with having lower self-pay prices. However, the self-pay price for a new outpatient urology appointment and the factors associated with those prices have not been characterized. Additionally, the availability and structure of ambulatory financial assistance programs, including discounts and payment plans, are undescribed in surgical specialties such as urology.

Policy efforts have sought to improve price transparency, particularly in the hospital setting. The Federal Hospital Price Transparency Rule, effective in 2021, mandates hospitals to disclose service-level prices, including discounted cash prices most relevant to self-pay patients.10 In urology, this mandate has enabled several studies3,4,5 of hospital-reported self-pay prices for different urologic procedures, including prostatectomy, vasectomy, and cystoscopy. However, disclosure of self-pay prices for these urologic services has remained limited, with only 16% to 35% of hospitals reporting self-pay prices.3,4,5 Broader studies6,11 outside urology suggest that only approximately half of hospitals comply with transparency requirements. Additionally, the Hospital Price Transparency Rule applies relatively modest fines on hospitals that do not report service-level prices, often rendering it ineffective. Importantly, independent physician practices are not subject to the Hospital Price Transparency Rule.10 Because 40% of urologists practice in private settings,12 many outpatient urology clinics are exempt from federal transparency requirements. Thus, self-pay prices for urology appointments remain ambiguous within the US.

In this nationwide audit study of urology clinics across all 50 states, we characterized and quantified self-pay appointment prices while adjusting for regional cost-of-living differences. Additionally, we characterized the availability and types of financial assistance programs, such as discounts and payment plans, offered by these clinics.

Methods

Study Design

This national cross-sectional study was conducted with institutional review board approval from the University of California, San Francisco, with exemption of informed consent for sampled clinics because knowledge of participation could have biased responses. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies. We used an audit study approach, a well-established method in health services research for characterizing how care is delivered in routine clinical settings.13 Six callers were trained using a standardized call script (eFigure in Supplement 1). Training was performed by one of the authors (W.H.), who has extensive experience performing audit studies. All callers completed a supervised, 2-week training period of practice calls that were excluded from the analysis. Callers contacted 1150 urology clinics from July 1 through August 31, 2025, while posing as a family member of an uninsured adult patient who needed a urologic consultation for a kidney stone. This clinical scenario was selected because it represents a common acute urologic condition requiring timely specialty evaluation. Clinics were asked to provide the self-pay price, defined as the out-of-pocket liability for a self-pay patient, for a new patient urology appointment. All questions were directed to front-desk personnel and/or the billing office. No actual patient identifiers were provided, and no actual appointments were scheduled. To minimize interference with clinic operations, calls were limited to 5 minutes.

Data Source

Clinic contact information was assembled from publicly available listings on Google Maps using the Octoparse web-scraping platform. This process used a standardized Google Maps template within Octoparse to search for urology clinic in each state, generating a nationwide directory of urology practices, including clinic name, telephone number, street address, zip code, and website when available. To ensure sampling completeness, we systematically supplemented automated extractions with manual statewide searches. Lastly, accuracy and validation were achieved through direct manual cross-verification against Google Maps, followed by deduplication and exclusion of urology subspecialty practices before random selection. We excluded urology subspecialty practices that did not provide general adult urologic care (eg, pediatric urology, men’s health–only clinics, and urogynecology). We also excluded clinics embedded in closed or membership-restricted systems (eg, Veterans Affairs facilities and certain health maintenance organizations, such as Kaiser and Geisinger) because appointment information typically requires patient identifiers or member numbers. After compiling the clinic list, we randomly sampled up to 30 clinics per state, consistent with prior audit-study methods.14,15 If a selected clinic’s telephone line was disconnected or unreachable, another clinic was randomly drawn as a replacement until either 30 clinics were successfully contacted in that state or all eligible clinics were exhausted. The number of clinics called per state are listed in the eTable in Supplement 1.

Outcomes and Variables

The primary outcome was the self-pay price for a new urologic consultation for kidney stones. We excluded the price of laboratory tests, imaging, or other ancillary services. All self-pay prices in this study were adjusted for the regional cost-of-living differences using the 2024 Regional Price Parity (RPP) index published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis.16 Secondary outcomes included the availability and characteristics of financial assistance programs, specifically discounts and payment plans. Discounts were categorized as flat rate (eg, 30% off), sliding scale based on financial need, and prompt pay (discount was offered if the patient paid the full price at the time of the appointment). Payment plans were categorized as short term (≤6 months), long term (>6 months), and sliding scale based on financial need.

To evaluate clinic- and area-level factors associated with self-pay prices and financial assistance availability, we collected prespecified clinic characteristics: practice type (private, hospital based, or academic), location in a Medicaid expansion state, clinic-reported Medicaid acceptance, rural or urban designation, and the median household income of the clinic’s zip code. Practice setting was determined from organizational affiliations listed on clinic websites and categorized as follows: (1) private practice, defined as an independent solo or group practice not owned by or integrated within a hospital, health system, or academic system; (2) hospital based, defined as a practice owned by or employing surgeons within a nonacademic hospital or health network; and (3) academic, defined as a clinic operating within an academic or university-affiliated medical center. Clinics were classified according to whether their state had expanded Medicaid under the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act as of July 2025.17 Medicaid acceptance was assessed directly during the call by asking whether the clinic accepted Medicaid patients. Rural or urban designation was assigned by linking each clinic’s zip code to the corresponding 2020 Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code from the US Department of Agriculture.18 RUCA categories were grouped as metropolitan (urban areas of ≥50 000 residents), micropolitan (10 000-49 999 residents), and rural (≤9999 residents). Median household income was obtained by linking clinic zip codes to estimates from the 2024 American Community Survey.19

Statistical Analysis

Self-pay prices were normalized using the 2024 RPP index to account for geographic economic variance.16 Specifically, clinic-reported self-pay prices were divided by their respective state-level RPP percentages. Categorical data are reported as percentages and compared using the Pearson χ2 test. Nonparametric continuous data are reported as medians with IQRs and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for independent samples. The following independent variables were included a priori in the multivariable models: practice type, Medicaid expansion status, clinic-reported Medicaid acceptance, rural or urban designation, and median zip code income (scaled to $10 000 increments). We used multivariable linear regression to identify factors associated with self-pay prices and multivariable logistic regression to evaluate factors associated with the availability of self-pay prices and financial assistance programs. We used Huber-White robust SEs, akin to multilevel modeling, clustered at the state level for all multivariable regression analyses to account for the hierarchical nature of the data and potential correlation among clinics within the same state. This approach also relaxes the assumption of independence among observations within a state and provides more conservative variance estimates. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata, version 19.0 (StataCorp), with a 2-sided significance threshold of P < .05.

Availability of Financial Assistance Programs

Of the 1150 urology clinics contacted, 752 (65.4%) demonstrated awareness of internal financial assistance programs, including discounts and structured payment plans. Of these clinics, 484 (64.4%) offered any financial assistance programs, including discount only (71 [9.4%]), payment plan only (225 [30.0%]), or both (188 [25.0%]).

Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for state-level clustering evaluated factors associated with the availability of financial assistance programs (Table 4). Compared with private practices, hospital-based (OR, 2.64; 95% CI, 1.57-4.44; P < .001) and academic-based (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.01–2.69, P = .04) clinics were associated with higher odds of offering financial assistance. Furthermore, clinics that accepted Medicaid were associated with higher odds of offering financial assistance programs compared with clinics that did not accept Medicaid (OR, 3.17; 95% CI, 1.84-5.45; P < .001). Rural or urban designation, state Medicaid expansion status, and median income level were not associated with financial assistance availability.

Types of Financial Assistance Programs

Of the 272 clinics offering discounts, the most common discount structure was 30% or less off (78 [28.7%]) followed by 31% to 50% off (49 [18.0%]). Prompt-pay discounts (26 [9.6%]), discounts exceeding more than 50% (24 [8.8%]), and sliding-scale discounts (19 [7.0%]) were less common. Among the 413 clinics offering payment plans, sliding-scale structures were the most common (48 [11.6%]), whereas short-term (≤6 months) and long-term (>6 months) plans accounted for only 27 (6.5%) and 25 (6.1%) clinics, respectively. Among clinics that offered financial assistance programs, 76 (27.9%) could not provide specific discount details, and 313 (75.8%) could not provide specific payment plans terms.

Discussion

In this nationwide cross-sectional study of 1150 urology clinics across all 50 states, 865 practices (75.2%) provided a self-pay appointment price, with a national median (IQR) of $265 ($208-$361). These self-pay prices represent a substantial financial burden for uninsured patients and may act as a barrier to urologic care. For a single person at the federal poverty line, a $265 medical payment would consume approximately one-fifth of their total monthly income.20 Our study also demonstrates substantial variation in self-pay prices, a notable finding because patients managed by lower-priced physicians typically incur lower longitudinal health care costs than those treated by higher-priced physicians.21

The association between Medicaid expansion and higher self-pay appointment prices may reflect several underlying market dynamics. Institutional cost-shifting may occur because lower Medicaid reimbursement rates encourage clinics to increase self-pay prices to offset public-payer shortfalls.22 Medicaid expansion also increased the number of insured patients seeking ambulatory specialty care, potentially increasing demand for specialists.23 Increased demand may place upward pressure on appointment prices. Lastly, Medicaid expansion states may differ fundamentally from nonexpansion states in economic characteristics, including costlier commercial insurance reimbursement benchmarks or wealthier patient populations.24 Given the low R2 of the pricing model, many additional unmeasured market factors likely contribute to price variation.

Our findings reveal an important inversion across practice settings. Academic and hospital-based clinics were associated with lower odds of providing upfront price information and higher odds of offering financial assistance programs compared with private practices. This pattern suggests that institutional health systems may prioritize mission-driven financial safety nets over consumer-facing price transparency. The complexity of decentralized billing departments, intricate billing workflows, and bureaucratic friction may prevent front-end and billing staff from quoting upfront prices in academic and hospital settings. This finding aligns with previous studies6,11 demonstrating incomplete price disclosure despite hospital transparency requirements. In contrast, independent private practices may more readily provide standard price estimates but may lack the resources or institutional infrastructure necessary to support extensive financial assistance programs.

Financial assistance was not consistently communicated. Approximately two-thirds of clinics were aware of their discount and payment plan policies; among those, 484 (64.4%) reported offering financial assistance. However, specific details of such programs remained opaque, with more than one-quarter of discount programs and three-quarters of payment plan structures lacking clearly defined terms. This ambiguity may reflect limited administrative training for front-line and/or billing staff regarding specific billing protocols or that prices for self-pay patients rely on case-by-case negotiations. Regardless, patients who are told that assistance is available but cannot obtain information about eligibility or program terms remain unable to estimate their expected financial responsibility before a consultation.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, as an audit study relying on simulated patient calls, our data reflect the immediate knowledge of front-desk and billing personnel, which may not be up to date. Reported self-pay prices excluded ancillary services (eg, imaging and laboratory testing) and facility fees; consequently, final price estimates may differ from quoted prices after billing adjudication. Second, calls were limited to 5 minutes to avoid disrupting front office and billing staff. More time may have allowed additional clinics to provide pricing or financial assistance information. Third, self-pay prices and financial assistance terms may vary according to clinical visit complexity. Fourth, our sampling, based on publicly listed clinics and excluding closed systems, may not capture all practice settings. Practice type was also determined by affiliations listed on clinic websites. Therefore, possible misclassification may have occurred if organizational affiliations were not clearly disclosed online. Fifth, our sampling did not use population-based weighting, which leads to a relative overrepresentation of less populous states. Similarly, because patient volume varies across practice types, ranging from high-capacity academic centers to small private groups, our findings may disproportionately reflect the operational policies of smaller clinics rather than those serving a larger proportion of the patient population. Sixth, although self-pay prices were adjusted using state-level cost-of-living indexes, within-state price variation was not captured. Seventh, additional granular market factors, such as local labor costs, physician workforce density, and regional payer mix, were intentionally excluded from our pricing model because our objective was to characterize national rather than local pricing patterns. The low R2 of our pricing model suggests that many significant variables estimating price may not have been captured.

Conclusions

In this national cross-sectional study of 1150 urology clinics, approximately three-quarters of outpatient urology clinics provided self-pay appointment price estimates. Adjusting for cost of living, the median quoted self-pay price for a new appointment visit was $265. Hospital-based and academic clinics were associated with lower price transparency. These same clinics were also associated with greater odds of offering financial assistance programs. However, the widespread ambiguity regarding the specific terms of these financial assistance programs may limit their practical utility for self-pay patients attempting to navigate upfront health care costs. Improving front-end communication of self-pay prices and standardizing disclosure of prices and financial assistance programs may reduce financial uncertainty and facilitate more informed access to outpatient urologic care.
