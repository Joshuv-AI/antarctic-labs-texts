Isolation and transcriptional profiling of antigen-presenting cells from mouse lung and MLNs following intranasal immunization
Authors: Xiuyu Wang, Yihan Wang, Qiang Wang, Anita S. Chong, Shengbo Cao, Youhui Si
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDendritic cells (DCs) play a central role in the initiation of the adaptive immune response. Here, we present a protocol for isolating and transcriptionally profiling antigen-presenting cells (APCs) from the mouse lung and mediastinal lymph nodes (MLNs) following intranasal immunization. We describe steps for preparing single-cell suspensions from the lung and MLN, along with the detection and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of antigen-presenting DCs. This protocol offers a broadly applicable approach for identifying variations in DC subpopulations under diverse experimental conditions.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Youhui et al.1

Before you begin

Respiratory pathogens continue to pose a significant threat to global health, contributing prominently to mortality worldwide. Acting as a link between innate immunity and adaptive immunity, dendritic cells (DCs) play a crucial role in the generation of lung immunity.2,3,4 This protocol describes the analysis of DCs from the lung and mediastinal lymph nodes (MLN) following intranasal immunization with bioengineered nanofiber vaccines containing the MHC Class I-restricted epitope OVA257–264 (SIINFEKL) linked to self-assembling Q11 peptides (termed as SIIN-Q11).5,6 The subsequent steps provide a detailed procedure for intranasal immunization of mice with SIIN-Q11, the detection and sorting of SIINFEKL-presenting DCs, as well as sample preparation for RNA-Seq.

While this method is designed to investigate the involvement of distinct DC subsets in the response to respiratory antigens in vivo, slight modifications could also allow for isolation and analysis of the tissue migration of antigen-presenting cells from alternative sites, such as intestinal and mesenteric lymph nodes, in the context of infections with other pathogens.

Step-by-step method details

Part 1: intranasal immunization

Timing: 15 min

Timing: 15 min

These steps will guide you through the anesthesia procedure and intranasal delivery of SIIN-Q11. The provided time estimate is for inoculating one mouse intranasally. Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Harlan-Envigo Laboratories or Huazhong Agricultural University Laboratory Animal Centre. All animals are housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities within individually ventilated cage (IVC) systems, with continuous access to water and food. The ambient temperature is regulated to range between 22°C–25°C, with humidity levels are maintained at 60%–80%. The light-dark cycle consists of 10 h of light and 14 h of darkness.Note: Both male and female mice can be used.1.Anesthetize a single mouse intraperitoneally with 150 μL of a mixed anesthetic containing 0.075 g/mL ethyl urethane, 0.015 g/mL chloral hydrate, and 0.75 mg/mL xylazine, wait for 5–10 min.Note: Ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), administered intraperitoneally, can also be used to induce anesthesia in mice.2.Watch for breathing, as mice that are deeply anesthetized have slower breaths clearly visible by watching chest movement. Sacrifice the mice on the third day following immunization.CRITICAL: Anesthetic tends to cause hypothermia. Keep mice warm with heat lamp or gloves filled with warm water, watching their heartbeat until fully recovered from anesthesia.Note: Antigen-specific presenting cells in the MLNs are detectable from days 2–5 post-immunization. Cell isolation from MLN

18.Disrupt the membrane of MLN with 1 mL syringe needle, digest with collagenase for 30 min at 37°C.19.After digestion, gently pipette the digested MLN repeatedly to ensure dissociation of cells.20.Transfer the mixture into the flow tube by filtering it through the cell strainer. Rinse the cell strainer with 1 mL flow buffer.21.Wash twice with PBS.22.Resuspend the pellet with 1 mL flow buffer per MLN. CRITICAL: Each washing step may result in cell loss. Expected outcomes

This protocol provides a detailed protocol starting from mouse intranasal immunization, lung and MLN collection, preparation of single cell suspension, detection of antigen presentation, leading to sample preparation for RNA-Seq. By following this protocol, one can identify major types of DCs, such as antigen-non-presenting cDC1s, antigen-presenting cDC1s and different subtypes of cDC2s.

Limitations

While this protocol provides a method for isolating single cells from mouse lung and MLN, additional optimization may be necessary when working with other tissue types. Variables such as concentration of collagenase and duration of tissue digestion may need adjustment to achieve optimal cell yield and viability. The present strategy for detecting and identifying antigen-presenting cells via flow cytometry relies on the use of the 25-D1.16 monoclonal antibody, which specifically recognizes the ovalbumin-derived peptide SIINFEKL bound to MHC class I H-2Kb. Consequently, this method is limited to assessing the presentation of epitopes derived from the model protein OVA. Expanding this approach to evaluate antigen presentation of other proteins of interest would necessitate the generation of antibodies capable of recognizing the complex formed by peptide epitopes and MHC molecules, which can be time-consuming. Notably, we have also used the Y-Ae monoclonal antibody to detect pEα:I-Ab complexes; the pEα epitope is derived from the MHC class II I-Edα (α52–68).8 In transcriptome analysis of antigen-presenting cells, the number and viability of these cells are crucial considerations. However, the process, from tissue collection to sorting antigen-presenting cells, is time-consuming and yields of cells are relatively low. Therefore, operators must work with precision and efficiency to ensure the acquisition of high-quality RNA for sequencing analysis. This requirement adds complexity, extends the experimental timeline and limits the number of experimental groups and replicates in each group.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The stickiness of the nanofibers leads to a heightened risk of mouse mortality upon intranasal administration (Step 3).

Potential solution

Before intranasal administration, gently pipette the nanofibers several times to enhance their fluidity and prevent potential asphyxiation. For intranasal immunizations, it is advisable to administer nanofibers at a volume of less than 50 μL per mouse, ensuring a slow and controlled process. Higher volumes may increase the risk of mouse mortality. In addition, after immunization, monitor the breathing of mice, and if there is difficulty breathing, gently compress \ chest area of the mouse to help restore normal breathing.

Problem 2

The samples are prone to forming flocculent precipitates during the preparation of single-cell suspensions and staining, which can result in flow cytometer clogging during cytometry analysis (Steps 23–35).

Potential solution

The amount of DNase I used during digestion may be insufficient, and it may be necessary to increase the concentration of DNase I accordingly. Tissue homogenization and digestion should be performed gently. It is important to filter the single-cell suspensions prior to conducting flow cytometry analysis.

Problem 3

No SIINFEKL-presenting DCs were observed (Steps 35 and 41).

Potential solution

Use an unstained cell sample control to exclude interference from autofluorescence, especially crucial when dealing with lung samples. Ensure that compensation is accurately adjusted before conducting the flow cytometry analysis. Additionally, due to the weakening binding effectiveness of antibodies over time, it is advisable to redo the titration to determine the optimal antibody concentration. Prepare the fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls by omitting the 25-D1.16 antibody from the antibody cocktail to enhance the accuracy of discriminating SIINFEKL-presenting DCs.

Problem 4

The RNA quality is suboptimal and does not meet the quality control standards for RNA-Seq (Step 44).

Potential solution

Quickly remove the Lung/MLN samples and immediately place them in ice-cold flow buffer. Ensure that all samples are kept on ice at all times, and all reagents used are pre-incubated and maintained on ice. Be aware that higher temperatures, such as room temperature (RT), may reduce RNA quality. It is crucial to turn on the water recirculation unit to ensure that samples are maintained at 4°C during sorting. RNA-Seq for low cell numbers demands higher RNA quality, necessitating stringent handling procedures due to the limited number of sorted cells. These procedures should be executed rapidly and avoid processing an excessive number of samples simultaneously.
