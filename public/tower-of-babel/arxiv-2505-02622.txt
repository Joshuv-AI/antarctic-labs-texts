PLS-completeness of string permutations
Dominik Scheder, Johannes Tantow

Abstract
Bitstrings can be permuted via permutations and compared via the lexicographic order. In this paper we study the complexity of finding a minimum of a bitstring via given permutations. As a global optima is known to be NP -complete [ 1 ] , we study the local optima via the class PLS [ 8 ] and show hardness for PLS . Additionally, we show that even for one permutation the global optimization is NP -complete and give a formula that has these permutation as symmetries. This answers an open question inspired from Kołodziejczyk and Thapen [ 9 ] and stated at the SAT and interactions seminar in Dagstuhl [ 14 ] .

Introduction

Given a string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a couple of permutations in
S_{n}
,
we can apply a permutation to
\mathbf{x}
and obtain a new bit string. What is the lexicographically
smallest string we can obtain this way? This problem is known
[
1
]
to be
NP
-hard. What about
finding a local minimum, i.e., arriving at a bit string that cannot be further improved by
a single application of a permutation? In this paper, we show that this problem is PLS-complete.

To be more formal, we are given a string
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and permutations
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}
on the set

[n]
. When we view
\mathbf{x}
as a function
[n]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, the notation
\mathbf{x}\circ\pi
makes sense
and is the string obtained from
\mathbf{x}
by permuting the coordinates according to
\pi
. By

\langle\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}\rangle
we denote the subgroup of
S_{[n]}
generated by the
\pi_{i}
. The
problem
k
-Permutation Global Orbit Minimum
asks for the
\pi\in\langle\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\rangle
such that

\mathbf{x}\circ\pi
is lexicographically minimal. Babai and Luks
[
1
]
showed that this is
NP
-hard even for
k=2
.
In fact, we will see that it is
NP
-hard even for
k=1
, i.e., a single permutation.

k
-Permutation Local Orbit Minimum
asks for a local minimum.
That is, an element
\pi\in\langle\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\rangle
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\circ\pi\preceq_{\rm lex}\mathbf{x}\circ\pi\circ\pi_{i}\quad\textnormal{ for all $1\leq i\leq k$}\ ,

i.e., a single application of a permutation
\pi_{i}
cannot further improve the string
\mathbf{x}\circ\pi
.

A local optimum always exists and hence this is an instance of a total search problem. Total search problems where solutions are recognizable in polynomial time form the class
TFNP
.
Total search problems that can be stated as finding
a local optimum with respect to a certain

cost function
and a
neighborhood relation

constitute the subclass
PLS
(polynomial local search).
Known hard problems for
PLS
include finding pure a Nash-equilibrium in a congestion game
[
4
]
or finding a locally maximal cut (
LocalMaxCut
)
[
13
]
. Almost all known
PLS
-complete problems require quite involved cost functions. Our problem
k
-Permutation Local Orbit Minimum
has the benefit
of using the maybe simplest possible cost function -
the lexicographic ordering.
The only other
PLS
-complete problem using
a lexicographic cost function that we know of is

FLIP
, which asks to minimize the
m
-bit
output of a circuit
C
, where the solutions
are all
n
-bit inputs and the neighborhood relation
is defined by flipping a single bit.

Thus, our result unifies two desirable properties -
our
PLS
-complete problem is very combinatorial
in nature (in contrast to
FLIP
) and uses
a very simple cost function (in contrast to

LocalMaxCut
)

SAT Solving and Symmetry Breaking

When encoding a combinatorial problem as a CNF formula
F

(think of “is there a
k
-Ramsey graph on
n
vertices?”),
the formula will often contain many symmetries. To make the problem
easier for SAT solvers, one can take the statement

The satisfying assignment

should be a local lexicographical minimum
with respect to those symmetries,

encode it as a CNF formula
G
and feed
F\wedge G
to
the SAT solver. Clearly,
F\wedge G
is satisfiable
if and only if
F
is.
In case that
F\wedge G
is unsatisfiable,
SAT solvers are often expected to produce a proof of
unsatisfiability. A popular proof system used in this context is DRAT
[
15
]
.
However, it is not known to which extend DRAT can handle
symmetry breaking
[
7
]
, that is,
whether a short DRAT-refutation of
F\wedge G
can be transformed
into a short DRAT-refutation of
F
. In this context,
Thapen
[
14
]
asked whether there exists
a polynomial algorithm that, given a CNF formula
F
,
a handful of symmetries thereof, and a satisfying assignment
\alpha
,
finds a satisfying assignment
\beta
that is a local
lexicographical minimum with respect to those symmetries.
In this paper, we show that this problem is PLS-complete, which
is evidence that such a polynomial time algorith might not exist.

2.1
Total search problems and
PLS

The class
FNP
is the functional correspondent of
NP
.

TFNP

\subset

FNP
is the subset of
total

search problems, i.e., problems that always have a solution. As this is a semantic class,
TFNP
has no known complete problems, and thus it is usually studied via its subclasses. These subclasses are based of the combinatorial principle that proves the existence of a solution. These principles include the existence of sinks in directed acyclic graphs (
PLS
)
[
8
]
, the parity argument for directed and undirected graphs (
PPAD
,
PPA
) or the pigeonhole principle (

) (all introduced in
[
11
]
). Nonetheless, not all problems in

can be categorized in one of the known subclasses,

being a prime example.

By the above characterization,
PLS
requires finding a sink of a directed acyclic graph
G
. This would be possible in polynomial time
G
was given explicitly. Instead,
G
is always given implicitly via a circuit that computes the successor list of a given node. To make sure that
G
is acyclic,
we have a second circuit computing a topological ordering, that
is, a “cost” function that is strictly decreasing along
the edges. A solution to the problem is a sink of
G

or an edge
(u,v)
with
\textnormal{cost}(u)\leq\textnormal{cost}(v)
, i.e.,
violating the decreasing cost condition. This
guarantees totality of the problem.

An alternative definition is the following. For a
PLS
problem

we have a set of instances
I
. Each instance
i\in I
has a set of feasible solutions
S
(e.g. for the Euclidean traveling salesman problem the solutions are exactly
the Hamilton cycles of
K_{n}
). Additionally, we require the following polynomial-time computable algorithms (usually given as circuits):

1.

an algorithm that decides whether a given
s

is a feasible solution.

2.

an algorithm that computes a starting solution
s\in S

3.

an algorithm that computes for a feasible solution
s
the neighborhood
N(s)

4.

an algorithm that computes for a solution
s
the cost
\textnormal{cost}(s)

A feasible solution

is a

if

\textnormal{cost}(s)\leq\textnormal{cost}(s^{\prime})
for all
s^{\prime}\in N(S)
. The definition is given for a minimization problem, but can be also defined in terms of a maximization problem.

We can easily transform this into a directed acyclic graph by keeping only those neighbors in
N(s)
having strictly
smaller cost–or even keeping only the one neighbor

s^{\prime}\in N(s)
of minimal cost (breaking ties arbitrarily).

Similar to
NP
, there is also a hardness structure in

. Let
P
and
Q
be two problems in

and

. This was defined in

]
and the first natural

-bit strings, the cost is calculated by a given circuit

and the neighborhood are all

-bit strings with a Hamming distance of 1.

The obvious greedy algorithm to find a solution for a

-problem is to use the given algorithms to compute the start solution and always select the best neighbor until there is no better solution. This solution is called the

and the algorithm the

. For the problem

, finding the standard solution for a given start solution is

-complete
[

.

Reductions that preserve the

-completeness are called tight
[
13
]
. For this, we consider the transition graph

of an instance

of the problem

A

from

to

if for every instance

of

exists a set

of feasible solutions for

such that

1.

\mathcal{R}
contains all local optima of

2.

For every solution
s
of
I
it is possible to construct in polynomial time a feasible solution

such that

3.

If the transition graph of

contains a path from

to

such that both

and
q^{\prime}
are in
\mathcal{R}
and all other intermediate nodes are not in

be the corresponding solutions in

or there is an arc from

in

.

An interesting subclass of

is

=

\cap

[

]
.

3
Related Work

Many problems are known to be
PLS
-complete problems, whose reductions
mostly start from
FLIP
. An influential reduction technique was used by Krentel
[
10
]
to show that finding a local minimum of a weighted CNF-formula is
PLS
-complete. The idea is to have multiple copies of the circuit, so called
test circuits
that precompute the effects of a flip.
This idea is pushed further in
[
13
]
in order to show that finding a local minimum for weighted positive NAE(not all equal) 3-SAT is
PLS
-complete, and this is used to show that other problems as

or stable configurations of Hopfield networks are
PLS
-complete.

Other direct reductions from
FLIP
are used in
[
4
]
to show that finding a pure Nash equilibrium in an asymmetric network congestion game is
PLS
-complete and in
[
3
]
to show that maximum constraint assignment, a generalization of CNF-SAT, is
PLS
-complete. These reductions are especially of interest to us as
FLIP
is to the best of our knowledge the only problem in
PLS
with lexicographical weights and hence needed for our reduction.

Numerous optimization problems on permutation groups given via generators
\pi_{1},\dots\pi_{m}
are studied in
[
2
]
.
These include finding a
\pi\in\langle\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}\rangle
that minimizes
\Sigma_{i\in V}c(i,\pi(i))
for some cost function
c:V^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. All these problems are
shown to be
NP
-complete via a reduction from finding a fixed-point free permutation, which is shown to be
NP
-complete.

Our problem has previously been studied in
[
1
]
, but there the interest was in
global
optima. This was proven to be
NP
-hard even for abelian groups.

5.4
Proof of Correctness

In this section we now prove the correctness and tightness of our reduction. In order to show the correctness we have to show that if
\pi\in G
is a local optimum
of the
k
-Permutation Local Orbit Minimum
instance, i.e., if

Every permutation used in the reduction has an order of two, so it is involution. Still, these permutations do not form a commutative group due to the

Theorem 4

The given reduction is tight.

Proof.

6
Realizing the permutations in propositional formula

In the previous section, we have shown the problem to be
PLS
-complete for arbitrary permutation groups. In case of the application to SAT, it might be that no formula exists that has exactly these symmetries and hence the problem might efficiently solvable in this case. In this section, we show this to be false by constructing a formula whose symmetries are the permutations of the previous chapter.

A symmetry of a CNF (viewed as a set of sets of literals) is a map that maps variables to variables and results in the same CNF. An alternative definition maps literals to literals, but this is equivalent for our formula as it is almost entirely positive.

Our CNF uses every position of the above proof as a variable. Additionally, we add an output variable
o_{g}
(and also the negated
o_{g}^{\prime}
) for every gate in order to have an easier formula. We now describe the different components of the CNF that are combined by a big and.

Firstly, we add for each variable clauses that describe an xor between a variable
x
and its negation
x^{\prime}
(if the variable is already negated, we gain the positive variable again). This type of clause will be the only clauses that have a length of two.

Next, we don’t want any symmetries between the different input or output positions. We use the following construction to fix the positions. Let the inputs (or alternatively outputs) of the circuit be
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\dots i_{n}
. We add the following clauses:

\{i_{1},i_{1}^{\prime}\},\{i_{1},i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2},i_{2}^{\prime}\},\{i_{1},i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2},i_{2}^{\prime},i_{3},i_{3}^{\prime}\},\dots\{i_{1},i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2},i_{2}^{\prime},i_{3},i_{3}^{\prime},\dots,i_{n},i_{n}^{\prime}\}
.

As, the number of appearances is different for each input (or alternatively output) it prevents a symmetry between the inputs while still allowing to flip an input. There is also no symmetry possible with later clauses as these will have constant sizes whereas here the size depends on
n
. Due to the structure of the circuit, there will also no symmetry between the input and the output even if the number of inputs and outputs is the same.

Each gate
g
with its output is expressed with a CNF that describes that there is exactly one element that is different to the other gate elements and is identical to the output. Instead of
\neg x
, we use
x^{\prime}
to express the negation. For each such clause, we add another one, where every variable
x
is replaced by
x^{\prime}
to allow the inverting action described above.

We additionally add clauses that describe the effect on the following gates. If
x
is the first input to the gate
g
, we add clauses
\{x^{\prime},g_{10},g_{11}\}
and
\{x,g_{00},g_{01}\}
that will lead to the change of the minority position when
x
is inverted. Additionally, we need
\{x^{\prime},g_{10}^{\prime},g_{11}^{\prime}\}
and
\{x,g_{00}^{\prime},g_{01}^{\prime}\}
to allow the invert action on the gate.

Lemma 7
.

Any symmetry of this formula maps input variables to input variables, gate positions to gate positions of the same gate and output variables to output variables.

Proof.

As gate positions can only occur in clauses up to length 5, whereas input and output variables occur in clauses up to length
2n
, gate positions can only be mapped to other gate positions. There is also no way to map output variables to input variables or vice versa as the output variables only occur as the output clauses of a gate whereas the input variables only occur in the input clauses of a gate. As those differ in length there is no way to map input variables to output variables.

Now it only remains to show that the gate variables are not mapped to a different gate. We see from the clause
\{g_{00},g_{01},g_{10},g_{11}\}
that the gate variables must stay together and cannot be split. By an inductive argument on the distance from the inputs we see that there is no way to swap different gates. The first gates have all without loss of generality different inputs. As those are fixed, the gates can only permute among themselves.
∎

The formula so far represents a single circuit. In order to capture the setup with the test-circuits we use
(n+1)
disjoint copies of the clauses. This introduces new symmetries between the disjoint copies. These symmetries do however only allow to switch hole copies and not individual gates.
Now we finally need to express that the test-circuits differ in exactly one variable. We achieve this by adding a clique between all inputs using equivalence and xor to express this condition. The equivalence is needed to express that that all other positions of the test circuit are the same as the first circuit and the xor expresses the difference. We need this to be a clique to allow the symmetry between the circuits. If there would only be clauses, it would not be possible to swap the CNF for a test-circuit with the CNF for the first circuit.

We note that this CNF has additional symmetries that are not part of the group given in the previous chapter. We are allowed to swap test circuit i with test circuit j. This does not allow any simpler computation as the relative order between the test circuits does not matter for the hardness result.

7
Conclusion and open questions

We have shown that the problem is
PLS
-hard in general and hence a polynomial time algorithm is unlikely unless
P
=
PLS
. In the theory of
PLS
-complete problems we thereby demonstrated another example of a hard problem with lexicographic weights. The used permutations are realistic in the sense that they can occur in an actual CNF formula.

Our above reduction requires polynomial many permutations. Nonetheless, we have only found an efficient algorithm for the case of
one
permutation. What about other any other constant number of permutations?

Additionally, we see that finding a local minimum of an Abelian permutation group is P-hard.
Is this a strict lower bound, i.e. does there exist a polynomial time algorithm to compute a local optima in this case or can the reduction be modified to work also for Abelian permutation groups. Another possibility would be that this is in an intermediate class such as
CLS
, but it seems unclear to us how to deal with multiple permutations that all have the same successor permutation.

The neighborhood of a solution
\pi
is in our work defined as
\pi\circ\pi_{i}
for some generator
\pi_{i}
. An alternative neighborhood would be
\pi_{i}\circ\pi
, which would place the generator between the string and the current permutation. While the results of the one permutation case trivially hold again, the hardness proof does not transform to this formulation.

Condensed and expanded view.

We expand on the encoding of a position

The permutations make sure that

and
x_{1}
and swaps between circuits swap both elements. This allows us to always change a position by inverting it.
Additionally, we note that we can order the positions in a way such that any local optimum in the condensed view is a local optima is the expanded view and vice versa. Let

be the set of positions and

be a function mapping the positions to their importance. Then we can use the alternative map

with

and

.

In a local optima

of the condensed view there exists for every permutation

in the group a smallest index

at which

and

differ. Then they differ also at
2i
in the expanded view. All position before

do not differ in the expanded view as well and since

is a local optima, the position
2i
in the expanded view is mapped to zero. In the expanded view of

the position

must be mapped to one by the assumption and hence it is not improving here as well.
