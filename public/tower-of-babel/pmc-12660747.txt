Agronomic performance of the first ratoon of sugarcane under phosphorus supply from a mineral and an organic source
Authors: Evaldo Alves dos Santos, Frederico Antonio Loureiro Soares, Marconi Batista Teixeira, Edson Cabral da Silva, Wilker Alves Morais, Willian Marques Pires, Luciana Cristina Vitorino, Layara Alexandre Bessa, Antônio Evami Cavalcante Sousa, Luís Sérgio Rodrigues Vale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn soils of the Brazilian Cerrado region, particularly in oxisols, sugarcane productivity is directly influenced by phosphorus availability. However, the increasing demand for efficiency and optimization of phosphate fertilizers, along with their cost, has raised interest in organic sources. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of different doses of triple superphosphate, applied alone or in combination with poultry litter, on the biometric traits, technological quality, and stalk yield of the first ratoon crop of sugarcane. The experiment was conducted in an experimental area of Denusa – Destilaria Nova União S/A, located in Goiás, Brazil, on a eutrophic Red Latosol (Oxisol) of the Cerrado biome. The application of triple superphosphate and poultry litter doses exerts a positive influence on plant height, average stalk diameter, and tiller number. Poultry litter can be used as a cost-effective alternative for sugarcane fertilization, as the application of up to 4.67 t ha−1 of poultry litter increased yield. However, applying 2 t ha−1 of poultry litter together with 180 kg ha−1 of triple superphosphate proved to be the most effective strategy for maximizing total recoverable sugar, a key parameter that determines the crop’s economic value.Keywords: Saccharum officinarum, Triple superphosphate, Poultry litter, Organic phosphorus, Leaf nutrient analysisSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sugarcane (Saccharum spp.) is widely recognized worldwide for its high biomass production capacity, sequestering thousands of tons of CO₂ from the atmosphere during its growth cycle1. he crop presents a highly attractive sustainable balance regarding greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions during the industrial processing stage2. Therefore, sugarcane stands as the leading crop in economic exploitation in Brazil and is used for the production of clean and renewable energy, biofuels, sugar, and a variety of other products3–6.

Brazil is the world’s largest sugarcane producer and accounts for approximately 8% of global fertilizer consumption, ranking fourth behind China, India, and the United States. Together, soybean, corn, and sugarcane represent more than 73% of the country’s total fertilizer use7. In this context, the National Association for Fertilizer Dissemination (ANDA) reported that more than 70% of the fertilizers used in Brazilian agriculture are imported, with the country being highly dependent on external sources for potassium chloride (95%), nitrogen (80%), and phosphate (60%). Fertilizer imports increased from US$ 8.59 billion in 2018 to US$ 24.76 billion in 20228. This scenario highlights the need to seek alternative nutrient sources.

To meet the nutritional demand of sugarcane, the use of large amounts of mineral fertilizers is common, aiming to achieve high production within the plant cycle2. However, in Brazilian soils, phosphorus (P) is found in low quantities9. In the Cerrado region, a biome that occupies about 23% of Brazilian territory, P can be a limiting factor because of the high adsorption of phosphate ions by iron (Fe) and aluminum (Al) oxides present in the soil10,11. Therefore, phosphate fertilization with soluble sources is essential in these areas12.

In this context, triple superphosphate (TSP), a highly soluble source that ensures rapid phosphorus release, is widely marketed in Brazil. However, concerns remain regarding its long-term efficiency and optimization13.

Thus, the use of poultry manure has emerged as an alternative to replace or complement mineral fertilization in sugarcane cultivation14–16. The use of this material for soil fertility and plant mineral nutrition in agricultural production is an excellent way to meet the sustainable demands of the soil‒plant-environment system, especially considering that Brazil is a major producer and the world’s largest exporter of chicken meat17,18, which consequently generates a large amount of organic residues, particularly poultry litter.

The use of organic compounds in agriculture not only contributes to cost savings but also allows for the recycling of essential mineral nutrients for plants2. Additionally, it helps increase the soil’s cation exchange capacity (CEC) through the addition of organic matter, improving the soil physical structure, water retention, and phosphorus supply to the crop19.

After the application of phosphate fertilizers to the soil, a series of physicochemical processes occur, resulting in the transformation of phosphate into complex phosphatic compounds. These processes include adsorption reactions, precipitation, dissolution, and interactions with the soil organic matter.

When phosphorus is in solution or weakly adsorbed, it is considered to be in the labile form, meaning that it is available to plants. However, when adsorption occurs through stronger bonds, this interaction hinders the release of phosphorus into the soil solution, characterizing the nonlabile forms20.

However, there is limited information on the effects of poultry litter when combined with doses of triple superphosphate in the Brazilian Cerrado. Given the high cost of triple superphosphate and the fact that sugarcane is considered a semiperennial crop. Therefore, was hypothesized that the use of triple superphosphate together with poultry litter could positively influence on biometric growth of the first-ratoon sugarcane crop, reflecting improvements in technological parameters. Furthermore, it is believed that the effective combination of organic and mineral phosphorus sources may simultaneously benefit production.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of different doses of triple superphosphate and poultry litter on the biometric characteristics, technological quality, and productivity of the first ratoon crop of sugarcane.

Materials and methods

The experiment was carried out field conditions at Destilaria Nova União S/A, which is located in the rural area of the municipality of Jandaia, Goiás state, Central-West Region of Brazil (17°15’52.6” S, 50°08’23.2” W, at 519 m altitude). The municipality is located in the Southwest Region of Goiás, Brazil, with a tropical wet and dry climate (Aw), characterized by a dry winter and rainy summer, according to the Köppen-Geiger climate classification21.

According to data from weather stations installed onsite, during the rainy season, which typically starts in October and ends in March, the average precipitation is 1,403 mm per year. The experiment was conducted during the 2020/2021 harvest (the first ratoon crop). Rainfall (mm), temperature (°C), and average air humidity (%) were measured. The recorded climatological data were 1,139.40 mm, 24.19 °C, and 67.94%, respectively (Fig. 1).

The sugarcane was planted in 2019/2020, and the harvesting and evaluation of the first ratoon cane for this study were carried out in 2020/2021. The soil was classified as a typical eutrophic Red Latosol22 with a clayey texture and flat relief. Soil samples were collected at depths of 0.0–0.20 m and 0.20–0.40 m prior to the establishment of the experiment for chemical and granulometric characterization, with analyses performed according to the methodologies described in23 (Table 1).

The fertilization treatments consisted of supplying P through a mineral source, triple superphosphate (TSP), and an organic source, poultry litter (PL). The experimental design was a randomized complete block in a 5 × 5 factorial scheme with four replications. The treatments included five doses of triple superphosphate (TSP): 0, 60, 120, 180, and 240 kg ha⁻¹, equivalent to 0, 27.6, 55.2, 82.8, and 110.4 kg ha⁻¹ of P2O5, and five doses of poultry litter (PL): 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹, equivalent to 0, 32.7, 65.4, 98.1, and 130.8 kg ha⁻¹ of P2O5.

The mineral and organic fertilizer were applied jointly in two stages: (a) in the planting furrow during sugarcane establishment (June 2019); and (b) in the first ratoon, 30 days after harvesting the plant cane (July 2020). The applications were carried out manually and, subsequently, the fertilizers were incorporated into the soil with the aid of a shovel. Each experimental unit consisted of 150 m², comprising 10 rows of sugarcane, each 10 m long, with 1.5-meter spacing between rows.

The TSP used contained 46% P₂O₅ and 10% Ca, with granules between 2 and 4 mm in size. The poultry litter originated from a chicken farm located in the municipality of Palmeiras de Goiás, GO, from the first flock of chickens, composted, with the following chemical composition (average of two applications: plant cane and first ratoon): N = 25.40 g kg⁻¹; P = 7.40 g kg⁻¹; K = 7.40 g kg⁻¹; Ca = 0.19 g kg⁻¹; Mg = 0.04 g kg⁻¹; S = 15.20 g kg⁻¹; B = 0.30 mg kg⁻¹; Zn = 2.80 mg kg⁻¹; Fe = 25.00 mg kg⁻¹; Mn = 5.20 mg kg⁻¹; Cu = 3.40 mg kg⁻¹; mineral matter = 326.90 g kg⁻¹; organic matter = 673.20 g kg⁻¹; organic carbon = 413.00 g kg⁻¹; and moisture at 105 °C = 17.36%.

At planting (2019/2020) and 30 days after the plant cane was harvested, organic and mineral phosphorus fertilizers were applied according to each treatment. Nitrogen fertilization was performed via urea at a dose of 100 kg ha⁻¹, and potassium fertilization was performed via potassium chloride (KCl) at a dose of 80 kg ha⁻¹ of K₂O24.

The genotype IACSP95-5094 was used and planted vegetatively on 06/11/2019. The sugarcane furrows were opened to a depth of 0.25 m. This genotype is characterized by excellent performance, stalk density, and adaptation to clayey soils. The first ratoon harvest, corresponding to the data in this study, was carried out on 06/11/2021 via a John Deere tracked harvester, model 3520–3522, which was transported on a transfer truck. Biometric characteristics, technological quality, and sugarcane productivity were evaluated.

The plant height, average stalk diameter, and number of tillers were evaluated to characterize the growth of the first ratoon sugarcane crop. Plant height was measured as the distance between the plant’s stalk base (stalk collar surface) and the visible upper auricular region of the + 1 leaf, using a tape measure graduated in centimeters. The average stalk diameter was measured with a digital caliper graduated in millimeters at the basal, intermediate, and apical portions of the sugarcane stalk. The number of tillers per linear meter was counted, and the number of tillers was recorded the day before the plot was harvested.

Five days before each harvest, 10 stalks were manually collected from each plot by cutting them close to ground level for the determination of their technological attributes. After being cut, the stalks from each plot were bundled and sent to the technological analysis laboratory at Destilaria Nova União for evaluation of the following industrial parameters: total recoverable sugars, sugar yield, and alcohol yield, according to the system25,26.

Sugarcane productivity in tons of cane per hectare (TCH) was evaluated by the total weighing of stalks in each experimental plot. For this purpose, plants were cut close to the ground via a cane harvester and transported via a transfer truck equipped with a digital scale. The data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) via the F test at the 5% significance level. When significant effects were detected, regression analyses were performed. The effect of the crop season, when significant, was compared via Tukey’s test (p < 0.05). Statistical analyses were conducted via the SISVAR software®27.

The analysis of variance also revealed a significant interaction effect between TSP and PL on total recoverable sugars (TRS), sugar yield (SY), and alcohol yield (AY). Moreover, for the first ratoon sugarcane stalk productivity in tons per hectare (TCH), isolated effects of both factors (TSP and PL) were observed, but their interaction was not significant (Table 2). Biometric growth variables of the first ratoon sugarcane crop

The plant height (PH) of the first ratoon sugarcane crop responded quadratically to the different TSP doses, with an R² of 79.19% (Fig. 2A). The maximum PH (288.46 cm) was achieved at the estimated dose of 132.50 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP, while the minimum PH (278.61 cm) occurred in the absence of TSP application (0 kg ha⁻¹). This represents an increase of 3.41% in plant height at the optimal dose compared with the control treatment without P fertilization.

Sugarcane plant height as a function of triple superphosphate and poultry litter doses (A,B), stalk diameter (C,D), and number of tillers (E,F) under the breakdown of triple superphosphate doses within each poultry litter dose (C,E), and stalk diameter and number of tillers under the breakdown of poultry litter doses within each triple superphosphate dose (D,F).

The PH data in response to the various PL doses fit an increasing linear model, with an R² of 92.85% (Fig. 2B). The lowest plant height under the PL treatment was 279.17 cm, which was observed at the 0 t ha⁻¹ dose, whereas the highest was 289.32 cm, which was observed at the 8 t ha⁻¹ dose.

The average stalk diameter (SD) was influenced by the TSP and PL doses, as well as by the interaction between the TSP and PL (Table 2). In the breakdown analysis of TSP doses within each PL dose (Fig. 2C), the SD data were significant only at the 4 t ha⁻¹ PL dose, with the data fitting a quadratic distribution model and an average R² of 72.97%. The smallest SD was obtained at the 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose (23.92 mm), whereas the highest SD, estimated via the regression Eq. (26.37 mm), was 74.52 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP. SD data for TSP doses of 0, 2, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹ PL doses were not significant.

In the breakdown of the PL doses within each TSP dose, a significant effect on the stalk diameter (SD) was observed only at the 120 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose (Fig. 2D), with the data fitting a quadratic model and an R² of 48.39%. According to the regression equation, the highest SD (26.39 mm) was estimated at the 4.25 t ha⁻¹ PL dose. The SD results for PL doses of 0, 60, 180, and 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses were not significant under the mathematical models tested.

The number of tillers (NT) was significantly influenced by the TSP and PL doses, as well as by the TSP × PL interaction (Table 2). In the breakdown of TSP doses within each PL dose, a significant effect was observed for the 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹ PL doses on NT (Fig. 2E), with the data fitting a quadratic model with R² values of 51.30%, 98.21%, 80.67%, 92.78%, and 56.21%, respectively.

The TSP doses estimated by the regression equations within the 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹ PL doses were 153.88, 106.02, 105.50, 104.50, and 146.00 kg ha⁻¹, presenting NT values of 13.52, 15.04, 13.89, 14.84, and 14.34, respectively.

When the PL dose was decreased at each TSP dose, effects were observed at the 60, 120, and 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses (Fig. 2F). The PL doses within the 60 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose fit a quadratic distribution model, with an average R² of 41.54%. According to the regression equation, the PL dose at 60 kg ha⁻¹ TSP provided a maximum NT of 14.48.

The results obtained for the PL doses and the 120 and 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses fit a linear model (Fig. 2F). The 0 t ha⁻¹ PL dose at 120 kg ha⁻¹ TSP resulted in the lowest NT (12.86), whereas the 6 t ha⁻¹ PL dose resulted in the highest NT (14.72). However, at the 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose, the PL dose with the lowest NT was 8 t ha⁻¹ (11.43), with the highest NT observed at 2 t ha⁻¹ (14.61).

Technological quality of the first ratoon sugarcane crop

For total recoverable sugar (TRS), there was a significant TSP × PL interaction; therefore, the breakdown of TSP doses within each PL dose and of PL doses within each TSP dose was performed (Fig. 3A and B). The TRS varied with the TSP dose under the 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹ PL doses (Fig. 3A). The TRS data for the TSP doses at 0, 2, 4, and 6 t ha⁻¹ PLs fit a quadratic model with R² values of 94.97%, 45.34%, 92.05%, and 24.27%, respectively, whereas for the TSP doses at the 8 t ha⁻¹ PL dose, the results fit an increasing linear model, with an average R² of 53.42%.

For PL doses at the 180 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose, the decreasing linear model best fit the average TRS data, with an R² of 90.63% (Fig. 3B). The 2 t ha⁻¹ PL dose combined with 180 kg ha⁻¹ TSP provided a TRS of 165.22 kg t⁻¹, with a 2.94% reduction observed when the PL dose increased to 8 t ha⁻¹.

The behavior of the PL dose at the 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP dose shows a decreasing linear relationship (Fig. 3B). The TRS results at this TSP dose and PL dose showed a low fit (R² = 26.17%), with 73.83% of the data unexplained by the mathematical model. The TRSs obtained in this treatment were 156.98, 157.18, 152.65, 163.55, and 160.41 kg t⁻¹ for the 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 t ha⁻¹ PL doses, respectively.

Sugar yield (SY) was significantly affected by the combination of PL and TSP (Fig. 3C and D). Differences were observed among the SY doses at the 4 and 6 t ha⁻¹ PL doses (Fig. 3C). However, for the 4 and 6 t ha⁻¹ PL doses and TSP doses, the SY data fit a quadratic model with R² values of 47.99% and 67.46%, respectively (Fig. 3C). The sugar yield results obtained at the 4 t ha⁻¹ PL dose were weakly correlated.

The regression equation indicates that increasing the TSP dose to 122.50 and 127.75 kg ha⁻¹ resulted in increased sugar yield, estimated at 28.35 and 27.94 t ha⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 3C). The TSP doses and PL doses of 0, 2, and 8 t ha⁻¹ were not significant.

When the sugar yield at the PL and 0 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses were analyzed, a linear model fit was observed (R² = 60.23) (Fig. 3D). In the absence of mineral phosphate fertilization, the maximum PL dose (8 t ha⁻¹) resulted in a sugar yield of 25.42 t ha⁻¹, representing a 12.27% increase compared with the 0 t ha⁻¹ PL dose. The PL doses combined with the 60, 120, and 180 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses were significant, with the data fitting a quadratic model. However, the PL doses combined with the 60 and 120 kg ha⁻¹ TSP doses had very low coefficients of determination (R² = 30.88 and 41.25%, respectively), indicating that the estimated sugar yields at these levels are not representative.

The maximum PL doses estimated via the regression equations (4.28, 5.78, and 4.66 t ha⁻¹ of PL) combined with the TSP dose yielded sugar yields of 27.27, 27.27, and 27.31 t ha⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 3D). However, the sugar yield at the PL dose combined with 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP did not significantly differ.

The doses estimated via the regression equations (4.28, 5.78, and 4.66 t ha⁻¹ of PL) combined with the TSP doses resulted in sugar yields of 27.27, 27.27, and 27.31 t ha⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 3D). However, the sugar yield at the PL dose combined with 240 kg ha⁻¹ TSP was not significant.

For the results obtained at TSP doses and PL doses of 2, 4, and 6 t ha⁻¹, the data fit best to a quadratic model (Fig. 3E). According to the regression equation, the estimated TSP doses of 134.00, 132.00, and 131.38 kg ha⁻¹ resulted in alcohol yields of 19.20, 21.60, and 23.30 m³ ha⁻¹, respectively. No significant difference was observed for the TSP dose at the 8 t ha⁻¹ PL dose.

For alcohol yield (AY) in the breakdown of PL doses within each TSP dose, the TSP doses of 0, 60, 120, 180, and 240 kg ha⁻¹ were significant, with a quadratic fit of the results (Fig. 3F). According to the regression equations for the PL and TSP doses, by applying PL doses of 5.52, 4.20, 4.55, 4.52, and 4.54 t ha⁻¹ combined with TSP doses of 0, 60, 120, 180, and 240 kg ha⁻¹, respectively, the estimated AY would be 18.07, 20.10, 22.04, 22.44, and 18.60 m³ ha⁻¹. However, the TSP doses of 180 and 240 kg ha⁻¹ combined with the PL dose had the lowest coefficients of determination, with R² = 49.24% and 58.66%, respectively.

For productivity in tons per hectare (TCH), this variable was influenced by the doses of TSP and PL (Fig. 4A and B). When analyzing TCH in response to TSP doses, the data fit a quadratic model, with the highest TCH value observed at a dose of 138.10 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP (TCH = 164.04 t ha⁻¹).

The behavior of TCH was similar to that of PL, as productivity showed positive responses with increasing doses up to 4.67 t ha⁻¹ of PL. This resulted in an estimated TCH of 167.69 t ha⁻¹, representing a productive performance increase of 11.29% compared with the productivity under the TSP doses.

Discussion

Fertilization with both triple superphosphate (TSP) and poultry litter (PL) positively influenced plant height (PH) of the first ratoon sugarcane crop when applied individually, highlighting the importance of phosphorus supply, whether in organic or mineral form. The optimal TSP dose to increase plant height was 132.50 kg ha⁻¹, resulting in an average height of 288.46 cm. It is noteworthy that TSP is a soluble P source, which provides the nutrient rapidly for root absorption, promoting a quick increase in plant biomass. However, although effective in the short term, soluble sources can be more rapidly fixed in the soil, reducing their long-term availability.

On the other hand, the optimal PL dose to increase sugarcane PH was 8 t ha⁻¹, resulting in an average height of 289.32 cm. This performance may be related to the use of PL applied during the plant cane and first ratoon crops, which contributes to P mineralization, increasing the availability of this element in the soil and potentially promoting plant growth29. PL, as an organic fertilizer, is rich in nutrients, particularly N, P, and K, and is capable of increasing exchangeable cations such as calcium, while reducing exchangeable aluminum and soil acidity11. This potentially decreases the soil’s P-fixation capacity and, consequently, increases phosphorus availability in the soil-plant system30.

Regular fertilization with organic sources, such as poultry litter, in addition to supplying nutrients to the soil in a manner more synchronized with plant demand, improves moisture retention and reduces temperature fluctuations, factors that positively affect the longevity of the sugarcane field11.

The average stalk diameter (SD) of the first ratoon sugarcane crop was positively influenced by the interaction of TSP and PL doses. In this study, plants that received 4 t ha⁻¹ of PL combined with 74.52 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP exhibited the highest SD (26.37 mm). Conversely, treatments receiving higher PL doses in combination with TSP showed a reduction in SD. Santos et al.31 studying the same genotype as in the present study (IACSP95-5094), observed a similar variation in SD in the absence of PL and TSP. The same authors concluded that, in the absence of organic and mineral P in the production system, SD increased, reaching 27.40 mm.

Studies32,33 report that phosphorus deficiency reduces sugarcane SD. In another study, the authors state that the combination of mineral and organic fertilizers is very important for maintaining soil fertility and achieving high sugarcane yields34.

The first ratoon crop of sugarcane responded to the interaction between TSP and PL, promoting an increase in number of tillers (NT). The highest NT (14.72) was observed at the combination of 6 t ha⁻¹ of PL with 120 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP. The responses to TSP and PL application on tiller production can be physiologically explained by changes in plant growth and biomass production.

This occurs because phosphorus is responsible for protein formation, cell division, photosynthesis, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, sugar breakdown, respiration, sucrose formation, and promotion of root development20. In addition to P derived from organic matter, the efficiency of phosphate fertilization in sugarcane is influenced by factors such as soil texture and preparation, land use, and management history, among others.

Studies report that the interaction of mineral and organic phosphate sources can increase sugarcane tillering by up to 191%, with consequent improvements in the plant’s technological quality34.

The TRS content increased exponentially when the PL dose was 2 t ha⁻¹ combined with an increase of 180 kg ha⁻¹ in TSP. This result indicates a positive effect of the application of mineral and organic fertilizers. The TRS obtained at this dose was 165.22 kg t⁻¹, representing an increase of 2.88%. This increase in TRS production can be mainly explained by the phosphorus supplied by the mineral and organic sources, which promoted plant growth and development, positively affecting the final raw material.

Sugarcane growth and development are directly dependent on the availability of photoassimilates, mainly sucrose, produced in the photosynthetic tissues (leaves)35. These carbohydrates serve as the primary source of energy and carbon for essential metabolic processes, such as cell division and expansion, and stalk elongation36.

During stalk elongation, cells of the intercalary meristem and parenchyma require intense cell wall synthesis, active solute transport, and turgor, processes that depend on ATP37. Thus, adequate phosphorus nutrition increases energy availability in the form of ATP, enhancing photosynthetic processes and improving the plant’s capacity to synthesize and translocate sucrose to the stalks.

Oliveira et al.38 reported a positive response in the technological quality parameters of the first sugarcane ratoon at high phosphorus levels due to improved phosphorus supply in the system. The same authors reported a maximum TRS of 131.47 kg t⁻¹, which is lower than the result obtained in this study39. These authors attributed the increase in TRS to the application of P.

For the 2024/2025 harvest, the state of Goiás had an average of 137.30 t ha⁻¹, while the national average was 137.40 t ha⁻¹. Therefore, the maximum value obtained in this study exceeds the averages of both the state of Goiás and Brazil40.

In this study, the interaction between TSP and PL resulted in a 10.65% increase in sugar yield (SY). The application of 6 t ha⁻¹ of PL combined with 127.75 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP resulted in a SY of 27.94 t ha⁻¹. The increases in RY can be attributed to greater stalk production in response to phosphate fertilization, particularly due to an increase in the NT. These results indicate a positive response to P addition in both organic and mineral forms41 reported that the combination of inorganic and organic P sources increases P availability in the soil, leading to increased sugar production.

Korndorfer et al.42 reported that phosphorus use in the first sugarcane ratoon increases tillering, which leads to favorable TCH and SY. The application of mineral fertilizer and organic compost as P sources results in a positive interaction, which can be attributed to greater P availability resulting from reduced P fixation in the soil43.

Boschiero et al.44 reported that higher sugar productivity in some treatments was due to greater stalk productivity. Fernandes45 explained that TRSs are considered in the calculation of sugar productivity, highlighting the importance of both yield and raw material quality, which depends on adequate nutrient supply.

There was an interaction effect between the doses of TSP and PL on alcohol yield (AY), where AY increased exponentially at a dose of 4.55 t ha⁻¹ of PL combined with 120 kg ha⁻¹ of TSP, increasing the yield by 40.20% and reaching a maximum value of 22.04 m³ ha⁻¹. Since phosphorus was applied in the plant cane and the first ratoon (cycle under study), it is believed that phosphorus content in the first ratoon remained high, partly due to the residual P effect in the soil.

Similar results were observed by Santos et al.31 when evaluating the effect of mineral and organic phosphate fertilization in sugarcane. The authors report that the combination of TSP and PL applied in previous cycles increases P availability for the plant, promoting greater tillering and consequently enhancing AY.

Lavanholi46 reported that, from an industrial perspective, the importance lies in the higher TCH, which in turn provides greater energy potential for alcohol production. In this study, the variables TRS, SY and AY were extremely relevant, as they were positively correlated with each other, corroborating the final yield of the crop.

Stalk yield per hectare (TCH) of the first ratoon sugarcane crop was positively influenced by TSP and PL doses. However, the optimal TSP dose to increase TCH was 138.10 kg ha⁻¹, resulting in a TCH of 164.04 t ha⁻¹. Conversely, the optimal PL dose to increase TCH was 4.67 t ha⁻¹ (TCH = 167.69 t ha⁻¹), providing a positive increase of 11.29% through the use of the organic fertilizer source.

This increase in TCH can be attributed to the slow release of P by PL, which promotes greater mobilization of the soil’s organic and inorganic P reserves, ensuring a continuous supply of the nutrient to meet the crop’s demands throughout its phenological stages.

Sugarcane cultivation under slow-release organic fertilization can reduce the stock of the more labile P fractions due to greater nutrient uptake by the crop47. However, the same authors report that part of the P tends to accumulate in moderately labile fractions, which act as a reserve and maintain availability throughout the crop cycle. The presence of organic fertilizer may have contributed to the retention of soil P in both the more labile and less labile fractions.

PL is an organic material with a high P concentration, capable of increasing soil P levels and organic matter48,49. In the present study, PL application may have contributed to the improvement of soil structure, promoting better development of the root system in the first ratoon sugarcane crop and thereby enhancing nutrient uptake, which may have contributed to the significant increase in TCH. In addition to being a complex P source, PL contains other nutrients and organic matter that support plant development.

Simões et al.50 observed a 16.49% increase in the productivity of the first ratoon crop of sugarcane compared to the plant cane, which was attributed to appropriate postharvest nutritional management. Oliveira Junior et al.51 reported a TCH of 140.83 t ha⁻¹ was obtained with the application of up to 4.8 t ha⁻¹ of PL in a eutrophic Red Latosol, which belongs to the same soil class used in the present study. However, this result is lower than that obtained in this study (TCH = 167.69 t ha⁻¹ with the application of 4.67 t ha⁻¹ of PL).

Studies by Sinha et al.52 that used biofertilizers in the first ratoon crop of sugarcane reported a TCH of 79.6 t ha⁻¹. The increase in productivity observed in the second ratoon crop of sugarcane may be explained by the genetic characteristics of the IACSP95-5094 genotype, which shows enhanced regrowth capacity following the first harvest53,54.

Overall, the use of mineral and organic sources of P increased stalk productivity, industrial quality, and its derivatives (sugars and alcohol) in the first ratoon of sugarcane. The use of PL is a viable alternative for supplying P. Application of PL increases P availability in the soil and reduces its adsorption31, which favors biometric characteristics, industrial quality, and sugarcane productivity55.

Regardless of the P source, whether mineral or organic, its sole application requires higher doses of each source compared to their combined use.

It is also worth highlighting the large availability of poultry litter in Brazil, which is one of the world’s largest producers and exporters of chicken meat, resulting in the generation of a substantial amount of this organic fertilizer. Thus, its proper use represents a way to recycle nutrients back into the soil, which can contribute to maintaining soil productive potential and sugarcane field longevity (number of ratoon crops). This is particularly important, as planting costs during field renovation represent a significant portion of total production expenses.

Conclusions

Poultry litter proved to be a viable alternative for sugarcane fertilization in promoting plant growth. When combined with a mineral phosphorus source, it also contributes to an increase in tiller number.

In the absence of mineral phosphate fertilization, a dose of 4.67 t ha⁻¹ of poultry litter showed potential to promote better plant development, with effects on stalk yield and sugarcane technological attributes, resulting in higher total recoverable sugars and their derivatives.

Under the conditions of the present study, the use of 2 t ha⁻¹ of poultry litter combined with 180 kg ha⁻¹ of triple superphosphate is recommended to promote the best development of the sugarcane ratoon crop, aiming to increase productivity while maintaining adequate raw material quality.

This study provides support for addressing phosphorus limitations in the soils of the Brazilian Cerrado intended for sugarcane, contributing to the development of more sustainable agricultural practices in the region.
