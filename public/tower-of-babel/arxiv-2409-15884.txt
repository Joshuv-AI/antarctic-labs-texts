Interpolation Filter Design for Sample Rate Independent Audio Effect RNNs
Alistair Carson, Alec Wright and Stefan Bilbao

Abstract
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are effective at emulating the non-linear, stateful behavior of analog guitar amplifiers and distortion effects. Unlike the case of direct circuit simulation, RNNs have a fixed sample rate encoded in their model weights, making the sample rate non-adjustable during inference. Recent work has proposed increasing the sample rate of RNNs at inference (oversampling) by increasing the feedback delay length in samples, using a fractional delay filter for non-integer conversions. Here, we investigate the task of lowering the sample rate at inference (undersampling), and propose using an extrapolation filter to approximate the required fractional signal advance. We consider two filter design methods and analyse the impact of filter order on audio quality. Our results show that the correct choice of filter can give high quality results for both oversampling and undersampling; however, in some cases the sample rate adjustment leads to unwanted artefacts in the output signal. We analyse these failure cases through linearised stability analysis, showing that they result from instability around a fixed point. This approach enables an informed prediction of suitable interpolation filters for a given RNN model before runtime.

Ideally, any audio processing software should be able to operate at arbitrary sample rates—or at least the industry standard rates of
44.1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}

Problem Statement

Consider a continuous-time input audio signal
x(t)
that has been sampled at a rate of
F_{s}
to give
x_{n}
where
n
is an integer sample index. Here we consider RNN models of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{n}

\displaystyle=f\left(\mathbf{h}_{n-1},x_{n}\right)

(1a)

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle=g\left(\mathbf{h}_{n},x_{n}\right),

(1b)

where
\mathbf{h}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times 1}
is the hidden state of length
H
and
y_{n}
is the output signal. This class of model has been extensively used in recent years for modelling guitar amplifiers and effects pedals
[
7
,
18
,
12
,
19
]
. In this work we consider
f
to be a LSTM cell, and
g
an affine transformation (which is by definition sample rate independent).

Consider the model (
1
) as pre-trained on audio with sample rate
F_{s}
but at inference we wish to stream audio sampled at a different rate
F^{\prime}_{s}
, such that the model takes the input signal
x^{\prime}_{n}
and produces the output signal
y^{\prime}_{n}
. For an objective measure of quality we generate a target signal
\hat{y}_{n}
which is
y_{n}
resampled from
F_{s}
to
F^{\prime}_{s}
using a DFT-based sample rate conversion
[
20
]
, and measure the signal-to-noise ratio as:

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\hat{y}_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(y^{\prime}_{n}-\hat{y}_{n}\right)^{2}}

(2)

where
N
is the duration of the input signal in samples. The objective here is to design a sample rate independent RNN which results in a maximum SNR.

III
Sample rate independent RNNs

Adjusting the inference sample rate of an RNN can be achieved by maintaining the same delay duration (in seconds) seen by the RNN during training
[
13
,
14
]
. The modified RNN operating at
F^{\prime}_{s}
can be defined as:

\displaystyle{\bf h}^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=f\left({\bf h}^{\prime}_{n-1-\Delta},x^{\prime}_{n}\right)

(3)

where
\Delta=F^{\prime}_{s}/F_{s}-1
is the delay-line length adjustment in samples
[
13
,
14
]
. For non-integer conversion ratios, the state at non-integer time step
n-1-\Delta
can be approximated with a fractional delay FIR filter:

\displaystyle{\bf h}^{\prime}_{n-1-\Delta}\approx\sum_{k=0}^{K}l_{k}{\bf h}^{\prime}_{n-1-k}

(4)

where
K
is the filter order and
l_{k}
are the filter coefficients.

III-A

Lagrange interpolation

Lagrange interpolation is well known to be suitable for approximating a fractional delay
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, with the coefficients derived analytically
[
25
]
:

\displaystyle l^{\rm lagr.}_{k}=\prod_{j=0,j\neq k}^{K}\frac{\Delta-j}{k-j},\quad k=0,\dots,K.

(5)

This design has the benefit of giving the exact phase response and unity gain at DC, i.e.
\sum_{k=0}^{K}l_{k}^{{\rm lagr.}}=1
. If the desired delay is within a one-sample range and centered around
K/2
, the filter is maximally flat
[
21
]
. In this problem, however, we are restricted by the causality of (
3
) so we also consider non-centered designs. For
K=\{1,3\}
this filter was used for the task of oversampling (
\Delta>0)
in
[
14
]
, with
K=3
(non-centered) giving the best results across all the filters studied. Here we propose using a Lagrange extrapolation for approximation of a signal advance when
\Delta<0
.

III-B

Minimax design

Additionally we consider a
minimax
design which minimises the L-infinity norm of the error magnitude over a desired bandwidth
[
21
,
26
]
. Previous work applied this to a fractional delay problem
[
26
]
and showed the filter coefficients can be obtained via solution of a second order cone optimization
[
27
]
. Here, we set the bandwidth of optimization from zero to 0.25
F_{s}
to cover the typical frequency range of guitar and bass effects. An additional constraint was imposed to find solutions where
\sum_{0}^{K}l^{\rm minimax}_{k}=1
to ensure unity gain at DC. The coefficients were obtained using MATLAB’s
secondordercone
function.

The results of the oversampling experiment are shown in Fig.

1

19\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}

73\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}

Fig.
2

>

60\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}

In the following section, we show that linearised analysis of the target system can help identify which filters are likely to fail, allowing the user to rule these out before experimenting at run-time.

VI
Linearised analysis

The results in Sec.
V
show that the best choice of interpolation filter has a strong dependence on the target system, and a poor choice can severely degrade output quality. Here we show that linearised analysis of the RNN
[
29
]
can help identify which filters are likely to cause a failure.

Consider the modified RNN with delay-line interpolation (
3
) under zero input conditions
x_{n}=0
. We can linearise around some fixed point
{\bf h}={\bf a}
through a Taylor expansion:

{\bf h}_{n}\approx f({\bf a})+{\bf J_{a}}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}l_{k}{\bf h}_{n-1-k}-{\bf a}\right)

(6)

where
{\bf J_{a}}=\nabla f\left({\bf a}\right)^{T}
is the Jacobian matrix. This can then be rewritten as a one-step state space system:

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{n}={\bf A}{\bf v}_{n-1}+{\bf b}

(7)

where
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\cdot(K+1)\times 1}
. Defining vector
{\bf l}=[l_{0},\dots,l_{K}]
, we have block matrices:

{\bf A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf l}\otimes{\bf J_{a}}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c|c}{\bf I}_{(HK)\times(HK)}&{\bf 0}_{(HK)\times H}\\
\end{array}\end{array}\right],\>{\bf b}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c}f({\bf a})-{\bf J_{a}}{\bf a}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf 0}_{(HK)\times H}\\
\end{array}\end{array}\right]

(8)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product and
{\bf I}
,
\bf{0}
are identity and zeros matrices respectively. Note that for
K=0
(no interpolation),
{\bf A}\equiv{\bf J_{a}}
. System (
7
) can then be analysed by examining the pole locations, given by:

{\bf z}_{p}={\rm eig}\left({\bf A}\right)

(9)

In general we will have
H\cdot(K+1)
non-unique eigenvalues, therefore the order of interpolation increases the number of poles in the system. For stability around this fixed point we require
\max\left(\left|{\bf z}_{p}\right|\right)\leq 1.0
.

VI-A

Experiment

We approximated a fixed point for a given model by initialising the unmodified system with zero state and zero input, running for 10k samples and taking
{\bf a}
as the time-averaged states over the last 1k samples. We then computed
{\bf z}_{p}
for all model and filter combinations and predicted if each case would be either stable or unstable, depending on the pole locations. For each case the corresponding empirical result in Sec.
V
was labelled as either successful if the proposed methods gave a positive increase in SNR (relative to no interpolation) or a failure otherwise. This was repeated for both oversampling and undersampling to give 3200 experiments in total. The results can be seen in Table
I
. The results show a high correlation between the stability prediction and the empirical results: in 97.8% of cases (
(2868+261)/3200
) the linear analysis correctly predicts the binary empirical result.

VII
Conclusions and further work

This paper explored interpolation-based methods for adjusting the sample rate of RNNs to allow audio processing at an inference sample rate that differs from training. This builds on prior work that proposed implementing a fractional delay filter in the state feedback loop for the task of non-integer oversampling. Here we extended this method to the inverse task of undersampling, and proposed achieving this by approximating a fractional signal advance in the feedback loop. We considered Lagrange and minimax FIR filter designs, and evaluated the performance of the filters on 160 pre-trained LSTM models of various guitar amplifiers and distortion effects. The results showed that the best choice of filter was highly dependent on the effect-specific weights of the LSTM model. A good choice of filter may give up to
80\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
SNR when oversampling or up to
73\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
when undersampling. However, for certain models a poor choice of filter can result in poorer quality than if no interpolation was used. We showed that these failure cases can be predicted using linearised analysis of the original RNN around a fixed point. In future we will investigate model-specific optimal filter design, using the linearised analysis used to enforce stability as a design constraint.
Alternatively, this analysis may enable sample rate independence by adjusting the weights of the network, thus avoiding the need for interpolation or extrapolation entirely.
