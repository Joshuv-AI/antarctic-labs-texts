The revised version of significant quality of life measure (SigQOLM-36)
Authors: Mohamad Adam Bujang, Masliyana Husin, Wei Hong Lai, Yoon Khee Hon, Xun Ting Tiong, Alex Ren Jye Kim, Nurul Fatma Diyana Ahmad, Eileen Pin Pin Yap, Alan Yean Yip Fong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Significant Quality of Life Measure (SigQOLM) is a validated scale used to measure a broad spectrum of quality of life and well-being. Given that the original SigQOLM consists of 69 items, this study aims to revise and validate a shortened version, referred to as SigQOLM-36, containing 36 items.ResultsThe overall internal consistency of SigQOLM-36 was deemed excellent, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.923. The minimum Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for each domain was 0.733, McDonald’s omega coefficients ranged between 0.562 and 0.991, and with a minimum factor loading of 0.547. Additionally, the model fit for the overall framework and each domain of SigQOLM-36 was found to be excellent (Chi-square test < 0.30, RMSEA < 0.80, SRMR < 0.08, CFI > 0.9, and TLI > 0.9). The SigQOLM-36 maintains the original SigQOLM framework, featuring four elements and 18 domains. In conclusion, the SigQOLM-36 can be used to measure quality of life and well-being of people.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07595-4.Keywords: SigQOLM, Quality of life, Reliability, Validity, Well-being

Introduction

Quality of life (QOL) and well-being serve as crucial measures of individuals’ lives [1–3]. Both concepts encompass a broad spectrum and are characterized by dynamic, complex, and subjective elements that interact at both macro-societal and micro-individual levels [4]. Consequently, assessing QOL and well-being should extend beyond solely considering health factors. Instead, it should adopt a holistic approach with a comprehensive perspective [1, 3].

Human beings universally strive for optimal QOL and well-being, foundational pillars essential for societal flourishing. Governments and non-governmental organizations invest significant humanitarian efforts in enhancing people’s QOL and well-being across diverse domains [5, 6]. This collective endeavour underscores the recognition of QOL and well-being as fundamental aspects of human existence, influencing individual experiences and societal advancement alike. Amidst the considerable emphasis placed on QOL and well-being, individuals may contemplate how to discern and quantify whether their lives meet an ‘excellent’ standard or fall short thereof.

The Significant Quality of Life Measure (SigQOLM) is a validated scale designed to assess a broad spectrum of quality of life and well-being. This questionnaire comprises four elements: “Health”, “Relationships”, “Functional activities”, and “Survival”, developed based on 18 domains measured by 69 items [1]. In essence, individuals with excellent overall quality of life exhibit optimal health conditions, maintain close relationships with others and their spiritual beliefs, engage in functional activities, and possess survival capabilities.

SigQOLM assesses individuals’ perceptions of their QOL and well-being, encompassing both health and non-health aspects of daily living over the past two weeks. Respondents are asked to rate the frequency of certain events in their lives during this period using an ordinal scale based on a five-point Likert scale: “Never (4 marks)”, “Seldom (3 marks)”, “Sometimes (2 marks)”, “Normally (1 mark)”, and “Always (0 marks)” [7]. A higher score for each item indicates better QOL and well-being. The description of SigQOLM is summarized in a paper published in 2023 [1].

An important consideration when administering SigQOLM among participants is the potential inclusion of a substantial number of elderly individuals or patients with varying degrees of medical conditions. Due to the extensive number of items, respondents may require more than 20 min to answer all questions. A questionnaire with numerous items can prolong completion time, posing challenges for some patients and elderly individuals in responding to SigQOLM items. This may result in invalid responses due to hasty answers without proper consideration and evaluation of each item by respondents. Additionally, a high number of missing values is likely to occur, causing frustration for researchers as items may not be adequately responded to [7, 8].

While many items are necessary to measure a holistic quality of life, considering time and convenience factors, the development of a shorter version of SigQOLM is imperative. Therefore, the present study aims to develop a condensed version of SigQOLM while preserving its initial framework, consisting of four elements and 18 domains. Subsequently, this study conducts a validation study on the revised version of SigQOLM (SigQOLM-36) to assess its reliability and validity.

Methods

Items selection

Both content and face validity were previously conducted and approved in the earlier study [1]. Since this study utilized secondary data, it is unnecessary to repeat these processes. Two items were selected within each domain based on content validity, supported by excellent factor loading in the original version. The final version of SigQOLM-36 is finalized when the results of Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) are satisfactory.

Field-testing of SigQOLM-36

This study utilized secondary data obtained from a prior study that developed SigQOLM. Respondents were recruited from two government health facilities, resulting in a total of 406 records for analysis. The study design details are outlined in a previous publication [1].

Sample size planning

The guideline for determining the sample size is based on recommendations from a previous study [9]. This current study aims to develop a shorter version of SigQOLM (SigQOLM-36), which currently consists of 69 items. Two items were selected for each domain, and the statistical analyses used to validate SigQOLM-36 items followed the methods introduced in a previous study [1]. Therefore, the determination of the minimum sample size requirement relies on a rule of thumb for EFA. Given that the maximum number of items in a particular domain is 18 (e.g., the Health element), the rule of thumb suggests a 10:1 ratio for sample size determination. Consequently, a minimum sample size of 18 × 10 = 180 respondents would be required [10, 11]. By incorporating an additional allowance of 20.0% to cater for the possibility of non-response rate, the sample size requirement is now inflated to 225 respondents. Such sample size is also sufficient for conducting confirmatory factor analysis [12].

Statistical analysis

The primary objective of this study was to develop a condensed version of SigQOLM capable of assessing a holistic measure of QOL and well-being. In a previous study, SigQOLM demonstrated excellent construct validity in each domain. An EFA was conducted to retain only two items in each domain. Selection criteria included items with outstanding factor loadings and supported by content validity. EFA utilized Maximum Likelihood as the factor extraction method, coupled with the Varimax rotation method for data analysis [1]. To maintain SigQOLM framework, the analysis had forced the factor solution to mimic original SigQOLM framework, with nine factor solutions for “Health” and three factor solutions for “Functional Activities,” “Relationship,” and “Survival,” respectively.

Internal consistency was measured using both Cronbach’s Alpha test and McDonald’s omega. Following the successful construction of SigQOLM-36, the overall model fit was assessed using CFA. Various indicators, including the Chi-square test (< 3.0), Root Mean Square Error approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.9), Tucker Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.9) were estimated [1, 13]. All analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2011. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and RStudio Team (2020) (RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA).

Ethical and regulatory considerations/informed consent

This study utilized secondary data from a previous study that developed SigQOLM, and written informed consent were obtained from each of the study participants prior to their participation in the study. The original SigQOLM study received approval from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC), and the publication of its results was also sanctioned by the Director-General of the Ministry of Health, Malaysia. The ethical approval number for this study is NMRR ID-21–01979-XDL (IIR).

Results

A total of 406 study respondents participated in the survey and the characteristics on the study sample was presented in a previous study. The majority of respondents were aged between 18 and 35 years (53.0%), Malay (34.0%), college graduates holding either a certificate or diploma (65.3%), married (68.0%), and working in the nursing profession (63.3%) [1].

Validity of the revised version of SigQOLM with 36 items

The results indicate that SigQOLM-36 was successfully validated through both EFA and CFA. SigQOLM-36 maintains the previous framework, comprising four major elements with eighteen domains, but now with a total of 36 items. The construct for the “Health” element was determined to consist of nine domains with a minimum factor loading of 0.547. Additionally, each of the “Survival,” “Relationship,” and “Activities” elements was found to comprise three domains, with minimum factor loadings of 0.611, 0.621, and 0.747, respectively. All the items fall exactly in their respective domains without any cross factor loading. The overall model fit of the SigQOLM-36 framework, including all four major domains, was deemed excellent based on the assessment of three model fit indicators: Chi-square test < 0.30, RMSEA < 0.80, SRMR < 0.08, CFI > 0.9, and TLI > 0.9. Reliability

In the pilot study, the Kappa agreement for each item was reported to be at least 0.700, and the internal consistency of the SigQOLM-36 was excellent, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.952. The internal consistency of SigQOLM-36 in the field-test also exhibited excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.923. Specifically, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the Health, Activities, Relationship, and Survival elements were 0.879, 0.779, 0.736, and 0.794, respectively. Additionally, the minimum corrected item-to-total correlation was found to be 0.241. For McDonald’s omega, the coefficients ranged from 0.562 to 0.991, indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency (refer to Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that SigQOLM-36 is reliable and valid, akin to the original SigQOLM with 69 items. A major strength of SigQOLM-36 is its reduced number of items which addresses concerns about lengthy questionnaires being less likely to be completed. Studies have shown that shorter questionnaires tend to yield higher response rates [8, 14–18]. Response rate, defined as the proportion of respondents who answer a question compared to those recruited in the study, is crucial for determining the quality and generalizability of survey responses [19–21].

SigQOLM-36’s streamlined format not only enhances patient convenience but also promotes its use across diverse respondent groups. The authors recommend applying SigQOLM-36 in future research to obtain a more comprehensive measure of QOL across various study populations, including specific demographic or disease-based groups [22]. While the original SigQOLM offers detailed information for each domain, SigQOLM-36 provides a practical option, particularly for specific patient groups such as geriatric patients, as it requires less time to complete, thereby expediting the recruitment process.

Moreover, SigQOLM-36 has the potential for global use. Therefore, conducting validation studies in various languages is necessary. Despite challenges in deriving similar constructs across different populations and ethnicities, this study has taken steps to ensure high construct validity in the initial development of SigQOLM-36, facilitating future validation studies. Therefore, future research aiming to validate SigQOLM-36 should adhere to similar methods and statistical analyses proposed in this study to ensure comparable results and maintain the questionnaire’s original framework [7, 23, 24].

For the original SigQOLM, the EFA utilized Maximum Likelihood as the factor extraction method, coupled with the Varimax rotation method for data analysis. The factor solution should follow the original SigQOLM framework. Therefore, for future studies, researchers need to set nine factor solutions for “Health” and three factor solutions for “Functional Activities,” “Relationship,” and “Survival,” respectively in the extraction method. Setting the factor solution to be based purely on Kaiser’s greater-than-one rule is discouraged since it is necessary to maintain the original framework of SigQOLM. Previously, the framework, dimensions, domains, and items were validated based on content validity, EFA and model fit [1].

In the first-order model, the domains were assumed as they were not necessarily expected to be correlated. This assumption was supported by content validity and the distinct conceptual roles of each domain. For instance, sleep quality may not necessarily be related to body image, even though both are meaningful aspects of quality of life. In contrast, the second-order model was developed based on the hypothesis that all elements (health, relationship, functional activities, and survival) could be interrelated, collectively representing a holistic construct of quality of life and well-being.

From a theoretical standpoint, both assumptions are reasonable, as the nature of the relationships among these elements may vary depending on the characteristics of the population studied. The findings of this study supported the hypothesis that the four elements were correlated, thereby reinforcing the view that these domains together reflect a comprehensive and integrated model of quality of life and well-being. Nonetheless, it is acknowledged that the strength and pattern of these relationships may differ across populations, and future studies are encouraged to validate this framework in different cultural and clinical contexts. Overall, the assumptions underlying both models were guided by theoretical reasoning and empirical evidence. Most importantly, statistical results should be carefully interpreted with sound reasoning and proper justification rather than merely relying on p-values [25].

The quality of life and well-being are deeply intertwined with the concept of happiness, forming a complex relationship that defines human fulfilment. Quality of life encompasses various factors such as physical health, emotional stability, social connections, and economic security. When these aspects are in harmony, they collectively contribute to a person’s overall sense of satisfaction and joy. Well-being, on the other hand, is a broader term that includes not only the absence of illness but also satisfaction with life beyond health matter [1, 3]. Together, these elements create a holistic view of happiness that goes beyond mere momentary pleasures to include long-term contentment and fulfilment.

Happiness is often regarded as the ultimate goal of human existence, with achieving a high quality of life and well-being being essential to attaining this state. However, happiness is a subjective experience, making it challenging to evaluate. To address this, researchers have developed standardized scales to measure happiness, QOL, and well-being [1, 3]. Since happiness, QOL, and well-being are interrelated, the SigQOLM-36 can be applied as a quick scale to assess various factors contributing to overall satisfaction in these domains. By utilizing a standardized measure such as SigQOLM-36, policymakers and health professionals can implement strategies to enhance quality of life and well-being, ultimately fostering greater happiness within communities.

The scoring mechanism for SigQOLM-36 mirrors that of SigQOLM, with each item measured on a five-point Likert scale: Never = 4, Seldom = 3, Sometimes = 2, Normally = 1, and Always = 0 [26]. All items are treated with the same weightage and this concept also applies in other QOL scales [3, 7, 27]. Summation of scores provides an overall assessment of SigQOLM-36, its four elements, and 18 domains. Thus, a higher score indicates better QOL and well-being, as illustrated in Table 3 for easy calculation.

This study has limitation since it used secondary data from original data that developed SigQOLM in the analysis. Thus, future study may need to retest the reliability and validity based on a larger population such as in public health survey. However, the successful validation of SigQOLM-36, with its excellent level of reliability and validity, along with a good model fit, marks a significant achievement. This condensed version of SigQOLM, comprising four elements and 18 domains measured by only 36 items, has undergone rigorous validation through both exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis (Supplementary file 1). Given the robustness of its validation, it is now recommended that researchers conduct future studies to validate SigQOLM-36 in various other languages. This will enhance its applicability and usability across diverse populations and cultural contexts, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of quality of life and well-being worldwide.

Conclusion

The SigQOLM-36 preserves the foundational structure of the original SigQOLM framework, encompassing four key elements and 18 distinct domains. Despite its concise format of just 36 items, this instrument has undergone rigorous validation processes, demonstrating outstanding reliability and validity. Additionally, it has achieved a strong model fit, reinforcing its robustness. Beyond maintaining the construct, the revised scale is designed to maximize efficiency and minimize the burden of data collection. Consequently, the SigQOLM-36 serves as an time-effective tool for assessing the quality of life and well-being of individuals.
