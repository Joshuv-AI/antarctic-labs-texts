Procedural Sequence in Simultaneous TAVR and PBMV
Authors: Xingwei He, Nan Hu, Hu Zhao, Lei Zheng, Qiang Zhou, Hesong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombined aortic stenosis (AS) and mitral stenosis (MS) present a challenging multivalvular disease scenario. Simultaneous transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) has emerged as a potential option for selected high-risk patients, but the optimal procedural sequence remains unclear. We describe 3 patients with severe AS and MS who underwent combined TAVR and PBMV following heart team evaluation, with a focus on procedural sequencing. Postprocedural hemodynamics improved immediately, and short-term outcomes were satisfactory. Our experience suggests that a TAVR-first strategy may provide superior hemodynamic stability and facilitate more accurate assessment of PBMV endpoints in selected patients with combined AS and MS.Key words: aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis, percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty, procedural sequence, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Case 1

A 73-year-old man was admitted with a 10-year history of exertional dyspnea that had worsened over the past 6 months. He had severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (GOLD stage III) requiring long-term oxygen therapy at home, with evidence of secondary pulmonary hypertension (estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 55 mm Hg on echocardiography). He also had chronic kidney disease stage 3b (baseline serum creatinine: 1.6 mg/dL; estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]: 38 mL/min/1.73 m2). He had no history of hypertension, diabetes, or coronary artery disease. On physical examination, a grade III/6 coarse systolic ejection murmur and a moderate mid-diastolic rumbling murmur were heard at the aortic area; a moderate diastolic rumbling murmur was audible at the mitral area. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed markedly thickened mitral and aortic valve leaflets with severely restricted opening. The mitral and aortic valve areas were 0.9 cm2 and 0.61 cm2, respectively, with a peak aortic velocity of 3.84 m/s (peak gradient: 60 mm Hg; mean gradient: 42 mm Hg), and there was severe aortic regurgitation in diastole. Computed tomography angiography showed a tricuspid aortic valve with an annular circumference of 75.1 mm and area of 437.6 mm2; the left and noncoronary cusps were calcified and fused. The diagnosis was rheumatic heart disease with severe aortic stenosis (AS) (accompanied by severe regurgitation) and severe mitral stenosis (MS). Preoperatively, the patient was NYHA functional class III, and the EuroSCORE II was 9.16%. This high score, driven by the combination of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with oxygen dependence, significant pulmonary hypertension, poor renal function, and frailty, indicated a prohibitive risk for conventional double-valve surgery with sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass. After heart team discussion and patient consent, we proceeded with a single-session transcatheter double-valve intervention.

Intraoperatively, percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty (PBMV) was performed first using a 22-mm balloon catheter to dilate the mitral valve. Immediate transesophageal echocardiography revealed moderate mitral regurgitation (MR), so mitral balloon dilation was halted, and attention turned to transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). The aortic valve was predilated with a 22-mm balloon, and then a 27-mm self-expanding VitaFlow aortic prosthesis was implanted (Figure 1). However, the effect of mitral valve dilation was suboptimal, and repeat ultrasound examination at that time showed no MR. Therefore, balloon dilation was performed again. After repeat dilation, intraoperative echo showed the mitral valve area had increased to approximately 1.5 cm2 with no significant regurgitation. The procedure was concluded. The patient experienced no complications and was discharged uneventfully. At 2-year follow-up, his symptoms had markedly improved, daily activities were unrestricted, and his functional status had improved to NYHA functional class I.

Case 2

A 75-year-old man was admitted for intermittent chest pain over 1 year, which had worsened in the last 2 months. His medical history included hypertension, chronic renal insufficiency (baseline serum creatinine: 1.8 mg/dL; eGFR: 33 mL/min/1.73 m2), peripheral artery disease with prior aorto-bifemoral bypass, dermatomyositis requiring long-term low-dose corticosteroids, and a history of surgery for bladder cancer. On examination, a grade III/6 systolic murmur was heard at the right upper sternal border, and a mid-diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex. N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was 2,166 pg/mL. Echocardiography demonstrated a tricuspid aortic valve with thickened, calcified leaflets; peak aortic velocity: 3.78 m/s (peak gradient: 57 mm Hg; mean gradient: 38 mm Hg), continuity-equation area ∼0.36 cm2, indicating low-flow, low-gradient severe AS. The mitral valve area by planimetry was ∼0.9 cm2. Preoperatively, he was NYHA functional class III, and the EuroSCORE II was 11.21%. The heart team considered this score, further compounded by the nonscored risks of chronic immunosuppression and malignancy history, to represent a prohibitive surgical risk, and strongly favored a transcatheter approach. After multidisciplinary evaluation, we recommended a single-session transcatheter double-valve intervention.

First, via transfemoral access, a 27-mm self-expanding VitaFlow aortic valve was deployed. Postdeployment assessment showed the frame well-positioned with only mild paravalvular leak, and the transvalvular pressure gradient was markedly reduced. Next, under transesophageal echo guidance, a transseptal puncture was performed to carry out PBMV. The mitral apparatus was notably calcified; a 26-mm balloon was selected, and after repeated adjustments, balloon dilation was completed with a stepwise increase in diameter over 3 inflations, ultimately achieving a final diameter of 25 mm. Postdilation, mobility of the anterior mitral leaflet was significantly improved. Postprocedure echocardiography revealed that the mitral valve area had increased to approximately 1.5 cm2 with only mild regurgitation. At 6-month follow-up, the patient's symptoms were significantly relieved, his functional status had improved to NYHA functional class II, and no adverse cardiovascular events had occurred (Figure 2).

Case 3

A 66-year-old man presented with 1 month of chest tightness, triggered by exertion and worse in the supine position. His history included hypertension, prior ischemic stroke with mild residual speech disturbance, type 2 diabetes mellitus on insulin therapy (HbA1c: 7.8%), and chronic kidney disease stage 3b (baseline serum creatinine: 1.5 mg/dL; eGFR: 42 mL/min/1.73 m2). On examination, a grade III/6 systolic murmur was heard at the right upper sternal border, and a mid-diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex. Transthoracic echo showed a markedly enlarged left atrium. The mitral leaflets were thickened and fused with restricted opening, with a valve area of ∼1.0 cm2 and only trivial MR. The aortic valve was echogenic and calcified, also with restricted opening: peak aortic velocity: 4.06 m/s (peak gradient: 66 mm Hg; mean gradient: 38 mm Hg), continuity-equation area ∼0.7 cm2, with moderate aortic regurgitation. The diagnosis was rheumatic heart disease with severe MS, severe AS, and moderate aortic insufficiency. Preoperatively, he was NYHA functional class III, and the EuroSCORE II was 6.02%. Although the calculated EuroSCORE II was not markedly elevated, the heart team considered conventional surgery unfavorable because of frailty, multiple comorbidities, impaired functional reserve, and the anticipated high complexity of double-valve surgery. After heart team evaluation and patient consultation, simultaneous transcatheter intervention on both valves was planned.

Under general anesthesia, PBMV was performed first using a 26-mm balloon. After multiple inflations, the mitral orifice opened significantly and left atrial pressure decreased. Then, via transfemoral access, a 30-mm self-expanding VitaFlow aortic valve was successfully implanted. Intraoperative angiography and echocardiography confirmed the prosthesis was optimally positioned with no significant paravalvular leak, and the mitral valve area had increased to ∼2.0 cm2. Postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms were markedly relieved, and he was discharged uneventfully. At 3-year follow-up, his condition remained stable and his functional status improved to NYHA functional class I (Figure 3).

Discussion

AS combined with MS represents one of the most challenging forms of multivalvular disease, encountered in both rheumatic and degenerative contexts.1, 2, 3 Although surgical double-valve replacement remains the guideline-recommended standard, it carries substantial perioperative risk in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities.4,5 In this setting, combined TAVR and PBMV has emerged as a viable alternative for carefully selected high-risk patients.6 The 3 patients in our series (Table 1), aged 66-75 years, had severe dual-valve stenosis and multiple risk factors, and all underwent successful simultaneous transcatheter intervention after heart team deliberation, achieving immediate hemodynamic improvement and satisfactory short-term outcomes. A single-session strategy concurrently relieves both inflow and outflow obstruction, helps stabilize left ventricular (LV) loading conditions, avoids the risk window associated with staged procedures, and reduces healthcare resource utilization.

The optimal sequence of simultaneous TAVR and PBMV remains an unresolved issue. Some operators advocate performing PBMV first, reasoning that lowering left atrial pressure and improving pulmonary congestion can provide more stable conditions for the subsequent TAVR, and that it avoids potential mechanical interaction between the mitral balloon and the newly implanted aortic prosthesis.7,8 Nevertheless, procedural sequencing should be individualized according to patient characteristics, valve anatomy, and intraprocedural hemodynamic considerations. In case 3, PBMV was performed before TAVR because the patient had trivial MR, preserved ventricular function, no severe pulmonary hypertension, and favorable mitral valve morphology, making PBMV relatively predictable and hemodynamically tolerable. This case was treated during the early phase of our experience with combined transcatheter intervention, when the optimal procedural sequence remained uncertain. Although the outcome was favorable, the observations from cases 1 and 2 subsequently led us to increasingly favor a TAVR-first strategy in selected patients. These observations prompted us to consider whether a TAVR-first strategy might offer several theoretical and practical advantages in selected patients. In severe concomitant AS and MS, MS may paradoxically serve as a protective restriction on LV preload. Abrupt relief of MS before correction of the fixed LV outflow obstruction may therefore destabilize ventricular loading conditions. From a hemodynamic standpoint, the outflow obstruction imposed by severe AS may confer greater hemodynamic instability: if PBMV is performed first, the sudden increase in LV filling while aortic outflow remains severely restricted can cause a rapid rise in LV pressure, potentially precipitating acute pulmonary edema or circulatory collapse, particularly in patients with limited LV reserve.9,10 Furthermore, performing TAVR first facilitates a more reliable assessment of PBMV endpoints. When severe AS is still present, elevated LV pressure can cause overestimation of the severity of MR during mitral balloon dilation, which may lead to premature termination of PBMV. Once the AS is relieved and LV pressure declines, the degree of MR is often reclassified downward. This phenomenon was clearly observed in our case 1: PBMV was initially interrupted because of marked MR, but after TAVR and normalization of LV pressure, repeat echocardiography showed no significant MR, permitting completion of the mitral dilation. Thus, a TAVR-first strategy may offer superior intraprocedural hemodynamic stability, prevent pseudo-MR-driven procedural decisions, and enable more accurate endpoint assessment during PBMV.

Simultaneous dual-valve intervention demands meticulous planning. Preprocedural Wilkins score evaluation and precise computed tomography angiography-based annular sizing are essential for patient selection. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography is critical for guiding transseptal puncture and providing real-time assessment of procedural efficacy. When a TAVR-first strategy is adopted, the aortic prosthesis should be implanted at an appropriate depth to avoid potential interference from the mitral balloon during subsequent inflations.

This case series is limited by its small sample size and the lack of long-term follow-up. Larger studies are required to validate these observations and to establish standardized procedural protocols.
