Treatment patterns in relapsed/refractory marginal zone lymphoma: real-world evidence from seven countries
Authors: Harshali Patel, Lynn Huynh, Patrick Squires, Jennifer Prescott, Katherine Ryland, Maryaline Catillon, Tracy Guo, Kelvin Tamakloe, Steven Liu, Mei Sheng Duh, Monika Raut, Craig A. Portell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGiven the rarity of marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), real-world data on relapsed/refractory (R/R) MZL are limited.MethodsThis retrospective panel-based chart review study examined patient characteristics and treatment patterns among patients with R/R MZL who received a second or third line (2L or 3L) of therapy between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2021 in 7 countries. Treatment patterns were summarized overall and by MZL type.ResultsThe study included 526 patients, 513 with 2L and 13 with 3L as index treatment. The median age of 2L patients was 67.0 years. Most patients had stage III or IV disease and intermediate or high-risk prognostic scores. Around 60.0% of the patients had nodal, 22.8% had splenic, and 14.2% had mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) MZL. Most patients received chemoimmunotherapy (42.8%), Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi; 25.1%), or lenalidomide and rituximab/obinutuzumab (16.3%) in 2L. Treatment patterns varied across subtypes, with notable differences in 2L BTKi use.ConclusionsThese findings suggest variability and limitations in the treatment selection for R/R MZL beyond frontline therapy, with the choice of treatment mostly limited to chemoimmunotherapy and BTKi. This study provides much-needed real-world evidence that can help guide clinical management and the development of novel therapies.

Introduction

Marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) is an indolent neoplasm of lymphoid tissues with variable clinical presentation, accounting for approximately 7% of all cases of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1]. Comparative epidemiologic data for MZL from different regions of the world are scarce, but the incidence of lymphomas including NHL is reportedly higher in Western countries than in Asia [2–4]. There are three biologically distinct subtypes of MZL including extranodal MZL (ENMZL) of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT; 50%–70% of cases) and splenic (20%) and nodal (10%) subtypes [5]. These subtypes differ in terms of their clinical presentation, underlying etiology, treatment approach, and patterns of relapse [6]. MZL has orphan disease status with a variable geographic incidence. The age-standardized incidence per 100,000 ranges from 0.5 in Australia to 2.0–4.3 in the United States (US) and 2.6 in the United Kingdom (UK) [1,7]. Although the prognosis of MZL is generally favorable, with a 5-year relative survival rate from the time of diagnosis of ~70%–96% across subtypes [1,8–11], patients with disease progression within 2 years after initial treatment (18%–27%) have poor prognosis, with a 3-year overall survival (OS) rate of just 53% from the time of diagnosis and 83% from the initiation of first line (1L) treatment [12,13].

While most patients with MZL achieve good outcomes with frontline treatments, current advancements with novel therapies for relapsed or refractory (R/R) MZL are limited [12,14,15]. Often localized therapy, such as radiation, is employed for front-line, early stage patients, particularly of the ENMZL subtype. Frontline systemic treatments for more advanced stage MZL differ according to disease subtype but the most frequently used regimens are anti-CD20 antibodies (rituximab or obinutuzumab) monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy (i.e., chemoimmunotherapy) [16,17]. Relapsed, advanced-stage, non-transformed MZL that is asymptomatic can be managed with a watchful waiting approach; systemic treatments in second and later lines of therapy for symptomatic MZL include single-agent rituximab, rituximab plus chemotherapy, and lenalidomide plus rituximab [16–18]. Agents targeting the underlying disease mechanisms such as the Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi) are also recommended treatments for R/R MZL [16,18]; and B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2) inhibitors [19] as well as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy [20] and bispecific T-cell engagers [21] are under active investigation.

Given the rarity of MZL, there are limited data from clinical trials and real-world studies on the clinical burden and contemporary management of R/R MZL, but this information is critical for optimizing treatment algorithms for this heterogeneous and rare disease. In this context, the present panel-based chart review study conducted in 7 Western countries (US, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and UK) examined patient characteristics and treatment patterns of patients with R/R MZL.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective longitudinal global physician panel-based chart review study. The index date was defined as the initiation of a second or third line of therapy (2L or 3L, respectively) between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2021. The baseline period was the 12-month period prior to the index date. The observation period was the time from the index date to the earliest of death, loss to follow-up, or date of chart abstraction (between July and September, 2023).

Data source

Data from 7 countries were collected through Medefield America Limited LLC, a physician panel vendor. An online electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) was used to collect anonymized information from patients’ medical charts. Each physician completed the eCRF for 1 to 3 patient(s). A randomization technique for patient selection was employed to mitigate selection bias of patients and time periods. The study was double-blinded (i.e., physicians were blinded to the study sponsor and the study team was blinded to participating physicians). Quality assessments of the eCRF were conducted to ensure high data quality at the point of entry.

The study protocol was approved by a central Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to start of data collection (Pearl IRB, IN, USA). Analyses were performed using only retrospective medical records; no patient-identifiable information was collected, and data were fully anonymized in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 in the US and the General Data Protection Regulation in the European Union.

Study population

Eligible physicians were oncologists, hematologists, or hemato-oncologists who had completed specialty training and were practicing physicians. All physicians had access to complete medical records for at least 1 patient with R/R MZL who initiated 2L or 3L therapy for MZL between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2021.

The overall study population consisted of adult patients with physician-documented diagnosis of R/R MZL who initiated 2L or 3L during the study period. All eligible patients had complete medical information for all treatments received since their diagnosis of MZL, and had at least 12 months of follow-up time since the index date unless they had died. Patients were excluded from this study if they had another primary malignancy (except for non-melanoma skin cancer or cervical cancer) within the past 5 years prior to MZL diagnosis, hepatitis B infection, central nervous system disease, or clinically significant gastrointestinal abnormalities that could alter absorption.

Measures and outcomes

R/R MZL was defined as either relapsed disease (i.e., disease progression while in remission following the most recent therapy) or refractory disease (i.e., failure to achieve complete response or partial response with the most recent therapy). The characteristics of participating physicians (e.g., medical specialty, number of years in practice) were described. Patient demographics (e.g., age, sex), clinical characteristics (e.g., Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status [ECOG-PS] score, laboratory measurements), disease characteristics (e.g., date of diagnosis, date of R/R diagnosis, stage at diagnosis), and mutation status were assessed during the baseline period or on the index date.

Treatment patterns were assessed during the observation period. Treatments received in each of the first three lines of therapy (i.e., 1L to 3L) were described overall and stratified by MZL type. Time to next treatment (TTNT) was defined as the time from initiation of 2L or 3L to initiation of the next line of therapy. Patients without a next line of therapy were censored at the earlier of death or date of last recorded follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included means (standard deviation [SD]) and median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and frequency and proportion for categorical variables. Alluvial plots were used to describe treatment sequences observed in the first three lines of therapy (i.e., 1L to 3L). Treatment sequences were summarized descriptively overall and stratified by MZL type. Time-to-event outcomes (i.e., TTNT) were described using Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves; median TTNT and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The frequency and proportion of patients without a next line of therapy at 12, 24, and 36 months and their corresponding 95% CIs were also reported.

Results

Characteristics of physicians

A total of 268 physicians (Italy, N = 58; US, N = 49; France, N = 45; Spain, N = 41; Germany, N = 31; UK, N = 31; Canada, N = 13) extracted medical charts for 526 patients with R/R MZL in this study. The majority of physicians (92.2%) were hemato-oncologists or hematologists and 63.4% had been practicing for at least 15 years. Most physicians practiced in academic hospitals, in medium-sized or large practices. On average, each physician saw approximately 30 patients with R/R MZL per year.

Characteristics of the study population

Treatment patterns

Treatment patterns were analyzed in all 526 patients with 2L or 3L treatment as their index line of therapy.

In the 1L setting, treatment was dominated by chemoimmunotherapy (64.6%), followed by single-agent rituximab/obinutuzumab (24.5%), and BTKi (3.0%) (Table 2 and Figure 1). More than one-third of patients (32.1%) received maintenance treatment regardless of their main 1L treatment type. In 2L, most patients either received another round of chemoimmunotherapy (42.8%), or switched to novel targeted therapies, including BTKi (25.1%) and lenalidomide in combination with rituximab/obinutuzumab (16.3%) (Table 3 and Figure 1). Over a median follow-up of 43.1 months, 108 (20.5%) patients received 3L treatment; most of these patients received chemoimmunotherapy in 2L. Among 3L-treated patients, BTKi use increased to 36.1%, followed by lenalidomide and rituximab/obinutuzumab (15.7%), and chemoimmunotherapy (13.9%). Among the remaining 418 patients (79.5%) without a 3L therapy, 197 (47.3%) received 2L maintenance or consolidation treatments and 221 (52.9%) received no further treatments. Among 132 patients already treated with BTKi in 2L, 23 (17.4%) proceeded to 3L treatment. The 3L treatments were heterogeneous, with the most common being chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (26.1%), single agent CD20 monoclonal antibody (17.4%), lenalidomide and rituximab/obinutuzumab (17.4%), and chemoimmunotherapy (8.7%). In the stratified analysis by MZL type (Table 4), chemoimmunotherapy, BTKi, and lenalidomide in combination with rituximab/obinutuzumab remained the most common 2L treatments across all subtypes. Notably, BTKi use was more frequent among patients with nodal MZL, with 29.9% receiving this therapy relative to 16.4% of patients with splenic MZL and 16.0% with MALT MZL. Discussion

As MZL is a rare disease, there is limited information on the characteristics and management of real-world patients with R/R MZL. This chart review study contributes to this gap by providing detailed treatment and demographic data from patients across 7 countries, including the US, Canada, and several European nations. The findings highlight the variability and limitations in treatment selection beyond frontline therapy, underscoring the ongoing needs for novel agents with distinct mechanisms of action.

This study provides a detailed description of a contemporary and international patient cohort with R/R MZL. The patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly similar to those of other R/R MZL patient populations in the US (e.g., age >60 years and the majority of patients with ECOG-PS score of 0 or 1 and stage III or IV disease). The most common disease subtype among our patients was nodal, accounting for 60% of cases. Nodal MZL has been reported as a rare subtype at diagnosis [1,15]. The high proportion of patients with nodal MZL in this chart review study may be explained by the focus on R/R patients as patients with nodal MZL are prone to repeated relapses, unlike with the other subtypes [22]. Findings from the stratified treatment pattern analysis also support this explanation. While overall 2L treatment choices were consistent across subtypes, BTKi use was nearly twice as common in patients with nodal MZL relative to those with splenic or MALT MZL. This suggests that nodal MZL, which may follow a more aggressive or treatment-resistant course, is more likely to be managed with targeted therapies, such as BTKi, in the relapsed setting. These findings also highlight the importance of subtype-specific considerations in treatment planning, and the need to better understand how disease biology influences therapeutic decision-making in MZL.

The treatment patterns observed in this study were in line with current US clinical practice guidelines for R/R MZL, which recommend chemoimmunotherapy, antibody/immunomodulator combinations, and BTKi in second and later lines of therapy [23]. Chemoimmunotherapy was the most common treatment in 1L (65%) and 2L (43%), similar to reports from a registry-based study conducted in Germany (2009–2014) [24] and an administrative database study from Canada (2005–2018) [25]. The continued reliance on chemoimmunotherapy in 2L, often following prior exposure in 1L, suggests a lack of diverse treatment options in the relapsed setting. Although BTKi use increased in 2L and became the most frequently used regimen in 3L (36%), the overall treatment landscape remained narrow, with repeated use of similar therapeutic classes across lines. Fewer than 20% of patients in this study received 3L treatments during follow-up. While some patients may not have required additional treatment after 2L due to disease stability or watchful waiting strategies, the high proportion receiving no further therapy also suggests that many may have lacked viable or tolerable options. The heterogeneity of 3L regimens among patients previously treated with BTKi in 2L further highlights the absence of a defined treatment pathway in the relapsed setting. Despite receiving a targeted agent earlier in their treatment course, these patients did not transition to a consistent next-line option. Instead, treatment choices in 3L varied widely, highlighting the complexity of treatment selection and the influence of individual patient factors, physician preferences, and treatment availability. The absence of consistent treatment patterns and the reliance on previously used regimens underscore a treatment plateau where many patients may be left without viable next steps.

Given this limited therapeutic diversity, BTKi represent one of the few targeted approaches currently available for R/R MZL. BTK is an effector of B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling that is hyperactivated in B-cell NHL, including MZL [26]. BTKi are a recommended treatment for MZL in the 1L and R/R settings [17,23]. In the present study, BTKi (mainly ibrutinib, but also the second-generation BTKi zanubrutinib and acalabrutinib) were used by 25% of patients in 2L and were the most frequently used treatment in 3L (36% of patients). Ibrutinib was approved by the FDA for the treatment of R/R MZL in 2017 [27]; zanubrutinib was approved in 2021 [28]; and acalabrutinib has been investigated in phase II trials but has not yet received approval [29]. Studies are currently ongoing to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of zanubrutinib for the R/R MZL in real-world settings [30,31]. While early real-world evidence for R/R MZL treatment with ibrutinib showed a comparable overall response rate to clinical trials (58%) [32], which was higher in frontline settings (83%) [33], in December 2023, the approval of ibrutinib for this indication was withdrawn based on a lack of demonstrated clinical benefit in post-approval studies [34]. Additionally, the 2021 approval of umbralisib [35]—an inhibitor of PI3K, which acts downstream of BCR signaling—for the treatment of MZL was withdrawn in 2022 over concerns of toxicity [36]. The withdrawal of these agents has left a void in the treatment landscape for R/R MZL, particularly for those who were no longer candidates for chemoimmunotherapy.

Nonetheless, therapies targeting BCR signaling are still being actively investigated in trials and early results are promising. The phase III MAHOGANY trial is evaluating the efficacy and safety of zanubrutinib + rituximab vs. lenalidomide + rituximab with PFS as the primary efficacy endpoint in patients with R/R MZL [37–39]. In the phase II BRUIN study of the non-covalent BTKi pirtobruinib, patients with R/R MZL had an ORR of 50% (59%, 31%, and 67% for the nodal, splenic, and extranodal subtypes, respectively); among patients with prior covalent BTKi treatment, the ORR was 46% [40]. However, the duration of response was 12.7 months and 72% of these patients discontinued due to disease progression or adverse events. Another non-covalent BTKi, nemtabrutinib, has demonstrated clinical efficacy and safety in phase I and II trials of B-cell NHL, including MZL [41]. Finally, in an ongoing phase II study of the next-generation PI3Kδ inhibitor parsaclisib in R/R MZL, the ORR was 58%; but treatment-emergent adverse events resulted in discontinuation in 29% of patients [42]. The survival data for these studies are not yet mature, and will provide additional information regarding the value of these agents for the treatment of R/R MZL. These developments underscore the fragility of the current treatment landscape and support the continued development of novel therapies. A new generation of BTKi may help address this unmet need and expand the limited options available to patients who progress beyond early-line therapy.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that in contrast to previous real-world investigations, which focused on a specific treatment or were conducted at a single geographic location, we examined data for over 500 patients with R/R MZL in multiple countries from 2014 to 2021, providing comprehensive and recent evidence on this patient population. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, in this retrospective observational study, the identification of patients, disease characteristics, and treatments were limited by the availability and accuracy of patient charts and data collection procedures. Outcome assessments in routine clinical practice are not standardized and may vary across countries and over time. The use of a standardized, pilot-tested eCRF with pre-defined variable definitions and data validation checks mitigated this concern. Furthermore, the treatment landscape for MZL is rapidly evolving, especially with the therapeutic potential of highly selective non-covalent BTKis, including pirtobrutinib and nemtabrutinib, and the covalent BTKi orelabrutinib, which was approved in China for the treatment of R/R MZL in April 2023 [43]. These new therapies were not assessed in the present study but warrant investigation in future work. Moreover, the level of detail in the recording of variables of interest may have varied across countries; quality assurance procedures were therefore implemented to ensure data integrity. In addition, to protect physician and patient anonymity, the collected data could not be audited against medical records and the accuracy of data entries could not be verified. While the present study focuses on evaluating treatment patterns and TTNT rather than formal response-based endpoints, variability in response and progression is another potential limitation of retrospective multinational chart review studies. To ensure consistency in this study, R/R MZL was prespecified in the study protocol and incorporated into the eCRF used for chart abstraction. In addition, the eCRF included logic and data-validation checks. Chart abstraction was performed by practicing oncologists/hematologists/hemato-oncologists, and a majority of participating physicians had at least 15 years of practice experience. Nevertheless, response/progression assessments in routine clinical practice may vary across countries and over time. Accordingly, any heterogeneity in response assessment is most relevant to cohort classification and to the clinical decision-making underlying treatment transitions, rather than to a response endpoint reported in this study. In addition, disease stage is a clinically important factor in MZL, as early-stage and advanced stage disease can differ in presentation, management and clinical outcomes. Stage-stratified analyses were not performed in this study and represent an important area for future research. Finally, the generalizability of the results may be limited, for example if MZL-treating physicians who participated in the chart review fundamentally differed from those who did not, which is a limitation common to all chart review studies.

Conclusion

This study was among the first and largest real-world studies to comprehensively assess the characteristics and treatment patterns of patients with different MZL subtypes in North America and Europe. The findings reveal a limited set of therapeutic options beyond second-line treatment, with many patients receiving repeated regimens or discontinuing therapy altogether. These results underscore the need for new, well-tolerated therapies that expand the current treatment landscape. This study provides much-needed real-world evidence on treatment choices that can help guide clinical management and serve as a basis for the development of novel therapies that improve clinical outcomes of patients with R/R MZL.
