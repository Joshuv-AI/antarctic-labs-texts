Galton-Watson processes, simple varieties of trees and Khinchin families
Víctor J. Maciá

Abstract
. In this note, we introduce a unified analytic framework that connects Bienaymé–Galton–Watson processes, simple varieties of trees and Khinchin families. Using Lagrange’s inversion formula, we derive new coefficient-based expressions for extinction probabilities and reinterpret them as boundary phenomena tied to the domain of the inverse of the solution to Lagrange’s equation. This perspective reveals a structural bridge between combinatorial and probabilistic models, simplifying classical arguments and yielding new results. It also leads to a computationally efficient method for simulating Galton–Watson processes via power series coefficients.

1.
Introduction

Power series distributions play a significant role in the study of rooted trees (see, for instance,
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
11
,
18
,
19
]
). Although their primary role has traditionally been that of a tool rather than an object of intrinsic interest, they provide a natural bridge between combinatorial and probabilistic perspectives, offering valuable insights into the theory of random trees. In this note, using Khinchin families and Lagrange’s inversion, we relate the probabilistic behaviour of Galton–Watson processes to the analytic behaviour of the generating function of their combinatorial counterpart, the simple varieties of trees.

To each power series
f\in\mathcal{K}
(see the definition below) with radius of convergence
R>0
we associate a family of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,R)}
, the Khinchin family associated to
f
. For each
t\in(0,R)
the random variable
X_{t}
has mass function

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(X_{t}=n)=\frac{a_{n}t^{n}}{f(t)}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq 1\,.

with
X_{0}\equiv 0
. The theory of Khinchin families examines the behaviour of this family of random variables, and its normalized version, as the parameter
t\uparrow R
. See, for instance,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
.

One of the main results of this note is the following new coefficient-based expression for the probability of extinction of a Galton–Watson process: let
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
be a power series with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
, and let
Y_{t}
be the offspring distribution of our process, then we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle q(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{g(t/\psi(t))}{t}\,,\quad\text{ for any }t\in(0,R_{\psi})

here
A_{n}
is the
n
-th coefficient of the solution of Lagrange’s equation
g(z)
with data
\psi(z)
, these coefficients can be computed in terms of
\psi
by using Lagrange’s inversion formula. We refer the reader to Theorem
2
below for a detailed statement and a proof of this result.

For each fixed
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
, the coefficients
A_{n}
encode a simple variety of trees, that is, the sum of certain fixed weights over all rooted plane trees with
n
nodes, see Proposition
A
below. Specific choices of
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
yield concrete families of combinatorial trees; for instance,
\psi(z)=e^{z}
corresponds to rooted labeled Cayley trees, while
\psi(z)={1}/{(1-z)}
corresponds to rooted plane trees.

It turns out that the radius of convergence of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
is intimately related to the extinction probability of the corresponding Galton–Watson process with offspring distribution
Y_{t}
, marking the critical parameter at which the process shifts from almost sure extinction to positive survival probability. This phenomenon bears a clear resemblance to phase transitions in statistical mechanical models.

For instance if we fix
\psi(z)=e^{z}
and denote
(Y_{t})
the Khinchin family associated to
\psi
, then, for each
t\geq 0
, the random variable
Y_{t}
is a Poisson random variable of parameter
t\geq 0
, and therefore the probability of extinction of the Galton-Watson process with offspring distribution
Y_{t}
is given by the closed formula

\displaystyle q(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{n-1}t^{n-1}}{n!e^{nt}}=\frac{g(t/\psi(t))}{t}\,,\quad\text{ for any }t>0\,.

Here, we simultaneously consider all possible Galton–Watson processes with offspring distribution given by a Poisson random variable with parameter
t>0
. Notice that
q(t)\equiv 1
, for any
t\leq\tau=1
and also that the radius of convergence of
g(z)
is given by
\rho=\tau/\psi(\tau)=1/e
, see Theorem
1
below. When we cross the radius of convergence of
g(z)
, that is, when
t>\tau=1
, the inversion relation
{t}/{\psi(t)}=g^{-1}(t)
no longer holds, and consequently the extinction probability should satisfy the inequality
q(t)<1
. Notice that for
t>1
, the function
t/\psi(t)
is strictly decreasing, see Lemma
4.1
below.

After deriving formula (
1.1
), we analyze the probability of extinction function in detail, establishing its continuity, differentiability (except at one point) and characterizing its asymptotic behavior as the parameter
t\uparrow R_{\psi}
. An explicit upper bound on the extinction probability function is also derived. Moreover, we establish several precise relationships between Khinchin families and random variables arising in Galton–Watson processes. We identify some of these random variables as appropriately reparameterized Khinchin families.

Beyond its theoretical interest, this framework offers an efficient method for simulating Galton-Watson processes by leveraging power series and the numerical computation of coefficients.
We believe that this perspective will be valuable to both combinatorialists and probabilists, as it provides an explicit bridge between these viewpoints.

This note is self-contained and intended to be accessible to researchers with backgrounds in probability, combinatorics, or complex analysis. Section 2 introduces the fundamental concepts of Khinchin families. Section 3 reviews rooted plane trees and Galton–Watson processes. Section 4 recalls Lagrange’s inversion formula and gives a closed form expression for the radius of convergence of the power series that solves Lagrange’s equation. Section 5 provides a combinatorial interpretation of the solution to Lagrange’s equation in terms of weighted rooted plane trees. Finally, Section 6 constitutes the core of this note: it develops the parametric Galton–Watson process, derives explicit coefficient-based formulas for various probabilities, including extinction probabilities, and explores their analytic and probabilistic behaviour.

2.
Khinchin families

In this section, we introduce the key elements of the theory of Khinchin families that are necessary to developed this note. A more comprehensive treatment can be found in
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

]
and more recently, in
[

We denote by
\mathcal{K}
the class of non-constant power series

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}

with positive radius of convergence
R>0
, which have non-negative Taylor coefficients and such that
a_{0}>0
. Since
f\in\mathcal{K}
is non-constant, at least one coefficient other than
a_{0}
is positive.

The
Khinchin family
of such a power series
f\in\mathcal{K}
with radius of convergence
R>0
is the family of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,R)}
with values in
\{0,1,\ldots\}
and with mass functions given by

\mathbf{P}(X_{t}=n)=\frac{a_{n}t^{n}}{f(t)}\,,\quad\mbox{for each $n\geq 0$ and $t\in(0,R)$}\,.

Notice that
f(t)>0
for each
t\in[0,R)
. We define
X_{0}\equiv 0
.
Formally, the definition of
X_{0}
is consistent with the general expression for
t\in(0,R)
, with the convention that
0^{0}=1
, meaning that
\mathbf{P}(X_{0}=0)=1
.

2.1.
Mean and variance functions.

For the mean and variance of
X_{t}
, we reserve the notation

m_{f}(t)=\mathbf{E}(X_{t})
and
\sigma_{f}^{2}(t)=\mathbf{V}(X_{t})
, for
t\in[0,R)
. In terms of the power series
f\in\mathcal{K}
, the mean and the variance of
X_{t}
may be written as

(2.1)

\displaystyle m_{f}(t)=\frac{tf^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}=t\frac{d}{dt}\ln(f(t)),\qquad\sigma_{f}^{2}(t)=tm_{f}^{\prime}(t)\,,\quad\mbox{for $t\in[0,R)$}\,.

Because the variance is always positive, for any
t\in(0,R)
, we have that
m_{f}
is an increasing function. For results about the range of the mean, see
[
3
]
.

2.2.
The classes
\mathcal{K}_{s}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\star}

A power series
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathcal{K}
lies in
\mathcal{K}^{\star}
if

M_{f}:=\lim_{t\uparrow R}m_{f}(t)>1,

so there is a unique
\tau\in(0,R)
(the
apex
) with
m_{f}(\tau)=1
, equivalently
\tau f^{\prime}(\tau)=f(\tau)
.
The existence of the apex prevents
f
from being a polynomial of degree 1. In particular, it implies that the second derivative at
\tau
satisfies
f^{\prime\prime}(\tau)>0
.

If
g(z)=z^{N}f(z)=\sum_{n\geq N}b_{n}z^{n}
, its associated Khinchin family
(Y_{t})
satisfies

\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=n)=\frac{b_{n}t^{n}}{g(t)},\quad n\geq N,

and
Y_{t}\overset{d}{=}X_{t}+N
, where
X_{t}
is the Khinchin family of
f
. The class
\mathcal{K}_{s}
comprises all such shifted power series (with associated shifted Khinchin family). See
[
8
]
, for further details.

3.
Rooted trees

In this section, we introduce basic definitions associated with rooted trees. Additionally, we provide an overview of the basic definitions related to the Galton-Watson processes.

3.1.
The combinatorial class of rooted plane trees

Rooted trees are graphs without cycles, with a distinguished node called the root. A
rooted plane tree
is a rooted tree in which the children of each node are assigned a left-to-right order. Equivalently, it is a rooted tree embedded in the plane, where the relative order of siblings is preserved.

Rooted plane trees can be defined recursively as follows:

•

A single node is a rooted plane tree.

•

If
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
are rooted plane trees, then a new rooted plane tree can be formed by adding a root node and attaching
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
as its ordered children (from left to right).

We denote by
(\mathcal{G},|\cdot|)
the combinatorial class of rooted plane trees, where the size function
|\cdot|
gives the number of nodes in a given tree. This family of rooted trees is codified, in the language of the combinatorial classes, by means of the specification

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{Z}\times\text{SEQ}(\mathcal{G})\,.

This structure assumes that the descendants of the root are ordered as a list of rooted trees. See
[
9
]
[p. 65] for further details.

3.2.
The Polish space of rooted plane trees

We denote by
\mathbb{T}
the set of rooted plane trees in which every node has a finite number of descendants (although the trees themselves may be infinite), see, for instance,
[
1
]
for further details. It is well known that
\mathbb{T}
, endowed with the metric

\delta(t,t^{\prime})=2^{-\sup\{h\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\,:\,r_{h}(t)=r_{h}(t^{\prime})\}},

is a Polish space (see, for instance,
[
1
]
). Here,
r_{h}(t)
denotes the restriction function that gives the tree obtained by cutting
t
at level
h
(i.e., keeping the nodes at level
h
as leaves).

We can express a partition of this space as
\mathbb{T}=\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
. Here,
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}=\mathbb{T}\setminus\mathcal{G}
denotes the set of infinite rooted plane trees, where each node has a finite number of descendants.

3.3.
Galton–Watson processes

A Galton–Watson process begins with a single ancestor whose offspring count
Y
follows the distribution
(p_{k})_{k\geq 0}
; each descendant independently reproduces according to the same law in successive generations, see, for instance,
[
2
]
. If
Z_{n}
denotes the population at generation
n
, then

Z_{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{Z_{n}}Y_{j},

with
Y_{j}
i.i.d. copies of
Y
. Its mean
m=\mathbf{E}[Y]
determines extinction: if
m\leq 1
, extinction occurs almost surely (
q=1
); if
m>1
, the extinction probability
q<1
. The probability
q
is the minimal solution of the equation

q=\sum_{k\geq 0}p_{k}q^{k}.

For further details about these processes see, for instance,
[
2
]
.

4.
Lagrange’s inversion formula

Let
\psi
be a power series in
\mathcal{K}
with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
. Let
g
be the power series which is the (unique) solution of Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
, i.e. satisfying:

(\star)\quad\quad g(z)=z\psi(g(z)).

The following classical result describes an explicit relationship between the coefficients of the power series
\psi
and the solution
g
, thereby providing an explicit power series representation of
g
as a solution to Lagrange’s equation
(\star)
.

Theorem A

(Lagrange’s inversion formula)
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
be a power series with radius convergence
R_{\psi}>0
, and consider the function
g(z)
defined as the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
:

g(z)=z\psi(g(z)).

Then, the coefficients of
g(z)
satisfy the recurrence:

A_{n}=\textsc{coeff}_{n}[g(z)]=\frac{1}{n}\textsc{coeff}_{n-1}[\psi(z)^{n}],\quad\text{for any }n\geq 1,

with the initial condition
A_{0}=0
.

More generally, for any holomorphic power series
H
such that
H(g(z))
is well-defined, we have:

\textsc{coeff}_{n}[H(g(z))]=\frac{1}{n}\textsc{coeff}_{n-1}[H^{\prime}(z)\psi(z)^{n}],\quad\text{for any }n\geq 1.

See, for instance,
[
10
]
for a proof of this result.

The inverse function of
g(z)
is given by
z/\psi(z)
. This will be relevant later on, particularly in connection with Lagrange’s inversion formula. This explicit form of the inverse enables a natural reparametrization of the Khinchin family associated with
g
. This result, as we will see later, is fundamental in the study of the combinatorial rooted trees and the random rooted trees.

We will denote by
(Z_{t})
the Khinchin family associated with
g
, the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
. This Khinchin family will play a relevant role later on. Notice that
g
, the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
, is, by definition, a power series in the class
\mathcal{K}_{s}
and therefore
(Z_{t})
is a shifted Khinchin family.

4.1.
Function
t/\psi(t)

Lagrange’s equation gives that
g
and
z/\psi(z)
are inverse of each other and thus that

g(z/\psi(z))=z\,,\quad\mbox{for $|z|\leq\tau$}\,.

In particular,

g(t/\psi(t))=t\,,\quad\mbox{for $t\in[0,\tau]$}\,.

The image of the interval
[0,\rho]
by
g
is the interval
g([0,\tau/\psi(\tau)])=[0,\tau]
.

For the function
t\mapsto t/\psi(t)
, we have

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
with apex
\tau\in(0,R_{\psi})
, the function
t/\psi(t)

(1)

is strictly increasing on the interval
[0,\tau)

(2)

is strictly decreasing on the interval
(\tau,R_{\psi})

(3)

has a maximum at
t=\tau
.

For
\psi\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the function
t/\psi(t)
is strictly increasing for all
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
.

Proof.

The result follows from the identity

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t}{\psi(t)}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\psi(t)}(1-m_{\psi}(t)),\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,R_{\psi}).

∎

Assume that
\psi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. From the previous lemma we obtain that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
the probability
\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=1)
increases, attains its maximum at
t=\tau
, and then decreases; whereas if
\psi\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the probability
\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=1)
is strictly increasing on
(0,R_{\psi})
.

4.2.
Radius of convergence of
g

Now we study the radius of convergence of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
be a power series with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
. Denote
g
the solution of Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
, that is,

\displaystyle g(z)=z\psi(g(z))\,,

then the radius of convergence of
g
is given by

R_{g}=\sup_{t\in(0,R_{\psi})}\frac{t}{\psi(t)}.

In case that
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
we have
R_{g}=\rho=\tau/\psi(\tau)
.

The radius of convergence of this power series, as we shall see later, is closely related to the behavior of various probabilities associated with a Galton-Watson process.

In the case where
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
and
\textsc{coeff}_{1}[\psi(z)]=b_{1}=1
, the radius of convergence of the solution
g
to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
coincides with the probability.

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(Y_{\tau}=1)=\frac{\tau}{\psi(\tau)}=\rho\,.

More generally: for power series
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, if
\rho=\tau/\psi(\tau)\geq 1
, then
b_{1}\leq 1
. This is equivalent to the claim:
b_{1}\geq 1
implies
\rho\leq 1
.

Note that when
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the Khinchin family
(Z_{t})
associated with the solution of Lagrange’s equation is naturally parametrized by
t\in[0,\rho]
, where
\rho=\tau/\psi(\tau)
. For further details about extending Khinchin families families to boundary values we refer to
[
8
]
.

4.3.
Otter-Meir-Moon asymptotic formula

We now present an asymptotic formula, for the coefficients
A_{n}
of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, originally due to Otter, Meir, and Moon, see
[
15
,
17
]
. For additional extensions, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
, where a proof based on Khinchin families is provided.

Theorem B

(Otter-Meir-Moon,
[
15
,
17
]
)
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
be a power series. Then, for indices
n\equiv 1
,
\mod Q_{\psi}
. the coefficients
A_{n}
of the solution
g
to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
satisfy the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle A_{n}\sim\frac{Q_{\psi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_{\psi}(\tau)}\frac{\psi(\tau)^{n}}{\tau^{n-1}}\frac{1}{n^{3/2}},\quad\text{as }n\to\infty

Here,
\tau
denotes the apex of
\psi
(i.e
m_{\psi}(\tau)=1
)

For any
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the previous asymptotic formula for the coefficients
A_{n}
of the solution to Lagrange’s equation
g
with data
\psi
gives that, for indices
n\equiv 1
,
\mod Q_{\psi}
, we have

\displaystyle A_{n}\rho^{n}\sim\frac{Q_{\psi}\,\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\psi}(\tau)}\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\,,\quad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty\,.

This implies that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the solution
g
extends continuously to the closure of the disk
{\mathbb{D}(0,\rho)}
. In particular,

\lim_{t\uparrow\rho}g(t)=\tau,

so it is natural to set
g(\rho)=\tau
. We will use this relation repeteadly along this note. Observe that here we use that
g
has non-negative Taylor coefficients.

5.
Lagrange’s equation and simple varieties of trees

Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
. A simple variety of trees is a family of weighted rooted plane trees whose weighted generating function is the solution of Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
, that is, is a power series
g
verifying the relation

\displaystyle g(z)=z\psi(g(z))\,.

Alternatively some authors define these simple varieties for power series
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, this is not the case here.

We now relate the simple varieties of trees with the class of rooted plane trees endowed with the weight

\displaystyle\omega(a)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j}^{k_{j}(a)}\,.

Here
k_{j}(a)
counts the number of nodes in
a
with exactly
j
descendants.
This is a standard result, see
[
18
]
for similar arguments.

Proposition A
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
a power series with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
, then

\displaystyle\textsc{coeff}_{n}[g(z)]=A_{n}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\omega(a)

here
g
denotes the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

The proof of this result follows by combining Lagrange’s inversion formula with the multinomial theorem (and counting rooted plane trees with the same list of outdegrees
(k_{0},k_{1},\dots)
); see
[
18
]
for similar arguments.

In a similar way, for a forest with
m
rooted trees and
n
nodes, the
n
-th coefficient of
g(z)^{m}
equals the sum of the weights of all rooted forests with
n
nodes and
m
rooted trees.

6.
The parametric Galton Watson process

Given a power series
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
and Khinchin family
(Y_{t})
, in this section we study the Galton-Watson process with offspring distribution
Y_{t}
. This construction gives rise to a family of parametric Galton-Watson process
T_{t}
which depends on
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
. For each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
the rooted random tree
T_{t}
takes values on the Polish space
\mathbb{T}=\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
.

6.1.
The Galton-Watson process with offspring distribution
Y_{t}

Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{K}
a power series with radius of convergence
R_{\psi}>0
, and denote by
(Y_{t})_{t\in(0,R_{\psi})}
the Khinchin family associated with
\psi
. Throughout this section, we consider these as fixed, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

For each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, we can generate a Galton-Watson process
T_{t}
with offspring distribution
Y_{t}
.
We denote by
(T_{t})_{t\in(0,R_{\psi})}
the parametric family of Galton-Watson processes, where for each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
,
the random tree
T_{t}
has an offspring distribution given by
Y_{t}
. The random variable
T_{t}
takes values in
\mathbb{T}
and has a probability mass function given by:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=j)^{k_{j}(a)}\,,\quad\text{ for any }a\in\mathbb{T}\,,

with the convention that
0^{0}=1
. Recall that
k_{j}(a)
counts the number of nodes in
a
with exactly
j
descendats. This measure assigns probability
0
to the singletons in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}
.

Before moving forward, we prove the following lemma. This result establishes a connection between the probabilistic and combinatorial interpretations of these weighted rooted trees.

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
. Then, for any
n\geq 1
and
a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)=\frac{\omega(a)t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}\,,

with the convention that
0^{0}=1
. Here,
\omega(a)
denotes the weight of the rooted plane tree
a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Proof.

For any
n\geq 1
and
a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=j)^{k_{j}(a)}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{b_{j}t^{j}}{\psi(t)}\right)^{k_{j}(a)}.

Recalling that for a tree
a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the following relations hold:

\displaystyle k_{0}(a)+k_{1}(a)+k_{2}(a)+\dots+k_{n-1}(a)

\displaystyle=n,

\displaystyle k_{1}(a)+2k_{2}(a)+\dots+(n-1)k_{n-1}(a)

\displaystyle=n-1.

These equations express that the tree has
n
nodes and
n-1
edges (or, equivalently,
n-1
nodes that descend from a parent node, we don’t count the root). Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}b_{j}^{k_{j}(a)}\frac{t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{\omega(a)t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}.

∎

6.2.
Probability generating functions

In this section, we study and prove various relations among the probability generating functions of certain variables associated with
T_{t}
and the Khinchin family of
g
, the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

We denote by
|T_{t}|
the total progeny of the random tree
T_{t}
, that is, the number of nodes in the random tree
T_{t}
. We have the following proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

For each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|T_{t}|=n)=\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{n})=\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq 1\,.

Moreover, if
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, then
Z_{t/\psi(t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|T_{t}|
, for all
t\in[0,\tau]
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{n})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\frac{\omega(a)t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}

In the last two equalities we use Lemma
6.1
and Proposition
A
. Recall that
A_{n}
denotes the
n
-th coefficient of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

To prove the second part of the proposition observe that for any
t\in[0,\tau]
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(Z_{t/\psi(t)}=n)=\frac{A_{n}(t/\psi(t))^{n}}{g(t/\psi(t))}=\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq 1\,.

In the last equality we use that for
t\in[0,\tau]
, the function
t/\psi(t)
is the inverse function of
g
, that is, we have the equality
g(t/\psi(t))=t
, for any
t\in[0,\tau]
.
∎

For each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, we let
\psi_{t}(z)=\psi(tz)/\psi(t)
be the probability generating function of the random variable
Y_{t}
; we set
\psi_{0}\equiv 1
which is consistent with
Y_{0}\equiv 0
.

We consider, for each
t\in[0,R_{\psi})
, Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi_{t}
and the corresponding power series solution
g_{t}(z)
:

(6.2)

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)=z\psi_{t}(g_{t}(z)).

The case
t=0
is a degenerate case:
\psi_{0}(z)\equiv 1
and
g_{0}(z)=z
.

Proposition 6.2
.

For
t\in[0,R_{\psi})
we have that

(6.3)

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)=\frac{g(tz/\psi(t))}{t},\quad\quad\mbox{for all $|z|\leq 1$}\,.

The holomorphic function
g_{t}
is continuous on
\partial\mathbb{D}
and satisfies
g_{t}(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}
. Here,
g
denotes the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

The power series
g_{t}(z)
is the probability generating function of
Z_{t/\psi(t)}
whenever either
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
and
t\in[0,\tau]
, or
\psi\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{\star}
and
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
.

Proof.

Solutions to Lagrange’s equation with a given data are unique. Let us check that both power series satisfy the same Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi_{t}
, and therefore, they must be the same power series.

The power series
g_{t}(z)
verifies Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi_{t}
by definition. For any
t\in(0,R)
, denote
f_{t}(z)={g(tz/\psi(t))}/{t}
. Notice that

\displaystyle z\psi_{t}(f_{t}(z))=z\frac{\psi(g(tz/\psi(t)))}{\psi(t)}=\frac{g(tz/\psi(t))}{t}=f_{t}(z)\,.

Here we use that
g
verifies Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
. Therefore, by the uniqueness of solutions, it follows that

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)=\frac{g(tz/\psi(t))}{t}\,,\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,R_{\psi})\text{ and }|z|\leq 1\,.

∎

Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
. Observe that, for any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, the radius of convergence of the power series
g_{t}(z)
is given by

R_{g_{t}}=\frac{\tau/\psi(\tau)}{t/\psi(t)}\geq 1,

and furthermore,
g_{t}(R_{g_{t}})=\tau/t
.

6.3.
Extinction probability

We now analyze the extinction probability function for the parametric Galton-Watson process
T_{t}
and derive explicit formulas for this probability in terms of the coefficients of the solution
g
to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

The remarkable phenomenon occurring here, well known in the literature, is the transition from a discrete probability measure to a mixture of two measures as the parameter
t
varies. Under the present setting, this behavior is reminiscent of a phase transition in statistical mechanics.

For each
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
denote
q(t)
the probability of extinction of the parametric Galton-Watson process
T_{t}
. This probability is given by

\displaystyle q(t)=\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G})\,,\quad\text{ for any }t\in(0,R_{\psi})\,.

We now provide an explicit expression for this probability in terms of the coefficients
A_{n}
of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

Theorem 2
.

The extinction probability of the Bienaymé-Galton-Watson process with offspring distribution
Y_{t}
satisfies the following explicit formula:

(6.4)

q(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{g(t/\psi(t))}{t},\quad\text{ for any }t\in(0,R_{\psi})\,,

where
A_{n}
are the coefficients of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

Proof.

Notice that, for any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(|T_{t}|=n)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}\,,\quad\text{ for any }n\geq 1\,.

Here we apply Proposition
6.1
, together with the fact that the family
\{\mathcal{G}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
forms a countable partition of
\mathcal{G}
.
∎

This formula provides an explicit characterization of extinction probabilities in terms of the coefficients of the solution to Lagrange’s equation with data
\psi
.

An alternative approach to the previous theorem is the following: observe that, for
\psi_{t}(z)=\psi(tz)/\psi(t)
, we have Lagrange’s equation

\displaystyle g_{t}(z)=z\psi_{t}(g_{t}(z))

and therefore
g_{t}(1)=\psi_{t}(g_{t}(1))
. Because the probability of extinction is the smallest fixed point of the probability generating function
\psi_{t}(z)
, then
q(t)=g_{t}(1)
. Observe that since
\psi_{t}(z)
is a probability generating function, it satisfies
\psi_{t}(1)=1
.

In the previous theorem, the most interesting behavior occurs when
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, since in that case a
phase transition
takes place at
t=\tau
.

We now study the properties of the extinction probability
q(t)
as a function of
t\in[0,R_{\psi})
. Our first result states that
q(t)
is a continuous function.

Proposition 6.3
.

The probability of extinction
q(t)
is continuous on the interval
[0,R_{\psi})
.

Proof.

If
\psi\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, we have
q(t)\equiv 1
, for any
t\in[0,R_{\psi})
, which is continuous. Assume that
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
, the function
t/\psi(t)
is continuous for any
t\in[0,R_{\psi})
. Now, combining Theorem
1
with Lemma
4.1
, we find that

\frac{t}{\psi(t)}\leq\frac{\tau}{\psi(\tau)}=\rho=R_{g},\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,R_{\psi}),

and therefore, since the solution
g
to Lagrange’s equation is continuous on the closure of the disk
\mathbb{D}(0,\rho)
, we conclude that

q(t)={g(t/\psi(t))}/{t}

is continuous for all
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
.

For
t=0
, observe that

g(t/\psi(t))=A_{1}\frac{t}{\psi(t)}+o(t)=\psi(0)\frac{t}{\psi(t)}+o(t),\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0,

and therefore we conclude that
q(0)=1
, as expected. Here we apply Lagrange’s inversion formula to obtain
A_{1}=\textsc{coeff}_{0}[\psi(z)]=\psi(0)
.
∎

We now analyze the asymptotic behaviour of
q(t)
, as
t\uparrow R_{\psi}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

If
\lim_{t\uparrow R_{\psi}}\psi(t)/t=+\infty
, then

\displaystyle q(t)\sim\frac{\psi(0)}{\psi(t)}\,,\quad\text{ as }t\uparrow R_{\psi}\,.

Proof.

Theorem
2
gives that

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{g(t/\psi(t))}{t},\quad\text{for any }t\in(0,R_{\psi}),

therefore, using the fact that
g(z)
is continuous on the closure of
\mathbb{D}(0,\rho)
, together with the limit
\lim_{t\uparrow R_{\psi}}t/\psi(t)=0
, we find

(6.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\uparrow R_{\psi}}tq(t)=\lim_{t\uparrow R_{\psi}}g({t}/{\psi(t)})=g(0)=0.

Now, using that
q(t)
is a fixed point of
\psi_{t}(z)=\psi(tz)/\psi(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\psi(tq(t))=q(t)\psi(t),

and hence, using equation (
6.5
), we conclude that

\displaystyle q(t)\sim\frac{\psi(0)}{\psi(t)},\quad\text{as }t\uparrow R_{\psi}.

∎

Remark 7
.

The cases where the ratio
\psi(t)/t
remains bounded, as
t\uparrow R_{\psi}
, are the following:

•

If
R_{\psi}=\infty
, then
\psi(t)
is a polynomial of degree 1. In this case
M_{\psi}=1
and
q(t)\equiv 1
for all
t\geq 0
.

•

If
R_{\psi}<\infty
, then
\lim_{t\uparrow R_{\psi}}\psi(t)<\infty
and the probability
q(t)
can be extended to
t=R_{\psi}
.

\boxtimes

Now we give an upper estimate for the extinction probability.

Lemma 7.1
.

Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
. For any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, the extinction probability satisfies

q(t)\;\leq\;{\tau}/{t}.

Proof.

Recall that
g_{t}(R_{g_{t}})=\tau/t
. Since
g_{t}(z)
has non negative Taylor coefficients, and
R_{g_{t}}\geq 1
for any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, we find that

q(t)=g_{t}(1)\leq g_{t}(R_{g_{t}})=\tau/t,\quad\text{ for any }t\in(0,R_{\psi})\,,

which yields the desired upper bound on the extinction probability. Note that this estimate is meaningful only when
t>\tau
, otherwise
q(t)\equiv 1
.
∎

The function
q(t)
is differentiable for all
t\neq\tau
. The following proposition precisely describes the discontinuity in its derivative at that point.

Theorem 3
.

Fix
\psi\in\mathcal{K}^{\star}
. The extinction probability
q(t)
is differentiable on the interval
(0,R_{\psi})\setminus\{\tau\}
, with a jump discontinuity of its derivative at
t=\tau
. Concretely,

\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{q(t)-1}{t-\tau}=-\frac{2}{\tau},\qquad\lim_{t\uparrow\tau}\frac{q(t)-1}{t-\tau}=0.

Moreover, for all
t\in(0,R_{\psi})\setminus\{\tau\}
, one has the closed‐form for the derivative

q^{\prime}(t)=\frac{q(t)}{t}\left(\frac{1-m_{\psi}(t)}{1-m_{\psi}(tq(t))}-1\right).

Proof.

Observe that
t=\tau
is the unique point at which
q(t)
may fail to be differentiable. By Lemma
4.1
, the function
t/\psi(t)
is strictly decreasing on
(\tau,R_{\psi})
. Hence for all
t>\tau
,

\frac{t}{\psi(t)}<\frac{\tau}{\psi(\tau)},

and since
g
is analytic (and thus differentiable) on
\mathbb{D}(0,\tau/\psi(\tau))
, it follows that

q(t)={g(t/\psi(t))}/{t}

is differentiable for every
t>\tau
. For
t<\tau
we have
q(t)\equiv 1
, then
q(t)
is differentiable. Let’s prove that
q(t)
is not differentiable at
t=\tau
. For the left-limit we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\uparrow\tau}\frac{q(t)-1}{t-\tau}=0.

This conclusion follows directly from the fact that
q(t)\equiv 1
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Now we compute the derivative of
q(t)
, for
t\in(\tau,R_{\psi})
. Using that
g(z)=z\psi(g(z))
we find

(7.1)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\psi(g(z))}{1-z\psi^{\prime}(g(z))}\,,\quad\text{ for any }|z|<\rho\,.

For any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})\setminus\{\tau\}
, combining Theorem
2
, equation (
7.1
) and the identity
\psi(tq(t))=q(t)\psi(t)
we obtain that

(7.2)

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(t)=\frac{q(t)}{t}{\left(\frac{1-m_{\psi}(t)}{1-m_{\psi}(tq(t))}-1\right)},

here we use that
q(t)
is differentiable on
(0,R_{\psi})\setminus\{\tau\}
.

Now we compute the right derivative of
q(t)
, at
t=\tau
. Taylor’s expansion around
t=\tau
gives

1-m_{\psi}(t)=-m_{\psi}^{\prime}(\tau)\,(t-\tau)\,(1+o(1))\,,\quad\text{ as }\quad t\downarrow\tau\,.

Notice that
m_{\psi}^{\prime}(\tau)>0
, the mean
m_{\psi}
is strictly increasing. From the previous asymptotic relation it follows that

(7.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1-m_{\psi}(t)}{1-m_{\psi}(tq(t))}\sim-\frac{(t-\tau)}{(\tau-tq(t))}\,,\quad\text{ as }\quad t\downarrow\tau\,.

Denote
h(t)=\psi(t)/t
, for
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
. This function is invariant under
tq(t)
meaning that
h(tq(t))=h(t)
. Taylor’s expansion around
t=\tau
gives that

\displaystyle h(t)=h(\tau)+(h^{\prime\prime}(\tau)/2)(t-\tau)^{2}+O((t-\tau)^{3})\,,\quad\text{ as }t\rightarrow\tau\,.

From the previous expansion we obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{h(tq(t))-h(\tau)}{(tq(t)-\tau)^{2}}=\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\psi}^{2}(\tau)}{2}\frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau^{3}}>0

and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{h(t)-h(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{2}}=\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\psi}^{2}(\tau)}{2}\frac{\psi(\tau)}{\tau^{3}}>0\,.

Using that
h(tq(t))=h(t)
we find

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{(tq(t)-\tau)^{2}}{(t-\tau)^{2}}=1

and this implies that

(7.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{tq(t)-\tau}{t-\tau}=-1\,.

Recall that
tq(t)\leq\tau
, for all
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
; see Lemma
7.1
. Hence, combining equations (
7.2
) and (
7.3
) with equation (
7.4
), we conclude that

\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}q^{\prime}(t)=\lim_{t\downarrow\tau}\frac{q(t)-1}{t-\tau}=-\frac{2}{\tau}\,.

∎

Remark 8
.

From the expression

q^{\prime}(t)=\frac{q(t)}{t}\!\left(\frac{1-m_{\psi}(t)}{1-m_{\psi}(tq(t))}-1\right),\quad\text{ for any }t\in(\tau,R_{\psi}),

we deduce that
q^{\prime}(t)<0
for all
t\in(\tau,R_{\psi})
, and therefore
q(t)
is strictly decreasing on that interval.
\boxtimes

8.1.
The
uniform
probability

We have previously discussed the weight associated to the family of rooted plane trees. This weight is defined as follows: for any
a\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\omega(a)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j}^{k_{j}(a)}\,,

with the convention that
0^{0}=1
. By means of
\omega
, see Proposition
A
above, we can build all the families of simple varieties of trees.

Now, for any
a\in\mathcal{G}
, consider the probabilistic weight

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbf{P}}(a)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(Y_{t}=j)^{k_{j}(a)}=\frac{\omega(a)t^{|a|-1}}{\psi(t)^{|a|}}=\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)\,,

again with the convention that
0^{0}=1
. Assume, for simplicity, that
A_{n}\neq 0
for any
n\geq 1
, otherwise, we can restrict
n\geq 1
to values satisfying
n\equiv 1\mod Q_{\psi}
. First, we consider the measure on
\mathcal{G}
with weights
\omega_{\mathbf{P}}
and
\omega
. For a rooted plane tree
a\in\mathcal{G}
we compute its measure as follows

(8.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(\{a\})=\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{P}}(a)}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{\mathbf{P}}(a)}=\frac{\omega(a)t^{|a|-1}/\psi(t)^{|a|}}{\sum_{n\geq 1}A_{n}t^{n-1}/\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{\omega(a)t^{|a|-1}/\psi(t)^{|a|}}{q(t)}=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)}{q(t)}

We will show below that this measure coincides with conditioning the Galton–Watson tree on the extinction event.

Now we compute the measure
\mu
with respect to
\omega
and
\omega_{\mathbf{P}}
, respectively, for the set of rooted plane trees with
n
nodes. For any
a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\omega,n}(\{a\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(a)}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\omega(a)}=\frac{\omega(a)}{A_{n}},

\displaystyle\mu_{\omega_{\mathbf{P}},n}(\{a\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{P}}(a)}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\omega_{\mathbf{P}}(a)}=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}=a)}{A_{n}t^{n-1}/\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{\omega(a)t^{n-1}/\psi(t)^{n}}{A_{n}t^{n-1}/\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{\omega(a)}{A_{n}}.

Thus, the measures on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
with either the probabilistic or the combinatorial weight are exactly the same measure. This reflects the fact that conditioning the random tree
T_{t}
on the event
|T_{t}|=n
yields the
uniform
probability distribution over subclasses of weighted trees. Here, ”uniform” is understood in a broad sense, which we will clarify in the following.

Fix
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
as a subclass of rooted plane trees, and for any
n\geq 1
, define

\displaystyle R_{n}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}}\omega(a)\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Regarding this coefficient (the previous sum of weights), we have the following result:

Theorem 4
.

For any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}\mid|T_{t}|=n)=\frac{R_{n}}{A_{n}},\quad\text{ for any }n\equiv 1\mod Q_{\psi}\,,

and this probability is independent of the parameter
t
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in(0,R)
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}\mid|T_{t}|=n)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}\text{ and }|T_{t}|=n)}{\mathbf{P}(|T_{t}|=n)}=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}_{n})}{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{n})}

For any
n\equiv 1,\mod Q_{\psi}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}_{n})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}\frac{\omega(a)t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}=\frac{R_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}

finally Proposition
6.1
gives that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{G}_{n})=\frac{A_{n}t^{n-1}}{\psi(t)^{n}}

Hence, the result follows by taking the ratio of the two probabilities.
∎

If we condition upon extinction we get the following result.

Theorem 5
.

For any
t\in(0,R_{\psi})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}|\text{ extinction })=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_{t}\in\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{G})}{q(t)}\,.

Here
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.

This coincides with the measure defined earlier on the set of finite rooted plane trees
\mathcal{G}
; see equation (
8.1
).

Extensions of this approach to Galton–Watson forests (the parametric Galton-Watson forest) follow analogously by considering products of power series and using standard independence arguments. Similar coefficient‑based extinction formulas follow immediately. For brevity, we do not develop these results in here.

9.
Conclusion and further directions

The ideas developed in this note extend naturally to subclasses of rooted plane trees defined by various types of restrictions (and also to random forests). Moreover, when combined with the results in
[
8
]
, this perspective yields precise asymptotic formulas across a broad range of settings. A key strength of the approach is that it unifies and simplifies classical arguments, often reducing them to algebraic manipulations, while making the connection between combinatorial and probabilistic viewpoints explicit. The alternative proof of the Otter–Meir–Moon Theorem in
[
8
]
, based on Khinchin families and including several extensions, serves as a clear illustration of this principle. The same methodology can be applied to derive asymptotic formulas for the probabilities associated to Galton-Watson processes studied here, and also to simulate them efficiently using power series coefficients.

Finally, we believe that the underlying structure revealed by these results is broadly applicable, suggesting that this methodology may be extended to other weighted combinatorial classes through the framework of Khinchin families.
