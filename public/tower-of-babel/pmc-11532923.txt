Protocol for exploring the relationship between sound localization and the representation of the body in space
Authors: Daniel Paromov, Karina Moïn-Darbari, Assan Mary Cedras, Maxime Maheu, Benoit-Antoine Bacon, François Champoux
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe influence of multisensory integration on spatial hearing has received more attention in recent years. Notably, incongruent sensory inputs can bias auditory spatial processing. Here, we present a protocol for producing an illusory shift in the localization of a sound source by inducing an unconscious shift in the representation of the body in space. We describe steps for screening participants and evaluating vestibular and hearing abilities. We then detail procedures for performing auditory localization tasks both with and without disorientation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Paromov et al.1

Before you begin

In contrast to the significant body of work confirming the influence of auditory cues on body perception and movement-related activity,2,3,4,5,6 the influence of body representation on spatial hearing has received less attention. Indeed, the study of auditory processing in relationship with body representation has been limited to exploring the impact of vestibular and visual perturbations. In a recent article published in iScience,1 we used a whole-body disorientation task to assess whether an unconscious change in the body’s orientation in space could lead to an illusory shift in the localization of a sound source. While most of the participants were able to locate the sound source with great precision in the baseline condition, they all made substantial errors in judging the position of the same sound source following the body-orientation-altering task. These results demonstrate that a change in perceived body orientation can have a significant impact on the auditory processes underlying sound localization. The illusory errors not only confirm the strong connection between the auditory system and the representation of the body in space but also raise questions about the importance of audition in determining spatial position.

Materials and equipment

The procedure must be conducted in a large quiet room. The experimenter must have at his disposal a speech sequence (e.g., ISTS) with minimal pauses and a minimal duration of 1 min, at a pre-established normal auditory level (i.e., 55 dB SPL, measured prior to the examination using a sound level meter). A central starting position should be marked on the floor, along with three additional marks positioned exactly at 0, 45, and 90 degrees, each at a distance of 2 m from the central mark. These are the positions where the sound source will be placed during the perturbation task. After each trial, the experimenter will mark the participant’s final position and direction on the floor using a dry-erase marker. The experimenter will then use a goniometer (± 1°) to measure the angle of rotation, and the localization error based on the participant’s final body orientation. Finally, the experimenter will need a blindfold, a cervical collar, a measuring tape, a speaker, and a laser pointer.

Vestibular evaluation

Timing: 10 min

Timing: 10 min

Vestibular evaluation is performed using the Vestibular Head Impulse Test (vHIT) which ensures normal semi-circular canal function. Refer to the key resources table (ICS Impulse and Otosuite Vestibular software) for the equipment that can be used.4.Vestibular Head Impulse Test.Note: Normal gains should be observed, without asymmetries or catch-up saccades. Refer to Curthoys et al. (2014)9 for normative values and detailed steps.a.Seat the participants approximately 2 m from a wall with an earth-fixed target (i.e., A dot or cross fixed at the participant’s eye level).Note: Ensure the target can be easily moved to accommodate different heights.b.Perform the test for the lateral, anterior, and posterior canals.i.Instruct the participants to keep their gaze on the fixed target.ii.Proceed with the test for all six canals.Note: The head impulses consist of a passive and unpredictable head rotation at an amplitude of 10° to 20°, with an average speed varying from 150° to 250°/s and an acceleration between 3000° to 4000°/s. Expected outcomes

At the end of the experiment, all participants must testify that they were unaware of any change in body orientation. They should feel confident of the orientation of their body in relation to the sound source during the entire testing session. During the task, a rotation of the body in the horizontal plane will be observed. The disorientation task will lead to a significant illusory shift in the localization of a sound source. Indeed, participants will be unable to localize a sound source with precision while performing the task. Trials in which participants were aware of the error in localization and did not make the required correction in the orientation of their body should be excluded.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Error in localization is computed as the difference between the real position of the sound source – and the perceived position of the sound source. To test for differences in the perceived position of the sound source, a repeated measures 3 × 2 ANOVA can be carried out using both variables. Post-Hoc analysis for each of the follow-up tests can be carried out using the Bonferroni correction. Limitations

This protocol provides us with the ability to verify the relative contribution of the representation of the body in space on auditory localization, but the psychoacoustic parameters underlying performance are not completely understood. Indeed, the position of the sound source had an impact on the error amplitude in both of the auditory localization tasks (with and without disruption of the body representation in space). Moreover, it is unclear if the increased error in localization during the disorientation task results from a continuous increase or major steps with corrections in between.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Participant’s mean absolute error in localization during the auditory localization task (step 6) is over 8 degrees (reference value from the original publication1).

Potential solution

Participants may present with some level of auditory processing disorder resulting in the inability to locate sounds with precision (assuming normal peripheral auditory function). Depending on the population that is examined, excluding these participants from the analysis should be considered if normal peripheral and central function is required.

Problem 2

The participant’s stepping rhythm is too slow/fast (step 11).

Potential solution

Instruct the participant to modify the stepping rhythm accordingly when the trial is ended. Provide a visual example of the expected pace.CRITICAL: Do not give instructions while the participant is performing the stepping task. Problem 3

The participant rotates their head while localizing the auditory sequence (steps 6).

Potential solution

Instruct the participant to keep the head fixed, fixing an imaginary point in front of the participant. If movement is still observed, consider using a cervical collar to limit head rotations.CRITICAL: The trial in which the participant moved the head relative to the body should be excluded from analysis as it provides increased accuracy and might void the disorientation task.

CRITICAL: The trial in which the participant moved the head relative to the body should be excluded from analysis as it provides increased accuracy and might void the disorientation task.

Problem 4

The participant is experiencing fatigue during the stepping task (step 11) (ask the participant after each condition if they need a break).

Potential solution

Give the participant the necessary time to relax between each trial. If necessary, include a 5 min break between each spatial position that is tested.

Problem 5

The disorientation task allows the participant to not only rotate their body, but also have a displacement in both the X and Y axes. Such displacement does not automatically induce an error in localization if accompanied by rotation (i.e., the sound source is kept at the original position).

Potential solution

A variation of the Fukuda-Unterberger stepping test can be used, which provides a physical reference point at the center of one’s body position. A small pole can serve as such reference point giving feedback to the participant if displacement occurs.12

Problem 6

The presented protocol requires repetition of every trial three times per angle condition, increasing the probability that the participant becomes aware of the displacement (i.e., illusory effect).

Potential solution

The data presented in the original article6 shows no significant variability between each trial. This suggests the number of trials can be reduced, lowering the probability of such awareness.
