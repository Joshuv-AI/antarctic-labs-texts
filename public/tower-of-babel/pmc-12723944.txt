A history of suicide attempts among individuals with schizophrenia is associated with tryptophan degradation via the salivary kynurenine pathway
Authors: Yi Yin, Ting Xie, Jinghui Tong, Junchao Huang, Baopeng Tian, Mengzhuang Gou, Song Chen, Shuping Tan, Zhiren Wang, Yongsheng Tong, Yanfang Zhou, Leilei Wang, Yunlong Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTryptophan, an essential precursor metabolite within the kynurenine pathway, has been implicated in the neurobiological mechanisms underlying both schizophrenia and associated suicidality. Despite this link, the lack of validated biomarkers to identify patients with dysregulated kynurenine pathway activity hinders patient stratification, thereby impeding the clinical development of pathway-targeted interventions.MethodsThis study enrolled 288 individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia (36 with prior suicide attempts, 252 without) and 202 healthy controls. Saliva samples were collected to quantify concentrations of tryptophan and associated kynurenine pathway metabolites using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).ResultsPatients with a history of suicide attempts exhibited significantly elevated salivary tryptophan concentration (p = 0.006) and a reduced kynurenine/tryptophan ratio (p = 0.028) compared to patients without such a history. However, within the schizophrenia cohort, no differences in the level of kynurenine, kynurenic acid, quinolinic acid, the kynurenic-acid-to-kynurenine ratio, or the quinolinic-acid-to-kynurenic-acid ratio were observed between patients with and without a history of suicide attempts.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that a history of suicide attempts is associated with alterations in salivary tryptophan concentration and the metabolic shift from tryptophan to kynurenine. These results contribute to the understanding of the potential biochemical factors involved in suicidal behavior, especially among individuals with schizophrenia. Given its non-invasive nature, monitoring salivary kynurenine metabolic shifts may serve as an early-warning biomarker for suicidal behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07574-w.Keywords: Tryptophan, Kynurenine, Schizophrenia, Suicide, Saliva, Biomarkers

Introduction

Suicidal ideation and behavior are transdiagnostic clinical features [1]. The lifetime, 1-year, and 1-month prevalence of suicide attempts in schizophrenia were 26.8%, 3%, and 2.7%, respectively [2]. Among individuals with schizophrenia, 4%~20% end their lives by suicide [3, 4]. However, the neurobiology of suicidality in schizophrenia is a complex and poorly understood phenomenon. Suicide risk assessments are mainly based on clinical evaluations.

Tryptophan (TRP) serves critical physiological functions, including acting as a precursor to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) [5]. Studies have linked the reduced TRP level with a history of suicide attempts in individuals with mood disorders [6]. However, it remains unclear whether similar abnormalities in TRP metabolism are present among individuals with schizophrenia and a history of suicide attempts.

Over 95% of peripheral TRP is metabolized through the kynurenine (KYN) pathway, not the serotonin pathway [7, 8] (Fig. 1). The tryptophan-kynurenine pathway yields metabolites such as quinolinic acid (QUIN), an agonist of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptors, and kynurenic acid (KYNA), an antagonist of NMDA, (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid) AMPA, and kainate glutamate receptors [9]. Some enzymatic activity within the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway is modulated by stress [10]. At the same time, stress is a well-recognized risk factor for suicidality [11]. This immuno-responsive and stress-reactive nature has prompted researchers to investigate the potential association between abnormalities in this pathway and a history of suicide attempts. Nonetheless, relevant research, particularly within the context of schizophrenia, is relatively scarce [12].

KYNA, a metabolite of KYN, is synthesized in and released from brain astrocytes. It is important to note that KYNA cannot cross the blood-brain barrier [13], and therefore, peripheral blood KYNA levels are not indicative of central KYNA levels [14]. Over recent decades, research has indicated that increased brain KYNA is specifically associated with schizophrenia [15]. Previous research has found that individuals with schizophrenia spectrum psychosis and a history of suicide attempts exhibited significantly lower levels of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) KYNA compared to non-suicidal patients [16]. A small-sample cohort study found that patients with psychosis and lower CSF KYNA concentrations were at a higher risk for later attempted suicide [16]. CSF KYNA levels have been observed to decrease by approximately 35% over two years after a suicide attempt, and low CSF KYNA levels have been correlated with more severe depressive and suicidal symptoms during follow-ups [17].

QUIN, another metabolite in the KYN pathway, can influence glutamate neurotransmission through its agonistic action on N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors. Accumulation of QUIN may lead to neuronal damage through overstimulating glutamate receptors [18]. Antagonists of NMDA receptors, such as Esketamine, can affect the glutamate neurotransmission pathway, resulting in a rapid anti-suicidal effect [19]. Studies have found that there were increased QUIN levels in the CSF of those with mental disorders and a history of suicide attempts compared to healthy controls, but the difference between depression and other mental disorders was not significant [17, 20].

However, the detectability of differences in TRP, QUIN, or KYNA levels via non-invasive methods (e.g., saliva) requires further validation. Saliva-based biomarkers offer several advantages, including ease of collection, reduced patient discomfort, and the potential for repeated sampling in real-world settings—making them particularly valuable for longitudinal studies and clinical monitoring. Previous studies have identified elevated salivary KYNA as a potential biomarker for schizophrenia, especially under stress [21–23], supporting the investigation of this approach for exploring its association with suicidality. Such methodology could facilitate future integrated studies examining the interplay between salivary cortisol and the TRP-KYN metabolic pathway.

This study aimed to investigate whether salivary metabolites of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, including TRP, KYN, KYN/TRP, KYNA, QUIN, the KYNA/KYN ratio, and the QUIN/KYNA ratio, were significantly associated with a history of suicide attempts among individuals with schizophrenia. The potential changes in the tryptophan pathway following suicide attempts may reveal a predisposition to neuroinflammation-related vulnerabilities in individuals with schizophrenia.

Methods

Participants

Patient participants were hospitalized at Beijing HuiLongGuan Hospital, a psychiatric hospital in Beijing, China. Patients were included if they were diagnosed with schizophrenia based on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV [24]. Exclusion criteria applied for individuals with the following conditions: organic brain disorders, current comorbidity with other Axis I mental disorders, organic auditory or visual perception disorders, absence of oral lesions, oral ulcer, or gingival bleeding, unstable major medical conditions (e.g., cancer), significant adverse reactions to medications, or those who were pregnancy or current breastfeeding.

Healthy controls were recruited from the local community. Eligible participants​​ aged 16–60 years met the following criteria: (a) no personal or family history of mental disorders; (b) no lifetime suicide attempts; (c) absence of oral lesions, oral ulcer, ​​or​​ gingival bleeding (clinically confirmed); (d) no unstable major medical conditions (e.g., active cancer, decompensated organ failure); (e) no significant adverse drug reactions; (f) exclusion of pregnancy or current lactation.

Assessments

A lifetime history of suicide attempt was defined as a previous nonfatal deliberate self-harm with the intent to end one’s life. The variable was determined by integrating information from interviews with the patients and their families, suicide risk management documentation, and other pertinent medical records.

The severity of psychiatric symptoms was assessed using the 30-item Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [25, 26]. This scale is a standardized clinician-administered instrument quantifying three domains​​: positive symptoms (e.g., delusions/hallucinations), negative symptoms (e.g., blunted affect), and general psychopathology.

Saliva collection, processing and calculations

Saliva was collected to measure the salivary levels of TRP, KYN, KYNA, and QUIN. Participants provided unstimulated saliva by passive drooling ​​directly​​ into cryovials. Participants were instructed to abstain from eating, smoking, or brushing their teeth for one hour prior to collection. Participants rinsed their mouths with water, and saliva samples were collected ten minutes thereafter.

Saliva was obtained via passive drool into cryovials. Saliva samples ​​visibly contaminated​​ with blood (e.g., exhibiting pink/red discoloration) were immediately discarded or recollected later. Saliva was immediately stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. The TRP-KYN pathway metabolites, including TRP, KYN, KYNA, and QUIN, were separated according to established protocols [23]. For processing, 200 µL of internal standard solution was added to each 50 µL aliquot. The mixtures were then vortex-mixed for 5 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 200 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a fresh vial containing 100 µL of distilled water, followed by 1-minute vortex-mixing and an additional centrifugation step under identical conditions (12,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C). Technicians who were blinded to group assignments and other information conducted the assay. Quantification of the kynurenine metabolites was performed by a certified third-party laboratory using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC/MS) on an API 6500 + system (AB SCIEX, Singapore). Analysis employed the internal standard (IS) method for quantification, utilizing stable isotope-labeled analogs (SIL-IS) as internal standards.​​ Kynurenine metabolites and their isotope-labeled internal standards were procured from TRC (Toronto Research Chemicals, Canada).

The TYP/KYN ratio was determined as an indirect indication of Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) activity [8]. Additionally, the QUIN/KYNA ratio was assessed to evaluate the potential stimulation of NMDA receptors, considering the interactive effects of QUIN (an antagonist) and KYNA (an agonist) on NMDA receptor function [27].

Statistical analysis

Group differences in categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square (χ2) tests. Extreme outliners, defined as values more than three times the interquartile range (IQR) above the third quartile or below the first quartile, were treated as missing data. No samples were excluded after data transfer. For variables that violated normality assumptions, the Kruskal-Wallis equality-of-populations rank test was employed to compare the variables across the three groups, and the Mann-Whitney U rank-sum test was utilized to determine trends between the two groups. The independent samples T-test was applied to evaluate the difference between two specific groups.

Multivariate robust linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the impact of group differences (patients with a history of suicide attempts, patients without such a history, and healthy controls) on each salivary tryptophan-kynurenine pathway metabolite, with adjustment for age, sex, and smoking status.

Statistical analysis software was Stata 15.0 for Windows (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Data visualization was performed using R software version 4.5.1 [28], with bee-swarm plots were generated using the “ggbeeswarm” package. The Significance level was two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Ethics and consent

The local Institutional Review Boards of the Beijing HuiLongGuan Hospital approved this study. Patients and/or their legal guardians (for participants under 18 years old) provided written informed consent. Our research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [29].

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Saliva samples were collected from 490 participants: 288 individuals with schizophrenia and 202 healthy controls (see flowchart in supplementary Table S1). The median age of the individuals with schizophrenia was 32 years (interquartile range [IQR], 25–46 years). Nearly half (45.5%) were females. Within the patients, 36 individuals had a history of suicide attempts, classified as the SZ-SA group, while 252 individuals had no lifetime history of suicide attempts, classified as the SZ-NSA group. The three groups did not differ in age and sex (all p values ≥ 0.05, Table 1). However, the proportion of current smokers was significantly higher in the SZ-SA group (36.1%) compared to both the SZ-NS group (15.9%) and healthy controls (16.3%) (Chi-square χ2 = 9.26, p = 0.010).

Salivary QUIN concentration and both the KYNA/KYN and QUIN/KYNA ratios were related to the positive symptom subscale of the PANSS (Table S5). Levels of TRP, KYN, KYNA, and QUIN demonstrated positive correlations with the PANSS negative symptom subscale score.

Discussion

TRP metabolism plays a key role in emotion regulation, immunity, and neurocognitive function. This study extends prior work by examining the association between suicidality and the tryptophan kynurenine pathway in schizophrenia. This study demonstrated that individuals with schizophrenia and a history of suicide attempts exhibited a higher level of salivary TRP compared to those without such a history. Additionally, these patients also exhibited a decrease in the KYN/TRP ratio.

The elevated TRP levels may be attributed to reduced activity of tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism. The salivary KYN/TRP ratio may reflect local kynurenine pathway activation in the oral cavity, primarily mediated by IDO in immune cells and oral mucosal tissues, rather than hepatic TDO. IDO can be induced by pro-inflammatory mediators, such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-2 (IL-2), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) [8]. Previous studies reported that suicide risk is related to some inflammation abnormalities [30–32]. However, systemic influences of TDO cannot be entirely excluded. TDO is regulated by TRP levels and glucocorticoid hormones [8]. Studies have shown that individuals with a history of suicide attempts often experience increased stress perception [33], altered baseline cortisol, or cortisol reactivity compared to those without such a history [34–36]. Suicide risk is linked to hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation [37]. Extensive evidence indicates that stress-induced HPA axis activation elevates cortisol levels, which subsequently upregulates TDO activity, leading to reduced TRP availability and an elevated KYN/TRP ratio. Theoretically, hepatic TDO activation under stress or inflammation may alter peripheral TRP availability, thereby indirectly modulating salivary TRP/KYN levels. However, this hypothesis requires further validation.

Given the scarcity of studies exploring the difference in the tryptophan kynurenine pathway between individuals with and without a history of suicide attempts in schizophrenia, we can only compare our findings with the studies in mood disorders. Our findings of increased TRP in those with a history of suicide attempts align with some findings in mood disorders [38], but contrast with others [6] or studies showing no difference [39]. The lower KYN/TRP ratio in our study is inconsistent with previous research on mood disorders, which associated suicide attempts with a higher KYN/TRP ratio [6]. The lower kynurenine/tryptophan ratio among individuals with a history of suicide attempts may indicate the stress-reactive nature of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway. This discrepancy may reflect differential patterns in TRP metabolism between schizophrenia and mood disorders [40]. Dysregulation of kynurenine has been identified in schizophrenia [41], with patients showing lower peripheral concentrations of TRP, 5-hydroxyindole (5-HI), KYN, and QUIN compared to healthy controls [42, 43].

Our study did not detect a group-level difference in QUIN. QUIN, an excitotoxic NMDA receptor agonist, can lead to excitotoxicity and neuronal death at high concentrations, but also a precursor to the essential coenzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), an important coenzyme. The short half-life of QUIN, approximately 20 min, may contribute to the difficulty in identifying it as a consistent biomarker of suicidality.

The findings suggest that future studies examine whether the salivary TRP/KYN screening could be a potential biomarker for predicting suicide risk. Key regulatory enzymes, IDO or TDO, may be potentially targeted for easing suicide risk. Further research is needed to determine whether metabolic differences between those with or without a history of suicide attempts are influenced by stress vulnerability. Future studies should expand the metabolic profiling to incorporate endocannabinoids, endorphins, cortisol, and inflammatory cytokines, enabling comprehensive analysis of their interactions with the TRP-KYN pathway in suicide risk. Given its non-invasive nature, repeated monitoring alterations in salivary kynurenine pathway metabolites may provide an early-warning biomarker for suicidal behavior trajectories.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not collect detailed information on dietary intake, which could confound TRP concentration levels, given that the human body cannot synthesize TRP. However, all patient participants in our study were from the inpatient department with similar diet plans, minimizing this potential confounder for the concentration of TRP. Second, patients were classified based on the lifetime history of suicide attempts rather than recent acute suicidal crises, reflecting more “chronic” metabolism characteristics. Third, most patients were receiving antipsychotics without a wash-out period; nevertheless, olanzapine-equivalent doses did not differ between groups among the patients. We did not control the use of antidepressants in the multivariate regression due to a high percentage (95%) of TRP conversion to KYN over serotonin [8] and low prevalence of antidepressant use among patients without a history of suicide attempts, which would have limited statistical power. The use of psychiatric drugs prevents us from isolating drug-related alterations in the TRP–KYN profile. Consequently, medication effects represent a potential confounder that may obscure the identification of biomarkers with the sufficient sensitivity and accuracy required for clinical translation.

Conclusions

The lifetime history of suicide attempts is associated with elevated salivary tryptophan and reduced KYN/TRP ratios among individuals with schizophrenia, independent of the underlying tryptophan metabolism abnormalities characteristic of the disorder.
