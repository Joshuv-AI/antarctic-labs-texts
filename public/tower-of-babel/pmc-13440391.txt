Anisotropic variable negative-Poisson-ratio metamaterials for tunable multispectral camouflage and electromagnetic energy harvesting
Authors: Xiaofen Yang, Kaixia Yang, Meiwan Ying, Zhengchen Wu, Wenbin You, Bicheng Li, Lei Wang, Liting Yang, Renchao Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adaptive electromagnetic compatibility, camouflage, and energy conversion raises challenge in the swiftly responsive modulation. Herein, we designed pixelated negative-Poisson-ratio metamaterials that enable fast control over structural anisotropy and thus electromagnetic properties. One-dimensional conductors are highly oriented in each pixel through sequential shear, stretch, and alignment processes. The uniaxial anisotropy leads to the highest conductive ratio of 2.8 × 106 among different directions. This enables the angle-dependent electromagnetic compatibility across transparency–absorption–shielding regions. The meta-framework realizes strain-reliant anisotropic tunability. The deformation continuously adjusts electromagnetic wave absorbance from 0.2 to 0.9 (reflection loss from −0.8 to −17 decibels). Through chess-like assembly, the framework can also maintain deformation-insensitive absorbance beyond 0.9. The proof-of-concept devices harvest environmental electromagnetic energy to electricity. The generator outputs more than 0.5 volts and the cell generate a peak power of 0.75 milliwatt. Besides, the devices demonstrate tunable camouflage in microwave, infrared, and visible spectra.

INTRODUCTION

Electromagnetic response decides compatible stability and defensive concealment. It relies on polarization relaxation of intrinsic electric and magnetic dipoles, which is fixed after material fabrication (1). As the complexity in electronic and radar systems increases, active control over electromagnetic waves in free space becomes increasing critical for compatibility and camouflage (2, 3). This drives the urgent need for electromagnetic analogues of chameleon that enable tunable reflection, absorption, and transmission (4–6). The key challenge lies in instant and reversible turnability of electric properties. Insulative–conductive phase transition can tune the surface reflectivity by thermal and electrochemical means, such as monoclinic/rutile phase transition of VO2 and 2H/1 T phase transition in MoS2 (7–9). Besides, ions injection into two-dimensional graphene and MXenes yields abundant free charges and thus enhances the reflectivity. All these approaches suffer from sluggish switching process and/or weak reversibility due to the reactive nonuniformity (10, 11). Deformation method aims to adjust porous attributes of conductive foam, such as graphene- and carbon nanotube (CNT)-based aerogels (12–14). The behind mechanisms include regulation of microcurrent path length and wave scattering and reflection interspace. Despite their instantaneity, plastic deformation severally harms the responsive invertibility. Recently, electromagnetic lattice crystals have demonstrated absorption–transmission transition through structural collapse. However, their centimeter-scaled thickness can hardly satisfy the miniaturization and integration in the real-world scenarios.

Anisotropic materials can steer waves in specific directions. Their orientation-dependent resistance leads to incident-angle-reliant sensitivity of impedance. Therefore, both reflection and absorption solely rely on the geometrical rotation without constituent variation and structural destruction, showing competitive advantages in active modulation. Typical anisotropic materials include layered composites (15–17), aligned fibers (18–20), and architected lattices (21–23). However, they show constrained regulation scope of reflectance, primarily due to weak conductive anisotropy at microscale. For example, in MXene-based aerogel with vertical channels, lamellae bridges channels to reduce the conductive and reflectance difference in between radial and axial directions (11). Current fabrication strategies such as ice templating, directional sintering, or porous-precursor-guided alignment can indeed induce long-range order at the macroscale (24–27). Yet, they fail to precisely regulate microstructural orientation due to uncontrollable nucleation or local inhomogeneity during the assembly process. Realizing structural anisotropy with both microscopic and macroscopic orders remains a major challenge.

The advancement in auxetic materials with artificial superlattice has demonstrated potential applications in sensors, energy absorbers, display, etc. (28–30). Rotating polygons is a typical kind of auxetic materials. Upon stretching loading, the deformation merely arises from rotation of connected rigid polygons without shearing (31, 32). This special deformation feature ideally solves the actuator problem for the aforementioned anisotropic modulation. In a hypothetical scenario of camouflage coating, auxetic structure brings more convenience in rotation-motivated electromagnetic modulation without additional rotary actuators. Nevertheless, applying auxeticity in active control of electromagnetic response has rarely been reported.

In this work, we introduce a negative Poisson’s ratio metaframework to access continuous variation of electromagnetic response across transparency, absorption, and shielding, and thus realize tunable multispectral camouflage and energy harvesting. We firstly designed a quasi-uniaxial anisotropic structure that exhibits orientation alignment of carbon nanotubes from micro- to macroscale through a synergistic shear, stretching, and alignment strategy. The composite is insulative with a high conductive ratio of 2.8 × 106 among different directions. By adjusting the orientation angle relative to incident electromagnetic waves, the effective impedance and loss pathways can be precisely tuned, leading to controllable shifts in both absorption intensity and resonance frequency. Subsequently, the composite film as the pixel are bridged into a rotating-polygon framework to allow to dynamically alter its internal orientation distribution and electromagnetic (EM) response. Inspired by movable-type printing, conductive anisotropy in each pixel can be individually designed to tunable response in microwave, infrared, and visible spectra. Response in each one or two bands can be varied through stretching while that in the rest can be maintained. We also demonstrated its energy harvesting capabilities. The auxetic metamaterial can adaptively maximize the absorption of electromagnetic waves and converted energy into electricity when coupled with thermal battery or P–N junction components.

RESULTS

The anisotropic structure possesses a preferential orientation that enables direction-dependent performance. Given the strong correlation between resistance and impedance, increase in conductive anisotropy is expect to enlarge the regulation scope of electromagnetic response. Conventional anisotropic materials are typically fabricated using template such as ice and woods. The orientated pores are constructed at first and then functional components are filled in. These aerogel materials face significant challenges in precisely controlling the microscopic alignment within the pore template. Besides, their response modulation requires additional rotary actuator which increases system complexity. We propose a bottom-up paradigm. One-dimensional conductors are firstly orientated within pore templates and then pore array is assembled in a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) matrix (Fig. 1A). This composite can realize quasi-uniaxial conductive anisotropy across micro- to macroscale. It is insulative with the highest electric conductance of 2.8 × 10−4 and can be employed for electromagnetic compatibility of electronic components (figs. S1 and S2). The conductive ratio between vertical and parallel directions reaches 2.8 × 106. The composite is further cut into a square framework with auxeticity. The in-plane rotation of each square can be derived by stretching, enabling anisotropic tunability of the entire framework, much more convenient than three-dimensional rotation actuation for traditional anisotropic aerogels (Fig. 1B). The conductive anisotropy decides the surface impedance and interior attenuation towards the incident electromagnetic waves. Thus, all the reflectance, absorptance, transmittance can be adjusted simultaneously based on elongation degree (Fig. 1C).

The orientation at microscale is realized using a wet-spinning approach. Under shearing and stretching, randomly distributed carbon nanotubes are highly aligned within alginate threads (Fig. 1D and fig. S3). The orientation degree is positively associated with draw ratio, as that increase in draw ration leads to enhanced tensile strength (fig. S4). Threads with intrinsic flexibility and knotting capability are parallelly assembled into an orientation array by a rotary collector (Fig. 1, E and F, and figs. S5 and S6). Eventually, array is casting with PDMS to obtain a composite film with the thickness of ∼1 mm (Fig. 1G). The alignment anisotropy of the film can be reflected in the higher intensity ratio of the G peak between parallel and perpendicular Raman configurations and concentrated diffuse halos in selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 1H). The quasi-uniaxiality solves the contradiction between conductivity and anisotropy. The films acquire a high conductive anisotropy value of 2.8 × 106, much superior to reported anisotropic materials such as gelatin-based anisotropic hydrogels (33) and poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA)-based eutectogels (34) (Fig. 1I and table S1) (15, 18, 20, 33–47). This enables the direction-switchable electrical transport, proved by the on/off state of a circuit, which lays a foundation for electromagnetic modulation.

Two control groups are employed to highlight the anisotropy dependence of electromagnetic response, including CNTs and short alginate/CNTs fibers in random distribution (fig. S7). Small-angle scattering spectra reveals the strongest anisotropic scattering intensity of the oriented threads (Fig. 2, A to C). Consistently, polarized Raman spectra show that the G-band intensity ratio (I0°/I90°) increases from 1.37 for the isotropy powder film to 2.38 for the randomly fragmented short fibers film, and reached the highest value in the oriented thread film (fig. S8). These data suggests that synergistic orientation in microscale (inside alginate fibers) and macroscale (inside PDMS matrix) significantly enhance the entire structural anisotropy. We measured their electromagnetic response under different direction of electric vector of electromagnetic wave (fig. S9). For oriented thread film, the specific shielding effectiveness (SET) reaches the highest of 42.87 dB when orientation follows the electric vector of electromagnetic wave. Increase in the angle between orientation direction and electric vector gradually reduce the SET to 2.67 dB, demonstrating the electromagnetic tunability (Fig. 2, A to C, and fig. S10). By comparison, films containing isotropy powder or short fibers shows nearly unchanged SET values. Such electromagnetic tunability can be attributed to dual-scaled synergy of orientation. First, the alignment of threads enables the electromagnetic response variation. We compared the SET difference of carbon black-filled fibers (fig. S11). By rotating aligned fibers, the SET value can be increased by six times (fig. S12). Besides, two stacked films with the coincident alignment dominance consistently demonstrated superior shielding performance than others with increased superposition angle (fig. S13). Second, the alignment of one-dimensional carbon nanotubes further increases the responsive regulation scope. Fibers with increasing stretching ratios showed improved shielding performance due to the enhanced structural anisotropy. The highest magnification of SET after rotation reaches 25 (fig. S14).

Through rotating the orientated film, reflectance is decreased from 0.90 to 0.22, transmittance is increased from 5.16 × 10−5 to 0.54, and absorbance is firstly enlarged to 0.52 and then reduced to 0.23 (Fig. 2E). The orientation-dependent response is governed by a competition between impedance matching and intrinsic attenuation (figs. S15 and S16) (48–50). At 0°, the electric field is parallel to the aligned conductive fibers, corresponding to the direction of maximum conductivity (fig. S9). Although this configuration provides strong conductive loss and a large attenuation capability, the severe impedance mismatch gives rise to dominant surface reflection, enabling the film to shield nearly 90% of incident microwaves. When the orientation angle is increased to 45°, the impedance matching is improved while the attenuation constant remains relatively high, allowing incident waves to enter the film and be effectively dissipated, which leads to the maximum absorptance of 0.52 with moderate reflectance. At 90°, the electric field becomes perpendicular to the fiber axis, corresponding to the direction of minimum conductivity. In this case, the improved impedance matching suppresses reflection, but the weakened attenuation capability allows a large fraction of the incident wave to transmit through the film, giving a transmittance of 0.54. Therefore, the progressive improvement in impedance matching reduces reflection, while the simultaneous decrease in attenuation causes the absorption to first increase and then decrease, eventually leading to enhanced transmission.

To our best knowledge, it is the first material that can be simultaneously applied for electromagnetic wave shielding, absorption, and transparent. It can shield 90% waves as the jammer, attenuate 52% waves with 31% reflectance as the stealth material, and allows 54% transmission waves as the radome. Such tunability is much better than traditional electromagnetic wave absorption and shielding materials (Fig. 2D and table S2) (51–60). Beyond microwave behavior, anisotropy-induced differences are also extended into the infrared regime, which relies on the electromagnetic-thermal energy conversion (Fig. 2F). As the fiber orientation angle increased, the peak heating temperature exhibited a non-monotonic trend, first increasing and then decreasing, corresponding to the transition from reflection-dominated to transmission-dominated electromagnetic behavior. The macroscopic alignment of the oriented fibers is illustrated in Fig. 2G. Furthermore, CST Studio Suite simulations were conducted to analyze the spatial distribution of electromagnetic power loss within the films at various orientation angles (Fig. 2H). Figure 2I presents the infrared thermal images of the films after three minutes of microwave irradiation, further confirming the anisotropic thermal generation characteristics.

In consideration of real-world scenario, stretching deformation is much more convenient and controllable than rotation. Therefore, a negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) lattice is proposed to bridge the rotation of units and extension of the entire system (Fig. 3A). 3 × 3 cm2 anisotropic film patches with different orientation angles are integrated into a NPR framework, which is anchored on a wave-transparent substrate (fig. S17). The deformation-derived evolution of electromagnetic response is evaluated using the arch method (Fig. 3B). The extension dependence of reflection loss (RL) values varies with frequencies. When the NPR framework undergoes stretching deformation, the RL shows the same trend as the effective absorption bandwidth (Fig. 3C). The RL decrease in low frequency spectrum, keep almost unchanged within 6 to 10 GHz, and increase in the rest high-frequency band (Fig. 3D and fig. S18). Such difference originates from synergy between orientation-determined absorption and deformation-depended interior reflection. Microwaves with long wavelength can be reflected due to the rotation of orientation, as discussed in the above (Fig. 2, A to C), whereas some with the short wavelength can also propagate into porous architecture, which significantly increases the propagation and reflection pathways for energy dissipation.

Inspired by movable-type printing, we further reconfigured the orientation distribution of each film patch into reprogrammable layouts to satisfy the specific modulation of electromagnetic response (Fig. 3E). A total of 7 ordered combinations were proposed. Fig. 3F shows three typical checkerboard-type combinations with 1 × 1 (ai+j), 2 × 2 (bi+j), and 3 × 3 (ci+j) patches as repetitive units, where i and j are orientation angles. During stretching process, repetitive units would show different responsive variation according to their electromagnetic anisotropy, thereby extending the regulation scope of electromagnetic response (Fig. 3G and fig. S19). Through stretching deformation, the metafamework can be transformed from an effective absorber to reflector with the RL value from −15 dB to −0.08 dB, or holds an inverse trend (Fig. 3H). It can also maintain deformation-independent effective absorption (Fig. 3I). The feature of programmable response endows our NPR assembles with broad application potentials (61–63). For example, it can realize real-time camouflage in complex environments, where each part of equipment holds same reflectance as the background (tress, water, buildings, etc.) to blind radar detectors. Besides, it also satisfies the deformation-insensitive stealth scenario.

The programmable NPR framework realizes tunable real-time modulation of camouflage in microwave, infrared, and visible bands (Fig. 4A). Traditional camouflage technology demonstrates concurrent variation in multiple spectra. For example, changing band structure can alter the electron photoluminescence and hot luminescence, leading to tight coupling variation from visible to terahertz spectra (64–67). We designed a two layered patch composed of orientation film substrate and thermochromism coating and assemble them into an NPR framework (Fig. 4B). The microwave reflectance is governed by the collective orientation composition of the entire negative-Poisson-ratio (NPR) framework (Fig. 3F), whereas the infrared emission and visible color are mainly determined by the local orientation-dependent heating behavior of each patch (Fig. 2F), thereby enabling tunable multispectral responses. Patches with different orientation angles were fixed within a mold and irradiated with a microwave source, forming a checkerboard pattern that evolved from disorder to order, corresponding to the transition from pattern 1 to pattern 2 (fig. S20). The thermal insulation sponge is placed among patches to prevent the thermal cross-talk (figs. S21, S22). Specifically, the overall microwave absorption performance can be programmed by controlling the ratio and spatial arrangement of 0°, 45°, and 90° oriented units within the NPR framework. In contrast, the visible patterns are regulated by the microwave-induced heating profiles of individual units and the transition threshold of the thermochromic ink. For example, when a thermochromic ink with a transition temperature of 35°C is used, the 45°-oriented units can trigger a yellow-to-red color transition under microwave irradiation, whereas the 0°-oriented units remain below the color-transition threshold. Meanwhile, the infrared patterns originate from the spatial temperature contrast among units with different microwave-heating efficiencies. Based on this design principle, patches with different orientation angles were integrated into the NPR framework and irradiated with a microwave source, producing reconfigurable checkerboard-like multispectral patterns upon mechanical stretching. For Pattern #1, stretching it into Pattern #1–1 induces pronounced changes in the infrared and visible images, while its microwave absorption performance remains nearly unchanged (Fig. 4C). In contrast, stretching Pattern #2 into Pattern #2–2 leads to obvious variations across the microwave, infrared, and visible channels (Fig. 4D). Moreover, Pattern #1 and Pattern #2 exhibit similar infrared features but distinct microwave absorption and visible responses, whereas Pattern #1–1 and Pattern #2–2 display comparable visible and infrared characteristics but different microwave absorption behaviors. The thermal-lag effect was observed only when the patch was rotated from 0° to 45° or 0° to 30°, for which the color transition times were 10 s and 30 s, respectively. For the other rotation conditions, such as 35°-45° and 45°-90°, the patches had already reached the temperature threshold of thermochromic transition before rotation, and therefore the subsequent color change was not significantly affected by thermal lag (fig. S23). These results offering an effective strategy for the design of adaptive and intelligent stealth materials.

The NPR framework exhibits multifunctional responses across the microwave, infrared, and optical bands, and further enables the adaptive microwave-electricity conversion. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, the orientation film was attached to a P–N junction substrate. Upon microwave irradiation, the film generated localized heating, inducing a temperature gradient between the upper and lower ends of the P–N junction, which in turn produced a measurable thermoelectric voltage (fig. S24). When the orientation angle of the orientation was varied from 0° to 90°, the maximum discharge voltage first increased and then decreased, exhibiting an optimal response at an intermediate angle (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, when patches with different orientation angles were arranged into specific patterns and subjected to stretching deformation, their discharge characteristics evolved correspondingly (fig. S25). As the stretching angle increased from 0° to 45°, the maximum discharge voltage gradually increased (Fig. 5C). This result demonstrates that the present system can function as a mechanically regulated energy-conversion platform, which may be promising for applications such as non-contact thermoelectricity self-powered mechanical sensing, and soft actuation systems requiring tunable electrical signals.

In addition, a gel-based film was fabricated to further verify the energy conversion behavior. We coupled our microwave-responsive absorber with a gelatin-based ionic thermoelectric gel, which consists of gelatin, KCl, and the Fe(CN)64−/3− redox couple. In this gel, gelatin provides a quasi-solid ionic transport matrix, while KCl-driven ion thermodiffusion and Fe(CN)64−/3−-mediated thermogalvanic reactions synergistically generate a large thermovoltage. Upon microwave irradiation, our absorber converts electromagnetic energy into localized heat and transfers it to the thermoelectric gel, establishing a temperature gradient that drives ion redistribution and redox-potential variation, thereby producing an electrical output (Fig. 5D). As the orientation angle varies from 0° to 90°, the discharge rate initially increases and then decreases, with optimal discharge efficiency observed at intermediate angles (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, when patches with different orientation angles are arranged on the gel-based film and subjected to tensile deformation, their discharge behavior changes correspondingly. As the stretching angle increases from 0° to 45°, the maximum discharge voltage gradually rises, and the time required to reach the peak discharge voltage is progressively shortened (Fig. 5F). This configuration also enables the battery to achieve an output power of 0.75 mW (Fig. 5G).

The Seebeck coefficient, power density of our gel-based devices are 17 mV·K−1, and 0.83 W·m−2. A performance comparison with contact-type thermoelectric devices is presented in table S3 (68–74). The proposed device delivers prominent superiority in power density characteristic parameters. It breaks away from the inherent dependence on fixed heat sources by introducing microwave irradiation as an externally controllable heat input, thereby enabling non-contact thermal activation and tunable electrical output. In scenarios where continuous heat sources are unavailable, direct integration of conventional heaters is impractical, or remote triggering is desired, the microwave-driven approach exhibits unique advantages over conventional contact devices. In addition, our systems demonstrate the advantages of simple architecture and a wide operating frequency band compared with radio frequency (RF) harvesting rectenna systems (table S4) (75–84). For example, RF devices usually consist of a receiving antenna, a low-pass filter, a rectifying circuit based on diodes or transistors, an impedance/output matching network, and an output filter for direct-current stabilization and efficiency enhancement. Restricted by antenna performance, their operation frequency is limited to a narrow band. In contrast, the microwave-triggered thermoelectric framework merely consists of sandwiched electrodes, fiber-arranged film, and thermoelectric hydrogels, and demonstrates the broad absorption bandwidth of 8.2–18.0 GHz (fig. S19). Such superiorities greatly extend its potential application prospects, such as environmental electromagnetic energy harvesting, distributed low-power supply, and remote powering of small electronic units.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we demonstrate a quasi-uniaxial anisotropic composite that achieves hierarchical alignment through coupled shear–stretch orientation. The multiscale structural anisotropy establishes a direct linkage between orientation and electromagnetic interactions, resulting in tunable, angle-dependent absorption and reversible transitions among transparent, absorption, and shielding states. Incorporation of a negative Poisson’s ratio framework enables deformation-sensitive or -insensitive modulation of electromagnetic wave reflectance and tunable camouflage across microwave, infrared, and optical regimes. Beyond tunable EM behavior, the composite efficiently converts absorbed microwave energy into electrical outputs, realizing self-powered and energy-harvesting functionalities. This work outlines a general design concept that integrates orientation engineering with mechanical adaptability, opening new directions for intelligent, multifunctional electromagnetic materials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and materials

Aqueous dispersion of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (TNWDM-M8) was purchased from Chengdu Organic Chemistry Co., Ltd. of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Calcium chloride (CaCl2), and sodium alginate were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. PDMS and curing agent (DC184/SYLGARD184) were purchased from Dow Corning Co., Ltd. All the reagents were directly used without further purification.

Synthesis of quasi-uniaxial anisotropic films, auxetic frameworks, and thermoelectric devices

Fibers was fabricated via wet spinning. The spinning solution contained 14 wt% of multiwalled carbon nanotubes and 86 wt% sodium alginates. It was cured in calcium chloride solution with the spinning rate of 7.5 ml/min and a draw ratio of 1.0. The wet-spun fibers were tightly wound on a paperboard, then casted with polydimethylsiloxane, and finally cut into square films. These films were glued into a rotating-polygon auxetic frameworks. The size of films is 1.5 × 2.5 cm2 for electromagnetic waveguide measurement, 3 × 3 cm2 for arch measurement, and 1 × 1 cm2 for thermoelectric devices. For cell-type thermoelectric device, the films were respectively attached to the P-N junction substrate and gelatin-base films of the same size (85). Using a microwave source as the heat source, the temperature difference between the upper and lower surfaces of the materials was created to achieve the thermoelectric conversion.

Material characterization

The morphology and structure were observed by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (S-4800,5 kV) and a field-emission transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL, JEM- 2100F, 200 kV). The crystalline phase of the products was analyzed by Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Bruker, D8-Advance X-ray diffractometer, Germany) using Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation. The small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) patterns were obtained with a Xenocs XEUSS 2.0 SAXS system utilizing and a Cu-Kα source and a Thesea Pilatus 3 R 200 K-A detector, and the polarized Raman spectra (Raman, Renishaw in via spectrometer) for evaluating the fiber orientation. A commercial microwave source (2.45 GHz, maximum power 200 W) was used to irradiate the quasi-uniaxial anisotropic composite material. An infrared (IR) thermal imager (FLIR E75) was used to detect the IR thermal radiation temperature of hyper-uniaxial anisotropic composite material. The voltage and current curve data were measured using the Keithley 2636B source meter. The electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding effectiveness (SE) parameters were recorded in the X and Ku bands based on the wave-guide method using a Keysight N5234A vector network analyzer, which was calibrated using the through-reflect-line method. The reflection loss data measured in the microwave anechoic chamber were obtained from the arch-shaped testing system (Keysight/E4991B).
