Characterizing dynamics of PRRSV RNA positivity over time in sows after live virus exposure and associated risk factors based on TOSc samples
Authors: Peng Li, Paul Yeske, Swami Jayaraman, Danyang Zhang, Guilherme Arruda Cezar, Mafalda Mil-Homens, Isadora Machado, Daniel C A Moraes, Thinh Tran Pham Tien, Mariah Negri Musskopf, Elisa De Conti, Elly K Kirwa, Ching-Yang Wang, Gustavo S Silva, Giovani Trevisan, Daniel C L Linhares
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the sows constitute a major source of porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) infection in piglets, undetected PRRSV in sows poses a significant challenge to the success of PRRSV control and elimination programs. The dynamics of PRRSV persistence in individual sows remains unanswered majorly because of the lack of practical individual sampling tools. The objective of this study was to characterize the dynamics of PRRSV RNA positivity in sows and associated risk factors using a practical Tonsil-Oral-Scrubbing (TOSc) collection method on sows over a complete reproductive cycle. This field study was conducted in a commercial breed-to-wean farm intending to eliminate PRRSV by herd closure and using live virus inoculation (LVI) for whole herd exposure after an outbreak. Pre- and post-LVI, periodic TOSc samplings at four-week intervals were performed on 275 conveniently selected sows with evenly distributed parity categories (Parity 0, parity 1–2, and parity 3 and above) and production phases categories (wean-breed, early gestation, middle gestation, late gestation, and farrowing) until 24 weeks post-LVI.ResultsOverall, PRRSV RNA positivity declined over time from the first sampling point- 2 days before LVI until the end of the trial. At 16 weeks post-LVI, the PRRSV RNA positivity reached 4.43%, below the defined threshold of a low prevalence scenario of 10% positivity, and remained low prevalence until the end of the study. In the low prevalence scenario, there was a rebound of positivity to 8.00% at 24 weeks post-LVI. Parity demonstrated a significant effect on PRRSV RNA positivity in sows. At a high prevalence scenario (>10% positivity), both P0 and P1-P2 groups constantly showed significantly higher PRRSV RNA positivity than the P3 + group (p<0.05 for all pairwise comparisons). The P0 positivity was consistently numerically higher than P1-P2. The production phase effect was evident when the herd reached low prevalence (<10% positivity); sows in farrowing had consistently higher PRRSV RNA positivity than sows in other production phases. The mean positivity increased to 28.56% and 34.49% in the farrowing group at 20 weeks post-LVI and 24 weeks post-LVI, respectively.ConclusionsPRRSV RNA positivity in sows based on TOSc samples decreased over time and reached low prevalence at 16 weeks post-LVI. PRRSV RNA positivity decreased significantly with the increase in parity. And the production phase effect on PRRSV RNA positivity was more evident at low prevalence compared to high prevalence scenarios.Keywords: PRRSV, Detection, TOSc, Sows, Across time, Parity, Production phase

Background

One major problem for PRRSV control and elimination in the breeding herd is the persistence of PRRSV at herd level, increasing time-to-stability (TTS) [1–3], which is defined as number of weeks from the outbreak to achieve diagnostic evidence of a sustained lack of viremia in pigs at weaning for 13 consecutive weeks according to the American Association of Swine Veterinarians (AASV) [4]. It is not uncommon for producers to report a positive PRRSV RNA result after consecutive PCR-negative results from piglet samples before reaching stability [2]. This indicates that the virus manages to circulate in the herd at low prevalence for an extended time. There are several well-established population-based sample types, such as processing fluids (PF) [5], family oral fluids (FOF) [6, 7], and post-mortem tongue fluids (TF) [8], to monitor PRRSV activity in farrowing rooms. These sampling approaches majorly target suckling piglets, with FOF targeting both sows and piglets in the farrowing room, and could miss PRRSV activity at the sow level, especially those still in gestation. As sows are one of the major sources of PRRSV infection to piglets [9–11], understanding the dynamics of prolonged PRRSV infection in individual sows is essential to developing successful control and elimination programs.

PRRSV carrier state is defined as clinically normal but persistently infected individuals after PRRSV infection [12]. It was reported that around 4 weeks post live virus inoculation (LVI), serum was consistently PCR-negative for PRRSV RNA, indicating the end of viremia [13, 14]. However, PRRSV RNA was still detected in lymphoid tissues including the tonsils for up to 251 days, demonstrating the possibility of shedding for extended time [13, 14]. Those carriers can potentially contribute to the persistence of PRRSV infection at the herd level [15–17]. However, most of the knowledge related to PRRSV dynamics was done on weaned piglets under controlled conditions [13], and little research has been done to understand the dynamics of PRRSV detection in individual sows under contemporary commercial conditions (i.e., naturally infected sows of mixed immune status in large breeding herds). Commonly used sample types for sows, such as serum and tonsil scraping, are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and animal-invasive because they require the snaring of sows [12, 18–21].

Despite the clinical consequences associated with deliberately exposing sows to wild-type PRRSV, whole-herd LVI in breeding herds is still a practice adopted by some veterinarians in the US as part of the PRRSV outbreak response plan [22]. Some questions on the dynamics of PRRSV infection in the sow population remain unanswered. For example, how long does it take to reach low prevalence in sows after LVI? What are the risk factors affecting the sows’ PRRSV dynamics? These questions remain unanswered mostly because of a lack of easy and practical individual sampling tools to screen sows for PRRSV. Currently, an easy and practical Tonsil-Oral-Scrubbing (TOSc) collection method was developed by taking biological samples consisting of fluids from the oral and tonsillar area of a sampled sow within seconds without snaring the sows and shows comparable positivity with tonsil scraping [18, 19, 23]. Thus, the objective of this study was to characterize PRRSV RNA positivity in sows and associated risk factors using TOSc samples over a complete reproduction cycle-from breed to farrowing. Two specific aims were:

Aim 1– To reveal the dynamics of PRRSV RNA detection in individual sows over time following whole-herd exposure to LVI.

Aim 2– To characterize risk factors associated with PRRSV RNA positivity based on TOSc samples.

Results

Characterization of PRRSV RNA positivity changes in sows based on TOSc over time

In general, the PRRSV RNA positivity decreased over time since the first sampling point- 2 days before LVI. At 16 weeks post-LVI, the positivity reached 4.43%, which was below the defined threshold of low prevalence scenario of 10% positivity and remained low prevalence until the end of the study. In the low prevalence scenario, there was a rebound of positivity, which was defined as an increase of mean positivity compared to the previous sampling point, to 8.00% at 24 weeks post-LVI. Discussion

PRRSV is a difficult virus to eliminate as it can persist for an extended time at the herd level [2]. After acute infection, PRRSV can still be detected in lymphoid tissues such as tonsil up to 251 days post experimental infection [20, 21]. PRRSV carrier state is defined as clinically normal but persistently infected pigs after PRRSV infection [12]. Within herds, the virus becomes endemic through transmission from carrier animals to susceptible animals introduced into the population through birth or purchase [15–17]. It was estimated that 7% of pigs harbor live PRRSV in tonsillar areas 25 weeks after exposure under controlled settings [13]. To investigate the role and proportion of PRRSV carriers is crucial to the control and elimination of the virus in the field.

Current PRRSV herd status classification [4] and monitoring are based on suckling piglet sample types and might miss active PRRSV activity in sows [5, 6, 8]. Under commercial settings, reproductive performance returned to baseline production 12–16 weeks post outbreak, while PRRSV RNA can still be detected in piglet samples such as PF and serum until around 34 weeks post outbreak [2], which however somehow can be extended and lead to delayed time to stability. The reason of why PRRSV RNA remained longer than reproductivity recovery and occasionally extended over time remains unknow. As sows are a major source of PRRSV to suckling piglets [9], it is crucial to investigate those carrier sows, by employing sample types targeting tonsillar areas. In contrast to conventional tonsil scraping methods, which requires snaring the sows, TOSc is a deliberately designed and developed sampling method which can target sow’s tonsillar areas without snaring them while showing similar decreasing trend in detecting PRRSV RNA over-time [18, 19, 23].

This study characterized changes of PRRSV RNA positivity over time on 275 sows using TOSc samples and analyzed associated risk factors of parity and production phase. Ideally, a mixed probit model would be used with individual sow identification number as random effects. However, the model would not converge by incorporating random effects under the context of perfect separation as P3 + groups were all PRRSV RT-rtPCR negative at 16 and 20 weeks post-LVI. Thus, a bias reduction model was employed [3, 24].

Results showed that PRRSV RNA positivity showed a linear trend of decrease since the first sampling time point- 2 days before LVI with an overall positivity of 80%, indicating that most of the sows were already infected 45 days after outbreak. Then PRRSV RNA positivity decreased to 4.43% at 16 weeks post LVI, under 10%, the threshold of low prevalence, which was defined in accordance with AASV guidelines of PRRSV status classification based on weaned piglets [4]. This decrease was consistent with a previous study, which documented a similar decrease of positivity from 66.3% at 30 days post-LVI to 3.28% at 90 days post LVI in 61 sows using TOSc [18]. A meta-analysis based on 19 refereed publications, mostly carried out on weaned piglets under controlled settings, reported a mean PRRSV PCR positivity of 30% at 12 weeks post-LVI based on tonsillar samples [13]. In our study, at 12 weeks post LVI, PCR positivity on sows TOSc was 13.86%. The difference in positivity can potentially be attributed to two factors: (i) age of the animals: the previous study was based on studies of weaned piglets, while this study was carried out on sows. The older the animal, the quicker the virus tends to be cleared [25]; (ii) pre-existing immunity: the previous study was based on data of tonsillar samples from naïve animals under controlled settings while our study was carried on sows under commercial settings and LVI was performed when the sows were already infected.

A rebound of PRRSV RNA positivity after the sow herd reached low prevalence was also documented in this study. As sows are one of the major sources of PRRSV transmission to piglets [9], the reported rebound of PRRSV positivity might contribute to explaining the delayed time to stability based on samples from suckling piglet. Particularly in this case, the herd did not show signs of reaching stability 24 weeks post-LVI, as suggested by low Ct values and high positivity in PF samples from suckling piglets (data not shown in this manuscript). The rebound in PRRSV positivity in sows was attributed to the persistence of same virus causing outbreak rather than an introduction of a new strain into the herd as whole genome sequencing result of TOSc and PF samples 24 weeks post LVI demonstrated contigs showing over 99.2% similarity with the strain from LVI material (data not shown in this manuscript). The extension and rebound of PRRSV positivity in sows also underscore the need for further research in investigating the roles of those carrier sows and developing and evaluating new strategies of managing them such as test and removal.

Some reports documented intermittent PRRSV RNA detection in pigs, most of which were performed in weaned piglets using sample types of serum and processing fluids [5, 26]. In this study, an intermittent detection of PRRSV RNA in TOSc samples from the same individual sow was also observed. An intermittently detected sow (IDS) was defined as a sow having TOSc tested positive at a certain timepoint X but TOSc tested negative at any timepoints before X. No obvious characteristics were observed within IDS at high prevalence. However, most of the positive sow were IDS, and the majority of IDS were at the farrowing phase at low prevalence, especially at 20 and 24 weeks post-LVI. Thus, the IDS contributed significantly to the rebound in the positivity at low prevalence. Nevertheless, whether the intermittent detection in TOSc samples was attributable to the fact of consistency of this sample type warrants further research.

Some veterinarians in the US still use LVI part of their strategy to control PRRSV in breeding herds after outbreaks [22]. The intent of LVI is to homogenize exposure of the breeding stock to the circulating farm-specific PRRSV strain, which may accelerate herd immunity development and lead to quicker TTS compared to modified live virus (MLV) vaccine [1, 27]. In addition, the LVI strategy avoids the introduction of vaccine-like virus strains into the herd, which may require additional diagnostic tests for differentiation between wild-type and vaccine-like [28]. However, LVI carries inherent risks, including increased clinical disease severity, higher mortality, and particularly if the field strain is highly pathogenic or not well-characterized [1]. Also, when there are multiple PRRSV strains present in the LVI, there is risk of recombination, generating new wild-type strains. LVI may also contain other pathogens beyond PRRSV, which contribute to breeding herd health instability. Moreover, regulatory restrictions, biosecurity concerns, and animal welfare considerations may limit the feasibility of using LVI in certain regions or production systems.

Test and removal of positive sows based on antibody and RT-PCR testing results using serum samples has been reported as a method for eliminating PRRSV in sow farms, however, the reported herd sizes were small relative to the USA’s current average, ranging from 200 to 1,500 sows [29, 30]. As the field practitioners are dealing with much larger herd sizes nowadays, using molecular assays on TOSc samples, a more labor-efficient and animal-welfare-friendly sample type than serum, to test and remove PRRSV-positive sows at the end of herd closure might serve as a potentially practical approach for achieving stability sooner [18, 19, 23].

The inverse correlation of PRRSV RNA positivity and sow parity was consistent with previous reports documenting the effect of parity and age on PRRSV positivity [25]. The parity effect was numerically consistent with but not statistically evident at low prevalence scenarios compared with high prevalence scenarios. This could be possibly due to the inadequate sample size required to separate subtle differences in positivity, for example 5%, at low prevalence. Moreover, sows in farrowing were more likely to test positive than contemporary gestating sows at low prevalence. However, gestating sows had greater positivity at high prevalence. This contrasting result can possibly be because farrowing is a stressful event for sows, which could possibly re-activate the replication and shedding of PRRSV [14], but the effect of farrowing was covered by other factors such as parity at high prevalence. Taken together, the effect of parity and farrowing phase on PRRSV RNA positivity has important applications when identifying and managing carrier sows, especially in low prevalence scenarios.

In this study, the TOSc sampling of 275 sows was completed within 6 h with a group of 3 persons at each time point. This is consistent with reports that conducted test-removal of ASFV-infected sows in China, from which the idea of TOSc originated [31]. This also demonstrated the feasibility and ease of using TOSc for sampling a large number of sows in real settings and highlighted the potential application of TOSc as an easy individual sample type, especially when a large sample size is needed in the sow population, including but not limited to (i) investigate positivity on sows for PRRSV status classification of sows in the herd; (ii) estimate PRRSV prevalence in sows at low prevalence scenarios; and (iii) to test and segregate/remove individually infected sows.

One limitation of this study is the lack of information on other traits of studied sows, such as body score [32] or whether the sows were recycled or not. Another limitation was that the observation and analysis was generated from data from only one herd. Further studies are needed to include more herds and to investigate the effect of those factors on PRRSV positivity in sows with larger sample size.

Conclusions

PRRSV RNA positivity in sows based on TOSc samples decreased over time and reached low prevalence 16 weeks post-LVI. PRRSV RNA positivity was higher in lower parity sows compared to higher parity. The production phase effect was more evident at low prevalence with farrowing phase exhibiting higher PRRSV RNA positivity than other production phases compared to high prevalence scenarios.

Methods

Study design

This field study was conducted in a Midwestern USA commercial breed-to-wean farm intending to eliminate PRRSV by herd closure and LVI process after a PRRSV outbreak. The farm had 2,500 sows and operated on a continuous production flow. The farm was classified as PRRSV status III, i.e., provisional negative with undetectable PRRSV RNA by RT-qPCR in weaning age suckling pig population, according to AASV PRRSV breeding herd classification, before the most recent outbreak with a PRRSV L1C.5 strain of RFLP 1-4-4 [4]. Before-LVI (45 days after outbreak) and post-LVI intramuscularly, periodic TOSc samplings at four-week intervals until 24 weeks post-LVI were performed on conveniently selected 275 sows with evenly distributed parity and production phases. Fifty-five sows were conveniently allocated in each of five production phases: wean-breed, 1–4 weeks after breeding; early gestation, 5–8 weeks after breeding; middle gestation, 9–12 weeks after breeding; late gestation, 13 weeks after breeding until farrowing; farrowing, from farrowing to weaning. In each production phase, three categories of parities were distributed among 55 sows: parity 0 animals (P0) (n = 19, range = 16–21), parity 1 (P1) and parity 2 (P2) animals (P1/P2) (n = 18, range = 16–20), and parity 3 and above animals (P3+) (n = 18, range = 16–20).

LVI procedure

As previously described [23], serum was collected from suckling piglets with clinical signs of PRRS (i.e., weak, fuzzy, and fevering.) within 30 days of outbreak and sent for PRRSV RNA testing by RT-rtPCR. All the confirmed positive samples were pooled and stored in a -20℃ freezer until use. As per veterinary recommendations, serum was diluted with PBS to appropriate CTs (around 30). Two mL of diluted serum was injected intramuscularly into each gilt, sow and boar in the herd.

Sampling scheme and data collection

TOSc samples were collected over time from the same animals (275 sows) at 2 days before LVI and 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 weeks post-LVI. Sows were traced by stapling unique stickers to the sow card. Information on sow ID, production phase were collected and verified for each sampling points.

TOSc collection for sows

TOSc samples were collected with a team of 3 people without restraining the sows as previously described [18, 19, 23]. Briefly, people A in the front of the row identified the sow by referring to the sow card and helped open the crate. Then collector, held by people B, was inserted into the sow’s mouth, directed toward the oropharyngeal and tonsil area, and scrubbed back and forth for ten seconds (Fig. 4). The qualified sample was viscous and mucous-like. In the end, the tip of the rod with the TOSc sample was severed by people C and transferred to a 50 ml tube (Corning Science, Tamaulipas, Mexico) with 3 ml of PBS. The 50 ml tubes were then labeled by people C. The clipper to sever the clip was disinfected with a disinfecting wipe (Clorox® Disinfecting Wipes, Clorox Company, US) and then dried with paper towel before next round of use.

TOSc collector and TOSc collection process. (A) Example of a TOSc collector made of artificial insemination catheter, rubber thimble and cotton gauze. (B) TOSc collection process in a non-restrained sow

Sampling size justification

A group of 275 sows were repeatedly sampled at different time points, which assured enough sample size (249 sows) to estimate prevalence from 1 to 10%, assuming RT-qPCR test with a herd sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 95%, and a confidence level of 95% and 80% power using Epitools (https://epitools.ausvet.com.au) with an additional of 10% more sows (25 sows) in case of loss to follow up [33].

A minimum sample size of 49 sow was justified to detect 15% difference of positivity between different groups when positivity ranged from 10 to 95% in a two-sided test with a confidence level of 95% and 85% power using Epitools (https://epitools.ausvet.com.au). Around 10% more sows (5 sows) than needed were added in case of loss to follow up [33].

Diagnostic testing

All samples were tested individually at the Iowa State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory for PRRSV RNA by real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-rtPCR), on a fee for services basis and using commercially available assays. Ct value < 40 was considered PRRSV positive [18, 19, 23].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to report changes in PRRSV RNA positivity and characteristics of IDS over time. A Probit regression model using the brglm function (Bias Reduction in Binomial-Response Generalized Linear Models) was used to assess the effects on PRRSV RNA positivity as a function of collection time, parity and production phase and the interactions between collection time and parity, interactions between collection time and production phase, and interactions between parity and production phase [3, 24]. The Tukey-Kramer test was used to compare the post hoc pairwise differences in positivity between groups at each collection time. All analyses were performed using the brglm package from R program 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2019).
