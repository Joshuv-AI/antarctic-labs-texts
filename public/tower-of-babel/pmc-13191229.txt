Open transosseous repair of proximal deltoid muscle rupture following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty in a patient with os acromiale: a case report
Authors: Elise Ketelaar, Heinz Hoenecke, Josiah Valk, David Sosnoski
Published: 2026

Introduction

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has become increasingly utilized to restore shoulder function, improve quality of life, and reduce pain in patients with glenohumeral osteoarthritis, massive irreparable rotator cuff tears, rotator cuff arthropathy, among other conditions. Reported outcomes following rTSA have been generally favorable, including patients with os acromiale.3 However, rTSA is not without complications. Commonly reported complications include scapular notching, infection, instability, implant loosening, and periprosthetic fracture. Acromial and scapular spine fractures are well recognized, with a reported incidence rate of 0.6%-15.8%, and are thought to be due to the altered biomechanics of the shoulder following reverse shoulder arthroplasty, placing a greater demand on the deltoid muscle, especially with more modern shoulder arthroplasty design.1

Deltoid rupture following rTSA is far less commonly reported but may occur secondary to altered shoulder biomechanics that place increased tension on the deltoid muscle. Medialization of the center of rotation increases deltoid recruitment while reducing reliance on the rotator cuff. This increased mechanical demand may lead to muscle fatigue, rupture of the muscle belly, or avulsion of the deltoid origin, most commonly involving the anterolateral portion of the deltoid. Loss of this abduction moment can result in profound functional limitations, particularly with overhead activities. Treatment options for deltoid rupture following rTSA are limited and include direct repair, rotational muscle flaps, or tendon transfer procedures such as pectoralis major or latissimus dorsi transfer.

To our knowledge, there is limited published literature addressing deltoid rupture following rTSA, particularly in patients with os acromiale. Whatley et al4 reported 3 cases of deltoid rupture following rTSA performed through a deltopectoral approach; all patients had a history of prior open or mini-open rotator cuff repair. The authors suggested that inadequate reattachment of the deltoid to the acromion during prior surgery may have contributed to failure in 2 cases and noted that massive rotator cuff tears represented an additional risk factor.

We report a case of proximal deltoid muscle rupture avulsion following rTSA in a patient with a meta-meso os acromiale and describe a novel repair technique utilizing margin convergence, sleeve advancement, and intra-osseous acromial bone tunnels. The patient provided informed consent for publication of this case.

Case presentation

Description of index procedure

The patient initially presented with significant pain in the right shoulder and was found to have significant glenohumeral osteoarthritis with a high-riding humeral head and decreased acromiohumeral distance. She did have a known asymptomatic os acromiale. (Figs. 1a-c). The patient elected to undergo rTSA. The reverse shoulder arthroplasty components inserted were a 36-mm Stryker Tournier Aequalis glenosphere with a Tournier Perform humeral stem. A 3-mm polyethylene was used and was found to be stable with trial reduction. Stryker Blueprint software was used for pre-operative planning. Lateralization and distalization was accounted for and measured. With the patient's significant glenoid wear, there was substantial medialization, with approximately 25 mm measured glenoid vault. Due to significant medialization, a 3-mm humeral head graft was used to create a slight wedge and correct for retroversion and medialization. This did result in slight lateralization of the glenosphere. Therefore, a standard 36-mm glenosphere with no built-in lateralization was used. Following rTSA the patient was able to achieve up to 80° of active forward flexion and 30° of external rotation at neutral compared to the nonoperative shoulder, which reached 110° of forward flexion, 30° of external rotation, and 90° of abduction.Figure 1(a-c) Pre-operative radiographs demonstrating considerable glenohumeral osteoarthritis, a high-riding humeral head with decreased acromiohumeral distance, and a significant os acromiale (black arrow).

Initial examination

The patient was an active 57-year-old female who presented 11 months after right rTSA with an acute inability to actively elevate the arm. On physical examination, active shoulder elevation was limited to approximately 30°-40°, achieved primarily through scapulothoracic motion. Passive range of motion (ROM) of the shoulder was 140° of flexion, 90° abduction, 30° external rotation at arm neutral; the patient was only able to reach the iliac crest with internal rotation at arm neutral. The contralateral shoulder ROM included 140° of active and passive elevation and 90° of abduction. A palpable defect was appreciated along the lateral border of the deltoid. Ultrasound imaging demonstrated rupture of the lateral deltoid with approximately 4 cm of distal retraction. Radiographs on post-operative day 1 and at 11 months confirmed appropriate positioning of the shoulder prosthesis without evidence of loosening or hardware failure (Figs. 2a-c and 3a-c). These radiographs also demonstrated that the os acromiale had not displaced when compared to pre-operative and post-operative films from the initial procedure.Figure 2(a-c) Post-operative radiographs following index procedure and os acromiale (black arrow).Figure 3(a-c) Radiographs of shoulder prosthesis at 11 months post-operatively with unchanged os acromiale (black arrow).

After discussion of nonoperative and operative management options, the patient elected to proceed with surgical repair. Risks, benefits, and alternatives were discussed in detail prior to surgery.

Surgical technique

The patient was positioned in a semisitting beach-chair position, and the operative extremity was supported using an Arthrex arm holder. After standard sterile preparation and draping, a saber-type incision was made lateral to the acromion.

The anterior deltoid remained attached to the acromion. Palpable motion was noted at the acromion, consistent with the known os acromiale. Dissection was carried through the subcutaneous tissue to the lateral edge of the acromion, where dense scar tissue extending toward the deltoid muscle was identified. This tissue was excised, and adhesiolysis was performed to mobilize the retracted deltoid edge. It was confirmed that the os acromiale was intact and had not been disrupted/displaced.

Four FiberTape sutures were placed through the deltoid in a Mason-Allen configuration (Fig. 4). Given relatively poor acromial bone quality, intraosseous tunnels were created using a blunt needle, and a 0 Vicryl suture shuttle was passed from inferior to superior through the acromion medial to the os acomiale. The authors made sure to avoid any suture tunnels through the os itself. The FiberTape sutures were passed through these tunnels and used to reapproximate the deltoid to its acromial origin (Fig. 5).Figure 4FiberTape sutures were placed through the blunt edges of the deltoid (black arrows) in Mason-Allen configuration.Figure 5Two intraosseous bone tunnels were used to pass FiberTape sutures through the acromion (black arrow).

The sutures were tied over the superior surface of the acromion to reduce the risk of pullout. The remaining suture limbs were then used to perform sleeve advancement of the lateral deltoid edge to the proximal deltoid using horizontal mattress sutures (Fig. 6).Figure 6The suture ends were used to approximate the lateral edge of the acromion.

The wound was thoroughly irrigated, and vancomycin powder was applied prior to layered closure. The arm was placed in an abduction brace maintaining approximately 45°-60° of abduction.

Post-operative follow-up

At 1 week post-operatively, the patient reported well-controlled pain without complications. The incision was healing appropriately, and she remained neurovascularly intact. ROM testing was deferred. The patient was instructed to continue immobilization in the abduction sling for an additional five weeks.

At six weeks, pain had significantly improved. A residual palpable defect of approximately 1 cm mediolateral and 4 cm anteroposterior remained but was improved compared to pre-operative examination. The patient was advised to gradually wean from the abduction pillow while continuing sling use for an additional 2 to three weeks. Physical therapy (PT) was initiated with passive and active-assisted forward flexion, which was limited by patient tolerance level, with a gradual increase in passive ROM and active assisted ROM with PT only. Active forward flexion and abduction were restricted until nine weeks post-operatively, and non–weight-bearing status was maintained. After 9 weeks active flexion and abduction were restricted to 90°, and the patient continued with passive and active assisted ROM.

At two months, the patient continued to improve clinically, with a stable palpable defect. Passive and active forward flexion exercises were continued.

At six months, the palpable defect persisted with no change in dimensions. Ultrasound evaluation demonstrated intact deltoid continuity and insertion. The authors believe that the residual defect may have been due to intraoperative removal of scar tissue, after significant overgrowth of muscle scarring from the injured deltoid musculature, giving a perceived defect on examination when, in reality, the underlying tendon remained completely intact. Active forward flexion measured approximately 25° in the standing position and 120° supine, with approximately 40° of abduction. Passive motion was noted to be 130° of forward flexion. PT was continued.

At nine months, the patient reported continued improvement, with approximately 50° of abduction, 45° of external rotation, and internal rotation to L1. Patient-reported outcome measure was noted to be 130° of forward flexion. Therapy was continued.

At twelve months, the patient reported minimal pain and demonstrated approximately 70° of active forward flexion and abduction with improved strength compared to pre-operative examination. Notably, this did compare to the patient's active ROM shortly after the initial rTSA, which as described previously was approximately 80° of forward flexion. The patient elected to continue PT given ongoing functional gains.

Discussion

As briefly noted, deltoid rupture following rTSA may be attributed to the increased stress/tension placed on the muscle due to alterations in the normal biomechanics of the shoulder. These alterations include medialization of the center of rotation, which leads to an increased moment arm and recruitment of the deltoid muscle. Fenwick et al2 investigated the changes in deltoid tension following rTSA using shear wave elastography. They measured pre-operative and post-operative muscle elasticity and stiffness in 18 patients who underwent rTSA and found a higher muscle tension in the deltoid in patients post rTSA compared to nonoperated extremity across all examined areas under relaxed conditions.2 This difference was most significant in the pars clavicularis and pars acromialis.2 Under isometric load all regions of the deltoid demonstrated significantly increased elasticity. However, they found no correlation between clinical or functional outcomes and elastography findings.2 This supports the idea that increased deltoid stress following rTSA may put patients at higher risk for deltoid injury.

That being said, deltoid rupture following rTSA is a rare complication and the exact incidence of this problem remains largely unknown, with most published literature involving altered mechanics following rTSA causing acromial stress fractures. To date, there has been little published literature detailing treatment options for proximal deltoid injury following rTSA, and to our knowledge, this is the first report of such injury in a patient with os acromiale.

Although biomechanical studies specific to this scenario are lacking, we hypothesize that instability at the os acromiale fragment may have contributed to altered deltoid force transmission, predisposing the muscle to fatigue and eventual rupture rather than the more commonly observed acromial stress fracture. Prior studies have demonstrated that downward tilting of an os acromiale fragment may lead to subacromial impingement and rotator cuff pathology; however, the effects on deltoid origin stability remain poorly defined. Further biomechanical investigation is warranted.

In addition, given the patient's significant glenoid wear, a humeral head graft was used to correct for retroversion/medialization during the initial rTSA procedure. After consideration and review of literature regarding the significance of this patient's medialized glenoid, the authors did opt for the added benefit of implant fixation at the risk of potentially increasing lateralization to cause further stress on the acromion and accompanying deltoid muscle. Regardless, this may have been a factor that contributed to deltoid failure in this patient as the added lateralization could have placed more stretch on the musculature.

This case demonstrates that direct deltoid repair using margin convergence, sleeve advancement, and intraosseous acromial fixation can restore meaningful function while avoiding more invasive reconstructive procedures such as muscle transfers or even shoulder arthrodesis as a salvage procedure. Although full preinjury function was not achieved, the patient experienced significant functional improvement and pain relief.

Conclusion

Open direct deltoid repair using intraosseous suture tunnels with sleeve advancement augmentation may represent a viable surgical option for proximal deltoid rupture following rTSA in patients with os acromiale. This technique may restore upper extremity function while avoiding complex reconstructive procedures. Further clinical and biomechanical studies are needed to better define optimal management strategies for this rare complication.
