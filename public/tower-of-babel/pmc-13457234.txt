Comparative efficacy of irrigation activation systems in root canal cleanliness: a region-specific analysis of debris and smear layer removal
Authors: Yuwang Tu, Yanqing Wang, Guanwen Sun, Suli Xiao, Yijun Li, Lixuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare the efficacy of four irrigation activation systems against conventional needle irrigation (CNI) in removing debris and smear layer from root canal. Forty human single-rooted teeth premolars were instrumented to a consistent size and randomly divided into five groups (n = 8/group), namely CNI, and other activation groups including EDDY, Gentlefile, passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), and shock wave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming (SWEEPS) techniques. After irrigation, the roots were split longitudinally and debris and smear layer in coronal, middle, and apical thirds were evaluated by scanning electron microscope (SEM). Scores were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction. For debris removal, the EDDY and SWEEPS groups showed significantly lower scores than CNI in the coronal and apical thirds (p < 0.05), while PUI and Gentlefile did not; in the middle third, all activation groups achieved significantly lower scores than CNI (p < 0.05). For smear layer removal, only SWEEPS was superior to CNI in the coronal third (p < 0.05); in the middle third, all activation groups achieved significantly lower scores than CNI (p < 0.05); in the apical third, EDDY and SWEEPS performed better than CNI (p < 0.01), but PUI and Gentlefile did not. Activated irrigation techniques show region- and target-dependent efficacy. Introduction

Successful root canal treatment relies on mechanical instrumentation and chemical debridement. However, mechanical instrumentation often results in debris accumulation and the smear layer often persists after mechanical instrumentation, particularly in anatomically complex areas where conventional antiseptics and irrigants struggle to penetrate [1]. The smear layer covers the root canal and the openings of the dentin tubule, which consists of bacteria, their by-products and necrotic tissue [2]. This layer not only facilitates microbial survival and proliferation but also enables pathogens to infiltrate deeper dentinal structures. Furthermore, residual debris and the smear layer hinder irrigant penetration and compromise the seal of obturation materials, ultimately threatening the long-term success of treatment [3, 4].

Root canal irrigation is a crucial part of root debridement that aims to flush debris, remove smear layer and eliminate biofilm from root canal [5]. Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) remains the gold-standard irrigant for organic tissue dissolution and antimicrobial action, while ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is used adjunctively to chelate and remove the inorganic component of the smear layer [6]. However, studies indicate that chemical irrigation alone has limited efficacy in smear layer removal, particularly in the apical third of prepared canals, regardless of instrumentation or irrigation protocols [7, 8]. Conventional needle irrigation (CNI) fails to remove debris and smear layer from the root canal irregularities adequately, with effectiveness highly dependent on needle design, placement, and canal anatomy [9].

To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed various adjunctive irrigation techniques that generate fluid agitation through distinct physical mechanisms, including acoustic microstreaming, cavitation, and photoacoustic streaming [10, 11]. Passive Ultrasonic Irrigation (PUI) emerges as a notable technique, leveraging a stainless steel file to vigorously agitate the irrigant, generating intense acoustic microstreaming and hydrodynamic cavitation [12]. Nevertheless, its applicability in curved root canals is constrained by its inability to conform to irregular root anatomies [13]. Similar to PUI, EDDY activation relies greatly on cavitation and acoustic streaming [14]. With a sonic frequency of 5000 to 6000 Hz, EDDY activates irrigation solution with a non-cutting flexible polymer tip. EDDY has been shown to be more effective than CNI in bacteria reduction, debris, and smear layer removal [15]. Laser activated irrigation (LAI) has been explored and demonstrated to enhance cleanliness and disinfection of the complex root canal system. Photon Induced Photoacoustic Streaming (PIPS) technique using Er: YAG laser, has been demonstrated to improve fluid dynamics and enhances irrigant penetration through photoacoustic streaming, resulting in superior debridement of complex canal morphologies [16]. Similar to PIPS technique, a novel shock wave-enhanced emission photoacoustic streaming (SWEEPS) modality for Er: YAG laser has been proposed, which is based on the delivery of pairs of laser pulses, properly timed to generate enhanced irrigant streaming and shock wave emission. In the SWEEPS mode, when the initial bubble is in the final stage of its collapse, the secondary bubbles exert pressure on the collapsing initial bubble to accelerate its collapse [17]. These pulses are precisely timed so that the collapse of the initial bubble is accelerated by secondary bubbles, generating enhanced shock waves and photoacoustic currents, thereby improving cleaning and antimicrobial effectiveness [18, 19].

Recently, mechanical activation systems have been introduced as an alternative irrigation approach. The Gentlefile system, a novel nickel-titanium (NiTi) instrument, features a non-cutting tip and low torque to minimize procedural risks such as ledging, canal transportation, or lateral perforation during preparation [20, 21]. The system comprises four files with a uniform 4% taper, one of which (the “yellow” file) is specifically designed for cleaning. This cleaning file consists of six flexible stainless‑steel wire strands that expand outward at high rotational speeds, generating centrifugal movement and vigorous fluid agitation. This mechanism is claimed to enhance irrigant contact with canal walls and improve debris removal. While ultrasonic, sonic, laser, and mechanically-driven irrigation systems are collectively referred to as ‘activated irrigation’, they operate through different physical principles. Whether these mechanistic differences translate into clinically meaningful differences in regional cleaning efficacy remains unclear. Moreover, numerous studies have compared the efficacy of various adjunctive irrigation techniques, most have focused on pairwise comparisons, leaving a gap in the literature regarding a comprehensive, multi-technique evaluation. Therefore, the segmental cleaning efficacy (coronal, middle, apical) of these techniques, particularly the novel Gentlefile system, remains underexplored. Therefore, this in vitro study aimed to compare the effectiveness of CNI, EDDY, PUI, Gentlefile, and SWEEPS in removing smear layer and debris from different thirds of the root canal.

Materials and methods

Tooth samples

This in vitro study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Stomatological Hospital of Xiamen College (HS20231208005). The participants were from between 18 and 30 years, and written informed consent was obtained. All freshly extracted human mandibular single-rooted premolars for orthodontic purposes were used in this study. An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software and 9 teeth per group were allowed for comparison of the quantitative variables between groups at alpha error probability of 0.05 and power of 0.95. Due to the limited availability of freshly extracted human mandibular premolars meeting the strict inclusion criteria, a total of 40 teeth (n = 8/group) were included in this study. This sample size is consistent with published in vitro irrigation studies [22, 23]. The inclusion criteria were teeth with caries-free, no cracks and resorption, or calcification, and mature root apices. The exclusion criteria are teeth with caries, restoration, root cracks, periodontal diseases and open apical foramen. The presence of only one root canal of teeth was verified through Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) before experiments. After collection, soft tissues and calculus were removed from tooth surface by scaling curettes. All samples were stored in 2% thymol solution at 4 ℃ until root canal preparation.

Preparation of root canals

After the access cavity was created, a #10 K-file was inserted into the canal until reaching root canal foramen and working length was determined by subtracting 1 mm from this measurement. The root samples were standardized to a working length of 16 mm by trimming the coronal portion of the tooth perpendicular to its long axis using a high-speed, water-cooled diamond disc [24]. To simulate in vivo clinical conditions, teeth were sealed apically using blue glue, and to prevent the glue from entering the canal, a #10 K-file was inserted before the apex was sealed [25].

The samples were mechanically prepared using Protaper Next rotary system (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) to an apical size of 25 and 0.06 taper in accordance with the manufacture’s instruction. Canals were rinsed with 1 mL 3% NaOCl between each instrument using a 31-gauge side-vented needle. Final instrumentation was followed by a 5 mL distilled water rinse to remove residual NaOCl before experimental irrigation protocols. To confirm the baseline presence of smear layer and debris, additional three teeth were randomly selected, not used in the subsequent experiments, then split longitudinally and examined under scanning electron microscope (SEM) before any irrigation or activation was performed.

Irrigation protocols

The samples were randomly distributed into a control group and four experimental groups (n = 8/group). For control group (CNI group): The irrigation procedure was performed by moving a 31-G side-perforated irrigation needle back and forth at a distance of 2 mm from the working length. The irrigant was delivered in three separate aliquots of 2 mL each for NaOCl, then three aliquots of 2 mL each for EDTA, and finally three aliquots of 2 mL each for NaOCl. Between each 2 mL delivery, a 10‑second interval was allowed, but no activation device was used. The needle was gently oscillated (± 1–2 mm) during delivery to prevent canal wall binding; no separate agitation was performed.

The irrigation and activation protocol for each experimental group was standardized as follows: (1) NaOCl irrigation: For each cycle, 2 mL of 3% NaOCl was delivered into the root canal (needle tip placed 1 mm short of the apical foramen). The assigned activation device was then operated for 20 s. After activation, the solution was aspirated, and a fresh 2 mL of 3% NaOCl was introduced for the next cycle. This procedure was repeated for a total of three cycles (total 6 mL NaOCl), with a 10‑second interval between cycles. (2) EDTA irrigation: Immediately afterward, 17% EDTA was applied using the same schedule: three 20-second cycles of activation, each with a fresh 2 mL of EDTA, separated by 10-second intervals (total 6 mL EDTA). (3) Final NaOCl irrigation: Another three cycles of 3% NaOCl (2 mL per cycle, total 6 mL) were performed following the same regimen as the first NaOCl irrigation. Upon completion of the final cycle, the root canals were flushed with 4 mL of distilled water and dried with sterile paper points.

PUI group: The irrigation was carried out by using ultrasonic tip (#15/0.02) with an ultrasonic device at a power setting of 6 (P5 Newton; Satelec Acteon). The ultrasonic tip, which was placed 2 mm away from the working length, was placed centrally in the root canal and passively moved up and down within the root canal.

PUI group: The irrigation was carried out by using ultrasonic tip (#15/0.02) with an ultrasonic device at a power setting of 6 (P5 Newton; Satelec Acteon). The ultrasonic tip, which was placed 2 mm away from the working length, was placed centrally in the root canal and passively moved up and down within the root canal.

EDDY group: After mechanically prepared, the irrigation was activated with EDDY (VDW, Munich, Germany), which was attached to a sonic cavitron device and oscillates at higher frequency (6 kHz). The polymer tip (#20/0.04) was inserted into the root canal at 3 mm from the working length with back-and-forth movements.

The polymer tip (#20/0.04) was inserted into the root canal at 3 mm from the working length with back-and-forth movements.

Gentlefile: A Gentlefile Yellow instrument (#23/0.04, MedicNRG, Israel) was inserted into the canal and positioned 2–3 mm short of the working length, and the instrument was activated using the Gentlefile Drive (MedicNRG, Israel) at 6500 rpm. The file was moved in a gentle up-and-down motion and the instrument was removed from the canal while it was rotating.

The file was moved in a gentle up-and-down motion and the instrument was removed from the canal while it was rotating.

SWEEPS: The irrigation sequence and activation times were same to the other activation groups.

Activation was performed using the same Er: YAG laser (LightWalker AT, Fotona, Ljubljana, Slovenia) at 20 mJ, 15 Hz, and 0.3 W in SWEEPS mode without air or water spray. The SWEEPS fiber tip (600/9 Sapphire Tip; Fotona) was positioned in the access cavity (pulp chamber), approximately 1–2 mm above the root canal orifices, with the tip directed toward but not inserted into the canal openings.

SEM analysis and evaluation

Before SEM observation, two grooves were prepared along the axis of roots with a diamond saw and split longitudinally into two halves. The root canals were dehydrated using serial concentration of ethanol, sputter coated with gold and observed under SEM. Two observers performed blind evaluation independently after examining 24 specimens for calibration purposes. After screening of the entire canal wall, three photomicrographs of each specimen were taken to visualize the coronal (8 mm), middle (5 mm), and apical portions (2 mm) [26]. The area presenting with the greatest amounts of debris and smear layer was selected and photographed for each third of the canal. Microphotographs from the apical to the coronal thirds of the root canal were taken at × 200 for debris and × 2000 for smear layer evaluation respectively. The grading system for debris and smear layer assessment was cited from Hulsmann et al. [27].

Statistical analysis

The agreements between the 2 examiners regarding the debris and smear layer scores were evaluated with Cohen kappa. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to analyze the normality of the variables and the data are not normally distributed. Comparison among groups were analyzed using Kruskal‑Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test with Bonferroni correction, statistical significance level was set at p<0.05. The data were analyzed with the statistical software GraphPad Prism 9.

Results

The kappa value for intra-observer agreement was 0.833, it was indicated that there was a very high agreement between the evaluators. Figure 1 showed the representative images of debris removal among all groups. Table 1 revealed the statistical analysis of debris scores among groups. In the coronal third, the EDDY and SWEEPS groups achieved significantly lower scores than the CNI group (SWEEPS vs. CNI: p < 0.05; EDDY vs. CNI: p < 0.05). PUI and Gentlefile demonstrated no significance different from either the CNI group or the SWEEPS and EDDY group (p > 0.05). In the middle third, the EDDY, PUI, Gentlefile, and SWEEPS groups performed significantly better in debris removal compared to CNI groups (PUI vs. CNI: p < 0.05; EDDY vs. CNI: p < 0.001; Gentlefile vs. CNI: p < 0.05; SWEEPS vs. CNI: p < 0.001). And there is no significance among EDDY, PUI, Gentlefile, and SWEEPS regarding debris removal in middle third (p > 0.05). In the apical region, the EDDY and SWEEPS groups removed significantly more debris than the CNI group (SWEEPS vs. CNI: p < 0.01; EDDY vs. CNI: p < 0.01). PUI and Gentlefile demonstrated no significance different from either the CNI group or the SWEEPS and EDDY group regarding debris removal (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The physical and chemical actions of endodontic irrigants facilitate the dissolution and partial elimination of smear layer on root canals. However, extensive research has shown that endodontic irrigants are not capable of removing the smear layer or debris entirely via syringe irrigation. Consequently, the advantages of incorporating activation techniques as adjunctive measures during root canal irrigation for enhanced removal of smear layer and debris are universally recognized [26, 28, 29]. This study provides the first comprehensive, segment-specific comparison of five irrigation techniques for removing smear layer and debris. To emulate clinical scenarios with precision, a tightly sealed apical system was employed to prevent air entrapment in the apical third, which could otherwise impede irrigant penetration.

In the present study, it has been shown that only SWEEPS and EDDY resulted in less debris than traditional needle irrigation in the coronal third of the root. Regarding smear layer removal, only SWEEPS activation removed more smear layer than syringe irrigation at coronal third. This discrepancy between debris and smear layer removal may be attributed to the distinct properties of these two entities. Smear layer is a mixture of bacteria, necrotic tissue, and dentin debris that adheres tightly to the dentin surface and may penetrate into dentinal tubules. In contrast, debris consists of loose granular residues, namely dentin chips, pulp tissue fragments, and bacterial aggregates, which accumulate in the root canal lumen or adhere loosely to instrument surfaces during preparation. Although PUI and Gentlefile did not outperform CNI in coronal debris and smear layer removal, this should not be interpreted as inefficacy. Rather, it reflects that in the coronal third, the baseline cleaning efficacy of CNI is already substantial for debris removal, and the performance of these activation techniques is comparable to this baseline. The wide coronal region allows achieve a relatively high baseline of cleaning and may conceal the real benefits of activation techniques like PUI or Gentlefile. The larger dentin tubules and the high efficacy of chelating agents in the coronal region make it an anatomically less challenging area.

When comparing the cleaning effectiveness in the middle third, the results revealed that PUI, EDDY, Gentlefile and SWEEPS techniques display significantly better smear layer and debris removal compared to CNI. Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the superiority of activated irrigation over conventional methods in the middle third [26, 30]. When it comes to middle third, the canal diameter in this area is large enough to accommodate the file tips or allow uninhibited bubble expansion. Moreover, the middle third is less prone to the “vapor lock” effect. The continuous fluid exchange in this region ensures that the chemical action of NaOCl and EDTA is fully potentiated by physical agitation, allowing for synergistic disruption of both loosely adherent debris and the more tenacious smear layer. Interestingly, Gentlefile showed inspiring results in removing smear layer and debris at middle portions, achieving an effectiveness comparable to other activation techniques. The satisfactory result of Gentlefile was also reported by previous study, where evaluated the cleaning ability of Gentlefile and demonstrate that Gentlefile exhibits superior smear layer removal compared to the Propaper NiTi system [31]. The high rotational speed of the Gentlefile likely generates sufficient hydrodynamic shear stresses, facilitating the detachment of adherent substances from the root canal walls [32].

According to the results of our study, in all activation methods, the least removal of smear layer and debris was observed in the apical third region. Our results showed that, for debris removal, both SWEEPS and EDDY resulted in significantly less debris compared to CNI in the apical third, while PUI and Gentlefile demonstrated no significant difference from CNI. For smear layer removal, both SWEEPS and EDDY resulted in significantly less smear layer than CNI. This result aligns with previous reports highlighting the apical third as the most challenging zone for irrigant activation [33, 34]. The narrower diameter, increased curvature, and reduced irrigant exchange in the apical third likely limit the physical effects of cavitation and acoustic streaming. From the data obtained in this study, SWEEPS technique showed low scores on smear layer and debris removal of the whole section of root canal, although it was not statistically superior to EDDY, PUI, or Gentlefile. This observation partially aligns with those reports that demonstrated favorable cleaning efficacy of SWEEPS [26, 35, 36], but it also suggests that the advantage of SWEEPS over other activation methods may be less pronounced than previously thought. The favorable performance of SWEEPS over CNI in the apical third may be attributed to its unique mechanism: the precisely timed laser pulse pairs generate shock wave emissions that create strong shear forces independent of canal morphology [37]. Unlike acoustic streaming-dependent techniques, SWEEPS does not require free tip vibration and thus is not constrained by the narrow apical space. Meanwhile, EDDY was also effective for smear layer and debris removal in the apical third. Although both PUI and EDDY rely on the acoustic‑streaming effect, unlike EDDY, PUI showed no significant difference from CNI in the apical third. The comparison of cleaning efficacy between PUI and EDDY was reported a wide range of results, with EDDY demonstrating instances where it removed significantly less, nearly equivalent, or even substantially more debris or smear layer than PUI [38]. The ultrasonic tip typically operates at a frequency of 30 kHz and an amplitude of 75 μm, while the EDDY tip operates at a frequency of 6 kHz and an amplitude of 346 μm. In the apical third, the oscillation amplitude may have a greater impact on the penetration of irrigation fluid than the oscillation frequency [39]. The higher amplitude may facilitate the removal efficacy of EDDY in apical segments. Gentlefile also displayed no significant difference in comparison with CNI concerning smear layer and debris removal in apical third. The reason behind the phenomenon may be the reduced canal diameter of apical area restricts the free rotation and lateral movement of the Gentlefile instrument, thereby diminishing the hydrodynamic forces responsible for debris displacement and smear layer disruption. It should be noted that, even though SWEEPS and EDDY are superior to CNI, they did not achieve complete cleaning in the apical third. This persistent limitation underscores the fundamental challenge of apical cleaning and suggests that optimal results may require combined strategies such as prolonged EDTA rinsing time or the use of chelators with enhanced wetting properties.

However, there were some limitations in the present study. Firstly, it should be pointed that the in vitro results based on straight root canals, the findings may not be fully translatable to the complex anatomy of curved canals and isthmuses commonly encountered in clinics. Secondly, the evaluation was focused on smear layer and debris removal and did not assess critical biological outcomes such as biofilm elimination or long-term treatment success. Thirdly, the SEM analysis was performed at high magnification on a limited number of fields per sample, which may not fully represent the overall cleanliness of root canal due to the potentially irregular distribution of debris and smear layer. This sampling bias is inherent to SEM‑based studies. These limitations, however, directly point to necessary future research directions: validating these findings in curved canals and employing biofilm models to assess antimicrobial efficacy.

Conclusion

Activated irrigation techniques show region- and target-dependent efficacy. All activation groups demonstrated significant improvements over CNI in the middle third, but only EDDY and SWEEPS maintained this significant improvement in the apical third.
