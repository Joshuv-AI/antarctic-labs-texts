Near-SUSY to Non-SUSY Crossover
Dan Kondo, Hitoshi Murayama, and Bea Noether

Abstract
Gauge theories can be solved exactly slightly away from the supersymmetric (SUSY) limit softly broken by anomaly mediation when the size of SUSY breaking is much smaller than the dynamical scale ( m ≪ Λ m\ll\Lambda ). We show empirical evidence that the near-SUSY limit is continuously connected to the non-SUSY limit ( m ≫ Λ m\gg\Lambda ) in SU ⁡ ( N c ) \mathrm{SU}(N_{c}) gauge theories with N f N_{f} quarks in the fundamental representation. The evidence includes the behavior of quark bi-linear condensate and gluon condensates, light hadron spectra, and consistency with the large N c N_{c} limit. In addition, we present new predictions when N f / N c ≳ O ⁡ ( 1 ) N_{f}/N_{c}\gtrsim O(1) .

Introduction

Understanding strong interaction in particle physics has been a challenging process. When Chadwick discovered the neutron
Chadwick:1932wcf
, it quickly became clear that all atoms were made of nuclei consisting of protons and neutrons surrounded by a cloud of electrons. Yet it was a major mystery how nuclei could bind despite the Coulomb repulsion among protons. Yukawa hypothesized pions to hold protons and neutrons together
Yukawa:1935xg
, which was later discovered in cosmic-ray experiments with much confusion with muons of a similar mass
Neddermeyer:1937md
. It was puzzling, however, why pions are so much lighter than nucleons so that the binding force extends beyond their size for the nucleons to be bound. Nambu and Jona-Lasinio argued that the pions are light because of the spontaneously broken chiral symmetry
Nambu:1961tp
;
Nambu:1961fr
, drawing from an analogy to the superconductors that spontaneously break the
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge symmetry of electric charge by the condensation of Cooper pairs in the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) state
Bardeen:1957kj
.

But it was only the beginning of the discovery of zoo of strong-interacting particles collectively called hadrons. We can now count more than one hundred hadrons listed by Particle Data Group
ParticleDataGroup:2024cfk
. The spectrum of light hadrons appeared to have a linear relationship between their spin and mass-squared in Chew-Fraustschi plots
Chew:1962eu
, which may be explained with a linear potential between constituents. Gell-Mann proposed an organizational principle based on the
\mathrm{SU}(3)
flavor symmetry
Gell-Mann:1961omu
, which eventually led to the hypothesis of quarks
u,d,s
with fractional charges as their fundamental constituents by Gell-Mann and Zweig
Gell-Mann:1964ewy
;
Zweig:1964ruk
. Nambu proposed that the fundamental constituents have color degrees of freedom bound together by color-octet gluons
Han:1965pf
. Eventually they were put together in Quantum ChromoDynamics (QCD) with non-abelian gauge theory based on the
\mathrm{SU}(3)
color group with three flavors
u,d,s
by Fritzsch, Gell-Mann, and Leutwyler
Fritzsch:1973pi
.

On the other hand in 1968, deep inelastic scattering (DIS) experiments at SLAC revealed nearly free “partons” inside proton, the name coined by Feynman
Feynman:1969ej
. Gross and Wilczek
Gross:1973id
, and independently Politzer
Politzer:1973fx
, discovered that non-abelian gauge theories can exhibit asymptotic freedom, allowing the fundamental constituents to behave like free particles at higher energies. Yet the idea of fractionally charged constituents and the confinement that does not allow quarks to be liberated were the major stumbling block for the acceptance of quarks and gluons. Only after
J
and
\psi
particles were independently discovered at Brookhaven
E598:1974sol
and SLAC
SLAC-SP-017:1974ind
;
Abrams:1974yy
, respectively, followed by discovery of charmed mesons
PhysRevLett.37.255
, quarks bound by gluons became the accepted picture of all hadrons.

Yet the idea of confinement by a linear potential that the fundamental constituents can never be liberated and observed in isolation, as well as that the dynamics spontaneously breaks the chiral symmetry, proved elusive for theoretical understanding. Mandelstam proposed that the confinement might be understood as the “dual Meissner effect.” He noted that the magnetic flux is squeezed in the Abrikosov flux tube in Type-II superconductor due to the condensate of Cooper pairs
Mandelstam:1974pi
. The energy of magnetic flux tubes is proportional to the length, and it would give rise to a linear potential between a magnetic monopole and an anti-magenetic monopole if they are embedded inside the superconductor. Considering the electric-magnetic duality, he argued that there should be a linear potential among charged particles if there is a condensate of magnetic monopoles. But it was not clear if there are magnetic monopoles in the
\mathrm{SU}(3)_{C}
gauge theory, and how they are connected to the dynamics of chiral symmetry breaking.

Much later, formulation of QCD as a path integral on a latticized Euclidean spacetime allowed for a breakthrough
Wilson:1974sk
. We can now show the linear potential, or equivalently the area law of the Wilson loop, in QCD without dynamical quarks
degrand2006lattice
;
Gattringer:2010zz
;
lellouch2011modern
. In the case with dynanical quarks, which is still a major technical challenge in lattice QCD, simulations show that there are bilinear condensates of quarks as order parameters of chiral symmetry breaking. However the numerical computer simulations do not provide us better insight into the basic understanding of both confinement and chiral symmetry breaking. In addition, it remains a technical challenge to define chiral symmetry, include a large number of dynamical fermions, deal with larger gauge groups. Chiral gauge theories such as standard model cannot be simulated still today.

In 1990s, Seiberg and Witten showed how
N=2
supersymmetric gauge theories can be solved exactly, and demonstrated confinement by monopole condensation upon perturbation to
N=1

Seiberg:1994rs
. Yet
N=2
theories do not accommodate full chiral symmetry we need to study QCD. Subsequently, Seiberg, Intriligator, and Pouliot worked out
N=1
vector-like gauge theories
Seiberg:1994bz
;
Intriligator:1994uk
;
Intriligator:1994jr
;
Intriligator:1995ne
;
Intriligator:1995au
. They exhibited a wide range of phases from no ground state with run-away behavior, infinite number of vacua with quantum constraints, confinement without chiral symmetry breaking, and exotic magnetic theories and superconformal dynamics. Unfortunately, none of them are similar to what we expect in non-SUSY QCD.

More recently, beginning from
Murayama:2021xfj
, the applications of soft-supersymmetry breaking effects via anomaly mediation to the solutions to
N=1
vector-like and chiral gauge theories
Csaki:2021xhi
;
Csaki:2021aqv
;
Csaki:2021jax
;
Csaki:2021xuc
;
Kondo:2022lvu
;
Leedom:2025mcg
, allows for exact solutions as long as the supersymmetry breaking effects are much smaller than the dynamical scale

. They resembled very much like what we expect in the non-SUSY limits
m\rightarrow\infty
. It suggested that changing

to non-SUSY

In this paper, we present more evidence that the near-SUSY and non-SUSY limits belong to the same universality class and it is a crossover. This behavior is akin to the BCS–BEC cross over in cold atoms. In particular, we show evidence in three different areas:

1.

Correct scaling

BCS–BEC Crossover

In this section, we briefly review the concept of BCS-BEC crossover (see
e.g.
,
parish2015bcs
;
chen2024superconductivity
for reviews), and its analogy to the supersymmetric QCD.

The BCS–BEC crossover concerns a system of fermions at the zero temperature. When there is no attractive force among fermions
g=0
, it is a degenerate Fermi gas with unbroken
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry for the conservation of number of particles. On the other hand, once
g
is turned on, the Fermi surface becomes unstable and the ground state becomes the BCS state
Bardeen:1957mv
where the fermion bilinear has a condensate which spontaneously breaks the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry. We can work out quantitative details using the variational ground state with perturbation in small couplings. As the coupling
g
is increased, analytical study becomes impossible. Yet numerical simulations show that the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry remains broken. Once
g
is very large, we arrive at a different description of the system. Namely that fermions form a bosonic bound state, and this bound state boson undergoes Bose–Einstein Condensation (BEC). There is no phase transition as a function of
g
and hence this phenomenon is called BCS–BEC crossover. Note that the point
g=0
is special with no symmetry breaking, while other points with
g>0
share the same universality class.

We show a schematic picture of the BCS-BEC crossover in the upper part of
fig.

1
.

Figure 1:
The illustration of BCS–BEC crossover, by changing the attractive interaction
g
. Only the left-most point
g=0
does not break the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, while from the BCS state with small
g
to the BEC with infinite
g
is a crossover and the universality class of broken
\mathrm{U}(1)
remains unchanged. We expect a similar crossover for the deformation of near-SUSY SQCD (
m\ll\Lambda
) into non-SUSY QCD (
m\rightarrow\infty
), while SQCD itself (
m=0
) belongs to a different universality class.

In this paper, we will argue and show some evidence that a similar crossover occurs in supersymmetric
\mathrm{SU}(N_{c})
QCD with
N_{f}
vector-like quarks in the fundamental representation, as illustrated in the lower part of
fig.

1
. The SUSY limit
m=0
is the famous exact result obtained by Seiberg
Seiberg:1994bz
, whose ground states do not resemble the non-SUSY limit at all which is expected to be either chiral symmetry breaking
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})_{L}\times\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})_{R}\rightarrow\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})_{V}
or an IR fixed point.

For a relatively small number of quark flavors, when a small soft SUSY breaking is turned on via anomaly mediation
m\ll\Lambda
, we can still solve the theories exactly and show that the chiral symmetry breaking occurs. In this near-SUSY limit, we can
derive
the chiral Lagrangian, its Wess–Zumino–Witten term, and various non-perturbative condensates including quark bilinear and gluon condensates quantitatively. This is our “BCS” theory. As
m
is increased to
m\sim\Lambda
, we lose our theoretical control. Once
m\gtrsim\Lambda
, we need to switch our description based on composite pions with its chiral Lagrangian. This is our “BEC” theory. Yet the non-SUSY limit
m\gg\Lambda
with the standard chiral symmetry breaking appears to be continuously connected without a phase transition, so that the theories remain in the same universality class. This is true for all
N_{f}<1.48N_{c}
.

For larger
N_{f}
, what is continuously connected may be a local minimum in the near-SUSY limit, while the global minimum breaks
\mathrm{U}(1)_{B}
. They switch when
m\simeq\Lambda
. Namely there is phase transition. But it is possible to follow the local minimum from
m\ll\Lambda
which appears to be continuously connected to the non-SUSY limit. Therefore, studying the near-SUSY limit on a local minimum is still useful to gain insight into the non-perturbative dynamics. In particular, we can work out the non-perturbative condensates in the large
N_{c}
limit with comparably large
N_{f}
which has not been possible with other methods.

3.5
Conformal Window

In SQCD, the conformal window is where
\frac{3}{2}N_{c}<N_{f}<3N_{c}
. The SUSY theory in this range flows to SCFT in the IR. Both the “Electric”

\displaystyle W=

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{c}\left(\tilde{\Lambda}^{3\tilde{N}_{c}-N_{f}}\det\frac{M}{\mu_{m}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\tilde{N}_{c}}}.

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\frac{3\tilde{N}_{c}-N_{f}}{N_{f}},\ \alpha=21\tilde{\epsilon}^{2},

where unknown

4.2
Chiral Lagrangian for
N_{f}>N_{c}

For
N_{f}>N_{c}
, the description with the fundamental quark fields
Q
and
\tilde{Q}
is no longer valid. On the other hand, we have shown that the meson field acquires an expectation value. The Kähler potential for the meson field is what gives the chiral Lagrangian. Namely that with the minimal Kähler potential,

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\theta{\rm Tr}M^{\dagger}M={\rm Tr}\partial_{\mu}M^{\dagger}\partial^{\mu}M.

(28)

We introduce the chiral field as

\displaystyle M=\frac{f_{\pi}}{2}U.

(29)

Then we can read off

\displaystyle f_{\pi}^{2}=4\frac{1}{N_{f}}{\rm Tr}\langle M\rangle^{\dagger}\langle M\rangle.

(30)

In the s-confinement and free magnetic phase, the meson is weakly interacting and this is a justified derivation of the chiral Lagrangian. In the conformal window, this derivation is valid at the lower and upper edge of the window, which extends to the entire conformal window assuming the continuity in
N_{f}/N_{c}
. We regard this derivation highly plausible.

4.3
Color Factor: 1PI coupling vs holomorphic coupling

It is important to consider the difference of the coupling between holomorphic coupling
g_{h}
and 1PI coupling
g_{c}
. They are related by the rescaling anomaly
Arkani-Hamed:1997lye

\displaystyle\frac{8\pi^{2}}{g_{h}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi^{2}}{g_{c}^{2}}+C_{A}\log g_{c}^{2},

(31)

where
C_{A}=N_{c}
is adjoint Casimir. (Here we are ignoring another correction that stems from the Konishi anomaly due to the wave function renormalization factor
Z
which is not important here.)

The authors of the paper
Martin:1998yr
claimed that softly-broken SQCD and QCD are disconnected with a phase transition from the non-SUSY limit, as evidenced by an incorrect scaling of
f_{\pi}^{2}\sim N_{c}^{0}
they found when
N_{f}<N_{c}
. However, it seems that the authors did not relate the dynamical scale to the t’Hooft coupling correctly. We find explicit results for
f_{\pi}^{2}
in ASQCD for all values of
N_{f}<3N_{c}
. In the particular case of
N_{f}<N_{c}
and
N_{c}\gg 1
, we find that the correct
f_{\pi}^{2}\propto N_{c}
scaling is reproduced. We also show new results for the scaling of
f_{\pi}^{2}
in regimes where
N_{f}
is not small compared to
N_{c}
.

For SQCD,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}\mathrm{e}^{-8\pi^{2}/g_{h}^{2}}=\mu^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}(g_{c}^{2})^{-N_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{-8\pi^{2}/g_{c}^{2}}.

(32)

With t’Hooft coupling
N_{c}g_{c}^{2}=g_{t}^{2}
, this becomes

\displaystyle\Lambda^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}(g_{c}^{2})^{-N_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{-8\pi^{2}/g_{c}^{2}}=\mu^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}N_{c}^{N_{c}}(g_{t}^{2})^{-N_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{-8\pi^{2}N_{c}/g_{t}^{2}}.

(33)

We found that for
N_{f}<N_{c}

\displaystyle f_{\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\phi^2}=\left[\left(\frac{N_{c}+N_{f}}{3N_{c}-N_{f}}\right)^{N_{c}-N_{f}}\frac{\Lambda^{3N_{c}-N_{f}}}{m^{N_{c}-N_{f}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{N_{c}}}

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu^{3}}{g_{t}^{2}m}\left(\frac{1}{3}N_{c}+\mathcal{O}(N_{f})+\mathcal{O}(N_{c}^{-1})\right)e^{-8\pi^{2}/g_{t}^{2}},

(35)

which implies
f_{\pi}^{2}\sim N_{c}
for
N_{c}\gg 1
and
N_{f}\ll N_{c}
.
Therefore, the scaling of
f_{\pi}^{2}
in ASQCD agrees with that of QCD and there is no evidence of the phase transition previously claimed in the literature.

Derivation of Wess-Zumino-Witten (WZW) term

We can derive the WZW term based on the technique in
DHoker:1984izu

\expectationvalue{Q^a_i}=\expectationvalue{\tilde{Q}^a_i}=v\delta^{a}_{i}

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=v\xi+\theta\psi_{Q}+\theta^{2}F,\qquad\tilde{Q}^{T}=v\xi+\theta\psi_{\tilde{Q}}+\theta^{2}\tilde{F}.

(36)

Note that the VEV

is diagonal in color and flavor indices. It seems that gluino
\lambda

Let us consider an effective action

\displaystyle W=-i\log\left[\int dQd\tilde{Q}dV\exp\left[i\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}\right]\right].

(37)

When we transform fermion by

\displaystyle D(\xi^{*}Q)D(\tilde{Q}\xi^{\dagger})\rightarrow DQJ(\xi^{T})D\tilde{Q}J(\xi),

(38)

\displaystyle J(\xi)=\exp[iN_{c}\int\operatorname{Tr}(\xi^{\dagger}d\xi)^{5}],\ J(\xi^{T})=\exp\left[-iN_{c}\int\operatorname{Tr}(\xi d\xi^{\dagger})^{5}\right],

(39)

\displaystyle W^{\prime}

\displaystyle=W-i\log J(\xi)-i\log J(\xi^{T})

\displaystyle\supset N_{c}\int\operatorname{Tr}\left[(\xi^{\dagger}d\xi)^{5}-(\xi d\xi^{\dagger})^{5}\right]=N_{c}\int\operatorname{Tr}[(U^{\dagger}dU)^{5}],

(40)

where we have shown the calculation in the last line up to local counter terms in

5.2
Method for ASQCD

Wherever we have identified a
\chi
SB minimum we can explicitly obtain the expectation value of the meson operator
\expectationvalue{M}
. As a chiral superfield

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{M}=

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\tilde{q}^*\tilde{q}}+\theta^{2}\expectationvalue{\bar q q}.

(50)

We get the lowest component when identifying the vacuum by minimizing the scalar potential (see
Section

3
). The F-component can be obtained by the equations of motion. Generically the
F
-term part of the Lagrangian in AMSB in canonical normalization is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset

\displaystyle F_{\tilde{X}}\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}W}{\partial{\tilde{X}}}}+h.c.+F_{\tilde{X}}F_{\tilde{X}}^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{c_{X}Z_{X}}m(1-\frac{\gamma_{X}}{2})(F_{\tilde{X}}\tilde{X}^{\dagger}+h.c.).

(51)

Up to an overall dimensionful scale, then, we will have

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\bar{q}q}=

\displaystyle-\expectationvalue{
\dpd{W}{\tilde{M}}
}^{\dagger}-m\expectationvalue{
(1-\frac{\gamma_M}{2}) \frac{\tilde{M}}{Z_M}
},

(52)

where here this is generically a matrix expression. It is sometimes more convenient to calculate in terms of the non-canonically normalized
M=\tilde{M}/\sqrt{Z_{M}}
:

\displaystyle-\expectationvalue{\frac{1}{\sqrt{ Z_M}}\dpd{W}{M}}^{\dagger}-m\expectationvalue{(1-\frac{\gamma_M}{2}) \frac{M}{\sqrt{ Z_M}}}.

(53)

Here, the derivative is well-defined, and the expectation values should evaluated at
\mu=\phi_{\text{min, can}}
, where the subscript denotes the field value at the potential minimum, expressed in the canonical normalization of the matter field.

In this way, we can obtain the fermion and sfermion bilinear condensates from the existing calculations of the vacua.

Now we will derive how to calculate the gluon and gluino condensates. First, the Lagrangian for the vector multiplet is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{vec}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi i}\int d^{2}\theta\,\tau W_{\alpha}W^{\alpha}+\text{ h.c.}

(54)

Here, we regard the holomorphic coupling as spurion superfield

\displaystyle\tau=

\displaystyle\,\tau+\sqrt{2}\psi_{\tau}\theta+\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\theta^{2}.

(55)

Throughout the following analysis we set
\psi_{\tau}=0
, as it does not play a significant role. The scalar and F-components of this spurion then act as sources for the gluon and gluino condensates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{vec}}\supset

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16\pi i}\tau(GG+\bar{\lambda}i\not{D}\lambda)+\frac{1}{16\pi i}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\lambda\lambda.

(56)

Take the path integral to be

\displaystyle Z=\int D[\text{fields}]\exp{i\int d^4x \left(\int d^4\theta K + \int d^2\theta W + \int d^2\bar\theta W^*\right)}.

(57)

As a function of the spurion superfield
\tau
, the superpotential can be expanded as following

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\theta\ W(\tau+\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\theta^{2})

\displaystyle=\int d^{2}\theta\ W(\tau)+\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}W}{\partial{\tau}}}.

(58)

Treating
\tau
and
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
as sources, we can calculate the condensates via the path integral like so

\displaystyle 16\pi i\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\tau}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{GG+\bar{\lambda}i\slashed{D}\lambda}=\expectationvalue{GG},

(59)

\displaystyle 16\pi i\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}.

(60)

In the case of ASQCD we can directly determine the minima of the theory, and thus evaluate the path integral explicitly. We can then use our determination of the minima to calculate the gluon and gluino condensates like so

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{GG}=

\displaystyle 16\pi i\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\ln\mathcal{Z}=16\pi i\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{V}}{\partial\tau},

(61)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}=

\displaystyle 16\pi i\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{\tau}}\ln\mathcal{Z}=16\pi i\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{W}}{\partial\tau},

(62)

where
\expectationvalue{V}
is the vacuum energy and
\expectationvalue{W}
is the expectation value of the scalar component of the superpotential. The dynamical scale is given by the holomorphic gauge coupling

\displaystyle\Lambda=

\displaystyle\mu\,e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3N_{c}-N_{f}}\tau}\,,

(63)

where
\mu
is the UV cutoff scale. We then have

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{GG}=

\displaystyle-\frac{32\pi^{2}}{3N_{c}-N_{f}}\Lambda\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{V}}{\partial\Lambda}\ ,

(64)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}=

\displaystyle-\frac{32\pi^{2}}{3N_{c}-N_{f}}\Lambda\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{W}}{\partial\Lambda}\ .

(65)

But note that these are not canonically normalized (with respect to the gauge and gaugino kinetic terms). To normalize them properly, we must multiply by
\tau/4\pi i
such that

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{GG}_{c}=

\displaystyle 4\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}\expectationvalue{V}}{\partial{\Lambda}}}\ ,

(66)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}_{c}=

\displaystyle 4\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}\expectationvalue{W}}{\partial{\Lambda}}}\ .

(67)

\expectationvalue{GG}
and
\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}

, we can obtain the quark condensate via

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Lambda}\expectationvalue{\bar q q}=

\displaystyle-\expectationvalue{\frac{\partial W}{\partial M}}-m\expectationvalue{M},

(76)

and hence

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\bar q q}=

\displaystyle-m\Lambda^{2}.

(77)

For the gluon condensate, we calculate at leading order

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{GG}=

\displaystyle-8N_{c}m^{2}\Lambda^{2}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}.

(78)

For the gluino condensate, using the leading order minima gives
\expectationvalue{W}=0
. However, expanding to higher order in
m/\Lambda
we find

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{W}=

\displaystyle\frac{m^{3}}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{3N_{c}-1}{2N_{c}\lambda^{4}}\frac{m^{5}}{\Lambda^{2}}\ ,

(79)

such that

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}=

\displaystyle 4\frac{3N_{c}-1}{\lambda^{4}N_{c}}\frac{m^{5}}{\Lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\mu}\ .

(80)

Note that the ADS and Free Magnetic results both have
\expectationvalue{\lambda\lambda}\propto(N_{f}-N_{c})
, consistent with a leading-order cancellation when
N_{f}=N_{c}
.

It is important to note that the stability of this minimum is ambiguous, due to unknown coefficients in the Kähler potential
Csaki:2022cyg
. Moreover, this minimum is not exact but rather the leading order result in powers of
m
. The results we have for this phase should be viewed with caution, but are bolstered if one assumes a degree of continuity between the ADS phase below and the
s
-confining phase above. Moreover we have checked that the inclusion of higher order Kähler terms – non-negligible when field values are order
\Lambda
– only changes the coefficients of the sub-leading terms of the minimum and thus does not affect this leading-order cancellation.

5.7
Conformal Window

To understand the impact of AMSB which is expected to deflect the flow away from the IR fixed point, we need to understand how the couplings approach the IR fixed point from their UV values. For the bulk of the conformal window, we lack analytical methods to determine this behavior. Therefore, we study the lower and upper edges of the conformal window and interpolate the behavior in between.

Near the lower edge of the conformal window where

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\lambda^{2\frac{N_{f}}{\tilde{N}_{c}}}\alpha(2+\alpha)}{4N_{f}^{3-\frac{N_{f}}{\tilde{N}_{c}}}}\frac{m^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\Lambda^{2},

6
Calculation of Mass Spectrum

In this section, we present the mass spectrum of low-lying states. We warn the readers that the crossover does not necessarily guarantee that the low-lying spectra continuously change from the near-SUSY to the non-SUSY limit except for the massless states (Nambu–Goldstone bosons). Yet we observe that the
0^{+}
states of the meson superfield appear to show the same feature in both limits. The
0^{+}
states are established experimentally only rather recently, and it is curious that we can understand their spectrum.

We show the result based on the specific
N_{c}=3
case. We hope that we can extract lessons from these results. The flow chart of the calculation of this section is in the following way. From the calculation of the potential from superpotential in
section

3
, we know the vacuum and its field values. We introduce fluctuation of the fields around the vacuum, and then we calculate the mass matrix by taking second derivatives with respect to the fields. We decomposed the meson field
M=M^{a}T^{a}
into real part and imaginary part

M^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma^{a}+i\pi^{a}).
We turn on the finite quark mass to see how it affects, we treated the quark mass as a perturbation much smaller than the AMSB scale
m
. We consider the region such that the vacuum is slightly perturbed by the quark mass to avoid run-away direction. We denote the configuration of the minimum by
\sigma_{0}+\delta\sigma
. We consider the potential as vacuum (massless) part and finite (quark) mass part
V=V_{0}+V_{\text{fin}}
. In order for this configuration to be realized as a vacuum, the potential should satisfy the stationary condition

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial V}{\partial M}|_{M=\sigma_{0}+\delta\sigma}=\frac{\partial V_{0}}{\partial M}|_{M=\sigma_{0}+\delta\sigma}+\frac{\partial V_{\text{fin}}}{\partial M}|_{M=\sigma_{0}+\delta\sigma}\simeq\frac{\partial^{2}V_{0}}{\partial M^{2}}(\sigma_{0})\delta\sigma+\frac{\partial V_{\text{fin}}}{\partial M}(\sigma_{0}),

(105)

\displaystyle\delta\sigma

\displaystyle\simeq-(\text{Mass}^{2})^{-1}\frac{\partial V_{\text{fin}}}{\partial M}.

(106)

Then we can evaluate the mass at the point

. We note that the second derivative of the potential is nothing but the mass square matrix.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented evidence that the SQCD softly broken by small AMSB
m\ll\Lambda
(the near-SUSY limit) is continuously connected to the limit of large SUSY breaking
m\gg\Lambda
(the non-SUSY limit) akin to the BCS–BEC crossover. We showed how non-perturbative condensates of composite operators can be computed for squarks, quarks, gauginos, and gluons. They exhibit continuous behavior across the theoretically inaccessible region
m\sim\Lambda
. For
N_{f}<N_{c}
, their behavior is consistent with the large
N_{c}
limit expected in non-SUSY QCD. For
N_{f}>N_{c}
, we show how the large
N_{f}/N_{c}\sim O(1)
can modify the large
N_{c}
behavior, not possible with previously available methods. We can derive the chiral Lagrangian from first principles for the entire range for
N_{f}<3N_{c}
with a modest assumption of continuity in
N_{f}
. Surprisingly, we find the low-lying spectrum in the meson superfield to be consistent not only for the
0^{-}
pseudo-Nambu–Goldstone bosons (pions) but also for the recently established
0^{+}
states. We regard this consistency as a pleasant surprise which strongly supports the crossover from the near-SUSY to the non-SUSY limit in SQCD.

\displaystyle=2v^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{4}(\xi\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\dagger}-\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi)^{2}+\left(g\rho_{\mu}-\frac{i}{2}(\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi+\xi\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\dagger})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{v^{2}}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\xi\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\dagger}-\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi)^{2}+\frac{v^{2}}{2}\left(2g\rho_{\mu}-\frac{i}{4}(\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi+\xi\partial_{\mu}\xi^{\dagger})\right)^{2}.

(133)

From the second line to the third line, we took transpose, trace is invariant under transpose. When we rewrite in terms of
U
, with
\partial_{\mu}U=(\partial_{\mu}\xi)\xi+\xi\partial_{\mu}\xi
,
U^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}U=(\xi^{\dagger})^{2}\partial_{\mu}\xi+\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi
,
\operatorname{Tr}U^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}U=2\xi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\xi
, and their Hermitian conjugate, you can reach the last line in
section

4.1
.

Appendix B
Trace Calculation Up To Local Counter Term

In this section, we show the equality of the WZW term and the Jacobian of fermion determinant up to local counter term. We use
U=\xi^{2}
as a matrix. Then

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}dU

\displaystyle=\xi^{\dagger}(\xi^{\dagger}d\xi+d\xi\xi^{\dagger})\xi,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[(U^{\dagger}dU)^{5}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\xi^{\dagger}d\xi+d\xi\xi^{\dagger})^{5}]=\operatorname{Tr}[(\xi^{\dagger}d\xi-\xi d\xi^{\dagger})^{5}].

(134)

Here, we set
A=\xi^{\dagger}d\xi
,
B=-\xi d\xi^{\dagger}
. We can see the following relations

\displaystyle dA

\displaystyle=-A^{2},\ dB=B^{2}.

(135)

The WZW term is in the following form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[(A+B)^{5}]=\operatorname{Tr}[A^{5}+5A^{4}B+5A^{3}B^{2}+5A^{2}BAB+5A^{2}B^{3}+5ABAB^{2}+5AB^{4}+B^{5}].

(136)

By replacing higher order term by derivative using above relations, we can obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[(A+B)^{5}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[A^{5}+B^{5}+5d(AdAB)-\frac{5}{2}d(AB)^{2}-5d(AdBB)\right].

(137)

Therefore, we can see the equality of Jacobian and WZW term up to local counter terms.

Appendix C
Origin of Factor
N_{c}
in the WZW Term

In this section, we identify the origin of the overall factor
N_{c}
in the Wess–Zumino–Witten (WZW) term in the chiral Lagrangian in our derivation of the chiral Lagrangian in
section

4
. It arises from two independent contributions from the Jacobians in the path integral when the fermion mass matrix is diagonalized. We specifically study the ADS case.

Recall the form of the VEVs in
eq.

2
. The fermion components split into the upper
N_{f}\times N_{f}
block and the lower
(N_{c}-N_{f})\times N_{f}
block,

\displaystyle Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v\xi^{T}+\theta\psi\\
\theta\chi\end{array}\right),\qquad\tilde{Q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v\xi+\theta\tilde{\psi}\\
\theta\tilde{\chi}\end{array}\right).

(Here, we dropped the
F
-components we are not interested in.)
Their transformation laws under
g\in G_{\rm global}
are

\displaystyle Q\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{c}vh\xi^{T}g_{L}^{T}+\theta h\psi g_{L}^{T}\\
\theta\chi g_{L}^{T}\end{array}\right),\qquad\tilde{Q}\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{c}vh^{*}\xi g_{R}^{T}+\theta h^{*}\tilde{\psi}g_{R}^{T}\\
\theta\tilde{\chi}g_{R}^{T}\end{array}\right).

On the other hand, the gaugino fields also split into blocks

\displaystyle\lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{11}&\lambda_{12}\\
\lambda_{21}&\lambda_{22}\end{array}\right)\rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h\lambda_{11}h^{\dagger}&h\lambda_{12}\\
\lambda_{21}h^{\dagger}&\lambda_{22}\end{array}\right).

The scalar-fermion-gaugino interaction induces the mass terms

\displaystyle\int d^{4}\theta{\rm Tr}Q^{\dagger}e^{V}Q\supset v{\rm Tr}(\xi^{*}\lambda_{11}\psi)+v{\rm Tr}(\xi^{*}\lambda_{12}\chi)+{\rm h.c.},

(150)

and

\displaystyle\int d^{4}\theta{\rm Tr}\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}e^{-V^{T}}\tilde{Q}\supset v{\rm Tr}\xi^{\dagger}\lambda_{11}^{T}\tilde{\psi}+v{\rm Tr}\xi^{\dagger}\lambda_{21}^{T}\tilde{\chi}+{\rm h.c.},

(151)

which are indeed invariant under
G_{\rm global}
.

On the other hand, the ADS superpotential depends on

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}^{T}Q

\displaystyle\supset\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v^{2}\xi^{T}\xi^{T}+v\theta\xi^{T}\psi+v\theta\tilde{\psi}^{T}\xi^{T}+\theta^{2}\tilde{\psi}^{T}\psi+\theta^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{T}\chi\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\xi^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v^{2}+v\theta\psi\xi^{*}+v\theta\xi^{*}\tilde{\psi}^{T}+\theta^{2}\xi^{*}\tilde{\psi}^{T}\psi\xi+\theta^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{T}\chi\end{array}\right)\xi^{T}.

The mass matrix can only depend on the combinations

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\psi\xi^{*}\xi^{*}\tilde{\psi}^{T}),\quad{\rm Tr}\tilde{\chi}^{T}\chi.

(154)

In order to diagonalize the mass matrix, we need to perform the reparametrization

\displaystyle\psi\rightarrow\psi\xi^{T},\qquad\tilde{\psi}\rightarrow\tilde{\psi}\xi,

(155)

yielding a factor of
N_{f}
for the Jacobian, while anothe reparameterization

\displaystyle\chi\rightarrow\chi\xi^{T},\qquad\tilde{\chi}\rightarrow\tilde{\chi}\xi,

(156)

yielding a factor of
N_{c}-N_{f}
for the Jacobian. Combining them together, there is an overall factor of
N_{c}
.
