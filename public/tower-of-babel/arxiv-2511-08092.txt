Pruning as Regularization: Sensitivity-Aware One-Shot Pruning in ASR
Julian Irigoyen, Arthur Söhler, Andreas Søeborg Kirkedal

Abstract
We challenge the conventional view of neural network pruning as solely a compression technique, demonstrating that one-shot magnitude pruning serves as a powerful implicit regularizer for ASR. Using Whisper-small, we combine gradient- and Fisher-based sensitivity diagnostics with targeted, component-wise pruning. This reveals architectural asymmetries: decoder FFNs are pruning-fragile, whereas decoder self-attention and the last encoder layers contain redundancy that, when removed, improves generalization. Without fine-tuning, pruning 50% of decoder self-attention reduces WER by 2.38% absolute (20.44% relative) on LibriSpeech test-other ; pruning the last four encoder layers at 50% instead yields a 1.72% absolute (14.8% relative) improvement. Gains persisted on Common Voice and TED-LIUM datasets. Beyond regularization benefits, our sensitivity-aware approach enables more aggressive one-shot compression. At 40% sparsity, where established global pruning approaches catastrophically fail, our method preserves near-baseline accuracy. This positions pruning as a first-class architectural design tool: knowing where to prune is as important as how much to prune.

INTRODUCTION

Over-parameterization in deep neural networks facilitates training convergence but often introduces redundancies that can impair generalization. Conventional regularizers such as dropout
[
22
]
, weight decay
[
12
]
, and batch normalization
[
8
]
mitigate this through stochastic or parametric constraints. Beyond these established techniques, we demonstrate that architectural analysis can reveal specific over-parameterized components where pruning itself acts as regularization.

While pruning has traditionally been studied as a compression technique aimed at reducing computational cost
[
6
,
14
,
7
]
, we provide empirical evidence that
post-training one-shot magnitude pruning
without fine-tuning can
improve
ASR performance. We identify redundant parameters using gradient and Fisher-based sensitivity diagnostics, and validate them through component- and layer-wise pruning experiments. These diagnostics enable targeted pruning that can yield meaningful performance gains and improve robustness to unseen acoustic conditions. They can also guide aggressive one-shot pruning and surpass standard pruning methods that collapse at similar sparsity levels.

Our contributions are threefold:

1.

An architectural sensitivity analysis that quantifies per-component and per-layer tolerance to one-shot magnitude pruning.

2.

A pruning-as-regularization procedure that leverages sensitivity analysis to improve ASR generalization across diverse acoustic conditions.

3.

Aggressive one-shot compression that surpasses standard pruning methods by leveraging the same sensitivity insights to preserve accuracy at high sparsity.

Methodology

3.1
Theoretical Foundation: Pruning as Implicit Regularization

Classical regularization augments the empirical risk with explicit penalties like
\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}
(weight decay) or
\|\theta\|_{1}
(lasso)
[
12
,
24
]
. Magnitude pruning instead acts as
implicit regularization
by directly removing parameters rather than penalizing them. For a network with parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and target sparsity
\rho
, we retain the
(1-\rho)d
parameters with largest absolute values, creating a pruned network
f_{\theta_{\mathcal{M}}}
where
\mathcal{M}\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
indexes retained parameters:

\theta_{\mathcal{M}}=\{\theta_{i}:i\in\mathcal{M}\},\quad|\mathcal{M}|=(1-\rho)d.

(1)

This sparsification constrains the network to operate with a sparse parameter subset. It preserves essential features while eliminating parameters that potentially encode spurious correlations, providing a deterministic alternative to dropout’s stochastic masks.

3.2
Sensitivity Analysis Framework

We study pruning robustness at module and layer granularity within the encoder (
\mathcal{E}
) and decoder (
\mathcal{D}
) of the Whisper Transformer architecture. Sensitivity is assessed via (i) first-order (gradient-based) and (ii) second-order (curvature-based) diagnostics, complemented by (iii) empirical, component-wise pruning sweeps.

First-order sensitivity

Inspired by
[
15
]
, for module
m\in\{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}\}
with parameters
\theta_{m}
we define a normalized gradient score

S_{g}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\left\|\nabla_{\theta_{m}}\mathcal{L}(x_{i},y_{i})\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\theta_{m}\right\|_{2}},

(2)

which is scale-normalized and captures immediate loss sensitivity to small perturbations of
\theta_{m}
.

Second-order sensitivity

To capture curvature, we approximate the diagonal of the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) (valid for negative log-likelihood losses, e.g., cross-entropy)
[
11
]
:

F_{jj}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\nabla_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{L}(x_{i},y_{i})\Big)^{\!2},\quad j\in\mathcal{I}_{m},

(3)

where
\mathcal{I}_{m}
indexes the parameters belonging to module
m
; and summarize per module by

S_{h}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{m}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{m}}F_{jj}.

(4)

Large
S_{h}^{(m)}
indicates high curvature (parameters constrained by the loss), suggesting lower pruning tolerance.

Pruning-based empirical sensitivity

Beyond analytic diagnostics, we empirically assess pruning sensitivity by measuring pruning-induced degradation. Components that maintain or improve performance when pruned reveal opportunities for implicit regularization. We employ one-shot, unstructured magnitude pruning, ranking each weight by its absolute value (magnitude) and removing the lowest-magnitude fraction for a target sparsity. We prune each architectural component independently in both Encoder and Decoder, as well as split into layer subsets (early/middle/late layers). For target sparsity and component
(c,\rho)
, we zero weights
|\theta_{j}|\leq\tau(\rho,c)
, leaving all other parameters unchanged. We vary
\rho
in 10% increments.

3.3
Model, datasets and metrics

We use the publicly released Whisper-small model
[
20
]
, an encoder–decoder Transformer (12 encoder + 12 decoder layers) evaluated with Whisper’s official beam search decoding pipeline. Primary evaluation uses
LibriSpeech

test-clean
and
test-other

[
18
]
to contrast acoustically clean vs. challenging conditions; treating
test-other
as our primary reporting metric due to its greater acoustic variability. To probe generalization, we additionally validate on
Common Voice v15 English
(diverse accents/recording conditions) and
TED–LIUM Release 3
(conference talks with reverberation) using sampled subsets from their test splits.

We quantify ASR performance using word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER). Resilience to pruning is measured through
\Delta
WER on
test-other
; lower values indicate higher resilience, while negative values indicate improved performance. As a note: unstructured masks are not exploited by dense inference kernels, hence real-time factor (RTF) remains unchanged in our setting.

4.2
Architectural Sensitivity Analysis

4.2.1
Encoder vs. Decoder

Building upon the previous diagnostics, Figure
2
reports WER on
test-other
when pruning (i) the full model, (ii) the encoder only, or (iii) the decoder only. Whole-model pruning shows a catastrophic failure region between 30–40% sparsity. Separate pruning of encoder and decoder reveals that the decoder drives this failure: at 40% sparsity, decoder-only pruning yields 30.11% WER, whereas encoder-only pruning remains relatively close to baseline (12.49%).
This asymmetry is particularly counterintuitive given the parameter distribution: encoder components comprise only 36.47% of model parameters while decoder components account for 63.53%. Despite having more parameters, the decoder proves less tolerant to pruning than the encoder. Notably, encoder-only pruning at 30% slightly
improves
accuracy (-0.57% WER), suggesting that selective pruning can indeed induce beneficial regularization.

CONCLUSION

This work demonstrates that one-shot magnitude pruning serves as a powerful implicit regularizer for ASR, challenging its traditional role as solely a compression technique. By combining gradient-based and Fisher Information diagnostics with component-wise empirical validation, we reveal architectural asymmetries: decoder FFNs exhibit higher sensitivity than encoder modules, while decoder self-attention and late encoder layers contain substantial redundancy. Most notably, pruning 50% of decoder self-attention weights improves WER by 2.38% absolute (20.44% relative) on
LibriSpeech

test-other
without any fine-tuning. Similarly, pruning 50% of the last four encoder layers yields a 1.72% absolute improvement. These regularization benefits persist across
Common Voice
and
TED-LIUM 3
.

The same diagnostics enable sensitivity-aware one-shot compression, demonstrating that understanding where to prune is as important as how much to prune. Where standard global pruning collapses near 40% sparsity, our allocation reaches 40.8% sparsity with near-baseline WER (+0.20%) and improved CER (-1.06%), reducing parameters (241→143M) and compute (4.55→2.77 GFLOPs).

The practical implication is clear: pruning should also be treated as a first-class architectural design tool rather than just a post-hoc optimization. While our Whisper-small results are model-specific, the sensitivity analysis methodology is broadly applicable. Practitioners can apply this sensitivity analysis framework to identify pruning opportunities in other Transformer architectures to improve robustness to unseen acoustic conditions while delivering efficiency gains with no fine-tuning.
