Carbon-rich carbon nitride for singlet-oxygen-driven photocatalytic degradation of benzophenone-3 and coral vitality recovery
Authors: Shien Zhu, Xiaofei Yang, Di He, Yanyan Zhang, Hang Liu, Xiaoyu Zhou, Dawei Su, Guoxiu Wang, Tianyi Wang, Chengyin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoral reefs, vital marine ecosystems, are increasingly threatened by global warming and chemical pollutants such as benzophenone-3 (BP-3), a widely used UV filter. To address this challenge, we present a carbon-doped graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) photocatalyst with efficient singlet oxygen (1O2) generation for BP-3 degradation and coral vitality restoration under simulated seawater conditions. The photocatalyst exhibits improved stability and photocatalytic performance in complex seawater environments, with enhanced charge separation and an approximately eightfold increase in the apparent degradation rate of BP-3 compared with pristine g-C3N4. Toxicity assessments indicate reduced ecological risks of degradation intermediates and significant recovery of zooxanthellae density and coral–algal symbiosis. Immobilized catalyst architectures further minimize material loss and provide a suitable substrate for coral attachment. In this work, we demonstrate a seawater-stable, 1O2-driven photocatalytic strategy that enables efficient BP-3 removal and promotes coral vitality recovery, thereby offering a scalable approach for marine environmental remediation and coral ecosystem restoration.Subject terms: Marine chemistry, Pollution remediation

Introduction

Coral reefs, often described as rainforests of the sea, are among the most productive marine ecosystems, supporting 30% of marine species despite covering only 0.25% of the ocean’s surface1. These ecosystems provide critical ecological and economic services, including biodiversity conservation and coastal protection2. However, global warming and chemical pollutants are driving widespread coral bleaching, threatening reef survival3. For example, Australia’s Great Barrier Reef has experienced five major bleaching events in just 8 years4.

While rising temperatures are the primary driver of bleaching, pollutants, such as benzophenone-3 (BP-3), a UV filter widely used in sunscreens and cosmetics, have exacerbated coral decline5. BP-3 metabolizes into photoreactive glucosides in marine organisms, increasing coral mortality under UV exposure6. It also disrupts endocrine systems in fish, destabilizing marine ecosystems7,8. Despite existing remediation efforts like adsorption and oxidation, these methods are inadequate for the persistent presence of BP-3 in coastal waters9–11.

Photocatalysis has emerged as a promising strategy for removing organic pollutants in aquatic environments12. Among various photocatalysts, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has attracted particular interest due to its metal-free composition, chemical stability, visible-light response, low cost, and scalable synthesis13,14. Previous studies have demonstrated that g-C3N4 and its derivatives can effectively degrade a wide range of organic pollutants, including pharmaceuticals and endocrine-disrupting chemicals11,15,16. More broadly, recent advances in photocatalytic material design have shown that structural engineering and hybridization strategies can substantially enhance organic pollutant degradation efficiency17–21.

Building on these advances, we developed a carbon-doped g-C3N4 photocatalyst with enhanced visible-light absorption and singlet-oxygen (1O2) generation. The photocatalyst achieved efficient BP-3 degradation and supported coral vitality restoration under simulated seawater conditions, demonstrating both improved photoreactivity and ecological relevance.

Results

Morphology and structure characterization of photocatalysts

Melamine and pyridine-3,5-dicarboxylic acid (PA) were used as molecular precursors to construct a pyridine-containing carbon nitride photocatalyst. A supramolecular intermediate (denoted as Py-M) was first obtained via hydrothermal assembly and subsequently converted into pyridine-modified carbon nitride (Py-CNx) through thermal polymerization.

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. 1a) showed that Py-M exhibited a diffraction pattern distinct from those of pristine melamine and PA, indicating the formation of a distinct supramolecular crystalline phase rather than a physical mixture. Consistently, Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR) (Supplementary Fig. 1b) revealed pronounced changes in the N-H and C-N vibrational regions, confirming strong intermolecular interactions within the Py-M precursor. With increasing PA content, the color of the resulting carbon nitride gradually darkens after thermal polymerization (Supplementary Fig. 2), reflecting variations in carbonization degree and structural evolution rather than a monotonic enhancement of photocatalytic performance.

The morphological characteristics of the photocatalysts were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Fig. 1a–c) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Fig. 1d–f). Pristine g-C3N4 exhibited a typical bulk-like morphology, whereas Py-CNx displayed a more open, network-like structure. Such structural evolution was expected to increase the accessible surface area and expose more active sites. TEM observations further revealed that Py-CN25 evolved from thick bulk g-C3N4 into a thinner and more porous network-like architecture. Additional high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 3) provided direct evidence of the crystalline features of the carbon nitride framework. Clear lattice fringes corresponding to the (002) plane were observed for both samples, consistent with the layered structure of graphitic carbon nitride. In addition, SEM-EDS elemental mapping and EDS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4) confirmed the homogeneous distribution of C and N elements in both samples, while revealing an increased carbon content and a slightly decreased nitrogen content in Py-CN25, consistent with the successful incorporation of pyridine-derived carbon species. The XRD patterns (Fig. 1g) of g-C3N4 and Py-CNx exhibited two characteristic diffraction peaks assignable to the hexagonal phase of carbon nitride. The peak at 13.5° corresponds to the (100) plane, reflecting the in-plane periodic arrangement of tri-s-triazine units, while the peak at 27.7° is attributed to the (002) plane, associated with interlayer stacking of aromatic layers. For Py-CNx, the (002) reflection became broader and weaker, accompanied by a slight shift toward a higher diffraction angle. Consistently, HRTEM analysis showed that the interlayer spacing decreased from 0.348 nm in pristine g-C3N4 to 0.289 nm in Py-CN25, indicating a more compact stacking structure after modification22.

These observations suggest that the introduction of pyridine-derived carbon species is associated with the rearrangement of the carbon nitride framework, leading to altered interlayer ordering and increased structural disorder, in good agreement with the structural evolution inferred from XRD analysis22–25.

The FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1h) further elucidated the structural features of the catalysts. Both g-C3N4 and Py-CNx exhibited a characteristic peak at 804 cm−1, corresponding to the symmetric stretching vibrations of tri-s-triazine units. The intensity of this peak was significantly higher for Py-CNx, attributed to the out-of-plane bending vibration of C-H bonds in the pyridine ring. Additional peaks at 1461 and 1639 cm−1 in the 1180–1650 cm−1 range correspond to the stretching vibrations of C-N heterocycles26, while broad absorption bands in the 3150–3300 cm−1 region are associated with terminal amino groups. These observations confirmed the successful incorporation of pyridine moieties and the structural features of the synthesized catalysts27.

N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 were obtained and are shown in Fig. 1i. Pristine g-C3N4 exhibited a relatively low N2 uptake, corresponding to a limited specific surface area of 22.65 m2 g−1. In contrast, Py-CN25 displayed a markedly enhanced adsorption capacity over the entire relative pressure range, yielding a significantly increased BET surface area of 118.78 m2 g−1, indicative of a more open porous structure after modification. Pore size distribution (PSD) curves derived from the BJH desorption branch (Supplementary Fig. 5) showed that pristine g-C3N4 possessed broad pores centered at 30–40 nm, whereas Py-CN25 exhibited pronounced mesoporosity mainly in the 5–20 nm range with increased pore volume, consistent with the enhanced BET surface area.

Figure 2a shows the molecular modeling of g-C3N4 and Py-CN25. The solid-state NMR spectra (Fig. 2b) revealed a significantly higher β-to-α peak ratio in Py-CN25 compared to pristine g-C3N4, indicating a greater incorporation of pyridine units within the structure. These pyridine units modified the triazine framework, altering the chemical environment around the C-N bonds and enhancing the β peak signal, thereby affecting the relative intensities of the α and β peaks.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 6) confirmed that the photocatalyst was primarily composed of C, N, and a small amount of O. In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Fig. 2c), peaks at 284.26 and 287.56 eV were attributed to C-C and C=N-C bonds, respectively, with the former arising from adventitious carbon. For Py-CN25, a new peak at 287.21 eV corresponded to C-HNx bonds28, while the overall C 1s binding energy shifted to higher values, reflecting the electron-withdrawing effect of the pyridine group. The N 1s spectrum (Fig. 2d) displayed peaks at 398.05 eV (C-N=C), 399.87 eV (N-(C)3), and 403.52 eV (C-NHx). For Py-CN25, a slight shift to lower binding energies suggested electron enrichment at nitrogen sites29. Near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (NEXAFS) spectroscopy was conducted to probe the electronic structure of pyridine-modified carbon nitride. As shown in the C K-edge spectra (Fig. 2e), the π*C=C excitation exhibited a blue shift from 283.64 to 283.84 eV after pyridine incorporation, indicating enhanced bond strength and π-electron delocalization induced by the introduction of carbon-rich aromatic rings. Meanwhile, the reduced intensity at 287.5 eV, corresponding to the π*C-N=C transition, suggested that pyridine incorporation perturbed the heptazine conjugated framework, leading to partial reconfiguration of the local electronic environment. These results confirmed that carbon doping via pyridine units effectively modulated the chemical bonding and electronic structure of carbon nitride, demonstrating the successful structural modification of the heptazine framework by the incorporated pyridine rings. The opposing shifts of C 1s (to higher values) and N 1s (to lower values) binding energies confirmed the electron-withdrawing effect of the pyridine group, which enhanced the exposure of C-NHx active sites and increased external carbon content23,30,31.

Photoelectrochemical properties of photocatalysts

The optical and electronic properties of the photocatalysts were systematically investigated using UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy, and electrochemical analyses. As shown in Fig. 3a, pristine g-C3N4 exhibited moderate light absorption mainly in the ultraviolet to blue region (200–450 nm). After pyridine-assisted modification, Py-CN25 displayed markedly enhanced absorption intensity together with an extended absorption edge toward the visible region. This enhancement could not be solely attributed to morphological effects, but was closely related to modification-induced regulation of the electronic structure, including changes in C/N orbital contributions and enhanced electron delocalization, as discussed below.

The bandgap energies of the materials were calculated using the Tauc plot method, as presented in Fig. 3b. The bandgap of g-C3N4 was determined to be 2.79 eV, while Py-CN25 exhibited a reduced bandgap of 2.61 eV. The flat-band potentials of the photocatalysts were estimated from Mott–Schottky plots recorded at 1.0, 1.2, and 1.4 kHz (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Both g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 showed positive slopes, consistent with the characteristics of n-type semiconductors. The VB positions were independently determined by VB-XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9). The flat-band potentials derived from the intercepts were approximately −1.03 V versus NHE for g-C3N4 and −0.99 V versus NHE for Py-CN25. For n-type semiconductors, the conduction band (CB) edge can be approximated from the flat-band potential, giving CB positions of −1.03 and −0.93 eV for g-C3N4 and Py-CN25, respectively. The pyridine-assisted modification resulted in a slight shift of the CB position by approximately 0.04 eV, indicating a modification-induced change in the electronic structure. Additionally, VB-XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9) was performed to determine the VB positions of the photocatalysts. The tangent method indicated VB positions of 1.76 and 1.68 eV versus NHE for g-C3N4 and Py-CN25, respectively. Based on these results, the energy band structures were constructed (Fig. 3c), revealing a reduction in the bandgap from 2.79 to 2.61 eV, which was consistent with the modification-induced redistribution of electronic states involving C and N atoms. The optical excitation process mainly involved the intrinsic π → π* transition within the conjugated heptazine framework, together with an enhanced n → π* transition associated with lone-pair electrons on nitrogen atoms after pyridine incorporation. This reduced bandgap improved light absorption, facilitated photoexcitation, and promoted electron transfer for subsequent oxygen activation and singlet oxygen generation. Figure 3d shows the PL spectra, which were used to analyze the separation behavior of photoinduced electron-hole pairs, an essential aspect of the photocatalytic mechanism. The introduction of pyridine groups significantly reduced the photoluminescence intensity, with Py-CN25 exhibiting the lowest emission peak, suggesting suppressed radiative recombination. This indicated that Py-CN25 exhibited more efficient separation of photogenerated charge carriers among the tested samples.

Figure 3e shows the EIS spectra of the materials, where Py-CN25 exhibited a smaller semicircle radius, indicating that the pyridine-modified carbon nitride material possessed a lower charge transfer resistance. Under light illumination, the charge transfer resistance of the material further decreased, suggesting that the photogenerated electron-hole pairs were more efficiently separated and transferred. Furthermore, the photocurrent response test (Fig. 3f) demonstrated the transfer and transport ability of photogenerated electrons in the photocatalyst. Py-CN25 exhibited an increased photocurrent response, indicating improved photogenerated electron transfer capability, which was consistent with the previous characterization results.

As shown in Fig. 3g, a stronger Lorentzian signal peak was observed at g = 2.002 for Py-CN25, indicating the presence of more unpaired electrons in the material compared to g-C3N4. This enhancement in the signal was observed by EPR and may have been attributed to the increased oxygen content (from 1.65 to 3.62%, Supplementary Fig. 6). It was speculated that the use of pyridine dicarboxylic acid as a precursor introduced partial oxygen defects, leading to the formation of more unpaired electrons and defect centers in the material.

Furthermore, to investigate the active species generated during the photocatalytic degradation of BP-3, DMPO was used to capture ·O2− (Fig. 3h), and TEMP was employed to capture 1O2 (Fig. 3i). The results showed that with the addition of PA, the EPR signal intensity initially increased and then decreased, indicating that a 25% incorporation level yielded the optimal performance. This aligned well with the photocatalytic degradation experimental results.

The observed trend could be attributed to the role of oxygen defects and active sites introduced by pyridine groups, which enhanced the generation and stabilization of reactive oxygen species (ROS). At the optimal level of 25%, the efficient formation of ·O2− and 1O2 was facilitated, promoting the effective separation and utilization of photogenerated carriers. However, excessive modification may have led to reduced signal intensity due to the saturation of active sites or increased recombination of photogenerated carriers, thereby weakening the ROS formation efficiency32,33. This EPR analysis provided a clear mechanistic understanding of the contributions of active species to the photocatalytic degradation process34,35.

The vitality recovery of corals and the degradation of pollutants

As shown in Fig. 4a, during the acclimation phase, the soft coral’s polyps did not extend externally. After a 10-day acclimation period, the tentacles opened and exhibited strong fluorescence under blue light illumination, indicating robust coral vitality and a healthy state of symbiotic zooxanthellae. Because BP-3 had limited solubility in seawater, a trace amount of acetonitrile was required to prepare the stock solution for the exposure experiments. As organic solvents may themselves interfere with coral toxicity assessments36, two sets of control experiments were conducted prior to the BP-3 degradation studies to exclude potential interference from the solvent and the photocatalysts themselves during coral vitality assessments.

First, a solvent control experiment was performed to evaluate the biological effect of acetonitrile at the concentration used in this work. The results showed that acetonitrile at 0.07% (v/v) did not induce detectable changes in coral morphology, fluorescence, or symbiotic zooxanthellae abundance over a 20-day period (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Text S6). In parallel, control experiments were carried out in the absence of BP-3 to assess the intrinsic biocompatibility of the photocatalyst-immobilized substrates under light irradiation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, no observable adverse effects on coral vitality were detected, indicating that the photocatalysts alone did not compromise coral health under the experimental conditions.

Subsequently, exposure experiments were conducted by increasing the BP-3 concentration from 1 to 15 mg L−1. This escalation resulted in a gradual decline in the density of symbiotic zooxanthellae within the coral, as depicted in Supplementary Figs. 12–20. Through half-inhibition concentration (EC50) analysis, the EC50 of BP-3 was determined to be 6.625 ± 0.514 mg L−1 (Fig. 4b). Based on these findings, subsequent experiments aimed at assessing coral vitality recovery were standardized using a BP-3 concentration of 7 mg L−1.

The degradation of BP-3 was used to evaluate the pollutant removal performance of the photocatalysts. Prior to irradiation, the suspensions were stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. As shown in Fig. 4c, all samples exhibited limited physical adsorption toward BP-3, with Py-CN25 showing the highest adsorption capacity, which was consistent with its increased surface area and mesoporosity. In contrast, the control experiment without photocatalysts showed negligible BP-3 removal during the dark stage.

Figure 4d shows the photocatalytic degradation of BP-3 under light irradiation. In the absence of photocatalysts, BP-3 exhibited only minimal photolysis, with a degradation efficiency of approximately 8% after 120 min. By comparison, Py-CN25 achieved over 95% degradation within 120 min, whereas pristine g-C3N4 showed a much lower degradation efficiency of approximately 25% under identical conditions.

Since the value of C was very small, KC was neglected in this calculation. Thus, Eq. 4 was simplified as Eq. 5, where Ct and C0 represent the reactant concentrations at time t and at the beginning of the reaction, respectively, and kapp represents the apparent reaction rate constant.

According to the calculations presented in Supplementary Fig. 21, the apparent reaction rate constant of BP-3 by the catalyst increased from 0.003 min−1 to a maximum of 0.023 min−1, resulting in an approximately 8-fold enhancement in the degradation rate.

As shown in Fig. 4e, the coral exposed to 7 mg L−1 BP-3 for 10 days exhibited poor condition, with retracted tentacles and impaired physiological state. After transferring the coral to a nitrogen-carbon photocatalyst-coated purple sand substrate and culturing it for 21 days (Supplementary Fig. 22), its vitality was restored, fluorescence returned to normal, and the tentacles fully extended.

As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 23, the BP-3 solution exhibited a gradual color change from light yellow to colorless during the photocatalytic degradation process, indicating the effective decomposition of BP-3 over the reaction period. To evaluate the long-term operational stability of Py-CN25, consecutive photocatalytic degradation cycles were conducted under identical conditions. Each cycle lasted 2 h, after which a fresh BP-3 solution was added to restore the concentration to 7 mg L−1, while the catalyst loading, light intensity, solution volume, and reaction temperature were maintained constant throughout the 30 h cycling test. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24a, Py-CN25 maintained a high BP-3 degradation efficiency over 15 consecutive cycles, with only a slight decrease in removal performance (about 9%) observed after prolonged operation. Notably, after 15 consecutive photocatalytic degradation cycles (30 h in total), the XRD pattern exhibited a discernible increase in the intensity of the diffraction peak at 13.5°, corresponding to the (100) plane of Py-CN25 (Supplementary Fig. 24b). This enhancement suggested an increase in in-plane structural ordering during prolonged cycling, which may have arisen from the rearrangement of initially disordered regions or partial stacking of polymeric layers under continuous light irradiation. Such gradual structural evolution may have partially reduced the accessibility of surface-active sites or promoted local densification of the polymeric framework, thereby plausibly accounting for the modest decrease in photocatalytic efficiency observed after 15 cycles.

The removal efficiency of BP-3 in the samples was measured using the total organic carbon (TOC) method (Supplementary Fig. 25). After 120 min of illumination, the total organic carbon content in the sample decreased from 7.740 to 3.124 mg, achieving a mineralization rate of 59.6%. Notably, even after BP-3 was degraded to a colorless state, the sample continued to exhibit a trend of further mineralization, indicating that the residual organic carbon was converted into inorganic carbon. To elucidate the reactive species responsible for this photocatalytic degradation, radical scavenging experiments were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 26). Benzoquinone (BQ) was used to quench superoxide radicals (·O2−), sodium azide (NaN3) to quench singlet oxygen (1O2), and EDTA-2Na to quench photogenerated holes (h+), alongside a control without scavengers. After 2 h irradiation, the BP-3 degradation efficiency decreased from 96% (control) to 54% with NaN3, 28% with BQ, and 33% with EDTA-2Na. These results indicated that singlet oxygen was the dominant reactive species, while ·O2− and h+ contributed indirectly via the formation of 1O2 in the sequential pathway (O2 + e− → ·O2−; ·O2− + h+ → 1O2). This finding provided mechanistic support for the observed mineralization behavior and highlighted the critical role of 1O2 in the photocatalytic process.

Research on pollutant degradation pathways and product toxicity

Based on the tentatively identified intermediates from LC–MS signals and literature-supported transformation pathways, a plausible photocatalytic oxidation mechanism of BP-3 over Py-CN25 was proposed in Fig. 5a, primarily divided into four pathways. In Pathway I, 1O2 generated by Py-CN25 under light irradiation facilitated the successive addition of hydroxyl groups to the aromatic rings of BP-3, leading to the formation of intermediates P1, P2, P3, P4, and P5, highlighting the critical role of 1O2 in promoting hydroxylation and subsequent oxidative transformations. Pathway II involved two branches, where the first branch progressed from BP-3 to P6 and then to P7, and the second branch proceeded from BP-3 to P8 and subsequently to P9, suggesting the incorporation of carboxyl groups and the cleavage of aromatic structures mediated by 1O2. Pathway III may involve the effective cleavage of complex aromatic bonds, accompanied by carbonylation and carboxylation processes. Pathway IV revealed the possibility of BP-3 restructuring in complex environments. Additionally, the presence of low-molecular-weight signals (m/z 70–118, P13–P15) indicated the occurrence of fragmentation and the formation of lower molecular weight compounds during degradation. Overall, the proposed degradation mechanism underscored the critical role of 1O2 in mediating the oxidative breakdown of BP-3 when utilizing nitrogen-doped carbon photocatalysts. The generation of reactive oxygen species by Py-CN25 under light irradiation initiated multiple degradation pathways, culminating in the transformation of BP-3 into smaller, more oxidized compounds and small molecular byproducts. This multifaceted degradation process highlighted the potential of nitrogen-doped carbon materials in effectively mitigating pollutant levels through advanced photocatalytic strategies.

Figure 5b, c presented the toxicity levels of most degradation products. Data for polymerization products were absent due to their intricate molecular structures and high molecular weights, which resulted in unreliable calculations. These polymerization products generally exhibited low solubility and were therefore unlikely to persist in the aqueous phase, thereby posing minimal risk to biological and ecological systems. In contrast, all other degradation products demonstrated lower toxicity compared to BP-3. The EC50 values of BP-3 for the three tested organisms were 8.01, 5.24, and 6.98 mg L−1, respectively. The EC50 values for the degradation products exceeded these figures and increased progressively along the degradation pathways. Specifically, the products generated at the conclusion of Pathways I and III were classified as not harmful (EC50 > 100 mg L−1). While the product from Pathway II retained some toxicity due to the incorporation of carboxyl groups, its toxicity was markedly reduced compared to the original BP-3 compound37. Figure 5d illustrated the geographic locations of the natural seawater sampling sites used in this study. Seawater samples were collected from representative coastal waters in Xiamen (Fujian, China), Sanya (Hainan, China), and Wollongong (New South Wales, Australia), spanning subtropical nearshore, tropical coral-associated, and temperate open-coast marine environments. These sites represented typical coral-associated coastal environments and spanned both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres, encompassing distinct seawater temperature and salinity regimes. Using seawater from these regions enabled a representative evaluation of BP-3 behavior under realistic and geographically diverse marine conditions. In addition, population density was overlaid as a grayscale map to provide contextual information on anthropogenic pressure in the adjacent coastal regions. The selected sampling locations, therefore, represented coastal marine environments with distinct salinity regimes and biogeochemical characteristics, spanning river-influenced nearshore waters to open-ocean-dominated seawater.

Figure 5e compared the photocatalytic degradation kinetics of BP-3 in natural seawater collected from the three regions under identical experimental conditions. Following a 30 min dark adsorption equilibration, visible-light irradiation led to efficient BP-3 degradation in all three natural seawater matrices, with pronounced site-dependent differences. Seawater collected from Fujian exhibited the fastest apparent degradation rate, followed by New South Wales and Hainan. These differences were attributed to variations in salinity, ionic strength, and background organic constituents, which collectively influenced mass transfer and reactive species availability in natural seawater. Overall, the results demonstrated that Py-CN25 maintained high photocatalytic activity across diverse real-seawater environments, highlighting its robustness and practical potential for mitigating BP-3 pollution in marine ecosystems.

DFT calculation of photocatalysts

Structural relaxation and self-consistent atomic calculations were performed using the Device Studio program38, with the atomic coordinates provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2. Additionally, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to gain an in-depth understanding of the changes in electronic structure and bandgap. The calculated band structures of g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 are shown in Fig. 6a, d, respectively. A significant reduction in the bandgap of Py-CN25 was observed, indicating that the introduction of pyridine groups into the triazine rings of carbon nitride photocatalysts effectively narrowed the bandgap. This, in turn, enhanced simulated solar irradiation absorption, which was consistent with the observations from the UV–vis spectra.

Prior to the electronic structure analysis, the atomic structures of g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 were fully relaxed, and the corresponding electron distribution within the heptazine rings was examined (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). The optimized structures indicated that the modification altered the local electronic environment of the heptazine units, leading to a redistribution of electron density around the C and N atoms. The optimized atomic coordinates for g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2, respectively, confirming that the geometries were well converged.

The density of states (DOS) for g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 (Fig. 6b, e) revealed that the valence band (VB) edge was primarily contributed by the 2p orbitals of N atoms, while the conduction band (CB) edge mainly originated from the 2p orbitals of both N and C atoms39. This finding was consistent with previous studies. Compared to g-C3N4, the contribution of the N 2p orbitals to the CB in Py-CN25 was reduced, which could be attributed to the incorporation of pyridine ring units. This structural modification affected the relatively stable local C-N=C configurations in the material, resulting in the narrowing of the bandgap in Py-CN25. It is worth noting that, due to the inherent underestimation in DFT calculations, the computed bandgap values for photocatalytic semiconductors are always lower than experimentally measured values. Furthermore, the calculated bandgap of Py-CN25 remained consistently smaller than that of g-C3N4, in good agreement with the experimentally observed bandgap narrowing40,41.

The electron localization function (ELF) was calculated to analyze the distribution of localized electrons within the triazine structure, as shown in Fig. 6c, f. The electron cloud distribution in carbon nitride exhibited good reproducibility and periodicity. However, at the pyridine ring of Py-CN25, the electron density near the carbon atom adjacent to the pyridinic nitrogen was significantly reduced. This was attributed to the weaker electron-withdrawing ability of carbon atoms compared to nitrogen atoms, leading to the delocalization of electrons. In this case, some electrons transitioned from a localized bonding state to a more delocalized state, resulting in an electron cloud that was distributed over a larger spatial range. Such modification-induced electron delocalization was expected to directly influence the surface interaction with molecular oxygen.

To further quantify the impact of electronic delocalization on oxygen activation, density functional theory calculations were performed to evaluate the adsorption behavior of O2 on g-C3N4 and Py-CN25 surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 29). The calculated O2 adsorption energy on Py-CN25 (Ead = − 2.116 eV) was markedly more negative than that on pristine g-C3N4 (Ead = − 1.886 eV), indicating a stronger interaction between O2 molecules and the pyridine-modified carbon nitride surface. This enhanced O2 adsorption provided a thermodynamically favorable basis for subsequent electron transfer to adsorbed O2, thereby facilitating oxygen activation and singlet oxygen (1O2) generation.

The delocalization of electrons provided a higher population of mobile electrons within the C-N framework, which could not only participate more efficiently in the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs but also move more flexibly to photocatalytic adsorption and reaction sites. This ultimately enhanced the photocatalytic reaction rate. These findings were consistent with experimental observations, including XPS and UV–vis spectroscopy.

Discussion

This study presents a carbon-doped carbon nitride photocatalyst designed to address the dual challenges of marine chemical pollution and coral degradation. By integrating pyridine-based structural modifications, the photocatalyst shows enhanced singlet oxygen generation, visible-light absorption, and photogenerated charge separation. These changes correspond to an approximately eightfold improvement in the apparent degradation rate of BP-3, together with reduced predicted ecological toxicity and improved coral vitality recovery. The immobilization of the photocatalyst within simulated reef structures further reduces material loss and supports coral attachment, indicating its potential for marine-relevant applications.

Toxicity assessments of degradation intermediates indicate reduced ecological risks and support the environmental compatibility of this approach. Beyond mitigating the impacts of BP-3 pollution, the findings suggest the potential of carbon nitride photocatalysts for marine ecosystem restoration. This work provides a basis for integrating photocatalytic technologies with environmental conservation strategies, linking laboratory-scale material design with ecological application. Future studies could further optimize the durability of the photocatalyst under diverse marine conditions and extend its application to the degradation of other persistent marine pollutants.

Methods

Materials

Unless otherwise specified, all reagents were of analytical grade or higher and were used without further purification. Melamine, pyridine-3,5-dicarboxylic acid, NaOH, NaCl, anhydrous sodium sulfite, and sodium oxalate were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Deionized water was used throughout the experiments.

Preparation of pristine g-C3N4

Pristine g-C3N4 was prepared by direct thermal polymerization of melamine. Briefly, 3.320 g of melamine was heated in a tubular furnace to 550 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 and maintained for 4 h. The obtained light-yellow powder was denoted as g-C3N4.

Preparation of Py-CNx photocatalysts

Pyridine-modified carbon nitride photocatalysts were prepared through a hydrothermal treatment followed by thermal calcination. For Py-CN25, 3.783 g (0.03 mol) melamine was dissolved in 100 mL deionized water and stirred for 2 h. Pyridine-3,5-dicarboxylic acid (1.253 g, corresponding to 25 mol% relative to melamine) was then added and stirred for another 1 h. The mixture was transferred into a PTFE-lined autoclave and heated at 190 °C for 12 h. After washing and drying, the resulting precursor was calcined under the same conditions used for pristine g-C3N4. Py-CNx samples with different pyridine-3,5-dicarboxylic acid contents (x = 10, 20, 25, 30, and 50) were prepared using the same procedure.

Preparation of immobilized catalyst devices

The immobilized catalyst devices were fabricated using soft purple clay as the supporting substrate. The clay was molded into cubic substrates and calcined in a muffle furnace at 1100 °C with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 to obtain rigid bases. The catalyst precursor containing melamine and the carbon nitride precursor was then uniformly coated onto the surface of the purple clay substrate, followed by a secondary calcination process under the same conditions used for g-C3N4 synthesis.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance powder diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 5–80°. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Gemini SEM 300). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analyses were carried out using a Hitachi HT7800 microscope and an FEI Tecnai G2 F30 S-TWIN microscope, respectively. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired using an Antaris II FT-NIR spectrometer. Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz wide-bore solid-state NMR spectrometer. UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra were measured using a Cary 5000 UV–visible-near-infrared spectrophotometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using an ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded using an A300-10/12 spectrometer. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were collected using an FS-5 spectrofluorometer with an excitation wavelength of 380 nm. Transient photocurrent responses, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and Mott–Schottky measurements were performed using a CHI660B electrochemical workstation. BP-3 concentrations were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS, Agilent 6460).

Additional instrumental details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

DFT calculations

Spin-polarized density functional theory calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with plane-wave basis sets and the projector augmented-wave method. The exchange-correlation potential was described using the generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional. The initial C26N32 and C26N30 structural models were prepared using VASPKIT and Device Studio, and structural relaxation and self-consistent calculations were performed using VASP. The plane-wave energy cutoff was set to 500 eV, and Brillouin-zone sampling was performed using a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. The structures were fully relaxed until the maximum atomic force was below 0.01 eV Å−1, with an energy convergence threshold of 10−5 eV. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was applied perpendicular to the slab, and van der Waals interactions were considered using Grimme’s DFT-D3 correction.

Seawater preparation

Artificial seawater was prepared using a commercial synthetic marine salt (Blue Treasure, Qingdao Sea-Salt Aquarium Technology Co., Ltd., China). The salt was dissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 33–35 g L−1 to obtain a salinity of 33–35 PSU, consistent with typical tropical reef seawater conditions. The prepared seawater was aerated for 24 h before use to ensure complete salt dissolution, equilibration with atmospheric oxygen, and stabilization of pH. No additional nutrients or chemical supplements were added.

Coral rearing and fragmentation

Starburst Polyp Coral (Pachyclavularia sp.) was used as the experimental organism. Coral specimens were legally obtained from Huadu Hui Market in Yangzhou City, and only healthy individuals showing normal morphology and vigorous polyp extension were selected for the experiments. After transportation to the laboratory, the corals were maintained in a simulated marine aquarium at the School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, Yangzhou University. The rearing conditions were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C, with a salinity of 35.0 ± 0.3‰, a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, and a water flow rate of 1800 L h−1. A water circulation system equipped with an impurity filtration unit and a protein skimmer was used to maintain water clarity and system stability. No external nutrient solutions or chemical additives were introduced into the seawater system.

During the acclimation period, coral health was monitored daily based on polyp extension, coloration, and fluorescence. After acclimation, coral samples were fragmented into approximately 4 × 4 cm units using a sterile scalpel and fixed onto ceramic bases or custom-made purple clay substrates with fine threads. The fragments were then monitored daily. Coral fragments showing full polyp extension and fluorescence recovery after approximately 30 days were used for subsequent BP-3 exposure and recovery experiments.

Solvent control experiment

To evaluate the potential biological effects of acetonitrile used for BP-3 dissolution, a solvent control experiment was conducted before the BP-3 exposure experiments. Corals were cultured in simulated seawater containing 0.07% (v/v) acetonitrile without BP-3 for 20 days under the same conditions used for the exposure experiments. During the cultivation period, coral morphology, polyp extension, coloration, and fluorescence signals were continuously monitored. Coral tissues were sampled at day 0, day 10, and day 20, homogenized, and the abundance of symbiotic zooxanthellae was quantified using a hemocytometer-based cell counting method.

BP-3 exposure experiments

A 14-day laboratory exposure experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of BP-3 on Pachyclavularia sp. under controlled conditions. Corals were exposed to BP-3 at concentrations of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15 mg L−1 in simulated seawater. Each concentration group was maintained in an individual acrylic container to prevent cross-contamination between treatments. BP-3 (purity ≥99%, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was dissolved in acetonitrile to prepare a 10 g L−1 stock solution and then diluted with simulated seawater to obtain the target BP-3 concentrations. The final acetonitrile concentration in all exposure experiments was maintained at 0.07% (v/v).

Photocatalytic degradation of BP-3

Photocatalytic degradation experiments were conducted under simulated solar irradiation using a 300 W xenon lamp equipped with an AM 1.5 G filter. The light intensity was calibrated to 100 mW cm−2 using an optical power density meter. The experiments were carried out in aqueous systems containing photocatalyst-immobilized simulated reef substrates. Before light irradiation, the BP-3 solution containing the photocatalyst-loaded substrates was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. During the photocatalytic process, aliquots of approximately 3 mL were withdrawn at 20 min intervals and immediately filtered through membrane filters to remove insoluble matter. BP-3 concentrations in the filtrates were quantified using LC–MS/MS.

ECOSAR-based toxicity prediction

The acute and chronic aquatic toxicity of BP-3 and its identified degradation intermediates was estimated using the Ecological Structure Activity Relationships model (ECOSAR, version 2.2) developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Toxicity endpoints were predicted for three representative aquatic organisms, including fish, daphnid, and green algae, based on the molecular structures of the corresponding compounds. Polymerization products were excluded from the toxicity evaluation because of their complex molecular structures, high molecular weights, and limited aqueous solubility, which made reliable ECOSAR prediction unsuitable.

Natural seawater collection and degradation experiments

Natural seawater samples were collected from nearshore surface waters at three representative coastal locations: Xiamen, Fujian, China (24.45°N, 118.08°E), Sanya, Hainan, China (18.22°N, 109.50°E), and Wollongong, New South Wales, Australia (34.43°S, 150.90°E). Samples were collected from approximately 0–20 cm below the water surface using pre-cleaned polyethylene containers and transported under dark conditions. Before use, the seawater samples were filtered through 0.45 μm membranes to remove suspended particulate matter while preserving dissolved ionic constituents. Photocatalytic degradation experiments in natural seawater were conducted under the same conditions as those used in simulated seawater. BP-3 was added to the target concentration, followed by the introduction of photocatalyst-immobilized reef substrates. Before illumination, the systems were magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium, followed by visible-light irradiation under identical xenon lamp conditions.

Statistics and reproducibility

Unless otherwise stated, photocatalytic degradation experiments and coral-related biological experiments were repeated at least three times. Coral vitality recovery experiments were conducted using three separately cultured coral groups. Zooxanthellae density was quantified using a hemocytometer-based counting method. For BP-3 degradation experiments, liquid samples were collected and analyzed in triplicate, and the mean values were used for data analysis. Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation where applicable. Sample sizes and experimental replicates are specified in the corresponding figure legends.
