An ensemble Kalman approach to randomized maximum likelihood estimation
Pavlos Stavrinides, Elizabeth Qian

Abstract
This work proposes ensemble Kalman randomized maximum likelihood estimation, a new derivative-free method for performing randomized maximum likelihood estimation, which is a method that can be used to generate approximate samples from posterior distributions in Bayesian inverse problems. The new method has connections to ensemble Kalman inversion and works by evolving an ensemble so that each ensemble member solves an instance of a randomly perturbed optimization problem. Linear analysis demonstrates that ensemble members converge exponentially fast to randomized maximum likelihood estimators and, furthermore, that the new method produces samples from the Bayesian posterior when applied to a suitably regularized optimization problem. The method requires that the forward operator, relating the unknown parameter to the data, be evaluated once per iteration per ensemble member, which can be prohibitively expensive when the forward model requires the evolution of a high-dimensional dynamical system. We propose a strategy for making the proposed method tractable in this setting based on a balanced truncation model reduction method tailored to the Bayesian smoothing problem. Theoretical results show near-optimality of this model reduction approach via convergence to an optimal approximation of the posterior covariance as a low-rank update to the prior covariance. Numerical experiments verify theoretical results and illustrate computational acceleration through model reduction.

The contributions of this work are the following:

1.

We propose ensemble Kalman RMLE, a new method which builds on ensemble Kalman inversion methods to perform randomized maximum likelihood estimation.

2.

We present linear analysis of our new method. Our results show exponential convergence of (i) particles to perturbed least squares minimizers, (ii) the ensemble mean to the standard minimum-norm least squares minimizer, and (iii) the ensemble covariance to the Hessian of the optimization evaluated at the minimizer.

3.

We show that application of our theoretical results to a suitably defined regularized minimization yields exponential convergence of the particles to samples from the posterior distribution of a linear Gaussian Bayesian inverse problem, and of the ensemble mean and covariance to the Bayesian posterior mean and covariance.

4.

For the specific problem of inferring the initial condition of a high-dimensional linear dynamical system, we propose a strategy for reducing the computational cost of our method using the balanced truncation approach of
[
86
]
that yields near-optimal posterior covariance approximations.

5.

We demonstrate numerically our convergence theory, convergence to the Gaussian posterior and computational acceleration via model reduction.

Background

This section defines the inverse problem of interest in
Section

2.1
and introduces the basic ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI) method in

2.1
Problem formulation

We consider the inverse problem of inferring an unknown parameter of interest
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from observations
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
described by the following measurement model:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(1)

where
\mathbf{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a called the forward map and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents measurement error. One way to estimate
\mathbf{v}
from
\mathbf{y}
is to minimize the data misfit:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v}))^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v}))\equiv\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v})\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a weight matrix. In the statistical inference context, if the noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
in (
1
) has probability distribution
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
, then minimizers of (
2
) are maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) of
\mathbf{v}
.

If the optimization problem (
2
) is ill-posed, and/or if prior information about the unknown
\mathbf{v}
is available from domain knowledge, it is common to reformulate the parameter estimation as a regularized minimization,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big[(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v}))^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v}))+(\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}(\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}})\big],

(3)

which adds a penalty on the distance from the estimate for
\mathbf{v}
from a reference value
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
weighted by positive definite
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Note that the regularized minimization (
3
) can be expressed as a least-squares minimization of the form (
2
) as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}-\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}(\mathbf{v}))^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}-\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}(\mathbf{v}))\equiv\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}-\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}(\mathbf{v})\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}^{-1}},

(4)

where we have defined the following regularized least-squares (RLS) quantities:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}(\mathbf{v})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v})\\
\mathbf{v}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}&\\
&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

The minimizer of (
4
) has the following connection to the Bayesian inferential approach: if the parameter
\mathbf{v}
is endowed with a Gaussian prior distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}})
, then the Bayesian posterior density
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{y})
has the form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{y})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}-\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}(\mathbf{v})\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}^{-1}}\right),

(6)

The minimizer of (
4
) therefore coincides with the Bayesian maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator
[
59
,
57
,
58
,
1
]
.

We have thus far considered inverse problem formulations for a general nonlinear forward operator
\mathbf{H}
. When
\mathbf{H}
is
linear
, further connections between the optimization and statistical perspectives are possible.
Note that for linear
\mathbf{H}
, the standard the norm-minimizing solution of (
2
) is

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\star}=\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{y}\equiv\mathbf{H}^{+}\mathbf{y},

where
\dagger
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse and
\mathbf{H}^{+}=\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}
is the weighted pseudoinverse.
Then, samples from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{v}^{\star},(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{\dagger}\big)
may be obtained via
randomized maximum likelihood estimation
(RMLE)
[
35
,
75
]
, which solves instances of the following randomized least squares problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}}^{2},

(7)

where one draws one realization of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
per sample.
For linear Gaussian Bayesian inverse problems, applying RMLE to the regularized least-squares operators (
5
) yields samples from the Bayesian posterior distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}})
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}}=\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}}\big).

(8)

Note that in the optimization perspective, the posterior mean is the norm-minimizing solution of (
4
), i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos}}=\mathbf{v}^{\star}_{\text{RLS}}=\mathbf{H}^{+}_{\text{RLS}}\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}}
, and the posterior precision is the Hessian of (
4
) evaluated at the minimizer, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}}^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}
.

2.2
Ensemble Kalman Inversion (EKI)

Ensemble Kalman Inversion (EKI)
[
31
]
is a derivative-free, iterative method for solving weighted least squares problems of the form (
2
).
The method begins with an initial ensemble of size
J
,
\big\{\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(J)}\big\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, e.g., drawn from a suitable prior distribution, and evolves the ensemble for iterations
i=0,1,2,\ldots
, via the update step,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\big(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(j)}\big),\text{ where }\mathbf{K}_{i}=\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(1:J)},\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(1;J)}\big]\cdot\big(\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(1:J)}\big]+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1},

(9)

and
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}\big)
, for all
j=1,\ldots,J
. Throughout this work, we use
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}
to denote the true expected value and covariance, respectively, and
\mathsf{E}
and
\mathsf{Cov}
to denote the empirical ensemble mean and covariance, respectively. That is, given
\{\mathbf{a}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
and
\{\mathbf{b}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, we define:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\big[\mathbf{a}^{(1:J)}\big]=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{a}^{(j)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{a}^{(1:J)},\mathbf{b}^{(1:J)}\big]=\frac{1}{J-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big(\mathbf{a}^{(j)}-\mathsf{E}\big[\mathbf{a}^{(j)}\big]\big)\big(\mathbf{b}^{(j)}-\mathsf{E}\big[\mathbf{b}^{(j)}\big]\big)^{\top},

and let
\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{a}^{(1:J)}\big]=\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{a}^{(1:J)},\mathbf{a}^{(1:J)}\big]
for brevity.
The observation
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}
used to define the update step (
9
) can be defined in two ways, yielding two variants of the basic EKI algorithm:

Deterministic EKI:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{y},

(10a)

Stochastic EKI:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}^{(j)},\text{ with }\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\text{ i.i.d.}

(10b)

In stochastic EKI, it is common to set
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, so that the perturbations in (
10b
) are drawn from a distribution that is consistent with the statistical interpretation of the noise in (
1
).

Algorithm

1
summarizes both the deterministic and stochastic versions of basic EKI.
We emphasize that the EKI algorithm is applicable to nonlinear
\mathbf{H}
and is adjoint- and derivative-free, only requiring forward evaluations of
\mathbf{H}
.
The work
[
57
]
shows that EKI can be applied equally well to solve regularized least-squares problems of the form (
4
) by simply running
Algorithm

1
using the RLS operators (
5
).

For linear
\mathbf{H}
, particles in both the deterministic and stochastic variants of the basic EKI algorithm have been shown to collapse to the minimum norm solution
\mathbf{v}^{\star}
of (
2
) in a subspace of the range of the ensemble that is associated with observable directions under
\mathbf{H}
, at a
1/\sqrt{i}
rate
[
44
]
. Thus, in the
i\to\infty
limit, the algorithm is viewed as an optimizer and not as a sampler. Several EKI-related approaches have been proposed for sampling from Bayesian posteriors, for example by integrating the continuous-time limit of the EKI iteration up to
t=1

[
29
]
, designing a system based on Langevin dynamics that converges to the posterior
[
60
]
, or using deterministic quadrature rules to compute statistics
[
63
]
. In this work, we propose a new sampling algorithm that combines classical EKI with randomized maximum likelihood estimation.

3.1
Ensemble Kalman Randomized Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We propose a new ensemble Kalman method based on randomized maximum likelihood estimation. Our method is a variant of the basic EKI algorithm with
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}
in the update step (
9
) defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\equiv\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)},\quad\text{where }\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}),

(11)

for all
j=1,\ldots,J
. We will show that for linear
\mathbf{H}
, the
j
-th ensemble member therefore solves

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\lVert\mathbf{y}^{(j)}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{v})\big\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}}^{2},

(12)

which is an instance of the randomized least-squares problem solved by randomized maximum likelihood estimation (
7
).
We call our method
ensemble Kalman randomized maximum likelihood estimation
, and summarize its implementation in
Algorithm

2
.

One key difference between our method and basic EKI is in the data perturbation used to define the update step. Deterministic EKI (
Algorithm

1
with the choice (
10a
)) uses a zero perturbation for all particles for all iterations. Stochastic EKI (
Algorithm

1
with the choice (
10b
)) perturbs the data with a perturbation that is independently drawn for each particle
at every iteration
. In our method (
Algorithm

2
), the update step for each particle uses a perturbation that is independently drawn for each particle, but
constant across iterations
. Another key difference between our method and basic EKI is that EKI (
Algorithm

1
) returns a point estimate, namely, the ensemble mean, whereas our method (
Algorithm

2
) returns a collection of samples which allows mean and covariance estimates to be computed.

3.2
Linear mean field analysis

We now summarize our main theoretical results concerning convergence of our proposed method, deferring detailed technical proofs and intermediate results to
Section

5
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the initial ensemble is drawn from a distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and positive (semi-)definite covariance
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. This allows us to interpret an arbitrary initial ensemble as being drawn from a distribution with mean and covariance given by the initial sample mean and covariance.
We also assume that the data perturbations
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
are independent of the initial ensemble.

For
j=1,2,\ldots,J
, let
\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}
denote the norm-minimizing solution of the perturbed least-squares minimization (
12
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}=\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\equiv\mathbf{H}^{+}\mathbf{y}^{(j)},

and denote by
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{v}_{i+1}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}
the residual between the
j
-th particle and its corresponding minimum-norm least squares solution. Let
\mathbf{C}_{i}\equiv\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(1:J)}\big]
. Then:

Proposition 3.1
.

The
j
-th particle residual satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)},

(13)

where we define
\mathbf{M}_{i}\equiv\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{H}
and, for
i\geq k
, we introduce:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{i:k}\equiv\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i-1}\cdots\mathbf{M}_{k+1}\mathbf{M}_{k}=\prod_{\ell=k}^{i}\mathbf{M}_{\ell}.

(14)

Proof.

For linear
\mathbf{H}
, note that
\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(1:J)}\big]=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}
, and
\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(1:J)},\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(1:J)}\big]=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}
, so that
\mathbf{K}_{i}=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}
. Then, the update rule in
eq.

11
becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\big(\mathbf{y}^{(j)}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}\big)=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}.

(15)

Substituting (
15
) into the residual
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}
yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{i+1}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{i}\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}\big)+\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}+\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{K}_{i}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big).

(16)

To simplify the above expression, we note that via Woodbury identity:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{H}\equiv\mathbf{M}_{i}.

Another application of the Woodbury identity yields:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}

Combining the above allows us to re-express the Kalman gain
\mathbf{K}_{i}
as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}-\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\big(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{i}.

Finally, we notice that
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}
in (
16
) is the least squares misfit of the norm-minimizing solution, and thus
\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big)\in\mathsf{ker}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)
, so
\mathbf{K}_{i}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big)=\mathbf{M}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big)=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}^{(j)}
. The second equality of (
13
) follows directly.
∎

Note that in (
13
), the misfit iteration matrices
\mathbf{M}_{i}
are random variables due to their dependence on
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, which depends on the initial ensemble. We will therefore analyze the particle behavior in the mean-field limit where ensemble size
J\to\infty
. We denote by
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
the mean field ensemble covariance, and similarly denote by
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
the mean-field limit of
\mathbf{M}_{i}
, corresponding to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\big(\mathbf{I}+\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1},

(17)

and define
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i-1}\cdots\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}
. These mean-field operator limits define an iteration which governs the behavior of the mean-field residual,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}^{(j)}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}.

(18)

Understanding the evolution of the particle residual therefore requires an understanding of the spectral properties of the map
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
, which are characterized by the following eigenvalue problem,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}=\lambda_{\ell,i}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,d.

(19)

Denote by
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{(j)}
the mean-field particle corresponding to the mean-field residual
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}
, and note that (
18
) implies:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\big)\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\big)\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}.

(20)

Then, the mean-field covariance satisfies:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}=\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}^{\top}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\big)\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\big)^{\top}.

(21)

We find that under (
21
), the eigenvectors of (
19
) are constant across iterations, and that the corresponding eigenvalues can be given in terms of the sequence of preceding eigenvalues:

Proposition 3.2
.

The eigenvectors of (
19
) are constant, satisfying
\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i+1}=\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}
for all
i\geq 0
. The eigenvalue associated with the
\ell
-th eigenvector satisfies:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=1-2\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}+(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}.

(22)

Notice that all eigenvalues of (
19
) are non-negative because nonzero eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}
must be equal to nonzero eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, with zero eigenvalues differing only in multiplicity. From (
22
), we see that zero eigenvalues remain zero and strictly positive eigenvalues remain strictly positive across all iterations. We show in
Section

5
that for all
i\geq 1
, nonzero eigenvalues of (
19
) are monotone increasing across iterations and bounded above by
1
, and further, that:

Proposition 3.3
.

Nonzero eigenvalues of (
19
) converge exponentially fast, i.e.,:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=1\text{, with }1-\lambda_{\ell,i+1}\leq 2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0}})^{-1}e^{-(i-1)\gamma_{\ell}},

where
\gamma_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell,0}(1+2\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}
.

Let
r
denote the number of strictly positive (nonzero) eigenvalues of (
19
). At
i=1
, arrange the
r
strictly positive eigenvalues (and their respective eigenvectors) in non-increasing order
\lambda_{1,1}\geq\lambda_{2,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r,1}
.
Corollary

5.3
establishes that this ordering is preserved across all iterations
i\geq 1
. Since the eigenvectors of (
19
) are constant, we drop the iteration index
i
on the eigenvectors and consider the set
\{\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{r}
, normalized so that
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{r}]
satisfies
\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}=\mathsf{diag}(\lambda_{1,i},\ldots,\lambda_{r,i})
. Then,
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}
, and
\mathsf{ran}(\mathbf{U})
is an invariant subspace under
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
. This subspace and its complement may be defined in terms of spectral projectors of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}
, as follows:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}
, and
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}
. Then,
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{S}
are complementary spectral projectors of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}
. That is,
\mathbf{P}^{2}=\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{S}^{2}=\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{S}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{S}
, with
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
.

The spectral projectors
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{S}
introduced in
Proposition

3.4
define two complementary subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})
and
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{S})
. Note that
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})=\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{U})
is the span of the eigenvectors of (
19
) associated with nonzero eigenvalues; these directions lie in
\textsf{ran}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i})
and also must not lie within
\textsf{ker}(\mathbf{H})
. The subspace
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})
may thus be interpreted as being ‘populated’ by the ensemble while being observable under
\mathbf{H}
.

Proposition

3.4
allows the mean field residual
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
(
18
) to be decomposed into two components, one lying in the observable and populated space
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})
, and the other in the complementary subspace
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{S})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}+\mathbf{S}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}+\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}.

Our next result shows that the residual component in
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})
converges to zero, while the component in
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{S})
remains constant for all iterations.
We note that the analysis of basic ensemble Kalman inversion in
[
44
]
further divides the non-convergent subspace
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{S})
into two subspaces associated with (i) observable but populated directions and (ii) unobservable directions. While such a division is also possible in our case, we choose to present results for just the convergent space
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})
and the entire non-convergent space
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{S})
to simplify the presentation.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\gamma=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq r}\gamma_{\ell}
, where
\gamma_{\ell}
is defined as in
Proposition

3.3
. For all particles
j=1,\ldots,J
, the following hold for all
i\geq 0
:

1.

\mathbf{S}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}

2.

\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\leq e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}
.

Theorem

3.5
establishes the existence of a limit for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
, and exponential convergence. The next result identifies the limit for each particle and, consequently, the limit for the ensemble mean and covariance.

Theorem 3.6
.

In the mean field limit, as
i\to\infty
, for all particles
j=1,\ldots,J
, it holds that

1.

\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}^{(j)}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}
,

2.

\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}^{(j)}\big]=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{\star}+\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
,

3.

\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}^{(j)}\big]=\mathbf{P}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}^{\top}+\mathbf{S}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{S}^{\top}
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Theorem

3.6
establishes that our method performs randomized maximum likelihood estimation in the
\mathbf{P}
subspace. Namely, we have that
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\to\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}^{(j)}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}_{\star}^{(j)}
, i.e., mean field particles become randomized maximum likelihood estimators in the
\mathbf{P}
subspace, and we also have convergence in distribution of
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
to
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{\star},\mathbf{P}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\big)
, i.e., we converge to the projection of the RMLE distribution under
\mathbf{P}
.

The results of
Theorems

3.6
and
3.7
show exponential convergence (guaranteed by
Theorem

3.5
) of mean field particles to randomized maximum likelihood estimators distributed according to the RMLE distribution, in the
\mathbf{P}
subspace. In particular, these results contrast with the behavior of stochastic and deterministic versions of basic EKI (
Algorithm

1
), where
all
particles exhibit ensemble collapse to
same
limit in the
\mathbf{P}
subspace
[
44
]
at a slow
1/\sqrt{i}
rate of convergence. In the Bayesian inference setting, we define a suitably regularized least-squares problem (
4
), and apply our method (
Algorithm

2
) with
\mathbf{y}_{\text{RLS}},\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{RLS}}
to solve the Tikhonov-regularized optimization (
4
). Application of
Theorem

3.6
to this regularized problem shows that the proposed method yields samples from the linear Gaussian Bayesian posterior:

Corollary 3.8
.

In the mean field limit, as
i\to\infty
, for all particles
j=1,\ldots,J
, it holds that

1.

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos}}
,

2.

\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
.

Corollary

3.8
establishes that
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos}},\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos}}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\big)
, which is the projection of exact Gaussian posterior of
eq.

8
onto the
\mathbf{P}
subspace. This means that, in the
\mathbf{P}
subspace, mean field particles become samples from the Gaussian posterior. We emphasize that results are derived for the mean-field limit of the iteration: we will demonstrate in

that if the ensemble size is insufficiently large, the true algorithmic iterations will lie far from their mean field counterparts.

Remark 3.9
.

While the main results presented in this section concern the state space residual, similar results can be shown regarding the behavior of the observation space data misfit,

. This analysis can be found in

.

4
Model reduction for ensemble Kalman RMLE for the linear Bayesian smoothing problem

We introduce in
Section

4.1
the
smoothing problem
, a special case of the inverse problem (
1
) describing the inference of an unknown initial condition of a linear dynamical system from noisy observations taken after the initial time. Ensemble methods for solving such inverse problems can be computationally expensive because the dynamical system must be evolved for each particle at every iteration. To reduce the computational cost in this setting, we describe in
Section

4.2
a model reduction approach from
[
86
]
tailored to this smoothing problem.
Section

4.3
proposes a strategy for reducing the computational cost of our proposed ensemble Kalman RMLE approach using this model reduction approach, and describes approximation guarantees for this strategy, building on our results in
Section

3.2
.

4.1
Inverse problem formulation

We consider a linear dynamical system with linear output:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)&=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t),\\
\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}(t),\end{split}

(23)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d}
, and the initial state
\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{v}
is unknown. We take noisy measurements,
\mathbf{y}_{k}
, of the system output
\boldsymbol{y}
at positive times
t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{m}
according to the following measurement model:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{k}\equiv\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}(t_{k})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k}=\mathbf{F}e^{\mathbf{A}t_{k}}\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k},

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\eta})
,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{out}}}
. Our goal is to infer the unknown initial state
\mathbf{v}
of the system (
23
) from the noisy measurements (
24
). This defines a linear measurement model of the form (
1
) where:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}e^{\mathbf{A}t_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{F}e^{\mathbf{A}t_{m}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\eta}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\eta}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(25)

with
n=m\cdot d_{\text{out}}
. We endow
\mathbf{v}
with the centered Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}})
. Then, the posterior mean and covariance are given by
eq.

8
(with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pr}}=\boldsymbol{0}
), whose mean is the minimizer of the Tikhonov-regularized optimization given by
eqs.

4
and
5
.

In many application settings, solving Bayesian smoothing problems of this form is computationally challenging because
\mathbf{H}
is only implicitly available through simulating a high-dimensional linear system. In our proposed ensemble Kalman RMLE approach, the system must be evolved once per particle per iteration, which may be computationally expensive. To combat this, we next introduce a balanced truncation (BT) model reduction approach from
[
86
]
that is tailored to this smoothing problem.

4.2
Model reduction via balanced truncation for Bayesian inference

Balanced truncation
[
89
,
90
]
is a system-theoretic method for projection-based model reduction that was adapted to linear Bayesian smoothing problems in
[
86
]
. The key idea is to project the high-dimensional dynamical system (
23
) onto state directions that maximize the following Rayleigh quotient:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\mathbf{z}},

(26)

where
\mathbf{Q}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mathbf{A}^{\top}t}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\eta}^{-1}\mathbf{F}e^{\mathbf{A}t}\,\mathrm{d}t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(27)

so that
\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}
can be interpreted as an energy based on integrating, over all positive time, the squared Mahalanobis distance between the equilibrium of the dynamical system and noisy outputs. Directions that maximize (
26
) are given by the leading eigenvectors of the following generalized eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}_{\ell}=\delta_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\mathbf{z}_{\ell},

and can be computed as follows. Let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top},\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
(e.g., via Cholesky factorization) and let
\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}=\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and note that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}
is invertible with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
. Generalized eigenvectors
\mathbf{z}_{\ell}
of
\big(\mathbf{Q},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\big)
are columns of
\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and become columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}
under the renormalization
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Denote by
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\rho},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rho}
the first
\rho
columns of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
respectively, and let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\rho}
be the upper
\rho\times\rho
block of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
. Then, define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\rho}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\rho}^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{\top},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho}=\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\rho}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(28)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho}=\mathbf{I}_{\rho}
. The order-
\rho
balanced truncation approximation of (
23
) is a Petrov-Galerkin projection given by restricting the state
\mathbf{x}
to lie in
\mathsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho})
and by enforcing the system (
23
) on
\mathsf{ker}\big(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}^{\top}\big)
. This results in the reduced-order approximation of (
23
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}(t)&=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\\
\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\widehat{\mathbf{F}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\end{split}

(29)

with reduced operators in (
29
) given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{\rho\times\rho},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times\rho}.

(30)

The reduced operators (
30
) can be used to cheaply integrate (
29
), which is now
\rho
-dimensional, thus yielding efficient approximate numerical simulations of (
23
). The reduced operators in (
30
) also implicitly define reduced forward maps

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{BT}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}e^{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}t_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}e^{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}t_{m}}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{\rho}^{\top},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS,BT}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{\text{BT}}\\
\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix},

(31)

and corresponding posterior mean and covariance approximations,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos,BT}}=\big(\mathbf{H}_{\text{BT}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\text{BT}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}^{-1}\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos,BT}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos,BT}}\mathbf{H}_{\text{BT}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(32)

The work in
[
86
]
shows that if
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}
satisfies
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\preceq\boldsymbol{0}
, then
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pr}}
can be interpreted as the result of spinning up the system (
23
) from
t=-\infty
with a white noise input. This interpretation coincides with the system-theoretic definition of a reachability Gramian
[
78
]
and allows the reduced system to inherit system-theoretic error bounds and stability guarantees
[
86
]
. Furthermore, the work
[
86
]
shows that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos,BT}}
converges in certain continuous-time limits of the observation process to an optimal posterior covariance approximation for linear Gaussian Bayesian inverse problems defined in
[
91
]
.

4.3
Near-optimality of BT-accelerated ensemble Kalman RMLE

For linear Bayesian smoothing problems of the form described in
Section

4.1
, we propose a strategy to reduce the computational cost of our proposed ensemble Kalman RMLE algorithm by approximating the original high-dimensional system (
23
), which is expensive to integrate, with the low-dimensional reduced system (
29
) which may be simulated more cheaply.
We now provide a convergence result for this proposed acceleration strategy. The following corollary follows from
Theorem

3.6
when applied to the least-squares problem defined by the approximate forward operator
\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS,BT}}
in (
31
). We emphasize that the forward operator
\mathbf{H}_{\text{RLS,BT}}
would not generally be explicitly formed as part of the computation; instead, the forward operator evaluation would rely on integrating the low-dimensional reduced system (
29
) rather than high-dimensional original system (
23
).

Corollary 4.1
.

As
i\to\infty
, for all
j=1,\ldots,J
, the mean field particle update (
20
) defined with reduced operators (
31
) converges in the following ways:

1.

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos,BT}}
,

2.

\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos,BT}}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
.

Corollary

4.1
establishes that
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{pos,BT}},\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{pos,BT}}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\big)
, which is the projection of the reduced Gaussian posterior given by (
32
) under
\mathbf{P}
. Note that by definition,
\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{P})\subset\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{\rho})
, the balanced truncation approximation subspace. The error of the balanced truncation approximation of our ensemble Kalman RMLE approach is therefore lower-bounded by the projection error of the balanced truncation reduced model. We will demonstrate numerically in
Section

6
that when this projection error is low, sampling error due to finite ensemble size dominates the error of the method. In particular, when the discrete observation process in the smoothing problem is close to its continuous-time limit, then the balanced truncation projection error is near optimal
[
86
]
. In such cases, the reduced cost of the balanced truncation models enable larger ensemble sizes to be computed, leading to overall reductions in error relative to the error associated with using a smaller ensemble of particles evolved with the original high-dimensional model.

5.1.1
Mean field observation space misfit

For all particles
j=1,\ldots,J
and iterations
i\geq 0
, we denote by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}
the
mean field data misfit
, which captures the deviation of the mean field iteration (
20
) under the action of
\mathbf{H}
from the perturbed data
\mathbf{y}^{(j)}
. From (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}+\mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\big)\mathbf{H}^{+}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}.

(33)

Define the
mean field misfit iteration map

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big(\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}
which, via Woodbury identity, can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big(\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}=\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)^{-1}.

(34)

Using the above, we can derive the identity:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i}\mathbf{H})=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i})\mathbf{H}=\big(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}\big)\mathbf{H}=\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}\mathbf{H},

where we have defined the mean field Kalman gain
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i}=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big)^{-1}
. Repeated use of the above identity yields
\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}
, where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\equiv\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}\cdots\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}
, and allows us to express the misfit (
33
) as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{+}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big)=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)},

(35)

where we expand each
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
using the second equality of (
34
) and use the fact that
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{+}
is the
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathsf{ran}(\mathbf{H})
. Understanding the evolution of data misfit will therefore require an understanding of the map
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
.

5.1.2
Spectral analysis of mean field misfit map

Spectral analysis of the mean field misfit map
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
will reveal fundamental subspaces of the observation space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that remain invariant under the action of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
. Consider the following generalized eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}=\lambda_{\ell,i}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=1,\ldots,n,\quad i\geq 0,

(36)

and note that all generalized eigenvalues of (
36
) are nonnegative.

Proposition 5.1
.

The eigenvectors of (
36
) are constant across all iterations, that is
\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i+1}=\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}
for all
i\geq 0
. The eigenvalue associated with the
\ell
-th eigenvector satisfies:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=1-2\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}+(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}.

(
22
)

Proof.

The proof will follow by induction on
i
. Note that (
21
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}^{\top}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)^{\top},

(37)

where we used the identities that led to (
35
). Then, observe that if
(\lambda_{\ell,i},\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i})
is an eigenpair of (
36
), we have that:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}={(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})^{-1}}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}={(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}.

(38)

Using (
38
), we can prove the base case:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}\big)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,0}{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}+\left(1-2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}}+{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\left(1-2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}}+(1+\lambda_{\ell,0}){(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}=\underbrace{\lambda_{\ell,0}{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0}})^{-1}}_{\lambda_{\ell,1}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0},

which directly implies
\mathbf{w}_{\ell,1}=\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}
and the expression (
22
) for
i=0
. Now, assume that the
\ell
-th eigenvector is constant up to, and including, iteration
i
, i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}=\cdots=\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}
. Repeated application of (
38
) yields:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}=\left(1-{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}.

(39)

Then, the following also hold:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,0}{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i},

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\left(1-2{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}.

Applying all of the above to (
37
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,0}{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}+\left(1-2{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(1-2{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+(1+\lambda_{\ell,0}){\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\right)}_{\lambda_{\ell,i+1}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i},

which directly implies that
\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i+1}=\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}
and completes the induction.
∎

Equation

22
shows that (non)zero eigenvalues remain (non)zero across all iterations. To understand the convergence behavior of eigenvalues of (
36
), we first prove some intermediate results.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all iterations
i\geq 1
, the positive eigenvalues of (
36
) are bounded above by
1
, i.e.,
\lambda_{\ell,i}\leq 1
for all
i\geq 1
, and are strictly monotone increasing, i.e.,
\lambda_{\ell,1}<\lambda_{\ell,2}<\cdots<\lambda_{\ell,i}<\cdots
, and therefore converge.

Proof.

Note that (
22
) may be equivalently expressed as:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=1-2(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}+(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}.

(40)

We first show the upper bound. Using (
40
), we may bound each eigenvalue as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}

\displaystyle\leq 1-2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=1-{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}\leq 1.

Next, we show monotonicity by showing that
\lambda_{\ell,i+1}-\lambda_{\ell,i}>0
. From (
40
), we write

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}-\lambda_{\ell,i}=2{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}-2{\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}-{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}.

Multiplying both sides of the above expression by
\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})
gives,

\displaystyle(\lambda_{\ell,i+1}-\lambda_{\ell,i})\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})

\displaystyle=2(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})-2+{\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}-(1+\lambda_{\ell,i}){\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}

\displaystyle={\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+\lambda_{\ell,i}-\lambda_{\ell,i}{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}+\lambda_{\ell,i}-{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,i}-\lambda_{\ell,i}\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}+\lambda_{\ell,i}-\lambda_{\ell,i}\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}>0.

Since
0<\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}<1
for all
i\geq 0
, it follows that
\lambda_{\ell,i+1}>\lambda_{\ell,i}
.
∎

We now prove
Proposition

3.2
, which characterizes the limit and convergence rate of the sequence (
22
).

Proof of
Proposition

3.3
.

Starting from (
40
), we see that we can express,

\displaystyle 1-\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}-{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}}\leq 2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}.

By the monotonicity of the eigenvalue sequence (
Lemma

5.2
), we may bound:

\displaystyle 2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,1})^{-i}}=2{(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\ell,0}}{1+\lambda_{\ell,0}}\right)^{-i}}

\displaystyle=2(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{i-1}{(1+2\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-i}}=\frac{2}{1+2\lambda_{\ell,0}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{\ell,0}}{1+2\lambda_{\ell,0}}\right)^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{1+2\lambda_{\ell,0}}e^{-(i-1)\gamma_{\ell}},

(41)

where
\gamma_{\ell}=\frac{\lambda_{\ell,0}}{1+2\lambda_{\ell,0}}
, and we made use of the fact that
\lambda_{\ell,1}=\frac{\lambda_{\ell,0}}{1+\lambda_{\ell,0}}
and the inequality
(1-x)^{\beta}\leq e^{-\beta x}
. Now, fix
\epsilon>0
. By choosing,

\displaystyle i\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\ell}}\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)+\log\left(\frac{2}{1+2\lambda_{\ell,0}}\right)\right]+1,

we guarantee that
1-\lambda_{\ell,i+1}<\epsilon
, which satisfies the definition of the limit of a sequence and establishes the promised result.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\{(\lambda_{\ell,1},\mathbf{w}_{\ell,1})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
denote the eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs of (
36
) at
i=1
, arranged in non-increasing order so that
\lambda_{1,1}\geq\lambda_{2,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n,1}
. This ordering is preserved for all
i>1
.

Proof.

Note that, by working with the expression for
\lambda_{\ell,i}
in (
22
), we can express
\lambda_{\ell,i+1}
as:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,i+1}=2\lambda_{\ell,i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})^{-1}-\lambda_{\ell,i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})^{-2}(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2},

and note that
0<(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}<1
. The claim follows by noticing that the map
2x(1+x)^{-1}-\alpha x(1+x)^{-2}
is monotone increasing for all
x\in[0,1]
and
0<\alpha<1
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{W}=[\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{r}]
denote the matrix of positive eigenvectors of (
36
), ordered as described in
Corollary

5.3
, noting that we have dropped the iteration index from
\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}
because the eigenvectors are constant for all iterations. We impose the normalization
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Then,
\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{W}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}
, with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}=\textsf{diag}(\lambda_{1,i},\ldots,\lambda_{r,i})
as before. Note that
\mathsf{ran}(\mathbf{W})
is an invariant subspace under the action of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
. We can define this subspace and its complement using spectral projectors of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
as follows:

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
are spectral projectors associated with the misfit map
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
. This means that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
.

Proof.

We begin by establishing that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}},

where we used the fact that, under our normalization,
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. The results
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}=\boldsymbol{0}
follow by definition. Next, we note that we can decompose
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
. Indeed:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\big)\big(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big[\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big]\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}+\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}.

Using the above decomposition, we can evaluate:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\big(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\big)=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}.

Successive applications of the above result give the claim
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
, with the analogous results for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
following directly.
∎

The subspace
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}})=\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W})
is associated with the eigenvectors of (
36
) that have nonzero eigenvalues. These directions are associated with directions that are observable under
\mathbf{H}
and populated by the ensemble in the sense that
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W})=\textsf{ran}(\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i})
. We will show that the data misfit converges in this observable and populated space, and that the misfit fails to converge in the complementary subspace
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}})
.

5.1.3
Misfit convergence analysis in observation space

Proposition

5.4
shows that the mean field misfit
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
(

) can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}.

(42)

In order to understand how
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
evolves as
i\to\infty
, we need to understand how the misfit evolves in each invariant subspace,
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}})
and
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}})
. We first present a lemma that shows how we can construct
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}
using eigenvectors and eigenvalues of (

).

Lemma 5.5
.

We have that:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,k}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}.

(43)

Proof.

Recall that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{W}\big(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,i}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}.

Equation

43
follows by using the above expression for each
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
combined with the properties of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}
and the
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-orthogonality of the eigenvectors.
∎

We are now ready to state our main result regarding the convergence of the mean field misfit.

Theorem 5.6
.

For all particles
j=1,\ldots,J
, the following hold for all
i\geq 0
:

1.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}
,

2.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\leq e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}
,

where
\gamma=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq r}\gamma_{\ell}
, and
\gamma_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell,0}/(1+2\lambda_{\ell,0})
.

Proof.

From (
42
), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)},

which follows from
Lemma

5.5
and gives the result in (a). To show (b), note that,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,k}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)},

where we applied
Lemma

5.5
, and used the
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-orthogonality of the eigenvectors. From
eq.

41
, it follows that
\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}\leq e^{-(i-1)\gamma_{\ell}}\leq e^{-(i-1)\gamma}
. Using this, we may bound:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)}=e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{(j)},

as desired, where we used the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

Theorem

5.6
establishes that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\to\boldsymbol{0}
as
i\to\infty
for all
j
. Moreover,
Theorem

5.6
directly implies the existence of a limit for
\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\equiv\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}
for each
j
. In order to characterize this limit, we need an intermediate result.

Lemma 5.7
.

We have that:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}},

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}.

(44)

Proof.

For the first result, we use
Lemma

5.5
to conclude:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\lim_{i\to\infty}\prod_{k=0}^{i}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,k}}\right)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\lim_{i\to\infty}e^{-(i-1)\gamma}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}},

where
\gamma
is defined as in
Theorem

5.6
. The second result follows by definition of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

We are now ready to state our main result.

Theorem 5.8
.

As
i\to\infty
, we have that for all
j=1,\ldots,J
:

1.

\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\infty}^{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{h}_{0}^{(j)}
,

2.

\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\infty}^{(j)}\big]=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
,

3.

\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]\to\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\infty}^{(j)}\big]=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}
.

Proof.

Statement (a) is a formal consequence of
Theorem

5.6
. For (b), we compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(j)}+\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\big]=\big(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\big)\mathbf{y}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{i:0}\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},

and apply
Lemma

5.7
. Finally, for (c), note that
\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}\big[\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\big]=\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}
and apply
Lemma

5.7
to
eq.

37
.
∎

5.2.1
Spectral analysis of mean field residual map

We begin by showing that the eigenvalues of (
19
) are the same as the eigenvalues of (
36
), and that the eigenvectors of (
19
) can be given in terms of the eigenvectors of (
36
).

Proof of
Proposition

3.2
.

Let
\{(\lambda_{\ell,i},\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i})\}_{\ell=1}^{n}
be eigenpairs of (
36
) ordered as described in
Corollary

5.3
. For
\ell\leq r
, let
\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}={\lambda_{\ell,i}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}
. Then, for all
\ell\leq r
,
\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}
is an eigenvector of (
19
) with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell,i}
. The relationship can be verified by substitution. We now show, via induction on
i
, that eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}
are constant across iterations. First, note that using
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\big(\mathbf{I}+\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1}
it is possible to show that
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}=(1+\lambda_{\ell,i})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}
. Then, from (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\ell,1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,1}=\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}+\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0})\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\lambda_{\ell,0}(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,0}+\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\big(1-(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{0})\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}.

Using the above, we may evaluate the base case:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big(\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\lambda_{\ell,0}(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,0}+\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\big(1-(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})\big)^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{0})\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,0}\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,1}^{-1}\big(\lambda_{\ell,0}^{2}(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-2}+\lambda_{\ell,0}\big(1-(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\big)-\lambda_{\ell,0}(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})^{-1}\big(1-(1+\lambda_{\ell,0})\big)^{-1}\big)\mathbf{u}_{\ell,0}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,0}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,0}.

The base case is confirmed. Now, assume that
\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}=\cdots=\mathbf{u}_{\ell,0}
. The inductive hypothesis can be used to show that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i+1}=\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i+1}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{0}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}+\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\left(1-\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}\right)(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0})\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\lambda_{\ell,0}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}\right)+\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\left(1-\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-1}\right)(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0})\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{w}_{\ell,i},

which in turn can be used to show that:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\lambda_{\ell,i}\lambda_{\ell,0}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})^{-2}\right)+\lambda_{\ell,i+1}^{-1}\lambda_{\ell,i}\left(1-\prod_{k=0}^{i}(1+\lambda_{\ell,k})\right)^{2}\right)\mathbf{u}_{\ell,i}.

This completes the induction and shows that eigenvectors of (
19
) are constant across all iterations.

∎

Proposition

3.4
uses the eigenvectors of (
19
) to define complementary spectral projectors
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{S}
of state space, similar to
Proposition

5.4
in observation space.

Proof of
Proposition

3.4
.

We begin by verifying:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{I}_{r}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{P}.

The claim
\mathbf{S}^{2}=\mathbf{S}
follows by definition of
\mathbf{S}
and by
\mathbf{P}^{2}=\mathbf{P}
. Using the definitions, we can also verify
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Note that we can express
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\mathbf{U}\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{I}+\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)\big(\mathbf{U}\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\big[\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}\big]\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}.

Using the above, we may verify that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{U}\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{U}\big(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{1:r}^{(i)}+\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S}\big)\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{P}.

Successive application of the above result gives
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{P}
and the results for
\mathbf{S}
follow by definition.
∎

Residual convergence analysis in state space

Theorem

3.5
states that the mean field residual
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i+1}^{(j)}
converges to zero in
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}})
and remains constant in
\textsf{ran}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}})
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.5
.

Statement (a) follows the same logic as in the proof of
Theorem

5.6
. To arrive at (b), we note that:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,i}}\right)\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S},

which follows by noticing that,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,i}}\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{S},

and by applying the weighted normalization of the eigenvectors. Using the above, we express

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{i}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{\ell,k}}\right)\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{0}^{(j)},

and we follow the same steps as in the proof of
Theorem

5.6
.

∎

Theorem

3.6
uses the results of
Theorem

3.5
to establish a limit for each mean field particle, and subsequent limits for the ensemble mean and covariance.

Proof of
Theorem

3.6
.

Theorem

3.5
shows that
\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{i+1}^{(j)}\to\boldsymbol{0}
as
i\to\infty
; this gives the result presented in (a). A direct analog of
Lemma

5.7
shows that:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\mathbf{S},

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i:0}=\mathbf{P}.

For (b), take the expectation (with respect to the particles and the perturbations) of (
20
) and apply the above limits. Finally, for (c), we use (
21
) and apply the above limits.
∎

The last result of this analysis,
Corollary

3.7
, shows convergence in distribution.

Proof of
Corollary

3.7
.

First, note that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{\infty}^{(j)}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^{+}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^{+}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{\star}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(j)},

where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{I})
. By definition, the above is a multivariate normal random vector with mean
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{\star}
and covariance
\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\big(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{\top}=\mathbf{P}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
.
∎

7
Conclusion

This work presents ensemble Kalman randomized maximum likelihood estimation (RMLE), a new derivative-free algorithm for randomized maximum likelihood estimation. The new method, which has connections to ensemble Kalman inversion, evolves an ensemble so that each ensemble member solves a randomized maximum likelihood estimation optimization problem. Linear analysis of the new method investigates convergence in two fundamental invariant subspaces for each the observation and state spaces. Theoretical results demonstrate that ensemble members converge exponentially fast to RMLE samples in the subspace that is observable and populated, and, when the method is applied to solve a suitably regularized optimization problem, ensemble members converge to samples from the Bayesian posterior.
The work also considers the problem of inferring the initial condition of a high-dimensional linear dynamical system, and shows how a balanced truncation model reduction approach can be used to accelerate the proposed method in this setting with negligible loss in accuracy.

Numerical experiments on a randomly generated inverse problem verify linear theory, by illustrating convergence to the proposed infinite iteration ensemble mean and covariance limits. We also perform numerical experiments on a benchmark problem from the model reduction community that demonstrate convergence to the linear Bayesian posterior and reduced models that achieve accuracy close to the full model with an order-of-magnitude reduction in model size.

Although this work is mostly concerned with the linear case, we note that our method is also well-defined for nonlinear inverse problems. Understanding the algorithm’s convergence in nonlinear and non-Gaussian problems is therefore a subject of study for future work. Other directions for future research include comparisons with other ensemble Kalman algorithms that sample from the Bayesian posterior, and development of methods for model reduction in nonlinear inverse problems.
