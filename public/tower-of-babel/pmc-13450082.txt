Twelve‑month Response and Safety of Intravitreal Dexamethasone Implant in Treatment‑Naïve and Recalcitrant Cystoid Macular Edema Secondary to Branch Retinal Vein Occlusion (TREAT-BRVO Study)
Authors: Somnath Chakraborty, Jay U Sheth, Santanu Ganguly, Preesha Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare anatomical and visual outcomes following dexamethasone intravitreal implant (Ozurdex) in treatment-naive versus recalcitrant cystoid macular edema (CME) secondary to branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO).Patients and MethodsThis retrospective study included 45 eyes with BRVO-related CME treated with Ozurdex and followed for 12 months. Eyes were classified as treatment-naive (n=13) if they received no prior treatment, including intravitreal therapy or laser photocoagulation. Eyes were classified as recalcitrant (n=32) if previously treated with at least three anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents. Primary outcomes were best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in logMAR and central macular thickness (CMT). Secondary outcomes included changes intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal fluid (SRF), hyperreflective intraretinal foci (HRIF), disorganization of retinal inner layers (DRIL), lens status, and retreatment rates.ResultsBaseline BCVA and CMT were comparable between groups. A mean of 1.74±0.81 dexamethasone implants was administered, with no difference between groups. BCVA improved significantly from 0.625±0.262 to 0.299±0.14 logMAR at 12 months (P<0.001), with similar gains and no between-group differences (month 12 P=0.492). CMT decreased from 522.6±141.3 µm to 266.9±66.6 µm (P<0.001); despite mild recurrence, reductions remained significant versus baseline throughout. The intraocular pressure (IOP) remained stable, with one medically managed elevation. The optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging showed marked resolution of IRF (84.4%), SRF (95.2%), and HRIF (100%). Retreatment, including repeat implants and/or laser, was frequent (88.9%). Cataract progression occurred in 12.1% of phakic eyes.ConclusionIntravitreal dexamethasone implant was associated with significant anatomical and functional improvements in both treatment-naïve and anti-VEGF refractory BRVO-related CME. While no statistically significant differences were identified between the groups, adequately powered prospective studies are required to determine whether treatment outcomes differ according to prior anti-VEGF exposure.

Introduction

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) represents the second most common retinal vascular disorder after diabetic retinopathy (DR), with an estimated prevalence of 0.6% in individuals over 40 years.1 The visual morbidity in BRVO stems primarily from cystoid macular edema (CME), which develops in approximately 60% of cases and leads to progressive central vision loss if left untreated.2 The pathophysiology involves venous stasis, capillary hyperperfusion, and breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier, culminating in fluid accumulation in the affected region.3

Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents have emerged as first-line therapy for BRVO-related CME, supported by robust evidence from the BRAVO, VIBRANT, and SCORE2 trials.4–6 These agents effectively target the upregulated VEGF that drives vascular permeability and edema formation. However, a significant proportion of eyes demonstrate incomplete response or require frequent injections to maintain anatomical improvement.7,8 The burden of monthly or bimonthly injections, coupled with variable treatment response, has generated interest in alternative therapeutic approaches.

Corticosteroids address BRVO-related CME through multiple complementary mechanisms, including stabilization of the blood-retinal barrier, downregulation of inflammatory mediators, and inhibition of VEGF expression.9,10 The dexamethasone intravitreal implant (Ozurdex; Allergan, Inc) delivers sustained-release 0.7 mg dexamethasone over approximately 6 months.11 The GENEVA study established the efficacy of dexamethasone implant in retinal vein occlusion (RVO), demonstrating superior visual outcomes compared with observation.12 Subsequent real-world studies have confirmed these findings, though most focused on mixed RVO populations without separately analyzing BRVO-specific outcomes.13,14

A critical gap exists in our understanding of how dexamethasone implant performs in different BRVO patient subgroups. Treatment-naive eyes represent a distinct population with relatively early disease, less cumulative retinal injury, and preserved responsiveness to anti-VEGF therapy. In contrast, eyes with persistent or recurrent CME despite prior anti-VEGF therapy (recalcitrant or refractory BRVO-CME) often represent more chronic disease. Prolonged edema, repeated treatment exposure, and associated macular ischemia may result in irreversible structural retinal alterations, including photoreceptor and retinal pigment epithelium damage, which can adversely affect both anatomical and functional recovery.15 Furthermore, the pathophysiology in these eyes may shift from predominantly VEGF-mediated vascular permeability to a more complex inflammatory milieu, providing a biological rationale for corticosteroid therapy. Whether treatment-naive and recalcitrant eyes respond similarly to dexamethasone implant, however, remains unclear.

This distinction has practical implications for treatment algorithms. If recalcitrant eyes show reduced response to dexamethasone implant, earlier use may be justified before anti-VEGF resistance develops. Conversely, if responses are similar, the implant could serve as an effective second-line option regardless of prior therapy. Understanding retreatment patterns and durability also helps set patient expectations and plan follow-up.

This study was designed to compare anatomical and functional outcomes of dexamethasone implant in treatment-naive versus recalcitrant BRVO-related CME. Secondary objectives included evaluating retreatment needs, safety, and qualitative changes in retinal fluid over 12 months.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study included consecutive patients with BRVO-related CME who received dexamethasone intravitreal implant between January 2022-December 2025 at Retina Institute of Bengal, Siliguri, India. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained (Retina Institute of Bengal Ethics Committee, Siliguri, India; RIB/IRB/0007/2025), and all procedures conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for treatment anonymised data use was obtained from all patients. All eyes with BRVO and presence of center-involving CME documented on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT); with a minimum 12-month follow-up after initial dexamethasone implant were included. Exclusion criteria included: (1) concurrent retinal pathology; (2) prior vitreoretinal surgery; (3) glaucoma; and (4) previous intravitreal corticosteroid within 6 months.

Eyes were classified into two groups: treatment-naive if they received no prior including intravitreal therapy or laser photocoagulation for BRVO, and recalcitrant if they had received at least three anti-VEGF injection with persistent or recurrent CME on SD-OCT, with or without incomplete visual improvement, prompting a switch to dexamethasone implant. The choice and number of prior anti-VEGF injections were at the discretion of the treating physician according to routine clinical practice. All patients underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examination including BCVA measurement using Snellen charts (converted to logMAR for analysis), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement by Goldmann applanation tonometry, and dilated fundus examination. SD-OCT imaging (Heidelberg Spectralis) was performed at baseline and all follow-up visits. Central macular thickness (CMT) was measured using automated segmentation with manual correction when necessary. Presence of intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal fluid (SRF), hyperreflective intraretinal foci (HRIF), and disorganization of inner retinal layers (DRIL) was recorded qualitatively. All OCT images were evaluated by an experienced retinal specialist.

The 0.7 mg dexamethasone implant was administered intravitreally using standard sterile technique. Patients were examined at 1 week, and months 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12. Retreatment was considered in eyes demonstrating persistent or recurrent CME on SD-OCT associated with reduced or suboptimal visual improvement after the initial implant. The choice of repeat dexamethasone implant or supplemental anti-VEGF therapy was individualized based on the anatomical response, visual status, lens status, intraocular pressure, and treating physician’s clinical judgment. Focal laser photocoagulation was considered in eyes with persistent non-center-threatening leakage or chronic edema despite pharmacotherapy, when deemed appropriate by the treating physician based on fluorescein angiography and clinical findings. Development of cataract was noted in phakic eyes.

Primary outcome measures were change in BCVA and CMT from baseline to each follow-up visit. Secondary outcomes included proportion of eyes with IRF, SRF, HRIF, and DRIL at each timepoint; retreatment frequency; and adverse events. Statistical analysis employed paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for continuous variables depending on normality. McNemar’s test assessed paired categorical outcomes. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Discussion

This study examined the role of Ozurdex in CME secondary to BRVO, with a particular focus on comparing treatment-naive eyes with those deemed recalcitrant after prior intravitreal therapy, including anti-VEGF therapy or steroids. Several clinically relevant observations emerge. First, a single dexamethasone implant produced substantial and statistically significant improvements in BCVA and CMT that were maintained, albeit with some fluctuation, over 12 months in the overall cohort. Second, the magnitude and time course of visual and anatomic responses were notably similar in naive and recalcitrant eyes, suggesting that previous therapy with anti-VEGF or steroids did not blunt the therapeutic effect of Ozurdex in this BRVO population. Third, OCT biomarkers of edema and inflammation, IRF, SRF, and HRIF, showed high rates of resolution, again without any obvious blunting of effect in recalcitrant eyes. Finally, retreatment was common in both subgroups, and the safety profile was consistent with known steroid-related risks, particularly cataract progression. This study also builds on our group’s ongoing effort to generate real-world evidence on dexamethasone implant across chorioretinal diseases; while our prior TREAT-DME cohort demonstrated comparable outcomes in treatment-naïve and recalcitrant eyes with a similar pattern of early response, retreatment requirement, and safety profile,16 the present analysis specifically extends this framework to eyes with BRVO-associated CME.

The GENEVA trials established dexamethasone implant as an effective option for macular edema due to RVO, demonstrating improved BCVA and reduced retinal thickness compared with sham, with a peak effect at around 60 days and waning efficacy thereafter.12,17 Our findings in BRVO eyes align with this pharmacodynamic pattern: CMT reached its lowest levels at months 1–2, with partial rebound by month 3 and 6, though remaining significantly below baseline at all measured timepoints. The BCVA trajectory similarly showed early gains that were largely sustained, with mean vision improving from approximately 0.63 to 0.30 logMAR at 12 months. These outcomes are broadly consistent with the magnitude of improvement seen in prior RVO cohorts treated with Ozurdex, taking into account differences in baseline characteristics and study design.12,17,18

Head-to-head data from COMRADE-B and related studies indicate that ranibizumab pro-re-nata (PRN) achieves greater mean letter gains than a single dexamethasone implant over six months in BRVO, at the cost of a higher injection frequency and different safety trade-offs.19 However, those trials did not explicitly separate treatment-naive and previously treated eyes, nor were they designed to address the question of steroid rescue in recalcitrant CME. In contrast, observational series and retrospective cohorts have evaluated Ozurdex as second-line therapy in RVO eyes unresponsive to anti-VEGF, showing meaningful structural and functional improvements.20,21 Our data support and extend this literature by showing that, in BRVO-related CME, recalcitrant eyes, defined pragmatically as those with persistent or recurrent edema after prior injections, can achieve visual and anatomic outcomes comparable to those seen in treatment-naive eyes when treated with Ozurdex.

Systematic reviews have further highlighted that dexamethasone implant is effective across RVO subtypes and that its benefits are accompanied by a higher incidence of IOP elevation and cataract formation compared with anti-VEGF monotherapy.18,21,22 The present study mirrors this balance. We observed robust reductions in CMT and high rates of resolution of IRF, SRF, and HRIF, but also a cumulative new cataract or cataract progression rate of about 12% among baseline phakic eyes at 12 months. This cataract risk is similar in magnitude to that reported in prior RVO dexamethasone series, particularly with repeated implants, and underscores the importance of careful lens status monitoring and informed consent.17–20

The most distinctive feature of this study is the direct, side-by-side comparison of Ozurdex outcomes in treatment-naive and recalcitrant BRVO eyes within a single protocol and imaging framework. In daily retina practice, the decision to introduce a steroid implant often arises in complex, previously treated eyes that have failed to achieve satisfactory control with anti-VEGF monotherapy.20–22 Yet clinicians frequently worry that such “recalcitrant” eyes might have a lower ceiling for recovery due to underlying ischemia, chronic edema-related damage, or tachyphylaxis. The present data offer a reassuring message: on average, recalcitrant eyes experienced similar improvements in BCVA and CMT as naive eyes, and the time course of response was broadly comparable.

From a structural standpoint, IRF, SRF, and HRIF, all markers of active exudation and inflammatory or lipid-laden deposits, showed high resolution rates overall and in both subgroups. IRF resolved in 84.4% of baseline-positive eyes by month 12; SRF resolved in 95.2% of eyes with baseline SRF; and HRIF resolved completely in eyes where it was present at baseline. These observations reinforce the notion that the anti-permeability and anti-inflammatory effects of dexamethasone can meaningfully modulate both fluid compartments and intraretinal deposits in BRVO, regardless of prior anti-VEGF exposure.18,20

Another strength is the detailed follow-up at week 1 and at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months. This design captures the early, mid-phase, and late behavior of the implant more efficiently than many routine clinical series. The early improvement in BCVA and CMT by week 1 and month 1 in our cohort is in line with prior reports that dexamethasone can reduce edema rapidly, often within the first week.17,21 The partial recurrence of CMT around month 3–6, despite sustained BCVA gains in many eyes, is also consistent with the known pharmacokinetics of the implant and with trial data suggesting retreatment intervals of about 4–6 months in responsive eyes.17,18,20

Finally, we incorporated real-world retreatment patterns including repeat PRN injections and various laser modalities, rather than restricting management to a rigid protocol. Over 12 months, nearly 89% of eyes underwent some form of additional treatment, and about two-thirds required both further injections and laser. Importantly, retreatment rates did not differ significantly between naive and recalcitrant eyes, underscoring that both groups, once on a dexamethasone-based pathway, carried a similar long-term treatment burden.

For the practicing retina specialist, several practical messages emerge. First, in BRVO-related CME, a single Ozurdex implant can produce meaningful and sustained improvements in vision and anatomy over a year, even when a high proportion of eyes have already failed prior intravitreal therapy. This supports considering dexamethasone implant not only as a second-line rescue agent but also as a viable early-line option in selected patients for whom frequent anti-VEGF injections are impractical or less desirable; for example, elderly patients with systemic comorbidities, patients with poor clinic access, or eyes demonstrating a clear inflammatory component.18–20

Second, the absence of significant differences in BCVA and CMT between naive and recalcitrant eyes suggests that prior anti-VEGF exposure per se does not negate the potential benefits of Ozurdex in BRVO CME, at least within the limits of this cohort. Instead, response appears driven more by the underlying disease biology and structural integrity than by treatment history alone. This finding reinforces current practice patterns of switching to dexamethasone in anti-VEGF non-responders, with reasonable expectations of anatomical and functional gains.18,20,22

Third, the robust resolution of IRF and SRF and the disappearance of HRIF in most affected eyes provide qualitative support for the use of Ozurdex in BRVO cases with pronounced intraretinal and subretinal exudation, especially when these biomarkers persist despite anti-VEGF therapy. Although DRIL changes were less clear in this dataset, likely due to small numbers and the complexity of grading, the overall structural normalization seen on OCT supports the use of dexamethasone in eyes where chronic edema has led to multilayer disruption.

Fourth, the retreatment pattern in this study highlights that CME secondary BRVO may not respond to a single dose of Ozurdex. A high proportion of these eyes may require additional injections and/or laser over 12 months, reflecting the chronic, relapsing nature of the disease. The implant can, however, reduce overall visit and injection burden compared with monthly anti-VEGF regimens in some patients, particularly when used thoughtfully as part of a mixed strategy that may include adjunctive laser and intermittent anti-VEGF.20–22

Finally, safety remains a central consideration. The observed cataract progression rate in phakic eyes is in line with known steroid risks and emphasizes the need to counsel patients about the likelihood of cataract surgery over time, especially with repeated implants.20,22 IOP elevations were generally manageable with topical drops in our cohort, again consistent with the literature.17–22 In glaucomatous eyes or those with advanced optic nerve damage, careful risk–benefit assessment and close IOP monitoring are essential, and in some cases, Ozurdex may be best reserved for short-term rescue rather than repeated use.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. The modest sample size, particularly in the treatment-naïve subgroup, limited the statistical power for between-group comparisons and the ability to detect subtle differences in anatomical and functional outcomes, including less frequent OCT biomarkers such as DRIL. In addition, conventional statistical methods were used for longitudinal analyses; larger prospective studies employing repeated-measures or mixed-effects models may provide a more robust assessment of treatment effects over time. There was no contemporaneous anti-VEGF-only control group; therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as a direct comparison between dexamethasone implant and anti-VEGF therapy for BRVO-related CME. Furthermore, treatment histories within the recalcitrant group were heterogeneous, including different anti-VEGF agents and variable prior injection burden, while baseline macular ischemia and disease chronicity were not systematically stratified. Nevertheless, the study reflects real-world clinical practice and is strengthened by standardized follow-up, detailed longitudinal OCT evaluation, and a focused analysis of treatment-naïve and recalcitrant BRVO-related CME.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dexamethasone intravitreal implant was associated with significant anatomical and functional improvement in both treatment-naïve and anti-VEGF–recalcitrant BRVO-related CME. Similar outcomes were observed in both groups, although the study was not powered to establish comparable efficacy. High retreatment rates in both cohorts underscore the chronic nature of BRVO-related CME and the need for sustained therapeutic intervention. Overall, these findings support dexamethasone implant as an effective therapeutic option across the spectrum of BRVO-related CME, with appropriate patient selection and monitoring.
