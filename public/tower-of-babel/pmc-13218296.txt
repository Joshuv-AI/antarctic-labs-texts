Microjoining for biomedical device fabrication: a perspective
Authors: Kaiping Zhang, Y. Norman Zhou, Peng Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microjoining plays a pivotal role in the fabrication of advanced biomedical devices. The ongoing drive toward miniaturization, multifunctional integration, and high reliability in medical implants and interventional systems further necessitates precise and robust joining of diverse materials via various joining methods at the microscale. This perspective provides a concise overview of recent advances in microjoining technologies for biomedical device applications. Through representative case studies on materials for joining, joining methods, and resultant joint properties, we elucidate the fundamental concepts and governing mechanisms of microjoining in the context of biomedical device fabrication. By providing insights from both experimental investigations and mechanistic analyses, key issues such as material weldability, metallurgy in the fusion zone, processing physics, and functional performance of micro-joints are discussed. Finally, future opportunities and challenges in this field of research are outlined, highlighting key bottlenecks and potential directions for the development of microjoining in biomedical device fabrication.

Introduction

With the rapid evolution of healthcare technologies and the expanding global market for medical devices [1], the fabrication of advanced biomedical systems increasingly demands the integration of multiple functional components at the microscale [2, 3]. For medical implants and interventional devices, a primary design consideration is the minimization of surgical trauma and postoperative complications [4]. Achieving these goals requires devices that are not only miniaturized but also mechanically reliable and functionally controllable. In this context, microjoining, namely the joining of small-scale components such as thin sheets (thickness up to 0.5 mm) and fine wires (diameter ≤ 1 mm), has emerged as a focal area of research in biomedical device manufacturing [5]. Representative examples illustrate the complexity and precision required for such integrations [6–8]. A multifunctional continuum robot designed for minimally invasive surgery, for instance, comprises a joined assembly of low-melting-point alloys, silicone tubes, miniature magnets, heating wires (D = 0.1 mm), Ag wires (D = 0.05 mm), enamel-coated wires (D = 0.1 mm), and polymer tubes (OD = 0.6 mm) [9]. Similarly, smart stent systems have been fabricated using laser microwelded polymer-metal hybrid architectures [10].

Concepts of different joining processes are broadly defined by material dimensions: thin sheets and fine wires for microjoining (typically < 1 mm), nanomaterials for nanojoining (< 100 nm), and larger components for macrojoining. The applicable specific joining techniques therefore may vary to accommodate the size scale. For example, arcs used in macrojoining are typically confined into plasma-based processes for microjoining (Table 1) [11]. The fundamental mechanisms governing macrojoining and microjoining share many similarities, while they diverge significantly when extended to the nanoscale [12]. In both macro- and micro-laser lap welding, for example, heat is generated at the surface of the top workpiece and subsequently conducted into the underlying workpiece to achieve bonding. In contrast, laser nanojoining can generate heat directly at the interface of the lapped nanomaterials by the localized surface plasmon resonance [13]. Even so, when joining materials at the microscale, both the processing dynamics and the resultant joint properties still exhibit many different behaviors compared with those observed at conventional and large scales, particularly when evaluated within specific application contexts. For instance, phenomena such as capillarity-dominated flow, rapid solidification, interfacial diffusion and size-dependent heat transfer play increasingly important roles in microjoining. As the characteristic dimension decreases during the joining formation, surface and interfacial effects become progressively more dominant. The thermal transfer and solidification kinetics are strongly influenced by the limited material volume and high surface-to-volume ratios. Accordingly, the thermal stress and strain of micro-joints will also differ from those in conventional macro-joints. It is therefore both necessary and instructive to discuss microjoining as a separate and specialized topic. In this perspective, we focus on the emerging development of microjoining techniques for advanced biomedical device fabrication. We briefly summarize the current progress on materials, joining methods, and characterizations of joint properties, highlighting how microjoining governs the reliability and functionality of biomedical device systems composed of various micro-joints. We further highlight recent advances in microjoining, delineating the key challenges that constrain current capabilities and the opportunities that could shape future directions in this field. This perspective aims to inform and inspire the broader research community by not only summarizing recent progress but also offering new insights into the welding and joining of materials at the microscale. By highlighting key challenges and emerging opportunities, this work seeks to advance the understanding of microjoining and to support the development of reliable joining strategies for biomedical device fabrication.

Joining materials

Microjoining processes are involved in nearly every component of integrated biomedical devices, underscoring the diversity and universality of materials used for such integrations [5, 14]. Broadly, the materials employed in microjoining can be classified into three groups: (1) metals, (2) polymers and composites, and (3) ceramics, with metals being the most prevalent. Conventional metallic materials such as stainless steel (SS) [15, 16], titanium (Ti) [17, 18], and magnesium (Mg) [19] alloys have long been utilized in biomedical engineering owing to their favorable mechanical performance, biocompatibility, and cost-effectiveness. The use of SS for surgical implants dates back to the 1920s [20], and the demand for joining these metallic components has grown substantially with the advent of increasingly complex and miniaturized devices designed for high-precision surgeries and interventions. For example, in a pressure-sensor-integrated cardiological stent, laser micro-welding is employed to join the SS die of the pressure sensor to the SS antenna (Fig. 1a) [21]. Shape-memory alloys (SMAs), particularly nickel-titanium (NiTi) alloys, represent another critical class of biomedical metals [22, 23]. Driven by their reversible martensitic phase transformations, SMAs exhibit unique functional properties, namely the shape memory effect (SME) and superelasticity (SE) [24], which render them indispensable in medical applications such as shape-memory NiTi archwires with outstanding capability for orthodontic correction [25, 26]. Accordingly, reliable joining of SMAs, either to themselves or to dissimilar materials at the microscale, is of great importance. For instance, fusion welding between NiTi microtubes and PtIr wires has been demonstrated to tailor the transformation temperature range for device-specific needs (Fig. 1b) [27]. Beyond these widely used systems, other metallic materials are selectively employed to meet specialized biomedical requirements. Biodegradable Mg-based and Zn-based alloys [28], noble metals including Au, Ag and Pt [29], and refractory alloys like Nb and Zr [30] are all utilized in the fabrication of advanced devices. Owing to their excellent deformability, corrosion resistance, and machinability, polymers and polymer-based composites offer distinct advantages for integration into multifunctional biomedical devices [37]. For instance, when a biomaterial transitions from an osseointegration zone to a structural zone in bone implants, polymers are often introduced to replicate the compliant motion of natural bone, providing mechanical flexibility superior to that of single metallic components [34]. Three-dimensional polymer fabrics have been successfully microjoined via laser welding to enhance the wear resistance of the resulting woven structures used in orthopedic devices [34]. Proper control of the laser heat input enables reliable fiber-to-fiber bonding without inducing surface damage or incomplete joining (Fig. 1c). Polymers can also be effectively microjoined to dissimilar materials, as demonstrated in polyimide-Ti [38] and polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG)-SS [39] systems. Another representative example highlights the necessity of microjoining a metal-coated chip to the inner surface of a polymer stent to achieve structural integration and functional performance of the device [40]. In the tip side of a commercially available product FARAWAVE™ pulsed field ablation catheter [41], the reliable joining between metal-ring electrodes and polymer wires are required to achieve the flexible motion or deformation of the catheter meanwhile acquire the X-ray mapping and signal feedback of the device. Increasingly complex multifunctional systems, exemplified by the variable stiffness magnetic catheter including guidewire composed of shape-memory polymers, Teflon and silicone, as well as external metallic magnets (Fig. 1d) [35], commonly require reliable microjoining between dissimilar polymers and metals. Some of the polymer materials, such as perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), are about to be replaced by environmentally sustainable polymers [42], which may also challenge the future microjoining of these new materials.

Ceramics, in bioengineering typically categorized as oxide- and nitride-based bioinert ceramics, bioresorbable calcium phosphate materials, and bioactive glasses or glass–ceramics [43], are extensively employed in biomedical replacement applications, particularly for bone and dental restoration. However, reports on the miniaturization and microjoining of bio-ceramics remain scarce, likely due to their inherently low machinability and poor weldability. The high melting points and limited plastic deformability of ceramic materials significantly restrict the feasibility of both fusion- and solid-state-based joining techniques, particularly at the microscale. Consequently, soldering/brazing and adhesive bonding are more commonly employed for the microjoining of ceramics. Emerging prototypes of advanced biomedical devices have demonstrated the feasibility of such approaches. For instance, the implanted neural stimulator exemplifies the micro-brazing of PtIr pins into ceramic substrates [44], highlighting the potential of ceramic-based microjoining in next-generation implantable systems. Another case study (Fig. 1e) illustrates the design and fabrication of an implantable structure [36], in which metallic components are micro-brazed to the ceramic substrate, and the ceramic components are co-fired with Pt tracks (a process that can also be regarded as a form of micro-brazing). To further expand the use of ceramics in microjoining applications, their inherently low machinability and poor weldability should be addressed through the improvement or modification of microjoining techniques.

Different classes of materials used in microjoining present distinct joining challenges. While relatively good weldability is typically achieved in similar-metal systems (for example, SS-SS joints) [37], the joining process can nonetheless alter the intrinsic properties of the base materials. In SMAs, for instance, excessive heat input may lead to grain coarsening in the weld zone of the joint, thereby degrading SME and SE [45]. Mg- and Zn-based biodegradable alloys and polymers possess low melting and boiling points, making them susceptible to vaporization-induced defects during fusion-based joining [46]. In contrast, the microjoining of refractory metals with high melting points requires substantially higher heat input, which can damage adjacent structures within the device assembly. The high optical reflectivity of noble metals (especially to typical welding laser wavelengths) poses another challenge, limiting the feasibility of laser-based microjoining for these materials [47]. Dissimilar material microjoining remains particularly problematic owing to significant mismatches in thermophysical and metallurgical properties of base materials, often resulting in poor reliability. The formation of brittle intermetallic compounds (IMCs) is a major concern in dissimilar metal micro-joints, decreasing both strength and ductility [48, 49]. When metals are microjoined to polymers or ceramics, additional issues arise, including the increase of high residual stress and reduced strength in brazed seam, both of which can undermine long-term mechanical and functional stability.

Joining methods

From joining mechanisms, microjoining retains many of the fundamental principles of macroscale welding and joining, but the fabrication of microscale assemblies often necessitates the miniaturization of tools, electrodes, and heat sources to accommodate fine wires and thin sheets. In general, welding and joining processes can be categorized into four main types: fusion welding, solid-state welding, brazing and soldering, and adhesive bonding [11]. Table 1 summarizes the principal joining methods suitable for these different joining regimes [11].

Fusion welding, the most widely used category, involves the melting and re-solidification of base materials to form a bond [50]. Non-contact energy sources such as laser beams, electron beams, and plasma arcs, as well as contact-type resistance welding, are commonly employed for the fusion joining at the microscale. Among these, laser micro-welding has become one of the most prevalent techniques due to its high energy density, precise controllability, flexibility, and ability to confine heat input to a narrow weld zone [51, 52]. Fusion micro-welding is typically performed in spot-welding mode, and various process innovations, such as wire rotation or beam offsetting [25, 53], have been developed to improve the joining quality between fine wires or thin foils; these process innovations are rarely necessary or applicable at macroscale welding. The important feature of fusion welding is the mixing of molten base materials, which promotes robust metallurgical bonding but can also introduce challenges associated with melting and re-solidification, particularly in dissimilar-material joints. For example, in laser micro-welded NiTi-SS wire joints (often used as fundamental building blocks for multifunctional archwires, stents, and guidewires [54, 55]), the rapid heating and cooling inherent to the process lead to uneven mixing and non-equilibrium solidification. These conditions can give rise to the formation of a complex, brittle, IMCs-filled fusion zone (FZ) in the joint (Fig. 2a) [56]. To address such limitations, resistance micro-welding has been explored as an alternative technique. The process employs specially designed miniature electrodes (for instance, flat CuCr tips with diameters of approximately 1 mm) to join wires as thin as 400 µm [57]. The thermomechanical processing of resistance welding demonstrates unique controlling capacity for wires microjoining, successfully squeezing the molten liquid out of the interface and realizing the quasi-solid-state direct bonding between NiTi and SS (Fig. 2b). Notably, this “squeezing-out” event may not be observable in large-scale welding, because the increased mobility of the molten FZ typically prevents such behavior or electrode force is not too large. Solid-state welding achieves joining through the interdiffusion of materials across contacting surfaces under applied pressure, without the melting of base materials [63]. This category includes processes such as friction-based welding, ultrasonic welding, and diffusion bonding. To adapt these techniques to the microscale, specialized miniature tooling is required, exemplified by the development of pin-tip tools for micro-friction stir welding (Fig. 2d) [58]. In contrast to fusion welding, solid-state approaches offer distinct advantages, particularly in mitigating the challenges of excessive melting, element vaporization, and brittle IMCs formation commonly encountered in both similar and dissimilar material fusion welded joints. For example, ultrasonic micro-welding of thin Ti sheets can produce highly confined interfacial regions of less than 5 µm in thickness (Fig. 2e) [59]. Similarly, vapor foil actuator welding (VFAW) has been applied to join NiTi wires to brass sheets in medical device prototypes (Fig. 2f) [60], producing solid-state interface without significant alloy intermixing (Fig. 2g) and thereby suppressing IMCs formation. Nevertheless, the localized severe plastic deformation inherent to solid-state welding can induce phase transformation and degradation of base material properties [64], particularly in soft polymers or functional materials such as SMAs, where mechanical or functional performance is sensitive to strain-induced microstructural changes.

Low-melting-point fillers are essential for brazing, soldering, and adhesive bonding, during which the fillers are melted (or in liquid state) to form the bonding while the base materials remain solid [65, 66]. These techniques offer valuable alternatives for microjoining complex configurations or dissimilar materials that exhibit large property mismatches, particularly in cases where welding heat sources or mechanical tooling are impractical or detrimental to the device. Furnace micro-brazing of SMA wires has been utilized to fabricate medical stents featuring intricate open-cell honeycomb architectures (Fig. 2h) [61]. This approach allows the simultaneous joining of multiple seams within a single heating process, and the resulting robust micro-brazed joints effectively preserve the superelastic response of the stent. In contrast, micro-brazing of dissimilar materials using typical heat sources, such as furnace, microwave, or infrared radiation, is rarely reported, likely due to the huge thermophysical mismatches between microscale base materials, which make them highly sensitive to global heating. To overcome this limitation, laser micro-brazing has emerged as a promising localized heating approach. For example, a Pt ring can be laser micro-brazed to a Cu-coated SS wire (Fig. 2i) [49], forming a part of interventional biomedical devices where Pt provides X-ray visibility and the SS wire ensures electrical signal feedback. This structure demands both material integrity and property preservation after joining; these requirements are successfully achieved using laser micro-brazing by a low-melting-point interfacial Cu as the filler. Adhesive bonding is predominantly employed for materials with low melting points, such as polymers and plastics. In microfluidic devices used for bioanalysis and diagnostics, adhesive bonding plays an indispensable role in assembling thermoplastic substrates with microscale slots (Fig. 2j) [62]. It should be emphasized that the surface condition of base materials is a critical factor governing the reliability of these filler-based joining methods. Accordingly, pre-joining surface treatments, such as plasma cleaning, are often performed to enhance wetting, adhesion, and overall micro-joint quality [67, 68].

The selection of microjoining methods should be guided by several factors governing the process, including the weldability of base materials, the required joint properties, the production efficiency, etc. Although each case requires specific evaluation, the weldability of the base materials is typically considered first when determining a suitable microjoining approach: (1) metals with favorable metallurgical characteristics and sufficient deformability can generally be joined by all four types of methods; (2) polymers are commonly joined using fusion welding, brazing/soldering, or adhesive bonding; and (3) ceramics are primarily joined by brazing/soldering owing to their low deformability and high melting points.

Joint properties

Basic mechanical responses (especially joining strength) and failure modes for micro-joints still need to be evaluated in biomedical device applications. Moreover, the distinct characteristics of microjoining, such as the high surface-to-volume ratio of materials and high residual stress effects, can significantly influence the properties of micro-joints. For instance, numerical simulations have indicated that high stress concentration at the fusion-zone boundary in laser micro-welded wire joints promote crack initiation and guide crack propagation along the wire circumference [53], which could be different from the observations in macrojoining. Advanced strain characterization techniques can also be employed to probe the deformation behavior of micro-joints at high spatial resolution. For instance, digital image correlation (DIC) has been applied to assess the tensile strain distribution in resistance welded SMA thin sheets (400 µm thick), as shown in Fig. 3a, b [69]. The results reveal high strain localization around the weld nugget, which serves as the nucleation site for cracking. This observation is further verified by EBSD dynamic recrystallization analysis (Fig. 3c). However, the use of DIC remains limited for mechanical testing of micro-welded wire joints, primarily due to the extremely small surface area, which challenges the spatial resolution of optical strain mapping. In contrast, such wire joints are well suited for in-situ testing within a SEM [53], whereas larger-scale joints typically require machining into miniature specimens for similar analysis. In general, micro-joints, particularly dissimilar ones, exhibit reduced mechanical performance relative to their base materials. For example, in laser micro-welded NiTi-SS joints, the formation of brittle IMCs within the FZ significantly lowers the joint strength compared with both NiTi-NiTi joints and the NiTi base material (Fig. 3d) [70]. Such joints typically fail at the early stage of elastic deformation, without displaying the characteristic superelastic behavior of NiTi. Functional performance of micro-joints is critical for precise device control during surgery or treatment, while fatigue resistance becomes particularly important for long-term implanted components subjected to cyclic loading. Taking biomedical SMA micro-joints as example, the residual strain during loading–unloading cycles serves as a key indicator of functional integrity: lower residual strain reflects a more ideal SE or SME response, enabling finer mechanical control of the integrated device. However, the joining process, particularly fusion welding and bonding with fillers, inevitably introduces a FZ or brazed/soldered/adhesive seam with microstructures and properties distinct from those of base materials. For example, arc micro-welded NiTi wires exhibit a markedly increased SE residual strain compared with the base material (Fig. 3e) [71], which is generally attributed to grain coarsening, local composition variation, and evolution of dislocations and precipitates within the FZ and heat-affected zone (HAZ). Thermomechanical fatigue testing, involving cyclic transformations between martensitic and austenitic states under constant or varying stress or strain, has been performed on laser micro-welded SMA wires (Fig. 3f) [72], which is instructive considering the complex mechanical-thermal environment of the human body. Consistent with the aforementioned mechanisms, the welded joints display reduced fatigue life compared to the base material. Post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) can effectively homogenize the microstructure within the weld zone, thereby improving the fatigue resistance of micro-joints. Other study has suggested that PWHT is also an effective way to mitigate the residual stress of micro-joints along with parameter optimizations [74]. Moreover, by avoiding melting of base materials or fillers, solid-state microjoining offers a promising route to retain both functional performance and fatigue reliability, even for dissimilar micro-joints for biomedical applications, as exemplified in Fig. 3g [60].

Investigating the corrosion behavior and biocompatibility of micro-joints, particularly implantable ones, is of importance in biomedical devices, as these factors directly determine their reliability and safety within physiological environments [75–77]. The relevant evaluations typically include electrochemical corrosion, hydrogen embrittlement, heavy-metal ion release, cytotoxicity tests, etc., conducted either in vivo or in vitro. Several studies have suggested that the mixing of beneficial alloying elements (such as Ti and Cr) during fusion micro-welding can facilitate the formation of the continuous and protective passive film, thereby significantly improving the electrochemical corrosion resistance of the resulting micro-welded joints under some particular human-body environments [57, 78, 79]. As illustrated in Fig. 3h, i [57], the NiTi-SS wire micro-joint exhibits higher corrosion potential and pitting potential, together with a substantially reduced pitting density compared with the NiTi base material. In contrast, dynamic recrystallization occurring during solid-state welding may lead to significant deviation of grain size at the interfacial region of the joints [80], which in turn limits the formation of a uniform and protective passive film on the weld-zone surface. Composition design during microjoining should be approached with particular caution, since it critically governs both the corrosion behavior and biocompatibility of the micro-joints. The addition of certain elements intended to enhance mechanical strength may unintentionally deteriorate corrosion resistance or biocompatibility of micro-joints. Toxic metallic elements such as Cu, Co, and Ni should be avoided due to their potential to trigger adverse biological responses. Even elements generally regarded as biocompatible can exert detrimental effects when introduced in excessive amounts or when altering local electrochemical activities. For instance, in micro-joints of poly lactic acid (PLA)-based PLA-Mg composites developed for cardiovascular stent applications (Fig. 3j) [73], increasing the Mg content within the composite significantly accelerates both the corrosion rate and hydrogen release rate of the joints, as shown in Fig. 3k, potentially compromising the biocompatibility of the fabricated devices. In clinical applications, the reliability of such micro-joints is a critical concern. For example, wire-based micro-joints (e.g., those formed between Kirschner wires and cerclage wirings) used in orthopaedic and orthodontic settings have been reported to exhibit risks of low torsional stability and pin-tract infection [81]. These challenges highlight the need to consider both torsional fatigue resistance and corrosion/biocompatibility during the microjoining process. For microjoining, surface preparation and cleaning prior to the joining process may play a more important role in joint properties. For example, the wire surface pre-welding machining (i.e., creating a cup-to-cone butt joint) can effectively optimize wire fixing and interfacial bonding [82]. For biomedical device applications, post-joining processing of micro-joints (especially sterilization) is also a critical consideration for enhancing functional stability and biocompatibility. Such processes include, but are not limited to autoclaving, gamma irradiation and plasma sterilization.

Opportunities and challenges

Microjoining for biomedical device fabrication remains a relatively specialized and narrow research area. A synthesis of existing case studies nevertheless reveals several key bottlenecks that challenge joint formation, restrict the practical application of these micro-joints and limit further development of the field. These include the adaptation of welding/joining methods, knowledge and characterization techniques from conventional macroscales to microscales, the comprehensive evaluation of joint performance in the context of biomedical applications, and a range of specific joining challenges, such as brittleness in dissimilar-material systems and the preservation of functional properties of base materials. Addressing the opportunities and challenges outlined below could further accelerate progress in this field.

(1) The continuous extension of human lifespan, together with the rapid advancement of medical technologies, is driving the demand for increasingly sophisticated and smarter biomedical devices. In cardiology, for instance, the need to detect “in-stent restenosis and occlusion” has spurred the development of next-generation stents and guidewires equipped with multifunctional sensors and adaptive mechanical architectures [21]. The realization of such integrated systems critically depends on the reliable microjoining of miniature sensors and structural components to the stent or guidewire base. To satisfy these requirements, new materials and complex configurations are being introduced, necessitating the application of diverse microjoining strategies. A representative example is the integration of microelectromechanical system (MEMS) sensors into a fractional flow reserve (FFR) guidewire (Fig. 4a) [83], which is based on the packaging of pressure sensors by soldering wires on the flexible printed circuit board (FPCB), joining of FPCB to guidewire surface by adhesive bonding and the joining of radiopaque coil to the guidewire tip by fusion welding. (2) The microjoining processes should be precisely controlled and further optimized to improve the reliability of micro-joints. Such optimization efforts should be tailored to address the predominant joining challenges associated with specific material combinations and joining methods, for instance, the suppression of brittle IMCs formation during laser micro-welding of dissimilar SMAs. A recent study demonstrates that applying an electromagnetic field during laser micro-welding of NiTi and SS wires (Fig. 4b) [84] can significantly improve joint quality. By adjusting the electromagnetic field parameters, the induced Lorentz force and associated stirring effects drive the liquid FZ toward the SS side, thereby reducing the mixing between dissimilar molten metals and effectively limiting the formation of brittle Fe-Ti phases. Compared with directly welded joints, the electromagnetic-field-assisted micro-joints exhibited notable enhancements in both tensile strength (by 28%) and ductility (by 137%). While electromagnetic control has been extensively studied in macroscale welding [87], it is expected to offer a stronger controlling capability for microjoining because of the smaller size of the liquid phase (i.e., FZ). Additional optimizations, such as advanced electrode design in resistance micro-welding [87], customized tool-tip geometries in friction-based micro-welding [58], and surface modification techniques like ultrafast laser texturing for brazing, soldering, and adhesive bonding [88], also hold considerable promise for achieving defect-free and high-performance micro-joints.

(3) Deepening the fundamental understanding of microjoining, particularly from the perspectives of materials science and processing physics, is crucial for advancing this research frontier. The capillary flow may dominate gravity at the microscale [49]. Such microscale nature of both materials and processes presents unique challenges to conventional experimental setups and characterization techniques. In-situ monitoring techniques, including high-speed imaging, electrical signal feedback and spectrum diagnosis, are being widely applied for welding processes. They are providing powerful capacities on the explanation of welding phenomena by solid and direct evidence: for example, the keyhole dynamics and the resultant porosity formation during laser welding have been directly observed by the in-situ ultrahigh-speed X-ray imaging [89]. However, rare studies of these techniques on micojoining processes are reported yet, which is also probably limited by the spatial and temporal resolution at the microscale. Beyond the continued development of these advanced experimental methods, modeling and simulation provide powerful complementary tools for elucidating the complex material dynamics involved in microjoining. As an example, smoothed particle hydrodynamics simulations have been employed to model the evolution of interfacial waveforms during high-velocity impact welding of thin Ni-SS sheets (Fig. 4c) [85]. The formation and propagation of these characteristic waveforms, commonly observed in various impact micro-welding processes [90–92], are governed by a jet indentation mechanism, in which jet velocity, normal stress, and flyer horizontal welding speed collectively control the resulting interfacial morphologies. In addition, emerging approaches such as in situ diffraction techniques and density functional theory (DFT) calculations are expected to further illuminate pathways on uncovering microjoining process mechanisms.

(4) To advance and expand the practical application of microjoining in biomedical devices, the properties of micro-joints, including their mechanical responses, functional performance, and corrosion or biocompatibility characteristics, should be rigorously evaluated in the context of specific device requirements. This requires the development of specialized testing methodologies tailored to microscale configurations. For instance, a cantilever bending fatigue design has been developed for superelastic wires (Fig. 4d, e) [86], which can also be adapted to assess the performance of micro-wire joints. The extension of material characterization techniques into microscales, like micro-DIC [93, 94], could be employed in the deformation or microstructural evaluations of micro-joints. Due to the distinct scales and geometries involved compared with conventional joints, standardized joining and testing protocols for micro-joints remain scarce. This significantly limits cross-study comparison and hinders industrial adoption. For example, the properties of brittle-dominated dissimilar micro-joints are expected to be strongly influenced by size effects. In this context, direct comparison of joint strength across different studies may lack clear significance. Moving forward, coordinated efforts within the research community will be essential to establish standardized procedures for both microjoining processes and the corresponding evaluation of joint reliability and functionality.

Concluding remarks

Microjoining is an indispensable process in the fabrication of modern biomedical devices. With the rapid evolution of biomedical engineering, this field is entering a stage that demands both technological advancement and deeper scientific understanding. The range of materials used for joining continues to expand. While metallic materials still dominate, the use of polymers and composites has grown rapidly in response to the need for multifunctional systems. All four major categories of joining techniques, namely fusion welding, solid-state welding, brazing and soldering, and adhesive bonding, can be adapted to the microscale through the miniaturization of heat sources or machining tools. Each method offers distinct advantages and limitations depending on the specific material combinations involved. Beyond the evaluation of basic mechanical properties and failure modes, the functional performance, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility of micro-joints are also critical under biomedical conditions. Special phenomena associated with the microjoining process (e.g., capillarity-dominated flow and size-dependent heat transfer) should be deeply investigated to uncover the microjoining mechanisms. Moreover, new research directions could be explored, including AI-assisted process optimization, hybrid joining strategies and bio-inspired interfaces. While these approaches are increasingly being developed in macrojoining, their exploration in microjoining remains largely at an early stage. Looking ahead, both opportunities and challenges coexist, particularly in the joining of next-generation smart devices, optimization of processing, advancement of fundamental understanding, and the development of standardized methodologies for joining and testing in microscale.
