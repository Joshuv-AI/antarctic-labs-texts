Tourniquet use in total knee arthroplasty and systemic inflammation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Caglar Tuna Issi, Recep Altin, Andac Akbas, Bilge Kagan Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumatic tourniquet use during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains controversial. Although it reduces intraoperative blood loss, tourniquet-induced ischemia–reperfusion may amplify systemic inflammation. Hematologic immune-inflammatory indices such as the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI) provide simple and cost-effective measures of systemic inflammation. This study aimed to investigate the independent effect of tourniquet use on early postoperative SII and SIRI following primary TKA.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 120 patients (60 tourniquet, 60 non-tourniquet) undergoing primary TKA for Kellgren–Lawrence grade IV osteoarthritis were analyzed. Preoperative and 24-hour postoperative complete blood counts were used to calculate SII and SIRI values. Between-group comparisons, within-group time-dependent analyses, correlation testing, and multivariable linear regression modeling were performed to determine independent predictors of percentage increases in SII and SIRI. Effect sizes were reported alongside statistical significance.ResultsBaseline demographic characteristics and preoperative SII/SIRI values were comparable between groups. Tourniquet use was associated with significantly lower postoperative hemoglobin decline (2.64 ± 0.99 vs. 3.53 ± 0.75 g/dL, p = 0.001). However, postoperative inflammatory activation was markedly higher in the tourniquet group. Postoperative SII (2387.0 ± 1009.2 vs. 1900.8 ± 928.6, p = 0.003) and SIRI (8.54 ± 2.53 vs. 5.29 ± 2.38, p = 0.001) were significantly elevated. Percentage increases in SII and SIRI were substantially greater in the tourniquet group, with large effect sizes. In multivariable regression analyses, tourniquet use independently predicted both SII increase (β = 91.8, p = 0.001, R²=0.34) and SIRI increase (β = 272.8, p = 0.001, R²=0.35).ConclusionsTourniquet application during primary TKA is independently associated with a significantly greater early systemic inflammation, despite reduced perioperative hemoglobin loss. These findings suggest that tourniquet use is associated with higher early postoperative systemic inflammatory marker levels beyond local tissue effects. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether minimizing tourniquet exposure can improve postoperative recovery and clinical outcomes.Level of evidenceLevel IV, retrospective cohort study.

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a progressive degenerative joint disorder characterized by cartilage degradation, osteophyte formation, subchondral sclerosis, and synovial inflammation, ultimately leading to pain, functional limitation, and reduced quality of life [1, 2]. Among weight-bearing joints, the knee is most frequently affected, and advanced disease is commonly classified using the Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grading system [3]. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is widely accepted as the definitive surgical treatment for advanced knee osteoarthritis, particularly in patients with end-stage disease [4–6]. However, TKA is also a substantial physiologic stressor that triggers a systemic inflammation, which may influence early recovery and postoperative morbidity [7, 8].

TKA is one of the most commonly performed orthopedic procedures worldwide [9, 10]. During TKA, pneumatic tourniquets are frequently applied to create a bloodless surgical field, improve visualization, reduce intraoperative blood loss, facilitate cementation, and shorten operative time [11, 12]. Despite these theoretical benefits, tourniquet use remains controversial due to its association with postoperative pain, quadriceps weakness, wound complications, and neuromuscular injury. Yet, accumulating evidence indicates that tourniquet use may introduce clinically relevant trade-offs—especially through ischemia–reperfusion injury, endothelial activation, and inflammation—contributing to early pain, quadriceps dysfunction, wound complications, and other adverse outcomes [13, 14]. Contemporary randomized evidence syntheses have highlighted that routine, full-duration tourniquet strategies may not consistently reduce clinically meaningful endpoints (e.g., transfusion, venous thromboembolism), while potentially increasing pain and local morbidity [14, 15].

In recent years, systemic inflammation has gained increasing attention as a determinant of postoperative recovery and complications following major orthopedic surgery [16, 17]. In parallel, there has been growing interest in simple, inexpensive inflammation-derived biomarkers obtained from standard complete blood counts. Hematological inflammation-based indices derived from routine complete blood count parameters offer a simple and cost-effective method for evaluating systemic inflammation. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI) integrate innate and adaptive immune components (neutrophils/monocytes/platelets vs. lymphocytes) and have shown prognostic relevance across surgical and orthopedic populations. For instance, SII/SIRI-related metrics have been linked to postoperative infectious complications and recovery trajectories in elderly surgical cohorts [18] and to broader frailty-related outcomes in elective orthopedic surgery [19]. Although these indices have recently been investigated in orthopedic populations, data regarding their behavior following TKA—particularly in relation to tourniquet use—remain limited. In TKA specifically, recent work suggests that postoperative SII/SIRI may be clinically informative, including association with adverse outcomes such as periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) [20]. However, the independent effect of tourniquet use on early postoperative systemic immune-inflammatory markers (SII/SIRI) after primary TKA has not been adequately defined.

This study investigated the association between tourniquet use during primary TKA and early postoperative changes in SII and SIRI. We hypothesized that tourniquet application would be associated with greater early increases in both indices, reflecting a stronger systemic inflammation.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Orthopaedics and Traumatology, XXX University. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Meeting number: 2025/11, Date: 05 September 2025) and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective and non-interventional nature of the study, informed consent was waived.

Study population

Patients aged 60–85 years who underwent primary TKA under epidural anesthesia for KL grade IV knee osteoarthritis, had complete pre- and postoperative laboratory data, and a minimum postoperative follow-up of 6 months were included, whereas individuals younger than 60 or older than 85 years, with active perioperative infection, rheumatologic or inflammatory joint disease, neurological or peripheral neuropathies (including diabetic polyneuropathy), prior tumor- or trauma-related lower-extremity surgery, immunosuppressive therapy, revision TKA, incomplete medical records, or severe systemic disease such as advanced hepatic or renal failure and anti-inflammatory drug use in the last 15 days were excluded.

A total of 186 patients who underwent primary total knee arthroplasty during the study period were initially screened for eligibility. Of these, 42 patients were excluded based on predefined criteria. The reasons for exclusion included incomplete perioperative laboratory data (n = 14), presence of active infection or inflammatory joint disease (n = 8), prior lower-extremity surgery or trauma (n = 7), use of immunosuppressive or recent anti-inflammatory medications (n = 6), and severe systemic comorbidities such as advanced hepatic or renal failure (n = 7). Following these exclusions, a total of 120 patients with complete and consistent clinical and laboratory data were included in the final analysis and were equally allocated into the tourniquet (n = 60) and non-tourniquet (n = 60) groups.

All procedures were performed under epidural anesthesia. In all patients undergoing tourniquet application, the cuff pressure was standardized at 150 mmHg above the measured systolic blood pressure. Operative duration and total tourniquet application time were systematically recorded for each patient. Postoperative analgesic requirements during the first 48 h were managed via an epidural catheter.

Data collection

Medical records were retrospectively retrieved from patients’ files. Patients were stratified into tourniquet and non-tourniquet groups based on intraoperative pneumatic tourniquet use, demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index, and operated side) and laboratory data were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical record system, and systemic inflammatory markers levels—the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI = monocyte count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count)—were calculated using preoperative and early postoperative values for analysis. The percentage increase was calculated using the following formula: [(postoperative value − preoperative value) / preoperative value] × 100.

Preoperative parameters were derived from complete blood count analyses obtained during routine preoperative anesthesia evaluation, whereas postoperative parameters were based on complete blood count measurements collected at 24 h following surgery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with histogram inspection and skewness–kurtosis values. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and when appropriate, as median (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Within-group preoperative and postoperative comparisons were conducted using the paired-samples t-test. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between inflammatory indices and clinical variables. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to determine independent predictors of percentage increases in SII and SIRI. Regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and R² values were reported.

In addition to statistical significance testing, effect sizes were calculated. Cohen’s d was used for continuous variables and Cramer’s V for categorical variables. Effect sizes were reported for all comparisons regardless of statistical significance. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post-hoc power analysis was performed based on the primary outcome variables, demonstrating that the study had sufficient power to detect the observed differences between groups.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 120 patients were included, with equal distribution between the tourniquet (n = 60) and non-tourniquet (n = 60) groups. The mean age was 73.05 ± 8.58 years, and the mean body mass index was 31.73 ± 4.21 kg/m². The right knee was operated on in 65% of cases. In the between-group comparison, the proportion of right-sided procedures was 53.3% in the non-tourniquet group and 76.7% in the tourniquet group. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.007). There were no statistically significant differences between groups with respect to age, BMI, or sex distribution (p > 0.05). However, right-sided procedures were more common in the tourniquet group (p = 0.007). The mean operation time was 96.20 ± 19.67 min in the tourniquet group and 91.47 ± 12.45 min in the non-tourniquet group (p = 0.944). The mean tourniquet duration was 59 min in the tourniquet group (Table 1).

Hemoglobin changes

When postoperative hemoglobin decline was evaluated, the mean decrease was 3.53 ± 0.75 g/dL in the non-tourniquet group, compared with 2.64 ± 0.99 g/dL in the tourniquet group. The difference between groups was statistically significant (p = 0.001). Hemoglobin decline was significantly greater in the non-tourniquet group (Table 1).

Systemic inflammatory markers levels

Preoperative SII values were 577.0 ± 463.1 in the non-tourniquet group and 515.9 ± 200.2 in the tourniquet group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.175). Similarly, preoperative SIRI values were 1.29 ± 0.76 in the non-tourniquet group and 1.26 ± 0.62 in the tourniquet group, also showing no significant intergroup difference (p = 0.412). In the postoperative period, however, a marked divergence in inflammatory response was observed. Postoperative SII values were significantly higher in the tourniquet group (2387.0 ± 1009.2) compared to the non-tourniquet group (1900.8 ± 928.6) (p = 0.003), with this difference supported by a moderate effect size. When the percentage increase in SII was analyzed, the mean increase was 366.9 ± 89.85% in the tourniquet group, whereas it remained at 261.2 ± 91.72% in the non-tourniquet group; this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.001). Postoperative SIRI values were 5.29 ± 2.38 in the non-tourniquet group and 8.54 ± 2.53 in the tourniquet group, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.001). Analysis of the percentage increase in SIRI revealed a mean increase of 666.9 ± 290.6% in the tourniquet group compared to 368.8 ± 154.0% in the non-tourniquet group, demonstrating a statistically significant difference with a large effect size (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Within-group time-dependent evaluation of systemic immune-inflammatory indices

In the tourniquet group, a significant increase was observed in both SII and SIRI values. For SII, the preoperative mean value was 515.9 ± 200.2, which increased to 2387.0 ± 1009.2 postoperatively. This change was statistically significant (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = − 2.23), indicating a substantial and clinically meaningful increase. Similarly, SIRI values increased from a preoperative mean of 1.26 ± 0.62 to a postoperative mean of 8.54 ± 2.53. This increase was also statistically significant (p < 0.001), with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d = − 3.12) (Table 2). In the non-tourniquet group, SII values increased from 577.0 ± 463.1 preoperatively to 1900.8 ± 928.6 postoperatively. This change was statistically significant (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = − 2.13). SIRI values increased from a preoperative mean of 1.29 ± 0.76 to a postoperative mean of 5.29 ± 2.38. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = − 2.02) (Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis revealed that one of the strongest positive associations was observed between preoperative SII and postoperative SII (r = 0.71, p < 0.001). A moderate-to-strong positive correlation was also identified between postoperative SIRI and postoperative SII (r = 0.63, p < 0.001). Additionally, significant positive correlations were found between SII percentage increase and postoperative SIRI (r = 0.59, p < 0.001), preoperative SIRI and postoperative SII (r = 0.54, p < 0.001), preoperative SIRI and preoperative SII (r = 0.48, p < 0.001), and preoperative SIRI and postoperative SIRI (r = 0.45, p < 0.001). Furthermore, SII percentage increase correlated positively with postoperative SII (r = 0.27, p = 0.003), and SIRI percentage increase showed a strong positive correlation with SII percentage increase (r = 0.56, p < 0.001) as well as a positive correlation with postoperative SIRI (r = 0.39, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

A noteworthy negative correlation was observed between SII percentage increase and preoperative SII (r = − 0.33, p < 0.001). Similarly, SIRI percentage increase showed a negative correlation with preoperative SII (r = − 0.31, p < 0.001) and with preoperative SIRI (r = − 0.53, p < 0.001). In addition, hemoglobin decline was negatively correlated with postoperative SII (r = − 0.26, p = 0.004) and with SIRI percentage increase (r = − 0.26, p = 0.004) (Fig. 1).

Multivariable linear regression models

Two separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed to evaluate the determinants of percentage increases in SII and SIRI, including age, body mass index (BMI), sex, tourniquet use, and surgical side as covariates. In the model explaining SII percentage increase, the overall explanatory power was R² = 0.34. Tourniquet use emerged as the strongest and statistically significant predictor (β = 91.8, 95% confidence interval [CI], p = 0.001). Surgical side demonstrated a significant negative association with SII increase (β = −47.2, 95% CI − 81.3 to − 13.12, p = 0.007). In contrast, age (β = 0.99, 95% CI − 1.18 to 3.16, p = 0.370), BMI (β = −0.67, 95% CI − 4.43 to 3.09, p = 0.724), and sex (β = −35.3, 95% CI − 76.8 to 6.12, p = 0.094) were not statistically significant predictors (Table 3). In the model examining SIRI percentage increase, the explanatory power was R² = 0.35, indicating a similar level of model fit compared with the SII model (Table 3). Overall, when both models were considered together, tourniquet use independently and robustly increased both SII and SIRI percentage changes.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that tourniquet use during primary TKA is associated with a significantly greater early systemic inflammation, quantified by higher postoperative SII values and, more prominently, by large-magnitude increases in both SII% and SIRI%. Importantly, in multivariable regression analyses, tourniquet use remained independently associated with higher postoperative inflammatory marker levels (R² ≈ 0.34–0.35). These findings indicate that tourniquet use is associated with increased systemic inflammatory marker levels beyond local tissue effects.

Tourniquet use has been suggested to be associated with clinically relevant trade-offs, potentially related to ischemia–reperfusion processes, endothelial activation, and inflammatory responses. Experimental and clinical studies have reported cellular-level effects of tourniquet use in TKA patients [21, 22]. Reperfusion following ischemia has been linked to oxidative stress and inflammatory activation, which may contribute to early postoperative symptoms such as pain, quadriceps dysfunction, and wound-related complications, particularly with prolonged tourniquet use [14]. In this context, our findings demonstrate that tourniquet use is associated with higher early postoperative SII and SIRI levels, supporting the role of these indices as markers of postoperative inflammatory status, as also suggested in previous studies [18, 19].

Recent studies have explored SII and SIRI as postoperative risk markers in arthroplasty-specific settings, including associations with adverse outcomes such as periprosthetic joint infection and delayed recovery [19, 20]. Although the present study did not directly evaluate infectious or long-term clinical endpoints, our findings suggest that higher postoperative inflammatory marker levels associated with tourniquet use may be related to early postoperative physiological changes and potentially to downstream complications. These results support further investigation into whether minimizing tourniquet exposure is associated with improved outcomes, particularly in patients with elevated baseline inflammatory risk.

In TKA, tourniquet use has been reported to increase oxidative stress during ischemia and reperfusion phases within the first 24 h postoperatively [22]. While pneumatic tourniquets effectively reduce intraoperative blood loss and postoperative hemoglobin decline, they are also associated with ischemia–reperfusion processes, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory activation, as shown in previous studies [14, 21, 23–25]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that tourniquet-assisted TKA was associated with lower hemoglobin loss but higher early postoperative SII and SIRI values compared with non-tourniquet TKA.

The strengths of this study include the use of a real-world arthroplasty cohort, balanced exposure groups with equal numbers of tourniquet and non-tourniquet cases, and robust statistical methodology incorporating effect size reporting and multivariable regression modeling.

This study has several limitations: its retrospective design limits causal interpretation and key perioperative factors such as tranexamic acid use, drain placement, transfusion thresholds, and perioperative anti-inflammatory or steroid administration were not consistently available due to the retrospective design, and these variables may have influenced both hemoglobin changes and systemic inflammatory markers. Another limitation of this study is the lack of conventional inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), as these parameters were not consistently available due to the retrospective design; however, SII and SIRI, derived from routine complete blood count data, were preferred as they provide a practical, cost-effective, and integrative assessment of systemic inflammatory response. Although all patients had a minimum follow-up of 6 months, the present study was not designed to evaluate clinical outcomes such as pain, wound complications, or functional recovery. Another limitation is the absence of functional outcome measures such as pain scores (VAS) and range of motion (ROM), which limits the ability to directly correlate systemic inflammatory markers with clinical recovery. Therefore, no direct correlation between inflammatory indices and long-term clinical endpoints was performed. Since the analysis focused on early postoperative inflammation, prospective studies are needed to better define the clinical significance of high SII and SIRI after TKA.

An additional point that warrants consideration is the imbalance in operative side between the study groups. Although operative side is not typically regarded as a primary determinant of systemic inflammatory response, its unequal distribution may reflect residual confounding or surgeon-level selection preferences inherent to the retrospective design. Furthermore, operative side was identified as a variable in the multivariable model for SII increase, suggesting a potential interaction with the observed outcomes. To address this, a sensitivity analysis adjusting for operative side was performed, and the main findings remained consistent. Nevertheless, this imbalance should be interpreted cautiously and acknowledged as a potential source of bias.

Conclusions

Tourniquet use during TKA is associated with higher early postoperative systemic inflammatory markers, as reflected by increased SII and SIRI values. Although tourniquet application may reduce perioperative blood loss, its potential contribution to systemic inflammation should be considered when planning surgical strategies. Whether these early hematologic changes translate into clinically meaningful adverse outcomes requires further prospective investigation.
