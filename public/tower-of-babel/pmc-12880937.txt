A not too Fragile Bacteroides
Authors: Siddartha Guru, Nadim Jaafar, Rahul Sharma, Poonam Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a rare case of Bacteroides fragilis vertebral osteomyelitis, in a 20-year-old female with no underlying medical conditions. She presented with a three-week history of lower back pain, myalgias, rigors, and night sweats. The patient was febrile and tachycardic, with borderline low blood pressures. Her initial labs showed elevated inflammatory markers with no leukocytosis. MRI of the thoracic and lumbar spine demonstrated T12/L1 discitis/osteomyelitis with phlegmonous changes and concerns for epidural abscess, causing mild to moderate spinal stenosis. Bacteroides fragilis grew in an anaerobic bottle, and a CT-guided biopsy of the T12/L1 lesion grew Bacteroides fragilis. E-test sensitivities revealed sensitivity to metronidazole. The source of bacteremia was thought to be from gut translocation thus concerned about possible co-infection with E. coli from the gastrointestinal tract. Another source of E. coli could have been from her recent E. coli urinary tract infection, whose growth from blood cultures could have been suppressed from recent course of cephalexin. She was treated with six weeks of metronidazole for the Bacteroides fragilis and ceftriaxone for possible co-infection with E. coli, resulting in improvement of her back pain and down-trending inflammatory markers on clinic follow-up after six weeks.

Introduction

Bacteroides fragilis, a gram-negative bacillus, is an obligate anaerobe and a constituent of the colonic microbiota. It is the most common cause of anaerobic bacteremia and can cause septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, skin and soft tissue infections, endocarditis, and meningitis.1,2 Anaerobic infections are usually seen in patients with poor oral hygiene, malignancy, immunosuppression from steroids, diabetes, or chemotherapy, and usually present with abscess formation.3 Vertebral osteomyelitis is a rare presentation of Bacteroides fragilis infection, with only 20 previously reported cases, most of which were present in patients with underling diabetes mellitus, sickle disease or recent gastrointestinal procedures.4–6 Unlike our patient with no underlying medical conditions or recent gastrointestinal procedure. We present a case of a 20-year-old female with sepsis secondary to Bacteroides fragilis bacteremia and T12/L1 discitis/osteomyelitis.

Case report

2.1. Presentation

A 20-year-old healthy female presented to the emergency room (ER) with a three-day history of myalgias, rigors, intermittent fevers, and lower back pain. The back pain was non-radiating and described as throbbing and burning in nature, worsening with movement and lying flat. There was no associated lower extremity numbness, weakness, or urinary or stool incontinence. A week prior, she had a three-day, self-limiting, profuse, watery diarrhea. On arrival, she was found to have urinary retention requiring catheterization. A urinalysis suggested infection, with cultures growing 50,000–100,000 colonies of pan-sensitive Escherichia coli. An MRI of her lumbar spine showed no abnormalities, and CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast showed no abnormalities. She was discharged on a 10-day course of cephalexin 500 mg four times daily for a urinary tract infection (UTI).

Three days later, she returned to the ER with worsening back pain and intermittent fevers despite being on cephalexin. Additional investigations done at that time including lumbar puncture were negative. Thus, she was discharged home and instructed to continue her antibiotic course. Upon course completion, the patient returned to the ER due to worsening symptoms. She was found to be febrile (38.4 °C) and tachycardic (131 BPM) with low blood pressure (90/60 mmHg). Physical examination was significant for tenderness of the lumbar spine and left lower abdominal quadrant, with no rigidity or peritoneal signs. Cardiopulmonary and neurological assessment was otherwise normal. On further questioning, she owns two cats and rabbits but has no scratches or bites. Moreover, her travel history included river rafting in the northeast USA a few months prior and interacting with dairy cows at a community fair. The patient was admitted for further investigation and management.

2.2. Diagnostics

Initial labs revealed an unremarkable complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel. Inflammatory markers were elevated with a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 14.9 mg/dL and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 53 mm/h. Blood cultures obtained prior to broad spectrum antibiotics, cefepime and vancomycin revealed no growth but repeat blood cultures obtained 24 h later after broad spectrum antibiotics, grew Bacteroides fragilis in one anaerobic bottle. A repeat CT of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrated T12 -L1 discitis/osteomyelitis and phlegmonous changes, with concern for an epidural abscess causing mild to moderate spinal stenosis (Fig. 1). MRI findings mirrored those seen on the thoracolumbar CT scan. Further investigations, including sexually transmitted infections (STI) screening (HIV, hepatitis, syphilis, and gonorrhea/chlamydia) was negative. A CT-guided T12/L1 lesion bone biopsy performed after being on broad spectrum antibiotics for about 12 h, grew Bacteroides fragilis with sensitivities as described in Table 1.

2.3. Medical intervention

Neurosurgery evaluated the patient and recommended no acute surgical intervention. The patient was initially started on broad-spectrum antibiotics with vancomycin and cefepime. Upon recovering Bacteroides fragilis from the T12/L1 biopsy, vancomycin was discontinued, cefepime was narrowed down to ceftriaxone, and metronidazole was added. A peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) line was placed, and the patient was discharged on a six-week course of intravenous ceftriaxone two grams twice a day and oral metronidazole 500 mg three times a day.

2.4. Follow-up

Two weeks later, she was seen in clinic with a complaint of GI upset which was thought to be secondary to metronidazole; hence, the dose was decreased to 500 mg twice daily. Her CRP decreased from 14.9 to 2.3 mg/dL, with back pain improving. She was seen again in the clinic six weeks after discharge, and her back pain was resolved, with no fevers or chills.

Discussion

Bacteroides fragilis and other gut flora organisms cause bacteremia when there is intestinal dysbiosis or disruption in the barrier.7 Our patient had profuse diarrhea for three days, which could have caused the translocation of gut bacteria, leading to transient bacteremia, which could have seeded to the spine. In one of the retrospective studies investigating B. fragilis bacteremia, 77/142 patients had polymicrobial bacteremia, with 24/77 (31 %) having concomitant Escherichia coli bacteremia.8 Our patient had an Escherichia coli urinary tract infection, which was treated with a 10-day course of cephalexin. This may have suppressed the Escherichia coli in the blood and bone cultures. Thus, the decision was made to treat patient with antibiotics to cover E. coli and Bacteroides fragilis.

The choice of antibiotics for E. coli was based on her recent urine cultures that were pan-sensitive, though cefazolin could have covered it for easier administration at home, ceftriaxone was chosen instead. Unlike other gram-negative rod anaerobes, B. fragilis contain many resistance genes against penicillins, tetracyclines, clindamycin, and cephalosporins but very rarely do they contain the nim gene which codes for metronidazole resistance.9,10 Thus, metronidazole was chosen to cover the Bacteroides fragilis. However, it is important to note that worldwide metronidazole resistance is increasing; for instance, in Pakistan, metronidazole resistance rose from 12.3 % in 2010–2011 to 17.5 % in 2014–2017.11

On review of current literature, the duration of antibiotics for vertebral osteomyelitis is six weeks.12 Unless if patient has Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection, undrained paravertebral/psoas abscesses, and end-stage renal disease which pose a high risk of infection recurrence, thus eight weeks is recommended for these patients.13

Bacteroides fragilis bacteremia is associated with a 20–31 % mortality rate when treated and an upwards of 60 % mortality when left untreated.2,3 Hence, it is crucial to keep Bacteroides fragilis in the differential when thinking about possible etiologies vertebral osteomyelitis, especially when bacteremic with Bacteroides fragilis or if there is concern for possible intrabdominal source of infection.

Conclusion

In this article, we report a rare case of Bacteroides fragilis bacteremia with vertebral osteomyelitis and discitis in an immunocompetent patient with no underling risk factors. Highlighting the importance of including Bacteroides fragilis in the differential when thinking about possible etiologies vertebral osteomyelitis, especially when bacteremic given the associated high mortality rate. We further discuss the treatment challenges and implications.
