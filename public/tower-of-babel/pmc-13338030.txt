The comprehensive evaluation of urban business under deep learning and Siamese neural network
Authors: Xiaoyue Zhang, Xuesong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The intensifying global competition among cities necessitates accurate and efficient evaluations of urban business environments to drive economic growth, attract investment, and foster innovation. Traditional assessment methods, often reliant on expert opinions and manual analysis, are prone to subjectivity and inefficiency. To address these limitations, this study introduces an optimized Siamese Neural Network model designed to improve the accuracy and efficiency of urban business environment evaluations. The model leverages feature extraction and multidimensional learning to analyze key indicators, including economic development, infrastructure integrity, policy friendliness, and market entry difficulty, utilizing publicly available datasets. Additionally, the model incorporates emerging technologies, including the Internet of Behaviors and generative artificial intelligence (AI), to bolster capabilities in capturing and analyzing complex behavioral data. The Internet of Behaviors enables the collection of real-time dynamic behavioral data from various urban activities, providing a comprehensive and detailed understanding of the business environment. Generative AI, on the other hand, generates predictive models from existing data, simulating future trends and scenarios, thereby enhancing the accuracy and foresight of decision-making. Performance comparison experiments demonstrate the model’s superiority over baseline models across all evaluation metrics. Specifically, the optimized model achieves F1 Scores of 0.874, 0.879, and 0.882 on the Doing Business Indicators, Urban Land Cover Classification, and Open Cities Artificial Intelligence Challenge datasets, respectively, significantly outperforming the Graph Neural Network for Business Environment and Transformer-based Business Environment Evaluation models. Furthermore, the model exhibits exceptional efficiency, with training times of 29.648 s, 31.327 s, and 32.843 s on the respective datasets. In terms of scalability and adaptability, the model achieves Scalability Scores and Generalization Capabilities of 0.821 and 0.876 on the DBI dataset, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling large-scale, multidimensional data. A comprehensive evaluation of urban business environments revealed specific strengths and weaknesses in cities A, B, and C. City A excelled in economic development (8.5) and infrastructure integrity (9.0) but scored lower in market entry difficulty (5.5). City B showed balanced performance across all metrics, while City C demonstrated strengths in policy friendliness (8.5) and market entry difficulty (8.0) but lower scores in infrastructure integrity (6.5). These results highlight the model’s utility in identifying areas for improvement and fostering targeted interventions. This study advances the theoretical and practical application of deep learning techniques in urban business environment evaluation, offering city administrators an efficient and objective decision-support tool. By enabling data-driven policy formulation and resource optimization, the proposed model provides a robust strategy for enhancing urban competitiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51434-w.

Introduction

In the era of accelerated globalization and urbanization, the quality of a city’s business environment has emerged as a pivotal factor in attracting investment, fostering economic growth, and enhancing international competitiveness1. A well-developed business environment not only provides fertile ground for enterprise growth but also acts as a core driver of sustainable regional economic development. However, as urban scales and complexities continue to expand, the scientific evaluation of business environments faces significant challenges2,3. Traditional evaluation methods, which often rely on expert assessments or single-indicator analyses, are limited by subjectivity, inefficiency, and an inability to process complex, multidimensional data. These approaches are increasingly inadequate for meeting the demands of modern urban management and policymaking, which prioritize objectivity, real-time insights, and precision4–6. Conversely, the rapid advancement of big data and artificial intelligence (AI) technologies has highlighted the potential of deep learning to address these challenges. Specifically, Siamese Neural Network (SNN), known for their strength in multi-category similarity assessment and feature learning, offer a promising solution for tackling complex evaluative tasks7. The integration of AI technologies into urban business environment evaluation enhances the precision and efficiency of assessments and provides intelligent, data-driven support for urban governance. This approach represents a significant step toward meeting the evolving demands of urban policy and management in an increasingly interconnected world. The rapid advancements in the Internet of Behaviors (IoB) and generative AI technologies have significantly enhanced data richness and predictive capabilities for evaluating the urban business environment. The IoB facilitates real-time collection and analysis of diverse data from urban settings, while generative AI constructs predictive models that simulate future trends.

Although deep learning methods have been increasingly introduced into studies of urban governance and comprehensive evaluation in recent years, several critical limitations remain unresolved in the existing literature. First, most current approaches rely on traditional classification or regression models to directly score the business environment. Such methods have limited capacity to capture relative similarity and structural differences among cities within a high-dimensional indicator space. As a result, the interpretability of evaluation outcomes for comparative analysis and policy diagnosis is constrained. Second, although some studies have incorporated models such as graph neural networks or Transformers, these approaches often emphasize feature aggregation or temporal modeling. Their ability to represent complex and nonlinear interactions among high-dimensional indicators remains insufficient. In addition, model architectures typically depend on extensive prior assumptions, which restricts their generalization capability. Furthermore, when dealing with multi-source heterogeneous data, existing models commonly suffer from low training efficiency and limited cross-dataset transferability. These issues make them difficult to deploy in practical urban management scenarios that require stability and scalability.

To address these limitations, this study proposes an optimized Siamese neural network model for urban business environment evaluation. Unlike conventional single-stream prediction models, the proposed approach explicitly learns similarity relationships between city samples. It reformulates business environment evaluation as a distance-based, multi-dimensional contrastive learning problem. This design enables more effective characterization of comprehensive differences among cities across dimensions such as economic foundations, institutional environments, infrastructure, and organizational culture. Through targeted optimization of the network architecture and loss function, the model significantly improves training efficiency and cross-dataset generalization while maintaining evaluation accuracy. These improvements make the model more suitable for complex, high-dimensional urban business environment assessment tasks. Overall, the proposed approach addresses the shortcomings of existing methods in similarity modeling and multi-dimensional structural representation, and provides a more interpretable and practically valuable technical pathway for the scientific evaluation of urban business environments.

Literature review

There is a significant difference between traditional and modern approaches to evaluating urban business environments8. Yin et al.9 revealed an overreliance on qualitative analysis in existing evaluation methods, indicating that such methods are inadequate for addressing the challenges posed by increasingly vast datasets9. Wu et al.10 emphasized the inefficiencies of traditional evaluation methods in processing multidimensional data, noting their lack of flexibility and depth when faced with complex business environment data10. Deep learning technology has made significant advancements in data analysis and processing. Essamlali et al.11 showcased the superior performance of deep learning techniques in handling large-scale, complex datasets, particularly in extracting key features and identifying hidden patterns. The optimization and application of SNN have recently become a focal point of research. Qin et al.12 demonstrated the high accuracy and reliability of optimized SNN in complex data recognition tasks, emphasizing the importance of optimization strategies in enhancing network adaptability12. Research by Organisciak et al.13 has demonstrated that the automatic scoring capability for divergent thinking has been significantly enhanced through the use of large-scale language models, surpassing previous evaluation methods that relied solely on semantic distance. This advancement contributes to the understanding of cognitive skills and creativity13. In a separate study, Lyu14 proposed a confidence-based communication method for agents aimed at personalized medical imaging diagnoses. Their findings indicate that this approach effectively improves the accuracy of medical image diagnoses and the level of personalized service, thereby providing new technical support for the intelligent development of medical diagnostics14. Additionally, Kor et al.15 conducted a review of research on trustworthy representation learning across domains, highlighting both the potential and challenges of learning methods in various application contexts. Their research underscores the importance of building trustworthy models in multi-domain representation learning and suggests future research directions15. Danach et al.16 demonstrated that the deep integration of AI technologies into supply chain management could significantly enhance system efficiency and sustainability. This improvement was achieved through data-driven predictive analytics, optimized resource allocation, and multi-agent collaborative decision-making mechanisms. From the perspectives of intelligent algorithms, data integration, and system optimization, their study systematically elaborated on the pathways through which AI operates in complex economic systems. The authors emphasized that AI can improve the operational efficiency of individual components, while also strengthening overall system resilience and adaptability through cross-agent and cross-level information integration16.

Previous studies indicate that the evaluation of urban business environments has largely relied on expert opinions and qualitative analyses. While these methods can be effective in specific contexts, they often suffer from subjectivity and challenges in handling complex data. Traditional methods struggle to address the complexities associated with large-scale economic data and market dynamics, leading to evaluation outcomes that lack objectivity and timeliness. Despite significant advancements in data analysis through deep learning, its application in the evaluation of urban business environments remains in its early stages, with existing research primarily focused on basic applications of models without the necessary optimization and in-depth exploration tailored to specific needs.

The innovation of this study lies in the introduction of deep learning techniques into the evaluation of urban business environments and the optimization of the SNN model. Unlike existing research, this study enhances the model’s ability to handle complex, multidimensional business environment data by adjusting its structure and parameters, thereby addressing the limitations of traditional methods when faced with nonlinear and dynamically changing data. The optimized model provides more accurate and timely evaluation results, filling gaps in existing research regarding model performance and application breadth, and offering a novel technological solution for evaluating urban business environments.

A core characteristic of this architecture is its use of two or more identical sub-networks that share the same parameters and weights. This design enables the parallel processing of two independent inputs, allowing for the comparison and correlation of their features in the final stage of the network26–28. The architecture of the optimized model outlined in this study consists of several critical components. Initially, raw data is received by the input layer, marking the commencement of the data processing sequence. The data proceeds to the preprocessing layer, where standardization or normalization is applied to ensure uniformity in data distribution, preparing it for subsequent operations. Following preprocessing, the data is directed through a series of convolutional layers, where convolution operations extract relevant features, a vital process for identifying patterns and features within images or multidimensional datasets. The next stage involves the pooling layer, which performs spatial dimensionality reduction, decreasing the data’s dimensionality. This operation enhances computational efficiency while retaining key features. Upon passing through the pooling layer, the data enters the activation layer, where nonlinear transformations are applied. This step bolsters the model’s capacity to handle complex nonlinear relationships within the data. After undergoing convolution and pooling, the data enters the connection and comparison layer, where similarities or differences between the input data are computed. This layer plays a significant role in tasks that require comparing distinct samples, particularly in SNN applications, where similarity calculations are essential. Once comparisons are made, the data flows into the fully connected layer, where further processing occurs via the weighted combination of features, culminating in classification or prediction results. In addition to the aforementioned layers, extra processing layers are incorporated to further refine or extract features from the output of the connection and comparison layer, strengthening the model’s classification or regression capabilities. The final output is produced by the output layer, which generates the ultimate prediction or classification label.

DBI is sourced from the historical Doing Business database and its accompanying methodological documentation published by the World Bank (URL: https://archive.doingbusiness.org/en/data). This database provides country/city-level business regulatory indicators, scores, rankings, and related descriptions. The fields used in the study, including country names, city names, indicator names, indicator values, years, and rankings, are organized accordingly. Historical Doing Business data can be accessed through the World Bank’s Doing Business archive page, and the relevant methodological descriptions and download entries have been publicly released, eliminating the need for separate academic access applications. OCAI is sourced from the Open Cities AI Challenge: Segmenting Buildings for Disaster Resilience project (URL: https://www.drivendata.org/competitions/60/building-segmentation-disaster-resilience/page/219/). ULC is a dataset publicly released by the OpenDataLab platform, which is targeted at urban land cover identification and classification research and is a publicly accessible data resource (URL: https://opendatalab.com/OpenDataLab/Urban_Land_Cover). Based on this public data and combined with relevant statistical information at the city level, this study further organizes and structures some features to form an analysis sample suitable for the comprehensive evaluation task of urban business environment.

The datasets used in this study encompass diverse and multidimensional features, providing robust support for the comprehensive evaluation of urban business environments through deep learning models. For the experimental analysis, a random sampling approach was adopted, selecting 5000 records from each dataset. This strategy was implemented to ensure an adequate sample size for effective model training, validation, and testing, while avoiding excessive computational resource demands and prolonged training times. Additionally, this approach preserved the diversity of the training data by adequately representing the primary feature distributions across the datasets. By maintaining the representativeness and variability of the data, this sampling method effectively balanced computational efficiency and model performance, enabling reliable and comprehensive evaluation experiments.

To ensure effective fusion and stable training of multi-source data within the Siamese neural network, a unified data preprocessing procedure was conducted on the DBI, ULC, and OCAI datasets prior to model construction. The overall pipeline consisted of five stages: data cleaning, feature encoding, missing value handling, data normalization, and sample pair construction. Together, these stages formed a complete and reproducible data processing workflow. During the data cleaning stage, consistency checks were first applied to the raw datasets. Records with obvious anomalies or duplication were removed, including entries with excessively high proportions of missing values or logical inconsistencies among indicators. City names and temporal fields across different datasets were then standardized to ensure correct alignment of samples during cross-dataset integration. In the feature encoding stage, categorical variables such as city names and country names were transformed using numerical mapping to prevent categorical information from being ignored during model training. Continuous indicators, including economic indices, infrastructure indices, and GDP per capita, retained their original numerical structure for subsequent normalization.

For missing value handling, a statistical imputation strategy based on samples from the same city or with similar evaluation levels was adopted. When an indicator was missing in only a small number of samples, the mean value of that indicator within comparable samples was used for imputation. If a record contained multiple missing values on key indicators, the entire sample was removed to avoid introducing noise that could interfere with model learning. In the data normalization stage, all continuous features were normalized to a unified scale to eliminate the influence of differing measurement units on model training. Before normalization, indicators exhibited substantial differences in numerical range and distribution. After normalization, feature distributions became more concentrated and scale-consistent, which facilitated faster convergence and improved training stability. During the construction of Siamese network sample pairs, training pairs were generated based on business environment evaluation results and indicator similarity. City samples with similar evaluation levels or belonging to the same rating interval were constructed as positive pairs. City samples with large evaluation differences were constructed as negative pairs. This design enabled the model to learn the relative similarity structure among cities rather than relying solely on absolute score prediction.

Through the above preprocessing and sample construction procedures, the consistency, comparability, and representativeness of the multi-source data were effectively ensured. This process provided a solid data foundation for the stable training of the Siamese neural network and for reliable experimental validation in subsequent analyses.

To ensure training stability, experimental reproducibility, and fair comparison across different methods, this study adopted unified settings for the architectural parameters, training hyperparameters, and key strategies of the Siamese neural network. The rationale for major parameter choices was provided in detail, taking into account the characteristics of the urban business environment evaluation task. In the input layer design, each city sample was represented by a one-dimensional feature vector composed of multiple business environment indicators. This vector integrated information across several dimensions, including economic foundations, institutional environment, infrastructure, and organizational culture. The Siamese neural network took two structurally identical city feature vectors as inputs, corresponding to the two city samples to be compared, thereby enabling joint modeling of inter-city similarity and difference. During the feature embedding stage, the network employed multiple convolutional layers and nonlinear mappings to compress the original high-dimensional indicators into low-dimensional embedding vectors. The embedding dimension was set to 64, a value determined through extensive preliminary experiments. This setting preserved essential information while avoiding redundancy and reducing the risk of overfitting associated with excessively high dimensionality. Experimental results indicated that this configuration achieved a reasonable balance between representational capacity and computational efficiency. The convolutional backbone consisted of two convolutional layers, each containing 32 convolutional kernels. This depth was sufficient to capture local combinational relationships among different indicators while avoiding training instability caused by overly deep architectures. Such a structure was well suited to urban business environment data, which are high-dimensional yet relatively structured. For sample pair construction, a similarity-based pairing strategy grounded in evaluation results was adopted. City samples with similar business environment levels or belonging to the same rating interval were constructed as positive pairs, whereas samples with large evaluation differences were constructed as negative pairs. This strategy guided the model to focus on learning relative structural relationships among cities rather than relying solely on absolute evaluation scores. Regarding the margin parameter setting, multiple candidate values were compared during preliminary experiments. The final choice ensured effective aggregation of positive sample pairs and sufficient separation of negative sample pairs. This configuration exhibited favorable convergence stability and discriminative capability during training, thereby enhancing the separability of city samples in the embedding space. The similarity threshold was determined based on the statistical distribution of distances between positive and negative sample pairs in the training set. By analyzing distance variations in the embedding space, a threshold with high discriminative power was selected as the similarity decision criterion. When the distance between a sample pair fell below this threshold, the two cities were considered highly similar in terms of business environment characteristics; otherwise, they were regarded as significantly different. This threshold was further adjusted on the validation set to improve model generalization. For distance measurement and decision logic, Euclidean distance was employed to quantify similarity between embedding vectors. This metric provided an intuitive measure of overall differences between city samples in the embedding space and was consistent with the training objective of the Siamese network. The embedding vectors can be interpreted as high-level semantic representations of urban business environments, capturing latent relational structures among multiple indicators.

Regarding the training parameter configuration, the learning rate was set to 0.01. This value represents a compromise that ensures rapid model convergence while avoiding instability or oscillations during training. The batch size was set to 64 to balance computational efficiency and the stability of gradient estimation. All parameters were validated through preliminary experiments and were kept consistent across all experiments to ensure comparability and reliability of the results. Specifically, before conducting the main experiments, multiple preliminary trials were performed on the training and validation sets to assess the rationality and stability of key parameter settings. During this stage, core parameters—including the learning rate, batch size, embedding dimension, and network depth—were varied individually, while all other parameters were held constant to prevent confounding effects caused by simultaneous changes in multiple factors. Model convergence speed, validation performance trends, and training stability were compared across different parameter configurations to identify a balanced setting with robust overall performance. Particular attention was paid to performance fluctuations on the validation set, rather than relying solely on training set results, in order to reduce the risk of overfitting. In addition, training time and computational resource consumption under different parameter settings were recorded to ensure the practical feasibility of the final configuration in real-world applications. Based on the outcomes of these preliminary experiments, the final set of model parameters was determined and consistently applied in all subsequent comparative experiments, thereby ensuring fair experimental conditions and meaningful comparisons across different models.

Performance evaluation

(1) Comparative experiment on the performance of the optimized SNN.

The experiment evaluated the proposed model against two baselines: Graph Neural Network for Business Environment (GNN-BE) and Transformer-based Business Environment Evaluation (T-BEE). The GNN-BE model, grounded in graph neural network (GNN) principles, is tailored to capture the intricate relationships and interactions among cities and regions. By utilizing the structural framework of graphs, GNN-BE adeptly handles complex associative data, facilitating the analysis of interconnected urban elements. In urban business environment evaluation, this model incorporates relationships across urban regions, economic factors, and market dynamics, structured within the graph framework. Through embedding nodes and edges, GNN-BE effectively learns and forecasts urban business performance by capturing implicit connections between cities across multidimensional data. Conversely, the T-BEE model employs transformer architecture, traditionally associated with sequence modeling tasks, to assess urban business environments. Transformers have excelled in fields such as natural language processing and image analysis due to their ability to process long-range dependencies. In T-BEE’s application to urban business environment evaluation, the model focuses on multidimensional feature learning by capturing long-term dependencies and global features. A direct comparison between GNN-BE and the optimized SNN model emphasizes key distinctions. GNN-BE’s graph structure processes complex urban relationships, excelling in domains like social network analysis and recommendation systems. As a baseline model, GNN-BE provides valuable insights into how effectively multidimensional relationships between cities can be captured, validating the strengths of the optimized SNN model in addressing nonlinear complexities. T-BEE serves as a comparative model due to its ability to handle long-range dependencies and intricate data patterns. Its design philosophy enhances the analysis of long-term correlations between multidimensional factors such as economy, policy, and market within the urban business environment. In contrast to traditional neural network models, T-BEE optimizes feature selection and information integration via its self-attention mechanism, offering a useful benchmark for the advancement of the optimized SNN model.

Regarding the validation of the model optimization effects, this study does not introduce an explicit “unoptimized baseline Siamese neural network” as a separate comparison model. Instead, the effectiveness and necessity of the proposed optimization strategies are indirectly validated through systematic comparisons with baseline models that differ substantially in structure and modeling philosophy. Specifically, the selected comparison models—GNN-BE and T-BEE—represent two mainstream deep learning paradigms in the field of urban business environment evaluation: graph-based relational modeling and Transformer-based global dependency modeling, respectively. These two classes of models are highly representative in terms of architectural complexity, feature modeling strategies, and their ability to capture nonlinear relationships. Rather than conducting an internal comparison between optimized and unoptimized versions of the same model, the experimental design places greater emphasis on evaluating the comprehensive performance advantages of the optimized Siamese neural network relative to well-established and widely adopted methods in realistic application scenarios. A basic Siamese neural network lacks dedicated architectural designs for handling the multidimensional characteristics of urban business environments, integrating heterogeneous data sources, and modeling complex similarity relationships. As a result, its performance is typically constrained by limited feature representation capacity and training stability. Consequently, treating such a basic Siamese network as an independent baseline may fail to adequately reflect the practical value of the proposed optimization strategies in complex, real-world evaluation tasks.

The comparison was conducted using four key metrics: F1 Score, which reflects the balance between precision and recall in sample classification and is crucial for assessing the accuracy of urban business environment evaluations; Training Time, which provides insights into computational efficiency under consistent data and hardware conditions; Scalability Score, measuring the model’s performance as data volume increases, indicating its adaptability to larger datasets; and Generalization Capability, which assesses the model’s performance on unseen data, demonstrating its applicability across diverse cities, regions, and data dimensionalities. For the experimental setup, the datasets (DBI, ULC, OCAI) were treated as the independent variable, while the evaluation metrics were the dependent variables. Each dataset was split into 70% for training and 30% for testing, with the training set further divided into five subsets for a five-fold cross-validation approach. This method ensured that each subset was alternately used as a validation set, while the remaining four subsets served as the training set, and the performance was averaged over the five runs to assess stability. The advantages of cross-validation include reducing potential biases from random data splitting, ensuring consistency across data distributions, and providing more reliable evaluation metrics. This experimental design ensures that the results offer consistent and robust insights into the comparative strengths and limitations of the evaluated models.

Discussion

Although the optimized Siamese neural network proposed in this study demonstrates strong performance across multiple experimental metrics, it remains necessary to carefully examine potential confounding factors that may affect model performance. First, the urban business environment is inherently complex. Its evaluation outcomes are influenced by economic conditions, infrastructure, and policy factors, and uncontrollable elements such as macroeconomic cycles, regional development strategy adjustments, and sudden external events. These exogenous variables are often difficult to fully capture at the data level, which may affect the similarity structures learned by the model and lead to fluctuations in evaluation results across different time periods or regional contexts. Similarity-based learning models also exhibit inherent limitations in urban business environment evaluation. Siamese neural networks characterize similarity and dissimilarity between cities by learning relative differences among samples. This approach is well suited for comparative analysis and ranking tasks, but it is less effective in explaining the “absolute level” of an individual city. Moreover, when cities display structural differences across multiple dimensions while exhibiting similar overall performance, similarity-based models may fail to fully reveal the underlying divergence in their development trajectories. This limitation constrains the interpretability depth of the model outputs to some extent. With respect to scalability, although the optimized Siamese neural network shows favorable scalability in the experiments, its training process still relies on pairwise sample construction. As the number of cities and the data scale increase, the number of sample pairs grows exponentially, which may lead to substantial increases in computational and storage costs. In practical applications, reducing computational complexity through sampling strategies or hierarchical training mechanisms remains a key issue for large-scale deployment.

In addition, the datasets themselves may contain latent biases that affect model performance and generalization ability. Differences across data sources in statistical standards, update frequency, and data completeness may result in the overrepresentation or underrepresentation of certain cities or regions. Publicly available datasets also tend to favor economically developed cities or those with more comprehensive data disclosure, which may implicitly enhance model adaptability to specific city types while weakening performance in data-sparse or less developed areas. From an application perspective, the model may further face challenges such as unstable data quality, limited access to real-time data, and relatively high computational resource requirements. These constraints are particularly salient for urban management agencies or small- and medium-sized institutions, where computing capacity and data governance capabilities may limit practical adoption. Therefore, future research should further explore lightweight model designs, incremental learning mechanisms, and robust methods for handling low-quality or incomplete data.

When compared to prior studies, such as He et al.46, which implemented an expert-based scoring system that, while yielding relatively accurate results in specific smaller urban contexts, demonstrated limited capacity for handling rapidly changing, multidimensional data. In contrast, the optimized SNN model introduced here manages complex, multidimensional data more effectively, showing adaptability and generalizability in the context of evolving urban business environments. Structural adjustments and parameter optimizations equip this model to incorporate additional data dimensions and evaluate multiple cities, enabling more objective, data-driven analyses46. Similarly, Chui et al.47 applied basic machine learning algorithms to evaluate business environments, primarily focusing on single indicators such as economic development. Their approach lacked a multidimensional perspective. This study offers a more thorough and holistic reflection of the urban business environment by integrating multidimensional indicators (economic development, infrastructure completeness, policy friendliness, market entry difficulty) and utilizing deep learning techniques for feature extraction and comprehensive evaluation47.

In summary, this study is significant in terms of theory, management, and practice. It not only extends the theoretical framework of business environment evaluation but also provides scientific technical support and practical solutions for urban management and economic development. These findings offer valuable insights for future research and practical applications in related fields.

Conclusion

This study proposed an optimized Siamese neural network model based on deep learning for the comprehensive evaluation of urban business environments, and verified its effectiveness under specific data conditions through multiple sets of experiments. The results indicate that the proposed model effectively captures the differences among cities across multiple dimensions—including economic development, infrastructure completeness, policy friendliness, and market access difficulty—thereby providing a new technical pathway for the quantitative evaluation of complex urban systems. Within the scope of the experimental settings, the optimized Siamese neural network demonstrated certain advantages over the selected baseline models in terms of accuracy, computational efficiency, and the ability to model high-dimensional features. These findings suggest that the proposed model has promising application potential for handling structurally complex and high-dimensional urban business environment data. However, it should be emphasized that the observed performance was primarily derived from the available datasets and experimental scenarios. Its applicability and stability therefore require further validation across a broader range of city types, regional contexts, and more complex data environments. Accordingly, the conclusions of this study should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of the model’s potential advantages rather than a definitive assessment of its general applicability. From a practical perspective, the proposed method may serve as an auxiliary analytical tool for urban managers and policymakers by helping to identify relative strengths and weaknesses in urban business environments and providing quantitative references for policy optimization. Nevertheless, the model currently relies mainly on publicly available data sources, and the evaluation indicator system has not yet been fully tailored to different stages of urban development or institutional contexts. These limitations constrain its depth of direct application in real-world decision-making. Future research should therefore focus on expanding data sources, enhancing the model’s generalization ability across diverse city types and development stages, and exploring more context-sensitive evaluation indicator systems. In addition, the computational efficiency and stability of the model in large-scale data scenarios require more systematic testing and validation. Through these improvements, the practical value and credibility of the proposed approach in urban business environment evaluation are expected to be progressively strengthened. In addition, emerging technologies such as Internet of Things behavior data and generative artificial intelligence can serve as potential expansion directions in the future, providing new support for urban business environment evaluation in areas such as data enhancement, dynamic feature perception, and scenario simulation. However, their actual value still needs to be further tested in subsequent research, combining specific implementations and empirical analysis.

Overall, this study provides a new modeling perspective for the intelligent evaluation of urban business environments and offers exploratory methodological contributions. The findings offer a reference basis for subsequent research on model optimization, data construction, and application scenario expansion, while further validation and refinement in richer and more complex real-world settings remain necessary.
