Machine learning based on preoperative CT for noninvasive prediction of recurrence-free survival in gastrointestinal stromal tumors
Authors: Lian Zhao, Liming Zhao, Xiaonan Yin, Enyu Yuan, Yili Gu, Xiaohua Zheng, Bing Wu, Yuan Yin, Xijiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveClinicians need a reliable, noninvasive tool that can predict the risk of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) recurrence preoperatively. We aimed to develop a machine learning model based on preoperative contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) to predict recurrence-free survival (RFS) in GIST patients who underwent radical resection.Materials and methodsA total of 192 patients with intermediate- and high-risk GISTs who underwent radical resection and subsequently received adjuvant imatinib were included, with a minimum follow-up duration of 24 months. A machine learning model (Modelradiomic) based on preoperative CECT was built to predict RFS, which was compared with the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) index (ModelAFIP). The C-index and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test.ResultsThe C-index values of the Modelradiomic in the training, testing and validation cohorts ranged from 0.678 to 0.763, whereas the areas under the curves (AUCs) at 3 and 5 years were 0.744–0.790 and 0.703–0.833, respectively. The C-index values of ModelAFIP in the above datasets were 0.539–0.660, and the AUCs at 3 and 5 years were 0.497–0.649 and 0.503–0.630. Modelradiomic demonstrated a higher C-index than ModelAFIP did in the training and external validation cohorts (p = 0.010 and 0.042, respectively). Modelradiomic presented a greater AUC value than ModelAFIP did at the 5th year in the training cohort (p = 0.009).ConclusionWe found the machine learning-based preoperative CECT performed better than the AFIP index in prediction of RFS of GIST patients, especially at the 5th year, predicting recurrence risk in patients who underwent radical resection and receiving adjuvant therapy. This model may serve as a non-invasive tool to identify high-risk individuals who require more intensive surveillance and personalized management following radical resection.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) represent the most prevalent mesenchymal neoplasms of the digestive tract [1, 2]. Their biological spectrum is remarkably heterogeneous, spanning from indolent localized lesions to highly aggressive malignancies with a propensity for distant metastasis. While curative-intent surgical resection remains the primary therapeutic strategy, postoperative recurrence remains a formidable challenge, affecting up to 50% of high-risk patients even after complete (R0) resection [3]. The advent of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), most notably imatinib mesylate, has fundamentally shifted the treatment paradigm toward personalized molecular targeted therapy.

Despite these advancements, recurrence continues to be a primary determinant of long-term morbidity and a significant socioeconomic burden [4]. In current clinical workflows, risk stratification and subsequent decisions regarding adjuvant TKI therapy primarily rely on the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) criteria and the 2008 modified National Institutes of Health (mNIH) classification [5–8]. However, these conventional systems are hindered by several critical limitations: they exhibit suboptimal precision in categorizing intermediate-risk cases, frequently lead to the overtreatment of biologically indolent tumors, and conversely, may result in the undertreatment of aggressive subtypes. Furthermore, these criteria are predominantly based on postoperative histopathological parameters, such as mitotic count, which are subject to sampling bias and inter-observer variability [9]. This underscores an urgent, unmet clinical need for a robust, noninvasive preoperative biomarker capable of accurately predicting recurrence risk to facilitate tailored surveillance and adjuvant therapeutic strategies.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is the cornerstone of GIST management, providing essential data for initial diagnosis, staging, and longitudinal assessment [10–12]. Beyond macroscopic morphological evaluation, emerging evidence suggests that advanced imaging analytics—including radiomics and machine learning (ML) algorithms—can extract high-dimensional quantitative biomarkers that reflect intratumoral heterogeneity, microvascular density, and subtle microenvironmental alterations. These “sub-visual” features potentially capture biological nuances that conventional semantic analysis and even traditional pathology may overlook [13]. By converting standard voxel-based CT data into mineable, quantitative indices, AI-driven models have shown substantial promise in refining prognostic assessment and treatment response monitoring across various solid tumors [14, 15]. While preliminary investigations have indicated correlations between CT-derived features and GIST risk grades, large-scale, multicenter evidence linking these radiophenotypes directly to long-term recurrence-free survival (RFS) remains limited [16–18].

In this multicenter retrospective study, we aimed to develop and externally validate an ML-based model derived from preoperative CECT images to predict the risk of recurrence in patients with GISTs undergoing radical resection and subsequent targeted therapy.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations and patient selection

This retrospective multicenter study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of both participating centers (Center I: IRB No. 2022 − 449; Center II: IRB No. 2023 − 121). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Consecutive patients who underwent radical resection for primary GISTs between September 2011 and January 2023 at Center I, and between January 2018 and January 2023 at Center II, were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (1) preoperative abdominal contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) performed within 30 days prior to surgery; (2) histopathologically confirmed primary GIST following radical resection; and (3) moderate-to-high risk according to the 2008 modified National Institutes of Health (mNIH) criteria. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior neoadjuvant therapy (e.g., imatinib); (2) suboptimal CT image quality (e.g., significant motion artifacts or incomplete tumor coverage); (3) preoperative tumor rupture or distant metastasis; and (4) non-adherence to adjuvant imatinib therapy or loss to follow-up.

Clinical characteristics and follow-up data were retrieved from electronic medical records. The primary endpoint was recurrence-free survival (RFS), defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the first documentation of recurrence or metastasis via clinical examination, imaging, or pathology. The minimum follow-up duration was 24 months, unless recurrence occurred earlier. A total of 192 patients were ultimately enrolled (Fig. 1).

CT acquisition and image preprocessing

Standardized bowel preparation, including a low-residue diet and 10-hour fasting, was required. Patients consumed 600–1000 mL of water 40–60 min before the scan to ensure adequate gastric distension. Imaging was performed using eight different multi-detector CT scanners; detailed acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For radiomic analysis, portal venous phase images were selected for their superior depiction of tumor-parenchyma contrast. To ensure feature stability across different scanners, all images underwent three-dimensional linear interpolation to achieve a standardized isotropic voxel size of 0.75 × 0.75 × 5 mm.

Tumor segmentation and feature extraction

The workflow for model establishment is illustrated in Fig. 2. Three-dimensional (3D) regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated layer-by-layer on portal venous phase images using ITK-SNAP (version 3.6.0). To mitigate partial volume effects and contamination from peritumoral fat or adjacent structures, a 1-mm inward erosion was maintained from the visible tumor boundaries (Supplemental Figure S1). Initial segmentation was performed by one radiologist (Lian Zhao); a second radiologist (Xijiao Liu) independently re-annotated 25 randomly selected cases to evaluate inter-observer reliability.

A total of 105 high-dimensional quantitative biomarkers were extracted using the PyRadiomics framework (version 3.0.1). These comprised first-order statistics, shape-based features, and second-order texture indices—including Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray-Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), and Neighboring Gray-Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM)—to quantify intratumoral heterogeneity.

Radiomics pipeline and model development

Patients from Center I were randomly allocated into training and testing cohorts (8:2 ratio), while patients from Center II constituted the independent external validation cohort. A rigorous dimensionality reduction pipeline was implemented. Initially, redundant features with zero variance were excluded. Subsequently, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed as a preliminary filter (p < 0.05). To address multicollinearity, a Pearson correlation analysis was executed; for feature pairs with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.85, the redundant variable with lower prognostic significance was pruned. The most prognostically relevant features were further refined via a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-Cox regression model, with the optimal lambda value determined through tenfold cross-validation on the basis of the minimum error criterion. Finally, a random survival forest model with a depth of two was trained using the selected features. For ModelAFIP, univariate Cox regression analysis was used.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), compared using the independent-sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Model performance was quantified using the C-index and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) for 1- to 5-year RFS. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.8.10) and R (version 4.2.1). The statistical significance levels were all two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

The study included a dual-center cohort of 192 patients (99 males, 93 females). The total population was stratified into a training cohort (n = 113; mean age 53.6 ± 13.5 years), a testing cohort (n = 29; mean age 53.9 ± 10.5 years), and an external validation cohort (n = 50; mean age 57.6 ± 12.5 years). The mean maximal tumor diameter was 7.1 ± 0.2 cm (range: 2.0–24.4 cm), with 14.6% (n = 28) of lesions exceeding 10.0 cm. Primary tumor locations included the stomach (n = 122, 63.5%) and the small intestine (n = 62, 32.3%). Based on risk stratification, 27.1% (n = 52) were classified as intermediate risk, while 72.9% (n = 140) were categorized as high risk. Follow-up and recurrence outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 65.0 months (IQR: 44.0–87.0 months). Recurrence occurred in 24.0% (n = 46) of the study population, with a median recurrence-free survival (RFS) of 57.5 months (IQR: 36.3–84.8 months). Dominant sites of recurrence included the mesentery and peritoneal cavity (45.7%), followed by the liver (37.0%) and the surgical site (28.3%). Multisite recurrence was observed in 5 patients.

Radiomic feature extraction and selection

Inter-observer reliability for tumor segmentation reached a high level of consistency, with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.967 (95% CI: 0.949–0.983). Utilizing the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis, four robust radiomic features were identified as independent predictors for machine learning (ML) integration: original_firstorder_Kurtosis, original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity (GLNU), original_ngtdm_Busyness, and original_ngtdm_Coarseness.

Comparative performance of the ML model

The ML model demonstrated superior prognostic accuracy compared to the ModelAFIP. The C-indexes for the ML model across the training, testing, and external validation cohorts were 0.763, 0.746, and 0.678, respectively, significantly outperforming ModelAFIP (0.660, 0.616, and 0.539, respectively; p = 0.010 and p = 0.040 for training and validation). Time-dependent AUC analysis further corroborated this (Fig. 3). At the 5-year interval, the ML model achieved AUCs of 0.785, 0.833, and 0.703 in the three cohorts, whereas ModelAFIP yielded lower values of 0.630, 0.602, and 0.503 (p = 0.009 in the training cohort) (Table 2). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients in the high Rad-score group (median cutoff 3.785/3.676) exhibited significantly higher recurrence rates and diminished RFS compared to the low-score group (p < 0.001, Fig. 4).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study developed a ML model based on preoperative CECT to estimate postoperative RFS in patients with GIST following radical resection and adjuvant therapy. The ML model achieved a C-index ranging from 0.678 to 0.763 and 3-year and 5-year AUC values up to 0.790 and 0.833, respectively. Notably, the model significantly outperformed the AFIP index, particularly at the 5-year mark, demonstrating its potential as a robust, non-invasive tool for individualized risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Radiomics extracts high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images, transforming spatial data into mineable information that reflects tumor biology. In 2014, Aerts et al. reported radiomics findings for non-small cell lung cancer and head and neck cancer, demonstrating that radiomics can be used to analyze tumor prognosis [19]. Currently, radiomics holds great clinical application potential, represents an important tool in precision medicine, and is a major research focus in the field of imaging quantification [14, 20]. The application of AI in the imaging analysis of GISTs has expanded considerably in recent years [21–24].

In 2019, Tao Chen et al. developed and validated a prognostic nomogram to predict RFS after resection of localized primary GISTs in the absence of neoadjuvant therapy [16]. On the basis of the tumor size, site, mitosis and ResNet model built on preoperative CECT, the deep learning-based nomogram has AUCs of 0.947 (95% CI, 0.910–0.984) for 3-year RFS and 0.918 (0.852–0.984) for 5-year RFS. Recently, a study further demonstrated the clinical utility of CT radiomics in the management of high- and intermediate-risk GISTs [17]. A radiomics-based nomogram performed strongly in terms of RFS at 3, 5, and 7 years, with AUCs ranging from 0.76 to 0.84. Importantly, the radiomic nomogram outperformed the clinicopathological model (C-index: 0.864 vs. 0.733). These findings highlight the potential of radiomics-based models to enhance individualized risk stratification and inform adjuvant treatment decisions.

Our study revealed that the radiomics-based ML model demonstrated greater efficacy than the AFIP index in predicting the RFS of intermediate- and high-risk GIST patients who received radical resection and adjuvant therapy postoperatively. The inclusion of Kurtosis and GLNU as key predictors is particularly salient. From a pathophysiological perspective, high GLNU and Kurtosis values in CECT reflect an erratic distribution of voxel intensities. In GISTs, this imaging phenotype often correlates with internal areas of hemorrhage, cystic degeneration, or necrosis, which are hallmarks of high-grade malignancy and aggressiveness. Specifically, “Busyness” and “Coarseness” (NGTDM features) quantify the spatial rate of change in intensity; high “Busyness” suggests a complex stromal environment and rapid neoangiogenesis, which provides the metabolic scaffold for early recurrence and distant metastasis to the liver and peritoneum.

Recently, Bertsimas et al. [25] developed a highly interpretable AI model using a large-scale global cohort to predict GIST recurrence. While their model demonstrates exceptional performance using clinicopathological data, our study complements such efforts by leveraging preoperative 3D radiomic features. Unlike models relying on postoperative pathology including the AFIP criteria, which rely on representative sections and discrete thresholds (e.g., tumor size and mitotic count), our ML model integrates continuous radiomic data that reflect the whole-tumor volume. This bypasses the limitations of biopsy sampling bias and provides a complementary characterization for the tumor’s biological behavior.

Regarding clinical implementation, we emphasize that this preoperative CT-based machine learning model is designed to complement, rather than replace, established postoperative risk stratification systems (such as the AFIP criteria and the mNIH criteria). Currently, these conventional clinicopathological risk stratification systems serve as the cornerstone of GIST management and are highly effective at guiding routine postoperative adjuvant therapy. However, they are inherently limited by their reliance on postoperative resected tissue, rendering them inapplicable for early preoperative decision-making. Furthermore, the increasing clinical adoption of neoadjuvant imatinib therapy can confound postoperative pathological evaluation by altering tumor mitotic counts and staging. Our model addresses these critical gaps as a complementary tool. First, it enables non-invasive preoperative risk evaluation, assisting clinicians in developing individualized treatment strategies such as determining the suitability of neoadjuvant imatinib and the extent of surgery, in line with the surgical principles and multidisciplinary management recently reviewed by Cananzi et al. [26]. Second, by capturing spatial heterogeneity across the entire three-dimensional tumor volume, the model overcomes the sampling bias of pathology, helping resolve ambiguity in borderline or intermediate cases. Ultimately, this objective and reproducible model can be integrated into routine workflows to assist clinicians in personalizing postoperative surveillance and optimizing adjuvant therapy duration.

Another critical finding of this study is the model’s performance at both 3-year and 5-year milestones. The 3-year mark often coincides with therapeutic suppression by imatinib, while the 5-year mark typically reflects the risk after treatment cessation. Our results suggest that preoperative radiomics can identify biological signatures of both early resistance and delayed recurrence.

This study has several limitations. First, although this study utilized a multicenter design, the final sample size remains relatively small for machine learning applications. This may increase the likelihood of optimistic results due to sampling variance. To minimize this risk, we employed a rigorous feature reduction strategy and used an independent external validation cohort to confirm the model’s stability. The robustness of the selected features was further confirmed by the high inter-observer agreement (Dice coefficient = 0.967), suggesting that the extracted radiomic signatures are stable despite the modest sample size. Nonetheless, larger prospective cohorts are required to further refine the algorithm. Second, the lack of comprehensive clinical data on specific mutational status (e.g., KIT exon 11 vs. PDGFRA mutations) and the granular details of adjuvant imatinib dosage/duration represents a significant limitation. These factors are known to profoundly impact recurrence-free survival. While our CECT-based ML model aims to provide a non-invasive complementary assessmen for tumor aggressiveness, it cannot fully replace molecular profiling. Integrating radiomic features with genomic data (radiogenomics) will be a primary focus of our future work to enhance predictive accuracy. Third, the minimum follow-up of 24 months is relatively short, and a subset of patients may still have been receiving imatinib at their last follow-up. The lack of granular data on the exact duration of therapy and the timing of drug cessation limits our ability to analyze the ‘rebound’ recurrence risk post-treatment. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as an assessment of recurrence risk during or shortly after standard adjuvant therapy. Fourth, CT examinations were performed on eight different scanners. We assumed that this would increase the reproducibility and robustness of our models and potentially enable their wide diffusion. Furthermore, our study population was exclusively Chinese. Given that well-established differences exist in disease behavior and clinical prognosis across various ethnic groups, the generalizability of our ML model to Western populations remains to be determined. Future studies incorporating diverse, multi-ethnic cohorts are essential to validate these findings globally.

Conclusions

In this investigation, we developed and validated a ML model leveraging preoperative 3D radiomic signatures from CECT to predict RFS in patients with intermediate-to-high-risk GIST following radical resection and adjuvant imatinib therapy. Our ML model demonstrated superior prognostic performance compared to the conventional pathological AFIP risk classification system, particularly in identifying long-term recurrence risks. By capturing the spatial heterogeneity and underlying biological aggressiveness of the tumor through non-invasive imaging, this model facilitates more precise risk stratification at critical clinical decision points. While our results are promising, future prospective multicenter studies involving larger, multi-ethnic cohorts are essential to validate these signatures and explore the integration of genomic data (radiogenomics). Ultimately, such advancements will refine clinical decision-making, optimize the duration of adjuvant therapy, and improve long-term outcomes for patients with GIST.
