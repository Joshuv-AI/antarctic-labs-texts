Facilitators and barriers of implementing sin-tax policies: a scoping review
Authors: Marziyeh Najafi, Hamed Dehnavi, Lida Shams, Tejas Wakde, Roya Rajaee, Safoura Shahmoradi, Hamideh Javadinasab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sin taxes, or public health taxes, are excise taxes levied on goods that can harm health. These taxes aim to influence consumer behavior, enhance health outcomes, and generate revenue for health systems. While the effectiveness of sin taxes in influencing consumer behavior has been widely studied, there is limited synthesis of the barriers and facilitators to their implementation. This study specifically reviews these barriers and facilitators, providing insights into the successful sin-tax policy implementation. This study specifically reviews the barriers and facilitators to the implementation of sin-tax policies, focusing on the practical challenges to their adoption and enforcement. This scoping review was conducted in 2024. All documents related to Unhealthy product tax policies were identified using relevant keywords by searching four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) search engines. In the initial search, 1026 documents were extracted. Based on the selection criteria and reviewing full texts, 61 documents were selected for content analysis. Data analysis was performed using MAXQDA-20. The findings highlighted several barriers to the effective implementation of sin-tax policies. These included policy challenges, such as the influence of industrial lobbying and a lack of coordination among regulatory bodies; consumer-related barriers, like resistance to price increases and limited access to healthier alternatives; and industry-related issues, including the economic impact on businesses in regions dependent on unhealthy products. However, the review also identified key facilitators that could support successful implementation, including greater transparency, stronger community engagement, and the availability of affordable healthy alternatives. These factors could play a crucial role in overcoming the barriers to effective sin-tax policies. We should increase transparency, enhance cooperation, and improve access to healthy products to improve public health. Supporting the transition away from unhealthy options, raising awareness, involving communities, aiding low-income households, and promoting healthy alternatives are also crucial. Targeted subsidies and community engagement are vital to support low-income households impacted by taxes on unhealthy products. Raising public awareness can help shift cultural norms. A comprehensive approach that considers economic, social, and cultural factors is essential for establishing effective “sin taxes” that improve health outcomes and quality of life.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) contribute to 71% of global deaths, amounting to 41 million fatalities each year. The main risk factors for these diseases include alcohol abuse, smoking, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity [1].

Key products associated with these health issues comprise unhealthy foods, tobacco products, alcohol, and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). SSBs contain added sugars and include soft drinks, sweetened tea, flavored coffee, fruit juices, and sports drinks. Consuming these products can lead to various health problems, such as a false sense of fullness, loss of appetite, malnutrition, obesity, and a range of NCDs [2, 3].

Several countries have implemented various regulations to address the consumption of unhealthy products. These measures include restrictions on advertising, the imposition of taxes and duties, prohibitions on sales to children, and age limits for purchases of such products [4]. A type of taxation known as a sin tax or public health tax is an excise tax imposed on unhealthy products like tobacco, alcohol, and sugary drinks. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), these taxes are designed to discourage the consumption of these items, thereby promoting healthier choices and reducing associated healthcare costs [5]. A substantial body of global literature has already examined sin taxes, with systematic and critical reviews focusing primarily on their effectiveness in reducing consumption, improving population health, and influencing price elasticity. These reviews consistently demonstrate that taxation of tobacco, alcohol, and sugar-sweetened beverages can reduce consumption and generate revenue for health systems.

Large multinational companies in the processed food, alcohol, and tobacco industries are recognized as significant contributors to the NCD epidemic by producing, selling, and marketing products that are unhealthy to health. Furthermore, these industries often influence laws and policies to control behavioral risk factors associated with NCDs [6]. Evidence suggests that sin taxes influence consumer choices, leading to better health outcomes and generating revenue for healthcare systems [7, 8]. However, while effectiveness has been well documented, there is limited synthesis of the broader policy environment that influences whether sin taxes are successfully adopted and implemented. To date, no review has comprehensively mapped the barriers and facilitators of implementation across different settings. Addressing this gap is crucial, as understanding implementation challenges and enablers is necessary for translating evidence of effectiveness into sustainable policy action.

From an economic standpoint, excise duties are categorized as indirect taxation since they apply to goods or services rather than being levied directly on businesses or individual incomes. The revenue generated from sin taxes can assist countries in increasing funding for public services, enabling them to allocate financial resources for essential public spending without negatively impacting other areas of expenditure or destabilizing budget balances [7].

While taxes on unhealthy products like tobacco, alcohol, sugary foods, and other unhealthy goods have shown a positive impact on public health, there is limited research that brings together the various barriers and facilitators to their implementation. Although we know these taxes can be effective, we still lack a comprehensive understanding of what makes them succeed or fail in practice. This scoping review aims to fill that gap by mapping the factors that shape the implementation of sin-tax policies. By synthesizing the evidence from different sources, it offers fresh insights and practical guidance for policymakers looking to design and sustain effective taxation strategies on unhealthy products.

Study design

The present study was a scoping review that provides a picture of the current state of research in a given area. One of the most common reasons for conducting scoping reviews is to identify gaps in the literature [9]. The reasons for using a scoping review include identifying the breadth of evidence on the subject, mapping the relevant literature, examining the nature and scope of research, summarizing research methods used in the field, and identifying gaps in the thematic literature to determine whether a systematic review is warranted [10, 11]. Although systematic and critical reviews on sin taxes exist, they have primarily focused on specific outcomes such as consumption or health impacts. To our knowledge, no review has comprehensively mapped the barriers and facilitators of implementing sin-tax policies across contexts. A scoping review is therefore appropriate to capture this breadth of evidence and highlight areas for future systematic investigation.

This study aimed to explore a large body of literature to review and summarize current knowledge about Facilitators and barries of implementing unhealthy product tax.

Research design

The present study was conducted in 2020 using the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework for scoping review [12]. This framework has six steps, including the following: (1) Identification of research questions, (2) Identification of related studies, (3) Study Selection, (4) Charting the Data, (5) Collection, summarizing, and reporting the results, and (6) Optional consultation with experts (due to the optionality of this step, it was not done in this research).

Identification of related studies

The search was conducted according to the PRISMA guideline, which is commonly used for assessing, synthesizing, and reporting primary studies in systematic reviews [13].

Search strategy

We are searching for relevant studies using the appropriate keywords. The initial keywords were determined based on the opinions of a panel of experts, and subsequently, the relevant free texts were extracted from PubMed. The final combination of terms included: “tobacco tax OR Cigarette tax OR beverage tax OR food tax OR sugar tax OR salt tax OR fat tax OR alcohol tax OR Sin tax” that were searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, ISI/Web of Science database, and Google Scholar search engines. Database searches were conducted with a 10-year time limitation up to June 6, 2024, to identify the relevant articles.

Selection criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) were published in English, (2) had a full text available, (3) addressed at least one of our research questions on sin-tax policies, and (4) were published within the last 10 years (up to June 6, 2024). We accepted all empirical study designs (quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods) as well as systematic and scoping reviews relevant to sin-tax policies Studies were excluded if they: (1) were duplicates, (2) did not directly address taxation of unhealthy products, (3) focused solely on clinical/biological outcomes without policy relevance, or (4) were not accessible in full text. (5) Commentaries, opinion pieces, and editorials without empirical evidence were excluded.

These criteria were applied systematically during title, abstract, and full-text screening by two reviewers (MN and RR), with disagreements resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (HD).

Study selection

First, duplicates were removed; then two reviewers (MN and RR) independently assessed titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria; finally, potentially relevant studies were reviewed in full text. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion, with a third reviewer (HD) consulted when necessary. This process ensured rigor, transparency, and consistency, and ultimately 61 studies were included from an initial 14,732 records (Fig. 1).

Charting the data

During the fourth stage, key information was extracted from the selected documents. In scoping reviews, the selection of reporting items varies based on the review’s purpose and the types of documents identified [10]. It is essential to consider the implications of the findings from the scoping review [12]. Data regarding the publication year, author names, study locations (research settings), study types (methodology and content), and collaboration mechanisms were systematically classified and reported.

One researcher (MN) extracted data from each article, while another (RR) verified it. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and a third researcher (TW) was consulted when necessary. Authors were also contacted for clarification or to obtain missing information.

Collection, summarizing, and reporting the results

Following the framework used in this study, it was important to present the results in a tabulated format. A scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of existing studies, regardless of their quality. Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis method was utilized to analyze the data. This method involves familiarizing oneself with the data, identifying initial codes, searching for themes, and reviewing, defining, and reporting the results [14].

Analysis of the text extracted from selected documents was performed using MAXQDA20. Ethical considerations were carefully observed at all process stages, such as assessing the risk of bias during document selection, data extraction, data analysis, and other ethical issues associated with literature reviews.

Facilitators and barrires of implementing sin-tax policies

The most frequently mentioned challenge in the articles relates to health challenges, changing consumer behavior, and the lack of affordable and healthy alternative products. In this regard, the most important facilitator is to create incentives for producing healthy alternative goods and support more affordable access to them, which can help reduce the consumption of unhealthy goods.

Policy and regulation

The barriers faced by governments and the legislative sector of each country regarding sin taxes or harm-causing goods policies include an increase in smuggling or the creation of black markets, a decrease in government tax revenues, weak oversight of product quality and safety, poor collaboration among regulatory agencies, industrial lobbying and its impact on decision-making processes, conflicts of interest, and lack of transparency in policy-making processes. Identified facilitators from selected studies to reduce these barriers involve increasing the transparency of policies related to taxes on harm-causing goods, preventing the influence of industrial lobbies, and strengthening inter-agency collaboration.

Increasing taxes on harm-causing goods can sometimes lead to the growth of black markets and smuggling of these goods. In some countries, smuggling of items such as tobacco and alcohol has become a widespread business due to heavy taxes. This issue decreases government tax revenues and complicates product quality and safety oversight, indirectly leading to more significant health harm. Implementing tax policies requires careful monitoring to prevent tax evasion, smuggling, and illegal use of these goods. This necessitates collaboration among various regulatory agencies, the establishment of regulatory infrastructures, and increased transparency in the supply chain of harm-causing goods [15–20].

Industrial lobbies, especially in the tobacco and alcohol industries, have significant financial resources and political influence. These lobbies attempt to undermine or repeal tax policies by financing political campaigns, disseminating favorable information, and even influencing the media. In some cases, these industries even establish their own research centers to publish desired and misleading research results regarding the effects of taxes. In some governments, tax revenues from unhealthy goods constitute an essential part of the public budget. This conflict of interest can reduce governments’ motivation to implement strict tax policies. Instead of fully supporting high taxes, some governments may resort to milder policies to maintain tax revenues [21–27].

Governments can prevent the influence of lobbies in decision-making by increasing transparency and public information and involving society in the decision-making processes [24, 28]. Creating a transparent and robust monitoring system is essential to combat smuggling and tax evasion. International cooperation is very effective in controlling smuggling [29, 30].

Community and consumers

The tax on health-harming goods is designed as an economic policy tool aimed at reducing the consumption of these goods and promoting public health. These taxes, especially on products such as cigarettes, carbonated beverages, and unhealthy snacks, can significantly impact consumer behavior. However, this area has several barriers, including inconsistencies in tax laws, ineffective enforcement of restrictive advertising, and resistance from certain social groups against price increases. Furthermore, it should be noted that the revenue generated from these taxes can serve as a source for funding healthcare costs; however, allocating these resources is not carried out correctly. These barriers require careful examination and the development of operational solutions to improve public health and reduce the harm caused by consuming unhealthy goods. Many consumers of unhealthy products continue to use these products due to a lack of access to healthy alternative products. Tax policies on unhealthy goods should be accompanied by the development and availability of affordable and healthy alternatives (such as natural fruit juices instead of soft drinks) to guide consumers toward more nutritious choices [28, 31–45].

One of the factors that increases the effectiveness of taxes on unhealthy products is the level of public awareness regarding the harms of these products. Without sufficient awareness, many people may continue to consume these goods. For this reason, tax policies should be accompanied by awareness campaigns and public education so that people are properly informed about the negative effects of these products on their health and society [25, 27, 46].

One of the fundamental criticisms of taxes on unhealthy goods is that this type of tax can disproportionately affect low-income households. Studies show that low-income individuals often allocate more income to these goods. As a result, taxes on items such as tobacco and sugary drinks directly impact the living standards of these individuals and may expose them to greater poverty [31, 47–50].

Given the negative impact of taxes on low-income groups, supportive policies such as subsidies can alleviate the financial pressure on these households [46, 51, 52].

Supporting access to these goods at a lower cost can help reduce the consumption of unhealthy items. Effective implementation of taxes on unhealthy goods requires international cooperation and the establishment of joint commitments among countries to reduce global challenges such as smuggling and lack of oversight, thus maximizing the positive impacts of these policies on public health [28, 36].

Taxes alone are insufficient; awareness programs, social support, and supportive policies for alternative industries must accompany them [8, 28, 29, 32]. Governments can prevent the influence of lobbyists in decision-making by increasing transparency and public information and involving the community in decision-making processes [24, 44].

Industry and economic

Taxes on unhealthy goods within the industry and manufacturing sector serve as a key tool for reducing the consumption of unhealthy products and promoting public health, but they face multiple barriers. One of these barriers is the negative impact of these taxes on production costs and the final prices of products, which can reduce the competitiveness of producers. Moreover, the increased production costs may lead to changes in consumer behavior and a decrease in demand for unhealthy products, which can affect companies’ profitability. On the other hand, the lack of a clear legal framework for implementing these taxes may lead to confusion within the industry and create dissatisfaction among producers [53]. Therefore, policymakers need to consider these barriers and provide facilitator for implementing taxes on unhealthy goods, ensuring simultaneous support for producers while reducing the consumption of these products. The decrease in demand for unhealthy goods due to high taxes could lead to reduced investments and the closure of businesses in these areas. Some studies have indicated that certain regions economically depend on unhealthy industries, which are important for local employment and income [21, 54, 55].

The decrease in demand for unhealthy goods due to high taxes can lead to reductions in investments and the closure of businesses in these areas. As a result, policymakers in regions dependent on these industries may exert pressure to lower taxes [21, 27, 28].

Creating incentives for producing healthier alternative goods and supporting access to them at lower costs can help reduce the consumption of unhealthy goods. Overall, it can be stated that taxes alone are not sufficient; they must be accompanied by awareness-raising programs, social support, and policy measures to support alternative industries. [24, 28, 45, 55–58].

Discussion

Implementing “sin taxes” (levies on unhealthy products, such as tobacco, alcohol, sugary beverages, unhealthy food) presents multifaceted barriers across legislative, community, and industrial domains. Policy and implementation barriers include increased smuggling and the creation of black markets, decreased government tax revenues, and reduced product quality and safety oversight due to illegal production. There is often weak cooperation among regulatory agencies, with industrial sector lobbying influencing decision-making processes and creating conflicts of interest. Additionally, there is a pressing need for transparency in policy-making processes. On the community and consumer side, barriers such as resistance to price changes and specific consumer behaviors arise, along with insufficient access to affordable and healthy alternatives. Cultural and social norms further complicate these issues, leading to a more significant impact on low-income families and an unfair increase in poverty. Moreover, public awareness plays a crucial role in reducing consumption. From an industry and economic perspective, significant challenges exist, including the closure of businesses linked to unhealthy products and economic downturns in regions reliant on these industries. Job losses related to producing unhealthy goods and rising unemployment contribute to financial strain. A well-structured tax system can drive economic growth by efficiently mobilizing resources and enhancing people’s living standards. The Value Added Tax (VAT) is viewed as an effective mechanism due to its revenue neutrality, efficiency, simplicity, and equity [56]. While these barriers are significant, addressing them requires a deeper understanding of the broader public health issues at play. Specifically, understanding how these barriers intersect with health inequities, the economic burden on vulnerable groups, and the sustainability of policy efforts is critical to evaluating the long-term impact of sin taxes. It is essential to explore the ways in which policy decisions can be adjusted to account for these broader public health concerns and achieve more equitable outcomes for all sectors of society.

The findings of this study highlight several significant barriers legislators and policymakers face when implementing taxes on unhealthy products. Key issues include the rise of black markets and smuggling, decreased government revenues, inadequate oversight of product safety, and the strong influence of industrial lobbies—all of which can obstruct effective policy implementation [10–15]. High taxation often pushes individuals toward black markets and smuggling activities, jeopardizing public health goals and diminishing government revenues. Additionally, insufficient product safety oversight exacerbates the risks associated with illicit trade [57]. With the growing prevalence of black markets, internal and external obstacles, particularly those affecting monitoring and enforcement, will likely worsen the barriers linked to illicit trade and related activities [58]. One of the key public health issues embedded in this research is how industrial lobbies not only hinder policy enforcement but also prevent the allocation of resources to health-promoting initiatives. Addressing these power dynamics is essential for overcoming these barriers and ensuring that taxes on unhealthy products have the intended public health benefits.

Political transparency is essential for public health advocates, as it empowers them to monitor corporate political activities and safeguard policymaking from vested interests [59]. To effectively combat smuggling and ensure the successful implementation of “sin taxes,” it is crucial to enhance inter-agency cooperation and mitigate the impact of industrial lobbies. The industry frequently employs various strategies and adapts them over time for maximum effectiveness [60]. Consequently, governments should invest in monitoring and surveillance efforts to collect data that will aid in crafting adequate responses to the industry’s endeavors to undermine control policies.

Consumers face resistance to price increases and limited access to affordable, healthy alternatives. A recurring challenge in the literature is the lack of affordable healthy products. Consumers are increasingly reluctant to accept higher prices for goods and services, even when businesses raise prices due to external factors such as increased production costs or limited supply. Rather than acquiescing to these price hikes, consumers may seek alternatives, reduce their purchases, or demand more excellent value [61]. Promoting the search for alternative foods that ensure sustainable production and high nutritional quality is essential for guaranteeing regular access to food for the population [62]. This issue of affordability connects deeply with broader public health and equity concerns. The disproportionate impact of higher prices on low-income households exacerbates health disparities, as these populations are more likely to consume unhealthy products due to economic constraints. Addressing affordability in tandem with taxation can help create a more just and effective policy framework.

Another significant challenge refers to changing consumer behavior. Research indicates that segments of the population, including adults over 60, women, individuals living with obesity, and health-focused shoppers, are more inclined to pay a premium for healthier options. In contrast, younger consumers, those at healthy weights, and highly educated individuals tend to be less willing to pay higher prices [62]. Tabassum’s study (2020) explores the factors influencing consumers’ willingness to invest more in health and wellness food products, revealing that 44% of the population is willing to pay an average of 9% extra. Key factors influencing this willingness include income, education, health consciousness, product quality, taste, packaging, price, and convenience [63].

Moreover, low-income households are disproportionately affected by sin taxes, as they allocate more of their income to taxed goods. This trend results in low-income households spending significantly more on taxable goods and services than their high-income counterparts [64]. Higher tax rates have proven more effective than lower rates in reducing sales and consumption, particularly among low-income groups. Taxing unhealthy foods could lower their consumption [65]. However, modeling studies suggest potential unintended consequences, such as reduced consumption of healthy foods and increased intake of untaxed unhealthy options. Therefore, combining taxes on unhealthy foods with subsidies may enhance benefits [66]. Providing subsidies or social support to low-income households can alleviate the financial burden associated with these taxes. This finding underscores the importance of holistic policy design. To maximize the public health benefits of sin taxes, it is critical to combine them with strategies that support low-income populations, such as subsidies for healthy foods, to ensure that these taxes do not exacerbate existing health inequalities.

Cultural norms promoting unhealthy product consumption also pose a significant challenge. Kabir’s study (2007) underscores the socio-cultural acceptance of tobacco products as a substantial barrier to comprehensive tobacco control [62]. Policy design and sustainability initiatives often neglect cultural dimensions, prioritizing economic and environmental considerations. Incorporating cultural contexts into sustainability policies is crucial [63]. Raising public awareness about the harms of detrimental products and involving communities in decision-making are essential steps in shifting cultural norms and reducing resistance to change. Mobilizing public opinion can significantly influence decision-makers, making it crucial to employ various strategies and tools that effectively spread messages and gather support to shape public perspectives [67]. To enhance consumer awareness, there must be a comprehensive information strategy on chemical risks, active participation from target groups, and increased efforts from authorities and manufacturers to build trust and provide clear information [68]. Shifting cultural norms requires a long-term commitment to public education and community engagement. Incorporating these into policy frameworks is not only essential for consumer behavior change but also for building long-lasting public support for sin-tax policies.

Producers face significant barriers from an industry and economic perspective, including business closures, economic downturns in regions reliant on unhealthy industries, and rising production costs due to increased taxes. The decline of sectors associated with unhealthy products necessitates proactive measures to support economic transition and protect community health [30]. Incentivizing the production of healthier alternatives, aiding businesses in their transition to new markets, and providing financial assistance to regions dependent on unhealthy industries can help alleviate the adverse economic impacts of sin taxes. Supporting industries in transitioning to healthier alternatives is essential to ensure that economic impacts do not undermine the broader goals of public health. Policymakers must consider the economic challenges alongside the health benefits when designing tax policies, ensuring a balanced approach to addressing both economic and public health needs.

Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires a holistic approach encompassing robust policy frameworks, active community engagement, and strategic economic planning. This balance is critical to align public health objectives with socio-economic realities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, implementing “sin taxes” on unhealthy products poses several barriers that require careful consideration across various sectors. While these taxes can improve public health and generate revenue, their success depends on addressing black markets, consumer resistance, and socio-economic disparities. Legislative efforts should emphasize transparency and inter-agency cooperation to counter industrial lobbying and ensure effective enforcement. Understanding consumer behavior and offering affordable, healthy alternatives are vital for public acceptance.

Targeted subsidies and support mechanisms are essential to support low-income households affected by these taxes. Engaging communities and raising public awareness can help shift cultural norms around unhealthy products. Ultimately, a comprehensive approach that considers economic, social, and cultural factors will be crucial in establishing effective “sin taxes” that promote better health outcomes and enhance overall quality of life.
