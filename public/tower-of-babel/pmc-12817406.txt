Disaster preparedness of health systems in LMICs in the face of climate change: a systematic mapping review of vulnerabilities and response strategies
Authors: Kaylin Morris, Atena Pasha, Xiaoming Li, Shan Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change has intensified the frequency and severity of natural disasters, disproportionately impacting health systems in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) that face unique vulnerabilities due to limited infrastructure, workforce shortages, and constrained resources. This systematic mapping review examined disaster preparedness within LMICs’ health systems and identified common components of disaster preparedness, existing gaps, and adaptive strategies aimed at improving preparedness.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, and Web of Science was conducted in March 2025, following PRISMA guidelines. Empirical studies published in English that addressed climate-related disaster preparedness in LMIC health systems were included. Data extraction was performed using a structured template, and methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. A narrative synthesis was used to identify key themes.ResultsOf 518 records screened, 20 studies met the inclusion criteria. Study populations included health systems, healthcare workers, hospital administrators, disaster responders, and affected patients. Natural disasters examined included floods, earthquakes, droughts, cyclones, and landslides. Common preparedness components included workforce training, infrastructure capacity, stock and supply readiness, communication systems, and coordination and partnership. While some systems demonstrated adaptive strategies such as backup resources and coordination protocols, most faced persistent gaps in training, policy implementation, and system resilience. The findings indicate that enhancing resilience in LMICs requires investment in human capital, robust infrastructure, and coordinated emergency protocols.ConclusionThe findings underscore that strengthening disaster preparedness in LMICs’ health systems requires targeted investments in workforce development, infrastructure resilience, and cross-sector collaboration. These efforts are critical for building climate-resilient health systems capable of withstanding future emergencies.

Introduction

As evidenced by decades of scientific research and extensive environmental data, climate change poses a major threat to Earth’s natural and human systems. Over the past 125 to 175 years, global temperature has increased by nearly 2 degrees Fahrenheit, and this trend is continuing [1]. Increased emissions of greenhouse gases and other poor human habits have led to the shrinking of glaciers, shifting of animal geographic ranges, variations in flora blooms, rising sea levels, intense waves, changes in natural disaster patterns, and many other negative effects.

The impacts of climate change go beyond the environment, bringing global mental and physical health issues and public health challenges [2]. Increased temperatures and heatwaves can cause an increase in morbidity and mortality from noncommunicable diseases, mental illnesses, and occupational illnesses, as well as an increase in airborne and waterborne diseases [3]. Vector activities, such as those of ticks and mosquitoes, are also altered by temperature change, increasing the risk for vector-borne diseases. Additionally, this climactic shift can increase pathogen growth and exposure on crops, causing increased food-related infections.

Climate change has also caused large disruptions in the delivery of health services, demonstrating a lack of resilience in overall health systems [4]. Resilience is a term with rising importance in the public health and healthcare fields, which is defined as planning for future crises and adapting to stress or shocks [5–7]. For systems or organizations, resilience can be defined as the ability to adapt or bounce back from challenges or crises while maintaining essential functions [8]. While resilience has been deeply researched in a variety of disciplines, resilience among health systems in the face of climate change has not been greatly explored. With the imminent risk of climate change, it is imperative for health systems to establish ways of monitoring climate risks, creating an early warning system, and having a well-trained workforce [9].

Health systems most in danger of climate change risks are those in limited-resource settings or low and middle-income countries (LMICs). The World Bank Group classifies low-income economies as those with a Gross National Income (GNI) per capita of $1,145 or less in 2023 and lower-middle-income economies with a GNI per capita between $1,146 and $4,515 [10]. Despite minimal contributions to greenhouse gases and other habits that contribute to climate change, LMICs often take on most of its impacts, which can be attributed to social vulnerability, geographic vulnerability, adaptive capacity, conflicts, poor economics, resource availability, technology, manpower, and more [11]. The combination of these factors also decreases the ability to efficiently and effectively maintain the proper functioning of health systems under the pressure of climate change.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) operational framework for building climate resilient health systems published in 2015 [12], emergency (disaster) preparedness and management is one of ten elemental components to protect health system in the face of climate change, especially for the least developed countries (LDCs) or LMICs (See Fig. 1). As it is said, “climate-informed preparedness plans, emergency systems, and community-based disaster and emergency management are essential for building climate resilience” [12]. In 2019, the WHO published an Emergency and Disaster Risk Management (EDRM) for Health Policy Framework to consolidate approaches and practices of emergency preparedness for health systems to follow. The functions of this framework are policies, strategies, legislation, planning and coordination, human resources, financial resources, information and knowledge management, risk communications, health infrastructure and logistics, health and related services, community capacities for health EDRM, and monitoring and evaluation.

While frameworks like this exist and help guide nations, little data exists that evaluates the current use of measures of emergency preparedness and management among health systems as they relate to climate change and their success, highlighting a need for an analysis of current progress. Furthermore, current research, such as the review from Braithwaite and colleagues [13], focuses more on high-income economies, highlighting the need for a cohesive review of disaster preparedness in low and lower-middle-income countries. Therefore, we aimed to examine the effects of climate change on health systems and health system disaster preparedness in LMICs, with the specific objectives as follows:

Examine empirical evidence on disaster preparedness of health systems in LMICs to identify common components of disaster preparedness.Identify common challenges and the existing gaps in preparedness strategies.Provide recommendations for strengthening disaster preparedness in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design

This systematic mapping review followed the preferred items for systematic reviews (PRISMA) [14] to properly report how the review was conducted and to ensure credibility and reliability. The protocol was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework [15] (10.17605/OSF.IO/9PKBS).

Data sources

Four major electronic publication databases were searched in March 2025, including PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science. The combination of these databases ensured comprehensive coverage across biomedical, public health, and social science disciplines. An initial search was conducted on each database to ensure the relevance of the topic and refine the search strategy. The search was conducted using a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to “disaster planning,” “climate change,” “resilience,” and “resource-limited settings.” The search strategy was tailored to each database, and final search strings were recorded to ensure the reproducibility of the search.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were empirical studies published in peer-reviewed journals from 2000, written in English, conducted on human populations, and with a focus on disaster management related to climate change in limited resource healthcare systems. Limited resource settings were identified as LMICs as defined by the World Bank Group [10].

Exclusion criteria were applied to studies that focused on disaster management not related to climate change (i.e., terrorism, bioterrorism, man-made disasters) or on health systems not in limited resource settings (upper-middle- or high-income countries). Non-empirical studies, such as commentaries, editorials, review articles, or theoretical papers, were excluded. Papers that were not available in English or were based on non-human research were also excluded from the review.

Screening process

A two-part search strategy was used to identify eligible studies. First, databases were searched for published studies using a string of preselected keywords and phrases. All initial search results were imported into EndNote, a reference management software, where duplicates were removed. Then, references were transferred to another reference management software, RAYYAN [16], which was used to organize and facilitate the screening process. Two researchers (KM, AP) independently conducted the initial screening based on titles and abstracts, removing studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. After title and abstract screening, the full texts of potentially relevant articles were reviewed for eligibility as defined by the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. This multi-step process ensured all eligible studies were systematically identified and included in the final analysis.

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out using a Microsoft Excel sheet designed to capture key findings and characteristics. The following information was extracted from each included study: citation, title, abstract, keywords, journal, publisher, DOI, publication year, country/region, income classification, disaster discussed, tool used or developed, study population, methods, qualitative data gathered, quantitative data gathered, methods/measure, key vulnerabilities or stressors, disaster preparedness strategies, strategy effectiveness, limitations, guidance, and gaps. One researcher (KM) extracted data from the included studies and recorded it in a shared database. The form was reviewed by another researcher (AP) to ensure consistency and accuracy. Extracted data was used to identify common themes throughout each included paper regarding disaster preparedness strategies, challenges, and gaps.

Quality assessment

Two researchers (KM, AP) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included articles, using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) that was produced by researchers at McGill University [17] to appraise the methodological quality of studies falling in one of the following categories, qualitative, randomized controlled trials, non-randomized control trials, quantitative descriptive studies, and mixed methods studies.

The MMAT developers discourage calculating overall scores for formal appraisal [17], as a single number is not informative and conceals what study elements led to a poor overall quality. Instead, they suggest presenting details of each rating to prevent objective analysis, thus allowing researchers to explain their interpretation of study’s quality and its contribution to the topic. All questions of the MMAT were scored with “yes,” “can’t tell,” or “no.” Can’t tell was used when there was not enough information in the study to properly respond to the question. First, all articles were assessed with the screening questions to ensure they were empirical studies that could be appraised using the MMAT and could be included in the study. Then, the appropriate methodology category was chosen, and the criteria were rated using the outlined questions. For mixed methods studies, this required ratings in the qualitative and quantitative categories.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted to identify common themes found throughout each study, including the key vulnerabilities or needs within each health system, the existing strategies to enhance disaster preparedness and resilience, the suggestions provided based on study results, and the existing gaps identified. Data within each of these categories allowed for in-depth analysis of each study and for the studies to be placed in context with one another. This addressed the review’s research questions and aims, providing insight into the current state of disaster preparedness in the face of climate change among health systems in limited resource settings and highlighting areas for future progress.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Of the 518 records retrieved from the databases, 20 papers that met the inclusion criteria were entered into data extraction (see Fig. 2). As shown in Table 1, all of the included studies were carried out in nations classified as lower-middle income as outlined by the World Bank Group: India [4], Sri Lanka [3], Haiti [2], Pakistan [2], Tanzania [2], Lebanon, Nepal, Zimbabwe, Bangladesh, Solomon Islands, Vietnam, and Philippines. There was a wide range of disasters discussed, the majority being floods [18–25], earthquakes [26–28], drought [29, 30], tropical cyclone [31], and landslides [23]. The remaining papers focused on disasters in general [32–37], choosing to focus on overall resilience and preparedness.

Summary of included studies

The methodologies differed across the included studies. Ten studies used a mixed methods approach, utilizing cross-sectional surveys, focus groups, interviews, and direct observation [19, 20, 23–25, 30, 32, 34–36]. Six studies used only quantitative methods, carrying out cross-sectional surveys, retrospective data analysis, and questionnaires [18, 21, 29, 31, 33, 37]. Four studies used only qualitative methods such as qualitative descriptions, case studies, and qualitative data analysis [22, 26–28]. The study population of each study varied, but all were related to health systems. Health systems, hospitals, or relief camps were the focus of nine studies, some of which also focused on healthcare workers, which were explicitly mentioned in 12 studies. Other populations paired with health systems or responders were patients affected by the disaster (See Table 1).

Quality of the included studies

Due to the objectiveness of a single number score, the quality of studies was assessed by responses to each MMAT question or section and its overall contribution to understanding disaster preparedness of health systems in LMICs, common challenges, existing gaps in preparedness strategies, and further recommendations (Appendix 2). Nineteen of the 20 included studies were considered empirical and therefore appropriate for analysis using the MMAT, based on “yes” answers to the screening questions. While the article by Jain and colleagues [22] yielded a “can’t tell” to the screening questions, it was included because of its study characteristics and information on infrastructure issues and recommendations for collaboration between stakeholders. Seven studies [19–21, 26, 28, 29, 34] had a “yes” response in all categories of the MMAT for their respective study design, indicating high quality. Other studies ranged from having one “no” or “can’t tell” to six, and while differing in quality, they all provided relevant data that supported their inclusion.

Key findings of included studies

Workforce training

Thirteen studies highlighted the current role of the healthcare workforce, namely knowledge, training, willingness to prepare, resilience, and skills [18–20, 23–25, 27, 28, 30, 33, 34, 36, 37]. Many studies underscored the value of disaster preparedness training through simulation drills, specialized training, continuing education, and integration of emergency response into health worker curricula [18, 20, 25, 30, 34, 36, 37]. Facilities that had conducted routine training exercises, especially in flood- and cyclone-prone regions, such as India and Sri Lanka, reported faster mobilization and more effective patient triage during actual disasters.

Infrastructure capacity

Functional infrastructure was frequently discussed as foundational to preparedness. Eight studies mentioned the capacity of the health system or hospital for functional preparedness [18, 20, 21, 23, 26, 32, 35, 36]. Several studies highlight the connection between human resource capacity and the resilience of service delivery during disasters [29, 34, 36]. For example, Lebanese hospitals maintained access to portable medical equipment, and Tanzanian facilities employed backup generators and contingency supply plans [32, 35].

Stock and supply readiness

Ten studies described situations in which hospitals were prepared with adequate stock or supplies, specifically those of electricity, medical equipment, medication, and food or water [18, 20, 21, 25–27, 29, 31, 32, 35].

Communication systems

Reliable communication was essential for timely response and coordination. Four studies emphasized maintaining proper and vital communication and keeping track of information [27, 29, 31, 32]. In the midst of an emergency, health systems adapted and came up with strategies, including communication systems or coordination with other departments or medical centers [18, 19, 23, 32], patient tracking systems [27, 29, 32], and meetings to discuss plans and further action [19, 22, 23].

Coordination and partnerships

Effective disaster response was often tied to the strength of institutional coordination with external partners, such as NGOs, government agencies, and volunteer groups. In this regard, studies from Pakistan and Tanzania highlighted that partnerships enabled faster resource mobilization and improved communication across fragmented health systems [19, 23, 32, 36].

Challenges for disaster Preparation

Despite the above strengths, multiple cross-cutting barriers impeded preparedness across LMIC health systems. These include issues related to training, infrastructure, financing, and governance (Table 2).

Lack of training and drills

Most studies noted a lack of preparation among healthcare workers due to a lack of training, unawareness of disaster plans, and little to no drills conducted through the health systems. In this regard, field visits and interviews with staff in Sri Lanka revealed that only one disaster drill had been conducted, and responders were unaware of their roles during a disaster [36]. In Bangladesh, nurses reported experiential knowledge but lacked formal education on disaster preparedness [34], while in Vietnam, all study participants in health care facilities reported insufficient training [18]. It is imperative for health systems in limited resource settings to adopt general and disaster-specific training and drills and incorporate disaster-related curriculum in education to prepare the workforce for response and promote resilience.

Structural and operational infrastructure gaps

While infrastructure challenges were discussed earlier in the context of capacity, additional studies highlighted the fragility of physical infrastructure and its impact on the continuity of care [18, 19, 21–25, 31, 32]. For example, structural damage following floods [21] and cyclones [31] in India disrupted care delivery and patient safety; Facility damage shut down air-conditioning units and winds took out gas pipelines responsible for patient oxygen supply [31]. Furthermore, facilities identified equipment damage from flood waters as a root cause of dysfunction [21]. Physical infrastructure and technological readiness, including backup power, access to mobile diagnostic tools, and clear facility-level disaster plans, varied widely. A study assessing flood preparedness in Vietnam noted that in ten facilities, electricity, and communication sets, and specific treatment options were available at all, while only two had a generator [18]. Of 31 health facilities in a Sri Lankan study, only four had disaster plans [20], whereas all 24 of the hospitals evaluated in Lebanon had a formal hospital disaster and emergency preparedness plan [32]. Although some hospitals had contingency mechanisms, others lacked even basic emergency supplies, underscoring systemic vulnerability across LMICs. Such gaps reveal both structural deficiencies and a lack of contingency planning.

Funding limitations

In addition to structural weaknesses, several studies emphasized financial constraints as a critical barrier [18, 19, 21]. Insufficient and inconsistent funding was reported as a barrier to preparedness planning, personnel training, equipment procurement, and facility upgrades [18, 19, 21]. Inadequate and inconsistent funding restricted preparedness planning, particularly in relation to staff hiring, equipment purchasing, and post-disaster rehabilitation. Multiple studies stressed that without predictable funding streams, LMIC health systems cannot sustain the resilience-building measures needed for long-term adaptation.

Policy integration and governance

Several studies noted that disaster preparedness efforts were hampered by a lack of supportive policies and institutional frameworks [19–22, 28, 36]. Weak enforcement, fragmented regulations, lack of climate consideration, and poor political commitment created barriers to operationalizing preparedness strategies, especially in decentralized health systems.

Recommendations to promote disaster preparedness

Included studies also proposed recommendations to increase disaster preparedness within health systems in the future (See Table 2). The top recommendation for improving disaster preparedness is enhancing the capacity and resilience of the health workforce. Thirteen of the 20 studies highlighted a need for trained healthcare workers and integration of disaster preparedness into curricula. Several studies emphasized the importance of targeted training initiatives, including simulation-based disaster drills, integration of emergency preparedness into health education curricula, and on-the-job capacity-building programs. These efforts should be institutionalized within health systems to ensure that frontline providers are adequately equipped with the skills and confidence needed to respond effectively during crises. Promoting resilience within the healthcare workforce further supports overall health system resilience.

Another key recommendation emerging is the need for improved coordination and collaboration across sectors. Eleven studies advised international or interdisciplinary collaboration or collaboration with other health systems within the area of disasters [19–24, 26–28, 32, 37]. Health systems in resource-constrained settings frequently depend on external actors, including government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and volunteer groups, to fill operational gaps during disaster response. In this regard, studies advocated for formalized coordination frameworks and resource-sharing agreements to streamline responses and enhance system cohesion [19, 23]. As highlighted by Ashraf et al. [19] and Kiremeji et al. [23], multisectoral collaboration is essential for overcoming systemic challenges. The development of formal coordination mechanisms and contingency-based resource-sharing frameworks is recognized as essential for enhancing health system resilience.

Aligned with existing literature [38], a critical recommendation emerging from this review is the need to strengthen research and evaluation capacity within LMIC health systems. Although various disaster preparedness initiatives have been implemented, most included studies lacked standardized frameworks for evaluating their effectiveness. This absence of consistent methodological approaches limits the ability to assess long-term impacts or replicate successful strategies across contexts. For example, while Koka et al. [35] and Al-Hajj et al. [32] described the availability of backup generators and critical equipment during disasters, these accounts did not include structured outcome evaluations or follow-up assessments to gauge their effectiveness. Similarly, Younos et al. [34] conducted assessments of healthcare workers’ preparedness but did not utilize standardized evaluation frameworks, limiting the comparability and scalability of findings. Even studies such as Munasinghe and Matsui [36], which reported on hospital-level drills and emergency protocols, lacked pre- and post-evaluation comparisons to establish efficacy. These gaps underscore the importance of adopting unified metrics, longitudinal designs, and mixed-methods approaches in future research. Moreover, building capacity through training in evaluation methodologies and improving access to research infrastructure are essential steps toward generating a more robust and actionable evidence base to guide disaster preparedness in LMICs.

In parallel, there is a pressing need to integrate climate resilience into national and sub-national health policy frameworks. Although the health consequences of climate-related disasters are increasingly evident, policy responses in many LMICs remain fragmented or reactive. Several included studies highlighted the disconnect between facility-level preparedness and overarching governance mechanisms. For instance, Phalkey et al. [21] reported major disruptions in service delivery following floods in rural India, largely due to infrastructure vulnerabilities, yet no systemic policy response was implemented. Similarly, Kiremeji et al. [23] and Burnweit and Stylianos [27] described ad hoc adaptive strategies during emergencies in Tanzania and Haiti, respectively, which were developed in the absence of formal policy guidance or institutionalized frameworks.

Discussion

This study offers a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge and future directions for disaster preparedness within health systems in limited-resource settings. In contrast to prior reviews that have predominantly examined high-income countries, this review centers on LMICs, where the need for preparedness is especially urgent. These regions often face the greatest burden of climate-related health impacts yet possess limited capacity, infrastructure, and workforce to respond effectively [11, 39, 40].

It is notable that although LMICs face many challenges in disaster preparedness, some healthcare systems show resilience, thanks to flexibility and innovation during disasters. In Haiti, a field hospital implemented color-coded medical records and team-based care to sustain functionality post-earthquake [27]. Tanzania’s response to floods featured the rapid establishment of an Incident Management System to coordinate daily resource mobilization [23]. These examples highlight the importance of adaptable protocols and strategic improvisation to preserve essential services under pressure. However, formal evaluations of adaptation strategies in LMICs continue to be constrained by limited funding, short timeframes, and the unpredictability of climate hazards [41, 42], which in turn, hinder the formulation of evidence-based policy and scalable strategies.

Funding is a persistent challenge for LMICs. Health systems in LMICs often lack sufficient financial resources to implement preparedness activities on a scale. Increasing funding could facilitate the procurement of portable medical technologies, ensure adequate staffing, and improve supply chain security. However, financial investment alone is insufficient and unsustainable. Funding must be strategically allocated to support capacity-building, infrastructure enhancement, and ongoing research. Unfortunately, in some cases, recent funding cuts have further constrained already limited health systems. One proposed solution is for resilient health systems in high-income countries, such as those in Switzerland, Japan, or Germany, to share resources or provide targeted funding to support sustainable preparedness initiatives in LMICs [43].

Our review also reveals substantial gaps in disaster preparedness research within LMIC health systems. First, while studies reported adaptive strategies (e.g., emergency drills, contingency plans, or structural adaptations), these efforts were seldom embedded within formally designed or evaluated interventions. Most studies emphasized short-term activities, such as initial implementation or capacity assessments, but lacked outcome tracking, standardized performance metrics, or longitudinal follow-up. This limitation constrains the ability to assess long-term effectiveness or scalability across contexts. Only a few studies, such as Patnaik et al. [25], and Farley et al. [20], attempted structured assessments of preparedness activities, but even these were limited in scope or evaluation depth.

Another consideration not explored in this review is the role of climate change in the migration of health professionals, causing a migration of workers from LMICs to High income countries (HIC). HICs such as the United States and the United Kingdom are known to recruit skilled health professionals from LMICs to make up for an inadequate workforce [44]. In some HICs, international medical graduates make up between 25% and 32% of the doctoral workforce, with higher percentages among certain specialties [45]. This migration has had adverse effects on health service delivery in LMICs, which combined with adverse effects of climate change, places further strain on the health systems [44]. In the context of this review, a depleted workforce may have contributed to some of the shortfalls mentioned in several studies and could be remedied by further workforce training in HICs to mitigate the need for international healthcare workers.

Methodologically, the literature is dominated by cross-sectional studies and small sample sizes. Given that most of the participants in the studies were healthcare workers or administrators, very few studies incorporated the perspectives of patients, families, or community members [19, 22, 24]. The absence of patient and community voices restricts our understanding of context-specific vulnerabilities and undercut efforts to create responsive and inclusive preparedness plans. Additionally, while disasters disproportionately affect marginalized groups (e.g., women, children, rural populations), few studies explicitly analyzed preparedness through an equity lens [19, 30, 31]. The lack of disaggregated data or targeted interventions was a notable gap across the literature. Finally, inconsistencies in reporting, limited geographic diversity, and a lack of longitudinal follow-up undermine the ability to draw generalized conclusions or inform policy development.

There are some limitations of note for this review. First, although this study intentionally focused on LMICs, the geographic distribution of included studies was uneven, with many clustered in South and Southeast Asia and fewer from regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, or small island nations. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the range of disaster preparedness challenges and strategies across all LMIC contexts. Second, the included studies were published empirical studies in English, and there was no analysis of grey literature, such as reports or reviews, or research published in another language, which would provide further information and insights into current strategies. Third, caution is warranted in interpreting the findings given the variability in the quality of the included studies. Several studies scored relatively low in quality assessments, which may affect the reliability and robustness of the synthesized evidence.

Future studies should elaborate by including grey literature and diving more in-depth into the preparedness of specific health system factors, like the workforce. Additionally, future studies should prioritize methodological rigor by employing robust designs (e.g., mixed-methods design), standardized evaluation tools, and unified indicators of disaster preparedness and resilience. It is also suggested to employ diverse stakeholder perspectives, especially those of patients and marginalized communities, and employ standardized metrics to assess preparedness and resilience more comprehensively over time.

Conclusion

This mapping review critically examined the current state of disaster preparedness in limited-resource health systems in the context of climate change. By identifying common components, challenges, and gaps of disaster preparedness based on empirical evidence from 20 included studies, we first offer actionable insights into preparedness components such as workforce capacity, infrastructure, coordination, and policy integration. To address our second aim, we found while examples of promising practices exist, major challenges exist in training, infrastructure, collaboration, and effective evaluation, which revealed the need for further research in these areas. Finally, addressing our last aim, we suggested that strengthening health system resilience will require sustained investment, inclusive policymaking, and multisectoral collaboration. Prioritizing disaster preparedness in LMICs is not only a global health imperative, but also a necessary step toward climate adaptation and sustainable development.
