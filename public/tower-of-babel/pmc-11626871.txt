Unraveling the mechanisms underlying diabetic cataracts: insights from Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Wenlan Liu, Yiming Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDiabetic cataract (DC) is a major cause of blindness, with its pathogenesis involving oxidative stress and ferroptosis, according to recent studies.MethodsWe performed a Mendelian Randomization (MR) study using GWAS data to select SNPs and assess the causal link between diabetes and cataracts. DC datasets were analyzed for differential gene expression, WGCNA, and protein-protein interactions to identify key oxidative stress and ferroptosis genes. An SVM-RFE algorithm developed a diagnostic model, and ImmuCellAI analyzed immune infiltration patterns.ResultsMR analysis confirmed diabetes as a cataract risk factor and identified core genes related to oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC. Four key genes (Hspa5/Nfe2l2/Atf3/Stat3) linked to both processes were discovered. Immune infiltration analysis revealed an imbalance associated with these genes.ConclusionsA functional interaction between oxidative stress and ferroptosis genes in DC is suggested, with a 4-gene model, indicating their potential as a ‘bridge' in DC pathogenesis.KEYWORDS: Diabetic cataract, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, immune infiltration, Mendelian randomization, Hspa5, Nfe2l2, Atf3

Introduction

Diabetic cataract (DC) is a complication closely associated with diabetes, characterized by cloudy lenses that result in impaired vision [1–3]. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the development of DC remain poorly understood [1–3]. Previous studies have indicated that oxidative stress and cell death play important roles in diabetes and cataracts, yet the relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC requires further investigation [4].

Oxidative stress refers to the process in which reactive oxygen species, such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, are generated within cells [5–7]. Excessive oxidative stress leads to cellular oxidative damage, consequently triggering cell death [8–10]. Meanwhile, iron ions also play a significant role in oxidative stress reactions [11–13]. Recent studies have shed light on a novel form of cell death called ferroptosis. We hypothesize that there may be an interaction between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in the occurrence and progression of DC.

To investigate the relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC, we first collected relevant data associated with diabetes and cataracts from a genome-wide association study database and selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) highly correlated with diabetes [14–16]. Subsequently, through Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis, we assessed the causal relationship between diabetes and cataracts [17, 18]. In addition, we downloaded datasets related to DC from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, performed differential expression analysis and WGCNA, and conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis.

Using protein–protein interaction analysis, we identified key genes related to oxidative stress and ferroptosis and constructed a diagnostic model for DC using the SVM-RFE algorithm [19–21]. Moreover, with the aid of the ImmCellAI tool, we investigated the immune infiltration patterns in DC and analyzed the relationship between key genes related to oxidative stress and ferroptosis and immune infiltration.

In summary, our study aims to explore the relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in lens epithelial cells in DC and elucidate their roles in the pathological mechanisms of DC. The findings of this study hold promise in enhancing our understanding of DC, providing new scientific evidence and clinical significance for its treatment and prevention. By studying genes related to DC, we hope to offer practical and effective therapeutic strategies and new diagnostic methods to reduce the impact of DC on patients’ quality of life.

Materials and methods

MR analysis

To investigate the association between diabetes and cataracts, SNPs were downloaded from the GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The basic information of the dataset can be found in Table S1. Since all the data used are in the public domain, no additional ethical approval was required.

The ‘gwasglue’ package in R was used to perform correlation analysis on the exposure variable dataset, with a significance threshold set at P < 5e-05. SNPs that were strongly associated with the exposure factors were selected as instrumental variables.

To address potential linkage disequilibrium, the ‘TwoSampleMR’ package in R was applied with the following parameters: kb = 10000 and r2 = 0.001. The F statistic was calculated to validate these SNPs as strong instrumental variables (F > 10). Outlying SNPs were removed using the MR-PRESSO (MR-pleiotropy residual sum and outlier) methodology. The primary MR method used was inverse variance weighted (IVW).

To further control for potential pleiotropy and heterogeneity, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods were employed. Cochran's Q test and MR-Egger intercept were used to test for heterogeneity and pleiotropic effects. A significance level of P > 0.05 indicates no statistically significant pleiotropy or heterogeneity.

Lastly, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the dependence or bias of causal relationships on any single SNP [22]. The flowchart of the analysis process was provided in Figure S1.

Transcriptome data download

Transcriptome data sets related to DC were downloaded from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The data sets included GSE135000, GSE186248, GSE194074, and GSE223322. Table S2 provides detailed information about these data sets. To remove batch effects, the four data sets were merged, and the ComBat function from the ‘SVA’ package in R software was applied. Differential expression genes (DEG) were filtered with the threshold set at |log2FC| > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05, using the ‘limma’ package in R software. The expression of DEGs was visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps, generated using the ‘ggplot2’ package and ‘pheatmap’ package in R software, respectively. The analysis workflow was depicted in Figure S2 [23, 24].

WGCNA

WGCNA is a systematic biology method that can reveal gene correlation patterns among different samples and identify candidate biomarkers or therapeutic targets. The relationships between gene sets and phenotypes, as well as the interplay between different gene sets, play a crucial role. First, we performed a WGCNA analysis using the R package ‘WGCNA’ by selecting the top 25% of genes with the highest expression variance. Subsequently, the samples were clustered using the Hclust function, and an appropriate soft threshold β was selected using the ‘pickSoftThreshold’ function. The adjacency matrix was then transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Finally, module eigengenes (MEs) were obtained for different modules, and their correlation with phenotypes was calculated. We selected the DC-related module with the highest correlation coefficient and P < 0.05 and defined genes that met the criteria of gene significance (GS) > 0.5 and module membership (MM) > 0.8 as DC-related genes [25].

Screening of candidate target genes

We retrieved 646 genes from the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using the keyword ‘oxidative stress,’ identified as relevant with a Relevance score ≥ 10 and downloaded a list of 484 ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/). Using the ‘VennDiagram’ package in R language, we then obtained the intersection between DEGs and key module genes from WGCNA with oxidative stress-related genes (ORGs) and FRGs separately, identifying ORGs and FRGs that were associated with DC [24].

Gene functional enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis was performed using the ‘ClusterProfiler’ package in R. This analysis encompassed the examination of Biological Processes (BP), Molecular Functions (MF), and Cellular Components (CC), with the resulting GO enrichment results displayed as a bubble plot. KEGG enrichment analysis was carried out using the ‘ClusterProfiler’ package in R to analyze the candidate targets, and the results were visualized as a bubble plot. An FDR value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant [23].

PPI

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was utilized for PPI analysis, and the resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.8.2. To identify dense regions within the network, the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape was employed with parameter settings based on topological principles [23].

Identification of optimal diagnostic gene biomarkers for DC

To further identify characteristic genes, we performed Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis using the ‘e1071’ package in R language. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculation of the Area Under the Curve values for each selected feature gene were conducted using the ‘pROC’ package in R language to evaluate their predictive ability in DC diagnosis [26].

Immune infiltration analysis

The immune cell abundance identifier database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/ImmuCellAI) was utilized to assess the abundance of 36 immune cell types based on gene expression profiles in RNA-Seq or microarray data. Normalized gene expression data were uploaded to ImmuCellAI for immune infiltration analysis. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and correlation analysis was conducted using the Spearman method [23].

Establishment of a DC mouse model

Twenty male BALB/c mice aged 6-8 weeks, weighing 18–22 g, were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (http://www.cavens.com.cn). All mice were housed in SPF-grade animal facilities at a temperature of (23 ± 1)°C, with a humidity of (55 ± 5%) and a standard 12-hour light–dark cycle. The mice were randomly divided into a Control group and a DC model group (DC group). Prior to model establishment, blood glucose levels (fasting) were determined in both Control and model group mice by tail vein and abdominal vein blood sampling using a glucometer (U80, Guilin Leadt Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China). The model group mice were intraperitoneally injected with STZ (50 mg/kg, S0130, Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA), while the Control group mice received an equivalent amount of normal saline. Following standard feeding for 12 weeks, blood samples from fasting Control and model mice were collected via the tail vein for glucose measurement. The mice received ocular instillation of a 0.1 g/l tropicamide solution (HY-B0321, MCE) and were anesthetized with ether for observation of lens changes under a slit lamp. Lens opacity was graded as follows: Grade I, clear and transparent lens without opacities; Grade II, slightly opaque lens with few fluid bubbles; Grade III, moderately opaque lens with bubbles extending to the central area, and opacities appearing in the nucleus; Grade IV, highly opaque lens with bubbles extending to the nuclear area, haze, and increased opacity in the nuclear lens; Grade V, opacity leading to mature cataract formation in the nuclear lens. Results showed successful establishment of the DC model with blood glucose levels ≥14.0 mmol/l and lens opacity. Comparison between surviving diabetic mice and Control mice involved intraperitoneal injection of 1–2 ml of 1% sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, which caused the death of two individuals due to cervical dislocation. Both eyeballs were extracted under a surgical microscope (XSP-36; Shenzhen Boshida Optical Instrument Co., Ltd, China). The eyeballs were then immersed three times in 250 U/ml of saline solution (each time for 10 min) under sterile conditions, followed by cornea and iris removal, lens extraction, and rinsing with saline solution. The lens capsule was retrieved, stored in a 1.5 ml Ep freezing medium, and preserved in a – 80°C liquid nitrogen tank. All experimental procedures and animal protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xi'an Medical University [27].

Detection of oxidative stress levels

Tissue samples were collected and processed for dispersion. Following centrifugation, the supernatant treated with lysis buffer was collected, and the levels of reduced glutathione (GSH, A006-1-1) and reactive oxygen species (ROS, E004-1-1) were quantified using the methods provided by the reagent kit supplier (Nanjing Jiancheng) [28].

Cellular iron death levels

The Iron Assay Kit (ab83366, Abcam, UK) was utilized to measure the Fe2 + levels in tissues. The tested tissues were washed with cold PBS, lysed in the iron detection buffer, centrifuged at 16000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. An iron-reducing agent was added to both samples and standards, followed by a 30-minute incubation at 37°C. Subsequently, an iron probe was introduced, and incubation was continued for 60 min at 37°C under dark conditions. The output value of OD at 593 nm was determined using a microplate reader [28].

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentration and purity of the extracted total RNA were assessed using the Nanodrop2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (1011U, Nanodrop, USA, [https://www.thermofisher.cn/cn/zh/home.html]). Following the instructions of the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan, [https://www.takarabiomed.com.cn/]), RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA. The reverse transcription conditions were as follows: 37°C for 30–50 min and 85°C for 5 s. RT-qPCR was conducted using the Fast SYBR Green PCR kit (RR820A, Takara, Japan) with the ABI PRISM 7300 RT–PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 57°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. Each well included 3 replicates, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The relative gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, where ΔΔCt = (average Ct value of target gene in experimental group – average Ct value of housekeeping gene in experimental group) - (average Ct value of target gene in control group - average Ct value of housekeeping gene in control group). The experiment was repeated three times. The primers were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Company (Table 1). [29]

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF (Beyotime, P0013C) by incubating on ice for 30 min at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 8000 g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (ThermoFisher, 23227). Subsequently, 50 μg of protein was dissolved in 2× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 100°C for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to a PVDF membrane (ThermoFisher).

The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature and probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against COX2 (ab179800, Abcam), ACSL4 (ab155282, Abcam), NOX1 (ab131088, Abcam), GPX4 (ab131088, Abcam), Atf3 (ab254268, Abcam), Hspa5 (ab21685, Abcam), Nfe2l2 (#12721, CST), Stat3 (ab68153, Abcam), and GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam). After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L, 1:2000, ab97051/ab6728, Abcam) for 1 h.

Following TBST rinsing, ECL detection reagents (abs920, Absin Biotechnology) were applied to the membrane, which was then imaged using a Bio-Rad imaging system. Protein bands were analyzed using Quantity One v4.6.2 software, and the relative protein content was expressed as the grayscale value normalized to β-actin. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and average values were calculated [30].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using R software v4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcox test. Continuous data were presented as Mean ± SD. Non-paired two-tailed t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and One-way ANOVA analysis was used for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by post hoc testing using Tukey's method. Statistical significance was considered at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Diabetes as a confirmed risk factor for cataract development: evidence from MR analysis

MR analysis is an effective method that utilizes genetic variations as instrumental variables (IV) to study causal effects related to exposure and outcomes, thereby avoiding bias caused by confounding variables and reverse causation [31, 32]. To investigate the causal relationship between diabetes and cataracts, we conducted an association analysis using the ‘gwasglue’ package (P < 5e-08), resulting in 5967 SNPs related to diabetes being used as instrumental variables (Figure S3A-B). Additionally, after excluding SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (kb = 10000 and r2 = 0.001) and F-statistics lower than 10, we had 118 remaining SNPs for the subsequent MR analysis (Table S3).

No biased SNPs were detected in the MR-PRESSO test. According to the results of the IVW analysis, diabetes was identified as a risk factor for cataract development. Other MR methods also yielded similar but statistically insignificant results compared to IVW, except for the MR Egger method (Figure 1A-C, Table S4). To assess the robustness and reliability of these findings, we performed Cochran's Q test, MR-Egger intercept test, and Leave-one-out analysis. The P-value of Cochran's Q test was larger than 0.05, indicating no heterogeneity detected (Table S5). Likewise, the P-value of the MR-Egger intercept test was greater than 0.05, suggesting the absence of horizontal pleiotropy (Table S6). The symmetry of the funnel plot further confirmed the absence of heterogeneity (Figure 1D). Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated that the removal of any SNP did not significantly affect the results (Figure 1E).

In conclusion, our MR analysis revealed a causal relationship between diabetes and cataract development, with diabetes identified as a risk factor for cataract occurrence.

The crucial roles and functional interactions of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC

The reciprocal relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis has been observed in various human diseases, but its significance in DC remains unclear. We downloaded four transcription datasets related to DC from the GEO database, and after batch correction, we obtained an integrated dataset consisting of five control samples and thirteen DC samples. As shown in Figure 2A-B, the differences between the four datasets significantly decreased after removing the batch effects. Differential analysis between the merged control samples and DC samples identified 1843 DEGs, including 1055 upregulated genes and 788 downregulated genes (Figure 2C-D).

Furthermore, we intersected the DEGs and the key genes in the modules with ORG and FRG, resulting in the identification of 60 ORGs and 54 FRGs associated with DC (Figure S4A-B). Additionally, the Wilcoxon test was conducted to analyze the differences between these genes in the control and DC samples, excluding nine genes that did not exhibit significant differences. The remaining 57 ORGs and 48 FRGs were used for subsequent analysis (Figure S4C-D).

Next, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to explore the functions of these genes. In terms of ORGs, significant GO functions included responses to oxidative stress, aging, mitochondrial function, and oxidoreductase activity, among others (Figure S4E). Significant KEGG pathways included the HIF-1 signaling pathway, apoptosis, and ferroptosis (Figure S4F). For FRGs, GO analysis revealed functions such as reactive oxygen species metabolism, cell response to oxidative stress, heme binding, and trivalent iron binding (Figure S4G). KEGG analysis indicated enrichment in pathways related to cell death, aging, and oxygen metabolism regulation (Figure S4H).

Overall, these results demonstrate the important roles of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC and indicate a certain degree of functional overlap between DC-associated ORGs and FRGs.

A gene diagnostic model based on the PPI network and key genes demonstrates effective diagnosis for DC

To reveal the potential pathogenic genes and their mechanisms in DC, we analyzed the PPI of 57 ORGs and 48 FRGs using the STRING database, visualizing the results with Cytoscape (Figure 4A and C). Subsequently, we utilized the Cytoscape plugin MCODE to identify important modules, resulting in the identification of two gene modules from the PPI networks of ORGs and FRGs, each containing 20 core ORGs and 12 core FRGs (Figure 4B and D).

To construct the gene diagnostic model, we employed the SVM-RFE algorithm to identify key genes with the strongest predictive ability for DC onset. For better diagnostic and predictive performance, we constructed a column plot based on these four key genes (Figure 5D). We applied ROC curve analysis to evaluate the AUC values of each gene, determining their sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing DC. The crucial role of ORFG in the regulation of immune infiltration in DC samples

To determine the key immune cells in which ORFG plays a crucial role within DCs, we utilized the ImmuCellAI algorithm to analyze the infiltration of 36 different immune cell types (Figure 6A). We observed seven significantly different immune cell types between the control and DC groups. Specifically, Germinal_center_B, CD8_Tc, and CD8_Tcm were found to be more abundant in the DC group, while NKT, Marginal_Zone_B, cDC2, and Basophil were more abundant in the control group (Figure 6B). Furthermore, we performed a Spearman analysis to investigate the potential associations between ORFG and immune cells. Our results showed a positive correlation between Atf3 and M1_macrophage, while a negative correlation was observed with Marginal_Zone_B and Naive_CD4_T. Moreover, a positive correlation was found between Hspa5 and MoDC, and a positive correlation between Nfe2l2 and M1_macrophage, with a negative correlation observed with CD8_Tem, CD8_Tex, and cDC2. Additionally, Stat3 showed a positive correlation with Neutrophils but a negative correlation with Monocytes (Figure 6C). Taken together, these findings indicate that ORFG may play a crucial role in the regulation of immune infiltration in DC samples.

Elevated expression of ATF3 and STAT3 in DCs

The bioinformatics analysis revealed that four key genes, namely Atf3, Hspa5, Nfe2l2, and Stat3, known as Oxidative Stress and Iron Death-Related Genes (ORFG), showed high expression in DCs. To further confirm the expression differences of these factors in DCs and the status of oxidative stress and iron death in the crystalline lens (CL), we established a DC mouse model. qRT-PCR and Western blot experiments were conducted to assess the expression of Atf3, Hspa5, Nfe2l2, and Stat3 in the CL of each group of mice. Results indicated a significant increase in Atf3 and Stat3 expression in the DC group compared to the Control group, while no significant difference was observed in Hspa5 and Nfe2l2 expression (Figure 7AB).

By measuring ROS and GSH levels in the mouse CL using assay kits, we found that compared to the Control group, the DC group exhibited increased ROS levels and a significant decrease in GSH content (Figure 7CD).

Western blot experiments were performed to evaluate the expression of iron death marker proteins in the CL of each mouse group. The results showed a significant increase in the expression of COX2, ACSL4, and NOX1 proteins in the DC group compared to the Control group, while the expression of GPX4 was significantly reduced (Figure 7E). These findings indicate that Atf3 and Stat3 are highly expressed in DCs and are associated with oxidative stress and iron death.

Discussion

DC is one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide, yet its pathogenesis remains unclear [33, 34]. The aim of this study was to explore the relationship between lens epithelial cell oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC. Previous research has primarily focused on the genetic factors of DC, the relationship between diabetes and cataracts, and the role of immune abnormalities. However, this study provides an in-depth investigation into the potential role of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DC [35–38].

Compared to previous studies, significant differences exist in the study subjects and methods employed in this research [39]. While prior studies have mainly focused on the impact of single gene mutations, this study utilizes a comprehensive analytical approach. GWAS data were used to identify SNPs strongly associated with diabetes, and MR analysis was employed to assess the causality between diabetes and cataracts. This approach provides more accurate and comprehensive results for the study [40, 41].

Differential expression analysis and WGCNA were employed in this study to identify 20 core ORGs and 12 core FRGs related to DC using the DC-related dataset from the GEO database [42]. Compared to previous research, this study incorporates integrated data analysis methods to obtain a more comprehensive and accurate collection of differential genes, thus aiding in the unraveling of the pathogenesis of DC in greater detail [43].

By employing protein–protein interaction analysis, key genes associated with oxidative stress and ferroptosis were identified, and a DC diagnostic model comprising four ORFGs was constructed using the SVM-RFE machine learning method [44–46]. The model's performance was validated and demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity [47–49]. In contrast to prior studies, this research integrates multiple analytical methods, ranging from genomics to bioinformatics, to thoroughly investigate the significant roles of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of DC [50–52].

This study further analyzed the immune infiltration patterns in DC and found a significant correlation between Hspa5/Nfe2l2/Atf3/Stat3 and immune infiltration. This suggests that the pathogenesis of DC is not only limited to oxidative stress and ferroptosis but also involves immune dysregulation. Hspa5, usually located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), translocates to the cell surface, mitochondria, and nucleus when ER is under stress to form complexes with other proteins to carry out its functions [53]. At the cell surface, Hspa5 plays diverse roles, including influencing cell survival, proliferation, apoptosis, and adhesion, as well as regulating innate and adaptive immunity [54]. Moreover, reports suggest that Hspa5 is highly expressed in adipose tissue, with increased expression observed in older individuals, as well as in obese and diabetic humans and mice [55]. Nfe2l2, an essential protein associated with ferroptosis, interacts with its partner protein KEAP1, a thiol-rich protein containing numerous cysteine residues, some of which are adjacent to basic amino acids, enhancing the activity of thiolate anion. Under basal conditions, KEAP1 suppresses Nfe2l2 activity, while Nfe2l2 is released from KEAP1-mediated inhibition upon exposure to stress. The KEAP1-Nfe2l2 system serves as a cysteine-based sensor-effector system and plays a pivotal role in mounting adaptive responses [56]. Studies have also indicated that mice with Nfe2l2 knockout exhibit significant elevation in oxidative stress markers 4-HNE, autophagy markers p62/SQSTM1, Beclin-1, and LC, as well as protein aggregation marker ubiquitin, suggesting oxidative stress, impaired proteostasis, and compromised autophagic clearance, leading to oxidative stress-induced retinal pigment epithelium damage in age-related macular degeneration [57]. Aberrant expression of Nfe2l2 under diabetic conditions may similarly induce oxidative stress, impaired proteostasis, and compromised autophagic clearance, potentially contributing to cataract formation. Atf3, a stress-inducible transcription factor, plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism, immunity, and tumorigenesis [58]. Intriguingly, as a transcription factor, Atf3 can modulate the expression of Nfe2l2 [59, 60]. Stat3, a transcriptional regulatory factor, plays a critical role in vertebrate development and tissue function, including controlling inflammation and immunity [61]. Different subcellular localization patterns of Stat3 impact autophagy in various ways. For instance, nuclear Stat3 modulates autophagy by transcriptionally regulating several autophagy-related genes such as BCL2 family member BECN1, PIK3C3, CTSB, CTSL, PIK3R1, HIF1A, BNIP3, and microRNAs with autophagy-regulatory target sites, fine-tuning autophagy. Cytoplasmic Stat3 inhibits autophagy by sequestering EIF2AK2 and interacting with other autophagy-related signaling molecules (e.g. FOXO1 and FOXO3), constitutively suppressing autophagy. Additionally, mitochondrial translocation of STAT3 suppresses oxidative stress-induced autophagy, potentially protecting mitochondria from degradation by mitophagy [62]. These key genes may promote the development of DC by causing abnormalities in immune regulation. In comparison to previous studies, our research provides new theoretical and experimental evidence for the immunotherapy of DC by deeply analyzing the association between key genes and immune infiltration [63, 64].

In conclusion, this study investigated the relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in lens epithelial cells in the context of DC using MR analysis and transcriptome data from public databases. We confirmed the important roles of ferroptosis and oxidative stress in DC and proposed a 4-gene model based on Hspa5/Nfe2l2/Atf3/Stat3 that acts as a ‘bridge’ linking oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and DC. This model enriches the pathophysiological understanding of this disease.

The main findings of this study break new ground in understanding the relationship between diabetes and cataracts, as well as the roles of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of DC. These findings have significant scientific and clinical value in further elucidating the pathogenesis of DC, improving diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, and providing new directions and targets for its prevention and treatment.

Despite significant achievements in bioinformatics analysis, this study has several limitations. Firstly, it lacks empirical evidence to support the conclusions. Future research could validate the role of key genes identified in this study through clinical and cellular experiments, as well as explore the feasibility of intervention strategies. Secondly, the study did not utilize another known disease, such as diabetic nephropathy, to validate these findings and relate them to cataracts. Including such validations in future research could provide more robust evidence for the causal relationship between diabetes and cataracts. In conclusion, this study offers new clues and insights for investigating and treating DC, laying a foundation for unraveling its complex pathogenesis and developing more effective interventions.

In summary, this study provides an in-depth investigation into the roles of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of DC, revealing their importance in DC (Figure 8). These findings not only enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of DC but also provide new directions and targets for early diagnosis and treatment with significant scientific and clinical value. However, despite the relatively comprehensive and accurate methods and results of this study, there are still data limitations and constraints that require further research to validate the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Future studies should continue exploring multiple aspects of the pathogenesis of DC and combining experimental results with clinical practice to provide more effective strategies for the prevention and treatment of DC.
