Insular monoaminergic deficits in prodromal α‐synucleinopathies
Authors: Andrea Pilotto, Alice Galli, Cinzia Zatti, Fabio Placidi, Francesca Izzi, Enrico Premi, Silvia P Caminiti, Luca Presotto, Andrea Rizzardi, Marcello Catania, Alessandro Lupini, Leandro Purin, Maria P Pasolini, Nicola B Mercuri, Agostino Chiaravalotti, Mariana Fernandes, Carmen Calvello, Silvia Lucchini, Francesco Bertagna, Barbara Paghera, Daniela Perani, Daniela Berg, Alessandro Padovani, Claudio Liguori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethodsThis study assessed data from two cohorts of patients with alpha‐synucleinopathies (University of Brescia and University of Rome Tor‐Vergata cohorts). Consecutive participants with video‐polysomnography‐confirmed iRBD, Parkinson's disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and controls underwent neurological, clinical and 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging assessments. Individuals with iRBD were longitudinally monitored to collect clinical phenoconversion to PD or DLB. The main outcome was to identify whole brain 123 I‐FP‐CIT SPECT measures reflecting monoaminergic deficits in each clinical group as compared to controls.ResultsThe cohort (n = 184) included 45 patients with iRBD, 47 PD, 42 DLB and 50 age‐matched controls. Individuals with iRBD were categorized as RBD‐DAT− (n = 32) and RBD‐DAT+ (n = 13), according to nigrostriatal assessment used in clinical practice. Compared to controls, RBD‐DAT− showed an early involvement of the left insula, which increased in RBD‐DAT+, and was present in patients with Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. Longitudinal cox regression analyses revealed a higher risk of phenoconversion in individuals with iRBD and insular monoaminergic deficits [HR = 3.387; CI 95%: 1.18–10.27].InterpretationIn this study, altered insular monoaminergic binding in iRBD was associated with phenoconversion to DLB or PD. These findings may provide a helpful stratification approach for future pharmacological or non‐pharmacological interventions.

Introduction

Identifying in vivo markers that reflect risk for phenoconversion to clinically evident alpha‐synucleinopathies remains an unmet need. Alpha‐synucleinopathies include a spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders characterized by neuronal loss caused by the aggregation of misfolded α‐synuclein protein leading to dopaminergic deficits. Therefore, imaging measures able to identify dopaminergic deficits within basal ganglia play a key role in the diagnostic algorithm for Parkinson's disease (PD) and Lewy bodies dementia (DLB), as recently stated in the revised versions of both clinical criteria. 1 , 2 Recently, the spectrum of alpha‐synucleinopathies has been extended to include prodromal phases, which may present with idiopathic/isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD). 3 This clinical condition, characterized by unpleasant dreams and atonia during REM sleep, has been associated with a >70% risk of phenoconversion to a clinically evident alpha‐synucleinopathy (i.e. PD, DLB or Multiple System Atrophy) after 12 years of follow‐up. 4 Even though dopaminergic deficits within basal ganglia are present in a variable percentage of iRBD individuals, they have been associated with a greater risk of phenoconversion. 5 , 6 However, the clinical stratification at single‐subject level remains very difficult, due to the absence of detectable changes within basal ganglia, especially in very early disease stages. 7

Growing neuropathological evidence demonstrates a preferential damage of the autonomic system with a relative sparing of substantia nigra, perhaps resulting in a primary involvement of other pathways other than nigrostriatal dopaminergic network. 8

123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT is well established in clinical practice for differentiating degenerative disorders, 1 , 2 and it has been widely used also for research purposes in PD and DLB. 9 , 10 Moreover, 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging shows high affinity for both dopamine transporter (DAT) and serotonin transporter (SERT), 11 , 12 holding the potential for assessing in vivo both serotonergic and dopaminergic neurotransmitter systems—respectively involving nigrostriatal and extra‐nigrostriatal brain regions. 13 Recently, advanced molecular imaging techniques allowed the identification of extra‐nigrostriatal serotonergic and dopaminergic deficits—primary involving insula, thalamus and anterior cingulate cortex—in early stages of PD or DLB. 10 , 14 To date, however, such an assessment has not been reported in vivo in a large group of iRBD individuals. Here, we aimed to demonstrate extra‐nigrostriatal dopaminergic and/or serotonergic changes characterizing iRBD using a whole‐brain analysis. Further, we aimed at exploring the prognostic value of such changes regarding the risk of phenoconversion to clinically evident alpha‐synucleinopathy.

Methods

Participants

This prospective study enrolled a total of 195 patients from two cohorts: (i) Neurology Unit of the University of Brescia; (ii) Sleep Medicine Center of the University of Rome Tor Vergata. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Brescia Hospital, Brescia, Italy (DMA study, NP 1471) and Rome (RS: 76/14). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Individuals with vPSG‐confirmed iRBD 15 entered the study, according to the following inclusion criteria: (i) history of dream‐enacting behaviours; (ii) vPSG showing REM sleep without atonia or registration of RBD episodes; (iii) absence of known neurological condition; (iv) lack of motor and cognitive complaints, (v) normal structural brain imaging; (vi) lack of another sleep disorder and (vii) substance abuse; (vii) lack of motor or cognitive abnormalities at standard neurological examination.

Age‐matched patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) 2 or probable dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) 1 were included using the following exclusion criteria: (i) symptoms or features suggestive of atypical parkinsonism 2 ; (ii) cerebrovascular disease or significant abnormalities on structural imaging (iii) normal nigrostriatal dopaminergic imaging; (iv) bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, history of drug or alcohol abuse or impulse control disorder.

A control group (CG) similar for age was included for comparison. All CG subjects had a confirmed clinical diagnosis of isolated action tremor syndromes over a 4‐year follow‐up period and normal 123I‐FP‐CIT imaging (assessed visually and quantitatively by BRASS analysis), as in Italy DAT imaging is‐ because of the concomitant radiation—ethically only allowed and justified if there is a clinical question to be answered. 9 , 16 All CG subjects showed normal cognition as assessed with Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 17

Each participant underwent a standardized neurological examination, including the assessment of motor symptoms using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale‐ part III 18 and a global cognitive measure (using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment). 17

All iRBD subjects were longitudinally followed (mean time 39.66 ± 26.32 months) to collect information on clinical conversion to defined PD, 2 DLB, 1 multiple system atrophy (MSA) 19 or other neurological conditions.

123‐I‐FP‐CIT‐SPECT imaging procedures

Intravenous administration of 110–185 MBq of 123I‐FP‐CIT was performed 30 min after thyroid blockade (800 mg of KClO4) in all individuals. Brain SPECT acquisitions were performed 3 h after tracer administration using the Discovery 630, General Electric, Milwaukee, WI. Data were reconstructed by filtered back‐projection, with Butterworth 3‐dimensional (3D) post‐filter (order 10.0; cut‐off 0.50 cycle/cm) and corrected for attenuation (Chang's method coefficient 0.15/cm).

Antidepressant therapy (in those receiving it) was withdrawn 3 weeks before the SPECT assessment to minimize possible iatrogenic effects on nigrostriatal and extra‐nigrostriatal 123I‐FP‐CIT binding. Image assessment was performed to evaluate the integrity of basal ganglia DAT binding according to the EANM guidelines. 20 In detail, the assessment was made by two different raters blinded for the clinical diagnosis using ROIs standard cut‐off values for putamen and caudate used for clinical standard diagnosis to categorize subjects in DAT‐positive and DAT‐negative. Then, a quality check was performed for each reconstructed image. To perform further quantitative analyses, image pre‐processing and quantification were centralized at the Neurology Unit of Brescia. First, the origin coordinates of the 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT scans were manually set to the anterior commissure. Then, molecular images were spatially normalized to a high‐resolution 18F‐DOPA template (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/spmtemplates) using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). Spatial smoothing was not applied to limit blurring or spill‐over. Only for subsequent voxel‐wise analyses, pre‐processed parametric images were smoothed with an 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel to minimize the partial volume effect. 9 , 16 Finally, parametric images were generated for each subject using the Image Calculator (ImCalc) function in SPM12. In detail, specific binding ratios (SBRs) were calculated using the DAT count of the lateral superior occipital cortex as background reference region. Mean SBR values were extracted from standard anatomical regions of interest 21 using REX toolbox (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/rex/) on MATLAB v2022a to perform subsequent quantitative analyses.

Statistical analyses

Differences in demographic and clinical variables between the studied groups were assessed using one‐way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi‐squared for dichotomous variables. In longitudinal analyses, demographic, clinical and 123I‐FP‐CIT binding differences between iRBD stable and converters subjects were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA and chi‐square test for continuous and dichotomous variables, respectively.

Voxel‐wise whole brain analysis

A voxel‐wise group comparison between iRBD, PD, DLB and CG was performed on SPM12. Specifically, an ANCOVA analysis was performed to test differences in brain 123I‐FP‐CIT binding between the studied groups, without any prior assumption. Analyses were adjusted for age, sex and the site of brain SPECT acquisition. Significant threshold was set at P < 0.05, FWE‐corrected for multiple comparisons. Only clusters containing more than 100 voxels were deemed significant. For exploratory purposes, we compared iRBD converter and stable subjects to test possible differences in 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR at baseline, adjusting for age, sex, follow‐up time and site of brain SPECT acquisition. For contrasts not surviving FWE correction, the threshold was set at P < 0.001 uncorrected for multiple comparisons. Only clusters containing more than 100 voxels were considered significant.

A mean map of 123I‐FP‐CIT parametric images of CG with an uptake significantly higher than occipital binding was generated using ImCalc function on SPM12. The obtained map was used as explicit mask in voxel‐wise comparisons to select only alterations in tracer‐rich regions.

The resulting cluster obtained in the previous step in iRBD as compared to controls was transformed into a binary mask and overlaid to the spatial distribution of serotonin, noradrenalin and dopamine neurotransmitters. Further details about the neurotransmitter analysis are available in Data S1.

ROI‐based brain analysis

SBR mean values were extracted from nigrostriatal regions—known to be very rich in dopamine transporter, 22 namely putamen, globus pallidus and caudate. Additionally, extra‐nigrostriatal regions resulting from the voxel‐wise comparisons were extracted. All selected ROIs were obtained from Automated Anatomical Labels (AAL) atlas. 21 Individual mean 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR bindings in nigrostriatal and extra‐nigrostriatal regions entered an ANCOVA test to evaluate differences between the studied groups. Mean 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR of each considered ROI was considered as the dependent variable, while age, sex and sites of brain SPECT acquisition were included as covariates of nuisance.

Correlation analysis

Partial correlation analyses evaluated the relationship between SBR values in extra‐nigrostriatal regions and motor/cognitive alterations (MDS‐UPDRS‐III and MoCA scores) separately in different groups—adjusting for the effect of age and sex.

Longitudinal assessment and phenoconversion of iRBD individuals

Cox regression analyses were used to model the effect of (i) nigrostriatal and (ii) extra‐nigrostriatal on clinical conversion to PD or DLB at follow‐up. First, a Cox regression model considering the presence/absence of 123I‐FP‐CIT binding reduction within basal ganglia (i.e. putamen and caudate) was performed. Then, in order to test the prognostic role of these very‐early extra‐striatal alterations, Cox regression models were adjusted for the presence of 123I‐FP‐CIT binding reduction within basal ganglia at baseline, age and sex. Specifically, mean SBR values were extracted from the significant cluster obtained from voxel‐wise comparison. Then the obtained SBR values were categorized based on an arbitrary cut‐off set at one standard deviation below the mean of normal controls, in order to classify iRBD individuals. Analyses were confirmed with bootstrapping procedure with n = 500 samples to derive adjusted hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The cox‐regression analysis was also conducted separately for RBD‐DAT− and RBD‐DAT+ subgroups, adjusting for age at the time of image acquisition. Significance threshold P < 0.05 was established for all tests.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R foundation) and Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.

Results

The study cohort comprised 195 participants (n = 50 iRBD; n = 50 PD; n = 45 DLB), who underwent 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT and extensive clinical evaluation. A final sample of 184 participants, namely n = 45 iRBD (n = 22 BS; n = 23 RM), n = 47 PD (n = 33 BS; n = 14 RM), n = 42 DLB (n = 42 BS) were ultimately selected for analyses. Remaining n = 11 participants were excluded due to the presence of 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT image artifacts (see Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials for details about patients' inclusion). A group of n = 50 controls (n = 32 BS; n = 18 RM) were enrolled for subsequent comparisons (Table 1).

Within the 45 iRBD subjects, the clinical image assessment enabled the differentiation in 13 with abnormal (RBD‐DAT+) and 32 with normal (RBD‐DAT−) nigrostriatal imaging (see Figure S2). 20 The age distribution of the different groups was similar. However, the iRBD subgroups had a higher percentage of males than the other clinical groups (χ 2 = 14.996, P = 0.002). Sex was considered a covariate of non‐interest for all subsequent analyses. Patients with iRBD and PD had comparable global cognitive function, whereas PD and DLB patients presented by definition higher motor deficit severity compared to both the iRBD subgroups (F = 105.494, P < 0.001). Specifically, no subject with iRBD presented cognitive deficits or reached the threshold for mild parkinsonism, as defined by MDS prodromal PD criteria. 23 , 24

Voxel‐wise whole‐brain analysis

The voxel‐wise analysis revealed a selective involvement of the left insula in RBD‐DAT− as compared to CG. RBD‐DAT+ showed a considerable decline in 123I‐FP‐CIT bindings in the caudate, putamen, pallidum, bilaterally, in the left insula and right thalamus. PD showed more significantly decreased 123I‐FP‐CIT bindings in the caudate, putamen, pallidum, bilaterally and insula. Both DLB and PD showed a substantial decline in 123I‐FP‐CIT in the basal ganglia, insula and thalamus (See Table 2). The Figure 1A shows the progressive decline of nigrostriatal binding from RBD‐DAT+ to PD and DLB individuals, in comparison to controls. The voxel‐size alteration in the left insula increased from RBD‐DAT− to RBD‐DAT+ and overt α‐synucleinopathies—with similar bilateral involvement‐ in both PD and DLB (Fig. 1B).

ROI‐based brain analysis

Figure S4 indicated ROI‐based analyses for nigrostriatal binding differences among the clinical groups. PD, DLB and RBD‐DAT+ exhibited lower binding in the bilateral caudate, putamen and pallidus in comparison to HC. ROI‐based analysis has confirmed the early 123I‐FP‐CIT binding alteration in the left insula (t = 2.394, P = 0.025 after Bonferroni correction), which is progressively impaired from iRBD to PD (t = 3.795, P = 0.002 after Bonferroni correction) and DLB patients (t = 3.614, P = 0.004 after Bonferroni correction).

Correlation analysis

Partial correlations analysis showed the lack of relationship between insular binding and motor symptoms at baseline (namely MDS‐UPDRS‐III) in each clinical group (RBD‐DAT− r = −160, P = 0.466; RBD‐DAT+ r = −114, P = 0.740; PD r = −0.093, P = 0.545; DLB r = −0.066, P = 0.690). No relationship between global cognitive measure (i.e. MoCA score) and insular binding was found in the subgroups (RBD‐DAT− r = 0.81, P = 0.766; RBD‐DAT+ r = −0.255, P = 0.508; PD r = 0.111, P = 0.641) exception made for a marginal effect on DLB patients (r = 0.317, P = 0.050).

Longitudinal assessment and phenoconversion of iRBD individuals

All iRBD underwent follow‐up for an average duration of 39.66 ± 26.32 months. Over the course of the study, 15 patients developed a clinically evident alpha‐synucleinopathy with parkinsonism (mean time of conversion 26.67 ± 18.41 months) and fulfilled diagnostic criteria for Parkinson's disease (n = 5), 2 or dementia with Lewy bodies (n = 10). 1 No patients met the diagnostic criteria for multiple system atrophy at the follow‐up clinical evaluation. Compared to the stable subjects, iRBD converters were significantly older (F = 3.935, P = 0.028), but showed comparable levels of motor and global cognitive functions at baseline (Table S2). Among the whole iRBD converters, 53% showed a 123I‐FP‐CIT binding reduction within basal ganglia at baseline. Moreover, converters showed lower mean 123I‐FP‐CIT binding in bilateral insula, as compared to stable iRBD. Results are shown in Table S2.

The presence of a 123I‐FP‐CIT alteration in basal ganglia at baseline was significantly associated with an increased probability of conversion to a clinically evident alpha‐synucleinopathy with a hazard ratio of 2.959 [CI 95%: 1.06–8.236, P = 0.038] in the whole iRBD group.

To assess the effect of an extra‐nigrostriatal alteration, data‐driven cut‐off point of the 123I‐FP‐CIT insular SBR was set at 0.420, thus resulting in n = 15 RBD insula‐positive (RBD‐Ins+) subjects. Among them, n = 8 (53%) developed an overt alpha‐synucleinopathy over time. Conversely, n = 26 (77%) out of insula‐negative (RBD‐Ins−) patients remained stable (See Figure S2 in Supplementary Materials). The presence of a reduced insular 123I‐FP‐CIT binding (i.e. mean SBR lower than cut‐off value) was significantly associated with an increased probability of clinical conversion to PD or DLB with a hazard ratio of 3.387 [CI 95%: 1.18–10.27, P = 0.031] in the whole iRBD group, regardless the presence of the alterations of nigrostriatal binding at baseline, age and sex. Survival curves of reduced insular 123I‐FP‐CIT binding adjusted for basal ganglia alterations at baseline are shown in Figure 2. The same association was found after bootstrapping procedure (P = 0.020). When only considering RBD‐DAT−, the presence of an insular deficit was linked to a higher likelihood of conversion with a hazard ratio of 6.986 [CI 95%: 1.238–39.434, P = 0.028], adjusting for age. No significant association between insular binding and conversion probability was found in the RBD‐DAT+ subgroup (only n = 4 RBD‐DAT+ showed an insular reduced 123I‐FP‐CIT binding).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the monoaminergic system of iRBD patients using 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging. We employed both ROI‐based and whole‐brain voxel‐wise analyses to quantify deficits in 123I‐FP‐CIT uptake, as described in previous works. 9 , 10 , 16 Our findings demonstrate that insular 123I‐FP‐CIT deficits are core features of alpha‐synucleinopathies, even in very early disease stages. Indeed, those alterations are already present in individuals with iRBD, even when nigrostriatal binding reduction is still absent. Furthermore, in this study, insular deficits are associated with a higher risk of phenoconversion, particularly among individuals with negative DAT imaging of the basal ganglia.

Extra‐nigrostriatal monoaminergic deficits have been recently demonstrated in Parkinson's disease or Lewy bodies disease in both single‐centre and multicentre studies 10 , 25 using either voxel‐wise or ROI‐based analyses. However, their relevance in prodromal stages remains an unexplored and debated issue.

This multicentre study analysed the role of 123I‐FP‐CIT imaging in a large sample of PSG‐confirmed iRBD without parkinsonian signs. Insula was the only region that distinguished iRBD from controls in voxel‐wise adjusted analyses; 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR reductions in this region increased from DAT negative to DAT positive iRBD and have a similar extent in patients with clinically evident PD and DLB. Through the use of both voxel‐wise and ROI‐based analyses and longitudinal follow‐up, our findings demonstrate the crucial role of insular deficits to predict clinical phenoconversion in iRBD. The insula has long been considered a central hub of the limbic cortical system, but recent evidence on brain connectivity has revealed its role in many functions beyond this network. Its anterior portion—also known as agranular insula—receives dopamine projections from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) region and is highly interconnected with the basal ganglia 26 , 27 and other regions of the meso‐limbic dopaminergic system (i.e. hippocampus, amygdala, middle frontal regions). 28 , 29 Indeed, accumulating evidence suggests that the agranular insula may play a role as a cognitive hub in high‐level cognitive control by switching between large‐scale brain networks relevant to executive functions. These functions can be affected in PD because of D2 dopamine receptor dysfunction 30 and loss of dopaminergic modulation within the basal ganglia. 29 Moreover, the neuronal responses of insula can be modulated by the administration of SSRIs, suggesting an involvement in the serotonergic neurotransmitter system, as demonstrated by both experimental models 31 and functional neuroimaging studies. 32 , 33 Indeed, neuropathological studies on mouse models showed that the insular cortex contains dense serotonergic afferents, with a high expression of 5HT‐1a and 2a receptors. 34 , 35 The involvement of the insular cortex in both dopaminergic and serotonergic neurotransmitter systems has been further corroborated by the neurotransmitter analysis showing a high similarity between this region and dopamine transporter and serotonin receptor type 4 maps. The insular cortex is also affected by misfolded α‐synuclein depositions in both PD and DLB patients, as highlighted by post mortem neuropathological studies, with a high vulnerability of the agranular and dysgranular subregions. 36 The presence of monoaminergic alterations within the insular cortex in PD and DLB patients has been demonstrated also in vivo with 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging. 10 , 25

The included iRBD sample showed a higher proportion of men, in keeping with literature. 37 Among them, 15 patients showed an overt α‐synucleinopathy over follow‐up time. The moderately low percentage of converters may be explained by the heterogeneity of iRBD patients, as well as the relatively brief follow‐up period. Of note, the 66% of converters progressed to dementia with Lewy bodies, which aligns with the clinical and neuroimaging literature indicating a high risk of cognitive decline towards dementia in iRBD and alpha‐synucleinopathies with RBD. 38

The key outcome of the present study is the ability of early insular alterations detectable with 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging to predict conversion to an overt alpha‐synucleinopathy. The results were first detected by whole‐brain voxel‐wise analyses but also corroborated by ROI‐based SBR calculation—which have the significant advantage of potential application in pre‐existing retrospective multicentre cohorts with available 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT imaging. The cox‐regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR reduction in the insula and a greater probability of conversion, with a hazard ratio greater than three times in the whole iRBD cohort. This model was adjusted for the presence of nigrostriatal binding reduction at baseline, as we were interested in testing the specific role of very early extra‐striatal alterations in predicting clinical conversion. The analysis conducted separately for RBD‐DAT− and RBD‐DAT+ found an even higher association with the risk of conversion in those resulted negative to nigrostriatal visual inspection at baseline. This result may be explained by the small sample size of RBD‐DAT+ group, however the greater percentage of insula‐positive in the RBD‐DAT− (40% vs. 31%) may suggest a different vulnerability in the two subgroups.

The 53% of converters exhibited a positive 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT scan at baseline, which has been associated with a greater risk of conversion, consistently with previous research. 5 , 39 However, our finding regarding the prognostic value of the insular alterations is independent from the presence of clinically evident nigrostriatal binding reduction at baseline, perhaps suggesting an earlier mechanism of neuronal vulnerability. The voxel‐wise comparison between converters and stable iRBD patients revealed significant differences in baseline 123I‐FP‐CIT SBR in left putamen, insula and bilateral thalamus. Of note, thalamus is a highly vulnerable region in DLB possibly driving the differences with PD, as recently demonstrated in patients with confirmed clinical diagnosis. 10 These results may explain the higher percentage of iRBD converters to DLB and further highlight the potential value of extra‐nigrostriatal evaluation in profiling the individual risk of conversion and progression at a single‐subject level, already in the prodromal phases, even when nigrostriatal regions are still relatively preserved.

Future studies will be needed to broaden the iRBD cohort to larger‐scale multicentre setting with a longer follow‐up period, in order to confirm the prognostic value of the insular monoaminergic depletion to predict phenoconversion and specifically address the early differentiation between PD and DLB. Moreover, the inclusion of a larger sample‐size will allow a data‐driven subjects' classification and the subsequent use of multivariate analyses to explore the best combination of markers able to characterize the risk of short‐term phenoconversion at a single‐subject level.

We acknowledge some limitations of the study: first, the study lacks of standardized non‐motor (in particular autonomic and sleep data) and digital assessment at baseline and during follow‐up. Second, the absence of correction for partial volume effects might have led to subtle localization biases, despite we used anatomical probabilistic atlases for ROI segmentation. 21 Thus, we used non‐smoothed 123I‐FP‐CIT SPECT images in ROI‐based analyses. Third, the controls included were subjects with tremor syndromes and normal basal ganglia imaging and no evidence of cognitive decline. Therefore, the controls included for the comparisons are not representative of healthy ageing population and further validation using age‐matched healthy‐controls from available multicentre database should be planned within the next future.

Despite limitations, this is the first study describing early insular monoaminergic alteration in iRBD confirmed by both voxel‐wise and ROI‐based analyses. Our data strongly suggests an association between insular SBR depletion and an increased likelihood of conversion to a clinically overt α‐synucleinopathy. Overall, these data indicate a potential vulnerability in the insula that can predict short‐term conversion to PD or DLB, before overt nigrostriatal deficits occur.
