Inherited microcytic anemias due to disorders of iron and heme metabolism: An updated clinical review
Authors: Alexandros Makis, Ioanna Saougou, Eleftheria Hatzimichael
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrocytic anemia is among the most common hematological abnormalities in clinical practice and is usually attributable to iron deficiency, thalassemia traits, or anemia of inflammation. A small but clinically important subset of patients, however, has inherited disorders of iron metabolism or heme synthesis presenting with persistent, unexplained, familial, or iron‐refractory microcytosis. Over the past decade, advances in the understanding of the hepcidin–ferroportin axis, erythroid iron regulation, standardized hepcidin assays, and next‐generation sequencing have substantially refined diagnosis, while expert guidance for iron‐refractory iron deficiency anemia (IRIDA) has refined management, and hepcidin‐targeted therapies in early development signal a shift towards mechanism‐based treatment, though their role in IRIDA remains investigational. This review provides an updated, clinically oriented framework for inherited microcytic anemias caused by defects in systemic iron homeostasis, cellular iron transport, mitochondrial iron utilization, and heme biosynthesis. We propose a contemporary diagnostic algorithm integrating clinical features, iron biomarkers, hepcidin assessment, and molecular testing, and review current and emerging therapeutic strategies. Early recognition enables accurate diagnosis, avoids inappropriate investigations and treatment, facilitates genetic counselling, and helps prevent irreversible organ damage, particularly in iron‐overload disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Anemia classified according to mean corpuscular volume (MCV) remains one of the most informative initial approaches in diagnostic hematology. A reduced MCV—conventionally below 80 fL in adults, with lower age‐specific thresholds in childhood—directs the differential diagnosis towards disorders affecting iron availability, heme synthesis, or globin production. In clinical practice, most cases of microcytic anemia are attributable to iron deficiency, thalassemia traits, or anemia of inflammation and can be resolved through careful clinical assessment, peripheral blood film examination, hemoglobin analysis, and a limited panel of iron studies.1, 2, 3

A small but clinically important subset of patients presents with persistent microcytic anemia unexplained by these common disorders. Oral iron refractoriness, positive family history, early disease onset, or iron indices discordant with uncomplicated iron deficiency—particularly low transferrin saturation (TSAT) despite normal or increased ferritin, or increased TSAT associated with systemic iron overload—should prompt consideration of an inherited disorder of iron metabolism or heme synthesis. 4 , 5 Several of these conditions are characterized by progressive tissue iron accumulation, making early diagnosis essential to prevent irreversible hepatic, endocrine, cardiac, or neurological complications. 4 , 6

The field has evolved substantially since the 2014 practice guidelines by Donker et al., which established a systematic framework for inherited microcytic anemias. 4 Advances in iron biology have redefined many of these disorders as disturbances of iron distribution rather than absolute iron deficiency, driven principally by dysregulation of the hepcidin–ferroportin axis and its erythroid regulation by erythroferrone.6, 7, 8 Standardized hepcidin assays and interpretation of the TSAT/hepcidin ratio have become increasingly informative in specialist practice, while next‐generation sequencing (NGS) has largely replaced sequential single‐gene testing in atypical phenotypes, improving diagnostic yield and revealing genetically complex or overlapping disorders.9, 10, 11, 12 This expansion has been driven largely by NGS, which has uncovered novel disease genes and revealed atypical phenotypes, including clinically relevant disorders such as transferrin receptor 1 (TFRC) deficiency and heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) deficiency.13, 14, 15 Detailed expert guidance for iron‐refractory iron deficiency anemia (IRIDA) has also recently been published, and therapies targeting the hepcidin pathway have entered clinical development.16, 17, 18

We provide an updated, clinically focused overview of inherited microcytic anemias caused by defects in iron metabolism and heme synthesis. The paper outlines systemic and cellular iron homeostasis and examines the molecular pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management of the principal disorders. A contemporary diagnostic framework integrating clinical findings, iron biomarkers, and molecular testing is proposed, with emphasis on recent advances in classification, diagnosis, and targeted therapy.

This narrative review is based on a PubMed/MEDLINE search from May 2008, when TMPRSS6 was linked to IRIDA, to May 2026, combining disease names, gene symbols, and terms related to iron metabolism, hepcidin, heme synthesis, sideroblastic anemia, and hereditary microcytosis, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists. Priority was given to primary human studies, clinical guidelines, and authoritative English‐language reviews. The disorders discussed are illustrative rather than exhaustive. Thalassemias and other hemoglobinopathies were excluded except in the differential diagnosis, as are predominantly multisystem congenital sideroblastic syndromes, including PUS1‐ and YARS2‐related mitochondrial myopathy, lactic acidosis, and sideroblastic anemia (MLASA), TRNT1‐related sideroblastic anemia with immunodeficiency, fevers, and developmental delay (SIFD), SLC19A2‐related thiamine‐responsive megaloblastic anemia, and Pearson marrow–pancreas syndrome. These disorders should be suspected when sideroblastic anemia is accompanied by myopathy, lactic acidosis, recurrent fever, immunodeficiency, or developmental delay and are reviewed elsewhere. 19

PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS OF IRON HOMEOSTASIS AND HEME SYNTHESIS

Iron is indispensable for oxygen transport, mitochondrial respiration, DNA synthesis, and numerous enzymatic reactions, yet its redox activity makes it potentially toxic through generation of reactive oxygen species. Because iron has no regulated physiological mechanism of excretion, systemic balance depends almost entirely on intestinal absorption and macrophage‐mediated recycling. In healthy adults, only 1–2 mg of dietary iron is absorbed daily to compensate for obligatory losses, whereas erythropoiesis requires approximately 20–25 mg/day, most supplied through recycling of senescent erythrocytes by reticuloendothelial macrophages. 20 , 21

As illustrated in Figure 1, systemic iron homeostasis depends on coordinated interactions among duodenal enterocytes, reticuloendothelial macrophages, hepatocytes, and erythroid precursors. Enterocytes absorb dietary iron through divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1; SLC11A2) after reduction of ferric to ferrous iron by duodenal cytochrome b and export it through ferroportin (SLC40A1), the only known cellular iron exporter. Hephaestin oxidizes ferrous iron, permitting binding to circulating transferrin. Macrophages recover iron from senescent erythrocytes and export it through ferroportin, with ceruloplasmin providing complementary ferroxidase activity. Hepatocytes are the principal iron‐storage compartment and synthesize hepcidin, the master regulator of systemic iron homeostasis. Erythroid precursors acquire transferrin‐bound iron through transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1); STEAP3‐mediated ferric iron reduction and DMT1‐mediated endosomal transport subsequently deliver iron to mitochondria for heme synthesis. 20 , 22

Matriptase‐2 (MT2), encoded by TMPRSS6 and located at the hepatocyte plasma membrane, negatively regulates this pathway, at least partly through cleavage of membrane HJV. No direct regulatory connection between TFR1 and MT2 is implied. Inflammation, particularly interleukin‐6 signaling through STAT3, increases HAMP transcription. In response to erythropoietin‐driven erythropoiesis, erythroblasts secrete erythroferrone (ERFE), which sequesters BMP ligands—particularly BMP2/6 heterodimers—and thereby blunts BMP‐receptor activation and suppresses hepcidin transcription. Hepcidin does not inhibit ERFE. (C) Macrophage. Splenic, hepatic, and bone‐marrow macrophages recycle iron from senescent erythrocytes. Extracellular ceruloplasmin supports oxidation of exported Fe2+ to Fe3+, enabling its loading onto transferrin and redistribution, particularly to the erythroid marrow. Hepcidin binds macrophage FPN and induces its internalization and degradation, restricting iron release into plasma. (D) Erythroblast. HoloTF binds cell‐surface TFR1 and undergoes receptor‐mediated endocytosis. Acidification of the endosome releases Fe3+ from transferrin; STEAP3 reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, and DMT1, located in the endosomal membrane, transports Fe2+ into the cytosol. Cytosolic iron is directed to mitochondria for heme synthesis and iron–sulfur cluster biogenesis, stored in ferritin, or exported through plasma‐membrane FPN. Independently of this intracellular iron‐trafficking pathway, erythropoietin binding to its receptor stimulates ERFE production and secretion by erythroblasts. Circulating ERFE acts on hepatic BMP signaling to suppress hepcidin and increase systemic iron availability for erythropoiesis. (E) Erythroid mitochondrion. SLC25A38 supports mitochondrial glycine import. ALA is exported to the cytosol through an incompletely defined transporter, and the intermediate reactions of heme synthesis proceed in the cytosol before protoporphyrin IX (PPIX) returns to the mitochondrion. Ferrochelatase (FECH) catalyzes the final step by inserting Fe2+ into PPIX to generate heme. FLVCR1b is shown as the proposed mitochondrial heme exporter. GATA1 regulates transcription of globin and multiple heme‐biosynthetic genes independently of porphyrin intermediates. Hepcidin regulates iron availability by binding ferroportin and inducing its internalization and degradation, thereby restricting intestinal absorption and macrophage iron release. Hepatic hepcidin expression integrates systemic iron status, inflammation, hypoxia, and erythropoietic activity. Iron overload stimulates hepcidin predominantly through the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)–SMAD pathway. BMP6, produced mainly by liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, acts as a major physiological regulator of hepcidin transcription through interactions involving hemojuvelin (HJV), HFE, and transferrin receptor 2 (TfR2). Inflammation, particularly interleukin‐6 signaling, stimulates hepcidin, whereas hypoxia and increased erythropoietic demand suppress it. Matriptase‐2, encoded by TMPRSS6, negatively regulates BMP–SMAD signaling by cleaving HJV and suppressing hepcidin during iron deficiency. Loss‐of‐function TMPRSS6 variants abolish this adaptive response and cause IRIDA.6, 7, 23, 24, 25

Erythroid precursors are the largest single consumers of plasma iron, requiring iron for hemoglobin synthesis at a rate several‐fold greater than any other tissue. Diferric transferrin binds TfR1, expressed at very high density on developing erythroblasts, and the complex undergoes receptor‐mediated endocytosis. Endosomal acidification releases Fe3+ from transferrin; STEAP3 reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, and DMT1 exports Fe2+ across the endosomal membrane into the cytosol, while apotransferrin bound to TfR1 recycles to the cell surface. Cytosolic iron is delivered to mitochondria largely through mitoferrin‐1 (SLC25A37), the principal mitochondrial iron importer in erythroid cells, whose stability and function are enhanced by its binding partner ABCB10 26 ; within mitochondria, iron is used for terminal heme synthesis by ferrochelatase (FECH) and for iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster biogenesis. Erythroid iron handling is coordinated with heme synthesis and globin production: the heme‐regulated inhibitor kinase (HRI) phosphorylates eIF2α to suppress globin translation when heme or iron is insufficient, preventing accumulation of unstable, iron‐free globin chains, 27 while iron regulatory proteins (IRP1/IRP2) post‐transcriptionally coordinate TfR1 and ferritin expression according to cytosolic iron availability. 21 The relative contribution of a cytosolic labile iron pool versus direct organelle‐to‐organelle (“kiss‐and‐run”) iron transfer between endosomes and mitochondria remains incompletely resolved. Disruption at any step of this pathway, from transferrin‐receptor internalization to mitochondrial iron delivery, can impair heme synthesis and produce a hypochromic, microcytic erythroid phenotype, as illustrated by TFRC and DMT1 deficiency discussed below. 20 , 22

Active erythropoiesis further regulates iron homeostasis through erythroferrone, an erythroid hormone released in response to erythropoietin that suppresses hepcidin and promotes iron mobilization for hemoglobin synthesis. Although erythroferrone is increasingly recognized as an important regulator and potential biomarker of erythropoietic activity, measurement has not entered routine clinical practice. 8 , 28

Within erythroid mitochondria, iron is used primarily for heme synthesis and iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster biogenesis. Heme synthesis comprises eight enzymatic reactions, beginning with condensation of glycine and succinyl‐CoA by erythroid‐specific 5‐aminolevulinate synthase 2 (ALAS2) and culminating in insertion of ferrous iron into protoporphyrin IX by FECH. Proteins involved in mitochondrial iron transport, Fe–S cluster biogenesis, and regulation of heme synthesis—including SLC25A38, glutaredoxin 5 (GLRX5), ABCB7, and GATA1—are implicated in inherited microcytic anemias. Defects impair heme production and may promote mitochondrial iron accumulation and ring sideroblast formation, the pathological hallmark of congenital sideroblastic anemias (CSAs). 19 , 22

Thus, disruption at any level—from intestinal absorption and systemic iron regulation to cellular iron acquisition, mitochondrial iron utilization, and heme synthesis—may cause inherited microcytic anemia. DISORDERS OF LOW IRON AVAILABILITY FOR ERYTHROPOIESIS

IRIDA (TMPRSS6)

IRIDA is the prototypical inherited iron‐distribution disorder. Biallelic loss‐of‐function TMPRSS6 variants reduce matriptase‐2 activity, causing insufficient suppression of BMP–SMAD signaling and inappropriately high hepcidin relative to body iron stores.23, 24, 25 The resulting phenotype is a hypochromic, microcytic anemia with markedly reduced TSAT, typically below 10% or below 15% after iron supplementation, in the absence of inflammation. Ferritin is low or inappropriately low‐normal relative to the severity of iron restriction, oral iron produces little or no response, and intravenous iron achieves only partial correction. 16 , 29 Although classically autosomal recessive, milder phenotypes have been reported in monoallelic carriers. 30 , 31 A digenic mechanism has also been described, in which combined heterozygous TMPRSS6 and ACVR1A (encoding the BMP Type I receptor ALK2) variants produce an IRIDA‐like phenotype through additive impairment of hepcidin suppression, illustrating that non‐classical genetic combinations can phenocopy biallelic TMPRSS6 disease. 32

The phenotype is usually most pronounced in childhood, reflecting high growth‐related iron requirements and a higher developmental hepcidin set point, and frequently becomes milder with age. 11 , 16 Before genetic testing, acquired causes of refractory iron deficiency, particularly celiac disease, Helicobacter pylori infection, and autoimmune gastritis, should be excluded. 33

The most informative biochemical discriminator is hepcidin interpreted in relation to iron status. In IRIDA, hepcidin is inappropriately elevated for the markedly reduced TSAT, and a low TSAT/hepcidin ratio distinguishes TMPRSS6‐related disease from multi‐causal iron deficiency.9, 10, 11 In an index study using a standardized isotope‐dilution mass spectrometry hepcidin‐25 assay, a TSAT/hepcidin cut‐off of approximately 5.6%/nM discriminated IRIDA from other iron‐deficient controls when recent iron therapy and moderate‐to‐severe inflammation were absent. 10 Because the ratio varies with age and sex, interpretation against reference intervals, such as values below the 2.5th percentile for age and sex, is preferable to a universal threshold. 10 , 11

Two limitations are important. Both ferritin and hepcidin are acute‐phase reactants; inflammation may therefore obscure the diagnosis, and the TSAT/hepcidin ratio should not be interpreted shortly after iron therapy. Furthermore, despite international reference material, residual inter‐method variability persists, and assay‐specific units preclude direct transfer of thresholds between analytical platforms. 10 , 34 Where standardized hepcidin measurement is unavailable, diagnosis rests on the characteristic clinical and biochemical phenotype and TMPRSS6 sequencing. 29

Ferroportin disease (SLC40A1)

Heterozygous pathogenic SLC40A1 variants cause autosomal dominant hereditary hemochromatosis Type 4 and are conventionally divided into two functional classes. 35 , 36 Loss‐of‐function, or Type 4A, variants impair ferroportin‐mediated iron export, producing iron accumulation in macrophages and enterocytes, hyperferritinemia, low‐to‐normal TSAT, and preserved hepcidin responsiveness. Gain‐of‐function, or Type 4B, variants confer resistance to hepcidin and cause increased TSAT and parenchymal iron overload resembling other hereditary hemochromatoses. 37 , 38

Ferroportin disease is primarily an iron‐overload disorder rather than a cause of inherited microcytic anemia, and clinically significant microcytosis is uncommon. Anemia, when present, is generally mild and normocytic and often emerges during phlebotomy in patients with loss‐of‐function variants who mobilize iron poorly. 35 , 37 Hyperferritinemia from an early age associated with normal or low TSAT and a positive family history should raise suspicion.

Treatment is based on phlebotomy. In Type 4A disease, macrophage iron is mobilized slowly, and venesection may be poorly tolerated. Longer intervals between procedures and, occasionally, recombinant human erythropoietin (rHuEPO) support may therefore be required. 4 , 35 Treatment should target a low‐normal rather than profoundly reduced ferritin and be interrupted if hemoglobin falls, accepting a higher maintenance ferritin than in classical HFE‐related hemochromatosis. 35 , 38

Aceruloplasminemia (CP)

Biallelic CP variants cause aceruloplasminemia, an autosomal recessive disorder in which loss of ceruloplasmin ferroxidase activity impairs ferroportin‐mediated iron export. Iron accumulates in the liver, pancreas, retina, and characteristically the brain. 39 The classical triad of insulin‐dependent diabetes, retinal degeneration, and progressive neurological disease—including ataxia, dystonia, parkinsonism, and cognitive decline—typically develops during the fourth or fifth decade. 40 , 41

Mild microcytic or normocytic anemia with low serum iron, reduced TSAT, and elevated ferritin is an early and consistent finding that may precede neurological manifestations by years. Unexplained microcytic anemia associated with hyperferritinemia, low TSAT, and very low or undetectable ceruloplasmin should therefore prompt investigation for aceruloplasminaemia. 41 , 42

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating iron deposition in the basal ganglia, liver, and pancreas supports the diagnosis, which is confirmed by CP sequencing. 43 The anemia rarely requires specific treatment. Management is directed at reducing tissue iron accumulation with chelation, ideally before irreversible neurological injury. Neurological stabilization and reduced insulin requirements have been reported following chelation. 41

DISORDERS OF IRON ACQUISITION BY THE ERYTHROID PRECURSOR

Atransferrinemia/hypotransferrinemia (TF)

Congenital atransferrinemia is an exceptionally rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic TF variants and near‐complete absence of circulating transferrin. 44 Without transferrin‐mediated iron delivery, erythropoiesis is severely iron restricted despite abundant body iron. Simultaneously, non‐transferrin‐bound iron is avidly absorbed and deposited in parenchymal tissues.

Patients present in infancy with severe hypochromic microcytic anemia and systemic iron overload. 45 Ferritin is elevated and calculated TSAT may approach 100%, reflecting saturation of the minimal circulating transferrin, although TSAT is analytically unreliable when total iron‐binding capacity (TIBC) is extremely low. Hepcidin is suppressed. 46 , 47 Untreated patients may develop hepatomegaly, endocrinopathy, and fatal cardiomyopathy. 44 , 45

Diagnosis is based on absent or markedly reduced transferrin and TIBC and is confirmed by TF sequencing. Plasma or apotransferrin infusions restore iron delivery to the erythron and, by re‐establishing transferrin signaling, help normalize hepcidin. Phlebotomy or chelation may be required for tissue iron overload. Repeated transfusions and indiscriminate iron supplementation should be avoided. 4 , 46 Purified apotransferrin remains unlicensed and available only through compassionate‐use programmes at selected centers; consequently, most patients receive periodic plasma infusions, with phlebotomy or chelation according to tolerance. 46 , 48

DMT1 deficiency (SLC11A2)

Biallelic SLC11A2 variants impair DMT1‐mediated apical enterocyte iron uptake and iron egress from the erythroid endosome. Patients typically present in infancy with severe hypochromic microcytic anemia and a paradoxical iron phenotype characterized by increased TSAT and early hepatic iron overload. 49 , 50 The combination of defective DMT1‐mediated uptake and systemic iron overload reflects DMT1‐independent intestinal absorption, hepcidin suppression, and additional iron overload from transfusions or inappropriate iron therapy. Liver iron may be substantial despite only modest ferritin elevation. 50

The anemia is often transfusion‐dependent in early life but may improve with age. 50 rHuEPO has increased hemoglobin in some patients but does not prevent hepatic iron accumulation, whereas chelation may remove hepatic iron inefficiently and aggravate anemia by further restricting iron availability to the erythron. 49 , 50

Because ferritin may underestimate hepatic iron, liver iron concentration should be assessed by MRI R2 or R2* relaxometry at diagnosis and monitored periodically. Chelation should be reserved for clearly increased or progressive liver iron and titrated cautiously against hemoglobin. Management is individualized and includes judicious transfusion, cautious rHuEPO, avoidance of unnecessary iron, and molecular confirmation by SLC11A2 sequencing. 50 , 51

Transferrin receptor 1 (TFRC) deficiency

TfR1 deficiency, caused by pathogenic TFRC variants, has subsequently emerged as an important disorder linking cellular iron metabolism with adaptive immunity. A homozygous p.Tyr20His missense variant disrupting the TfR1 internalization motif, followed by additional disease‐causing variants, established TfR1 as essential for cellular iron acquisition and lymphocyte function. 13 , 14 The defect impairs receptor‐mediated endocytosis of transferrin‐bound iron, so intracellular iron uptake is reduced despite increased surface TfR1 expression.

The dominant phenotype is combined immunodeficiency, with recurrent sinopulmonary infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, impaired T‐ and B‐cell activation, intermittent thrombocytopenia or neutropenia, and failure to thrive. 14 Remarkably, anemia is generally mild despite the central role of TfR1 in erythropoiesis. Indeed, most reported patients first present to immunology or infectious‐disease services because of recurrent infection rather than to hematology, and mild microcytic anemia is often identified incidentally during this work‐up; recognizing the immunological phenotype is therefore critical to avoid diagnostic delay. Relative erythroid sparing has been attributed to high STEAP3 expression in erythroblasts, providing an accessory pathway that partially rescues transferrin uptake. 13

Thus, mild unexplained microcytic anemia in a child with recurrent infections, thrombocytopenia, or hypogammaglobulinemia should prompt consideration of TFRC deficiency. Management is focused on immunoglobulin replacement and prevention or treatment of infection; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may be curative. 14 , 52

DISORDERS OF HEME AND IRON–SULFUR CLUSTER SYNTHESIS

Disorders in this group share impaired heme synthesis or mitochondrial iron utilizations. Many are characterized by ring sideroblasts resulting from mitochondrial iron accumulation and frequently progress to systemic iron overload. They include the CSAs, erythropoietic porphyrias, and, mechanistically adjacent, the iron‐recycling disorder HMOX1 deficiency. NGS panels have substantially improved molecular diagnosis. 12 , 19 Although the disorders below are presented gene‐by‐gene for clarity, most patients today are identified through broad hereditary anemia panels or exome sequencing rather than sequential single‐gene testing, reflecting substantial phenotypic overlap among these entities. Diagnostic yield varies with cohort ancestry and phenotype but commonly falls in the 30%–60% range; negative or inconclusive results remain frequent because of variants of uncertain significance, deep intronic or regulatory variants (e.g., the ALAS2 intron 1 GATA1‐binding enhancer), structural variants not well captured by standard panels, and the need for segregation or functional studies to confirm pathogenicity. 53 , 54

SLC25A38‐related congenital sideroblastic anemia

Biallelic loss‐of‐function SLC25A38 variants, affecting the erythroid mitochondrial glycine transporter required for the first step of heme synthesis, cause the most common autosomal recessive non‐syndromic CSA. The phenotype is severe, with profound hypochromic microcytic transfusion‐dependent anemia presenting at or shortly after birth, often resembling transfusion‐dependent β‐thalassemia. Ring sideroblasts and early systemic iron overload are characteristic and may precede transfusion. 55 Although inheritance is classically autosomal recessive, apparent homozygosity does not always indicate biparental transmission of the same variant: uniparental isodisomy at the SLC25A38 locus has been reported to produce a homozygous genotype from a single heterozygous parent in the absence of consanguinity. 56 This mechanism should be suspected when segregation analysis is discordant with recessive inheritance, since it carries different recurrence‐risk implications than biparental heterozygosity and is directly relevant to genetic counselling.

A large multicenter study expanded the mutational spectrum and confirmed the consistently severe and pyridoxine‐unresponsive phenotype. 57 Management consists of regular transfusion and iron chelation. Allogeneic HSCT is the only established curative treatment and should be considered early in suitable candidates. 57 , 58

X‐linked sideroblastic anemia (ALAS2)

X‐linked sideroblastic anemia (XLSA), caused by hemizygous pathogenic ALAS2 variants, is the most common inherited sideroblastic anemia. 19 , 59 Reduced ALAS2 activity limits protoporphyrin production and heme synthesis, producing hypochromic microcytic anemia, ring sideroblasts, and mitochondrial and systemic iron overload. Most pathogenic variants impair enzyme activity or pyridoxal‐5′‐phosphate binding, whereas a smaller subset affects a GATA1‐binding enhancer within intron 1. 60

Clinical expression is highly variable. Affected males generally present during the first two decades with anemia or later with parenchymal iron overload. Female carriers may become symptomatic with age because of skewed X‐chromosome inactivation.59, 60, 61

A major distinguishing feature is frequent responsiveness to pyridoxine. Pharmacological doses, typically 50–200 mg/day, may improve anemia, after which the lowest effective maintenance dose should be used to minimize pyridoxine‐induced neuropathy. Patients should not be classified as pyridoxine‐refractory until significant iron overload has been corrected, because excess iron may impair treatment response. Iron overload is managed by phlebotomy whenever anemia permits; notably, hemoglobin may increase as iron stores decline. 59 , 62 , 63

Other congenital sideroblastic anemias: GLRX5, STEAP3, ABCB7, HSPA9, and HSCB

Several rarer disorders complete the non‐syndromic or predominantly hematological CSA spectrum. 64 GLRX5‐related disease is an autosomal recessive disorder of Fe–S cluster biogenesis causing microcytic sideroblastic anemia and iron overload through impaired Fe–S cluster synthesis and secondary repression of heme synthesis. 65 , 66 STEAP3‐related CSA results from deficiency of the erythroid endosomal ferrireductase and is characterized by severe hypochromic anemia, ring sideroblasts, increased ferritin and TSAT, hepatic iron overload, and hypogonadism. 67 ABCB7 deficiency causes XLSA with ataxia. Affected males develop childhood cerebellar ataxia, mild sideroblastic anemia, and increased erythrocyte protoporphyrin, notably without the systemic iron overload characteristic of many other CSAs. 68 Biallelic variants in HSPA9, encoding the mitochondrial chaperone mortalin, and HSCB, encoding a co‐chaperone required for Fe–S cluster assembly, cause microcytic‐to‐normocytic sideroblastic anemia. 69 HSCB deficiency impairs erythroid Fe–S cluster biogenesis, secondarily disrupts heme synthesis, and promotes ring sideroblast formation. 70

Treatment is largely supportive and disorder‐specific. Transfusion and iron chelation are required in severe iron‐overload phenotypes, whereas the mild anemia associated with ABCB7 deficiency generally requires no specific hematological treatment. Selected patients with GLRX5‐related disease may benefit from phlebotomy as body iron stores decline. Multigene hereditary anemia panels increasingly facilitate diagnosis, genetic counselling, and distinction from acquired sideroblastic anemia or other causes of childhood ataxia. 53 , 54

Erythropoietic protoporphyria and X‐linked protoporphyria (FECH, ALAS2)

Erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) results from reduced FECH activity, most commonly caused by a loss‐of‐function FECH variant in trans with a common hypomorphic allele. X‐linked protoporphyria (XLP) results from gain‐of‐function ALAS2 variants. Both cause accumulation of protoporphyrin IX. 71 , 72

The hallmark is severe, painful, non‐blistering photosensitivity beginning in early childhood. A minority develop progressive hepatobiliary disease from protoporphyrin deposition. Although photosensitivity dominates the phenotype, EPP and XLP are relevant to unexplained microcytosis because mild microcytic anemia with low‐normal iron stores may occur, reflecting impaired heme synthesis and altered erythroid iron utilization. 73

Diagnosis is supported by markedly increased erythrocyte protoporphyrin and confirmed molecularly. Fractionation of metal‐free and zinc protoporphyrin is essential. EPP and XLP are characterized predominantly by metal‐free protoporphyrin, with a relatively greater zinc protoporphyrin fraction in XLP, whereas isolated zinc protoporphyrin elevation suggests iron deficiency or lead poisoning. Samples should be protected from light. The anemia rarely requires specific treatment; management focuses on photoprotection, surveillance for hepatobiliary complications, and disease‐specific therapies that improve light tolerance. 71

HMOX1 deficiency

HMOX1 deficiency, a recently recognized disorder, is best classified as a disorder of iron recycling rather than heme synthesis. 74 HMOX1 catalyzes the degradation of heme to biliverdin, carbon monoxide, and ferrous iron and is the rate‐limiting step in macrophage iron recovery from senescent erythrocytes. Loss of HMOX1 causes toxic accumulation of unmetabolized heme within macrophages, defective iron recycling, and widespread tissue injury. 74 , 75

The autosomal recessive disorder presents with direct antiglobulin test‐negative intravascular hemolytic anemia, paradoxically low or normal bilirubin, marked erythrocyte fragmentation, hyperinflammation resembling macrophage activation syndrome, functional asplenia, nephritis, endothelial injury, and hyperferritinemia with tissue iron deposition.15, 75, 76, 77

Although the anemia is predominantly hemolytic, mild microcytosis is common, and the disorder may enter the differential diagnosis of unexplained anemia with abnormal iron indices. Hemolysis with disproportionately low bilirubin, recurrent febrile inflammation, functional asplenia, and hyperferritinemia is highly suggestive. 15 , 77 Management is supportive and includes treatment of inflammation and infection and transfusion when required. Prognosis remains guarded, although HSCT may be considered in severe disease. 76

A CONTEMPORARY DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

As shown in Figure 2, evaluation begins by confirming microcytosis using age‐appropriate reference intervals and excluding common causes: iron deficiency, malabsorption or blood loss, thalassemia traits, anemia of inflammation, and, particularly in children, lead exposure. 2 , 11 Peripheral blood film examination, hemoglobin analysis by high‐performance liquid chromatography or capillary electrophoresis, with molecular testing when indicated, and a baseline iron profile comprising serum iron, transferrin or TIBC, TSAT, and ferritin resolve most cases. Reticulocyte hemoglobin content and red‐cell distribution width provide additional information where available. 2 , 3

In persistent unexplained microcytic anemia, the pattern of iron indices directs further investigation. Three broad patterns are clinically useful. First, very low TSAT with low or inappropriately normal ferritin and oral iron refractoriness suggests IRIDA. In this setting, the TSAT/hepcidin ratio may discriminate TMPRSS6‐related disease from multi‐causal iron deficiency. 9 , 10 , 16

Second, hyperferritinemia with low or normal TSAT suggests disorders of iron export or recycling. Aceruloplasminemia should be considered when ceruloplasmin is absent or markedly reduced, particularly with neurological manifestations. Loss‐of‐function Type 4A ferroportin disease is suggested by familial hyperferritinemia with macrophage iron overload. HMOX1 deficiency should be considered when hyperferritinemia accompanies hemolysis, hyperinflammation, and functional asplenia.

Third, increased TSAT, with or without hyperferritinemia, suggests DMT1 deficiency, atransferrinemia, gain‐of‐function Type 4B ferroportin disease, or an iron‐overload CSA. In atransferrinemia, calculated TSAT may approach 100% because circulating transferrin is almost absent. Bone‐marrow examination demonstrating ring sideroblasts establishes a sideroblastic phenotype and narrows the differential diagnosis, although marrow examination may increasingly be avoided when the phenotype and molecular findings are concordant. 3

A separate diagnostic route applies when iron indices are relatively normal but characteristic extra‐hematological features are present. Painful photosensitivity suggests EPP or XLP, childhood ataxia suggests ABCB7‐related XLSA with ataxia, and immunodeficiency with thrombocytopenia or hypogammaglobuliaemia suggests TFRC deficiency. 2 , 4 , 6

NGS has transformed diagnosis. Targeted single‐gene analysis remains appropriate when the phenotype strongly indicates a specific disorder, whereas multigene hereditary anemia panels improve diagnostic yield in atypical and genetically heterogeneous presentations and may identify coexisting pathogenic variants or modifiers. 12 Molecular diagnosis refines prognosis, guides disease‐specific treatment, facilitates family screening and genetic counselling, and may prevent repeated invasive investigations. 4 , 12

Two diagnostic pitfalls deserve emphasis. First, age‐specific reference intervals are essential because MCV, hemoglobin, and ferritin vary substantially during infancy and early childhood. Physiological changes and common nutritional iron deficiency may both mimic and mask inherited disease. 2 Second, inherited microcytic anemias may coexist with thalassemia traits, chronic inflammation, blood loss, or nutritional iron deficiency. Microcytosis disproportionate to iron status, an unexpectedly high red‐cell count, or discordant hemoglobin analysis should prompt consideration of dual pathology rather than a single diagnosis. Multigene panels are particularly useful in these complex phenotypes. 12

MANAGEMENT

General principles

For most inherited microcytic anemias reviewed here, treatment remains supportive and is often determined more by the risk of iron‐related organ injury than by the severity of anemia. Three broad therapeutic principles apply across this group, though their relative importance differs by disorder: (i) correction or support of iron‐restricted erythropoiesis—iron supplementation in IRIDA, transfusion in severe CSAs; (ii) monitoring and reduction of body iron in iron‐loading disorders, managed by chelation or, where anemia permits, phlebotomy, with hepatic and cardiac MRI guiding therapy when serum iron markers underestimate tissue loading; and (iii) disease‐specific therapy directed at the underlying molecular defect, such as pyridoxine in ALAS2‐related XLSA, apotransferrin in atransferrinemia, or immunoglobulin replacement and HSCT for the immunodeficiency of TFRC deficiency. Chelation principles are not interchangeable across disorders

Although deferoxamine, deferiprone, and deferasirox are used across several disorders, principles derived from transfusional iron overload cannot be applied indiscriminately, because iron distribution, chelation efficacy, therapeutic targets, and treatment risks differ between disorders. In DMT1 deficiency, for example, chelation may inefficiently remove hepatic iron and worsen anemia by further restricting iron availability to the erythron. 50 , 51 In aceruloplasminemia, the major therapeutic concern is parenchymal and neurological iron deposition. 41 When chronic transfusion is required, iron loading should be monitored and treated appropriately. Disease‐specific treatment, including pyridoxine for ALAS2‐related XLSA 63 and apotransferrin for congenital atransferrinemia, 45 should be introduced whenever available.

Updated treatment of IRIDA

Recent expert guidance has refined the management of IRIDA. 16 IRIDA is increasingly viewed as a disorder of iron distribution in which iron is sequestered within reticuloendothelial macrophages rather than as simple absolute iron deficiency. Treatment should therefore provide sufficient iron to maintain hemoglobin and quality of life while avoiding excessive macrophage iron accumulation, rather than pursuing complete normalization of hemoglobin at any cost.

In treatment‐naïve children, oral elemental iron at approximately 3 mg/kg/day once daily is appropriate because younger patients and those with milder TMPRSS6 variants may respond. Higher doses, anecdotally up to 10 mg/kg/day, may partly overcome the hepcidin‐mediated ferroportin block, although oral treatment should be discontinued if ineffective despite adequate dosing and adherence. Alternate‐day dosing and ascorbic acid may improve absorption. A treatment period of approximately 3 months is generally required before concluding that oral therapy has failed. 16 , 78

In adults, in whom disease may be milder but oral iron has commonly already proved ineffective, intravenous iron is generally preferred. By delivering a substantial elemental iron load directly to macrophages, intravenous therapy partly bypasses hepcidin‐mediated restriction through iron‐responsive regulation of ferroportin expression. Smaller repeated intravenous doses, for example 500 mg elemental iron at intervals of several weeks, may be preferable to large boluses, which transiently increase hepcidin and may reduce subsequent responsiveness. Individual patients appear to have a ferritin threshold above which TSAT begins to increase, indicating availability of iron for erythropoiesis. Treatment should therefore be individualized according to symptoms and quality of life rather than a universal ferritin or TSAT target. 16

Hemoglobin, ferritin, and TSAT should be measured before infusions. Serum phosphate should also be monitored in patients receiving ferric carboxymaltose because fibroblast growth factor 23‐mediated hypophosphatemia may cause osteomalacia and necessitate a change in formulation. 79 , 80 The long‐term consequences of sustained reticuloendothelial iron overload in IRIDA remain uncertain. Experience from loss‐of‐function ferroportin disease suggests that macrophage‐predominant iron accumulation is less toxic than parenchymal iron overload in classical hereditary hemochromatosis despite high ferritin, supporting a conservative, symptom‐directed strategy. 16 , 35

Management of other iron‐acquisition and heme‐synthesis disorders

Management of the remaining disorders is disease‐specific and, other than in IRIDA, has been less extensively studied prospectively. In DMT1 deficiency, treatment is individualized around a central tension between transfusion need and hepatic iron accumulation: transfusion and cautious rHuEPO support hemoglobin, while chelation is reserved for clearly increased or progressive liver iron on MRI R2/R2* relaxometry and titrated cautiously against hemoglobin, since aggressive chelation can worsen anemia. 50 In TFRC deficiency, anemia is typically mild and refractory to iron supplementation; management centers on immunoglobulin replacement and prevention or treatment of infection, with allogeneic HSCT reserved as the only curative option for severe combined immunodeficiency. 14 , 52 In ALAS2‐related XLSA, a trial of pharmacological pyridoxine (typically 50–200 mg/day) is first‐line, with the lowest effective maintenance dose used long‐term to limit pyridoxine‐induced neuropathy; patients should not be labeled pyridoxine‐refractory until coexisting iron overload has been corrected by phlebotomy, since excess iron can itself blunt treatment response. 59 , 62 , 63 SLC25A38‐related CSA 57 , 58 and STEAP3‐related CSA 67 are typically transfusion‐dependent with early iron loading, for which regular transfusion and chelation are required and allogeneic HSCT is the only established curative treatment, ideally considered early in suitable candidates. ABCB7‐related XLSA 68 with ataxia and HSPA9/HSCB‐related CSA 69 , 70 generally require no specific hematological treatment beyond supportive care, reflecting their typically mild anemia. In EPP and XLP, the anemia rarely requires treatment; management instead centers on photoprotection, surveillance for hepatobiliary complications, and therapies that improve light tolerance. 73 In HMOX1 deficiency, management is supportive, directed at hyperinflammation, infection, and transfusion when required, with HSCT considered in severe disease, though prognosis remains guarded. 76

Emerging hepcidin‐pathway therapeutics

Therapies targeting the hepcidin–ferroportin axis represent a major recent advance in iron biology. Because IRIDA results from inappropriately increased hepcidin, pharmacological hepcidin antagonism is mechanistically attractive. Reduction of hepcidin activity could restore intestinal absorption and macrophage iron release. Strategies under investigation include inhibition of BMP–SMAD signaling through anti‐BMP6 or anti‐HJV antibodies and direct hepcidin antagonists. Several approaches have demonstrated proof of concept in hyperhepcidinemic inflammatory disorders, although none has been approved or formally evaluated in IRIDA. 17 , 18 , 80

Conversely, agents that increase hepcidin activity or restrict plasma iron are being developed for iron‐overload anemias with ineffective erythropoiesis, particularly β‐thalassemia. TMPRSS6‐lowering strategies, including N‐acetylgalactosamine‐conjugated antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNAs, increase endogenous hepcidin and reduce circulating iron. In experimental β‐thalassemia, these approaches improve ineffective erythropoiesis and limit iron overload, although severe pre‐existing iron overload may reduce efficacy. Ferroportin inhibitors and hepcidin mimetics pursue the same objective by restricting iron export into plasma. 17 , 81 , 82

Although primarily developed for β‐thalassemia and hereditary hemochromatosis, these agents validate the hepcidin–ferroportin axis as a therapeutic target. Hepcidin antagonists may ultimately have a role in IRIDA and other iron‐restricted disorders, whereas hepcidin agonists may benefit selected iron‐overload conditions. 17 Novel oral iron formulations designed to partly bypass the enteral hepcidin block are also under investigation, although long‐term comparative data in IRIDA remain limited. 83

Special populations

Physiological and clinical conditions modify both laboratory interpretation and management. During infancy and early childhood, age‐specific MCV and ferritin reference intervals, the physiological hemoglobin nadir, and the high prevalence of nutritional iron deficiency complicate diagnosis, while IRIDA is often most pronounced. Weight‐based oral and intravenous iron treatment should account for growth as a major competing iron requirement. 11 , 16

Pregnancy physiologically suppresses hepcidin and may improve iron availability in women with IRIDA, potentially reducing treatment requirements. However, iron demands increase markedly during gestation, and postpartum reassessment is frequently necessary. 16 Intercurrent inflammation increases both hepcidin and ferritin, exacerbates iron‐restricted anemia, and limits interpretation of the TSAT/hepcidin ratio. In iron‐overload disorders, preconception and perioperative evaluation should include assessment of organ iron burden. Common acquired contributors—including blood loss, nutritional iron deficiency, inflammation, and thalassemia traits—must be identified and treated alongside the inherited disorder.

Genetic counselling, family screening, and implementation

Identification of a pathogenic variant should prompt genetic counselling and cascade family screening according to inheritance. Autosomal recessive disorders warrant carrier testing of parents and siblings and, where consanguinity or shared carrier status is identified, consideration of partner and offspring testing. Autosomal dominant ferroportin disease is appropriately investigated by cascade screening of first‐degree relatives. In X‐linked disorders, female carriers require evaluation because skewed X‐chromosome inactivation may result in clinical manifestations.

Unexpected inheritance patterns should be anticipated in practice. Apparent homozygosity in a presumed autosomal recessive disorder may reflect uniparental disomy rather than biparental transmission, as described for SLC25A38‐related CSA, and should be suspected when only one parent carries the identified variant or when consanguinity is absent. De novo variants and parental germline or somatic mosaicism should also be considered, particularly in dominant or X‐linked disorders or when parental testing is unexpectedly negative. These mechanisms carry recurrence‐risk implications that differ from straightforward Mendelian transmission and should be discussed explicitly during counselling, with confirmatory testing (e.g., microsatellite or single nucleotide polymorphism‐array analysis for uniparental disomy) pursued where the inheritance pattern is ambiguous.

Counselling should address reproductive options, including prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing for severe disorders, and should involve a clinical geneticist when genotype–phenotype relationships are complex, as in IRIDA.

Implementation barriers remain substantial. Despite provisional international reference‐material standardization efforts, hepcidin assays are not yet uniformly standardized worldwide, remain available only in specialist or research laboratories in most health systems, and reimbursement is inconsistent; residual inter‐platform variability further limits direct comparison of results across centers. Diagnosis therefore frequently relies on phenotype and molecular testing. Access to NGS panels also remains uneven. Disease‐specific therapies present additional challenges: apotransferrin is available only on a compassionate‐use basis, and HSCT, the only curative treatment for several severe disorders, is restricted to specialized centers. Emerging hepcidin–ferroportin therapies will require prospective evaluation in rare inherited microcytic anemias using endpoints extending beyond hemoglobin to iron distribution, organ iron burden, and quality of life.

CONCLUSIONS

Inherited microcytic anemias caused by defects of iron metabolism and heme synthesis are individually rare but collectively constitute an important and expanding group of disorders. They should be considered whenever microcytosis cannot be adequately explained by iron deficiency, thalassemia, anemia of inflammation, or lead exposure.

Advances in molecular genetics and iron biology have fundamentally changed their classification, diagnosis, and management. The hepcidin–ferroportin axis now provides the central framework for understanding disorders of systemic iron homeostasis. Standardized hepcidin measurement and interpretation of the TSAT/hepcidin ratio increasingly inform specialist evaluation, NGS has become the preferred molecular diagnostic approach for heterogeneous phenotypes, and recognition of disorders such as TFRC and HMOX1 deficiency has broadened the clinical spectrum. Management is also evolving from predominantly supportive care towards individualized, mechanism‐based treatment, as reflected in contemporary IRIDA guidance and the development of therapies targeting the hepcidin–ferroportin pathway. In parallel, the increasing integration of genomic medicine is shifting the diagnostic paradigm from a primarily phenotype‐driven approach towards genotype‐supported diagnosis, in which molecular findings may precede, redirect or refine clinical suspicion rather than simply confirm it.

Microcytosis should be confirmed using age‐appropriate reference intervals and common acquired causes systematically excluded. Iron indices should be interpreted in terms of iron distribution and availability rather than body iron stores alone. Characteristic extra‐hematological manifestations—including immunodeficiency, neurological disease, photosensitivity, and systemic inflammation—may provide decisive diagnostic clues. Biochemical findings should be integrated with targeted or panel‐based molecular testing.

Equally importantly, in many inherited iron‐overload disorders the long‐term consequences of tissue iron accumulation are more clinically significant than the anemia itself. Early recognition and appropriate assessment of organ iron burden are therefore essential to prevent irreversible hepatic, endocrine, cardiac, or neurological injury.

Despite substantial progress, the evidence base remains limited and is derived predominantly from case reports, small series, and observational studies. International collaboration, comprehensive patient registries, and prospective studies are required to validate emerging biomarkers, refine genotype–phenotype correlations, establish optimal treatment strategies, and evaluate hepcidin‐targeted therapies. As precision diagnostics and mechanism‐based treatments continue to evolve, early molecular diagnosis will increasingly become the cornerstone of personalized management for patients with inherited iron‐related microcytic anemias.
