Clinical management of clonal hematopoiesis
Authors: Kelly S. Chien, Guillermo Garcia‐Manero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClonal hematopoiesis, particularly clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential and clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance, is an age‐related premalignant condition characterized by the expansion of hematopoietic clones carrying somatic mutations. Because of the accessibility of peripheral blood, it has been increasingly detected, even in otherwise healthy individuals. Although often asymptomatic, specific clonal hematopoiesis–associated mutations and clonal features are associated with an increased risk of progression to hematologic malignancies, including myelodysplastic syndromes and acute myeloid leukemia. Beyond malignant transformation, clonal hematopoiesis also has systemic implications and has been linked to a variety of nonmalignant conditions, such as cardiovascular and inflammatory disease. In this review, we focus on the clinical management of clonal hematopoiesis, including approaches to diagnosis, tools for risk stratification, and strategies for surveillance and comorbidity mitigation. In the absence of consensus guidelines and approved therapies, management remains individualized, underscoring the need for continued research and clinical trials to guide evidence‐based care and improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The development of somatic alterations in human tissue is pervasive and increases with age and exposure to selective pressures. 1 The accessibility of circulating blood cells has led to a better understanding of these clonal changes in the hematopoietic system. Clonal hematopoiesis refers to the presence of a genetically distinct hematopoietic clone arising from acquired alterations in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. These changes most commonly involve somatic mutations in genes associated with hematologic malignancies, although they may also result from chromosomal aberrations or other genetic changes. Clonal hematopoiesis is frequently detected in individuals without overt hematologic disease, with prevalence increasing substantially with age. 2 , 3 , 4 Several population‐based studies have demonstrated associations between certain clonal hematopoiesis mutations with various environmental factors, such as exposure to chemotherapy, tobacco, and infections, which may influence clonal selection and expansion. 5 , 6

The term “clonal hematopoiesis” encompasses a broad spectrum of clonal states, including clonal hematopoiesis with unknown drivers, 7 mosaic chromosomal alterations, 8 , 9 and mutations in genetic drivers of myeloid and lymphoid malignancies. 10 In this review, we focus on clonal hematopoiesis involving myeloid‐associated mutations, with emphasis on its detection, diagnostic classification, clinical consequences, and implications for patient management.

DETECTION AND CLINICAL CONSEQUENCES

Detection of clonal hematopoiesis involving myeloid mutations

Clonal hematopoiesis involving myeloid‐associated mutations includes both clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS). 11 , 12 CHIP is characterized by the detection of a somatic mutation with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of at least 2% in individuals with normal peripheral blood counts. In contrast, CCUS refers to the presence of a somatic mutation in individuals with otherwise unexplained cytopenias.

Both the World Health Organization and International Consensus Classification have added CHIP/CCUS to their myeloid neoplasm classifications, 13 , 14 with cytopenias for CCUS defined by World Health Organization criteria (anemia: hemoglobin <12 g/dL in females or <13 g/dL in males; thrombocytopenia: platelet count <150 × 109 cells/L; neutropenia: absolute neutrophil count <1.8 × 109 cells/L). The identification of both CHIP and CCUS requires the absence of diagnostic criteria for a defined myeloid neoplasm, including morphologic, cytogenetic, and molecular findings. Furthermore, CHIP/CCUS is considered a premalignant clonal condition on the spectrum of myeloid disorders (Figure 1), including myeloid neoplasms such as myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Beyond the molecularly defined MDS subtypes, distinguishing between CCUS and MDS requires the presence of >10% bone marrow dysplasia, which is inherently subjective. 15 , 16

Associated malignant outcomes

Hematologic malignancies

The presence of CHIP/CCUS leads to an increased risk of a hematologic malignancy, 2 , 3 which is gene‐specific. Studies have shown that healthy individuals with somatic mutations in IDH1/2, JAK2, TP53, and spliceosome genes, such as U2AF1 and SRSF2, are at an increased risk of developing AML. 17 , 18 , 19 Various additional factors, including VAF ≥10% to 20% and the presence of more than one mutation, have also been implicated in transformation to myeloid malignancies. 20 , 21 , 22

The main risk prediction tool remains the Clonal Hematopoiesis Risk Score (CHRS), a personalized model for risk of progression to myeloid neoplasms in healthy adults with CHIP or CCUS. 23 Accounting for specific mutations, VAF, age, and peripheral blood counts, red cell distribution width, and mean corpuscular volume, the CHRS risk stratified 11,337 healthy individuals with CHIP or CCUS from the UK Biobank into three groups. High risk (CHRS score ≥12.5, n = 123 [1.1%]) was associated with a 10‐year cumulative incidence of myeloid transformation of 52.2%, intermediate risk (CHRS score 10‐12, n = 1196 [10.5%]) 7.8%, and low risk (CHRS score ≤9.5, n = 10,018 [88.4%]) 0.7%. More recently, the three‐parameter Clonal Cytopenia Risk Score (CCRS) was developed to specifically risk‐stratify patients with CCUS based on the presence of splicing mutations, thrombocytopenia with platelets <100 × 109 cells/L, and two or more mutations. 24 Developed with data from 357 patients across 17 academic centers, the CCRS was able to stratify patients into high (CCRS score ≥5, n = 36 [10.1%]), intermediate (CCRS score 2.5‐5, n = 139 [38.9%]), and low risk (CCRS score <2.5, n = 182 [51.0%]) with a 2‐year cumulative incidence of myeloid neoplasm progression of 37.2%, 14.1%, and 6.4%, respectively. Furthermore, Myeloid Neoplasm‐Predict (MN‐Predict) was developed as a web application that generates separate time‐dependent predictions of MDS, AML, and myeloproliferative neoplasms based on peripheral blood laboratory values and mutational data. 25 However, these risk prediction mechanisms do not account for myeloid transformation risk in the context of specific clinical situations and environmental stressors, such as exposure to certain antineoplastic therapies. Furthermore, the presence of known antecedent clonal hematopoiesis before myeloid neoplasm development does not affect management of the subsequent leukemia. It has been shown that patients with CHIP/CCUS are likely to have expansion of preexisting clones, rapid disease progression, and inferior survival outcomes after myeloid transformation. 26

In the clinical setting of cellular and stem cell therapies, the impact of CHIP/CCUS is equally complex. For patients receiving CAR‐T therapy, preexisting CHIP or CCUS is associated with increased toxicities, 27 , 28 likely stemming from a hyperinflammatory immune environment. Additionally, lymphoma and myeloma patients with CHIP at the time of autologous stem cell transplantation had increased risk of therapy‐related myeloid malignancies and worse survival outcomes. 29 , 30 , 31 In allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, donor‐derived CHIP can engraft in the patient with varying consequences. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Although DNMT3A mutations may strengthen the graft‐versus‐leukemia effect and reduce relapse risk, they are also associated with higher rates of graft‐versus‐host disease. 36 , 37 Conversely, high‐risk donor mutations in genes like TP53 or splicing factors significantly increase the risk of donor‐derived leukemias. 36 Despite these risks and a large‐scale study of more than 2500 unrelated donor allogeneic stem cell transplants showing a trend toward increased mortality, 35 routine donor surveillance for CHIP/CCUS remains uncommon in current practice as the absolute risk of donor‐derived leukemia remains small.

Therapy‐related clonal hematopoiesis

In patients with non‐myeloid cancers, the presence of clonal hematopoiesis with myeloid‐associated mutations in the context of antineoplastic therapy significantly increases the risk of developing therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms and contributes to adverse clinical outcomes. 38 , 39 Though there was initially thought that antineoplastic therapy was causing the development of clonal hematopoiesis mutations, that CHIP/CCUS typically precedes chemotherapy or radiation therapy has been well‐established. In TP53‐mutated therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms, small hematopoietic clones with TP53 mutations were detected in patients’ peripheral blood or bone marrow samples before any exposure to antineoplastic agents. 40 In addition to TP53, other myeloid clonal hematopoiesis‐associated mutations, such as those in TET2, RUNX1, and SRSF2, were discovered to be common in patients with therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms at the time of their initial cancer diagnosis. 41 , 42 The natural history of patients with therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms and known antecedent CHIP/CCUS has been described, with expansion of preexisting clones resulting from environmental stressors and consequent inferior outcomes. 26 Exposure to platinum‐based chemotherapy, topoisomerase II inhibitors, and radiation creates a selective environment for mutations in DNA damage response genes, such as TP53, PPM1D, CHEK2, and ATM.5 This clonal fitness results in subsequent expansion and progression to therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms. 43 , 44 , 45 Recently, a proof of concept study demonstrated that CDK4/6 inhibition may mitigate chemotherapy‐induced expansion in clonal hematopoiesis clones with mutations in DNA damage response genes. 46

Though the CHRS was able to risk‐stratify healthy individuals with CHIP/CCUS into three risk categories for incidence of myeloid transformation, it is not as applicable in patients with cancer, especially those who have been exposed to cytotoxins or undergone cellular therapies. In two cohorts of patients with cancer with CHIP/CCUS, individuals deemed CHRS intermediate risk are indeed at high risk of myeloid transformation, with a median time to progression of 13.7 months. 26 , 47 On the other hand, the CCRS included 133 patients with a history of malignancies, with 66 of those having received prior chemotherapy or radiation but is not a dedicated tool to those with therapy‐related clonal hematopoiesis.

Impact on solid tumor oncology

In addition to high risk of myeloid transformation, the presence of CHIP/CCUS has been associated with increased incidence of solid tumors, such as lung and breast cancer, 48 , 49 and inferior clinical outcomes from the original nonmyeloid malignancy. 29 , 30 , 31 Clonal hematopoiesis mutations were recently discovered to be detectable in solid tumors, such as non‐small cell lung cancer, a phenomenon termed tumor‐infiltrating clonal hematopoiesis. 50 Tumor‐infiltrating clonal hematopoiesis is associated with increased risk of solid tumor disease recurrence or death, likely because of tumor immune microenvironment remodeling and consequent acceleration of tumor growth and cancer evolution.

Cardiovascular disease

Clonal hematopoiesis is a significant independent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, 2 carrying a risk profile comparable to traditional factors, such as smoking, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes. 52 Individuals with CHIP, specifically those harboring DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and/or JAK2, experience an increased incidence of coronary artery disease and calcification. 52 Furthermore, patients with clonal hematopoiesis, especially non‐DNMT3A mutations, are at increased risk of incident heart failure, 53 and those with DNMT3A or TET2 mutations suffer from inferior long‐term clinical outcomes. 54

Emerging evidence consistently suggests the presence of vascular inflammation and increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines in individuals with clonal hematopoiesis are associated with increased atherosclerosis. 52 , 55 , 56 , 57 Therefore, targeting inflammation has emerged as a potential strategy for mitigating clonal hematopoiesis‐related cardiovascular risks. The Canakinumab Anti‐Inflammatory Thrombosis Outcome Study (CANTOS) demonstrated that canakinumab, an anti–IL‐1β monoclonal antibody, reduced recurrent cardiovascular events. 58 A preferential reduction in these events was specifically seen in patients with TET2‐mutated clones. 59 Similarly, a randomized, placebo‐controlled clinical trial with low‐dose colchicine (LoDoCo2) showed that colchicine 0.5 mg daily led to a significantly reduced risk of cardiovascular events in patients with chronic coronary artery disease, 60 with attenuated clonal expansion noted in TET2‐mutated clonal hematopoiesis. 61

Autoimmune disease

There are increasing implications of specific clonal hematopoiesis subtypes in the pathogenesis of autoimmune and inflammatory disorders. Significant associations have been identified with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis; CHIP is detected in approximately 30% of individuals with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis. 62 In contrast, although rheumatoid arthritis is prevalent among patients with myeloid malignancies, no clear association has been determined regarding the mutations or rate of clonal hematopoiesis in these individuals. 63 It has been hypothesized that mutations in TET2 or IDH1/2 may elevate the risk for autoimmune conditions by driving T‐cell dysregulation. 64 Somatic TET2 mutations have been linked to an increased risk for development of giant cell arteritis and associated vision loss. 65

Considerable clinical interest has shifted toward VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X‐linked, autoimmune, somatic) syndrome, a severe autoinflammatory disorder caused by somatic mutations in the UBA1 gene. 66 This disorder highlights a unique clinical spectrum where clonal hematopoiesis directly manifests as systemic inflammation, including relapsing polychondritis and a significantly increased risk of developing MDS. 67 , 68 , 69 Beyond VEXAS syndrome, the diagnostic and therapeutic implications of clonal hematopoiesis on inflammatory and autoimmune diseases remain incompletely defined.

Other comorbid conditions

Although a mechanistic link between clonal hematopoiesis and various comorbidities is still being established, a growing body of evidence suggests that clonal hematopoiesis serves as a potent driver of systemic inflammation and age‐related disease. Individuals with clonal hematopoiesis exhibit a significantly higher risk of acute kidney injury, 70 chronic kidney disease, 71 , 72 , 73 type 2 diabetes, 2 gout, 74 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, 7 , 75 and osteoporosis. 76 An increased incidence of venous thromboembolism has been correlated with the presence of CHIP, particularly involving JAK2 and TET2, in older adults. 77 , 78 Similarly, clonal hematopoiesis has also been strongly linked to chronic liver disease, with mutations in JAK2 and TET2 associated with a 17.6‐ and 5.4‐fold increased risk of cirrhosis and nonalcoholic hepatic steatosis, respectively. 79 Individuals with clonal hematopoiesis have an increased susceptibility to infections, 80 including HIV and severe COVID‐19. 81 , 82 Interestingly, a notable exception to the inflammatory burden is Alzheimer disease, where clonal hematopoiesis appears to have a protective effect. 83

CLINICAL MANAGEMENT CONSIDERATIONS

Many academic cancer centers have established dedicated clinics to counsel and monitor individuals with clonal hematopoiesis, 84 , 85 focusing on comorbidity mitigation and potential leukemia prevention. Most patients are referred after incidental CHIP/CCUS detection on next‐generation sequencing of peripheral blood, bone marrow, or plasma cell‐free DNA that was ordered to evaluate cytopenia, nonmyeloid malignancies, or potential inherited cancer predispositions. Regarding circulating tumor DNA, mutations are considered hematopoietic in origin if they are absent in matched tumor tissue sequencing, detected in the peripheral blood buffy coat controls, or present at a higher VAF in peripheral blood than tumor tissue. Although identification of CHIP/CCUS may result in earlier recognition of evolving myeloid neoplasms, widespread surveillance also carries potential harm. As clonal hematopoiesis is prevalent with aging, biologically heterogeneous, and not currently amenable to risk‐reducing interventions, detection may result in overdiagnosis, psychological burden, unnecessary testing, and health care use without established clinical benefit. Despite the absence of established consensus guidelines for the optimal surveillance and management of individuals with clonal hematopoiesis, there is clear demand for integrated, multidisciplinary strategies to guide clinical decision‐making.

Comorbidity mitigation

Because of the heightened risk of cardiovascular disease, standard cardiovascular screening is recommended for all patients with CHIP/CCUS. Proposed clinical guidelines suggest that these individuals undergo laboratory monitoring every 6 to 12 months, specifically for hemoglobin A1c, thyroid function, and lipid panels. 86 In symptomatic patients presenting with angina, immediate referral for a stress test or heart catheterization is necessary. In asymptomatic patients, management is dictated by age and risk scoring. 87 The use of coronary CT angiograms to calculate coronary artery calcium scores may add additional risk stratification to standard cardiovascular risk scores. 88 Additionally, comanagement with rheumatology specialists is warranted for patients with clonal hematopoiesis and suspected autoimmune disorders. Last, routine health maintenance, including age‐appropriate cancer screening and vaccinations, 89 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 should also be considered.

Leukemia prevention

Given the risk of leukemic transformation, close hematologic surveillance is meaningful, though the intensity of monitoring should be tailored to an individual’s specific risk profile. High‐risk patients by the CHRS or CCRS generally require more frequent assessments. 23 , 24 Current management strategies include routine laboratory work, with complete blood counts every 3 to 12 months, depending on the presence of cytopenia. Repeat mutation testing should be considered annually in high‐risk patients or with any clinical change. A bone marrow evaluation should be considered if patients develop symptoms concerning for progressive hematologic disease.

Certain antineoplastic agents, such as alkylating agents, topoisomerase inhibitors, lenalidomide (particularly with TP53 mutations), and PARP inhibitors, are associated with the development of therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms in some patients with clonal hematopoiesis. 5 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 As current risk stratification methods are not applicable in patients with cancer, 26 , 47 those with therapy‐related CHIP/CCUS warrant closer monitoring. For patients with cancer with CHIP/CCUS, a multidisciplinary collaborative risk‐benefit analysis regarding their oncologic treatment plan is essential. 98 This discussion must weigh the efficacy of planned antineoplastic therapy against the future risk of leukemic transformation. 5 As data mature, these genomic insights may increasingly inform potential treatment modifications, such as altering the timing or selection of therapeutics to minimize the risk of developing therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms without compromising the management of the primary cancer.

Concluded clinical trials

Several clinical trials in CHIP and CCUS have concluded. Xie et al. conducted a clinical trial of 3 months of high‐dose intravenous ascorbic acid in TET2‐mutated CCUS (NCT03418038). 99 The primary endpoint was hematologic improvement by International Working Group 2018 criteria for MDS, 100 and no responses were observed at week 20. Though originally with a goal enrollment of 55 patients, the study was terminated after 10 were enrolled, with three patients developing myeloid neoplasms and one patient meeting the criteria for chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) at diagnosis in hindsight. Mikkelsen et al. presented EVI‐2, a multicenter, randomized, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind phase II study of oral ascorbic acid 1000 mg/day for 12 months in patients with CCUS or lower risk MDS/CMML (NCT03682029). 101 Though the study failed to meet its primary endpoint of change in VAF and median number of mutations, an overall survival difference of not reached in the vitamin C arm and 42.2 months in the placebo arm (p = .0025) was noted, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 0.35. Modulation of plasma concentrations of inflammatory cytokines was also noted with oral ascorbic acid. 102

Bolton et al. presented PIMMs‐1, a decentralized phase II trial preventing IDH1‐mutant myeloid malignancies with ivosidenib 500 mg daily for up to 5 years in patients with IDH1‐mutated CCUS (NCT05030441). 103 Ivosidenib was well‐tolerated and resulted in 90% hematologic response rates, with rapid and sustained hematologic improvement. Both patients who progressed to a myeloid neoplasm exhibited expansion of preexisting, complex, high‐risk clones. Furthermore, as IDH1/2 mutations drive a subset of autoimmune disorders, all patients with autoimmune disease at trial enrollment had subjective improvement in their inflammatory symptoms with ivosidenib. 104

Furthermore, two clinical trials studying DFV890, an oral NRLP3 inhibitor, were recently terminated because of sponsor portfolio reprioritization. One evaluated the safety and tolerability of DFV890 monotherapy in CHRS high‐risk CCUS or lower risk MDS/CMML (NCT05552469), 105 whereas the other studied changes in IL‐6 and IL‐18 levels with DFV890 and MAS825, a bispecific anti‐IL1β and IL‐18 monoclonal antibody, in DNMT3A‐ or TET2‐mutated CHIP patients with a history of a myocardial infarction (NCT06097663).

Considerations for clinical trial development

Strategic intervention in clonal hematopoiesis must account for a variety of unique considerations inherent to this premalignant state. First, because CHIP/CCUS involves an aging, predominantly elderly population, there will be both comorbid conditions that are not associated with this premalignant state and competing risks of death when analyzing survival. Moreover, patients with CHIP/CCUS do not have a hematologic malignancy; therefore, the risks and benefits of therapies and the psychological burden of knowing about this premalignant disease need to be considered.

In addition, the specific clinical objective must be defined, as interventions may aim either to forestall leukemic transformation or to mitigate nonmalignant comorbidities associated with CHIP or CCUS. Furthermore, the choice of therapeutic intervention, such as targeted therapies, chemotherapy, anti‐inflammatory agents, and metabolic approaches, should be considered. Extrapolating from experience with myeloid neoplasms, the effectiveness of targeted agents and chemotherapy may be evaluated through hematologic improvement and response. However, the efficacy of anti‐inflammatory approaches may be more accurately reflected by longitudinal changes in inflammatory biomarkers.

Last, a significant challenge in the development of leukemic prevention trials is the relatively low prevalence of high‐risk disease within the broader clonal hematopoiesis population. Data from the CHRS and CCRS indicate that only approximately 1% of CHIP/CCUS and 10% of CCUS patients are at high‐risk of transformation, respectively. Thus, the sample size for clinical trials studying leukemic prevention in CHIP/CCUS will be small, necessitating multi‐institutional collaborations.

CONCLUSION

Clonal hematopoiesis, specifically CHIP/CCUS, represents a clinically significant premalignant condition with implications that extend beyond the risk of hematologic malignancies. Despite advances in risk stratification and improved understanding of its associations with cancer and nonmalignant comorbidities, major gaps remain in translating this knowledge into standardized clinical practice. The absence of consensus guidelines, Food and Drug Administration–approved interventions, validated surrogate endpoints for clinical trial design underscores the urgent and unmet need for robust, prospective research efforts. Expanding and refining clinical trial design will be crucial to identify effective strategies for both leukemia prevention and comorbidity reduction. A coordinated, multidisciplinary approach is essential to improve outcomes for individuals with clonal hematopoiesis.
