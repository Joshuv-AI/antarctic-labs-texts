Maximal estimates for orthonormal systems of wave equations
Shinya Kinoshita, Hyerim Ko, Shobu Shiraki

Abstract
. This paper investigates maximal estimates of the wave operators for orthonormal families of initial data. We extend the classical maximal estimates for the wave operator by making partial progress on maximal estimates for orthonormal systems in low dimensions. Our novel approach is based on a geometric analysis of the kernel of wave operators within the framework of Schatten 2 2 estimates. In particular, we exploit Wolff’s geometric lemma on the intersection patterns of thickened spheres.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Let
n\geq 1
. For
s>0
, let
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denote the inhomogeneous Sobolev space of order
s
equipped with the norm
\|g\|_{H^{s}}=\|(1-\Delta)^{s/2}g\|_{L^{2}}
. For
g\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we consider the wave equation
\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u=0
with initial conditions
u(x,0)=g(x)
and
\partial_{t}u(x,0)=0
.
The solution
u
, denoted by
\cos(t\sqrt{-\Delta})g
, can be represented as

\cos(t\sqrt{-\Delta})g(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big(U_{t}+U_{-t}\big)g(x),

where
U_{t}g(x)
is the solution to the half-wave equation

(1.1)

i\partial_{t}u+\sqrt{-\Delta}u=0

under the initial condition
u(x,0)=g(x)
.
Furthermore,
U_{t}g(x)
has the explicit representation

U_{t}g(x)=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|)}\widehat{g}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Maximal estimates for various operators have drawn considerable attention from different perspectives. A classical result due to
[
10
,
21
]
establishes that maximal estimates for single wave operator:

(1.2)

\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}|U_{t}g|\Big\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{B}}^{n})}\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

holds if and only if
s>\frac{1}{2}
. Here,
\mathbb{B}^{n}(x,r)
denotes the
n
-dimensional ball of radius
r>0
centered at
x
and
\mathbb{B}^{n}=\mathbb{B}^{n}(0,1)
represents the unit ball.

Historically, local maximal estimates of this type were first explored in the context of the Schrödinger equation, famously known as Carleson’s problem
[
7
,
11
]
. While our focus here is on the wave equation, interested readers may refer to
[
6
,
12
,
13
]
for recent breakthroughs on the Schrödinger equation variant.
The analysis of (
1.2
) for the wave equation is notably more straightforward, following from relatively simple arguments, whereas the Schrödinger case requires more delicate recent techniques.
For a detailed discussion, see Appendix
A
.

The maximal estimates for orthonormal systems
(f_{j})_{j}
in
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
present a natural extension of (
1.2
), where we consider infinitely many initial data, rather than a single datum. This generalization can be formulated as

(1.3)

\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L^{\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\lesssim\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}.

Here,
(\lambda_{j})_{j}\in\ell^{\beta}(\mathbb{C})
and
\beta\geq 1
.
In fact, this is equivalent to

\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\big(\sum_{j}|U_{t}g_{j}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big\|_{L^{2\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\lesssim\Big(\sum_{j}\|g_{j}\|_{H^{s}}^{2\beta}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}

for orthogonal systems
(g_{j})_{j}
in
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and it is straightforward to see (
1.3
) holds for
\beta=1
if
s>\frac{1}{2}
as a consequence of the aforementioned classical result for a single datum, along with a simple application of the triangle inequality. Furthermore, if the regularity is sufficiently smooth, namely when
s>\frac{n}{2}
, the Bessel inequality guarantees (
1.3
) holds with
\beta=\infty
, hence for any
\beta\geq 1
by the embedding
\ell^{\beta}\subset\ell^{\infty}
. In fact, assuming
(g_{j})_{j}
is orthonormal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and thus so is
(\widehat{g}_{j})_{j}
, we observe

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{|\lambda_{j}|}{\|\lambda_{j}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}}|U_{t}\langle D\rangle^{-s}g_{j}(x)|^{2}\leq\sum_{j}\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|)}\langle\xi\rangle^{-s}\widehat{g_{j}}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|^{2}\leq\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{-s}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
\langle D\rangle:=(1-\Delta)^{1/2}
and
\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}
. The right-hand side remains bounded as long as
s>\frac{n}{2}
.
Consequently, this also shows that there is nothing particularly interesting in the case
n=1
.
Additionally, when
s=\frac{n}{2}
, the estimate (
1.3
) holds for
\beta\in[1,\infty)
, as a consequence of (
3.4
) below together with a minor adjustment at low frequency.

With those discussions in mind, in the current manuscript, we aim to establish (
1.3
) for the largest possible
\beta
(depending on
s
) for
n\geq 2
and
s\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n}{2})
by making use of the orthogonal structure of the data.

1.2.
Previous works

The study of Strichartz estimates for orthonormal system has been made by Frank, Lewin, Lieb, and Serringer
[
14
]
and Frank–Sabin
[
16
,
15
]
for some operators such as Schrödinger operators.
The result was later extended by Bez et al.
[
1
]
up to
a sharp range for non-sharp admissible cases considering homogeneous initial datum.
Strichartz estimates for various dispersive operators (cf.
[
5
]
)
and some of the boundary cases have been obtained by
[
3
,
2
]
.

The Strichartz estimates for orthonormal system for wave equation seem more difficult and the sharp characterization in terms of the function spaces remains an open problem. In particular, the following diagonal Strichartz estimate has been conjectured (for a more general statement, see
[
5
,
2
]
).

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
q\geq\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
. Then, we have

\Big\|\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x,t}^{\frac{q}{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}\lesssim\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}

for orthonormal systems
(f_{j})_{j}
in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
if
\beta\leq\frac{n}{2(n+1)}q
.

The study of maximal estimates in the context of orthonormal systems is even more recent. Bez, Lee, and Nakamura
[
4
]
were the first to obtain a maximal-in-time estimate for the Schrödinger equation in one dimension, achieving the largest possible
\beta
when the regularity is at its minimal value,
s=\frac{1}{4}
. Their method involves first establishing corresponding maximal-in-space estimates and then applying the technique of swapping the roles of space and time to derive the maximal-in-time estimates.
Subsequently, Bez and two of the present authors
[
3
]
adopted a more direct approach to extend these results beyond
s=\frac{1}{4}
, including for fractional Schrödinger operators. They also addressed cases where
s
is relatively close to
\frac{n}{2}
in higher dimensions.

1.3.
Main results

Similar challenges to those in the Strichartz estimates arise when considering the maximal estimates for wave operators. We address these challenges and obtain new, non-trivial results that represent partial progress toward the optimal bounds.

To begin, we propose our conjecture concerning the maximal estimates for orthonormal systems in the wave equation framework. The conjecture is based on modifications of the counterexamples from the single-datum setting in
[

2

[

for the Schrödinger equation.
Throughout the paper, we consider the orthonormal function of initial data
(f_{j})_{j}

However, all our results remain valid for

1.4.
Pointwise convergence

Naturally, the equation (
1.1
) can be extended to the operator-valued equation:

(1.8)

i\partial_{t}\gamma=[-\sqrt{-\Delta},\gamma]

under the condition
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}
,
where
\gamma_{0}
is a compact self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
Precisely, taking
\gamma_{0}
as the projection onto the span of
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the solution
\gamma(t)
of (
1.8
), described as
\gamma(t)=U_{t}\gamma_{0}U_{-t}
, is the projection onto the span of
U_{t}f
.

An immediate consequence of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
is the pointwise convergence of the density of an operator in a certain class. For
\beta\geq 1
let

\mathcal{C}^{\beta}
be the Schatten space of all compact operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
equipped with the norm
\|T\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}}:=\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}<\infty
. Here,
(\lambda_{j})_{j}
are the singular values of
T
.
Let us recall a precise definition of a density of an operator in the Schatten–Sobolev space
\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}
defined by

\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}=\{\gamma\in\mathrm{Com}(H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))\,:\,\|\gamma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}}:=\|\langle-\Delta\rangle^{\frac{s}{2}}\gamma\langle-\Delta\rangle^{\frac{s}{2}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))}<\infty\}.

Here,
\mathrm{Com}(H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
denotes the set of compact operators from
H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
to
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

For
\beta,s
satisfying the conditions in (
1.5
) or (
1.6
), the density of an operator in
\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}
can be represented as a limit of a sequence of density of finite-rank operators.
Precisely, let
\gamma_{0}
be an operator in
\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}
where
\beta,s
satisfy the conditions in (
1.5
) or (
1.6
). Suppose that
T:=\langle-\Delta\rangle^{\frac{s}{2}}\gamma\langle-\Delta\rangle^{\frac{s}{2}}
is a self-adjoint operator in
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
. Then there exists an orthonormal family
(f_{j})_{j}\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
(\lambda_{j})_{j}\in\ell^{\beta}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
where
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is the set of positive real numbers such that

T^{N}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}\Pi_{f_{j}}\to T\qquad(N\to\infty)

in
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
.
Here
\Pi_{f}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the span of
f
. The density of
T^{N}
is given by

\rho_{T^{N}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}|f_{j}(x)|^{2},

and then the density of
\gamma^{N}:=\langle-\Delta\rangle^{-\frac{s}{2}}T^{N}\langle-\Delta\rangle^{-\frac{s}{2}}
is formally given by

\rho_{\gamma^{N}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}|\langle-\Delta\rangle^{-\frac{s}{2}}f_{j}(x)|^{2}.

We define the density of
\gamma
as a limit of
\rho_{\gamma^{N}}
, namely,

\rho_{\gamma}(x)=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\langle-\Delta\rangle^{-\frac{s}{2}}f_{j}(x)|^{2}.

Since the solution of (
1.8
) with initial data
\gamma_{0}
can be written as
\gamma(t)=U_{t}\gamma_{0}U_{-t}
, the density of
\gamma(t)
takes the form

\rho_{\gamma(t)}(x)=\rho_{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\scriptsize{$U_{t}\gamma_{0}U_{-t}$}}}(x)=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\langle-\Delta\rangle^{-\frac{s}{2}}U_{t}f_{j}(x)|^{2}.

For further details, we refer to Section 6 of
[
3
]
.

Consider an analogue of classical pointwise convergence problems with finding the largest possible class of initial data
\gamma_{0}
for which the following holds:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\rho_{\gamma(t)}(x)=\rho_{\gamma_{0}}(x)

for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In view of Conjecture
1.2
, we expect that (
1.9
) holds provided

s>\max\Big\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2\beta},~\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{n-1}{4\beta}\Big\}.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n}{2})
and
1\leq\beta\leq\infty
. Then we have

(
i
)

For
n=3,4
, if
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta,s}
is self-adjoint with
\beta,s
satisfying

s>\max\big\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{2n-1}{4\beta},~\frac{2n-1}{4}-\frac{2n-3}{4\beta}\big\},

and

(

For

is self-adjoint with
\beta

satisfying

2.
Necessary conditions

In this section, we discuss the necessary conditions for
s,\beta
for which (
1.3
) holds.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\beta\geq 1
. Then there exists orthonormal families of functions
\{f_{j}\}
in
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for which the estimate (
1.3
) fails when

\beta>\min\Big\{\frac{n}{n-2s},\frac{n-1}{n+1-4s}\Big\}.

2.1.
Proof of
\beta\leq\frac{n}{n-2s}

Suppose (
1.3
) holds. To show the necessity of
\beta\leq\frac{n}{n-2s}
,
we employ the same example as in
[
3
]
. For the sake of completeness, we include a sketch of the proof. For an integer
N\gg 1
, let

J_{j}=[N^{-1}j,N^{-1}j+(100N)^{-1}]

for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
One may find
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
supported on the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{n}
such that
\widehat{\eta}
is non-negative, and
\int_{|\xi|\geq 1}\widehat{\eta}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\leq\epsilon\|\widehat{\eta}\|_{1}
for a small
\epsilon>0
.
Let
\mathit{J}:=\{\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:J_{j_{i}}\cap\mathbb{B}^{1}\neq\emptyset~\text{for all~}i=1,\dots,n\}
. Then, for

\textbf{j}\in\mathit{J}
,
set the orthonormal initial data

f_{\textbf{j}}(x)=cN^{\frac{n}{2}-s}\eta\big(10N(x-N^{-1}\textbf{j})\big)

for some appropriate constant
c>0
so that
\|f_{\mathbf{j}}\|_{H^{s}}=1
.
We also choose
\lambda_{\textbf{j}}=1
if
\textbf{j}\in\mathit{J}
and
0
otherwise so that
\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}\sim|\mathit{J}|^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\sim N^{\frac{n}{\beta}}
.

With our choice of
(f_{\textbf{j}})_{\textbf{j}}
and
(\lambda_{\textbf{j}})_{\textbf{j}}
, one may observe that for each
j
,

\displaystyle|U_{t}f_{\textbf{j}}(x)|=cN^{\frac{n}{2}-s}\Big|\int e^{i(10N(x-N^{-1}\textbf{j})\cdot\xi+10Nt|\xi|)}\widehat{\eta}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|\gtrsim N^{\frac{n}{2}-s}

whenever
t=t_{N}:=(100N)^{-1}
and
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}(N^{-1}\textbf{j},(100N)^{-1})=:{\mathbb{B}}_{\textbf{j}}^{n}

since the phase is fairly small (at most
\frac{1}{2}
).
Therefore, we obtain

\Big\|\sum_{\textbf{j}\in\mathit{J}}|U_{t}f_{\textbf{j}}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{t}^{\infty}}\geq\sum_{\textbf{j}\in\mathit{J}}\|U_{t_{N}}f_{\mathbf{j}}\|_{L_{x}^{2}({\mathbb{B}}_{\textbf{j}}^{n})}^{2}\gtrsim N^{n-2s}.

Hence, assuming (
1.3
), we obtain
N^{n-2s}\lesssim N^{\frac{n}{\beta}}
, which only holds for large
N
if
\beta\leq\frac{n}{n-2s}
.
∎

2.2.
Proof of
\beta\leq\frac{n-1}{n+1-4s}

Next, we shall show
\beta\leq\frac{n-1}{n+1-4s}

is necessary for (
1.3
) to hold. For an integer

\bar{\mathit{J}_{j}}:=[N^{-\frac{1}{2}}j,N^{-\frac{1}{2}}j+100^{-1}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}]

for

Let
\phi_{P}
be a Schwartz function essentially supported on

P=\{\xi=(\xi_{1},\bar{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:N\leq\xi_{1}\leq 2N,\ |\bar{\xi}|\leq N^{\frac{1}{2}}\}.

For
\bar{\textbf{j}}\in\overline{\mathit{J}}
where
\overline{\mathit{J}}:=\{\bar{\textbf{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n-1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}:\bar{J_{j_{i}}}\cap\mathbb{B}^{1}\not=\emptyset~\text{for all}~i=1,\dots,n\}
, we define an orthonormal initial data

\widehat{f_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}}}(\xi)=cN^{-\frac{n+1}{4}-s}e^{-i\langle\bar{\textbf{j}},\,\bar{\xi}\rangle}\phi_{P}(\xi),

where the constant
c>0
is chosen so that
\|f_{\,\bar{\mathbf{j}}}\|_{H^{s}}=1
and
(f_{\,\bar{\mathbf{j}}})_{\bar{\mathbf{j}}}
forms an orthonormal basis in
H^{s}
. We also choose
\lambda_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}}=1
if
\bar{\textbf{j}}\in\overline{\mathit{J}}
and
0
otherwise so that
\|\lambda_{\,\bar{\mathbf{j}}}\|_{\ell^{\beta}}=|\overline{\mathit{J}}|^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\lesssim N^{\frac{n-1}{2\beta}}
.
With our choice of
(f_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}})_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}
and
(\lambda_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}})_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}
, one may observe that for each
\bar{\textbf{j}}

|U_{t}f_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}}(x,t)|=cN^{-\frac{n+1}{4}-s}\Big|\int e^{i(\langle\bar{x}-\bar{\textbf{j}},\,\bar{\xi}\rangle+(x_{1}+t)\xi_{1}+t(|\xi|-\xi_{1}))}\phi_{P}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|\gtrsim cN^{\frac{n+1}{4}-s}

whenever
t=-x_{1}
and
x\in Q_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{n}(0,100^{-1}):|\bar{x}-\bar{\textbf{j}}|\leq 100^{-1}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}
since
|\xi|-\xi_{1}=|\bar{\xi}|^{2}/(|\xi|+\xi_{1})\leq|\bar{\xi}|^{2}/\xi_{1}
guarantees the phase is small enough. Therefore, noting
Q_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}
are disjoint and each has the volume
\sim N^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}
, we obtain

\Big\|\sum_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}|U_{t}f_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{t}^{\infty}}\gtrsim\sum_{\bar{\mathbf{j}}}\|U_{-x_{1}}f_{\,\bar{\mathbf{j}}}\|_{L_{x}^{2}(Q_{\bar{\textbf{j}}})}^{2}\gtrsim N^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2s}\Big|\bigcup_{\bar{\textbf{j}}}Q_{\,\bar{\textbf{j}}}\Big|\sim N^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2s}.

Hence, assuming (
1.3
), we obtain
N^{\frac{n+1}{2}-2s}\lesssim N^{\frac{n-1}{2\beta}}
, which only holds for large
N
if
\beta\leq\frac{n-1}{n+1-4s}
.
∎

3.
Localization and reductions

In this section, we introduce several tools to demonstrate Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
, some of which are fairly standard but included for the sake of completeness.

3.1.
Frequency localization

We start with a standard argument, also employed in
[
3
, Section 6.2]
. In the rest of the paper, we denote by
(f_{j})_{j}
an orthonormal basis in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and by
(\lambda_{j})_{j}
the sequence in
\ell^{\beta}
for appropriate
\beta\geq 1
in the context.
Let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
supported in
\{r\in\mathbb{R}:2^{-1}\leq r<2\}
and
\varphi_{k}=\varphi(2^{-k}\cdot)
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Associated with
\varphi
we define
the standard Littlewood–Paley projection operator
P_{k}
by
P_{k}f=(\varphi_{k}(|\cdot|)\widehat{f})^{\vee}
.

Then, Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
follow from the frequency localized estimates

(3.1)

\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\lesssim 2^{2\varsigma k}\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}

for
k\geq 1
,
where
\varsigma>\varsigma^{\star}
given by

\varsigma^{\star}=\begin{cases}\max\big\{1-\frac{9}{14\beta},~\frac{6}{7}-\frac{5}{14\beta}\big\},&\quad n=2,\\[4.30554pt]
\max\big\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{2n-1}{4\beta},~\frac{2n-1}{4}-\frac{2n-3}{4\beta}\big\},&\quad n=3,4.\end{cases}

In fact, for
s\in(0,\frac{n}{2})
the Littlewood–Paley decomposition yields

\displaystyle\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}\langle D\rangle^{-s}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}2^{-2sk}\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}.

The term with
k<0
can be controlled using the trivial bound

(3.2)

\Big\|\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{t}^{\infty}}\lesssim 2^{nk}\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\infty}}

which is obtained by Bessel’s inequality (refer to (
1.4
) or
[
16
,
1
]
).

Because of this, we restrict our attention to
k\geq 0
in the rest of the paper.

3.2.
Interpolation and reduction to
\beta=2

Showing (
3.1
) when
\beta=1
and
\beta=\infty
is relatively easy.
For (
3.1
) with
\beta=1
, we recall the classical result for a single initial datum (see (
1.2
) with
p=2
and Appendix
A
). This combined with triangle inequality gives

(3.3)

\Big\|\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{1}L_{t}^{\infty}}\lesssim\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\big\|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{\infty}}^{2}\lesssim 2^{k}\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{1}}.

By interpolating between (
3.3
) and (
3.2
), we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}\langle D\rangle^{-s}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\beta}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\lesssim 2^{(n-\frac{n-1}{\beta}-2s)k}\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}

for all
1\leq\beta\leq\infty
. Summing over
k\geq 0
, the estimate (
1.3
) holds whenever
s>\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-1}{2\beta}
, but this is significantly larger than the conjectured regularity
\max\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2\beta},\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{n-1}{4\beta}\}
.

A typical approach to the problem is to establish some (ideally, sharp) bounds for
\beta=2
, (
3.5
)
and interpolate the cases
\beta=1,2,\infty
.
Due to Proposition
2.1
, the best one can hope for is that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Big\|\sup_{0<t<1}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|U_{t}P_{k}f_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})}\lessapprox 2^{\frac{1}{2}(n+1-\sigma)k}\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{2}}

for
\sigma=\sigma_{n}
given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{if}\quad n=2,\\
1,\quad\text{if}\quad n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Here,
we write
A\lessapprox B
to mean
A\lesssim C_{\epsilon}^{\epsilon k}B
for
A,B>0
, and some constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
depending on arbitrary small
\epsilon>0
. Although the bound (
3.5
) alone is not sufficient to obtain sharp estimates for all
\beta
via interpolation, this becomes relevant only after (
3.5
) is established with the optimal exponent
\sigma=\sigma_{n}
. Further refinement is then required, as observed for instance in
[
3
]
in the context of the Schrödinger equation.

The trivial bound (
3.4
) coincides with (
3.5
) when
\sigma=0
.
As we mentioned in the Introduction, a natural approach would be to adapt the techniques from the single-datum setting, such as (
1.2
) or (
A.1
), to handle this conjecture. However, this approach proves inefficient in this context, as even resolving Conjecture
1.1
would still only give us (
3.5
) with
\sigma=\frac{n}{2(n+1)}
. Therefore, in the remainder of this paper, we will instead employ a geometric argument to obtain the following results. To state our results, set

(3.6)

\sigma_{n}^{\star}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{7}=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{28}\ &\text{if}\quad n=2,\\
\frac{1}{2}\ &\text{if}\quad n=3,4.\end{cases}

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n=2,3,4
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, (
3.5
) holds for
\sigma=\sigma_{n}^{\star}
.

Consequently, our goal (
3.1
) is obtained by interpolating this with (
3.3
) and (
3.2
). To obtain Proposition
3.1
, we make use of some geometric properties of cones and circles (Section
5
). Moreover, exploiting the self-similar structure of the inequality, we further refine our results for
n=2
.

3.3.
Duality

Recall that
\mathcal{C}^{p}
is the Schatten space
equipped with the norm
\|T\|_{\mathcal{C}^{p}}:=\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{p}}<\infty
where
(\lambda_{j})_{j}
are the singular values of
T
.
The most important property of the Schatten space in the current manuscript is that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm and coincides with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}
of the kernel of the corresponding operator.

We apply the following lemma to dualize (
3.1
) to the corresponding bound with respect to
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-norm.

Lemma 3.2

(Duality principle
[
16
,
1
]
)
.

Suppose
T
is a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
L_{x}^{r}L_{t}^{q}
for some
q,r\geq 2
. Also, let
\beta\geq 1
. Then,

\Big\|\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|Tf_{j}|^{2}\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{r}{2}}L_{t}^{\frac{q}{2}}}\lesssim\|\lambda\|_{\ell^{\beta}}

holds for all orthonormal systems
(f_{j})_{j}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and all sequences
(\lambda_{j})_{j}\in\ell^{\beta}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if

\|WTT^{*}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta^{\prime}}}\lesssim\|W\|_{L_{x}^{\widetilde{r}}L_{t}^{\widetilde{q}}}^{2}

for all
W\in L_{x}^{\widetilde{r}}L_{t}^{\widetilde{q}}
. Here,
\widetilde{\cdot}
denotes the half conjugate given by

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}}=\frac{1}{2}

and
\cdot^{\prime}
denotes the (usual Hölder) conjugate given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta^{\prime}}=1.

By Lemma
3.2
, Proposition
3.1
is equivalent to the following: Let
T_{k}=(U_{t}P_{k})(U_{t}P_{k})^{*}
, then

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|WT_{k}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}\lesssim 2^{\frac{(n+1-\sigma)}{2}k}\|W\|_{L^{4}_{x}L_{t}^{2}}^{2}

holds for all
W\in L_{x}^{4}L_{t}^{2}
with
\sigma=\sigma_{n}^{\star}
when
n=2,3,4
.
Here,

T_{k}F=F\ast K_{k}

where the kernel
K_{k}
is given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle K_{k}(x,t)=\int e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|)}\varphi_{k}^{2}(|\xi|)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

As we mentioned at the beginning of this section,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is the Hilbert–Schmidt class and the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-norm can be interpreted as

(3.9)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\|WT_{k}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}(L^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1}),L^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n+1}))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{B}}^{n+1}}\iint_{{\mathbb{B}}^{n+1}}\Big|W(x,t)K_{k}(x-x^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})W(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

Here, we may assume that
W(x,t)
,
\overline{W}(x,t)
are supported on
\mathbb{B}^{n+1}
since
U_{t}P_{k}f
is supported on
\mathbb{B}^{n}\times(0,1)
.

3.4.
Kernel estimates

We recall the well-known property of the kernel of the wave operator (see for example
[
18
]
), which can be derived by using polar coordinates and asymptotic behavior of the Bessel functions.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
. Then for any
N\geq 1
,

\Big|\int e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|)}\varphi_{k}^{2}(|\xi|)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|\leq C_{N}2^{nk}(1+2^{k}|x|)^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}(1+2^{k}||x|-|t||)^{-N}.

If one invokes Lemma
3.3
in a trivial manner, it follows from (
3.9
) that

\|WT_{k}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim 2^{2nk}\int|W(x,t)|^{2}|\overline{W}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})|^{2}(1+2^{k}|x-x^{\prime}|)^{-(n-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

A simple application of Young’s convolution inequality, for instance, immediately yields (
3.7
) with

\sigma=0,\quad n\geq 2.

However, this bound can be trivially obtained by an interpolation between the estimates (
3.3
) and (
3.2
).

Unlike the Schrödinger operator, the argument presented above does not yield sharp
\mathcal{C}^{2}
estimates.
It appears that the use of the additional properties of the kernel
K_{k}
, which is supported on a small neighborhood of the cone
\{(x,|x|):x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\}
, is crucial for proving the sharp results.
To overcome this difficulty, we exploit the support property of the kernel to derive the improved
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-estimates which are to be stated in the subsequent sections.

4.
Further reduction to geometric estimates

As briefly mentioned in the introduction, a bilinear integral formed by a geometry from the wave equation plays a central role in understanding Proposition
3.1
.
For
0\leq l\leq k
, let

\mathcal{I}_{k,l}:=\iint h(x,t)h(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\chi_{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}}(x-x^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

where

\mathbb{V}_{k,l}
denotes an upper cone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defined by

\mathbb{V}_{k,l}:=\big\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\times[0,1]:||x|-|t||\lesssim 2^{-k},~2^{-l-1}\leq|x|\leq 2^{-l}\big\}.

Then, we claim the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n=3,4
and
0\leq l<k
. For
\sigma=\sigma_{n}^{\star}
given by (
3.6
), then we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,l}\lessapprox 2^{-\sigma k}2^{-(n-1)l}\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}^{2}

holds for any
h\in L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}
.

We observe below that
Proposition
3.1
follows from Proposition
4.1
.
The proof of Proposition
4.1
will be postponed to Section
5
.

4.1.
Reduction from Proposition
3.1
to Proposition
4.1

By the reduction in (
3.7
), it suffices to show that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|WT_{k}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lessapprox 2^{(n+1-\sigma_{n}^{\star})k}\|W\|_{L_{x}^{4}L_{t}^{2}}^{4}

where
\sigma_{n}^{\star}
is given by (
3.6
).

Recalling the kernel
K_{k}
defined by (
3.8
),
we further apply a spatial decomposition, given by
(2^{l})_{l}
, to the kernel, namely, for a fixed
k
,
we define
\chi_{l}(t)=\chi_{[2^{-l-1},2^{-l})}(t)
for
0\leq l\leq k-1
and
\chi_{l}(t)=\chi_{[0,2^{-k})}(t)
for
l=k
.
We set
K_{k,l}(x,t)=\chi_{l}(|x|)K_{k}(x,t)

so that

T_{k}F=\sum_{0\leq l\leq k}T_{k,l}F:=\sum_{0\leq l\leq k}F\ast K_{k,l}.

Let us denote
h(x,t):=|W(x,t)|^{2}
so that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|WT_{k,l}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\!\!\iiiint h(x,t)h(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})|K_{k,l}(x-x^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

To analyze the integral, we consider the support of the kernel.
When
l=k
, Lemma
3.3
implies that the kernel
|K_{k,l}|
is bounded by
2^{nk}\chi_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}(0,2^{-k})}
, which is easy to handle.
In fact, by setting
B_{k}:=\mathbb{B}^{n+1}(0,2^{-k})
, Young’s convolution inequality yields

\|h(h\ast\chi_{B_{k}})\|_{L_{x,t}^{1}}\lesssim\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}\|h\ast\chi_{B_{k}}\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{\infty}}\lesssim 2^{-nk}\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}^{2}.

This estimate is sufficient to prove Proposition
4.1
with the conjectured value
\sigma=\sigma_{n}=1
in the case
l=k
.

When
0\leq l\leq k-1
, we decompose the domain into three parts: two corresponding to

\pm\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon}:=\big\{(x,\pm t)\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\times[0,1]:||x|-|t||\lesssim 2^{-(1-\epsilon)k},~2^{-l-1}\leq|x|\leq 2^{-l}\big\},

which denote the upper and lower cones,
and their complement

\mathbb{B}^{n+1}\setminus\big\{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon}\cup(-\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon})\big\}
. The complement is a minor part since, by Lemma
3.3
, the kernel satisfies

|K_{k,l}(x,t)\chi_{\mathbb{B}^{n+1}\setminus\{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon}\cup(-\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon})\}}(x,t)|\lesssim_{N}2^{-Nk}

for any
N\geq 1
.

This estimate suffices to consider the integral (
4.3
) over
\mathbb{V}_{k,\ell}^{\epsilon}\cup(-\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon})
.
We set

\mathcal{I}_{k,l}^{\epsilon,\pm}=\iint h(x,t)h\ast\chi_{(\pm\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon})}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

Since
|K_{k,l}(x,t)|^{2}\lesssim 2^{(n+1)k}2^{-(n-1)l}
provided
(x,t)\in\pm\mathbb{V}_{k,l}^{\epsilon}
by Lemma
3.3
, combining three cases yields

(4.4)

\displaystyle\|WT_{k,l}\overline{W}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}\lesssim 2^{(n+1)k}2^{(n-1)l}\sum_{\pm}\mathcal{I}_{k,l}^{\epsilon,\pm}+2^{-Nk}\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}^{2}.

By symmetry, it is enough to estimate
\mathcal{I}_{k,l}^{\epsilon,+}
.
Further allowing
2^{\epsilon k}
loss, we reduce our goal to estimating
\mathcal{I}_{k,l}
, which is the statement of Proposition
4.1
.

5.
Proof of Proposition
4.1
when
n=3,4

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
establishing Proposition
4.1
. The key step involves analyzing the interaction between two
2^{-k}
-fattened cones in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. In particular, the size of the vertically projected intersection plays a crucial role.

5.1.
Reduction to geometric intersection estimates

By the Hölder inequality, we observe that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,l}\leq\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}\big\|h\ast\chi_{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}}\big\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{\infty}}.

For simplicity, we denote by
w=(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
.
After taking a square and expanding the integral of the right-hand side, and then applying Fubini’s theorem, we see that

\displaystyle\|h*\chi_{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}}\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\iint h(w_{1})h(w_{2})\int\sup_{t}\chi_{\mathbb{V}_{k,l}(w_{1})\cap\mathbb{V}_{k,l}(w_{2})}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w_{1}\mathrm{d}w_{2}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\leq\iint h(w_{1})h(w_{2})|\mathbb{W}(w_{1},w_{2})|\,\mathrm{d}w_{1}\mathrm{d}w_{2},

where
|\mathbb{W}(w_{1},w_{2})|
is the Lebesgue measure of the set defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(w_{1},w_{2})=\textsf{Proj}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}(\mathbb{V}_{k,l}(w_{1})\cap\mathbb{V}_{k,l}(w_{2}))

and
\mathbb{V}_{k,l}(w):=\mathbb{V}_{k,l}+w
, the translation of
\mathbb{V}_{k,l}
by the vector
w
.
By dividing the intersection into level set
\{t=t_{n}\}
, it is now important to observe the intersection of the circles and spheres of thickness
2^{-k}
.

5.2.
Geometric estimates for sphere intersection

We now measure the size of the intersection of two thickened circles.
For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
r>0
, we denote a
\delta
-neighborhood of the sphere
r\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
centered at
x
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by

\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x,r)=\big\{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:||z-x|-r|\leq\delta\big\}.

The following is a slight variant of Wolff’s lemma
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
(see also
[
19
]
and
[
8
, Lemma B.2]
) which includes both the external and internal tangent cases. Define

\varDelta=\varDelta(x_{1},x_{2},r_{1},r_{2})
by

(5.4)

\displaystyle\varDelta:=\big||x_{1}-x_{2}|-|r_{1}+r_{2}|\big|\times\big||x_{1}-x_{2}|-|r_{1}-r_{2}|\big|.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
r_{1},r_{2}>0
, and

0<\delta\leq 1
.
Then,

(5.5)

|\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{1},r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})|\lesssim\frac{\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}(r_{1}+r_{2})}{(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{\delta(r_{1}+r_{2})}{\varDelta+\delta(r_{1}+r_{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The proof of Lemma
5.1
is almost identical to that of
[
22
]
, but stated in a more precise manner. For the reader’s convenience, we provide the detail in Appendix
B
.

There is also a natural extension of this result to higher dimensions, specifically for estimating the intersection of two spheres in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
. This was observed in
[
8
, Lemma B.1]
by employing an additional slicing argument alongside Lemma
5.1
.
For its proof, see Appendix
B
as well.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Let
r_{1},r_{2}>0
. Then

(5.6)

\displaystyle|\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{1},r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})|\lesssim\frac{\delta^{2}(r_{1}+r_{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\delta}\Big(\frac{\varDelta+\delta(r_{1}+r_{2})}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\delta}\Big)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}.

5.3.
Proof of Proposition
4.1
when
n=3,4

We claim that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t_{1},t_{2}<1}|\mathbb{W}(x_{1},t_{1},x_{2},t_{2})|\lesssim 2^{-k}2^{-(n-\frac{1}{2})l}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}

provided
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq 2^{-k}
when
n\geq 3
.
First, assuming (
5.7
) for the moment, we proceed to prove (
4.2
). Note that
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\lesssim 2^{-l}
by the definition of
\mathbb{V}_{k,l}
.
By Hölder’s inequality, the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is bounded by

\displaystyle 2^{-k}2^{-(n-\frac{1}{2})l}\|h\|_{L_{x}^{2}L_{t}^{1}}\big\|\|h(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{t}^{1}}\ast\big(|\cdot|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\chi_{|\cdot|\leq 2^{-l}}\big)(x)\big\|_{L_{x}^{2}}.

By Young’s convolution inequality, it follows that

Appendix A
Maximal estimates for single datum

In this section, we review classical maximal estimates
for single wave operators, providing detailed proofs. While these results are well-known (cf.
[
9
,
17
]
), the proof offers valuable insight into techniques used to prove analogs for orthonormal systems.

Consider initial data in the inhomogeneous Sobolev spaces
H^{s}
. Since the lower frequency plays no significant role in our analysis, our argument extends naturally to a general family of operators, including Klein–Gordon operators.
We define
U_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)=e^{it\sqrt{\alpha-\Delta}}f(x)=(2\pi)^{-n}\int e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t\sqrt{\alpha+|\xi|^{2}})}\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi
, where
U_{t}^{0}=U_{t}
corresponds to the wave operator. For
n\geq 1
, we have the following results.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
s\in[0,\frac{n}{2})
,
r\geq 2
, and
\alpha\geq 0
. Then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\big\|U_{t}^{\alpha}f\big\|_{L_{x}^{r}({\mathbb{B}}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}(0,1)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}

holds if

s>\max\Big\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{r},~\frac{n+1}{r}-\frac{n-1}{2r}\Big\}.

Furthermore, the regularity orders are sharp up to endpoints.

This is typically stated with
\|f\|_{H^{s}}
on the right-hand side, but it can be replaced by
\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
due to (
A.2
) provided that
s<\frac{n}{2}
.
The model case is, of course, the wave operator when
\alpha=0
(
[
10
,
20
,
9
]
), but a straightforward modification allows us to extend the result to general
\alpha>0
.
We present the proof of this extension below.

A.1.
Sufficiency

Let us show the sufficiency part of (
A.1
) first. Recall the frequency localization
P_{k}
associated with
\varphi_{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Set
{r_{\textsf{ST}}}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}
. Note that the choice
r={r_{\textsf{ST}}}
is equivalent to
\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{r}=\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{n-1}{2r}
whenever
n\geq 2
.
We have the trivial bound

\|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}(0,1)}\lesssim 2^{\frac{n}{2}k}\|P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

In particular, for
k<0
this provides

(A.2)

\sum_{k<0}\|U_{t}^{\alpha}f\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}({\mathbb{B}}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}(0,1)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}

whenever
s<\frac{n}{2}
. By Hölder’s inequality,
this trivially implies
\dot{H}^{s}
–

If we further establish

(A.3)

\displaystyle\|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f\|_{L_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}(0,1)}\lesssim 2^{\frac{k}{2}}\|P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}},

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f\|_{L_{x}^{r_{\textsf{ST}}}(\mathbb{B}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}(0,1)}\lesssim 2^{(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{{r_{\textsf{ST}}}})k}\|P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}},

for
k\geq 0
, a routine interpolation argument yields (
A.1
).

To derive (
A.3
) and (

), we use the Sobolev embedding, which follows directly from the fundamental theorem of calculus, and Hölder’s inequality. Indeed, for

,

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<1}|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f(x)|^{r}\leq|U_{0}^{\alpha}P_{k}f(x)|^{r}+2^{k}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f(x)|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{r-1}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}|U_{t}^{\alpha}\tilde{P_{k}}f(x)|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)

holds where
\tilde{P}_{k}
is a projection operator satisfying
U_{t}^{\alpha}\tilde{P}_{k}f=2^{-k}\partial_{t}(U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f)
. Since
\|\tilde{P}_{k}f\|_{2}\sim\|P_{k}f\|_{2}
, it suffices to consider
U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f
.

When
r=2
, by Plancherel’s theorem we have

\|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,1))}\lesssim\|P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}.

When
r={r_{\textsf{ST}}}
, Strichartz estimate (after appropriate rescaling) implies

\|U_{t}^{\alpha}P_{k}f\|_{L^{r_{\textsf{ST}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})}\lesssim 2^{(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n+1}{{r_{\textsf{ST}}}})k}\|P_{k}f\|_{L^{2}}.

Hence, by the Sobolev embedding those estimates give (
A.3
) and (
A.4
).
This finishes the proof.
∎

We now prove the necessity part.
We first show that

N\geq\alpha
. Hence
N^{\frac{n}{r}}\leq\big\|\sup_{t}|U_{t}^{\alpha}f|\big\|_{L_{x}^{r}}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim N^{s+\frac{n}{2}}
holds for large
N
. Taking
N\rightarrow\infty
, we see that
s\geq\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{r}
is necessary.

To see
s\geq\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{n-1}{2r}
is necessary for (
A.1
) to hold, we consider the function

\widehat{f}(\xi)=N^{-\frac{n-1}{4}}\phi_{P}(\xi)

for
P=\{\xi=(\xi_{1},\bar{\xi})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}:N\leq\xi_{1}\leq 2N,\ |\bar{\xi}|\leq c_{0}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\}
and a sufficiently small
0<c_{0}\ll 1
. Clearly,
|P|\simeq N^{\frac{n+1}{2}}
and
\|f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim N^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Rearranging the phase, we express

|U_{t}^{\alpha}f(x)|=(2\pi)^{-n}N^{-\frac{n-1}{4}}\Big|\int e^{i(\bar{x}\cdot\bar{\xi}+(x_{1}+t)\xi_{1}+t(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}-\xi_{1}))}\phi_{P}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|.

Using the fact that
\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+|\xi|^{2}}-\xi_{1}=\xi_{1}\sqrt{1+(\alpha^{2}+|\overline{\xi}|^{2})/\xi_{1}^{2}}-\xi_{1}=(\alpha^{2}+|\overline{\xi}|^{2})/\xi_{1}+O(c_{0}^{4})
provided that
\xi\in P
and
N\geq\alpha
.
One can verify that the phase is bounded by
\frac{1}{2}
when
(x,t)
belonging to the set
Q=\{(x_{1},\bar{x},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{R}}:|x_{1}+t|\leq\frac{1}{100}N^{-1},\ |\bar{x}|\leq\frac{1}{100}N^{-\frac{1}{2}},\ |t|\leq\frac{1}{100}\}
with
|\textsf{Proj}_{t}Q|\simeq N^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}
. Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\|U_{t}^{\alpha}f\|_{L_{x}^{r}L_{t}^{\infty}(Q)}\gtrsim N^{\frac{n+3}{4}}|Q|^{\frac{1}{r}}\simeq N^{\frac{n+3}{4}-\frac{n-1}{2r}}.

Therefore, it follows

N^{\frac{n+3}{4}-\frac{n-1}{2r}}\lesssim N^{s+\frac{1}{2}}
.
Letting
N\to\infty
, one obtains that

s\geq\frac{n+1}{4}-\frac{n-1}{2r}
.
∎

A.3.
Remarks on endpoint regularity

For applications such as the pointwise convergence problem, it is crucial to determine the least regularity in terms of
r\in[1,\infty)
, meaning that one can choose the optimal
r
that minimizes the required regularity. This is usually attained when
r=2
(sometimes it is the only case), and our case here is no exception. In fact,
\min_{r\geq 2}\max\{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{r},\frac{n+1}{r}-\frac{n-2}{2r}\}=\frac{1}{2}
when
r=2
. In this special case, the estimate

(A.5)

\|U_{t}^{\alpha}f\|_{L_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{n})L_{t}^{\infty}((0,1))}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{s}}

is known to hold if and only if
s>\frac{1}{2}
, as shown by Walther
[
21
]
.

To see the failure of (
A.5
) when
s=\frac{1}{2}
, let
\widehat{\zeta}\in C_{0}^{\infty}
be supported on
[\frac{1}{2},2]
with
\int\widehat{\zeta}(s)s^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,ds=1
. For fixed
0<r<1
, we set
\widehat{\zeta_{R}}(\xi)=R^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\widehat{\zeta}(R^{-1}|\xi|)
for
R\geq 1/r
. Using polar coordinates, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{t}^{\alpha}\zeta_{R}=R^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\int\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}e^{isx\cdot\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\,e^{it\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+s^{2}}}\widehat{\zeta}(R^{-1}s)s^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}s.

The asymptotic behavior of the Bessel functions yields

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}e^{isx\cdot\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\linebreak\sum_{\pm}c_{\pm}(s|x|)^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{\pm i|x|s}+O\big(s^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}|x|^{-\frac{n+1}{2}})
for some constants
c_{\pm}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Setting
t=|x|
, we have

\displaystyle U_{|x|}^{\alpha}\zeta_{R}(x)=c_{-}R^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\int e^{i|x|(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+s^{2}}-s)}\widehat{\zeta}\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)s^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s+O(R^{-1}|x|^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}).

Here, the integral involving the integrand
c_{+}(|x|/s)^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{i|x|(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+s^{2}}+s)}
can be shown to be absorbed into the error term
O(R^{-1}|x|^{-\frac{n+1}{2}})
through repeated integration by parts.

For the wave operator (
\alpha=0
), the above integral is straightforward to obtain. For general
\alpha>0
, Taylor series expansion gives
\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+s^{2}}-s=\alpha^{2}/(2s)+O(\alpha^{4}/s^{3})
. Thus, the phase function in the integral is smaller than
1/2
provided
|x|\leq 1
and for large enough
R\gg\alpha^{2}
. Therefore, there exists
|c(x)|\sim 1
such that

U_{|x|}^{\alpha}\zeta_{R}(x)=c(x)|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}+O(R^{-1}|x|^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}).

Now we take
f_{\lambda}
such that
\widehat{f}_{\lambda}=\sum_{1\leq 2^{k}\leq\lambda}\widehat{\zeta}_{2^{k}}
.
Then it follows that

U_{|x|}^{\alpha}f_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{1\leq 2^{k}\leq\lambda}c(x)|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}+O(1).

For
r\leq|x|\leq 1
and
\lambda\gtrsim r^{-2}
, we have
\sup_{0<t\leq 1}|U_{t}^{\alpha}f_{\lambda}|\gtrsim\log\lambda
. Consequently,

\big\|\sup_{0<t<1}|U_{t}^{\alpha}f_{\lambda}|\big\|_{L^{2}}\gtrsim\log\lambda.

Since
\|f_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1/2}}\sim(\log\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, assuming (
A.5
) holds leads to a contradiction. ∎

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
5.1
and
5.2

We begin with an elementary but crucial observation for the proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
-1<\mu<1
and
\Theta=\{\theta\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]:|\cos\theta-\mu|\leq\delta\}
.
Then

|\Theta|\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{|1-\mu|+\delta}}.

This estimate follows directly from the Taylor series expansion. We omit the detailed proof.

B.1.
Proof of Lemma
5.1

We now prove (
5.5
), following an argument in
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
.
We first divide the cases based on the separation of centers and deal with
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq 4\delta
and
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq 4\delta
separately.

Suppose first that
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq 4\delta
. Then it is clear that

|\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{1},r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})|\lesssim\delta r_{1}+\delta r_{2},

and this, together with
\Delta\lesssim\delta(r_{1}+r_{2})
, yields (
5.5
).

Now we consider the case
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq 4\delta
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{1}=(0,0)
,
x_{2}:=(y,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with
y>0
, and
r_{1}>r_{2}
. In this case, we observe that

r_{1}\sim r_{1}+r_{2}
, since it is clear that
r_{1}\leq r_{1}+r_{2}\leq 2r_{1}
.
We define the parameter

d=|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|r_{1}-r_{2}|,

which simplifies to
d=y+(r_{1}-r_{2})
.
We claim that

(B.1)

\displaystyle 4^{-1}d\leq y\leq d.

To establish the lower bound, we may assume that

y\geq{d}/2-\delta.

Otherwise, from the definition of
d
, we would have
y<d/2-\delta=y/2+(r_{1}-r_{2})/2-\delta
, which rearranges to
y+r_{2}<r_{1}-2\delta
. For
x_{2}=(y,0)
, this leads to

\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(0,r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})=\emptyset
.
Since
d\geq 4\delta
, we have
y\geq d/2-\delta\geq d/4
. The upper bound
y\leq d
is clear. This proves the claim (
B.1
).

Now we prove (
5.5
) under the assumption (
B.1
). To simplify the analysis, we rescale all parameters by
r_{1}^{-1}
and consider the intersection

\mathbb{O}_{\delta/r_{1}}(0,1)\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta/r_{1}}(x_{2}/r_{1},r_{2}/r_{1})
.
Under this scaling, we may assume that the radius is
r_{1}=1
.

In the normalized setting, let
z\in\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(0,1)\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})
. Then
|z-e^{i\theta}|\leq\delta
for some
\theta
.
Since
e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})
, we have
|\,|e^{i\theta}-(y+i0)|^{2}-r_{2}^{2}|\leq\delta
, which leads to

(\cos\theta-y)^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta-r_{2}^{2}=O(\delta)
. Consequently, we obtain

1+y^{2}-r_{2}^{2}-2y\cos\theta=O(\delta)

from which it follows that

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=(1+y^{2}-r_{2}^{2})/2y+O(\delta/y).

We set
\mu:=(1+y^{2}-r_{2}^{2})/(2y)
.
Recalling the definition (
5.4
), we may write

(B.2)

\displaystyle 1-\mu=\frac{r_{2}^{2}-(1-y)^{2}}{2y}=\frac{(r_{2}-1+y)(1+r_{2}-y)}{2y}=\frac{\varDelta}{2y}.

Next, by applying Lemma
B.1
with
\delta
replaced by
\delta/y
, and taking into account the thickness
\delta
of the circle, along with the approximation
y\sim y+\delta
(cf. (
B.1
)) we see

|\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(0,1)\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})|\lesssim\delta\cdot\frac{\delta}{y}\Big(\frac{y}{\varDelta+\delta}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\frac{\delta^{2}}{(y+\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\varDelta+\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

For general
r_{1}
, we replace
(\delta,y,r_{2})
by
r_{1}^{-1}(\delta,y,r_{2})
(thus replacing
\varDelta
with
r_{1}^{-2}\varDelta
), and then dilate the intersection by
r_{1}
. This gives

|\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{1},r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})|\lesssim\frac{\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}r_{1}}{(y+\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{\varDelta+\delta r_{1}}{\delta r_{1}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
r_{1}\sim r_{1}+r_{2}
and
y=|x_{1}-x_{2}|
, this completes the proof of Lemma
5.1
.
∎

For later use in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we note the following result:

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
.
The intersection
\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{1},r_{1})\cap\mathbb{O}_{\delta}(x_{2},r_{2})
is contained within a disc centered at
x_{2}+r_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}\nolimits(r_{1}-r_{2})\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}
with radius approximately

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(r_{1}+r_{2})\delta+\varDelta}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Indeed, under the assumption that
r_{1}=1>r_{2}
, it follows from (
B.2
) that
\theta
is contained in an interval of length approximately

\big((\varDelta+\delta)/(y+\delta)\big)^{1/2}.

Rescaling
(\delta,y)
by
r_{1}^{-1}(\delta,y)
(and thus
\varDelta
by
r_{1}^{-2}\varDelta
) followed by dilation by
r_{1}
as above, yields (
B.3
).

B.2.
Proof of Lemma
5.2

We may assume that the centers
x_{1},x_{2}
lies in a common
2
-dimensional plane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For simplicity, we write
x_{1}=(x_{1}^{\prime},0)
and

with
