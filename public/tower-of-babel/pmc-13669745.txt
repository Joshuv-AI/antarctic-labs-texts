Special care dentistry: The quiet crisis at the margins of modern healthcare
Authors: Yvonne M. Rooney, Zahra Shehabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Special care dentistry (SCD) provides oral healthcare for adults who are unable to accept routine dental care due to physical, intellectual, medical, emotional, sensory, mental, or social impairments. In England, demand for SCD services is increasing in the context of population ageing, rising multimorbidity, improved tooth retention into later life, and persistent inequalities in access to National Health Service dental care. Recent national survey data highlight substantial levels of untreated dental disease, increasing oral-health-related impacts on quality of life, and significant barriers to accessing care, with these burdens disproportionately affecting individuals with disabilities. Against this backdrop, this paper examines the growing challenges facing SCD in England and argues that these pressures provide a critical lens through which to evaluate equity, integration and resilience within the wider healthcare system. It highlights opportunities to improve service delivery and enable more equitable access to oral healthcare for medically and socially vulnerable populations.

Introduction

An uncomfortable reality in dentistry is that those with the greatest oral health needs are often the ones who face the greatest barriers to care. People requiring special care dentistry (SCD) frequently experience longer waits for assessment and treatment and may need to travel further to access appropriate services. These barriers are not confined to any single diagnostic group but affect people across the spectrum of physical, sensory, intellectual, mental health-related and medical impairment. Evidence shows that adults with intellectual disabilities are less likely to receive restorative treatment and more likely to undergo tooth extraction than the general population.1, 2

Special care dentistry, established as a recognised dental specialty in 2008, provides preventive and treatment services for adults who are unable to access routine dental care because of physical, intellectual, medical, emotional, sensory, mental or social impairment, or a combination of these factors.3 Demand for SCD services is increasing as populations age and multimorbidity becomes more common. Many patients also present with increasing frailty, polypharmacy and complex social care needs, all of which influence oral health and access to treatment.

The current challenges facing SCD are best understood in the context of its development. Historically, many people with disabilities, complex medical conditions or significant support needs experienced substantial barriers to accessing routine dental care. Advances in medicine, disability rights and healthcare policy have transformed expectations around access to care, and the recognition of SCD as a specialty in 2008 was an important milestone in that progress. However, workforce and service development have not always kept pace with changing demand, creating increasing pressure across many parts of the system.

While many of the issues discussed have relevance across the UK and internationally, this paper primarily draws on policy, workforce and service delivery evidence from England, reflecting the healthcare system within which the authors practise. It is not intended as a comprehensive review of the literature; rather, it offers a clinical perspective on the pressures currently facing SCD and the implications for future service delivery.

Care for patients requiring SCD is delivered across a tiered system, underpinned by a defined skill mix rather than a single setting. Much of this care is provided within general dental practice and community dental services, with referral to specialist-led care when complexity, risk or support needs exceed what can be safely managed at that level. Care pathways are dynamic and bidirectional, with patients moving between levels of care as medical, behavioural or functional needs change. Long-term maintenance is ideally delivered at the lowest appropriate level, with escalation occurring only when complexity requires specialist input.

Population oral health need and implications for SCD

The Adult Oral Health Survey (AOHS) 2023 provides the most up-to-date national assessment of adult oral health in England.5 Among dentate adults, 41% had clinically obvious dental decay, rising to 64% when enamel decay was included. Almost one in five adults (19%) had at least one potentially urgent oral health condition, such as dental pain or deep decay. Oral health-related quality of life has worsened, with 43% of adults reporting negative impacts on daily life, and only 52% reporting that they usually attend for routine dental check-ups.5

Although these findings are not reported specifically for SCD cohorts, they must be interpreted alongside established evidence on oral health inequalities. The Inequalities in oral health in England report demonstrates that these groups experience higher levels of untreated dental disease, increased tooth loss and poorer periodontal health compared with the general population.6 In particular, people with learning disabilities are consistently shown to have poorer oral health outcomes and lower access to early intervention primary dental services, resulting in a greater reliance on urgent, community, specialist and hospital-based care.1, 6 The consequence is later presentation, advanced disease, and increased demand for care delivered under sedation or general anaesthesia.6

At the same time, oral health needs among children in England remain exceptionally high, with tooth decay continuing to be the leading cause of hospital admission for children aged 5–9 years.7 In the financial year ending 2025, over 56,000 hospital tooth extraction episodes were recorded for children and young people aged 0–19 years, with more than 60% undertaken for dental caries.7, 8 While paediatric dentistry and special care dentistry are distinct specialties, the wider impact of high levels of unmet paediatric dental need extends beyond hospital services. Community dental services in many areas have been required to devote increasing capacity to the assessment and treatment of children with extensive dental disease. As a result, capacity for ongoing review, preventive care and active recall of adults requiring SCD may be reduced. These unintended system effects risk further amplifying oral health inequalities, particularly among individuals whose dental pain or disease may be under-recognised, non-verbally expressed, or identified only through changes in behaviour, mood, eating patterns or tolerance of oral care by families and carers.

Complexity in SCD: overlapping and evolving needs

The diversity of people requiring SCD means that complexity cannot be adequately understood through diagnostic labels alone.1, 2

Patient needs may fluctuate over time, with individuals moving between different levels of support as circumstances change. For example, a person with a learning disability may require reasonable adjustments related to communication and consent processes, including clinicians' confidence in applying mental capacity legislation, while otherwise remaining medically stable. Increasing tooth retention into older age has created a growing population of adults with complex dentitions who may develop frailty, multimorbidity or cognitive impairment over time.9, 10 A person living with dementia may initially manage routine dental care but later require additional support as cognition, dexterity, capacity and independence decline. Consequently, escalation within SCD is often influenced not only by disease severity, but by the support required to provide care safely and effectively.

Special care dentistry focuses on adapting environments, communication, clinical techniques and care pathways to enable equitable access to care.3

Sedation should therefore be viewed as an important enabler of equitable access to care. For a small number of patients, this may require delivery in theatre settings with anaesthetic support rather than conventional dentist-led sedation services.

Access to sedation services remains inconsistent within the NHS and is frequently constrained by funding arrangements, geographical variation and competing pressures on surgical waiting lists.

The implications often extend beyond access alone. Many patients who require SCD later in life have heavily restored dentitions, including complex prosthodontics and implants, which were entirely appropriate when they were younger and able to engage with routine care. Responsibility for managing the long-term consequences of these treatments therefore frequently falls to SCD services, who must address advancing disease, failing restorations and peri-implant complications in patients whose support needs have changed over time.10

Access, inequality and system pressure

In dentistry, inequities are more often shaped by service design than by patient need. Short appointment times, inflexible pathways, inaccessible premises and commissioning models that prioritise activity over complexity all contribute to unequal access to care.

The Equality Act 2010 places a statutory duty on healthcare providers to make reasonable adjustments so that disabled patients are not placed at a substantial disadvantage. Despite this, the Adult Oral Health Survey 2023 confirms that access to NHS dental care remains uneven, with difficulty securing NHS provision and affordability of charges persisting as major barriers.5

Prolonged waiting times for assessment, sedation and general anaesthesia can have particularly harmful consequences for people with learning disabilities. National evidence from the Confidential Inquiry into Premature Deaths of People with Learning Disabilities and the Learning from Lives and Deaths programme, which reviews the deaths of people with a learning disability and autistic people in England, has highlighted preventable harm, systemic failures in access to care and reduced life expectancy within this population.12, 13 In this context, untreated dental pain is not a trivial problem; it may present as behavioural distress, self-injury, nutritional compromise or deterioration in physical or mental health, sometimes leading to avoidable emergency or hospital presentations.

These delays also place a substantial and often unrecognised burden on SCD teams. Clinicians are required to manage escalating clinical risk alongside repeated engagement with families and carers, complex consent and best-interest decision-making, and, where necessary, Court of Protection processes. This work is time intensive and rarely acknowledged within commissioning or workforce planning, meaning that access constraints in SCD can contribute to both patient risk and sustained workforce strain.

Environmental barriers to care

Not all barriers to dental care arise from medical, cognitive or behavioural complexity. In some cases, access is determined by the physical environment in which care is delivered.

A clear example is the provision of dental care for people living with obesity. When a patient's weight exceeds the safe working limits of standard dental chairs, referral to alternative settings may be necessary. However, qualitative work by Geddis-Regan and colleagues demonstrates that referral pathways for bariatric dental care are often unclear, inconsistently applied and poorly aligned with patient experience.14 Access to appropriately equipped services could reduce stigma, improve patient safety and avoid unnecessary referral to community or specialist services.

More broadly, investment in accessible infrastructure, including dental chairs with higher weight capacities, adequate circulation space and adaptable surgery layouts, could enable more patients to receive care within routine dental settings.

The role of General Dental Practitioners and shared care

General Dental Practitioners play a pivotal role within SCD systems. Primary care is often both the first point of contact and the setting in which long-term oral health maintenance is delivered for many patients requiring SCD. With appropriate training, support and access to specialist advice, many patients with mild to moderate complexity can be successfully managed through prevention, reasonable adjustments and disease stabilisation.

However, translating this into routine practice remains challenging. While webinars and short courses may improve access to training, attendance is optional and often competes with clinical workload. Without protected time, contractual support or clear educational expectations, engagement with SCD-focused training remains variable.

It is therefore notable that all general dental practices, as Care Quality Commission-registered providers, are now required to ensure staff complete the Oliver McGowan Mandatory Training on Learning Disability and Autism under the Health and Care Act 2022.15 This statutory training establishes a baseline understanding of reasonable adjustments, communication and the needs of people with learning disabilities and autism. While it does not replace condition-specific clinical education, it provides an important starting point for clinicians working with people with learning disabilities and autism.

Improving confidence and experience within general dental practice should be viewed as supported shared care rather than substitution for specialist services. Achieving this requires a workforce with appropriate knowledge and support, yet exposure to SCD during undergraduate training remains variable and contributes to inconsistent confidence and referral practices.16

Workforce and training

The specialist SCD workforce in England remains relatively small and unevenly distributed. A mapping exercise identified 4,439 specialist titles held by 3041 individuals across 3,459 unique locations of work in the UK, highlighting the fragmented nature of specialist provision relative to population need.18 Workforce surveys reinforce these concerns. Around half of respondents worked part-time, many reported career breaks, and substantial numbers anticipated retirement over the following decade.17, 19 A significant minority also reported feeling inadequately remunerated, raising further concerns about recruitment and retention. Together, these findings raise concerns about the future availability and continuity of specialist services.

Many clinicians working in community dental services develop substantial expertise in managing patients with complex needs. However, opportunities to gain specialist registration through alternative routes remain limited, while specialty training posts are recruited nationally and may not be accessible to all clinicians. In addition, specialist registration is not required for many roles, reducing the incentive for some clinicians to undertake lengthy training. The wider dental workforce also has an important contribution to make, but recruitment and retention of dental hygienists and therapists within SCD services can be challenging.

Preparation for SCD practice also remains variable across UK and Ireland dental schools. Recent survey data found limited undergraduate teaching and clinical exposure to SCD, with reported barriers including insufficient clinical opportunities and a lack of appropriately trained academic staff.16

These findings sit uneasily alongside NHS England clinical standards, which rely on appropriately trained clinicians delivering care across different levels of complexity according to patient need.6

Innovation and adaptation in SCD

Many of the innovations seen within SCD have emerged in response to practical challenges in delivering care for patients with complex needs. Services frequently coordinate input across dentistry, medicine, nursing, psychiatry and social care to provide safe and effective treatment.

Digital technologies have played an increasing role. Virtual assessment, accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic, has improved access, triage and treatment planning. Advances in intravenous sedation techniques, including agents associated with more rapid recovery, have also helped increase capacity without compromising safety.

However, access to these advances remains uneven. Restorative and rehabilitative technologies such as intra-oral scanning, CAD-CAM workflows and co-located laboratory support are still unavailable in many SCD settings. Consequently, access to more complex restorative and rehabilitative approaches may vary according to local infrastructure and service configuration.

This raises important questions about whether limitations in infrastructure and investment are restricting access to restorative options for patients who already experience barriers to care.

Conclusion

Special care dentistry sits at the intersection of oral health need, clinical complexity and access to care. Contemporary evidence highlights increasing levels of dental disease, persistent oral health inequalities and continuing barriers to treatment for people who are unable to access routine dental services.

Across the dental system, delays in access, limitations in infrastructure and variation in service provision disproportionately affect those with the greatest need. This contributes to delayed presentation, advanced disease and increasing reliance on community, specialist and hospital services. In this context, SCD provides an essential role in enabling access to care for patients whose needs cannot be met through routine pathways.

Many of the challenges facing the specialty are consequences of progress. People are living longer, retaining more of their natural dentition and increasingly expecting healthcare systems to deliver equitable access and appropriate support. While these developments should be welcomed, workforce growth, infrastructure and service provision have not always kept pace with changing patterns and levels of need.

The result is a growing mismatch between need and capacity. If this gap continues to widen, those least able to navigate the healthcare system are likely to be affected first and most severely. Recognising and responding to these pressures is therefore not simply a matter for dentistry, but a wider test of how healthcare systems deliver equitable care for people with complex needs.
