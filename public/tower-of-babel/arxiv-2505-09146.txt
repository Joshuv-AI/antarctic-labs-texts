Optimization via First-Order Switching Methods: Skew-Symmetric Dynamics and Optimistic Discretization
Antesh Upadhyay, Sang Bin Moon, Abolfazl Hashemi

Abstract
Large-scale constrained optimization problems are at the core of many tasks in control, signal processing, and machine learning. Notably, problems with functional constraints arise when, beyond a performance-centric goal (e.g., minimizing the empirical loss), one desires to satisfy other requirements such as robustness, fairness, etc. A simple method for such problems, which remarkably achieves optimal rates for non-smooth, convex, strongly convex, and weakly convex functions under first-order oracle, is Switching Gradient Method (SGM): in each iteration depending on a predetermined constraint violation tolerance, use the gradient of objective or the constraint as the update vector. While the performance of SGM is well-understood for non-smooth functions and in fact matches its unconstrained counterpart, i.e., Gradient Descent (GD), less is formally established about its convergence properties under the smoothness of loss and constraint functions. In this work, we aim to fill this gap. First, we show that SGM may not benefit from faster rates under smoothness, in contrast to improved rates for GD under smoothness. By taking a continuous-time limit perspective, we show the issue is fundamental to SGM’s dynamics and not an artifact of our analysis. Our continuous-time limit perspective further provides insights towards alleviating SGM’s shortcomings. Notably, we show that leveraging the idea of optimism, a well-explored concept in variational inequalities and min-max optimization, could lead to faster methods. This perspective further enables designing a new class of “soft” switching methods, for which we further analyze their iteration complexity under mild assumptions.

1.1
Contribution

A close examination of the aforementioned results reveals that: (i) SGM’s analyses naturally build upon those for GD under corresponding non-smooth settings, e.g.,
[
22
]
, and (ii) SGM’s iteration complexity’s dependence on
\epsilon

Q1: Does SGM, like GD, benefit from smoothness of

and
g
and achieve faster rates?

Not much is formally established about the convergence properties of SGM under smoothness, even in the simplest scenario where
f
and
g
are convex and
L
-smooth.
This paper aims to fill this gap and provide a principled foundation for further studies of first-order switching methods.

In our pursuit towards answering the above question, we demonstrate that when

and
g
are convex and
L
-smooth, a simple adaptation of GD’s analysis still results in the iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
in contrast to GD’s
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})

To understand the source of this shortcoming, i.e., the inability to benefit from smoothness, we study SGM’s dynamics from two perspectives: (i) viewing SGM as GD for an unconstrained problem with a non-differentiable penalty, and (ii) the continuous-time limit of a continuous interpolation of SGM’s dynamics. Notably, these perspectives help us to identify the skew-symmetric and discontinuous nature of SGM’s dynamics as the main factors preventing it from achieving faster rates. These results suggest that the iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
is in fact inherent, motivating us to explore the following question:

Q2: Are there simple, duality-free switching methods that could potentially benefit from the smoothness of
f
and
g
?

In our pursuit to answer this question, our continuous-time limit perspective helps us to identify a connection to the literature on min-max optimization and variational inequalities. Much like SGM, the continuous-time dynamics underlying these problems are also generally skew-symmetric in nature, serving as the main reason for the lack of convergence of GD’s counterpart (i.e., gradient descent ascent or GDA for min-max optimization tasks). The developments in variational inequality literature hinges crucially on the idea of optimism as a mechanism to correct the
wrong direction
of the flow of skew-symmetric dynamics.

Leveraging such a connection, we develop representative algorithms and establish their guarantees in several settings. Additionally, our investigations lead to a novel framework with “soft” switching mechanisms to potentially design new duality-free first-order methods that use merely vector additions and leverage them in diverse settings where variants of (
P

[
23
]

Background

We start by stating some notation.
Let
[T]:=\{1,\dots,T\}
and
|{\mathcal{A}}|
denote the cardinality of set
{\mathcal{A}}
. Furthermore, let
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
denote the standard (binary-valued) indicator function, i.e.,

\mathbb{I}(x)=\begin{cases}1,\quad x>0\\
0,\quad x\leq 0,\end{cases}

(2)

and
[\cdot]_{+}=\max\{0,\cdot\}
.
Next we review the necessary definitions.

Definition 2

(Convexity, Lipschitzness, and Smoothness)
.

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a (differentiable) function is

•

Convex if

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle.

(3)

•

G
-Lipschitz for some
G>0
if

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq G\|x-y\|\quad\equiv\quad\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq G.

(4)

•

L
-smooth for some
L>0
if

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|.

(5)

We remark that the definitions of convexity and Lipschitzness stated above do not require differentiability, and one simply needs to replace
\nabla f(x)
with a subgradient of
f
at
x
, i.e.
u\in\partial f(x)
. In this paper, however, for simplicity of notation and derivations, we simply assume differentiability and use
\nabla f(x)
.

Finally, we remark on the generality of problem (
P
) in terms of handling multiple constraints, including functional equality constraints. If multiple constraints
g_{i}(w)\leq 0
exist, one can let
g(w)=\max_{i}g_{i}(w)
. Furthermore, in the case of equality constraints
h(w)=0
, we could equivalently and simultaneously impose
h(w)\leq 0
and
-h(w)\leq 0
.

3
Demystifying SGM’s Iteration Complexity under Smoothness

In this section, we analyze SGM under two settings and provide Theorems
1
and
2
. The first theorem aims to facilitate the ensuing analyses and further improve the existing results in certain aspects, e.g., improving constants and bringing the proof as close as possible to that of GD. The second theorem, to the best of our knowledge, is not formally stated in the literature. Afterwards, we provide two perspectives highlighting the inherent shortcomings of SGM when it comes to harnessing smoothness.

3.1
SGM for Convex and Lipschitz Functions

We start by stating our first result regarding the convergence properties of SGM for convex and non-smooth problems. Note that while this setting is well studied, e.g., Chapter 3.2.4 in
[
13
]
and
[
15
,
17
]
, here we state a different version with a different proof for completeness.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the problem in (
P
) and SGM in (
SGM
). Assume
f
and
g
are convex and
G
-Lipschitz. Define
D:=\|w_{1}-w^{\ast}\|
and

{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]\;|\;g(w_{t})\leq\epsilon\},\qquad\bar{w}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}w_{t}.

(6)

Then, if

\epsilon=\frac{DG}{\sqrt{T}},\qquad\eta_{t}=\eta=\frac{D}{G\sqrt{T}},

(7)

it holds that
{\mathcal{A}}
is nonempty,
\bar{w}
is well-defined, and
\bar{w}
is an
\epsilon
-solution for (
P
).

The above theorem establishes that SGM has an optimal iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
. We also note that instead of establishing the convergence of the average iterate, one could select
t_{s}\in{\mathcal{A}}
uniformly at random and, using a standard technique (see e.g.,
[
30
]
), show
w_{t_{s}}
is an expected
\epsilon
-solution.

3.2
SGM for Convex and Smooth Functions

Next, we state our result regarding the convergence properties of SGM for convex and smooth problems. Note that this result seems to be stated formally for the first time.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the problem in (
P
) and SGM in (
SGM
). Assume
f
and
g
are convex and
L
-smooth. Define
D:=\|w_{1}-w^{\ast}\|
and the following

\displaystyle\tilde{f}

\displaystyle:=\min_{x}f(x)>-\infty,\qquad\tilde{g}:=\min_{x}g(x)>-\infty,

(8)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\max}

\displaystyle:=\max\{f(w^{\ast})-\tilde{f},g(w^{\ast})-\tilde{g}\}\geq 0,

and

{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]\;|\;g(w_{t})\leq\epsilon\},\qquad\bar{w}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}w_{t}.

(9)

Then if

\epsilon=\frac{2LD^{2}}{T}+\sqrt{\frac{8LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}},\quad\eta=\min\Big\{\frac{1}{2L},\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}}{2L\Delta_{\max}T}}\big\},

(10)

it holds that
{\mathcal{A}}
is nonempty,
\bar{w}
is well-defined, and
\bar{w}
is an
\epsilon
-solution for (
P
).

The above theorem establishes that SGM has an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
, despite the smoothness of
f
and
g
. This rate falls short of the complexity of GD for this setting, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})

[
13
]
unless
\Delta_{\max}=0
.

A careful reader notices that the result of Theorem
2
resembles the standard, unconstrained convergence results of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) if we interpret
\Delta_{\max}
as the variance of the stochastic oracle
[
30
]
. The “variance” here, however, does not arise from a stochastic estimation of the gradients of
f
and
g
; rather, it arises due to SGM’s dynamics. Let us discuss this point somewhat informally next.

In particular, we show that SGM can be thought of as SGD for the dual of problem (
P
), i.e.

\min_{w}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}f(w)+\lambda g(w).

(11)

Let
\lambda^{\ast}\geq 0
be optimal dual variable. Suppose
\lambda^{\ast}
is not a function of
w
(which is not correct in general). Then,

\min_{w}f(w)+\lambda^{\ast}g(w)\quad\equiv\quad\min_{w}\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}f(w)+\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}g(w),

(12)

and the update of GD for (
12
) is as follows

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\Bigg(\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}\nabla f(w_{t})+\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}\nabla g(w_{t})\Bigg).

(13)

Let
z\in\{1,2\}
be a random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(z=1)=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}
, and
\mathbb{P}(z=2)=\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}
. Define the stochastic oracle

\nabla\tilde{\ell}(w_{t},z)=\begin{cases}\nabla f(w_{t})\quad w.p.\quad\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}\\
\nabla g(w_{t})\quad w.p.\quad\frac{\lambda^{\ast}}{1+\lambda^{\ast}}.\end{cases}

(14)

Consequently, GD’s update can be written equivalently as

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla\tilde{\ell}(w_{t},z)].

(15)

It is now evident that SGM acts as a stochastic approximation of the above update: when
w_{t}
is significantly infeasible,
\lambda^{\ast}
is large such that it is more likely for the oracle to return
\nabla g(w_{t})
as the gradient estimator. Conversely, when
w_{t}
is near-feasible,
\lambda^{\ast}
is small, such that it is more likely for the oracle to return
\nabla f(w_{t})
as the gradient estimator.

We again emphasize that the above discussion is informal and its main goal is to provide an intuitive way to interpret the result of Theorem
2
in light of those for SGD. Much like SGD that with and without smoothness it achieves a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
for convex problems due to the detrimental impact of the variance, SGM cannot enjoy faster rates under smoothness due to the detrimental impact of
\Delta_{\max}
either.

Let us also remark on the assumption that
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
are bounded from below, as it seemingly excludes affine functions for which the minimum value is
-\infty
. Note for affine functions
L=0
such that
L\times\Delta_{\max}=0\times\infty
, which can be defined to be
0
. Indeed, there are no smooth and convex functions such that
\Delta_{\max}=\infty
while
L\neq 0
.

3.3
SGM as GD for Unconstrained Problems with

Non-differentiable Penalty

Next, we provide an argument suggesting the complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
derived by Theorem
2
is inherent.

Consider the following unconstrained problem

\min_{w}f(w)+\rho[g(w)]_{+},\qquad\rho>0.

(16)

Evidently, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is feasible for (
P
) it holds that
g(w)\leq 0
, implying the objective in (
16
) becomes
f(w)
. Further, the two problems have the same solutions for large enough
\rho
(see, e.g., Chapter 4.1 in
[
8
]
). Let us now consider the following modification

\min_{w}f(w)+\rho[g(w)-\epsilon]_{+},\qquad\rho,\epsilon>0.

(17)

It is evident that when
g(w)\leq\epsilon
the (sub)gradient is
\nabla f(w)
while when
g(w)>\epsilon
the (sub)gradient is
\nabla f(w)+\rho\nabla g(w)
. Thus, for large
\rho
such that
\nabla f(w)+\rho\nabla g(w)\approx\rho\nabla g(w)
, GD for (
17
), i.e.

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\eta_{t}u_{t}

(18)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla f(w_{t})+\mathbb{I}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\rho\nabla g(w_{t}),

approximates SGM’s dynamics given in (
SGM
).

As the
\max
operator is non-differentiable, the complexity of GD for (
17
) is generally
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
under the first-order oracle despite the smoothness of
f
and
g
. Thus, the complexity of SGM for (
P
) is generally
\Omega(\epsilon^{-2})
as well, suggesting the tightness of the result of Theorem
2
.

One might expect to achieve
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
for (
17
) by leveraging accelerated methods with complexity
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\tilde{L}/\epsilon})

[
31
]
along with smooth approximations such that
\tilde{L}=\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(\rho,d,\epsilon^{-1}))

[
32
,
33
]
, thereby developing a simple approach to modify SGM and improve its complexity result. However, to ensure finding an
\epsilon
-solution one must set
\rho=\epsilon^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Thus, this strategy may fail to attain rates faster than
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
.

3.4
Continuous-Time Limit of SGM’s Dynamics

We now provide an alternative view that further supports the tightness of the result of Theorem
2
and could aid us in designing algorithms with possibly better rates.

Consider the following Ordinary Differential Inclusion (ODI)

\displaystyle\dot{w}

\displaystyle\in-\tilde{F}(w)

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(g(w)-\epsilon)\nabla g(w)+(1-\mathbb{I}(g(w)-\epsilon))\nabla f(w),

which is readily seen to represent the continuous-time limit of SGM’s dynamics in (
SGM
). Note that
\tilde{F}(w)
is discontinuous in
w
. Thus, the conditions of Caratheodory’s existence theorem
[
34
]
are not met, and the ODI may not even have a solution near any initialization. This again highlights the unfavorable structure of SGM’s dynamics. Accordingly, we instead consider the following Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)

\displaystyle\dot{w}

\displaystyle=-F(w)

(20)

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)\nabla g(w)+[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)]\nabla f(w)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta x)},

for some smoothing or temperature parameter
\beta>0
. Note that
\sigma_{\beta}(x)
is the sigmoid function.

Evidently, as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, the dynamics in (
20
) approaches that of SGM’s. The proposed ODE in (
20
) has potential benefits over the continuous-time limit of SGM’s dynamics. Firstly, it is continuous in
w
; thus, by Caratheodory’s existence theorem, it is guaranteed to have a solution near any initialization. Secondly, basic calculations show that under the assumption that
f
and
g
are smooth and Lipschitz,
F(w)
will be a Lipschitz operator. Consequently, (
20
) is further guaranteed to have a unique solution by the Picard–Lindelöf theorem
[
34
]
.

Next, we aim to study the properties of the Jacobian of the above flow in (
20
), which is denoted by
J_{F}(w)
.
Jacobian for such an ODE describes how the flow locally curves, rotates, contracts, and expands. It is well-known that when the Jacobian is purely symmetric (as is the case with the gradient flow
\dot{w}=-\nabla f(w)
), the flow does not exhibit any rotational behavior due to the fact that the underlying physical system is dissipative
[
35
]
. On the other hand, when the Jacobian has a skew-symmetric component (e.g., the flow of bilinear min-max games or, equivalently, the harmonic oscillator
\ddot{w}=-w
), the flow may exhibit rotation as a result of conserving energy. The next proposition establishes that despite the favorable (Lipchitz) continuity structure, the dynamics in (
20
) is inherently skew-symmetrical.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the ODE given by (
20
). We have

\displaystyle J_{F}(w)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}f(w)+\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)\big(\nabla^{2}g(w)-\nabla^{2}f(w)\big)

(21)

\displaystyle+\sigma^{\prime}_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)\big(\nabla g(w)-\nabla f(w)\big)(\nabla g(w))^{\top}.

Consequently, as long as
\nabla g(w)
and
\nabla f(w)
are linearly independent,
J_{F}(w)
has a skew-symmetric component.

Proof.

The Jacobian can be readily found by applying the chain rule and matrix calculus.
Clearly the only non-symmetric term is the outer product
\nabla f(w)(\nabla g(w))^{\top}
. Evidently,
ab^{\top}=ba^{\top}
iff
a
and
b
are linearly dependent (to see this, multiply both sides by
a^{\top}
and rearrange).
∎

Proposition
1
establishes that the flow is generally skew-symmetrical and the skew-symmetric component gets stronger as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
(corresponding to the derivative of
\sigma_{\beta}(\cdot)
) and
|g(w)|\rightarrow\epsilon
, i.e., as the proposed ODE in (
20
) approaches the continuous-time limit of SGM’s dynamics and the solution approaches the boundary of the feasible set.

Consider an arbitrary flow
\dot{w}=-F(w)
which by taking the integral can be written equivalently as

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+1}F(w_{\tau})\cdot d\tau.

(22)

The simple forward Euler discretization approximates
[
36
]

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+1}F(w_{\tau})\cdot d\tau

(23)

\displaystyle\approx w_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+\eta}F(w_{t})\cdot d\tau=w_{t}-\eta F(w_{t}).

Note that when
F(w)=\nabla f(w)
forward Euler discretization update stated above corresponds to GD and is known to enjoy favorable convergence properties under the Lipschitzness of
\nabla f(w)
, i.e., the smoothness of
f
. Note, in this case, the flow is purely symmetric as the Jacobian of
\nabla f(w)
, i.e., the Hessian of
f(w)
, is a symmetric matrix.

For general flows where
F(w)
is not necessarily a gradient and thus the flow is skew-symmetrical, it is very well known that forward Euler discretization is ineffective: The limiting solution does not converge to the equilibrium, and the ergodic average solution converges slowly.

In our context, as we demonstrated in Proposition
1
, the dynamics in (
20
) is inherently skew-symmetrical. Thus, the forward Euler discretization update of the continuous-time limit of SGM’s dynamics, which readily is the original SGM in (
SGM
), will be an ineffective approach as well and incapable of last-iterate convergence and faster rates under smoothness. Thus, this discussion highlights that SGM may fail to attain a complexity better than
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
due to the discontinuity of its flow and skew-symmetric nature of the flow’s continuous approximations.

4
Soft Switching Mechanism via Trimmed Hinge

Given the desired properties of the proposed dynamics in (
20
) discussed in Section
3.4
, notably guaranteed existence of a solution and the uniqueness under certain Lipschitzness properties, a natural question is whether its immediate forward Euler discretization, i.e.

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\eta F(w_{t})

(SSGM)

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)\nabla g(w)+[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)]\nabla f(w),

which we refer to as Soft Switching Gradient Method (SSGM),
enjoys any convergence guarantees in the sense of finding an
\epsilon
-solution per Definition
1
. It turns out we can provide theoretical guarantees for SSGM if instead of the sigmoid-based approximation we adopt a “trimmed-hinged” approximation:

\sigma_{\beta}(x)=\min\{1,[1+\beta x]_{+}\}=\text{Proj}_{[0,1]}(1+\beta x).

(24)

In particular, we provide the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the problem in (
P
) and SSGM in (
SSGM
). Assume
f
and
g
are convex and
G
-Lipschitz. Define
D:=\|w_{1}-w^{\ast}\|
and

{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]\;|\;g(w_{t})<\epsilon\},\quad\bar{w}=\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\alpha_{t}w_{t},

(25)

where

\alpha_{t}=\frac{1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}{\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)]}.

(26)

Then, if

The above theorem establishes that SSGM has an optimal iteration complexity of

Let us conclude this section by estimating the convergence properties of SSGM when

5
Optimistic Discretization

As we discussed in Sections
1.1
and
3.4
, any differentiable approximation of the flow of SGM will be skew-symmetrical in nature. For such flows, it is very well known that forward Euler discretization is ineffective. A simple discretization that is proven effective, however, is the backward Euler method
[
36
]

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+1}F(w_{\tau})\cdot d\tau

(32)

\displaystyle\approx w_{t}-\int_{t}^{t+\eta}F(w_{t+1})\cdot d\tau=w_{t}-\eta F(w_{t+1}).

We refer to this approach as optimistic discretization as it optimistically uses the direction specified by the predicted point to form the update.

It is then natural to explore the efficacy of this approach for the discretization of the SGM’s flow as well as (
20
) and studying the convergence of the resultant methods.
In doing so, we first explore the following simple method

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{t}-\eta F(w_{t+1})

(SSPPM)

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)\nabla g(w)+[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w)-\epsilon)]\nabla f(w),

which we refer to it as Soft Switching Proximal Point Method (SSPPM). The reason we choose this name is the well-known connection of the celebrated proximal point method
[
37
]

w_{t+1}=\arg\min_{w}\;\;f(w)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|w-w_{t}\|^{2},

(33)

and backward Euler discretization when the flow is described by the gradient of a convex function
w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla f(w_{t+1})
.

We state the following result for SSPPM.

Theorem 5
.

Consider the problem in (
P
) and SSPPM in (
SSPPM
). Assume
f
and
g
are convex. Define
D:=\|w_{1}-w^{\ast}\|
and

{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]\;|\;g(w_{t})<\epsilon\},\quad\bar{w}=\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\alpha_{t}w_{t},

(34)

where

\alpha_{t}=\frac{1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}{\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)]}.

(35)

Suppose the nonlinear equation in (
SSPPM
) has a solution for all
t
. Then if

\epsilon=\frac{D^{2}}{\eta T},\qquad\eta>0,\qquad\beta=\frac{2}{\epsilon},

(36)

it holds that
{\mathcal{A}}
is nonempty,
\bar{w}
is well-defined, and
\bar{w}
is an
\epsilon
-solution for (
P
).

Note that the theorem does not require Lipschitzness or smoothness of
f
and
g
. Yet, it suggests that SSPPM achieves the complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
as long as the nonlinear equation in (
SSPPM
) has a solution for all
t
. We will discuss this point in the remainder of this section. Another remark worth stating is the implicit nature of the update (
SSPPM
). We will discuss this aspect in the remainder of this section as well. Note that both of these considerations are generally applicable to the the original proximal point method for solving variational inequalities.

5.1
Fixed Point Existence and Calculation

As stated, Theorem
5
requires the existence of a solution for
w=w_{t}-\eta F(w)
for all
t
. At its core, this task is a fixed point problem.

There exist many fixed-point theorems that could be leveraged to establish conditions under which a solution for (
SSPPM
) exists. Notably, by the Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann Theorem
[
38
,
39
]
, a sufficient condition is non-expansiveness of
\eta F(\cdot)
. Thus, as long as
\eta G_{F}\leq 1
(
SSPPM
) will have a solution where
G_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F(\cdot)
. Uniqueness is guaranteed if
\eta G_{F}<1
according to the Banach fixed-point theorem
[
40
]
. The next proposition establishes a bound on
G_{F}
based on the properties
f
,
g
, and
\sigma_{\beta}(\cdot)
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume
f
and
g
are
G
-Lipschitz and
L
-smooth. Then,
G_{F}\leq 2(L+G^{2}\beta)
.

Before utilizing the result of this proposition, let us remark that due to the discontinuity of
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
(and as a consequence the lack of Lipschitzness), the hard switching counterpart of SSPPM in (
SSPPM
) is not guaranteed to have any solution.

Let us now use the result of Proposition
2
along with the result of Theorem
5
. Setting
\eta=1/2(L+G^{2}\beta)
and equating
\epsilon=2/\beta
and
\epsilon=D^{2}/\eta T
leads to a quadratic equation for
\beta
. Solving this equation determines
\epsilon=DG/\sqrt{T}
and
\beta=2/\epsilon
.
This result implies that in the worst case and to guarantee a solution exists for all
t
, the complexity of SSPPM is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
. Interestingly, while smoothness is leveraged in Proposition
2
, the expression for the best
\epsilon
and
\beta
stated above are independent of
L
. Let us also state that we believe this bound is highly pessimistic due to the fact that we used a potentially pessimistic bound to establish
\beta=2/\epsilon
(please refer to the discussion following Theorem
3
). Thus, treating
\beta
as a constant which is independent of
T
would still imply
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
complexity.

Now, suppose a solution exists. The next concern is the implicit nature of the SSPPM update. As finding
w_{t+1}
amounts to a fixed point calculation problem, many algorithms such as the classical fixed point iteration, Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann iteration, or their approximate and stochastic version can be leveraged
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. If

is set such that

is contractive, exact methods can generally converge linearly while stochastic ones may suffer from sublinear rates
[
44
]
. While generally leveraging fixed point iteration methods is a promising approach, we pursue an alternative solution next.

5.2

Here we explore a simple alternative by leveraging the connection between (

is not a gradient. However, we propose to simply replace the terms involving

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{w}\;\;h_{t}(w)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|w-w_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)g(w)+[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)]f(w).

In scenarios where

Finally, we demonstrate that the connection between (

Conclusion

In this paper, we started by analyzing SGM in the smooth setting and established that it may not enjoy faster rates compared to the non-smooth setting. We attributed this result to the discontinuous and skew-symmetric dynamics of SGM. This perspective was then leveraged to design Lipschitz dynamics by incorporating smoothing functions, notably the trimmed hinge. We then studied the convergence properties of the resulting methods when discretizing the dynamics by forward, backward, and approximate backward Euler discretizations.

There are numerous interesting directions of research. Firstly, as we stated our bound for the smoothing parameter is highly pessimistic, and we conjecture it may be possible to derive a tighter bound resulting in a
\beta
independent of
\epsilon
and, equivalently,
T
.
Also, as the new methods seem superior to SGM, it would be of interest to extend them beyond the deterministic and convex setting studied in this paper by leveraging tools from
[
45
,
17
,
7
,
19
]
which study extensions of SGM. Secondly, it is of interest to leverage the new methods in decision-making problems such as safe control and reinforcement learning
[
20
]
particularly for cyber-physical systems, equilibrium computation and learning in games with functional constraints
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, online learning and control with functional constraints
[
49
,
50
]
, and distributed optimization with functional constraints
[
51
]
. Finally, it is of interest to incorporate momentum mechanisms
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
into the proposed methods to further improve their convergence properties.

\frac{1}{2L}\leq\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}}{2L\Delta_{\max}T}}

\eta=1/2L
we have

2L\eta\Delta_{\max}\leq 2L\Delta_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}}{2L\Delta_{\max}T}}=\sqrt{\frac{2LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}},

(55)

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}f(w_{t})-f(w^{\ast})+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{B}}}g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})\leq\frac{2LD^{2}}{T}+\sqrt{\frac{2LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}}.

(56)

On the other hand, if
\frac{1}{2L}\geq\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}}{2L\Delta_{\max}T}}
such that
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}}{2L\Delta_{\max}T}}
the upper bound becomes
2\sqrt{\frac{2LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}}
.
Thus, in both cases,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}f(w_{t})-f(w^{\ast})+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{B}}}g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})\leq\frac{2LD^{2}}{T}+2\sqrt{\frac{2LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}}.

(57)

It is evident that we are now back to a setup analogous to the proof of Theorem
1
(i.e., (
45
)), but with a different bound. Accordingly, setting

\epsilon=\frac{2LD^{2}}{T}+2\sqrt{\frac{2LD^{2}\Delta_{\max}}{T}},

(58)

and following an identical argument furnishes the proof.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
3

Recall the update in (
SSGM
). Since
0\leq\sigma_{\beta}(\cdot)\leq 1
and
f
and
g
are
G
-Lipschitz, we have
\|F(w_{t})\|\leq G
. Thus, utilizing the analysis in the proof of Theorem
1
and notably (
43
) and setting
\eta=\frac{D}{G\sqrt{T}}
we have
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle F(w_{t}),w_{t}-w^{\ast}\rangle\leq DG\sqrt{T}
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]|g(w_{t})<\epsilon\}
and
{\mathcal{B}}=[T]\backslash{\mathcal{A}}=\{t\in[T]|g(w_{t})\geq\epsilon\}
. Note that for all
t\in{\mathcal{B}}
it holds that
\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)=1
and
g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})\geq\epsilon
. Further, for all
t\in{\mathcal{A}}
if
\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\geq 0
it holds that
g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})\geq g(w_{t})\geq\epsilon-1/\beta
. With these observations, using convexity of
f
and
g
and decomposing the sum over
t
according to the definitions of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
yields

\displaystyle DG\sqrt{T}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\big(g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})\big)

(59)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\big(1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\big)\big(f(w_{t})-f(w^{\ast})\big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{B}}}g(w_{t})-g(w^{\ast})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\big(1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\big)\big(f(w_{t})-f(w^{\ast})\big)

\displaystyle+\epsilon|{\mathcal{B}}|+\left(\epsilon-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon).

Similar to the previous proofs, we first need to find the smallest
\epsilon
to ensure
{\mathcal{A}}
is non-empty.

To find a lower bound on
\epsilon
assume
{\mathcal{A}}
is empty in (
59
) and observe that as long as condition
\frac{DG}{\sqrt{T}}<\epsilon
is met,
{\mathcal{A}}
is non-empty. We choose to set
\epsilon=\frac{2DG}{\sqrt{T}}
.

Now like before we consider two cases based on the sign of
\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\big(1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\big)\big(f(w_{t})-f(w^{\ast})\big)
. As before, when the sum is non-positive we are done by the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}
which implies
0<1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\leq 1
and the convexity of
f
and
g
.

Assuming the sum is positive, dividing (
59
) by
\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\big(1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)\big)
(which by the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}
is strictly positive), using convexity of
f
, and the definition of
\bar{w}
we have

\displaystyle f(\bar{w})-f(w^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{0.5\epsilon T-\epsilon|{\mathcal{B}}|-(\epsilon-\frac{1}{\beta})\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}{|{\mathcal{A}}|-\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}

(60)

\displaystyle=\epsilon+\frac{-0.5\epsilon T+\beta^{-1}\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}{|{\mathcal{A}}|-\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)},

where we used
|{\mathcal{B}}|=T-|{\mathcal{A}}|
.

Let us now find a lower bound on
\beta
to ensure the second term in the bound is non-positive. Note this is done for simplicity, and as long as the second term is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, an
\epsilon
-solution can be found.

Immediate calculations show the second term in the bound is non-positive when

\beta\geq\frac{2\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)}{\epsilon T}.

(61)

Since
\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)<T
, a sufficient (and highly conservative) condition for all
T\geq 1
is to set
\beta=2/\epsilon
.
Thus, we proved the suboptimality gap result. The feasibility result is immediate given the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}
and the convexity of
g
.

We will abuse the notation slightly and use

\displaystyle-F(y)=(1-\sigma_{\beta}(x))\nabla f(x)+\sigma_{\beta}(x)\nabla g(x)

(65)

\displaystyle+|\sigma_{\beta}(x)|\cdot\|\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(y)\|

and
g

. The term

2G
using the Lipschitzness of

and

. Now, with our abuse of notation and the non-expansiveness of convex projection

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\beta}(x)-\sigma_{\beta}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|1+\beta(g(x)-\epsilon)-1-\beta(g(y)-\epsilon)|

\displaystyle\leq\beta G\|x-y\|.

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem
6

Recall the update of SSPPM-E in (
SSPPM-E
). As
h
is convex, by the first-order optimality condition

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla h_{t}(w_{t+1}).

(67)

Now, for each
t

h_{t}
we can write

\displaystyle h_{t}(w_{t})-h_{t}(w^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{h_{t}(w_{t})-h_{t}(w_{t+1})}_{(\text{I)}}+\underbrace{h_{t}(w_{t+1})-h_{t}(w^{\ast})}_{\text{(}II)}

(68)

We can use the
G
-Lipschitzness of
f
and
g
as well as the boundedness of
\sigma_{\beta}
and the update in (
67
) to bound the
(I)
of (
68
) according to

\displaystyle h_{t}(w_{t})-h_{t}(w_{t+1})=|h_{t}(w_{t})-h_{t}(w_{t+1}))|

\displaystyle=|[1-\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)](f(w_{t})-f(w_{t+1}))+\sigma_{\beta}(g(w_{t})-\epsilon)(g(w_{t})-g(w_{t+1}))|
