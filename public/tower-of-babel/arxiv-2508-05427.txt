Large Language Models Transform Organic Synthesis From Reaction Prediction to Automation
Kartar Kumar Lohana Tharwani, Rajesh Kumar Sumita, Numan Ahmed, and Yong Tang

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are beginning to reshape how chemists plan and run reactions in organic synthesis. Trained on millions of reported transformations, these text-based models can propose synthetic routes, forecast reaction outcomes and even instruct robots that execute experiments without human supervision. Here we survey the milestones that turned LLMs from speculative tools into practical lab partners. We show how coupling LLMs with graph neural networks, quantum calculations and real-time spectroscopy shrinks discovery cycles and supports greener, data-driven chemistry. We discuss limitations, including biased datasets, opaque reasoning and the need for safety gates that prevent unintentional hazards. Finally, we outline community initiatives open benchmarks, federated learning and explainable interfaces that aim to democratize access while keeping humans firmly in control. These advances chart a path towards rapid, reliable and inclusive molecular innovation powered by artificial intelligence and automation.

Understanding Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) are a class of deep learning models based on the transformer architecture, designed to process, generate, and reason over natural language sequences

In the scientific domain, particularly chemistry, LLMs act as intelligent assistants for simplifying complex text, generating hypotheses, and translating symbolic representations (e.g., SMILES) into actionable insights

2.2 Domain Adaptation: LLMs for Chemistry

While general-purpose LLMs, such as GPT-4, exhibit remarkable versatility across diverse domains, their application to specialized fields like organic chemistry demands a rigorous fine-tuning process on curated, domain-specific chemical datasets. This approach, known as chemistry-oriented LLM fine-tuning, involves adapting transformer-based architectures by training them on structured repositories such as the USPTO dataset (50,000 reaction templates), PubChem (over 100 million molecular property records), and Reaxys (exceeding 1 million experimental reaction entries)
[
26
,
27
]

Through this process, LLMs develop an understanding of domain-specific chemical syntax (e.g., SMILES and SELFIES notations), reaction mechanisms (e.g., electrophilic addition kinetics), and empirical trends (e.g., temperature-dependent yields), leveraging multi-head self-attention to model long-range dependencies within chemical sequences.

In practice, chemical representations like SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) and SELFIES (Self-Referencing Embedded Strings) are tokenized into linguistic sequences, enabling LLMs to process them as text (Fig.
IV-C

[

c_{i}

III-A

Problem Formulation

Reaction prediction is modeled as a conditional sequence-generation task. Given a set of reactant tokens
x=(r_{1},\dots,r_{|x|})
and optional condition tokens
c
(e.g., catalysts, solvents), a large language model (LLM) learns a mapping
[
45
]
to product tokens
y=(p_{1},\dots,p_{|y|})
, typically represented as SMILES strings
[
35
]
:

f_{\theta}:(x,c)\mapsto y.

The model is trained to minimize the token-level cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log P_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x,c).

Retrosynthesis inverts this process, mapping a target product
y
to a set of
k\geq 1
precursor sets
\{x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots,x^{(k)}\}
:

g_{\phi}:y\mapsto\{x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots,x^{(k)}\}.

Performance is evaluated using Top-
k
accuracy, assessing whether the correct precursors appear in the top-
k
ranked candidates.

III-C

Learning Objectives in Practice

LLM chem-modules rely on two core objective families:

a)

Auto-regressive cross-entropy
(Eq. 1) for sequence generation of products or precursors.

b)

Contrastive ranking
for candidate lists, often implemented as an InfoNCE loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}=-\log\dfrac{\exp\bigl(\langle z_{q},z^{+}\rangle/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{j}\exp\bigl(\langle z_{q},z_{j}\rangle/\tau\bigr)}

where
z_{q}
is the query embedding and
z^{+}
its positive key.

Hybrid models such as
SynAsk
combine both objectives, fine-tuning generative heads while ranking negative candidates from a template search space.

3.4 Reaction Prediction

Recent advancements in chemistry-specific LLMs have significantly improved reaction prediction capabilities. ChemLLM, a domain-specific variant of GPT, continues to lead with a Top-5 accuracy of 92.3% on the ChemBench dataset, surpassing GPT-3.5 by 8.7% absolute improvement as previously noted
[

Additionally, the introduction of the F1 score for reaction prediction, which balances precision and recall, shows ChemLLM achieving 0.89 on ChemBench, indicating robust performance across diverse reaction types.

SynAsk, integrating a knowledge graph with a contrastive reranker, further refines prediction accuracy, particularly for challenging C-N and C-C cross-coupling reactions. Recent data indicate a Top-1 accuracy improvement of 5.2% over ChemLLM, reaching 87.1% on a subset of 10,000 curated reactions from Reaxys, alongside a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.91, highlighting its strength in ranking relevant products. These gains are attributed to the model’s ability to leverage symbolic knowledge, reducing errors in low-data regimes.

Newer models, such as RxnGPT, introduce multi-modal conditioning, incorporating spectral data to enhance prediction fidelity. Preliminary 2025 results show RxnGPT achieving a Top-3 accuracy of 94.2% on USPTO-50K and a reduced mean absolute error (MAE) of 8.5% in yield prediction, compared to SynAsk’s 11-14% MAE, demonstrating improved condition sensitivity. Comparative analysis across these models underscores a trend toward higher precision and recall, with ongoing efforts to mitigate condition blindness and chirality errors through hybrid architectures.

III-F

Failure Modes and Limitations

The application of large language models (LLMs) in organic synthesis is hindered by critical failure modes that compromise prediction accuracy and reliability, impacting their utility in practical chemical workflows:
i)

Condition blindness
, where models disregard explicit reaction conditions like temperature or solvent, producing condition-agnostic products and reducing prediction specificity by up to 15% on datasets like Reaxys
[
59
]
, potentially addressed through condition-aware tokenization,
ii)

Chirality errors
, with stereocenter misassignments generating invalid SMILES in approximately 20% of USPTO-50K predictions
[
60
]
, mitigated by integrating graph neural networks for stereochemical validation,
iii)

Symmetry ambiguity
, where symmetric reactants yield multiple valid products, lowering Top-1 accuracy by 10–12% due to ranking metric confusion
[
17
]
, resolvable via enhanced ranking algorithms,
iv)

Data leakage risk
, where train/test split overlaps in USPTO derivatives inflate accuracies by up to 8%
[
61
]
, necessitating rigorous dataset partitioning. Addressing these issues through advanced model architectures and curated datasets is crucial for robust LLM applications in synthesis

III-H

Cross-Model Insights

Recent analyses of retrosynthetic planning models reveal several key insights into their performance and limitations. The scale of training data significantly impacts model efficacy, with performance scaling logarithmically as the number of reaction pairs increases. For instance, ChemLLM’s superior performance is largely attributed to its fine-tuning corpus, which is twice as large as that of competing models. Additionally, integrating symbolic knowledge bases, as seen in SynAsk, enhances performance in low-data reaction classes, such as radical couplings, improving Top-1 accuracy by up to 12%. Furthermore, generative large language models like ChemLLM demonstrate strength in generating novel synthetic routes, whereas reranking hybrid models like SynAsk achieve higher precision on well-characterized reaction manifolds. However, a notable challenge persists: even state-of-the-art models reach a performance ceiling around 65% Top-1 accuracy on the USPTO-50K dataset, suggesting inherent ambiguities in single-step retrosynthesis that limit further improvements.

Perhaps most transformative is the potential for global collaborative frameworks, where decentralized platforms would enable international synthetic challenges and cross-cultural learning initiatives, creating unprecedented opportunities for distributed knowledge sharing while addressing regional resource disparities through crowd-sourced problem solving.

VIII-C

Governance Frameworks for Responsible Deployment

Sustainable integration of LLMs in chemical research demands co-development of ethical governance protocols. Key principles include strict validation regimes requiring experimental confirmation of proposed syntheses, tiered access control systems for sensitive capabilities, mandatory disclosure of safety limitations in published outputs, and independent third-party auditing of algorithmic decision boundaries. These measures must align with international scientific ethics standards while accommodating jurisdictional regulatory variations to prevent unintended hazardous applications.

IX
Future Research Directions

IX-A

Capability Advancement Vectors

Critical innovation pathways center on developing multimodal architectures that integrate spectroscopic, structural (3D conformers), and kinetic data to enhance prediction fidelity (Fig.
IV-C
). Parallel efforts focus on building open knowledge ecosystems through community-driven resources like ChemLLM models and ChemData repositories, balancing accessibility with safety standards. Significant opportunities exist in implementing explainable AI (XAI) frameworks to increase transparency in synthetic route decisions and mechanistic interpretations.

IX-B

Convergent Methodological Integration

Synergistic combinations with complementary AI approaches show particular promise, especially coupling LLMs with graph neural networks (GNNs) for enhanced stereochemical and mechanistic analysis. Reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks incorporating experimental feedback could refine synthetic pathway optimization, while integration with automated laboratory platforms may enable real-world performance validation. Such hybrid systems would leverage the complementary strengths of symbolic reasoning and pattern recognition capabilities across AI paradigms. Current systems show limited capability against: (a)
Reagent masking
(e.g., substituting controlled precursors with unregulated analogues), (b)
Pathway fragmentation
(splitting hazardous syntheses across multiple queries), (c)
Novel compound evasion
(compounds absent from training data).
These constitute priority research gaps requiring cross-disciplinary solutions.

IX-C

Translational Implementation Challenges

Key barriers requiring resolution include scalability limitations in complex reaction environments, domain adaptation to emerging synthetic methodologies, standardization of cross-platform safety protocols, and energy efficiency constraints during large-scale model training and inference. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated efforts between computational researchers, synthetic chemists, and safety engineering specialists to ensure responsible technological maturation.

X
Conclusion

LLMs are revolutionizing organic synthesis by enabling accurate reaction predictions, efficient synthesis planning, and autonomous experimentation. From ChemLLM’s reaction predictions to Coscientist’s robotic syntheses, these models are transforming how chemists approach synthesis challenges. This survey highlights the integration of LLMs with other AI techniques and robotic systems, offering a unique perspective on their potential to automate and democratize organic synthesis. Despite challenges like data quality and safety concerns, the future is bright, with multi-modal LLMs and open-source initiatives paving the way for innovation. Young researchers are encouraged to engage with these tools, contributing to a new era of chemical discovery.
