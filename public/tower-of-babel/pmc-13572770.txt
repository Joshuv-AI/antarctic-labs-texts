Cardiometabolic Health in P'urépecha Migrant‐Sending Communities in Michoacan, Mexico
Authors: Isaura Cruz, Amanda L Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMigration–health research frequently attributes cardiometabolic risk among migrants to post‐migration dietary and lifestyle change, despite limited empirical evidence from migrant‐sending communities. This assumption is particularly salient in studies of indigenous populations, where pre‐migration contexts are often treated as culturally traditional and implicitly protective. Using a mixed‐methods biocultural design, this study examines cardiometabolic risk among never‐migrant P'urépecha adults living in migrant‐sending communities in Michoacán, Mexico, to assess whether commonly used sociocultural indicators are associated with cardiometabolic outcomes and to situate origin‐community health within broader population trends. We collected anthropometric measures, cardiometabolic biomarkers, and survey data from 193 adults and integrated these data with ethnographic observations of local food environments and comparisons to national surveillance benchmarks from ENSANUT. Cardiometabolic risk was widespread. Seventy‐five percent of participants were overweight or obese, 59% were hypertensive, 34% exhibited dysglycemia (HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or medication use), and 77% met criteria for metabolic syndrome. In multivariable analyses, age and sex were consistently associated with cardiometabolic risk, while income, perceived stress, language proficiency, and cultural/ethnic identification were not independently associated with outcomes. Although bivariate differences by language group were observed, these associations disappeared after adjustment for age. When interpreted alongside ethnographic observations and population‐level benchmarks, these findings reveal that cardiometabolic risk is already embedded within the everyday environments of indigenous migrant‐sending communities. The lack of consistent associations between commonly used sociocultural indicators and health outcomes suggests that shared community‐level exposures may be more important than individual measures of language or cultural identification in shaping cardiometabolic health. By integrating biomarker, survey, and ethnographic data, this study demonstrates the value of a biocultural approach for understanding health before migration and provides a critical point of comparison for future migration‐health research.Keywords: biocultural approach, cardiometabolic risk, indigenous populations, life course health, migration and health

Introduction

Research on the health of Latin American immigrants in the US has consistently documented elevated risk for cardiometabolic conditions over time, often attributing these patterns to dietary and lifestyle changes that occur after migration as a result of acculturation into mainstream US society (Abraído‐Lanza et al. 2006). However, the majority of research exploring health outcomes of Latin American immigrants in the US lacks a comparison sample from the country of origin (Bostean 2012). This obscures some of the changes that occur with migration and enables pre‐conceived notions to stand in place of the real shifts experienced by immigrants and their US‐born descendants (Hunt et al. 2004). As an example, dietary changes after migration are thought to be detrimental and the diet pre‐migration has been treated as static, traditional, and protective against cardiometabolic risk (Himmelgreen et al. 2014). This view has not taken into account the fact that global processes have affected multiple regions over the past several decades. In the case of Latin America, the nutrition transition (NT; Popkin 1993) has been widely documented and described, with diets shifting toward a higher content of fat, sugar, and refined/processed foods. This shift has been observed in Mexico since 1988 with an increase in consumption of fat, soda and refined carbohydrates. At the same time, consumption of fruits and vegetables has declined (Rivera et al. 2002). Despite this shift, much health research continued to assume that the pre‐migration diet of individuals was healthier than post‐migration, without specifically delineating the elements that made it so (Ayala et al. 2008).

Although migration and health research has begun to take the diverse national origins of migrants into consideration, this does not account for the great deal of diversity that exists within the bounds of a country. In Mexico, for example, approximately 21.5% of the population identifies as indigenous (Instituto Nacional de Salud Publica [INSP] 2019). Specific indigenous populations range from several thousand to hundreds of thousands and live in highly diverse geographic and sociocultural contexts. In addition to this sociocultural diversity, Mexico has experienced uneven development leading to highly variable economic conditions (Hall and Patrinos 2006). These factors combine and generate vast differences in the lived experiences of Mexican populations. These differences are reflected in the body and have resulted in characteristics such as a declining north‐to‐south height gradient among indigenous adults, first noted in records from 1890–1950 (Faulhaber 1976) and still visible among indigenous children (Malina et al. 2018). The idea that this reflects various kinds of access to resources (Malina et al. 2018) is supported by reports from Mexico's Population Council that find the best socioeconomic conditions in the north and center of the country (Consejo Nacional de Población 2021). Indigenous groups in Mexico have experienced centuries of exclusion and decimation that have prevented equitable socioeconomic access (Rosas‐Vargas 2007).

Several studies have reported characteristics that were historically viewed as protective among indigenous populations in Mexico. Analyses of national nutrition surveys found that indigenous and rural households consumed fruits and vegetables more frequently than urban populations and maintained greater engagement with subsistence food production (Ramírez‐Silva et al. 2009). Earlier epidemiologic studies also reported lower prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension among indigenous populations relative to non‐indigenous Mexicans (Guerrero‐Romero et al. 2000; Rodríguez‐Morán et al. 2008; Stoddard et al. 2011). These findings contributed to the perception that indigenous communities retained dietary and lifestyle characteristics that protected against cardiometabolic disease.

More recent evidence suggests a more complex picture. National surveys have documented rising levels of overweight, obesity, diabetes, and other cardiometabolic risk factors throughout Mexico, including among rural and indigenous populations (INSP 2020; Shamah‐Levy et al. 2019). At the same time, qualitative and ethnographic studies have described increasing market integration, greater availability of processed and prepared foods, and changing dietary practices in indigenous communities (Asián Chaves and Pasos Cervera 2017; Chaudhari et al. 2013; Azcorra et al. 2013). In the case of P'urépecha communities, these changes in diet can be traced back to the 1940s (González‐Rivadeneira et al. 2022), well before significant migration‐health research occurred. Together, these findings raise questions about whether previously observed protective patterns persist and highlight the need for contemporary community‐level assessments of cardiometabolic health among indigenous populations.

This question is particularly important in the context of migration. A substantial body of research has documented worsening cardiometabolic health among Latin American immigrants and their US‐born descendants, often attributing these patterns to post‐migration dietary and lifestyle change (Abraído‐Lanza et al. 2006; Bostean 2012). Yet the health conditions present within communities of origin are rarely measured directly. Consequently, migration‐health research frequently relies on assumptions about the pre‐migration context rather than empirical data from migrant‐sending regions.

In this paper, we examine cardiometabolic risk factors among never‐migrant indigenous P'urépecha adults living in migrant‐sending communities in Michoacán, Mexico. Using a mixed‐methods design that integrates biomarkers, anthropometrics, survey data, and ethnographic observations, we assess the prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors and evaluate whether commonly used sociocultural indicators—including language use, cultural affiliation, perceived stress, and income—are associated with cardiometabolic outcomes. By directly documenting health conditions within communities of origin, this study provides empirical data on the context from which migration trajectories begin and evaluates assumptions regarding pre‐migration protection. Specifically, we ask: (1) What is the prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors among this population? (2) What sociodemographic and sociocultural factors are associated with cardiometabolic risk?, and (3) What is the everyday ethnographic context and how does it help to interpret the quantitative patterns observed? This explicitly situates biomarker and survey findings within lived experience and allows us to go beyond assumptions about the pre‐migration context.

Our findings show that elevated cardiometabolic risk is already present in migrant‐sending regions and highlight the importance of documenting origin‐community health conditions as a necessary foundation for future migration‐health research.

Methods

Study Population

This research was conducted among P'urépecha indigenous communities from Michoacán state in west‐central Mexico. The P'urépecha population has historically inhabited the area around the Lake Patzcuaro basin, the surrounding valleys, and mountains of the Trans‐Mexican Volcanic Belt (Romero Robles 2018). Figure 1 shows the P'urépecha region in relation to other indigenous groups in Mexico. Recent estimates place the population total at around 193 434 (Pueblos Indígenas de Mexico 2020). This group was chosen for this research because of Michoacán state's large contribution to US migration (Durand and Massey 2019) and this group's long tradition of migration to the US. In addition, there is variability in the group's migration strategies. Part of the population is composed of never‐migrants, residing permanently in Michoacán. Another sub‐group migrates seasonally to the US to work in agriculture or service industries and then returns to Mexico. The third group is composed of migrants remaining indefinitely in the US (Leco Tomas 2009). These migration streams provide opportunities for future comparative research. The present study focuses on never‐migrants in order to establish a baseline profile of cardiometabolic health within migrant‐sending communities prior to examining migration‐related variation.

Participants

Potential participants were those residing in indigenous communities in Michoacán and with no first‐hand history of migration to the United States. Participants originated in two communities surrounding Lake Patzcuaro and in one community in the area known as the P'urépecha Plateau in the Michoacán mountains. This project received approval from the institutional review board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (Study # 18‐1535). The first author obtained approval from each community's indigenous council, and the latter issued invitations to the community at large to participate. We set up collection stations in the town's cultural center. In addition, individuals visiting a local clinic as part of requirements for the vaccination of children and for a conditional cash transfer program were invited to participate. We collected data in July 2015 and between July and September 2019. A total of 193 adults participated in the study, including 149 women (77.2%) and 44 men (22.8%).

Measures

This research employed a mixed‐methods design integrating anthropometric and cardiometabolic measurements, survey‐based measures of demographic, socioeconomic, and sociocultural characteristics, and ethnographic observations. The survey component included measures frequently used in migration‐health research, such as language use, cultural identification, and perceived stress, while ethnographic observations provided contextual information on everyday community environments and practices that informed interpretation of the quantitative findings. Height was measured to the nearest centimeter using a portable stadiometer (SECA) on a firm surface with participants' shoes off. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.05 kg on a portable scale (Health‐O‐Meter 822KL) with shoes off and participants in light clothing. Height and weight data were used to calculate body mass index (BMI). Waist circumference (WC) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm following the NHANES protocol. Blood pressure was measured with an Omron automated monitor. Two readings were taken after participants were seated for 10 min and the average of these was recorded. Blood samples were obtained from a finger stick. High density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and triglycerides (TG) were analyzed using a lipid profile cassette for the Cholestech LDX analyzer. Glucose metabolism was assessed using an Affinion AS100 Analyzer (Alere). Hemoglobin A1c (A1C) was used because it provides a snapshot of glycemic control in the preceding 90 days and remains stable throughout the day. This makes A1C a more suitable measure of diabetic status in a field setting.

The guidelines provided by the American Heart Association were used to establish cut points for MetS risk factors. Following AHA, hypertension was defined as SBP > 130 mmHg and/or DBP > 80 mmHg, central adiposity was considered present if WC exceeded 101 cm for men and 88 cm for women. Participants with HbA1c ≥ 5.7% were classified as exhibiting dysglycemia, while those with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or reporting diabetes medication use were classified as diabetic.

TG were considered elevated if > 150 mg/dL for men and women, HDL‐C was considered low if < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women. Based on AHA guidelines, participants were classified as having MetS if they met three or more of the criteria described above. Waist to height ratio was used as an additional measure of adiposity.

Past U.S‐based immigrant health research has relied heavily on the concept of acculturation to explain worsening health outcomes. Acculturation tends to be operationalized through measures of language use and ethnic or cultural affiliation. These are used as proxies for broader processes thought to influence health. We deliberately included these variables here to evaluate the explanatory utility of the constructs most commonly used in migration‐health research. This also allows us to obtain baselines for direct comparison in future immigrant research of P'urépecha in the US. We used the Abbreviated Multidimensional Acculturation Scale (AMAS; Zea et al. 2003) to measure cultural identification and language ability. Prior to administering the AMAS scale we asked individuals the screening item “Do you speak the P'urépecha and/or Spanish language?” If an individual responded “yes,” we administered the relevant language sub‐scale. For cultural identification, the AMAS has a four‐point scale (from 1 “strongly disagree to 4 “strongly agree”) so that participants can respond to statements relating to cultural identity such as “I think of myself as being P'urépecha.” The languages spoken and ethnic identification sub‐scales further probe competence in language and ethnic/cultural affinity. An advantage of this scale is that it was designed to be tailored to circumstances where more than two cultures co‐exist (Zea et al. 2003). This is the case for research participants even prior to migrating to the US as they negotiate elements of P'urépecha and broader Mexican societal contexts. If these proxies for acculturation truly capture elements of navigating life between two cultural contexts, we should see associations between them and cardiometabolic health indicators. Given the association of stress and health, we used the Perceived Stress Scale (Cohen et al. 1983) to measure stress in the sample and its potential associations with health outcomes in this population.

Ethnographic Data Collection and Analytic Purpose

Interviews and focus groups in various P'urépecha communities in both Mexico and the US have continuously informed the study design itself. Potential participants shared the areas of concern regarding health both at the personal and community level and the sociocultural context more generally. These concerns were used to include or exclude research elements. For example, pilot work in Mexico suggested that diabetes and lipid measurements should also be included in the final study. In subsequent fieldwork, we expanded ethnographic observations to several settings within the community. Some of the relevant settings included the town's cultural center, a clinic, the town square, the premises of a restaurant, a dessert stall located off the main street, and several household cooking spaces. We also participated in more formal settings such as local conferences, meetings, and presentations with a focus on historical, cultural, and political topics of relevance for the community. These observations served as analytic data used to both guide the variables that should be included in the study as well as to interpret quantitative results and to evaluate the explanatory utility of commonly used sociocultural variables.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, socioeconomic, sociocultural, anthropometric, and cardiometabolic characteristics of the sample. Means and standard deviations are presented for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Sex differences were assessed using two‐sample t‐tests.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine associations between sociodemographic and sociocultural characteristics and cardiometabolic outcomes. Separate models were estimated for hypertension, central obesity, dysglycemia (HbA1c ≥ 5.7%), elevated total cholesterol, low HDL‐C, elevated LDL‐C, and elevated triglycerides. All models included age, sex, and household income as covariates. Sociocultural predictors included P'urépecha language proficiency, Spanish language proficiency, P'urépecha cultural affiliation, Mexican cultural affiliation, and perceived stress scores.

As a sensitivity analysis, linear regression models were used to examine associations between sociocultural variables and continuous cardiometabolic outcomes. Results were compared with the primary logistic regression analyses to assess the robustness of findings.

Because language use is frequently employed as a proxy for acculturation in migration‐health research, we conducted analyses examining whether speaking P'urépecha was associated with cardiometabolic outcomes. Independent t‐tests were used to compare continuous cardiometabolic, demographic, and sociocultural measures between P'urépecha speakers and monolingual Spanish speakers.

All analyses were conducted using Stata 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 193 adults participated in the study, including 149 women (77.2%) and 44 men (22.8%). Mean age was 42.0 years (SD 14.5) and mean monthly household income was 4747 MXN (SD 6705). Participants reported relatively high levels of affiliation with both P'urépecha (M = 3.30, SD = 0.56) and Mexican identities (M = 3.40, SD = 0.38). Spanish language proficiency scores were higher than P'urépecha language proficiency scores (M = 2.05, SD = 0.32 vs. M = 0.64, SD = 0.84). Mean Perceived Stress Scale score was 20.5 (SD 6.7). Cardiometabolic Profile

Table 2 summarizes the prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors in the study sample and compares these estimates with available state and national estimates. Overall, cardiometabolic risk was high across multiple indicators. Hypertension was prevalent, with 59% of participants meeting AHA thresholds for the condition. In this sample, 29.9% of participants met the criteria for diabetes (A1c above 6.4% or medication use) and 34% exhibited dysglycemia (HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or medication use). Based on body mass index, 45.8% of participants were classified as overweight and 29.2% met the criteria for obesity. Ethnographic Context of Food Access and Lived Experience

Ethnographic observations across communities indicate an active and unique food environment characterized by informal commerce. Small to medium food vendors include home‐based operations, semi‐permanent stalls, and mobile sellers distributed throughout town centers and within residential areas. This makes both ingredients and prepared foods widely available throughout the day. Any household is a short walk away from meat markets, tortilla sellers, grocers, and vendors offering prepared foods. Meals consumed outside the home were common and affordable. For example, in Cherán, pozole was among the most common offerings available for midday as well as for evening meals. A medium serving in summer of 2019 cost $10 MXN (around $0.50 USD), making it an accessible purchase. The prepared food landscape also includes foods outside of the historical menu, such as pizza or fried chicken. Purchasing prepared food was not restricted to special occasions but formed part of everyday household food provisioning. Ethnographic observations further suggested that food procurement practices were broadly shared across households. Despite variation in household income, residents generally relied on the same network of food vendors, markets, and prepared food sellers, resulting in similar exposure to the local food environment.

In addition to purchasing prepared foods and ingredients to cook, many households engaged in small‐scale agriculture and foraging. The most commonly cultivated foods include corn, beans, wheat, as well as a variety of fruit trees grown in household plots or parcels located a short distance outside of town. Foraged wild plants and animals also contributed to the diet in smaller measure than they did historically as they now require greater effort to obtain, especially as many items now grow in more distant areas or at a higher elevation. They are becoming scarcer still as some individuals will collect them to sell at high prices on market days. Because of these reasons, these food items served to supplement purchased or grown food items.

Households in P'urépecha communities were typically multigenerational, with extended family members residing within the same plot or even in the same dwelling. This familial structure facilitated shared meals, caregiving, and exchange of resources. Linguistic practices varied with age, with older individuals being more likely to speak P'urépecha either primarily or in addition to Spanish. All age groups had some fluency in Spanish, with younger individuals more likely to be monolingual Spanish speakers but retaining some ability to understand the P'uré language.

Taken together, the results highlight a disconnect between commonly used sociocultural indicators and observed cardiometabolic outcomes, while ethnographic observations point to shared community‐level exposures shaping daily life. In the Discussion, we integrate these findings to explain why cardiometabolic risk is broadly distributed across origin communities and to situate these results within migration‐health and biocultural frameworks.

Discussion

This study examined cardiometabolic risk factors among never‐migrant P'urépecha adults living in migrant‐sending communities in Michoacán. Our primary aim was to obtain a baseline profile for cardiometabolic risk in the place of origin. We also assessed whether sociocultural indicators commonly used in US‐based migrant health research were associated with health outcomes in this context. Finally, we integrated ethnographic observations to contextualize our biomarker and survey findings.

Overall, our findings indicate that cardiometabolic risk is prevalent in communities of origin, and that associations between language use, cultural identification, and health outcomes are limited once age and sex are taken into account. When interpreted alongside national surveys (ENSANUT) and ethnographic observations of everyday food environments, these results suggest that cardiometabolic risk may already be evident in migrant‐sending communities. These findings demonstrate that substantial cardiometabolic risk exists within migrant‐sending communities. Origin‐community conditions should be incorporated more explicitly into migration‐health research to better understand how these contribute to health trajectories across the migration process.

While the levels of cardiometabolic risk factors were high in our sample, state‐ and national‐level estimates from ENSANUT suggest that our findings are not unique; rather, our results reflect broader population‐level trends unfolding within specific local contexts. Comparisons with ENSANUT data indicate that the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and low HDL‐C is higher for this sample vs. national estimates. Risk factors such as overweight, obesity, and elevated triglycerides are close to the national estimates. Rather than interpreting these differences as deviations unique to this study population, the ENSANUT comparisons underscore that cardiometabolic risk is already well established within the broader population from which migrants originate. These patterns highlight the importance of accounting for origin‐community conditions when interpreting migrant health outcomes.

Here, ethnographic observations help bridge the gap between population‐level trends and lived experience. The widespread availability of prepared foods, dense informal food vending, and reliance on purchased meals documented in these communities illustrate how broader national changes are experienced in everyday life. Importantly, older participants described a food environment that differed substantially from the present one. While prepared foods were also available in previous decades, they more closely reflected foods historically produced within the community and were commonly prepared through methods such as grilling, roasting, or steaming rather than frying. Participants also described meals as less centered on animal‐source foods than those commonly available today. These accounts suggest that contemporary cardiometabolic risk is not the product of an abrupt replacement of “traditional” foods, but rather the cumulative result of long‐term transformations in local food systems. The coexistence of small‐scale agriculture with inexpensive prepared foods demonstrates how migrant‐sending communities remain embedded within broader economic and nutritional changes occurring throughout Mexico.

Explaining Sociocultural Associations Through Ethnographic Context

Across analytic approaches, age emerged as the most consistent predictor of cardiometabolic risk, while income, perceived stress, language use, and cultural identification were not independently associated with outcomes after adjustment. Ethnographic observations help explain these patterns. As described in the Results, food access and daily routines were broadly shared across households, regardless of language use or cultural identification. Prepared foods were widely available across neighborhoods, and participation in informal food economies was not restricted by income or identity.

Within this context, commonly measured sociocultural indicators may have limited explanatory power because exposures relevant to cardiometabolic risk are distributed across the community rather than concentrated in specific subgroups. Language use, in particular, appears to index generational position rather than distinct patterns of exposure. Older adults were more likely to speak P'urépecha and also exhibited higher cardiometabolic risk. However, ethnographic evidence suggests that language use itself does not correspond to substantially different food environments or daily practices. This helps explain why the bivariate associations seen with language were attenuated in adjusted models. This finding is notable because language use and cultural identification are among the most frequently employed measures in migration‐health research.

Rethinking Indigenous Protection Through Lived Environments

Migration‐health research often assumes that indigenous communities retain dietary and lifestyle characteristics that are protective against cardiometabolic disease prior to migration. Our findings challenge this assumption by showing that cardiometabolic risk is already widespread among never‐migrant indigenous adults and by demonstrating that cultural markers do not independently predict health outcomes once demographic factors are considered.

Ethnographic context is central to this reinterpretation. We see that households engaged in some degree of subsistence production and foraging, but these practices functioned as supplements rather than primary sources of nutrition. At the same time, prepared foods were readily accessible and embedded in everyday routines. Participants also emphasized that many foraged foods that once formed part of local diets have become increasingly difficult to obtain. Deforestation and land use change have pushed many edible plants and wild resources toward the margins of traditional territory, increasing the time and effort required for collection. As a result, some of these foods are now harvested primarily for sale at premium prices rather than for routine household consumption. These observations illustrate that the persistence of culturally valued food practices does not necessarily translate into substantial dietary intake. Rather than representing an intact alternative to contemporary food systems, subsistence activities increasingly coexist with and are constrained by broader environmental and economic transformations.

Contributions and Implications

This study makes three primary contributions. First, it provides rare origin‐community biomarker data from indigenous migrant‐sending regions and situates these findings within ENSANUT population benchmarks. Second, by linking quantitative associations to ethnographic descriptions of local food environments, it demonstrates why commonly used sociocultural indicators have limited power to explain cardiometabolic risk in contexts where exposure is widespread. Third, it demonstrates the value of documenting origin‐community health conditions directly rather than inferring them from destination‐based studies, thereby providing a baseline for future research examining health across migration trajectories.

These findings suggest that migration‐health research would benefit from greater integration of national surveillance data, community‐based biomarkers, and ethnographic context. Public health interventions that focus exclusively on migrants in destination settings may overlook critical opportunities for prevention and care in migrant‐sending regions where cardiometabolic risk is already embedded in everyday environments.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study is limited by its cross‐sectional design and the overrepresentation of women, reflecting gendered patterns of participation in community‐based research. Additionally, while ENSANUT provides valuable comparative benchmarks, differences in sampling strategies limit direct comparability. Future research should pursue longitudinal designs that track cardiometabolic trajectories across migration pathways and further examine how changes in local food environments shape metabolic health over time.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that substantial cardiometabolic risk is already present among never‐migrant indigenous adults living in migrant‐sending communities. By centering never‐migrant P'urépecha adults in migrant‐sending communities and integrating biomarkers, national surveillance benchmarks, and ethnographic context, we show that cardiometabolic risk is already widespread prior to migration and is shaped by long‐term structural and environmental conditions within origin communities. Rather than reflecting a simple replacement of traditional foods, cardiometabolic risk appears to emerge within food environments where subsistence practices, purchased ingredients, and prepared foods coexist.

Our findings challenge assumptions that indigeneity, language retention, or cultural identification confer inherent protection against cardiometabolic disease. Instead, commonly used sociocultural indicators showed limited independent association with cardiometabolic outcomes once demographic factors were considered. Ethnographic evidence demonstrates why these indicators fail to explain health variation: exposure to cardiometabolic risk is broadly distributed across everyday food environments and routines, rather than concentrated within specific cultural or socioeconomic groups.

By situating community‐based biomarker data alongside ENSANUT benchmarks, this study bridges a critical gap between national surveillance and local experience. It illustrates how population‐level trends are embodied within indigenous migrant‐sending regions and underscores the importance of examining health trajectories before migration occurs. This study establishes a baseline against which future studies of P'urépecha migrant populations can be evaluated.

These findings have implications for both research and intervention. For migration‐health scholarship, they highlight the necessity of incorporating origin‐community data and ethnographic insight into analyses of migrant health. Too often, the pre‐migration context is assumed rather than directly measured. By documenting cardiometabolic health within a migrant‐sending indigenous population, this study demonstrates the value of establishing empirical baselines in communities of origin. For public health practice, the findings suggest that efforts focused exclusively on migrants in destination settings may overlook important opportunities for prevention and care in migrant‐sending regions where cardiometabolic risk is already prevalent. Addressing cardiometabolic disease therefore requires attention not only to migration itself, but also to the community environments and lived experiences that shape health before migration occurs.
