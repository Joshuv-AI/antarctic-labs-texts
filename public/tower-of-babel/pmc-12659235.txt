The effect of psychoeducational ıntervention on postpartum care on postpartum depression and breastfeeding: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yasemin Özhüner, Nebahat Özerdoğan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsidering that postpartum depression can negatively impact the health of both mother and baby, preventive psychoeducational interventions are needed. Therefore, the aim of the study was to evaluate the effect of a psychoeducational intervention applied to postpartum care on postpartum depression and breastfeeding outcomes.MethodsThe research was a randomized pretest-posttest controlled group intervention trial. It was conducted with 102 pregnant women randomly assigned to intervention (n = 50) and control (n = 52) groups between 20 and 34 weeks of gestation in Family Health Centers. While all participants received standard care, the intervention group additionally participated in a psychoeducational program based on Watson’s Human Caring Model. This structured intervention was delivered at four timepoints: three sessions before delivery at weekly intervals, followed by sessions on postpartum day 10 and week 6.ResultsBefore the intervention, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups on the Antenatal Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scales scores. However, after the intervention, Postpartum Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scales scores were significantly higher in the intervention group compared to controls. Analysis revealed an inverse relationship in the intervention group, where increased breastfeeding self-efficacy coincided with decreased levels of postpartum depression. Statistical modeling showed that changes in breastfeeding self-efficacy explained 17.4% of the variance in postpartum depression levels among women receiving the psychoeducational intervention.ConclusionThe psychoeducational program based on Watson’s Human Caring Model effectively reduced the risk of postpartum depression while increasing breastfeeding self-efficacy among participants. These findings suggest that incorporating structured psychoeducational interventions into routine postpartum midwifery and nursing care can significantly improve maternal mental health and breastfeeding outcomes.Trial registration numberStudy registered on Clinical Trials.gov on November 12, 2024. Introduction

The postpartum period can be a positive experience for many women but may also involve physical, emotional, and psychosocial challenges. During this period, not only maternal health but also the well-being of the infant and the family may be affected. Therefore, it is essential that women are closely monitored for any complications that may arise [1].

Postpartum depression (PPD) is the most commonly observed psychiatric complication after childbirth and can persist for a long time. It is defined by international diagnostic criteria as a major depressive episode that begins within four weeks (for DSM-5) or six weeks (for ICD-11) after childbirth [2, 3]. Beyond its direct impact on maternal psychological well-being, PPD can also influence maternal behaviors and infant care practices, particularly.

Breastfeeding is one of the most important factors affecting maternal and infant health during the postpartum period. Mothers exhibiting depressive symptoms in the fourth and sixth weeks postpartum can negatively affect their breastfeeding processes. Depressive mood reduces mothers’ motivation and willingness to breastfeed, leading to challenges such as increased nipple pain, breastfeeding difficulties, and reduced breastfeeding self-efficacy. Mothers with low breastfeeding self-efficacy are more likely to discontinue breastfeeding prematurely, which may negatively impact both maternal health and infant development [4–6]. Thus, supporting maternal mental health plays a critical role in ensu.

Postpartum breastfeeding self-efficacy (PBSES) has been shown to act as a protective factor against the development of PPD. Higher PBSES strengthens maternal confidence in infant care and breastfeeding, reduces anxiety, and facilitates more positive postpartum experiences [7–9]. Conversely, low PBSES increases vulnerability to depressive symptoms, creating a bidirectional relationship between PPD and breastfeeding outcomes [9, 10]. Structured interventions such as midwife-led breastfeeding groups or nurse-led online counseling programs have been reported to simultaneously increase PBSES and reduce PPD severity [7, 11]. This evidence indicates that preventive strategies in postpartum care should not only focus on psychological well-being but also incorporate elements that enhance breastfeeding self-efficacy, given the interdependence of these two factors.

Preventive interventions applied before the onset of PPD are therefore of paramount importance. Such interventions can reduce risks associated with postpartum depression while supporting maternal–infant health more broadly. Preventive interventions applied before the onset of PPD are extremely important in preventing the risks associated with postpartum depression. Therefore, there is a need for preventive interventions. Psychoeducational interventions are one such type of intervention [12, 13]. Psychoeducation is a structured educational intervention that aims to enhance individuals’ coping skills, create desired behavior changes in healthcare professionals, and ensure their adaptation to problems [14]. Psychoeducation programs aim to reduce stress, provide emotional support, improve quality of life, and develop coping skills [15, 16].

When midwives and nurses implement psychoeducational programs, they need defined and organized theories and models to achieve effective and beneficial results [17]. The Watson Human Caring Model empowers psychosocially ill individuals and enhances their quality of life. The model emphasizes using a comprehensive approach to preserve, enhance, and improve a person’s mind-body-spirit harmony [18]. When examining studies on postpartum care aimed at preventing PPD and promoting breastfeeding, no study has been found that implemented a psychoeducational intervention based on Watson Human Caring Model.

The goal of the study

The goal of this study is to assess how a psychoeducational intervention on postpartum care affects PPD and breastfeeding.

Hypotheses of the research

1H1: There is a significant difference between the post-test depression score averages of women who received psychoeducation for postpartum care and those who received standard care.

2H1: There is a significant difference between the post-test breastfeeding self-efficacy score averages of women who received psychoeducation for postpartum care and those who received standard care.

Methods

Design and setting

This study used a randomized controlled design with pre-intervention and post-intervention assessments. The study was conducted in Family Health Centers (FHC) affiliated with the Provincial Health Directorate in the Central Anatolian province of Türkiye. Data collection took place between November 13, 2024 and March 1, 2025. In these health centers, qualified midwives and nurses provide routine monitoring and educational support to pregnant women and mothers throughout the perinatal period.

Both centers function as primary-level healthcare institutions offering similar maternal and child health services and following the same national guidelines and public health protocols. They serve demographically and socioeconomically comparable populations within the same province. According to information obtained from the Provincial Health Directorate, the breastfeeding and birth rates in these service areas are similar, which supports the methodological consistency of the study setting.

Women who were between the 20th and 34th weeks of pregnancy, over 18 years of age, literate, planning to breastfeed, without visual, auditory, or mental disabilities, and with a healthy fetus confirmed by ultrasound were included in the study. The 20–34 weeks gestation period was chosen based on methodological and practical considerations supported in the literature because it represents a physiologically stable period during which women are more receptive to psychoeducational interventions. This period provides sufficient time for participants to internalize information before birth and is consistent with previous studies and international recommendations that emphasize providing psychosocial and breastfeeding support between the late second and early third trimesters [19–26].

Women and newborns with depression and other mental illnesses diagnosed by a physician, those using medication related to mental illness, those who had preterm births, those with contraindications to breastfeeding, and newborns with anomalies such as anencephaly/spina bifida (head and spine anomalies), hydrocephalus/microcephalus (brain anomalies), those using active substances, or those diagnosed with HIV, etc., women who were included in the study in the 3rd trimester but had a birth weight below 2500 g, those whose newborns were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit due to any postpartum complications, and women who had premature babies (born before 37 weeks of gestation), and those who did not participate in more than one interview were not included in the study.

The universe and a sample of the research

The study population comprised pregnant women attending the FHC during the data collection period. Sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software, with parameters including 95% confidence level, 80% statistical power, effect size of d = 0.59, and type I error rate of 0.05, yielding a required sample of 94 participants (47 per group). Anticipating approximately 15% attrition [27, 28], researchers recruited 108 participants (54 per group).

The randomization process employed a simple randomization technique to allocate eligible pregnant women to either the intervention (n = 54) or control (n = 54) group. The research’s consort flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. Using the Random Integer Generator function from random.org, researchers created columns numbered 1 through 108. Each participant who met the inclusion criteria was assigned either number one or two based on their registration information.

CONSORT flow chart of groups

The designation of which number (one or two) corresponded to intervention or control status was determined through a drawing of lots conducted at the study’s initiation. Following this randomization procedure, pregnant women assigned number one were placed in the intervention group, while those assigned number two formed the control group. Subsequent research interviews were planned according to this random distribution.

The study adheres to CONSORT guidelines and include a completed CONSORT checklist.

Midwives numbered the pregnant women list to prevent randomization bias; the researcher was not involved. Due to the nature of the study, blinding of the researcher administering the intervention and the participants was not possible.

Data analysis was performed by a statistician blinded to group allocation, ensuring unbiased evaluation of outcomes. To maintain separation between groups and prevent cross-contamination, participants from intervention and control cohorts were scheduled to visit the facility on different days throughout the study period. This scheduling approach, combined with individual interview sessions for each participant, effectively prevented interaction between women from different study groups, thereby preserving the integrity of the experimental design.

Data collection abilities

Personal information form

The form created to determine the socio-demographic characteristics of the individuals included in the research was prepared by the researcher in accordance with the literatüre [29–33].

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)

The EPDS consists of ten self-reported items that assess maternal mood disturbances during the preceding week. Each question offers four response options on a Likert format, yielding a possible score range of 0–30. Elevated scores correspond to increased severity of depressive manifestations. The instrument employs a threshold value of 13; scores below this point suggest minimal risk, while scores at or above this threshold indicate potential postpartum depression [34].

Cox and colleagues introduced this screening ability in 1987, with subsequent Turkish validation performed by Engindeniz and team in 1996. The Turkish adaptation demonstrated strong internal consistency (α = 0.87) [35]. Our implementation of the scale maintained equivalent reliability metrics, with analysis confirming a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 across our study population.

Antenatal Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (ABSES)

This 20-item instrument, developed by Wells et al. (2006), assesses pregnant women’s confidence in their ability to breastfeed successfully [36]. The Turkish adaptation was completed by Aydın and Pasinlioğlu in 2018 [37]. The scale employs a 5-point Likert format, yielding total scores between 20 and 100, with higher scores indicating stronger breastfeeding self-efficacy perceptions. The Turkish version demonstrated strong reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 in the validation study. In the current research, the scale showed excellent internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90.

Postpartum Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (PBSES)

This instrument was originally developed by Dennis and Faux (1999) as a 33-item measure to evaluate mothers’ confidence in their breastfeeding abilities [38]. In 2003, Dennis created a more efficient 14-item short form, which is now the recommended version due to its ease of administration while maintaining assessment accuracy.

The scale uses a 5-point Likert format, with total scores ranging from 14 to 70. Higher scores reflect greater breastfeeding self-efficacy. Aluş and Tokat conducted the Turkish validation study in 2009, establishing excellent reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 [39]. In the current study, the scale demonstrated exceptional internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.98.

Stages of the research

In the initial preparation phase, the researcher responsible for delivering the psychoeducational intervention completed two specialized certificate programs in psychoeducation methodology before conducting the study. These included the “Psychoeducation Practitioner Course in Psychiatric Nursing: Examples from Two Different Fields” (8 h) organized by the Psychiatric Nurses Association and the “Psychoeducation Preparation and Practitioner Training” (20 h) organized by the Mental Health Association. Both programs provided theoretical instruction on psychoeducational design, cognitive-behavioral and group-based intervention techniques, as well as supervised practical sessions focused on developing, implementing, and evaluating psychoeducational modules.

In addition to these certified trainings, the researcher holds a doctoral degree in midwifery and has extensive professional experience in maternal and child health, including psychosocial counseling for women during pregnancy and the postpartum period. In Türkiye, midwives are authorized primary healthcare professionals who provide not only physical care but also emotional and psychological support to women throughout the perinatal process. Therefore, the researcher’s academic background and clinical expertise are fully consistent with the purpose and scope of the psychoeducational intervention implemented in this study.

Building upon this professional and educational foundation, the intervention content was then systematically developed based on Watson’s Human Caring Model (WHCM) and specifically adapted to address the educational and emotional needs of women during the postpartum period.The educational materials for the intervention group included a comprehensive booklet covering nine essential topics: postpartum period fundamentals and care practices, maternal role adaptation and mother-infant bonding, lactation principles and breastfeeding techniques, breast care practices, maternal nutrition and infant care, psychological transitions during the postpartum period, PPD (prevalence, etiology, symptomatology, and impacts on maternal, infant, and community health), diagnostic approaches and treatment options, preventive strategies, social support resources, and coping mechanisms.

The educational booklet underwent rigorous quality assessment using the DISCERN evaluation ability, which provides standardized criteria for evaluating health information quality. Eight subject matter experts from midwifery and nursing disciplines conducted this quality review to ensure content validity and appropriateness. The DISCERN tool was selected because it is a validated and internationally recognized tool for assessing the quality and reliability of written health information. It assesses accuracy, clarity, and evidence-based content, making it suitable for psychoeducational materials [40, 41].

Before initiating the main study, a pilot study was conducted with ten pregnant women who met the same inclusion criteria as the main study participants. The aim was to evaluate the feasibility of the intervention, assess the clarity and appropriateness of the data collection tools, and refine the structure and content of the psychoeducational sessions. Based on participant feedback, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity of questionnaire items and to optimize the duration and flow of the intervention sessions. The findings from the pilot study confirmed the feasibility, acceptability, and comprehensibility of the intervention, and no major modifications were required before implementing the main study.

In the second phase, the sampled pregnant women were informed about the research, and their written informed consents were obtained. Four interviews were conducted with the pregnant women one week apart before childbirth, and two interviews were conducted on the tenth day and sixth week after childbirth, making a total of six interviews. We conducted these interviews one-on-one and face-to-face with the women. Other than getting standard care at the health centers, the women in the control group got no interventions. In routine care, pregnant women were monitored for edema, varicose veins, weight, blood pressure, pulse, laboratory findings, fetal heart sounds, medication intake, chronic disease follow-up, and vaccination. We provided counseling to pregnant women whenever they had any questions. The postpartum women’s blood pressure, temperature, pulse, bleeding, and laboratory findings were monitored, and they were provided with counseling whenever they had any questions. During the first consultation, women were asked to fill out the Personal Information Form, EPDS, and ABSES. In the final meeting, the EPDS and PBSES forms were filled out, and the booklet of the psychoeducational intervention program based on the Watson Model for postpartum care, prepared by the researcher, was given to the women. The women in the intervention group underwent six sessions of the psychoeducation program. In the first meeting, the psychoeducation intervention program booklet based on the Watson Model for postpartum care was given to the women, and the interview was conducted as part of the program. The study’s psychoeducational intervention program for postpartum care was based on the Watson Model. The sessions were run using the WHCM improvement processes and psychoeducational techniques. The interviews lasted an average of 90 to 120 min. The researcher provided phone consultations to the pregnant women when they had questions about the training. The sessions began with warm-up exercises by the researcher, followed by the topics of the week being explained and discussed according to the program, with questions being answered and counseling provided. At the end of the sessions, feedback was gathered, and homework was assigned (a composition reflecting the pregnant woman’s feelings and thoughts about her baby, discussions about the postpartum process with her spouse, household chores and baby care, role-sharing in breastfeeding, and discussions about postpartum psychological changes, noting positive and negative feelings and thoughts related to childbirth and the postpartum process, methods for addressing negative thoughts, and reflecting on communication with her spouse and surroundings and emotional support received). We concluded the sessions with relaxation exercises and scheduled the date and time for the next meeting. We filled out the EPDS and PBSES forms in the final meeting and concluded the sessions. To improve methodological rigor and ensure transparency, a structured summary of the psychoeducational program is presented in the main text (see Table 1), which outlines the session schedule, main topics, and related activities. The full detailed content of the sessions remains available in the Supplementary file. The interview program was specifically developed for this study and has not been published elsewhere before.

Data analyses

Statistical evaluation was performed using the SPSS software platform (version 21.0) designed for Windows operating systems. To establish baseline comparability between intervention and control groups, chi-square tests and t-tests were employed to assess homogeneity across demographic and clinical variables.

Between-group comparisons of outcome measures utilized t-test procedures. Linear regression analysis was performed to examine relationships between variables and determine predictive factors. All statistical analyses were interpreted using a 95% confidence interval, with statistical significance established at p < 0.05.

Regression analysis findings

The regression model examined how breastfeeding self-efficacy affected PPD levels in both study conditions. Using EPDS scores as the dependent variable and PBSES scores as the independent variable, the analysis revealed distinct patterns across groups.

For the intervention group, the model demonstrated statistical significance (F = 10.111, p < 0.01), revealing a strong inverse relationship between breastfeeding self-efficacy and depression symptoms (β=-0.545). This suggests that as breastfeeding self-efficacy increased among women receiving the psychoeducational intervention, PPD levels decreased significantly. The model showed that breastfeeding self-efficacy explained 17.4% of the variance in PPD scores in the intervention group.

In contrast, the regression model for the control group failed to reach statistical significance (F = 2.648, p > 0.05), but a similar directional relationship was observed (β=-0.271). This suggests that the psychoeducational intervention strengthened the inverse relationship between breastfeeding confidence and depressive symptoms.

These findings, presented in Table 4, suggest that enhancing breastfeeding self-efficacy through structured psychoeducational support may serve as an effective mechanism to reduce the risk of postpartum depression.

Discussion

Postpartum depression has negative consequences for both babies and mothers in terms of public health. Therefore, it is of vital significance to identify women who are potentially at risk for PPD in advance and to prevent any negative outcomes that may arise later. Preventive interventions applied before the onset of PPD are important in preventing the dramatic consequences that PPD can cause [42]. Therefore, we applied a psychoeducation program based on the Watson Model to women in our study to prevent postpartum depression. After psychoeducation, we found that the post-test depression scores of the women in the intervention group were significantly lower than those in the control group.

Linear regression analysis demonstrated that breastfeeding self-efficacy significantly predicted postpartum depression scores. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that higher breastfeeding self-efficacy is associated with lower levels of depression among postpartum women [7–11]. The regression coefficients obtained in our study fall within the range reported in similar psychoeducational and maternal health research, supporting the validity and interpretation of the numbers. Moreover, the literature indicates that interventions based on the WHCM or similar psychosocial frameworks strengthen self-efficacy and psychological adaptation, thereby reducing depressive symptoms [43–46]. Taken together, these findings confirm the link between breastfeeding self-efficacy and PPD reduction, which is further supported by our intervention results. We found that “there is a significant difference between the post-test depression score averages of women who received psychoeducation for postpartum care and those who received standard care.” This backs up the hypothesis (Hypothesis 1 H1). Additionally, it was determined that the average post-test score of the women in the intervention group was statistically significantly lower than their pre-test score, while no difference was found between the pre-test and post-test scores in the control group. In line with our findings, previous studies have also reported a decrease in PPD levels after psychoeducational interventions [47–51].

Lavrance et al. (2024) in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the effectiveness of web-based psychoeducation for PPD and anxiety in 103 women found that in the intervention group where psychoeducation was applied, PPD symptoms significantly decreased after four weeks, and improvements continued two weeks after follow-up [47]. In the RCT conducted by Özhüner and Özerdoğan (2024) with 91 women, evaluating the effect of a psychoeducational intervention based on the Watson model in the prevention of postpartum depression, it was determined that the six-week application of the Watson model-based psychoeducational intervention reduced the risk of PPD in women and increased their level of social support [50]. Lewkowitz et al. (2024) did a meta-analysis of thirty-one RCT that looked at the effects of digital health interventions on PPD and anxiety in 5492 women. They found that digital interventions (psychotherapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and psychoeducation) greatly improved psychoeducation and PPD symptoms [48]. Ong et al. (2023) looked at twelve RCT with 2083 first-time mothers and found that three months after giving birth, there was a statistically significant drop in PPD levels [49]. A study by Park et al. (2020) looked at seven RCT that looked at the effects of psychoeducation on the mental health and partner relationships of 7119 pregnant women. They found that the psychoeducation intervention decreased PPD in mothers and improved partner relationships [51]. In our study, it is believed that the application of psychoeducation according to the improvement processes of WHCM positively contributes to women’s management of the postpartum period. At the end of each session in the implemented program, women were given the opportunity to express themselves, share their feelings and experiences, and ask questions. Women were provided with information on topics they were curious about, their misconceptions were corrected, their strengths were highlighted, and they were supported in believing in their own potential and resources and recognizing their emotions. Under the title of breastfeeding and breast milk, breastfeeding counseling has been provided to mothers. These initiatives may have transformed problems into goals, increasing breastfeeding self-efficacy and reducing the level of postpartum depression. Additionally, women were provided with information on topics that they were most concerned about and needed, such as medication use, birth management, the postpartum period, PPD and other psychological issues, and baby care. Also, these counseling methods talked about in the psychoeducation application are thought to help stop the development of PPD by quickly solving problems and giving women support. Research findings indicate that when women are not adequately supported and preventive measures are not taken, the development process of PPD becomes easier and its duration can be prolonged [33, 49–54]. Therefore, addressing the pregnancy period and implementing preventive measures for women, especially those in high-risk groups, is extremely important for ensuring a healthy postpartum process.

Supporting mothers to breastfeed their babies and ensuring continued breastfeeding during the postpartum period can be protective against PPD [55]. Thus, the purpose of our research was to raise breastfeeding self-efficacy in the context of PPD prevention. It has been determined that the psychoeducation program based on WHCM significantly increased the average postpartum breastfeeding self-efficacy scores in the intervention group compared to the control group. Accordingly, the hypothesis, “There is a significant difference between the final test breastfeeding self-efficacy score averages of women who received psychoeducation for postpartum care and those who received standard care” (Hypothesis 2H1), has been accepted. It is believed that the increase in breastfeeding self-efficacy levels among women who participated in the program is related to the researcher providing practical breastfeeding education to the woman and her surroundings and managing the breastfeeding process together. Additionally, in the sessions held, women frequently expressed that “they wanted more support from their spouses, but did not receive sufficient support, and their relatives did not understand them.” Therefore, in order for breastfeeding self-efficacy to be at an adequate level, the woman’s spouse and relatives were also included in the process during the interviews.

Multiple regression analysis was used to look at how breastfeeding self-efficacy affected PPD levels in women from the intervention and control groups. It was found that breastfeeding self-efficacy greatly lowered PPD levels in women in the intervention group. When the literature is reviewed, studies similarly show that the level of breastfeeding self-efficacy reduces the level of PPD [7–11]. Xia et al. (2022), in a meta-analysis study that included eight RCT examining the relationship between breastfeeding and PPD in 18.570 women, found that women who exclusively breastfed their babies had a 53% lower risk of PPD compared to those who did not breastfeed at all. Additionally, the study observed that women who breastfed less had an 8% lower risk of PPD compared to those who did not breastfeed [9]. In a study by Rodríguez-Gallego et al. (2024), they looked at how well a midwife-led breastfeeding group intervention could prevent PPD in 382 women. They found that the intervention group had higher breastfeeding self-efficacy, longer breastfeeding periods, and lower PPD four months after giving birth [7]. Sivas et al. (2024) found in their retrospective cohort study examining the effect of daily breastfeeding frequency on PPD scores in 1451 women without depression risk factors during the early postpartum period that women who breastfed more frequently had a lower risk of PPD [8]. Adjie et al. (2024) conducted a meta-analysis of 19 RCT examining the relationship between support and breastfeeding and the severity of PPD in 79.303 women; they found that breastfeeding and psychosocial support reduced the severity of PPD and were preventive against the development of depressive symptoms [10]. Şimşek Çetinkaya (2024) found in an RCT examining the effects of a nurse-led online breastfeeding counseling system and breastfeeding education program on mothers in 72 women; EPDS scores were significantly lower in the intervention group on the 1st day and at 3 and 6 months postpartum [11].

In our study, it was observed that the psychoeducational program based on WHCM provided a positive effect on women experiencing the postpartum period. In this program, considering the improvement processes in WHCM, respect was shown for the individual’s values, a communication environment based on trust was created through love and compassion, and the individual was enabled to express themselves comfortably. Based on the model’s processes of “Instilling Faith-Hope” and “Encouraging Interpersonal Teaching-Learning,” the individual’s needs were identified, and counseling was provided on what actions to take regarding these issues. Additionally, it was demonstrated how a support system could be established by relatives and how mutual solutions could be developed.

Furthermore, the psychoeducational program included several practical strategies that enhanced participant engagement and facilitated behavioral change. Homework assignments encouraged women to reflect on their emotions and experiences during pregnancy and the postpartum period, fostering self-awareness and continuity of learning between sessions. Family involvement, particularly the participation of spouses and close relatives, was promoted to strengthen emotional and social support networks. In addition, problem-solving exercises were incorporated to help participants develop coping skills and adaptive responses to common postpartum challenges. These structured, interactive components were likely instrumental in improving breastfeeding self-efficacy and reducing depressive symptoms, thereby increasing the intervention’s clinical relevance and replicability.

In the literature, no study has been found on a psychoeducation program based on WHCM for postpartum care, postpartum depression, and breastfeeding. However, when studies applying Watson Human Caring Model in different health conditions and populations are examined, it is seen that the applications have been beneficial [43–45, 56]. Yangöz (2021) found in a randomized controlled trial where he evaluated the effect of motivational interviewing and education based on Watson Human Caring Model on treatment adherence and quality of life in individuals undergoing hemodialysis; the method was beneficial in 60 patients where the care was applied [43]. Bidik et al. (2024) found an increase in health-promoting behaviors, self-care perception, and psychological resilience in 80 nursing students in the RCT where they implemented a conscious self-compassion program based on WHCM [44]. In the RCT conducted by Bagheri et al. (2023) evaluating the impact of Watson’s human care-based health improvement program on the caregivers of schizophrenia patients, it was found that the program facilitated interpersonal and holistic care among the caregivers of schizophrenia patients and improved their sense of well-being [45]. In the RCT conducted by Durmazoğlu et al. (2024) based on WHCM, it was found that the support program applied to 50 women improved their breastfeeding self-efficacy levels and was beneficial [56].

It is also important to note that all psychoeducational sessions were conducted face-to-face in private counseling rooms within the Family Health Centers, ensuring a supportive and confidential environment for participants. Each session lasted approximately 90–120 min, allowing sufficient time for education, discussion, and individualized counseling. These implementation details are essential for understanding the intervention’s practicality and for supporting the reliability and reproducibility of the study findings.

Strengths and limitations

The research was conducted at two centers, which increases the reliability of the findings compared to single-center studies. However, the research results are limited to the sample group in which the study was conducted and encompass only women in the 20–34 weeks of pregnancy, which restricts generalizability. In addition, the study had a relatively small sample size and data were collected through self-reported scales (EPDS and PBSES), which may be subject to reporting bias. The study also did not include long-term follow-up, limiting our ability to assess the sustainability of the intervention’s effects. Finally, cultural and contextual factors specific to the study population may affect the applicability of the findings to different settings. Although parity, previous breastfeeding experience, and socio-demographic characteristics did not significantly differ between groups or influence the main outcomes, the relative homogeneity of these variables may still limit the broader generalizability of the findings.

In addition, although participants’ breastfeeding experience was recorded, specific information regarding nipple cracks or previous negative breastfeeding experiences was not collected. This may be considered a limitation, as such factors could potentially influence breastfeeding self-efficacy and breastfeeding behavior.

Future studies should include more diverse samples to evaluate these factors across different maternal backgrounds and to enhance the external validity of the findings.

Conclusions

This study showed that the psychoeducational intervention aimed at postpartum care is effective on PPD and breastfeeding self-efficacy. It was observed that the implemented program was effective in increasing breastfeeding self-efficacy and reducing postpartum depression. In addition to these outcomes, the study has several methodological strengths that reinforce the reliability of the findings. Notably, the study adopted a randomized controlled design, employed validated measurement tools (EPDS, ABSES and PBSES), and delivered face-to-face, individualized sessions based on the Watson Human Caring Model. These features collectively support the robustness and credibility of the study results. Based on these findings, we recommend implementing midwife and nurse-led psychoeducational interventions for women at elevated risk for PPD during both perinatal and postpartum periods. To facilitate widespread adoption of this approach, healthcare systems should provide specialized in-service training for midwives and nurses on psychoeducational techniques. Additionally, educational institutions should incorporate psychoeducation methodologies into midwifery and nursing curricula to prepare future practitioners with these evidence-based skills. However, several potential barriers should be considered when translating this intervention into routine clinical practice. These may include limited time availability in busy healthcare settings, the need for additional staff training in psychoeducational methods, and institutional or resource constraints that could affect program sustainability. Addressing these challenges through organizational support and policy-level planning will be essential to ensure the feasibility and long-term impact of such interventions.
