Biomechanics of the tooth cervical region investigated using 3D digital image correlation, micro CT, and finite element analysis
Authors: Maayan Elnatan, Rachel Shlafstein, Maxime Levy, Ariel Pokhojaev, Rachel Sarig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cervical region of the tooth is a mechanically vulnerable transition zone that transmits occlusal loads from the crown to the root and constitutes a common site of clinical conditions, including dentin hypersensitivity and non-carious cervical lesions (NCCLs). However, the spatial distribution of stresses and strains induced by physiological and off-axis occlusal loading in this region, and their relationship to surface and subsurface damage, remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we investigated cervical tooth biomechanics under controlled axial and oblique loading. Ten extracted mandibular premolars were loaded quasi-statically up to 200 N at 0\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} (axial) and 135\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} (oblique) relative to the tooth long axis. Cervical biomechanics were characterized using two synchronized three-dimensional digital image correlation (3D-DIC) systems to map principal strains on the buccal and lingual surfaces, and high-resolution micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scans acquired before and after loading to detect internal structural changes. A finite element analysis (FEA) model derived from the pre-loading micro-CT data replicated the experimental geometry and boundary conditions to quantify internal stress-strain fields. Strain-force ratios (strain per unit force) were higher under oblique than axial loading for dentin (buccal, p < 0.01; lingual, p < 0.001) and lingual enamel (p < 0.05); under oblique loading, compressive strain magnitudes exceeded tensile magnitudes in enamel (p < 0.05) and dentin (p < 0.001). Oblique loading generated localized cervical strain concentrations in dentin, whereas axial loading produced more spatially uniform strain fields. The FEA results are consistent with these experimental patterns and predicted subsurface strain concentration within coronal dentin. Post-loading micro-CT revealed vertical mesiodistal cracks confined to coronal dentin, suggesting that subsurface microdamage can occur in the absence of clinically apparent surface failure. These findings emphasize the clinical importance of limiting eccentric loading; clinical interventions that reduce such forces may help mitigate the progression of subsurface microdamage and should be considered within comprehensive preventive care.

Introduction

Non-carious cervical lesions (NCCLs) are a prevalent dental condition characterized by the loss of tooth structure at the cervical region through non-carious mechanisms1,2. NCCLs affect nearly half of the adults2, occur most frequently in premolars3–7, and pose clinical challenges, including structural degradation, dentin hypersensitivity3,6, aesthetic concerns, and potential pulp exposure8,9. The etiology of NCCLs is considered multifactorial10, with proposed contributing factors including toothbrush abrasion, acid-mediated erosion, and biomechanical stresses arising from occlusal loading1,9. Among these factors, the contribution of the biomechanical stresses, often described by the abfraction theory, remains controversial1,9,11. The abfraction theory proposes that non-axial occlusal forces generate stress concentration in the cervical region, promoting microfractures and subsequent tissue loss12,13, predominantly under tensile stresses14. Although parafunctional activities such as bruxism have been associated with NCCLs15, direct experimental evidence remains limited. Previous investigations comparing axial and non-axial loading using finite element analysis (FEA) and strain gauge methods generally reported higher cervical stresses and strains under oblique loading, providing partial support to an abfraction-related mechanism16–18. However, these approaches also have inherent limitations: FEA depends on assumed material properties and boundary conditions, while strain gauges provide localized, pointwise measurements with limited spatial resolution19. Beyond conventional finite element approaches, recent dental biomechanics studies have also used physical simulations utilizing 3D printing20, and meshless numerical frameworks to simulate tooth biomechanics, such as SPH-based models20,21. Advancements in experimental mechanics enable empirical, area-based assessment of tooth surface strain. Three-dimensional digital image correlation (3D-DIC) is a non-contact optical technique that enables measurement of surface displacement and surface strain across complex geometries19,22. In contrast to conventional strain gauges, which provide localized one-dimensional measurements, 3D-DIC enables non-contact full-field strain mapping of surface deformation across broader regions of interest19. Compared with FEA, 3D-DIC acquires deformation data directly from the physical specimen, thereby reducing dependence on the model-specific material inputs and loading constraints. Although 3D-DIC is widely used in biomechanics19,23,24 and dental materials research22,25,26, its application to the cervical region of whole human teeth27, remains limited. Here we investigate the biomechanics of the cervical tooth region under axial and oblique loading, assessing whether the resulting strain patterns align with those proposed in mechanistic explanations for NCCL development. To characterize both external and internal structural changes in human mandibular premolars, we combine 3D-DIC surface-strain mapping with FEA under matched boundary conditions to examine internal stress-strain fields, complemented by micro-CT assessment of structural changes.

Materials and methods

Study sample

This study was approved by the Institutional Helsinki Committee of Tel Aviv University (approval number 0004989-2). Ten human mandibular premolars extracted for orthodontic purposes at the Goldschleger School of Dental Medicine, Gray Faculty of Medical & Health Sciences, Tel Aviv University, were included in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to extraction and use of the teeth for research purposes. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. The specimens were obtained from donors aged 14–18 years. As the tooth collection was anonymized, donor sex was not available. Previous work has shown no significant sex-related differences in secondary dentin deposition or pulp/dentin ratio in premolars from similarly aged individuals28. Only permanent premolars with complete root development were included. Teeth with caries, fractures, root resorption, or incomplete root development were excluded from the study. To remove adherent organic debris and surface contaminants all teeth were cleaned in 10% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min. Following the cleaning all teeth were thoroughly rinsed, and stored in 0.1% thymol solution at room temperature (25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C) until testing.

Micro-CT scanning and analysis

High-resolution micro-CT was performed to assess structural integrity before and after the experiments. Initial scans were acquired using a microfocus X-ray CT system (XT H 225 ST, Nikon Metrology NV, Leuven, Belgium) at 180 kV and 61 µA, without filter, with an isotropic voxel size of 12 µm. Post-loading scans were restricted to the cervical region of the mounted specimens to avoid scattering artifacts generated by the stainless-steel loading cap and cylindrical mount (Supplementary Fig. S1) that could otherwise interfere with image analysis. Post-loading scans were acquired at 120 kV and 42 µA, without a filter, with an isotropic voxel size of 6 µm to optimize visualization of subtle internal changes, as the lower tube voltage enhanced contrast for small internal density discontinuities, while the lower tube current reduced signal intensity. Scan volumes were reconstructed using CT Pro 3D software (version 6.9.1; Nikon Metrology NV, Leuven, Belgium). Pre- and post-loading datasets were rigidly superimposed, registered, and examined side-by-side in Amira software (version 2019.1; www.fei.com)

Teeth mounting and preparation

Following a previously reported protocol29, each tooth was aligned with its long axis oriented vertically using a parallelometer and embedded in acrylic resin (GC Reline, GC America, Alsip, IL, USA) such that the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) remained 3 mm above the resin surface. A hemispherical stainless-steel cap was filled with a self-adhesive resin cement (RelyX U200, 3M ESPE, Neuss, Germany) and bonded to the coronal third of each tooth crown to facilitate standardization across varying occlusal morphologies20, and to reduce variability in how the applied load was transferred to the crown (Supplementary Fig. S1). Embedded specimens were primer-coated white (Matte spray 465-XXX, White; Tambour, Netanya, Israel) and speckled black using an airbrush (Infinity, Harder and Steenbeck, Norderstedt, Germany) to generate a random high-contrast pattern. The black speckles were produced using black pigment-based acrylic ink (FW Acrylic Ink, Black; Daler-Rowney, Bracknell, UK).

3D-DIC experimental design

Specimens were mounted in a custom jig (Fig. 1a) and subjected to compressive loading linearly increasing from 0 N to 200 N using a universal testing machine (Instron 68TM-30, Instron, Norwood, MA, USA) equipped with a 25 kN load cell. Buccal and lingual surfaces were imaged simultaneously using two 3D-DIC systems (Correlated Solutions, Irmo, SC, USA), each comprising two digital cameras (GS3-U3-51S5M-C, Teledyne FLIR, Wilsonville, OR, USA) equipped with 100 mm macro lenses (AT-X M100 PRO D Macro, Kenko Tokina, Tokyo, Japan), LED illumination, and polarizers. 3D-DIC data analysis

All surfaces were imported into Vic-3D software (version 7.2.6; Correlated Solutions, Irmo, SC, USA). Approximately rectangular regions of interest (ROI) that were in focus were identified on each surface (Supplementary Fig. S2). Subset (square tracking window) and step size (spacing between subset centers) within the ROIs were selected to achieve an average uncertainty (estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}0.01 pixels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}75% subset overlap). Before mechanical testing, speckle-pattern quality was verified in Vic-3D following the manufacturer’s recommendations by confirming that the average matching error remained below 0.01 pixels. Based on this quality check, specimen-specific subset and step sizes were selected, and together with the mm/pixel ratio these parameters are reported in Supplementary Table S1 to facilitate repeatability and comparison with other studies. Because cementum is thin, its contribution to the surface strains measured by the 3D-DIC was assumed to be negligible. Accordingly, strain measured apical to the CEJ on the speckle-coated surface was interpreted as predominantly reflecting the mechanical response of the underlying dentin. Therefore, two polygonal areas of interest (AOIs) were manually defined on each surface: an enamel AOI-E (coronal to the CEJ) and a dentin AOI-D (apical to the CEJ) (Supplementary Fig. S2). To assist in precise identification of the CEJ on all speckle-painted surfaces, corresponding micro-CT scans were visualized in 3D in Amira software (version 2019.1; www.fei.com). Depending on the specimen-specific morphology and local optical constraints, the AOIs were defined individually for each tooth within the in-focus ROI while excluding regions including areas affected by blur, glare, shadowing, steep curvature, or edge-related potentially erroneous strain concentrations. Strain fields within each AOI were processed using a 90% center-weighted Gaussian decay filter, with filter size adjusted to the mm/pixel ratio, and time filtering applied using a cubic spline. Within each AOI, maximum and minimum principal strains (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _2$$\end{document}) were computed over time and plotted against the applied load. The strain-force ratios (strain per unit force) were derived for each tissue surface as the slope of the fitted linear trendline. Finite element analysis

Three-dimensional tooth surfaces were reconstructed from the pre-loading micro-CT scans. Enamel, dentin, and pulp were segmented in Amira (version 2019.1; www.fei.com) and exported as triangulated surface meshes, which were subsequently remeshed in Blender software (version 4.3; www.blender.org) to ensure watertight interfaces. The enamel, dentin, and pulp meshes were imported into FreeCAD software (version 1.0.2; www.freecad.org). A stainless-steel hemispherical cap and an acrylic resin embedding material (GC Reline, GC America, Alsip, IL, USA) were modeled in FreeCAD software and combined with the tooth geometry using Boolean operations. The pulp mesh served solely to define the internal cavity and was not assigned material properties in the final model. The assembly was discretized with linear 4-node tetrahedral elements with target size of 0.12–0.48 mm. Materials were modeled as linear elastic and isotropic, using property values derived from literature (Supplementary Table S3). Boundary conditions reproduced the experimental constraints, and quasi-static loads were applied for axial (0\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} to the long axis) and oblique (135\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} to the long axis) setups using the CalculiX solver in FreeCAD software. Post processing of principal strain components was performed in Paraview software (version 6.0.0; www.paraview.org) to calculate the Lagrangian principal strains (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _2$$\end{document}). Von Mises stresses distributions were obtained directly from the CalculiX solver.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in SPSS (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with n = 10 paired observations per comparison. Normality of paired differences was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and was not violated for any comparison. Two-tailed, paired Student’s t-tests were used to compare strain-force ratios for: (1) buccal vs lingual surfaces under axial loading; (2) axial vs oblique loading for each surface; and (3) buccal vs lingual surfaces under oblique loading. Enamel and dentin were analysed separately. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, with statistical significance set at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} < 0.0531.

Discussion

This study investigated the biomechanics of the cervical region in mandibular premolars under axial and oblique loading. Integrating two synchronized stereo 3D-DIC systems with pre- and post-loading micro-CT imaging and a finite-element analysis model enabled detailed mapping of buccal and lingual surface-strain fields, examination of internal structural changes, and evaluation of internal stress-strain fields under matched boundary conditions. Together, these modalities provided direct surface-strain measurements and detection of internal structural changes. The distinctive structural properties of enamel and dentin define their strain patterns. Enamel is a highly mineralized prismatic tissue with anisotropic mechanical behavior, exhibiting increased stiffness along prism orientations that may influence how occlusal loads are dissipated32. Dentin, by contrast, is a collagen-reinforced tubular tissue that is less brittle than enamel33, with tubule-related anisotropy that enhances fracture resistance when loading is oriented parallel to the tubules34,35. Finite element models, including the one used in the present study commonly represent enamel and dentin as isotropic and homogeneous materials17,36–39. Because enamel and dentin were modeled as isotropic materials in the present study, the finite element results should be interpreted with caution regarding absolute local stress magnitudes, as prior studies have shown that enamel anisotropy can substantially alter stress dissipation in teeth32, and that isotropic dentin models may overestimate displacement by up to 37% and strain by up to 35% relative to anisotropic formulations40.

These simplifications, while useful for predicting overall patterns, do not fully capture the directional and spatially varying properties of these tissues32,40,41, underscoring the importance of complementary experimental data. Axial loading produced relatively uniform surface-strain fields and significantly lower strain-force ratios compared with oblique loading (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), aligning with prior reports demonstrating higher stresses and strains under non-axial loading17,18,30,42 (Table 1). Under axial loading, surface strain fields were broadly distributed (Fig. 3a-b), in agreement with the findings of Machado et al.18. In contrast, oblique loading induced bending-type response characterized by tensile strains on the buccal surface and compressive strains on the lingual surface, with pronounced strain localization within cervical dentin near the embedding level (Fig. 3c-d). The FEA model reproduced these overall patterns: axial loading resulted in low-gradient, near-symmetric strain and von Mises stress fields, whereas oblique loading induced buccal tension and lingual compression with localized strain concentrations in cervical dentin (Fig. 6). Under oblique loading, compressive strain-force ratios significantly exceeded tensile ratios in both enamel and dentin (Fig. 2). Zaytsev et al.43 reported tension-compression-dependent behavior in dentin, suggesting that crack growth predominates when tensile stress is dominant, whereas deformation predominates when compressive stress is dominant. Prior studies have also reported higher compressive than tensile strength in enamel44,45 and dentin46. In this context, the present findings suggest that compressive loading may be accommodated through greater deformation, potentially facilitating energy dissipation and thereby limiting stress concentration.

Surface strain magnitudes in the present study were broadly comparable with those reported in previous strain-gauge investigations (Table 1), with the observed variability attributable to differences in loading configuration, measurement location, and representation of the periodontal ligament (PDL). The absence of a simulated PDL should be recognized as a limitation, as it may affect absolute strain magnitudes and load dissipation47. It was not reproduced in the current 3D-DIC experiment because precise physical simulation of a very thin and non-uniform PDL across specimens may affect repeatability, and the finite element model was therefore constructed to match the mounted experimental configuration derived from the micro-CT scan. Under axial loading, buccal dentin strains exceeded values reported for single-rooted specimens with PDL simulation16, yet were comparable to those obtained without PDL representation30. Although axial buccal cervical dentin strains were similar to those reported by Du et al.30, palatal cervical dentin strains differed. This discrepancy most likely reflects differences in loading configuration: Du et al.30 applied point loads at discrete occlusal sites, whereas the hemispherical cap used here distributed load across the occlusal table, producing a more buccolingually symmetric axial strain field. Under oblique loading, cervical dentin strain magnitudes fell within the range reported by Soares et al.16 on the buccal surface and were comparable to those reported by Du et al.30 on both buccal and palatal surfaces. Differences in enamel strain magnitudes relative to Palamara et al.48 and Du et al.30 are plausibly attributable to differences in measurement location and spatial sampling. In those studies, enamel strain was recorded at localized, CEJ-adjacent strain-gauge sites, where strain typically increases as measurements approach the CEJ. By contrast, the present study reports area-based strains averaged over broader enamel regions, which would be expected to yield lower magnitudes. Consistent with this, we observed a cervically increasing enamel strain gradient, with higher stresses adjacent to the CEJ (Fig. 6h). Palamara et al.48 similarly reported relatively low strains over the crown convexity that increased toward the CEJ, in agreement with the spatial gradients observed within the enamel.

Consistent with the experimentally measured oblique-loading strain patterns, the finite element model exhibited a flexural response, with a neutral axis traversing coronal dentin, separating buccal tension from lingual compression (Fig. 5b,d, Fig. 6b,d). In the same region, post-loading micro-CT revealed vertically oriented mesiodistal cracks confined to coronal dentin, recurring in 8 of 10 specimens, without detectable cervical surface tissue loss (Fig. 5a-b), suggesting that off-axis flexural loading may contribute to the observed crack formation. These observations align with prior analyses indicating that subsurface stresses in cervical dentin may exceed surface stresses under off-axis loading38, and with the working-side laterotrusive-contact model of Magne and Belser, which demonstrated an internal tension-compression transition with localized stress elevations near the buccal pulp-horn region49. In the present study, scattering artifacts associated with the stainless-steel loading cap limited reliable morphometric measurement of post-loading cracks, therefore, the micro-CT analysis was restricted to descriptive assessment of crack presence, location, and orientation. Quantitative crack morphometry and condition-specific comparison should be addressed in future studies using imaging protocols specifically designed for dedicated crack measurement, which was beyond the scope of the current study.

Taken together, these findings link loading direction with cervical surface strain patterns and subsurface structural changes. Interpretation should consider limitations, including quasi-static loading, idealized boundary conditions, and the absence of PDL simulation, each of which may influence strain magnitudes and load dissipation. Although cervical stress and strain concentrations are often discussed in the context of the abfraction hypothesis, the present study does not establish a causal relationship between off-axis occlusal loading and cervical tissue loss. Rather, off-axis loading was shown to cause localized subsurface mechanical responses in dentin without overt surface breakdown. Future studies should evaluate cervical tissue responses under repetitive off-axis loading and examine interactions with additional oral factors such as erosion and abrasion.

Conclusions

The present study characterized cervical biomechanics in human premolars under controlled axial and off-axis (eccentric) loading. By integrating 3D-DIC with pre- and post-loading micro-CT and specimen-informed FEA, we linked surface principal strain distributions to subsurface stress-strain fields and to post-loading internal structural changes. Relative to axial loading, off-axis loading produced higher strain-force ratios and cervical strain localization. Post-loading micro-CT of 8 out of 10 teeth further revealed mesiodistal cracks confined to coronal dentin despite an intact cervical surface, indicating that eccentric loading may induce subsurface microdamage without clinically apparent failure. These findings suggest clinical relevance to minimizing eccentric forces. Reducing off-axis loading through occlusal management or orthodontic intervention may help limit progression of underlying microdamage. Further studies are required to establish the clinical detectability, prevalence, and therapeutic relevance of these cracks before specific intervention strategies can be recommended.
