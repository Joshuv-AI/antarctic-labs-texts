From “when to stop” to “why continue today?”: operationalizing RRT weaning with UUEI
Authors: Arthur Orieux, Simon Bodot, Chloé Gisbert-Mora, Alexandre Boyer
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We thank Chaïbi and Gaudry for their thoughtful “Matters arising” on our multicentre study (D-STOP) evaluating a simple bedside trigger, the Urinary Urea Excretion Index (UUEI), to guide renal replacement therapy (RRT) discontinuation in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) [1]. We welcome the opportunity to clarify our aims, address methodological and physiological points, and outline where the field should go next.

What D-STOP set out to do, and what it showed

D-STOP was a pragmatic implementation study, not a definitive outcomes trial [2]. We prospectively operationalized a weaning trigger (UUEI ≥ 1.35 mmol/kg/24 h) across four ICUs and asked whether protocolizing discontinuation would reduce catheter exposure without harm. The primary endpoint, RRT catheter-free days by day 28, captured a meaningful care process tied to device-related risks and to the common problem of “default continuation” when explicit stopping rules are absent.

Compared with usual practice, UUEI-guided care increased catheter-free days and advanced the cumulative incidence of first withdrawal. Importantly, catheters were removed earlier, often before “significant diuresis” (> 0.5mL/kg/h) returned, yet short-term failures were rare (7-day failure: 3.8% vs. 1.7%, p = 0.29; reinsertion by day 28: 7 vs. 6 patients).

Why a process endpoint?

Chaïbi et al. argue that catheter-free days are clinician-dependent and not patient-centred. In an implementation study testing a behavioral change (daily standardized trigger), catheter exposure remains a relevant first target. However, we agree that a randomized trial focused on kidney- and patient-centered outcomes is required.

UUEI: more than urine output

UUEI is not a redundant restatement of urine output but a physiology-based composite integrating urine volume and tubular urea handling, standardized to body weight. Urea, the main urinary solute, depends on vasopressin-regulated tubular reabsorption and medullary recycling, thus reflecting tubular-medullary integrity and recovery signals that urine volume alone cannot capture [3]. In D-STOP, UUEI yielded an actionable signal earlier than diuresis: catheter removal before significant diuresis (>0.5 mL/kg/h) was more frequent with UUEI guidance (14.9% vs. 5.8%) without excess failures.

To further address Chaïbi et al., we analysed post-discontinuation trajectories of serum creatinine (SCr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) during the UUEI-guided phase. After excluding patients who died, required RRT reinitiation, or had missing data, 77 remained for analysis. Transient rises in both markers were frequent within the 3 days following catheter removal. Nearly half (48%) showed a transient rise in SCr, and BUN increases were more frequent (71%) within 24 h of catheter removal. As illustrated in Fig. 1, BUN generally rises more than SCr, suggesting slower normalization of nitrogen clearance compared with SCr kinetics. About one-quarter (24.7%) of patients reached BUN levels above 40 mmol/L (112 mg/dL), a threshold classically proposed for RRT initiation in the AKIKI trials, yet none required reinitiation of RRT. These findings indicate that UUEI-based guidance may enable earlier, yet safe, discontinuation, thereby avoiding unnecessary RRT sessions in a subset of patients.

Preliminary data from the PROSECO (n = 243) and DISCORT (n = 116) cohorts confirm a gap between declared and actual practice: while clinicians reported diuresis thresholds ≈ 600 mL/day, CRRT was often discontinued at diuresis < 300 mL/day [4]. This discrepancy illustrates that rigid diuresis cut-offs are rarely applied in practice and supports the use of physiology-based composites such as UUEI.

Finally, UUEI is practical and low-friction: automatically calculated from charted weight and urine output, easily displayed for morning rounds, it enables collegial decisions. It also remains one of the most accurate predictors of successful RRT discontinuation both in the original derivation cohort (AUROC ≈ 0.96) [5] and in our study (96% specificity). In contrast, urine output alone shows modest accuracy and poor reliability under diuretics [6].

Design transparency

A before-after design is inherently vulnerable to secular trends (e.g., later RRT initiation) and centre-specific effects. We reported such heterogeneity and adjusted for centre and illness severity. We fully agree, however, that only a randomized or stepped-wedge trial can provide definitive, patient-centred evidence.

Operationalizing the “continue today?” approach

Recent commentaries promote a restrictive, needs-based strategy, suspending RRT early unless new indications recur, to limit exposure and RRT-related injury [7]. Chaïbi et al. advocate a paradigm shift away from the default attitude of waiting for a recovery signal before discontinuing RRT. They emphasize that premature discontinuation rarely causes significant harm. We respectfully disagree that early cessation is uniformly benign. Its balance with fluid overload depends on the phase of circulatory failure (ROSE conceptual model). During rescue or optimization, restoring perfusion remains the priority; in the stabilization phase, especially in oligo-anuric patients, premature cessation can lead to venous congestion, both of which are associated with worse outcomes. Restarting RRT often requires aggressive ultrafiltration, which can increase hemodynamic instability, thereby exposing the kidney to renewed stress during a vulnerable recovery period and a potential “restart penalty”. This concern is echoed by national practice data from the PROSECO survey, which found that nearly half of intensivists (48%) reported continuing CRRT primarily for fluid management, not simply to replace solute clearance [4].

The Bayesian analysis cited by Chaïbi et al. against aggressive ultrafiltration involved only 61 highly selected EUPHAS-2 patients, with non-standardized management and strong confounding by indication; its outcome was mortality, not renal recovery. Rather than challenging fluid removal itself, it highlights the risk of unguided ultrafiltration during early shock. In contrast, controlled, hemodynamically guided strategies are safe after resuscitation and even during ongoing circulatory failure, as shown in REVERSE-AKI (a restrictive strategy halved RRT use and adverse events) [8] and GO-NEUTRAL (hemodynamically guided neutralization seems to improve outcomes without renal harm) [9]. Thus, the issue is not whether to remove fluid, but when and how; the risks of “too early” discontinuation are phase- and context-dependent and must be balanced against the risks of unnecessary continuation.

UUEI offers a physiology-based, reproducible aid to the daily question “should we continue today?” It is not prescriptive: clinicians were encouraged, not compelled, to stop when solute excretion appeared adequate, taking into account hemodynamics, acid–base status, and fluid balance. In D-STOP, this structure reduced default continuation without safety concerns.

Future research

Future research should test protocolized weaning strategies embedding dynamic markers, as in the ongoing NCT06214390 trial. Protocols should combine urine-output trends, daily urinary urea and creatinine excretion, and simple bedside algorithms with validated thresholds. We agree with Chaïbi et al. that discontinuation, particularly of continuous RRT (as intermittent RRT is, by definition, regularly interrupted), should rely less on recovery criteria and more on the disappearance of initiation triggers, thereby allowing earlier weaning. This rationale supports our national proposal, WESTOP (PHRC-24–0216, resubmitted 2025). Future trials should also predefine restart safety metrics (e.g., 24–96 h vulnerability window, hemodynamic tolerance) and patient-centred endpoints (dialysis-free days, sustained recovery or chronic kidney disease, ICU/hospital stay), in line with the recent SRLF-GFRUP consensus on standardized yet individualized weaning [10].

Conclusion

D-STOP is only a first step. It shows that a simple, low-cost bedside index integrated into routine practice can reduce catheter exposure, accelerate liberation, and maintain safety, conditions justifying a definitive trial focused on renal recovery and patient-centred outcomes. Pending such evidence, UUEI structures the daily question “should we continue today?” without rigidity, aligns with de-resuscitation principles, and helps move practice away from continuation by default.
