The activity of a new bicyclic peptide, PLP-3, against colistin-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms
Authors: Núria Martín-Vilardell, Doaa Higazy, Clara Ballesté-Delpierre, Jordi Vila, Niels Høiby, Oana Ciofu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
New antimicrobials are urgently required to treat multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections, a hallmark of chronic respiratory diseases such as cystic fibrosis (CF), in which biofilm-associated growth contributes to antibiotic tolerance and persistence. In this study, the activity of PLP-3, an in silico designed peptidomimetic protegrin-1, was evaluated against planktonic and biofilm-associated P. aeruginosa clinical isolates from CF patients, including both colistin-susceptible (ColS) and colistin-resistant (ColR) strains. Antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were assessed by determining the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs of 2 mg/L and ≥4 mg/L for ColS and ColR, respectively), as well as minimum biofilm inhibitory and eradication concentrations (MBIC and MBEC of ≥32 mg/L). Under dynamic flow-cell biofilm conditions, PLP-3 exhibited rapid and uniform killing across the biofilm structure, reducing surviving biovolume to 39.6% in P. aeruginosa ColS PAO1 and 52.8% in P. aeruginosa ColR strain 41782/98 after 24 h. In contrast, colistin effectiveness was significantly compromised in resistant biofilms (89.8% surviving biovolume, p < 0.001 vs. PLP-3). Based on these in vitro results, PLP-3 may represent a promising candidate for further investigation as a potential treatment for recalcitrant colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa infections.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the leading cause of chronic pulmonary infections and mortality in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients [1,2]. CF is a lethal autosomal recessive disease caused by CFTR gene mutations [[3], [4], [5]], that leads to thick mucus accumulation, chronic inflammation, and respiratory failure [4]. This thick mucus provides an ideal niche for bacterial colonization, acting as a physical barrier that compromises antibiotic diffusion. While CF pathology is polymicrobial, P. aeruginosa remains the most prevalent pathogen in this context [6].

Treating P. aeruginosa infections has become a challenge, leading the World Health Organization to classify it as a high-priority pathogen [7]. In CF frequent antibiotic use, poor lung penetration and dose-limiting toxicity accelerates the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains [8]. P. aeruginosa withstands antimicrobials via intrinsic, acquired, and adaptive mechanisms, notably biofilm formation [4]. Crucially, while antibiotic resistance is driven by genetic alterations that increase the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC), biofilm-mediated tolerance allows bacteria to survive transient antibiotic exposure without increasing MIC levels due to their protected microenvironment and metabolically heterogeneity [9].

P. aeruginosa biofilms in CF lungs are aggregates of bacteria encased in an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids [10]. This matrix shelters bacteria by acting as a physical and electrostatic barrier against antibiotic treatments and immune response [11]. For instance, negatively charged alginate and eDNA impair the diffusion of cationic antibiotics like aminoglycosides [12,13]. Biofilm thickness also generates metabolic heterogeneity: peripheral cells consume nutrients and oxygen, leaving the innermost core in anoxia and starvation [8,14]. These deep-seated dormant cells downregulate active biological targets, becoming tolerant persister cells that survive quinolone and beta-lactam treatments [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. This complicates the eradication of the infection and significantly increases the probability of relapse. This multifactorial phenomenon, integrating impaired antibiotic diffusion, genetic resistance, metabolic dormancy, and matrix maturation drive “Biofilm recalcitrance”, leading to persistent infections and frequent relapses [9,14,19].

In the clinical management of chronic P. aeruginosa infections in CF, colistin (polymyxin E) remains a last-resort antibiotic against MDR isolates [20]. However, its efficacy is increasingly compromised by the emergence of colistin-resistant (ColR) strains [[21], [22], [23]], This resistance mainly involves a reduction in the net negative surface charge via pmrAB and phoPQ mutations altering lipid A within lipopolysaccharide (LPS), alongside OprH overexpression that binds LPS divalent cation-binding sites [20,[24], [25], [26]].

Although inhaled colistimethate sodium minimizes systemic nephrotoxicity [27], lung delivery remains heterogeneous due to viscous mucus. At the microscopic level, colistin effectively targets the dormant inner core, but fails to eradicate the metabolically active periphery [15], where cells sense antimicrobial stress and induce resistance genes [9,28]. This highlights the urgent need for alternative antimicrobials capable of bypassing these barriers and eradicating recalcitrant ColR biofilms.

To address these challenges, PLP-3 was rationally designed as a protegrin-1 (PG-1)-like peptidomimetic, incorporating the key structural and physicochemical features underlying the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of protegrins while aiming to improve their toxicity profile. PG-1 is a cationic peptide that adopts an antiparallel β-hairpin stabilized by disulphide bonds, with an amphipathic arrangement of positively charged and hydrophobic residues that promotes interaction with and disruption of bacterial membranes. To retain these features while increasing structural rigidity, PLP-3 was designed as a constrained bicyclic peptide in which the flexible terminal regions of PG-1 were eliminated and the β-strands were connected in a head-to-tail fashion. D-Pro residues were introduced to promote β-hairpin formation, and a central disulphide bond was incorporated to further stabilize the structure. Using Rosetta-based computational design, amphipathic side chains and optimal disulphide-bonding sites were selected to generate a PG-1-like conformation. PLP-3 emerged as the lead candidate, adopting a slightly twisted β-hairpin closely resembling the bioactive conformation of PG-1 (backbone RMSD = 0.8 Å), while displaying increased amphipathic character. Experimental characterization confirmed its antiparallel β-sheet conformation, and PLP-3 showed potent activity against MDR and colistin-resistant Gram-negative pathogens with low haemolytic and cytotoxic effects [29]. Hence, here we evaluated the potential of PLP-3 against P. aeruginosa strains, including strains isolated from CF patients, to determine its efficacy as an alternative treatment for overcoming colistin resistance in recalcitrant biofilms.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

Twelve strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa were selected based on their susceptibility profile to colistin, comprising six colistin-susceptible and six colistin-resistant isolates.

The panel included two laboratory reference strains: P. aeruginosa PAO1 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (from the American Type Culture Collection), and ten clinical P. aeruginosa strains from Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, Spain (36a, 38a, S5, R2, and R3), and Rigshospitalet, Denmark (19848/85, 41782/98, 52242/04, 735A/01, and 40049/96), where the latter five were isolated from cystic fibrosis (CF) patients. The clinical conditions or specimen sources associated with the five isolates from Hospital Clínic de Barcelona were not available.

For flow-cell studies, two GFP-tagged strains were used: the colistin-resistant isolate P. aeruginosa 41782/98 [28] and the colistin-susceptible laboratory strain P. aeruginosa PAO1, both from Costerton Biofilm Centre. The colistin-resistant phenotype of the CF isolate 41782/98 has been genetically characterized, owing to a specific deletion (1089_1094delCCTGGG) in the PhoPQ two-component system and a point mutation (61C > T) in OprH [28].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antimicrobial activity was assessed by determining the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration capable of inhibiting the visible bacterial growth after 18-22 h of incubation at 37°C. MIC values were determined using the broth microdilution technique, following the guidelines of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), using Nunc™ MicroWell™ 96 well polypropylene plates (Cat. No. 267334, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The tested antimicrobial agents were PLP-3 (GenicBio Limited, Shanghai, China) and colistin (Cat. No. C4461, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) as an antibiotic comparator, starting at an initial concentration of 64 mg/L. The peptidomimetic PLP-3 was custom-synthesized following the already described synthesis method [29]. The chromatographic profile of the peptide was determined by Reverse-Phase High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (RP-HPLC) (Supplementary Fig. S1), and PLP-3 identity was verified by Mass Spectrometry (MS) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The final purity of synthetic PLP-3 was evaluated by integrating both the chromatographic purity (96.53%) and the net peptide content (73.52% w/w), yielding a net active peptide purity of 70.97%. For PLP-3 MICs, Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB) (Cat. No. 1214, Condalab, Madrid, Spain) was used, while for colistin MICs, Mueller Hinton Broth 2 (MHB-II) (Cat. No. 90922, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) was used. Three biological replicates were performed for each strain. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and PAO1 strains were used as quality controls.

Biofilm quantification

Static biofilm production was quantified using the Calgary Biofilm Device (CBD) method [30]. Briefly, overnight cultures of each strain were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1. These cultures were then diluted 1:1000 with LB broth, and 120 μL of this final dilution was dispensed into the wells of Nunc™ Edge™-96 well polystyrene plates (Cat. No. 167008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Afterwards, PEGs (PolyEthylene Glycol pins) from Nunc™ Immuno TSP lids (Cat. No. 445497, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) were immersed in the wells, and plates were incubated at 37°C for 24h or 72h in static conditions. For the 72h incubation, LB broth was refreshed every 24h.

After incubation, PEGs were gently rinsed three times in 0.9% NaCl solution and immersed in 0.1% a Crystal Violet (CV) solution for 20 min (Cat. No. V5265, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Then, PEGs were washed three times with MilliQ water and immersed into 96% ethanol for 15 min to solubilize CV. Biofilm quantification was performed by measuring the absorbance at OD590 using a 2030 Multilabel reader, VICTOR ™ X4 PerkinElmer spectrophotometer. Biofilm production was classified according to the established reference criteria by Stepanović [31]. The cut-off optical density (ODc) was defined as three standard deviations above the mean OD of the negative control (ODc = mean ODcontrol + 3xSD). Strains were categorized based on their mean OD values (OD) as follows: non-biofilm producers (OD ≤ ODc), weak producers (ODc < OD ≤ 2xODc), moderate producers (2xODc < OD ≤ 4xODc), and strong producers (OD > 4ODc).

To ensure experimental consistency, the initial starting biomass for biofilm studies was standardized by adjusting the incubation periods (ranging from 24h to 72h) until moderate-mature biofilms were achieved.

Antibiofilm activity determination in static biofilms

The Minimum Biofilm Inhibitory Concentration (MBIC) and Minimum Biofilm Eradicating Concentration (MBEC) values were determined in static conditions using the Calgary Biofilm Device (CBD).

Once biofilms were formed on the PEGs (24h or 72h incubation period), pins were gently washed three times with 0.9% NaCl solution. Subsequently, PEG lids were transferred into Nunc™ MicroWell™ 96-well polypropylene plates (Cat. No. 267334, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) containing a total volume of 200 μL per well of different PLP-3 or colistin concentrations in MHB, starting at 128 mg/L. PEG lids exposed to the antimicrobials were incubated for 24 h at 37°C.

After 24-h exposure, PEGs were rinsed twice with 0.9% NaCl solution, air-dried, and subsequently transferred into a new microtiter recovery plate with fresh LB broth. PEGs were sonicated for 5 min to detach the remaining viable cells. Then, PEG lids were replaced with standard lids, and plates were incubated at 37°C. OD600 of the recovery plates was measured after 8h incubation to determine MBIC and after 24h incubation to determine MBEC. MBIC was defined as the drug concentration resulting in an OD600 difference ≤ 10% relative to the mean of positive control after 8h incubation. Conversely, MBEC was defined as the drug concentration resulting in an OD600 increase of less than 0.05 units following a 24h recovery period [32]. Experiments were performed in triplicate, including both positive and negative controls.

Dynamic flow-cell biofilm experiments and confocal laser scanning microscopy

Flow-cell system setup and biofilm growth. A flow-cell biofilm system was utilized to evaluate biofilm architecture and PLP-3 activity under continuous culture medium flow, creating a dynamic, nutrient-limited hydrodynamic environment. 3D printed transparent flow-cell chambers consisted of three parallel and independent channels (each 1 x 4 × 40 mm), as previously described [33]. Channels were covered with a glass coverslip, whose surface was used for cell attachment and biofilm formation. Each channel was individually connected by silicone tubing to a reservoir of AB minimal medium (ABMM). The medium was prepared as previously established in a prior study [34], supplemented with 10% (v/v) A-10 phosphate buffer and 0.3 mM glucose. ABMM was continuously supplied through the silicone tubing and flowed through the chambers; the effluent was then collected in a waste container (Fig. 1). Both the flow-cell system design and the detailed experimental protocol were implemented according to the methodology previously established by the authors [33]. In order to set the system, all silicone tube connections and channels were sterilized with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution and rinsed with Mili-Q water. The flow was generated by Watson Marlow 250S peristaltic pump operated at 3 rpm (delivering a continuous flow rate of ∼0.05-0.1 mL/min per channel). Subsequently, ABMM was pumped with a continuous flow for 1 h to stabilize the system at room temperature. Bacterial inoculation and initial growth. From an overnight culture, a bacterial suspension was prepared and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.01. A volume of 200 μL of this suspension was injected into each flow-cell chamber using a sterile syringe. To facilitate bacterial adhesion to the coverslip surface, flow-cells were positioned upside down without the flow for 1 h. Afterwards, a flow of 1.75 rpm was established for 72 h.

Antimicrobial treatment. After 72 h of incubation, each channel within the chamber was exposed to PI and to a different condition for 24 h: Positive control (only ABMM), PLP-3 treatment, and colistin treatment. To ensure the direct comparison between the agents based on the number of molecules interacting with the biofilm, PLP-3 and colistin were tested at equimolar concentrations. Given that the molecular weight of PLP-3 (2267.83 g/mol) is approximately double that of colistin (1155.45 g/mol), the mass concentrations (mg/L) were adjusted accordingly to maintain comparable molar ratios throughout the assays. This approach normalized the dose to the number of antimicrobial particles, rather than mass. Thus, PLP-3 concentration was set at 50 mg/L, while colistin was at 25 mg/L. Dead cells were stained by adding 0.3 μM propidium iodide (PI) to all channels.

Biofilm Imaging and Analysis. Biofilm images were taken at different timepoints using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM). Flow-cells were directly positioned on the microscope stage for live imaging. A Zeiss LSM 880 was used for imaging P. aeruginosa PAO1, while a Zeiss LSM 980 was used for P. aeruginosa 41782/98. Biofilm imaging was performed using a Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.40 oil DIC objective. To visualize the biomass, GFP was excited at 488 nm (emission range 493–550 nm), while Propidium Iodide (PI) was excited at 561 nm (emission range 570–650 nm). To ensure consistency, all images were captured 5 mm downstream from the flow-cell inlet. Vertical Z-stacks were acquired at 1 μm intervals, with the total number of optical sections adjusted for each field of view to encompass the full height of the biofilm. A minimum of three representative Z-stacks were collected per channel at each time point. Post-acquisition analysis—including background subtraction, and smoothing was performed using Imaris 10 software (Bitplane AG, Zurich, Switzerland), which was also utilized to extract quantitative volumetric statistics. The ratio of live to dead to cells was determined by quantifying the respective biomass volumes of GFP (green) and PI (red) signals using Imaris 10 software (Bitplane AG, Zurich, Switzerland).

Erythrocyte haemolysis assay

The haemolytic profile of PLP-3 was evaluated using commercial human whole blood (Cat. No. CTIPB.1.21, Tebubio, Le Perray-en-Yvelines, France). Erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation at 1500 x g for 5 min at 40C and washed 5 times with PBS 1x. The erythrocyte pellet was subsequently resuspended in PBS 1x to yield working suspensions at final concentrations of 1%, 2.5% and 5% (v/v).

Assays were performed in Nunc™ MicroWell™ 96-well polypropylene plates. Two-fold serial dilutions of PLP-3 in PBS 1x were prepared starting at 64 mg/L, and 50 μL of each peptide concentration was mixed with 50 μL of the erythrocyte suspension. Triton X-100 (10% v/v in PBS 1x) and vehicle (PBS 1x alone) were included as positive (100% haemolysis) and negative (0% haemolysis) controls, respectively. Microplates were shaken for 20 s, incubated at 37 0C for 1h, and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min at room temperature. From each well, 50 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well polystyrene flat-bottom microtiter plate (Cat. No. 267544, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and haemoglobin release was quantified by measuring absorbance at 415 nm using an EPOCH 2 microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). The percentage of haemolysis was calculated relative to the positive control and subtracting the negative control absorbance. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effect of PLP-3 was evaluated on the human alveolar basal epithelial cell line A549 (ATCC CCL-185, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cat. No. 41966029, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat. No. F7524, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 10 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 mg/L streptomycin, and 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids. Cultures were maintained at 370C with 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity was determined using the XTT cell viability assay (Cat. No. CA031, Canvax Biotech, Córdoba, Spain). Briefly, A549 cells were harvested at 80% confluence using trypsin-EDTA and seeded into flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates at a density of 105 cells/well (50μL/well). Two-fold serial dilutions of PLP-3 were prepared in supplemented DMEM starting at 256 mg/L and added to the wells (50μL/well), yielding a final reaction volume of 100μL/well. Untreated cells in culture medium served as the positive viability control (100% viability), whereas cells treated with 50% (v/v) DMSO were used as the cell death control (0% viability). Plates were incubated for 20h (matching the incubation period used in the MIC determination) at 370C with 5% CO2.

Following incubation, 50 μL of reconstituted XTT reagent mixture was added to each well and incubated at 370C for 2h. Absorbance was measured at 480 nm and 650 nm using an EPOCH 2 microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Specific absorbance (A) was calculated as follows: A = A480, sample – A480, negative control – A650, sample. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to the untreated control. Experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate PLP-3 as an effective alternative treatment against colistin-resistant strains, MIC, MBIC, and MBEC values for PLP-3 and colistin were compared using Mantel-Cox Log-Rank test (Prism 8, GraphPad Software). This statistical test was chosen to account for censored values (e.g., >128 mg/L). Pairwise comparisons were performed to assess significant differences between ColS and ColR groups under the same treatment (PLP-3 ColS vs. PLP-3 ColR, and colistin ColS vs. colistin ColR), as well as to compare PLP-3 versus colistin treatments within each colistin resistance phenotype (PLP-3 vs. colistin within ColS, and PLP-3 vs. colistin within ColR).

As for flow-cell experiments, the surviving biovolume (calculated as the ratio of GFP/Total, where Total = GFP + PI) was analysed by performing a two-way ANOVA using Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA), by comparing the effects of the three different treatments (positive control, PLP-3 and colistin) and timepoints. The statistical significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05. Results are represented in the bar plots and p-values as: p > 0.05 (ns), p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**), p ≤ 0.001 (***).

When comparing the same treatment (PLP-3 or colistin) between ColS and ColR groups (Table 1), it can be seen that colistin MIC, MBIC, and MBEC values are significantly different against ColS and ColR strains. However, when comparing PLP-3 MIC, MBIC and MBEC, only MIC values are significantly different between ColS and ColR groups, but not for MBIC and MBEC, with p-values of 0.82 and 0.88, respectively.

Moreover, a statistical analysis was performed to determine if the observed differences between the two treatments reached significance for each resistance phenotype. As summarized in Table 2, PLP-3 treatment resulted in significantly lower MIC and MBEC values within the ColR group.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the rationally designed peptide PLP-3 exhibits potent antimicrobial activity against planktonic and mature biofilms of both colistin-susceptible (ColS) and colistin-resistant (ColR) P. aeruginosa clinical strains.

Using colistin as a reference comparator, PLP-3 activity was slightly influenced by the ColR phenotype, with MIC50/90 values shifting from 2 mg/L in ColS to 8/16 mg/L in ColR isolates. This trend aligns with previous reports for wild-type protegrin-1 (PG-1) against A. baumannii (2-4 mg/L in ColS vs. 8 mg/L in ColR) [36]. Furthermore, PLP-3 demonstrated superior potency compared to wt PG-1 activity against P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (MIC = 12.5 mg/L) [37]. This enhanced activity stems PLP-3 in-silico, where the enrichment of the amphiphilic character adding arginine and hydrophobic residues, combined beta-hairpin stabilization via optimized disulphide bonds and flexible tail removal, increased structural rigidity. These rational modifications not only enhance antimicrobial potency but also contribute to a lower toxicity profile compared to wt PG-1 [29].

Regarding antibiofilm activity, a key advantage of PLP-3 over colistin is the equivalence between its MBIC and MBEC values. This MBIC/MBEC equivalence suggests that PLP-3 efficacy is not compromised by biofilm maturation, ensuring that growth inhibition is accompanied by a total loss of viability. Furthermore, PLP-3 exhibited uniform MBIC90 and MBEC90 values (64 mg/L) for both ColS and ColR isolates. In contrast, colistin concentrations showed at least 4-fold increase in isolates (rising from 16 to >128 mg/L for MBIC90, and 32 to >128 mg/L for MBEC90). These results confirm that colistin-resistance mechanisms do not confer cross-resistance to PLP-3. Thus, while colistin remains more potent against ColS isolates, PLP-3 outperforms in ColR strains, positioning PLP-3 as a promising alternative for treating MDR P. aeruginosa infections.

PLP-3's lethal activity against ColR isolates suggests a distinct mechanism driven by dual electrostatic and hydrophobic properties. It is hypothesized that additional arginine residues facilitate membrane phosphate targeting through bidentate hydrogen bonding [38]. This electrostatic interaction, combined with strong hydrophobic insertion, may induce lipid reorganization (flip-flop mechanisms) and toroidal pore formation by monomeric, dimeric, or octameric assemblies. These pores would dissipate transmembrane potential by uncontrolled flux of water and ions (Na+, K+, Cl−), causing rapid cell death [39,40]. In ColR strains with reduced net negative charge, the membrane remains sufficiently anionic for initial binding. At this stage, PLP-3 rigidity and hydrophobicity enable it to reach a critical threshold concentration (possibly following the carpet model), exerting enough mechanical tension to collapse the bilayer and promote pore formation [37], regardless of the reduced electrostatic pull [38].

Dynamic flow-cell experiments revealed distinct killing kinetics for both PLP-3 and colistin. In P. aeruginosa ColS PAO1, PLP-3 induced rapid killing, reaching 46.4% surviving biovolume after only 1h, primarily targeting outer layers, cationic binding to the anionic EPS matrix [4,10,11,41]. The reduction remained stable over 24h (39.6% viable biovolume) without growth rebound, consistent with its MBIC/MBEC equivalence. Conversely, colistin exhibited slower initial killing (79.6% at 1h), reaching maximum reduction at 3h (15.3%), but showed a significant growth rebound after 24h (46.9%), localized in the inner core. This regrowth is likely driven by high metabolic tolerance in the hypoxic core, and electrostatic sequestration by matrix alginate and eDNA [42], correlating with the increased values of MBEC (24h) compared to MBIC. The superior 24h reduction of PLP-3 suggests that PLP-3's balanced amphipathic profile and structural rigidity enhance deep layer penetration, rendering its activity less dependent on the metabolic state and electrostatic environment.

For the P. aeruginosa ColR 41782/98 clinical strain differences between treatments were more pronounced. Colistin showed no significant biovolume reduction compared to the control, demonstrating minimal core activity and confirming its inability to overcome lipid A modifications [42]. In contrast, PLP-3 reduced surviving biovolume to 52.8% at 24h. Interestingly, PLP-3 survival in ColR strain was slightly higher in the outer layers than in the core. This spatial distribution can be explained by the localized PhoPQ-regulated oprH overexpression in metabolically active outer layers, which the uptake of polycationic molecules by replacing divalent cations [43]. This diminished outer electrostatic sequestration may paradoxically facilitate deeper PLP-3 penetration into the innermost layers, which remain more negatively charged and susceptible.

Note that PLP-3 MBIC and MBEC values (64 mg/L), as well as the concentration applied in the dynamic flow-cell assays (50 mg/L), fall within an acceptable biological window. Comprehensive biocompatibility evaluations showed even at the highest anti-biofilm concentration tested (64 mg/L), haemolysis was maintained below 25% (24.8%) under physiological conditions (5% human erythrocyte suspension), dropping to 6.9% at 32 mg/L. Although this value approaches the 25% threshold typically associated with an increased risk of haemolytic activity [44], it remains below this boundary at maximum effective doses. More importantly, in A549 human lung epithelial cells, the primary target model for lung infections in cystic fibrosis [45]. PLP-3 displayed no detectable cytotoxicity up to 256 mg/L after 20 h of exposure, maintaining cell viability near 100%. These findings confirm that the anti-biofilm efficacy observed at 50-64 mg/L is driven by specific bactericidal action rather than non-specific eukaryotic toxicity. Thus, despite slightly lower activity in ColR than ColS strains, PLP-3 remains a robust alternative when colistin-mediated selection pressure renders conventional therapies ineffective.

PLP-3 exhibits a highly competitive antimicrobial and antibiofilm profile compared to other state-of-the-art peptides, despite inter-study assay variations due to the current lack of standardization among antibiofilm assays [15,46]. For instance the clinical-stage cyclic peptidomimetic murepavidin shows slightly lower MIC90 (1 mg/L) and MBEC90 (64 mg/L) values against cystic fibrosis P. aeruginosa isolates, achieving comparable microfluidic biovolume reductions as PLP-3 via LptD transporter inhibition rather than membrane lysis [47,48]. In contrast, many other cationic peptides lose efficacy against biofilms: cathelicidins, lysine-based peptides and IDR-1018 derivatives require ≥5-fold higher concentrations to eradicate biofilms than to inhibit planktonic growth, often reaching levels that may trigger cytotoxicity or target only the biofilm periphery [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Conversely, alternative strategies such as emulsions offer low in vivo toxicity in models like G. mellonella but require significantly higher concentrations to achieve antibiofilm effects [55]. Structurally related PG-1 displays a comparable MBIC (50 mg/L) compared to PLP-3, induces membrane disruption, but lacks true biofilm eradication [37]. Ultimately, PLP-3's ability to eradicate biofilm at concentrations within a narrow range of its inhibitory values, avoiding the drastic loss of potency typically seen in other AMPs and conventional antibiotics, positions it as a robust candidate for the treatment of recalcitrant P. aeruginosa infections. Given that in vitro hemolysis occurs near antibiofilm active concentrations, local routes such as inhalation or topical application represent the most viable clinical strategies.

Clinically, the efficacy and high penetration of PLP-3 in dynamic biofilms suggest it could represent a valuable strategy against colistin resistance in CF lungs. To enhance translational relevance, further studies should assess PLP-3 under more physiologically relevant conditions, such as synthetic cystic fibrosis medium 2 (SCFM2), which mimics CF airway secretions. Additionally, expanding sample size and testing synergistic combinations with ciprofloxacin [42] or low-biofilm penetrating antibiotics like aminoglycosides [56] will be essential future steps.

Pulmonary administration via inhalation represents the optimal strategy to minimize systemic toxicity while maximizing local efficacy. Formulation techniques used for other peptides, such as lipid nanoparticles to protect peptides [57] or spray-drying with mannitol [58], offer viable pathways for that PLP-3 aerosolization. Ultimately, its robust mechanism of action and growth rebound prevention position PLP-3 as a strong candidate against recalcitrant respiratory biofilms.

Conclusions

PLP-3 has demonstrated high activity against planktonic and biofilm-associated ColS and ColR P. aeruginosa clinical strains. In a dynamic flow-cell model, PLP-3 achieved a rapid reduction, prevented bacterial rebound and penetrated into the inactive biofilm core. Observed in vitro antibiofilm activity supports future investigation of PLP-3 in clinically relevant infection models, including studies assessing safety, delivery, and efficacy.
