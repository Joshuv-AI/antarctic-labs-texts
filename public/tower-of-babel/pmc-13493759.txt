Patient Profile and Treatment Characteristics of Adults and Adolescents Prescribed Ritlecitinib for Alopecia Areata in the USA
Authors: Justin Ko, Genevieve Gauthier, Samantha K. Kurosky, Liza Takiya, Mojgan M. Sadrarhami, Griffith Bell, Yi-Chien Lee, Benjamin Ungar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn June 2023, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ritlecitinib (50 mg once daily) for treatment of severe alopecia areata (AA) in patients ≥ 12 years. This study aimed to assess the characteristics of patients prescribed ritlecitinib within the first 10 months following approval.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from the Komodo Healthcare Map® (Komodo) and OMNY Health Foundation databases. Patients were aged ≥ 12 years with ≥ 1 ritlecitinib prescription on or after June 23, 2023, ≥ 1 AA diagnoses on or before index date (first prescription date), and ≥ 12 months of continuous enrollment before study entry. Two cohorts were assessed: patients with Komodo data (cohort 1) and linked Komodo and OMNY data (cohort 2). Clinical characteristics and AA treatment history were assessed over the 12-month pre-index period and stratified by age (12–17 and ≥ 18 years).ResultsCohort 1 included 2562 patients; of these, 61.8% were prescribed ritlecitinib by a dermatologist, 58.9% were female, and 35.2% were adolescents. Approximately 24% had alopecia totalis/alopecia universalis as the closest diagnosis prior to index, 12.4% had ≥ 1 other autoimmune disorder, 23.9% had ≥ 1 atopic disorder, and 23.7% had ≥ 1 diagnosis of a mental health condition captured. In cohort 1 during the 12 months before index date, 26.6% of patients received no AA treatments, 31.9% received systemic immunomodulators, and 31.3% received injectable corticosteroids. Cohort 2 included 381 patients; of those in cohort 2 with reported disease location, hair loss primarily occurred on the scalp (90.5%) and face (43.2%). In cohort 2, 77.2% of patients with a scalp hair loss (SHL) assessment had ≥ 50% SHL.ConclusionsIn the first 10 months following US approval, ritlecitinib was prescribed to a broad range of patients, including those with and without prior treatments and comorbidities. This suggests that ritlecitinib may provide new opportunities to engage or re-engage patients in AA-directed care.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease that has an underlying immuno-inflammatory pathogenesis and is characterized by patchy or complete nonscarring hair loss on the scalp, with or without additional loss of facial hair, body hair, or both [1, 2]. The AA disease course is unpredictable, and AA is associated with psychosocial comorbidities and quality-of-life impairment in adolescents and adults [3–6]. Until 2022, only off-label therapies were available for the treatment of AA, with high levels of patient dissatisfaction due to limited efficacy and adverse effects [7, 8]. In June 2022, baricitinib, an oral Janus kinase (JAK)1/2 inhibitor was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of adults (≥ 18 years of age) with severe AA [9]. Ritlecitinib, an oral selective JAK3 and TEC family kinase inhibitor, received FDA approval for the treatment of severe AA in adults and adolescents 12 years of age and older in June 2023 [10].

The safety and efficacy of ritlecitinib in AA have been shown in clinical trials, including the ALLEGRO phase 2b/3 randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial, which demonstrated a significantly greater proportion of patients achieved 80% or more scalp hair coverage at 24 weeks with ritlecitinib 50 mg than with placebo [11]. However, evaluating early real-world use of ritlecitinib is essential to understanding how practitioners have integrated it into clinical practice relative to clinical trial settings and to identifying potential gaps in care and access. The objectives of this study were to describe the demographics, clinical characteristics, and treatment history of patients who were prescribed ritlecitinib in real-world settings within the first 10 months following FDA approval.

Methods

Data Source and Patient Population

This retrospective observational study analyzed deidentified data from the Komodo Healthcare Map® (Komodo) and OMNY Health Foundation (OMNY) databases (January 2016 to May 2024). Komodo is a large US database composed of open and closed medical and pharmacy claims, primarily from commercial, Medicaid, and Medicare Advantage health plans. OMNY is an electronic medical records (EMR) database containing data from US health systems, including dermatology practices. The study included two patient cohorts: (1) patients from the Komodo database and (2) patients with linked Komodo and OMNY data. In accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) regulatory framework, Mirador Analytics Inc. has conducted a statistical disclosure risk analysis of the Komodo and linked Komodo + OMNY datasets. This constitutes an Expert Determination as set out in 45 CFR § 164.514(b)(1). The Expert Determination confirmed that the dataset met the HIPAA Expert Determination deidentification standard. Accordingly, approval from an institutional review board was not required and informed consent was not obtained.

Study Period and Variables

The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription for ritlecitinib on or after the FDA approval (June 23, 2023) (Fig. 1). Patients were required to be ≥ 12 years of age at index date, to have ≥ 12 months of continuous enrollment (medical and pharmacy benefits) prior to the index date (or eligibility proxy for patients with open claims), and to have ≥ 1 claim with a diagnosis code for AA (ICD-10 codes L63.x) on or before the index date. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and treatment history were evaluated at the index date and during the 12-month period prior to index date (pre-index baseline period). AA subtype was identified based on the closest diagnosis prior to or on the index date. Baseline comorbidities assessed included select atopic, autoimmune, and mental/emotional health conditions. Use of selected AA medications during the baseline period was also characterized and included injectable, oral, and topical corticosteroids, topical immunomodulators, systemic immunomodulators (which included azathioprine, cyclosporine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, sulfasalazine, and dupilumab, as well as the JAK inhibitors—baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and ruxolitinib), contact or allergen immunotherapy, and other treatments (which included oral and topical minoxidil, bimatoprost, narrowband ultraviolet B, excimer laser, and psoralen with ultraviolet A). Among patients with linked Komodo and OMNY data (cohort 2), extent of scalp hair loss (SHL) and affected anatomical regions at index date were assessed. Data Analysis

All analyses were descriptive and were analyzed overall and stratified by adolescent and adult age groups (12–17 years and ≥ 18 years, respectively). Binary and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages within each stratum. Continuous variables were reported using means and standard deviations (SDs), as well as medians and interquartile ranges to capture central tendency and dispersion.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 2562 patients in cohort 1 (Komodo data) and 381 patients in cohort 2 (linked Komodo and OMNY data) met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study (Fig. 2). Overall, the mean (SD) age was 31.0 (17.2) years in cohort 1 and 33.3 (18.9) years in cohort 2 (Table 1). In cohort 1, a total of 903 patients (35.2%) were adolescents, most patients (58.9%) were female, and the vast majority of patients were commercially insured (pharmacy coverage, 78.1%; medical coverage, 78.3%). Additionally, most patients (61.8%) were prescribed ritlecitinib by a dermatologist (including dermatology, dermatopathology, procedural dermatology, and pediatric dermatology physicians), followed by nurse practitioners, physician assistants, or registered nurses (14.1%) and by primary care physicians (10.9%). Among the 2562 patients in cohort 1, 24.1% had alopecia totalis or alopecia universalis as the AA diagnosis closest to the index date (adolescents, 22.6%; adults, 25.0%). During the 12 months prior to the index date, 12.4% of all patients in cohort 1 and 13.7% of all patients in cohort 2 had a diagnosis recorded for ≥ 1 of the selected autoimmune diseases (Table 2). The most common autoimmune diseases in both cohorts were diabetes (cohort 1, 3.9%; cohort 2, 5.3%), Hashimoto’s disease (cohort 1, 3.0%; cohort 2, 2.1%), psoriasis (cohort 1, 2.5%; cohort 2, 3.9%), and vitiligo (cohort 1, 1.8%; cohort 2, 2.4%), with similar trends observed when the data were stratified by age (adolescents and adults). Among all patients in cohort 1, 23.9% had a diagnosis for ≥ 1 of the selected atopic diseases during the 12 months before index date, with similar rates observed in cohort 2 (25.5%). The most common atopic disorder in both cohorts was atopic dermatitis (cohort 1, 11.0%; cohort 2, 12.1%). A numerically higher proportion of adolescents in cohort 2 had atopic dermatitis compared with cohort 1 (19.7% vs 15.4%), while similar proportions were observed in adults between the two cohorts (8.5% vs 8.6%). Other common atopic diseases across both cohorts were allergic rhinitis (cohort 1, 9.8%; cohort 2, 12.1%) and asthma (cohort 1, 8.9%; cohort 2, 8.4%), with similar trends in adolescents and adults, although rates were generally higher in adolescents. During the 12-month pre-index period, 23.7% of all patients in cohort 1 and 22.8% of all patients in cohort 2 had ≥ 1 diagnosis of a mental health condition captured. The most common mental health conditions reported in all patients across both cohorts were anxiety (cohort 1, 15.9%; cohort 2, 15.2%) and major depressive disorder (cohort 1, 11.1%; cohort 2, 11.6%), with similar trends observed in adults. Treatment Patterns Prior to First Ritlecitinib Prescription in Patients with Claims Data (Cohort 1)

A total of 26.6% (n = 681) of all patients in cohort 1 did not receive any of the selected AA treatments during the 12 months prior to the first ritlecitinib prescription (pre-index period) (Fig. 3). During the 12-month pre-index period, the most common AA treatments received among all patients in cohort 1 (N = 2562) were systemic immunomodulators (31.9%, mainly driven by the use of JAK inhibitors [27.8%]), injectable corticosteroids (31.3%), and topical corticosteroids (26.0%) (Fig. 3a). Among adolescents, topical corticosteroids were the most commonly prescribed medications (32.6%), followed by injectable corticosteroids (30.3%) and systemic immunomodulators (26.1%; primarily JAK inhibitors [18.5%]). Approximately 1 in 3 adult patients received injectable corticosteroids (31.8%) and systemic immunomodulators (35.0%; mainly driven by the use of JAK inhibitors [32.9%; 24.5% received baricitinib and 10.5% other JAK inhibitors]). Systemic immunomodulators were the most common last treatment received by patients within the 12 months before the first ritlecitinib prescription overall (20.0%) and in adults (21.8%), which was also primarily driven by the use of JAK inhibitors (overall, 17.0%; adults, 20.0%) (Fig. 3b). Discussion

This retrospective study examined the baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of US patients aged ≥ 12 years old who were prescribed ritlecitinib in the first 10 months after FDA approval. The majority of patients were adult, female, commercially insured, and prescribed ritlecitinib by a dermatologist. In the 12 months before the index date, approximately 1 in 4 patients had an atopic disorder or had a medical visit during which a diagnosis for one of the selected mental health conditions was recorded. Approximately 30% of adolescents received injectable corticosteroids or topical corticosteroids and approximately one-third of adults received injectable corticosteroids and systemic immunomodulators (primarily driven by the use of JAK inhibitors other than ritlecitinib) in the 12 months prior to the first ritlecitinib prescription. Among the patients with linked claims and EMR data (cohort 2) and for whom anatomical regions for hair loss were reported, the scalp and face were the most common anatomical regions reported. In addition, approximately 75% of patients in this cohort with a SHL assessment had ≥ 50% SHL; however, these results should be interpreted with caution. Only a small subset of patients in this analysis had recorded SHL, and patients with ICD-10 diagnosis codes for alopecia totalis or alopecia universalis were classified as having 100% SHL, which artificially increases the proportion of patients with 100% SHL.

The proportion of patients with atopic disorders was lower in the current study (23.9–25.5%) than in a US regional healthcare system cohort (38.2%) [12], and rates of allergic rhinitis and asthma were higher in a large US claims study than in our study (24.0% vs 9.8–12.1% and 12.8% vs 8.4–8.9%, respectively) [13]. Differences in rates of atopic disorders compared with prior studies may be related to variation in study periods (1-year baseline in our study vs up to 11 years in the regional healthcare system study and up to 3 years in the claims study) [12, 13], as longer timeframes may increase the likelihood of capturing more comorbidities. However, the rate of atopic dermatitis observed in the current study (12.1–11.0%) was similar to that reported in a previous meta-analysis (9.8%), which found a higher prevalence of atopic dermatitis in patients with AA than in control patients [14]. Additionally, the proportions of patients with anxiety and depression in the current study were consistent with results from previous studies of AA populations, which have reported a higher prevalence of anxiety and depression in patients with AA compared with the general population [5, 15, 16].

Before the approval of baricitinib in June 2022 for severe AA in patients aged ≥ 18 years [9, 17] and the approval of ritlecitinib in June 2023 for severe AA in patients aged ≥ 12 years [10], the treatment options for patients with AA included off-label use of medications—including topical, oral, and intralesional corticosteroids and hair growth stimulants such as minoxidil. Historically, corticosteroids have been the most commonly used treatment in patients with AA [18–21]. In the present study, corticosteroids were frequently used by all patients prior to the first ritlecitinib prescription (injectable, 31.3%; oral, 19.9%; topical, 26.0%). Fewer adult patients used injectable, oral, or topical corticosteroids than systemic immunomodulators (which included baricitinib and off-label JAK inhibitors), while more adolescent patients used injectable or topical corticosteroids than systemic immunomodulators. Overall systemic immunomodulator use was high compared with results from previous studies (31.9% vs 2.7–10%); however, these previous studies characterized AA treatment patterns before the approval of baricitinib and ritlecitinib for severe AA [18, 20, 22]. In the current study, the rate of systemic immunomodulator use excluding JAK inhibitors (5.3%) was similar to that reported in previous studies [18, 20, 22]. Overall, three-quarters of patients had received other treatments in the 12 months prior to the first ritlecitinib prescription, suggesting that existing treatments were suboptimal in meeting patients’ treatment goals. Baricitinib was prescribed to approximately 25% of adults during the 12-month pre-index period, and approximately 15% of adults used baricitinib as the last treatment during this 12-month pre-index period. These results highlight the value of additional treatment options, particularly those with different mechanisms of action.

In the 12-month period before the first ritlecitinib prescription, a substantial proportion of adolescents and adults did not receive any AA treatments (overall 1 in 4 patients). It is possible that these patients had not yet initiated AA treatment due to the limited availability of approved therapies, or patients may have discontinued previous treatments prior to this 12-month period due to ineffectiveness or intolerability. Thus, the availability of ritlecitinib offers adolescents and adults an opportunity to engage or re-engage in AA care using an AA therapy with demonstrated efficacy and safety [11, 23].

The patient data included in this study provide valuable insights into the characteristics of the patient population prescribed ritlecitinib in real-world practice. However, certain limitations of the study should be acknowledged. Firstly, for the analysis examining the proportion of patients who were prescribed ritlecitinib with concomitant treatment, only patients with a paid claim for ritlecitinib were included (approximately 40%). Restricting the analysis to paid claims ensured that patients received both ritlecitinib and the concomitant treatment. Rejected or reversed claims were observed for the remaining patients (likely due to recency of data); however, paid claims were later observed in 50% of these patients. In addition, most patients included in this study who were prescribed ritlecitinib were identified from open claims. Proxy for enrollment was used, but complete visibility into all treatments received by patients cannot be confirmed. There is no disease severity information in the Komodo database or information to confirm reasons for which a medication was prescribed. Additionally, the extent of SHL and anatomical regions with hair loss at index date were reported for a subset of patients with linked Komodo and OMNY data. Distribution of extent of SHL should be interpreted with caution and may not be fully representative. Patients with severe disease may have been more likely to receive SHL assessments, as they may have been more likely to be seen by specialists who routinely use standardized tools such as the SALT. In addition, recording of the anatomical region for hair loss is not a mandatory field; it is possible that clinicians documented only some areas of hair loss during a visit, such as the ones being actively treated, rather than providing a comprehensive assessment. Finally, the study sample consisted primarily of privately insured employees and their dependents. Accordingly, results may not be generalizable to the overall population of patients with AA in the USA.

Conclusions

In the first 10 months following US approval, ritlecitinib was prescribed to a broad range of patients with AA, including adolescents and adults, those with and without prior treatments, and those with and without various comorbidities. Three-quarters of patients received other treatments for AA in the previous 12 months, which may indicate that such therapies were suboptimal in meeting patients’ treatment goals. Additionally, 17.6% of all patients received baricitinib prior to their first ritlecitinib prescription, underscoring the value of additional treatment options with different mechanisms of action. Approximately one-quarter of patients had no evidence of prior AA treatment in the 12 months preceding their first ritlecitinib prescription. Thus, the results suggest that ritlecitinib provides patients with new opportunities to engage or re-engage in medical care for AA. As ritlecitinib continues to be integrated into clinical practice, additional exploration of uptake and clinical outcomes will be critical to evaluate the changing treatment landscape of AA.
