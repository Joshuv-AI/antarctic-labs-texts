A rare case of recurrent spinal hydatid cyst in a 17-year-old man with neurological deficits and balance impairment

Background

Hydatid cyst is a rare zoonotic infectious disease, caused by the larval stage of tapeworm parasites known as Echinococcus granulosus [1]. This infection predominantly manifests in regions characterized by prevalent agricultural and livestock practices, including parts of Latin America, Australia, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and India [2, 3]. Sheep, cattle, goats, pigs, and canines are frequently infected with this parasite [4]. Humans usually become intermediate hosts through hand, food, and water contaminated with parasite eggs [5]. The liver, lungs, and brain are most affected while Bone involvement is rare, occurring in 0.5%-4% of cases [6].

Treating spinal hydatid cysts requires a combination of surgical and medical therapies due to its aggressive nature. Despite intensive treatment, recurrence rates remain high, leading to considerable mortality and morbidity [6]. Early and accurate diagnosis along with a thorough understanding of the clinical and imaging characteristics, are crucial for developing an effective treatment strategy during preoperative evaluation and postoperative follow-up to minimize the recurrence risks. Herein, we report a rare case of recurrent spinal hydatid cyst in a 17-year-old man with advanced neurological impairment that required surgical intervention in the upper thoracic spine.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male patient with a history of residing in the Middle East, specifically Afghanistan, and low socioeconomic status visited our department (in Iran) with complaints of muscle weakness in his lower extremities, predominantly in the right leg, accompanied by back pain and impaired balance resulting from muscular discomfort experienced over the past month. Neither sphincteric dysfunction nor a history of trauma was reported by the patient. The patient was under no medication and no drug abuse. Two years ago, the patient experienced back pain and muscle weakness in his lower extremities and underwent surgery at another medical center in Afghanistan, where he was diagnosed with primary spinal hydatidosis (SH) located at the upper thoracic level, resulting in significant pain relief. He had been taking albendazole for several months but discontinued it without consulting his doctor due to lack of post-op follow-ups. Upon presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable. On clinical examination, a previous surgery scar on the dorsal midline at the level of C7 to T2 was seen. In neurological evaluation, normal cranial nerves function, reduced muscle force (upper right and left, proximal and distal: 5/5, lower left and right proximal: 3/5, lower right dorsiflexion: 1/5, lower left dorsiflexion: 5/5, lower right plantar flexion: 1/5, lower left plantar flexion: 5/5), sensory loss in the right lower limb, paresthesia in the left medial forearm, 3 + deep tendon reflex (DTR) in both legs and Babinski sign in the right plantar reflex were observed. The primary laboratory tests indicated normocytic anemia, no elevated inflammatory markers (erythrocytes sedimentation rate [ESR] and quantitative C-reactive protein [CRP]), and non-reactive human immunodeficiency virus antibody screening tests. Chest and cervical radiography of the patient revealed signs of T2, and T3 laminectomy, as well as a 20*15 mm well-defined rimmed round hypodense cystic mass with fine septa adjacent to the T2 level with extension to the left neural foramen along with widening of the neural foramen. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) T1- and T2-weighted scans of the lumbosacral spine were suggestive of a hydatid cyst, revealing a well-defined cystic mass with fine septations in the spinal canal at the T2 vertebral level. On T1-weighted imaging with contrast, the cyst wall demonstrated enhancement, indicating a distinct boundary, while the cyst fluids exhibited low signal intensity, consistent with a water-like signal. On T2-weighted images, the cyst contents appeared hyperintense, with the cyst wall showing low signal intensity. The lesion extends into the left neural foramen, resulting in foramen widening and suggesting compression of adjacent nerve structures. The liver and lungs were also evaluated as the common sites of hydatid cysts and no signs of involvement were seen. The chest computed tomography (CT) scan has been shown in Fig. 1.

Surgical procedure

Since our patient had a spinal hydatid cyst with neurological symptoms, both surgery and oral medication were required, regardless of the size of the mass. General anesthesia was used, and the patient was positioned in the prone position. After opening the previous surgery site on the dorsal midline and periosteal muscle opening, partial T2 laminectomy was performed, and a cystic lesion with a compressive effect on both sides of the dura was detected. A cystic tumor measuring 2*1.5*0.5 cm with 0.1 cm wall thickness was resected. SURGICEL was used for hemostasis and after closing the operation site, the patient was transferred to the recovery room. A pathological examination of the lesion was performed after surgical excision to confirm the definitive diagnosis. Pathological and histological investigation of the specimen demonstrated a hydatid cyst consisting of multiple fragments with an adjacent foreign body-type granulomatous reaction, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The patient was discharged two days after surgery and the neuromuscular complaints were resolved. No surgical site infection, bleeding, or hematoma was seen. After the surgical procedure, a six-month course of chewable Albendazole (400mg /q12h) was prescribed to the patient as a preventive measure against recurrence. Furthermore, the patient was recommended to undergo routine physiotherapy sessions after discharge. The patient underwent neurological assessments at three months, six months and one year after discharge. He reported no neuromuscular complaints and the clinical examination at one year revealed no signs of neuromuscular abnormalities.

Discussion

Hydatid cyst disease is globally distributed and is mostly found in pastoral regions [1]. As it is a zoonotic disease, it primarily affects animals and humans are accidental intermediate hosts and cannot transmit it. Upon entry into the body's circulation via hepatic sinusoids or portovenous shunts, Echinococcus parasites can manifest as lesions that demonstrate gradual expansion and colonization of areas within highly vascularized organs. Cystic echinococcosis (CE) affects over 270 million people across Central Asia, including regions of Mongolia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Afghanistan, Iran, Pakistan, and western China, representing 58% of the region's population [7]. A cross-sectional study conducted in Iran revealed that the prevalence of CE among 1,005 nomadic people was found to be 8.1% [8]. Another retrospective study in Iran reported an average surgical incidence rate of 0.74 per 100,000 population [9].

Mainly, the liver and lungs have the highest frequency of involvement in this incident [5]. Liver involvement occurs approximately in 70%-60% of cases, whereas the lungs are affected in 40%-30% [5, 9]. Other sites, including the spleen, heart, diaphragm, kidney, brain can be contaminated [9, 10]. Concurrent involvement of two organs was observed in 9–10% of cases, with the liver and lungs being the most common sites of involvement [9, 10]. Bone Complications are rare and affected in 0.5%-4% of cases [5], with approximately 45% involving the spine, with a high prevalence in the thoracic spine, followed by the lumbar, sacral, and cervical spine [11]. Hydatid cyst is a slow-progressing disease with a long period of asymptomatic incubation, often taking ten to twenty years to result in bone complications [12]. During the early stages, these cysts typically remain symptomless, leading to a delay in seeking medical treatment [4]. The clinical presentation witnessed in patients with SE is nonspecific and determined by the size and placement of the cyst. SHC usually appear when the cyst has extended to show spinal cord compression symptoms. Generally, it manifests as both back pain and neurological dysfunction (such as paraparesis, peripheral sensitivity loss, sphincter disturbance, paraplegia, and bladder dysfunction) [2, 4, 13]. Spinal hydatid cysts are divided into five types depending on where they occur: intramedullary, intradural, and extramedullary, extradural and intraspinal, vertebral body, and paravertebral [4]. These similarities to other space-occupying lesions in the spinal canal can lead to misdiagnosis or oversight. Other possible diagnoses of intradural spinal cord lesions include spinal malignancy or metastasis, Tuberculous spondylitis, vertebral pyogenic infection, abscess, and parasitic infection [14].

Common diagnostic techniques for spinal cord lesions include serological tests, X-rays, CT scans, and MRI [13]. Although laboratory tests including Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), indirect hemagglutination, and complement fixation are relatively accurate for diagnosing abdominal hydatidosis, their sensitivity and specificity declines considerably in detecting extrahepatic forms such as bone involvement [15]. As a result, MRI is the optimal diagnostic tool, with histopathology providing further confirmation [3].

The best treatment approach for bone hydatidosis is surgical removal of cysts without any rupture coupled with adequate drug therapy. The aim of the surgery is to relieve spinal cord pressure. The surgical approach may be posterior or anterior, depending on the cyst's location. If the cyst is confined to the epidural space without affecting the bone or dura, it can be removed on its own. However, if the cyst is attached to the dura, dural excision and duraplasty are required [16]. In the event of cyst rupture, there is a potential risk of spreading additional cysts, leading to severe reactions such as anaphylactic shock and, in severe cases, death [17]. The general mortality rate of cystic echinococcosis is approximately 2.2% to 6.5%, with an increased rate observed in individuals who remain untreated, receive insufficient treatment, or experience accidental cyst rupture during surgery [18]. The recurrence rate of SHC varies within the range of 41% to 89% [6, 19–21]. Typically, the reappearance of the condition is observed within the same organ or structure that was initially affected.

Nevertheless, the reappearance noted in our patient suggests that either the resection was not executed accurately, or the subsequent therapies were inadequate or mismanaged. It is also possible that both factors collectively contributed to the recurrence, emphasizing the importance of correct taking of treatment. Antiprotozoal and antihelmintic agents such as albendazole should be chewed and consumed with a full stomach to complete the medication.

It is also worth noting that to enhance the prevention and control of CE, effective education and awareness programs should be considered to improve the community's knowledge and attitudes toward the disease [22]. Raising awareness will provide a strong foundation for better management and prevention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although spinal hydatid disease is a rare condition, it should be considered when compression on the spinal cord is detected, especially in endemic areas. The preferred treatment is surgical resection and decompression of the spinal cord. It is crucial to combine surgery with adducent drug therapy followed by routine postoperative monitoring to minimize the probability of disease recurrence. Despite the use of modern diagnostic and therapeutic tools, managing hydatid cyst disease remains challenging in the long term for both patients and healthcare providers due to its high recurrence rate.
