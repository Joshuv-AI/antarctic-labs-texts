Preparedness of dental interns to identify oral potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer: a multi‑state cross‑sectional survey in India
Authors: Satya Ranjan Misra, Rupsa Das, Subhra Jyoti Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental interns are frontline clinicians who can detect oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) at an early stage. This study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices (KAP) regarding OPMDs and OSCC among a multi-state sample of dental interns in India recruited through online platforms.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 1220 dental interns across India (September–December 2025). Participants were recruited via professional WhatsApp groups, Facebook, and Instagram. A validated, structured online questionnaire comprised 8 knowledge items (multiple-choice/true-false), 4 attitude statements (5-point Likert scale), and 3 practice items. Content validity was established by five experts (item-level content validity index [I-CVI] 0.83–1.00); the instrument was pilot-tested on 20 interns. Reliability of the knowledge scale (KR-20 = 0.62) and attitude scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.88) was assessed. Descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation, one-way ANOVA, linear regression, and chi-square tests were applied (SPSS v26). Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsRespondents were from 23 states and 3 union territories. The largest representations were from Maharashtra (12.3%, n = 150), Karnataka (10.8%, n = 132), Tamil Nadu (9.7%, n = 118), Uttar Pradesh (8.9%, n = 109), and West Bengal (7.6%, n = 93). Mean knowledge score was 5.48 ± 1.08 (median 6, range 1–7) out of 8. While 99.2% had heard of OPMDs and 100% knew that early OPMDs are reversible, only 70.5% correctly identified the malignant transformation rate of leukoplakia (1–5%). Attitudes were strongly positive (mean 19.67 ± 1.09/20); >94% strongly agreed that early detection reduces mortality and that they have a responsibility to refer. In practice, 86.9% (n = 1060) always examined high-risk oral sites and 93.4% (n = 1140) always counselled on tobacco risks; 92.6% (n = 1130) reported having identified and referred a suspicious lesion. Knowledge correlated positively with practice (r = 0.42, p < 0.001). Linear regression showed knowledge was associated with practice frequency (β = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.29–0.49, p < 0.001). Interns who always screened or counselled scored higher in knowledge than those who did not (p < 0.001). Chi-square analysis demonstrated a significant association between knowledge tertile and consistent practice (χ² = 24.6, df = 2, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis multi-state online survey found that dental interns demonstrated moderate overall knowledge and strongly positive attitudes, with frequent self-reported screening and counselling practices. However, specific knowledge gaps regarding malignant transformation rates persist. Findings should be interpreted with caution given the convenience sampling and reliance on self-reported data. Targeted curricular updates and periodic hands-on training are recommended to strengthen early detection competencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08681-0.

Background

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) constitutes approximately 90% of all oral malignancies and ranks as the third most common cancer in India, with nearly 300,000 new cases reported globally each year [1]. The South Asian region, particularly India, bears a disproportionate burden, approximately one-quarter of the worldwide incidence, largely attributed to the widespread use of smoked and smokeless tobacco, areca nut, and gutkha [2]. About four-fifths of OSCCs are preceded by clinically identifiable oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), including proliferative verrucous leukoplakia, leukoplakia, erythroplakia, oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), oral lichenoid lesions, and lichen planus [3]. A recently published Indian cohort study reported a 5-year malignant transformation rate of 15.6% for leukoplakia and 18.6% for OSMF, underscoring the critical window for early diagnosis [4, 5].

Dentists, particularly dental interns who are often the first point of contact in outpatient departments, play a crucial role in the early recognition and referral of OPMDs [6]. Adequate knowledge of risk factors, clinical presentation, and malignant potential, coupled with positive attitudes and consistent screening practices, can significantly reduce OSCC morbidity and mortality [7]. Despite this, KAP studies among dental students and interns worldwide reveal variable levels of preparedness. For instance, a survey in Saudi Arabia found that while 97% of dental students agreed that early detection is important, only 63% correctly identified the most common site for oral cancer [8]. Similar knowledge gaps have been reported among Turkish and Malaysian dental trainees [9–11].

In India, numerous KAP studies have been published among dental professionals, but most have been regionally restricted to single states or cities. Large-scale, multi-state data on dental interns’ preparedness for early diagnosis of OPMDs and OSCC have been generally lacking [12–16]. The widespread use of online platforms for professional networking has made large-scale online surveys feasible, providing valuable insights into current educational gaps across diverse geographic regions.

The present study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices of dental interns regarding OPMDs and OSCC across multiple states in India, and to examine the association between knowledge scores and clinical practice behaviours.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional, questionnaire-based online survey was conducted between September and December 2025 among dental interns across India.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Institute of Dental Sciences, Bhubaneswar (Approval No. IEC/IDS/SOA/2024/III-31) and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained online from all participants prior to accessing the questionnaire.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were dental interns enrolled in any recognised dental college in India who had completed at least six months of clinical postings. Interns without clinical rotations or those who did not provide online consent were excluded. A convenience sampling approach was used: the survey link was disseminated through professional WhatsApp groups (including Indian Dental Association student groups and college-specific intern groups), Facebook pages dedicated to dental professionals, and Instagram accounts of dental student associations. To avoid duplicate responses, the Google Forms platform was set to allow only one response per verified email address. No incentives were provided. A total of 1220 fully completed questionnaires were received.

Sampling method and representativeness

Since the survey was distributed openly online, a sampling frame could not be defined, and a response rate could not be calculated. The resulting sample overrepresents states with a higher concentration of dental colleges and participants more active on professional social media. Consequently, the findings may not be fully representative of all dental interns in India. This limitation is discussed further in the Discussion.

Data collection procedure

The survey was administered via Google Forms. The link remained active from September to December 2025. On accessing the link, potential participants first viewed an information sheet and consent form; only those who provided consent proceeded to the questionnaire. Duplicate entries were prevented through email verification.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire was developed based on a thorough review of existing KAP instruments [1, 17, 18] and refined through the following steps:

✔ Content validity: The initial draft was evaluated by two oral physicians and two public health dentists. Each expert rated the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of each item on a 4-point scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant). The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts rating an item as 3 or 4. All items achieved I-CVI values between 0.83 and 1.00, indicating excellent content validity. No item fell below the threshold of 0.78.✔ Pilot study: The revised questionnaire was administered to 20 interns from a single institution to assess clarity, comprehension, and completion time (mean 8 min). Minor wording modifications were made based on feedback. Pilot data were not included in the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported to SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised responses. Knowledge scores were computed as the sum of correct answers. Attitude scores were summed across the four items (range 4–20). Practice scores for Q13–Q14 were averaged (range 1–5). Q15 was analysed categorically. Associations between knowledge and practice/attitude were examined using Pearson’s correlation. Independent t-tests compared knowledge scores between “Always” and “Not always” responders for Q13 and Q14. One-way ANOVA compared knowledge scores across Q15 response categories (after collapsing “No” and “Not confident” due to small frequencies). A simple linear regression examined the association between knowledge scores and mean practice score (Q13–Q14). Chi-square tests examined associations between categorical practice variables and knowledge tertiles (low: ≤5, medium: 6, high: 7). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 1220 dental interns participated (52.5% female, 47.5% male; mean age 23.7 ± 1.2 years). Respondents were from 23 states and 3 union territories across India (Table 1). The largest representations were from Maharashtra (12.3%, n = 150), Karnataka (10.8%, n = 132), Tamil Nadu (9.7%, n = 118), Uttar Pradesh (8.9%, n = 109), and West Bengal (7.6%, n = 93). The remaining states contributed between 1% and 5% each.

Knowledge

The mean knowledge score was 5.48 ± 1.08 (range 1–7; median 6). Only 20 interns (1.6%) scored ≤ 3, while 910 (74.6%) scored ≥ 6. No participant achieved the maximum score of 8, primarily because of the difficult item on leukoplakia transformation rate. Virtually all interns (99.2%, n = 1210) had heard of OPMDs (Q1). Performance was excellent for reversibility of OPMDs (Q4, 100% correct), the most common site of OSCC in India (Q8, 96.7% correct, n = 1180), and erythroplakia as the lesion with the highest malignant potential (Q3, 82.8% correct, n = 1010; Q5, 91.0% correct, n = 1110). However, only 70.5% (n = 860) correctly identified the malignant transformation rate of leukoplakia as 1–5% (Q7); 27.0% (n = 330) believed it to be 10–20% (Table 2).

Attitude

Attitudes were exceptionally positive. For the statement “Early detection of OPMDs significantly reduces oral cancer-related mortality,” 94.3% (n = 1150) strongly agreed and 3.3% (n = 40) agreed; only 0.8% (n = 10) disagreed. Similarly, 94.3% (n = 1150) strongly agreed that they have a responsibility to refer suspicious lesions. The mean attitude score was 19.67 ± 1.09 out of 20 (Table 3).

Practice

Most interns reported optimal screening behaviour: 86.9% (n = 1060) always examined high-risk oral sites, and 93.4% (n = 1140) always counselled patients on tobacco risks. Notably, 92.6% (n = 1130) stated that they had identified a suspicious lesion and referred it; 4.9% (n = 60) had identified a lesion but were unsure how to proceed, and 2.4% (n = 30) had never identified such a lesion or lacked confidence. The mean practice score (Q13–Q14) was 4.88 ± 0.38 out of 5 (Table 4).

Associations between knowledge and practice

Pearson’s correlation revealed a moderate positive relationship between total knowledge score and mean practice score (r = 0.42, p < 0.001). Linear regression analysis showed that knowledge scores were significantly associated with practice frequency (β = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.29–0.49, p < 0.001), explaining 17.6% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.175). Because of the cross-sectional design, this association should not be interpreted as causal.

Interns who always examined high-risk sites had significantly higher knowledge scores (5.59 ± 0.97, n = 1060) than those who did not (5.09 ± 1.17, n = 160; t(1218) = 5.15, p < 0.001). Likewise, interns who always counselled scored higher (5.53 ± 0.99, n = 1140) than those who did not (4.67 ± 1.21, n = 80; t(1218) = 6.21, p < 0.001).

One-way ANOVA showed a significant difference in knowledge scores across the three Q15 response categories (F (2,1217) = 28.64, p < 0.001). (Table 5) Post-hoc Tukey tests indicated that interns who had identified and referred a lesion scored higher (5.62 ± 0.98, n = 1130) than those who were unsure (4.67 ± 1.21, n = 60; p < 0.001) and those who had not identified/were not confident (4.33 ± 1.15, n = 30; p < 0.001). No significant difference was found between the latter two groups (p = 0.34).

Chi-square analysis demonstrated a significant association between knowledge tertile and consistent practice (defined as “Always” on both Q13 and Q14) (χ² = 24.6, df = 2, p < 0.001). No significant gender differences were observed.

Discussion

This large multi-state online study provides one of the most geographically diverse assessments to date of knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices about OPMDs and OSCC among dental interns in India. Overall, interns demonstrated moderate-to-high knowledge (mean 5.48/8), extremely positive attitudes, and frequently reported screening behaviours. These findings are encouraging and suggest that undergraduate training across India has instilled a strong sense of responsibility for oral cancer prevention. Nevertheless, the knowledge gap concerning malignant transformation rates, particularly for leukoplakia, indicates a need for curriculum refinement.

Knowledge level and comparison with other studies

The mean knowledge score of 68.5% aligns with several Indian investigations. Tendulkar et al. [19] found that 97.7% of dental undergraduates were aware of tobacco risks, but 56.3% had never received formal oral cancer screening training [19]. Agarwal et al. reported that dental students were significantly better than medical students at observing proliferative growth [12, 15]. Sharma et al. noted that while students had sufficient knowledge, they lacked confidence in diagnosing early symptoms [16]. Pazhanivel et al. found that 80.2% of dental students in Chennai were aware that OSCC is the most common oral cancer, yet 74.4% agreed additional training was needed [20]. Srivastava et al. reported that 100% of undergraduate dental students in Kanpur requested further information, and knowledge decreased from final-year to internship [21]. These regional variations underscore the importance of multi-state surveys such as the present one.

Internationally, a 2025 meta-analysis by Escoto-Vasquez et al. encompassing 41 studies reported that 98% of students demonstrated relatively good knowledge about oral cancer risk factors, with smoking most recognised (99%), followed by advanced age (68%) and alcoholism (57%) [22]. This global benchmark compares favourably with our findings. Studies from Yemen [23], Turkey [24], and Spain [17, 18] similarly show high awareness but variable confidence and screening rates. Only 70.5% of our sample correctly selected “1 to 5%” as the malignant transformation rate of leukoplakia, while recent longitudinal studies report 5-year transformation rates as high as 15.6% [5]. This discrepancy suggests that many interns may rely on older textbook figures, a gap observed globally [22]. Villa & Woo have argued that curricula must incorporate current epidemiological data to avoid underestimation of risk [25].

Attitudes: a uniform culture of vigilance

The nearly unanimous positive attitudes (> 94% strongly agreed with early detection benefits) are consistent with global trends [17, 23, 26]. This favourable attitudinal climate is a strong foundation for training. However, positive attitudes alone must be coupled with competence, which our data show is moderately correlated with knowledge.

Self-reported practice and the knowledge-practice gap

Over 92% of interns reported having identified and referred a suspicious lesion, a figure that compares favourably with international data [23, 24]. The significant correlation between knowledge and practice (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) is consistent with global findings, yet the modest variance explained (17.6%) indicates that factors beyond knowledge, such as clinical confidence, institutional support, and time constraints also influence behaviour. Importantly, because all practice data were self-reported, the present figures may overestimate true screening frequency due to social desirability bias; objective assessments like chart audits or OSCEs are needed to validate these findings.

Implications for dental education

Specific curricular updates are warranted. Integrating the 2014 Gupta cohort data on transformation rates and updating the standard teaching on leukoplakia risk would address the main knowledge gap [5]. A brief half-day workshop incorporating didactic review, OSCE stations, and case-based discussions modelled on the intervention by Gandhi et al. [27], which improved diagnostic accuracy from 58% to 84%, could be implemented during internship [27]. Training on adjunctive diagnostic aids (like toluidine blue, autofluorescence) and biopsy skills would further bridge the theory-practice gap.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. Convenience sampling via social media introduces selection bias and limits generalisability; the sample overrepresents states with many dental colleges and interns active in online professional groups. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Self-reported practice may be inflated, and the knowledge scale showed modest reliability (KR-20 = 0.62), which may reflect the small number of items and heterogeneous content, as well as some very easy items that contributed to a ceiling effect. The attitude scale also showed a ceiling effect, limiting discrimination. Future studies should employ stratified random sampling, objective outcome measures, and scales with better discriminative ability (like case-based questions).

Despite these limitations, this is one of the largest multi-state surveys of dental interns in India to date, with a robust sample size and a rigorously validated instrument, providing valuable insights into current educational strengths and gaps.

Conclusion

This multi-state online survey found that dental interns across India demonstrate moderate overall knowledge and strongly positive attitudes toward early detection of OPMDs and OSCC. Self-reported screening and counselling practices were frequently reported, and knowledge scores were positively associated with practice behaviours. However, specific knowledge deficits, particularly regarding the malignant transformation rate of leukoplakia, persist. Given the study’s limitations, including convenience sampling and reliance on self-reported data, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Targeted curricular updates and periodic hands-on training are recommended to strengthen early detection competencies. Future research employing representative sampling methods and objective outcome measures is needed to validate and extend these findings.
