A proof of the 1978 Briançon-Iarrobino Conjecture in three dimensions
Owen Mackenzie, Fatemeh Rezaee

Abstract
. We resolve the Briançon-Iarrobino Conjecture regarding the maximum singularity of ℋ = Hilb l ​ ( A 3 ) \mathcal{H}=\mathrm{Hilb}^{l}(\mdmathbb{A}^{3}) , where l l is a tetrahedral number, by refining the work of Ramkumar-Sammartano in [ 37 ] . This also immediately implies the conjectural necessary condition for a point of ℋ \mathcal{H} to have the maximal singularity, suggested by the second-named author in [ 40 ] . In a sequel to this article, [ 31 ] , we prove a generalized version of this conjecture for certain non-tetrahedral l l , via proving the conjectural necessary condition.

Introduction

The geometry of Hilbert schemes of points
\mathrm{Hilb}^{l}({\mdmathbb{A}}^{N})
has been studied for decades. Perhaps, the longest-standing open problem regarding the singularities of
\mathrm{Hilb}^{l}({\mdmathbb{A}}^{N})
is a conjecture by Briançon and Iarrobino in the 1970s, which predicted the maximum singularity when
l
is a (hyper)tetrahedral number. In this article, we give a short proof of this conjecture for
N=3
.

0.1.
History and relevant work

Since its invention by Grothendieck in the 1960s,
[
16
]
, the Hilbert scheme has been ubiquitous and studied in various fields in mathematics, including algebraic geometry (in the areas such as enumerative geometry and birational geometry), commutative algebra, combinatorics, representation theory, gauge theory, (e.g., see
[
13
]
), and knot theory, (e.g., see
[
11
]
). Because of its highly singular nature, studying the global geometry of the Hilbert scheme is highly complicated. To measure how significant the singularities are, we need to measure the dimension of the tangent space at a given point of the Hilbert scheme. The maximal singularity occurs at the points with the maximal dimension of the tangent space.

One of the major results on the global geometry of the Hilbert scheme appeared in the Ph.D. thesis of Hartshorne,
[
19
]
in the 1960s, proving the connectedness of the Hilbert scheme. In the late 1960s, Fogarty showed the smoothness of the Hilbert scheme for
N=2
,
[
9
]
. In the early 1970s, Iarrobino found the first example of a reducible Hilbert scheme of points in three dimensions for the case of
l=78
,
[
24
]
(other works on the (ir)reducibility in three dimensions include
[
6
]
in the late 2000s, and
[
20
,
7
]
in the 2010s). In the late 1970s, Briançon and Iarrobino predicted that ideals with maximal number of generators give the maximal singularity, while Sturmfels disproved it,
[
42
]
. In the early 2000s, Haiman used the Hilbert scheme to resolve a combinatorial conjecture,
[
18
]
(also see
[
33
]
). In the 2000s, Vakil proved Murphy’s law for Hilbert schemes of positive dimensional subspaces
[
43
]
, and more recently, Jelisiejew proved it for the Hilbert scheme of points
[
25
]
. In the 2000s, Sturmfels suggested that the maximal singularity of
\mathrm{Hilb}^{l}({\mdmathbb{A}}^{3})
is attained at an initial
monomial ideal of the generic configuration of
l
points, which was recently disproved in
[

. For connections to enumerative geometry, we refer to

and for the parity conjecture, we refer to

The latest progress regarding the Briançon-Iarrobino Conjecture was made by Ramkumar and Sammartano in

Lemma 1.14
.

For
J
and
J^{\prime}
as above, we have
t(J,J^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{Card}(B^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}\setminus A^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
h
be the height of
J^{\prime}
. Each point
\beta=(\beta_{y},\beta_{z})
counted by
t(J,J^{\prime})
can be mapped to
(-1,h-1)
and these correspond to distinct elements of
B^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}\setminus A^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}
, although not necessarily all of them. Since the value of
\alpha=(\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z})
used is
(-1,h-1)-\beta=(-1-\beta_{y},h-1-\beta_{z})
, we have
\alpha_{z}=h-1-\beta_{z}<0
, since by definition,
\beta_{z}\geq h
. Hence, it suffices to check that there is a connected component
U
of
(\tilde{J}+\alpha)\setminus\tilde{J^{\prime}}
, which is unbounded but only inside the upper-half-plane. First, we note that
\alpha_{y}=-1-\beta_{y}<0
so there is certainly an unbounded component
U
in the upper-left quadrant. However, by the definition of
\beta
and
h
, we have
\beta\notin\tilde{J}
and
(0,h)\in\tilde{J^{\prime}}
, respectively. The former implies that there is no
({\mathfrak{y}},{\mathfrak{z}})
in
(\tilde{J}+\alpha)
such that
{\mathfrak{y}}\leq-1,{\mathfrak{z}}\leq h-1
, as
\beta+\alpha=(-1,h-1)
. The latter implies that for any
({\mathfrak{y}},{\mathfrak{z}})
such that
{\mathfrak{y}}\geq 0,{\mathfrak{z}}\geq h
, we have
({\mathfrak{y}},{\mathfrak{z}})\in\tilde{J^{\prime}}
. Combining these implies that
U\subset(-\infty,-1]\times[h,\infty)
; thus, this corresponds to an element of
B^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}\setminus A^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}
. Note that for different values of
\beta
we get different values of
\alpha
, so we get
t(J,J^{\prime})
distinct elements of
B^{J,J^{\prime}}_{n}
.
∎

The proof

In this section, we prove the main results.

First, in order to prove Theorem
A
, we need a preparatory lemma and its immediate corollary concerning a lower bound for
t(I_{i},I_{j})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
I
be a
0
-dimensional Borel-fixed ideal in
{\mdmathbb{C}}[x,y,z]
, and let
\alpha=(\alpha_{x},\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z})
be a vector in
{\mdmathbb{Z}}^{3}
such that
\alpha_{x}<0
and
\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z}\geq 0
with
\alpha_{x}+\alpha_{y}+\alpha_{z}\leq-1
. Then, for any minimal generator
\gamma
in
\tilde{I}
, we have
\gamma+\alpha\notin\tilde{I}
.

Proof.

If
\gamma+\alpha\in\tilde{I}
, then by Borel-fixedness and that
\alpha_{x}<0
, we deduce that
\gamma-\mathbf{e}_{x}\in\tilde{I}
, which contradicts the fact that
\gamma
corresponds to a generator of
I
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

For
I
a 0-dimensional Borel-fixed ideal in
{\mdmathbb{C}}[x,y,z]
, with
I=\bigoplus_{i}x^{i}I_{i}
then

\binom{j-i+1}{2}\leq t(I_{i},I_{j}).

We now prove Theorem
A
, following a similar argument as in
[
37
]
, but using our slightly improved inequalities to get a stronger bound which we can use to deduce, for example, the Briançon-Iarrobino Conjecture for
N=3
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Recall that we have the decomposition
I=\bigoplus_{i}x^{i}I_{i}
. Any element of the tangent space
\mathrm{Hom}(I,R/I)
can restrict to a non-zero element of
\mathrm{Hom}(I_{i},S/I_{j})
for some
i,j
, and for basis elements, the restriction determines the original map.

Claim.
Let
i>j
. Then, maps in
\mathrm{Hom}(I_{i},S/I_{j})
with
\alpha
such that
\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{y}+\alpha_{z}\leq i-j-2
correspond to a single bounded connected component, but cannot extend to elements of the tangent space
\operatorname{Hom}(I,R/I)
, i.e., they are zero vectors.

Proof of Claim.

Let
\alpha_{x}=j-i
, then by Lemma
2.1
for any minimal generator
\gamma\in\tilde{I}
of
I
, we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\gamma+\alpha+\mathbf{e}_{y}\notin\tilde{I},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\gamma+\alpha+\mathbf{e}_{x}\notin\tilde{I}.

Therefore, (
2.1
) implies that for generators
\gamma
and
′
whose
y
-coordinates differ by
1
, we have that
\gamma+\alpha
and
{}^{\prime}+\alpha
are in the same component; hence, we have a single connected component in
(I_{i}+(\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z}))\setminus I_{j}
. Also, we note that since
\alpha_{y},\alpha_{z}\geq 0
, the component is bounded.

Similarly, (
2.2
) implies that there is only one connected component in
\tilde{I}_{\alpha}
, since for generators
\gamma
and
′
whose
z
-coordinates differ by 1, we have that
\gamma+\alpha
and
{}^{\prime}+\alpha
are in the same component. However, since
\alpha_{x}<0
, the component is unbounded.
∎

For fixed
i,j
, the number of maps in the claim above is
\binom{i-j}{2}
.

Also, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}\Bigg(\sum_{i=j+1}^{m_{1}}\binom{i-j}{2}\Bigg)=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}{m_{1}-j+1\choose 3}={m_{1}+2\choose 4}.

Thus, by the claim above, Corollary
1.15
, Corollary
2.2
and using (
2.3
) twice, it is straightforward to show

\displaystyle T(I)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hom}(I,R/I)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}\mathrm{hom}(I_{i},S/I_{j})-\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{m_{1}}\binom{i-j}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}\big(l_{i}+l_{j}-t(I_{i},I_{j})\big)-\binom{m_{1}+2}{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}m_{1}l_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}(m_{1}+1)l_{j}-\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{m_{1}-1}t(I_{i},I_{j})-\binom{m_{1}+2}{4}

\displaystyle\leq(2m_{1}+1)l-\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}\sum_{j=i+1}^{m_{1}-1}\binom{j-i+1}{2}-\binom{m_{1}+2}{4}

\displaystyle=(2m_{1}+1)l-2\binom{m_{1}+2}{4},

as required.
∎

Before proving Theorem
B
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

For a Borel-fixed ideal
I=(x^{m_{1}},y^{m_{2}},z^{m_{3}},\text{mixed terms})
in
{\mdmathbb{C}}[x,y,z]
with
{k+2\choose 3}\leq l(I)<{k+3\choose 3}
, we have
m_{1}\leq k
.

Proof.

Suppose that
m_{1}>k
. Then, since
I
is Borel-fixed, we have
m_{3}\geq m_{2}\geq m_{1}>k
. Therefore, each
m_{i}
is at least
k+1
; hence, again using Borel-fixedness,

{\mdmathbb{N}}^{3}\setminus\tilde{I}
contains at least a tetrahedron of size
k+1
. Hence,
the colength of
I
will be at least
{k+3\choose 3}
; contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B
.

For
l={k+2\choose 3}+\Delta
with
0\leq\Delta\leq{k+2\choose 2}-1
, we can write

\displaystyle{}^{\prime}(m_{1})=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial m_{1}}\Bigg(\frac{(4m_{1}+2)(k^{3}+3k^{2}+2k+6\Delta)-m_{1}(m_{1}+2)(m_{1}^{2}-1)}{12}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}(2k^{3}+6k^{2}+4k-2m_{1}^{3}-3m_{1}^{2}+m_{1}+1+12\Delta)>0.

The positivity is due to the inequality
m_{1}\leq k
(Lemma
2.3
).
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

For
l=\binom{k+2}{3}

\displaystyle\psi(k)=

\displaystyle(2k+1){k+2\choose 3}-2{k+2\choose 4}=\frac{(2k+1)(k+2)(k+1)k}{6}-\frac{(k+2)(k+1)k(k-1)}{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(k+2)(k+1)^{2}k}{4}={k+2\choose 2}{k+1\choose 2}=T(\mathfrak{m}^{k}).

The last equality is obtained from
[
5
, Proposition III.4]
or
[

.
Combining this with Theorem

implies the desired result.

∎

Proof of Corollary

Since for
