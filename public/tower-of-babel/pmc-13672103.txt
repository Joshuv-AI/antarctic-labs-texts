Single-molecule chromatin tracing reveals a diversity of megabase heterochromatin domains
Authors: Dania Camila Pulido-Barrera, Jia E. You, Angelo L. Angonezi, Alexander Kuznetsov, Fei Xu, Tobias Verheijen, Ahilya N. Saw, Pavel Kos, Luca Giorgetti, Nacho Molina, David Brückner, Susan E. Mango
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chromosomes fold into distinct domains that regulate transcription, replication and repair. In addition to well-characterized topologically associating domains (TADs) and compartments, the diversity of heterochromatin domains remains poorly defined at the sequence level. Here, using single-molecule tracing of nascent heterochromatin in Caenorhabditis elegans, we identify three classes of megabase-scale domains: (1) sharp-boundary, condensin I-dependent TAD-like domains (TADLs), which arise before zygotic genome activation; (2) similarly sized, but condensin-independent, elegans condensin-independent domains (elCIDs), one of which encompasses the 5S-spliced leader repeat locus; and (3) decompacted, diffuse structures that are abundant in the population but probably missed by crosslinking assays. Condensin I sits at the border of the TADL domain, whereas DNA-binding proteins, active histone marks and maternally active genes flank the elCID domains, suggesting that boundaries are established by distinct mechanisms for different types of domains. Our results uncover previously unrecognized heterochromatin architectures and implicate maternal transcriptional activity in shaping domain boundaries in early embryos in C. elegans.

Main

Recent advances in chromatin biology show that the genome is organized into domains across multiple scales. At the megabase scale, Hi-C revealed TADs, while histone modifications drive segregation into euchromatic and heterochromatic compartments1–4, shaping gene regulation, genome integrity and nuclear organization5–9. Microscopy-based approaches have revealed additional structures—packing domains, Polycomb bodies and the nucleolus—reflecting chromatin density at diverse scales10–15. Packing domains, for instance, do not always coincide with Hi-C-defined TADs or compartments, suggesting complementary modes of genome folding that elude crosslinking-based approaches.

Early models proposed a hierarchical architecture of multi-megabase A/B compartments subdivided into TADs3,16,17. High-resolution 3C-based maps now reveal a more dynamic, multilayered configuration, with CTCF-anchored loops, compartments and finer-scale features coexisting17–19, favoring mechanistic over size-based or contact-based classifications. In higher organisms, loop extrusion by structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) complexes shapes chromatin domains: cohesin generates transient loops within TADs7,20–26, whereas condensin builds interphase domains in vertebrate cells and C. elegans27–29.

In C. elegans, megabase-scale TADs occur on the X chromosome, where a specialized condensin I complex (condensin IDC) generates both TADs and their boundaries22,30. Condensin I also shapes autosomal organization, although clear megabase-scale TADs have not been observed22,23,31. Instead, autosomes feature small, cohesin-dependent TADLs spanning 20–40 kb32. This raises the question of whether larger chromatin domains also exist in C. elegans, like those in vertebrates.

Here, we explore the organization of autosomes at the megabase scale, focusing on a stretch of heterochromatin that undergoes large-scale folding33. Using chromatin tracing to achieve single-chromosome resolution33–35, we discover a diversity of chromatin domains with distinct genetic requirements and developmental timing.

Results

Single-molecule tracing defines well-insulated chromosome domains

We reasoned that this apparent lack of well-defined domains could arise from population averaging across heterogeneous chromatin conformations, including those linked to cell cycle, cell type or development. To address this issue, we used chromatin tracing (100 kb probe spacing; Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), a DNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-based technique, to determine chromatin conformations at the single-molecule level33,34,36. We excluded mitotic nuclei, yielding a dataset composed predominantly of interphase nuclei. We performed unsupervised clustering of single-molecule traces (Methods), revealing groups with similar conformations33,36. In parallel, we quantified insulation and boundary strength using a previously published sliding window approach22. This analysis identified seven distinct conformational clusters (Fig. 1d,e). Three clusters exhibited ~1 Mb domains with sharp boundaries and strong insulation scores (0.5 to −0.4), nearly fourfold higher than the population average (Fig. 1e (clusters C1–C3) and Extended Data Fig. 1e). Clusters C1 and C2 showed the strongest boundaries and together accounted for ~20% of traces; these domains did not coexist within the same trace, instead appearing in an either/or configuration or interacting to form larger domains resembling cluster C4. The remaining clusters displayed weaker but above-average insulation values and distinct boundary positions, underscoring structural variability.

Single-molecule traces from three replicates showed strong t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding overlap, confirming reproducibility (Extended Data Fig. 1d), and downsampled (50%) subsets yielded clustering similar to the full dataset (Extended Data Fig. 1f). Boundary position alternated between ~19.0 and ~19.7 Mb, enriched at both but preferentially at 19.0 (Extended Data Fig. 1g), indicating meaningful biological heterogeneity rather than sampling noise. We conclude that C. elegans chromosomes adopt diverse megabase-scale conformations, including a subset with sharply insulated domains; this heterogeneity probably explains the absence of clear domains in population-averaged maps and supports a model of dynamically regulated chromosome organization, consistent with recent live-imaging studies21,37.

Condensin I is required for a C.elegans TAD-like heterochromatin domain

Megabase-scale genomic domains are often grouped into loop-extrusion-associated domains (for example, TADs) versus compartments arising from preferential interactions between chromatin regions with similar epigenetic states2,4,8,19. In C. elegans, cohesin forms small loops (~20–40 kb)38,39, whereas condensin I folds the genome at a larger scale22,23. Given the megabase size of our domains, we focused on dpy-28 (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b), which encodes a condensin I subunit40,41. dpy-28(s939) is a strong loss-of-function allele impairing condensin I activity and dosage compensation40,42, with DPY-28 undetectable by antibody-based assays in mutant embryos40. Label-free liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis showed that DPY-28 remained detectable but at reduced levels in the mutant (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1). Consistent with condensin’s role in dosage compensation22,23,41,43,44, the most significantly changed proteins were X-linked (Extended Data Fig. 2d–f) and overlapped substantially with genes upregulated upon perturbation of the condensin I subunit DPY-26 (ref. 23). Condensin II remains intact in dpy-28 mutants, probably explaining their ability to undergo mitosis and survive to larval stages45.

At the population level, median distance matrices from three replicates revealed that condensin-deficient embryos displayed an alternating, checkered pattern of longer and shorter distances rather than the continuous domain-like interactions seen in wild type (n = 3432 traces; Fig. 2a–c), with nearly all probe pairs showing significantly increased distances, indicating reduced compaction (Fig. 2b,c). Radius of gyration (RoG) measurements confirmed significantly larger chromosomal volumes in dpy-28 mutants relative to wild type (P = 1.05 × 10−5; Fig. 2d), consistent with global decompaction. Two regions (18.6 and 19.1 Mb) showed increased proximity, indicating a loss of insulation (Fig. 2e), while a poorly insulated region in wild type (20.4 Mb) became more insulated. We performed clustering using the same parameters applied to the wild-type dataset (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 2g,h). We focused on clusters displaying the strongest boundaries in the wild type (Fig. 2f, top row). Two prominent wild-type domains (WT-C1, WT-C2) were differentially affected in dpy-28 mutants: the distal domain (19.7–20.9 Mb; C2) was lost, and based on this condensin dependence, sharp boundaries and size, we refer to it as a TADL. By contrast, the proximal domain (18.2–19.6 Mb; C1) persisted, although slightly decompacted, with weaker boundaries; we refer to this cluster as elCID, which was more abundant in condensin mutants than in the wild type (~14% vs ~8%). The remaining traces resolved into either highly open (clusters C2–C4; ~40%) or compact conformations spanning the region (clusters C5–C6; ~46%) (Fig. 2f, bottom rows).

Given that elCID persisted upon condensin I depletion, we tested whether cohesin also contributed to chromatin organization at this scale. Cohesin-dependent loops are detectable in Hi-C at ~10 kb resolution38,39 but were averaged out at the 100 kb resolution used here (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). The log2(fold change) contact maps showed pronounced alterations upon condensin depletion but minimal changes upon cohesin depletion across multiple chromosomes (Extended Data Fig. 3c–e). Contact probability scaling further supported this distinction: condensin depletion produced a steeper decay, indicating loss of intermediate and long-range contacts, whereas cohesin depletion yielded curves nearly indistinguishable from wild type (Extended Data Fig. 3f,g). Together, these analyses indicate that cohesin organizes chromatin at smaller scales but not at megabase resolution.

H3K9 methylation compacts chromosomes during large-scale heterochromatin formation46,50,51. We measured median distances and RoG in wild type and met-2; set-25 mutant embryos, which revealed increased distances across the region in mutant embryos, indicating reduced compaction (n = 4078 traces; Fig. 3b,c and Extended Data Fig. 4e). RoG analysis confirmed significantly larger chromosome volumes in mutants (P = 7.28 × 10−8), comparable to dpy-28 embryos (P = 0.429) (Fig. 3d), and downsampling controls ruled out sample-size bias (Extended Data Fig. 4f,g). These results show that H3K9 methylation is required for global chromatin compaction, affecting both condensin-dependent and independent domains, but is not required for either elCID or the TADL per se.

Clustering the pooled traces revealed genotype-specific differences in chromatin domain distribution (Fig. 4b–d and Extended Data Fig. 5c–h), with cluster composition differing significantly between genotypes (χ210 = 541.95, P = 4.7 × 10−110; Cramér’s V = 0.23). Standardized residual analysis identified strong enrichment or depletion of specific clusters within individual genotypes, indicating that distinct folding states were preferentially associated with different genetic backgrounds (Extended Data Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 2), visualized as signed −log10(false discovery rate (FDR)) enrichment scores relative to wild type (Methods and Fig. 4c). Observed and expected counts, standardized residuals and FDR-adjusted P values for all genotype–cluster combinations are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

The most pronounced difference was cluster C4, the TADL domain: present in wild type, enriched in H3K9me mutants but nearly absent in dpy-28 mutants (χ2 residual = –11.61, FDR < 10⁻6). Cluster C3, the elCID (18.2–19.6 Mb), was enriched in condensin mutants (χ2 residual = 5.3) but unaffected by H3K9me loss, highlighting that structurally similar domains can arise through distinct regulatory factors. Cluster C2, a more decompacted conformation, was significantly enriched in dpy-28 mutants (χ2 residual = 9.3) and roughly half as frequent in wild type and H3K9me mutants (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 5e).

We calculated the fraction of nuclei assigned to each cluster (Extended Data Fig. 5I), ranging from under 1% to over 40%; C3 and C4 were present in 6.4% and 9.4% of wild-type nuclei, respectively. Traces within each cluster share overall structural similarity rather than identical conformation, and individual traces probably vary in folding extent, including partially compacted or intermediate configurations. To confirm that the genotype-dependent enrichment patterns described above were not sampling artifacts, we performed three independent downsampling replicates (Extended Data Fig. 6a–i). These replicates reproduced the near-absence of C4 in condensin mutants (DS1 = 54, DS2 = 11, DS3 = 38 traces), supporting a genuine structural difference rather than a stochastic fluctuation in cluster assignment. This heterogeneity, together with the modest fraction of nuclei in any single state, may explain why these conformations are obscured in population-averaged maps and resolvable only through single-molecule clustering.

Across all three genotypes, the predominant configuration was cluster C1, a highly interactive domain spanning the entire region with intermediate folding and no clear boundaries (Fig. 4d), accounting for ~40% of traces per genotype. Subtle differences were evident: H3K9me mutants showed sharper proximity decline with genomic distance, consistent with a role in stabilizing long-range interactions, while dpy-28 mutants showed a checkered interaction pattern (also visible in compact domains like C3), weaker in wild type and smoother in H3K9me mutants. Together, these results reveal the genetic requirements of distinct domains at both large and finer scales.

To assess chromatin organization within elCID and TADL across conditions, we quantified pairwise inter-region distances across all loci within each domain on chromosome V (elCID, 18.2–19.6 Mb; TADL, 19.7–20.8 Mb) in wild-type and mutant embryos (Methods and Fig. 4e–h). The elCID region showed modest increases in median distances (0.62 µm vs 0.59 µm in wild type; Fig. 4e,f), indicating relative structural stability, whereas the TADL region displayed pronounced, reproducible decompaction (Fig. 4g) with significance maintained across nearly all iterations (Fig. 4h). These results indicate that TADL loci exhibit canonical, SMC-dependent TAD behavior, whereas elCID persists largely independently of condensin.

Domain-like chromatin organization across multiple chromosomal regions

To determine whether additional condensin I-independent domains exist, we extended chromatin tracing ~1.3 Mb upstream, uncovering a well-defined domain spanning 17.0–17.9 Mb, maintained in wild-type, H3K9me and condensin I mutant embryos. We refer to this region as the 5SL elCID domain, as it contains 5S rRNA and SL trans-spliced leader repeat genes. Unsupervised co-clustering of the 5SL elCID across genotypes, with normalized age distribution, identified discrete conformations detected in all genotypes but at differing frequencies (χ210 = 541.95, P = 4.7 × 10−110; Cramer’s V = 0.23; Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 7d). Cluster C2 was reduced in condensin mutants (22.9% vs 34.5% in wild type) and cluster C3 in H3K9me mutants (18.7% vs 26.7%; Fig. 5c,d), but no cluster was uniquely lost in either background, indicating that 5SL elCID architecture was largely preserved despite loss of condensin I or H3K9me3.

The 5S rRNA and SL1 loci are transcribed by RNA polymerase III and II, respectively. To assess whether the 5SL domain preferentially interacts with other regions, we analyzed intrachromosomal Hi-C contacts from a published dataset31. Contacts were observed with heterochromatin-rich regions on chromosomes X and III, but no enrichment for specific loci (Fig. 5e). Despite its role in ribosome biogenesis, the 5S rDNA locus did not associate with the nucleolus (median distance, 1,301 nm; 98.6% of measurements, >300 nm; FIB-1 marker; Fig. 5f,h and Extended Data Fig. 7e), indicating that the 5SL elCID is both spatially and genomically insulated. RNA FISH with immunostaining showed that most nuclei contained two distinct Pol III foci mapping to the 5S rRNA genes, which did not merge into one domain and did not always colocalize with H3K9me3 (Fig. 5f,g and Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). H3K9me3 was reduced within foci but enriched in surrounding chromatin, indicating that Pol III foci occupy transcriptionally permissive microenvironments adjacent to, but not embedded within, heterochromatin (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 7g).

Finally, we analyzed published chromatin tracing data at comparable resolution33, focusing on a 2 Mb region in another heterochromatic context (Extended Data Fig. 7h–j). This region lacked prominent domain structure at the population level (Extended Data Fig. 7h), but clustering revealed two clusters with clear boundaries, accounting for ~45% of traces, alongside highly extended conformations reflecting substantial cell-to-cell variability (C2–C3; Extended Data Fig. 7i,j). Together, these findings suggest that megabase-scale domain organization is a widespread feature of heterochromatin, with some regions resembling TADs and others elCIDs.

Distinct genomic and chromatin features at the boundaries of TADL versus elCID domains

In vertebrate euchromatin, CTCF marks many TAD boundaries. C. elegans lacks an obvious CTCF ortholog, raising the question of what serves as a boundary element here. We analyzed published chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq) data for candidate factors52 and showed that TADL and elCID boundaries had different attributes. At the TADL boundary (~19.7 Mb), we observed peaks for all five subunits diagnostic of condensin I (Fig. 6a), raising the possibility that autosomal boundaries, like those on the X chromosome, are established by condensin I, analogous to the condensin IDC complex22,30. This boundary was also marked by a HOT site, a break in H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 and peaks of DNA-binding proteins CEH-30, LIN-13, MEP-1 and CEH-26, previously linked to condensin binding52 (Fig. 6b,c), although only condensin I co-occupancy was unique to this position. At the elCID boundary (18.1 Mb), peaks of CEH-30, LIN-13 and MEP-1 coincided with strong H3K36me3, depletion of H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 and robust maternal but minimal zygotic expression (Fig. 6b–e). A secondary boundary at ~19.0 Mb showed similar but more broadly distributed features, with less distinct transcription factor occupancy, and a boundary at ~16.8 Mb, marking the start of the 5SL elCID, showed the same pattern: reduced repressive marks, enriched H3K36me3, weak transcription factor occupancy and strong maternal expression. These attributes suggest distinct boundary elements for each domain type: condensin I for TADL, both here and on the X chromosome, whereas chromatin marks and gene activity may define elCID boundaries.

The three boundaries (18.1, 19.0 and 19.7 Mb) were enriched for repeats relative to the genome average, with region-specific enrichment for long terminal repeat elements and DNA transposons at 18.1 Mb and satellite, simple and rolling-circle repeats at 19.7 Mb (Supplementary Table 3). Given their proposed role in boundary formation53, we also examined C2H2 zinc finger motifs and found modest enrichment of ZTF-6 near the 19.0 Mb boundary, confirmed by ChIP–seq at 19.0 Mb and 19.7 Mb with additional peaks near 18.1 Mb (Supplementary Table 4). These data suggest that different domain types rely on distinct factors and chromatin attributes to form boundaries.

Domain compaction and elCID, but not TADL domains, emerge progressively during development

To examine how heterochromatin domains arise during development, we analyzed chromosome structure across five embryonic stages: 2–4 cells (pre-zygotic genome activation (ZGA)), 5–8 cells (minor ZGA), 9–40 cells (gastrulation onset, first major ZGA) and 41–80 and 81–140 cells, coinciding with progressive H3K9 methylation accumulation50,51, using co-clustering and power-law scaling analysis to quantify compaction over time.

The TADL was detectable before ZGA in ~30% of wild-type and H3K9me mutant embryos (Fig. 7a). We analyzed published single-cell RNA-seq data54 at the same developmental stage and detected no transcriptional activity across the examined region, while during the minor ZGA, a few loci became weakly expressed in this region (Fig. 6d). The condensin-independent elCID domains became visible at distinct stages. The 5SL elCID was detected at the minor ZGA (cluster C2, ~40% of wild-type traces; Fig. 7b) in all three genotypes, although it did not fully extend across the region in condensin or H3K9me mutants at earlier stages (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b), suggesting independence from transcription, absent in this region before the 9-cell stage (Fig. 6d). The second elCID emerged at the 9–40-cell stage across genotypes (Fig. 7c), becoming less frequent suggesting a transient structure; its higher transcriptional activity and emergence coinciding with the onset of transcription suggest transcription may contribute to its formation.

Compaction and compartment formation occurred in wild type but failed in both H3K9me and condensin I mutants (Fig. 7d–f), with long-range interactions visible within and between the second elCID and TADL domains in wild type (Fig. 7d, magenta box). To quantify folding, we analyzed the scaling of mean spatial distances with genomic distance (R = a × Ns), where the exponent (s) reflects folding and the step size (a) reflects packing density. In wild type, scaling exponents declined from the 4-cell stage (s = 0.19 → 0.13; Extended Data Fig. 8c–g), consistent with progressive compaction. H3K9me mutants remained elevated throughout development, indicating persistent decompaction, while condensin mutants showed lower exponents than wild type early on (s = 0.16 vs 0.19) but converged to wild-type levels by the 80-cell stage. Per-trace 95% confidence intervals were relatively narrow, indicating consistent scaling despite moderate heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 5). The step sizes remained higher in both mutants at 1 Mb, reflecting expanded chromatin, and only condensin mutants showed elevated step sizes at 100 kb, pointing to a unique defect in local folding (Extended Data Fig. 8c,d). RoG measurements confirmed enlarged chromosome volumes in both mutants from the 9-cell stage onward (Extended Data Fig. 8h). Together, these results show that in the wild type, elCID and the TADL domain are compacted and appear to form a joint compartment over time (Fig. 7d).

Lastly, we tested whether these chromatin defects could be explained by nuclear size. H3K9me mutants maintained nuclear volumes comparable to wild type, indicating the chromatin changes are not caused by nuclear scaling (Extended Data Fig. 8i); dpy-28 embryos, by contrast, had significantly smaller nuclei despite increased chromatin volume, confirming these effects are not secondary to nuclear size.

Single-cell chromatin domains persist despite loss of population TADLs and arise from cooperative long-range interactions

A puzzling attribute of TADs in other animals is that structures similar to TADL domains persist in single-molecule traces of SMC mutants but vanish in population-averaged maps47,55. Similarly, single-molecule traces from condensin I and H3K9me mutant embryos retained domain-like structures (Extended Data Fig. 9a–e). We quantified boundary probabilities (Methods) and found elevated probabilities in wild type at 19.0 Mb (~20%) and 19.7–19.9 Mb (~15%), matching the boundaries identified by clustering and marking the elCID-to-TADL transition (Fig. 8a). These boundary probability percentages are similar to values reported for human cells47,55. In dpy-28 mutants, boundary probabilities were evenly distributed across the region (Fig. 8a, green line), indicating increased variability in boundary positioning. In H3K9me mutant embryos, boundary positions remained centered near 19.0 Mb and 19.8 Mb, with slightly reduced probabilities, and overall boundary strength was unchanged (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, P = 0.89; Extended Data Fig. 9b,c), suggesting a minor role for H3K9me in reinforcing boundaries. To distinguish biologically relevant variability from intrinsic polymer fluctuations, we applied a statistical framework modeling chromatin as a Gaussian process defined by a pairwise interaction matrix57,58. This framework provides a statistical null baseline for features, such as boundary probability and excess variability, that cannot be explained by generic polymer behavior alone. We generated three baselines of increasing complexity (10,000 conformations each; Fig. 8b–d and Extended Data Figs. 9f–h and 10a–d). Even the simplest case—a free polymer with nearest-neighbor interactions—produced apparent boundaries and insulation, calibrated to the experimental RoG, but failed to capture distance scaling and boundary probabilities (Fig. 8b,e,f and Extended Data Fig. 10c). Adding uniformly attractive long-range interactions improved agreement with wild-type scaling and compaction (root mean squared error (RMSE) reduced from 0.186 to 0.057) but still failed to reproduce boundary probabilities (Fig. 8c and Extended Data Fig. 10b,c), indicating that structured, locus-specific interactions are required.

As a third case, we used Distance Matrix to Ensemble of Structures (DIMES)58 to generate ensembles matching experimental mean pairwise distances (RMSE = 0 by design; Fig. 8d and Extended Data Fig. 10d). DIMES recapitulated boundary positions but with a shallower profile and significantly underestimated variance, particularly for proximal pairs (Fig. 8d,g–i and Extended Data Fig. 10e), demonstrating that average structure and polymer fluctuations alone cannot explain the heterogeneity observed in vivo. To determine whether specific biological perturbations alter this excess variability, we applied DIMES to mutant conditions (Extended Data Fig. 10f,g). In H3K9me mutant embryos, normalized variance remained comparable to wild type (Fig. 8j), indicating that H3K9 methylation does not substantially contribute to chromatin heterogeneity. In condensin mutant embryos, normalized variance was reduced, particularly at short genomic distances (Fig. 8k), identifying condensin I as a major source of structured chromatin variability. Normalized variance increased during development in all genotypes (Extended Data Fig. 10h) despite differences in compaction, suggesting that chromatin heterogeneity grows progressively and is only revealed by single-molecule measurements.

Introducing enhanced intra-domain interactions showed that contacts must be very strong to produce robust single-molecule domains with distinct boundary signals (Extended Data Fig. 10i–n). Simulating domain pairs at varying separations revealed that closely spaced domains cooperatively reinforce boundaries (Extended Data Fig. 10j,m,n), explaining why elCID boundary probability is reduced upon condensin loss (Fig. 8a): although elCID itself is condensin-independent, the neighboring condensin-dependent TADL domain normally reinforces its boundary.

Discussion

This study identifies megabase-scale domains in C. elegans heterochromatin, with distinct genetic requirements that define different categories. TADL domains are highly folded, with boundary probabilities comparable to mammalian cells47,55,56, and require condensin I. elCID domains resemble TADLs but are largely independent of condensin I or H3K9me. Although they depend on distinct factors for biogenesis, both domain types converge to promote compartment-like heterochromatin in older embryos.

The elCID and TADL domains differed in boundary composition: the left TADL boundary was marked by condensin I (the right coincides with the chromosome end), whereas elCID boundaries were associated with active histone marks. Both have precedent: condensin IDC is required for TAD boundary formation on the X chromosome22,31, while active genes and histone marks have been implicated in domain formation in C. elegans and Drosophila32,59,60. A surprising feature of the elCID boundaries is that they correlate with maternal rather than ongoing zygotic gene expression, consistent with the maternal inheritance of H3K36me3 (ref. 61). This raises the possibility that elCID boundaries rely on maternal transcriptional activity.

TADL domains were detectable before ZGA, indicating they initiate independently of transcription. Earlier studies in other organisms reported conflicting relationships between TADs and ZGA62–66. Our findings suggest that these discrepancies may partly reflect population averaging, which can mask structures present in only a subset of chromosomes. In C. elegans, TADL represented a substantial but still minority fraction of traces before ZGA (~28%). By contrast, the second elCID domain appeared coincident with zygotic transcription, suggesting that this domain class may rely on transcriptional activity.

Previous studies concluded that C. elegans lacks TADs, based on the absence of a CTCF ortholog and the failure to detect sharp boundaries at the population level23,67. Our data suggest instead that this absence reflects conformational variability rather than a genuine lack of domains. It remains to be tested whether this reinterpretation extends to euchromatic regions or other chromosomes.

Boundary formation in other systems typically involves cooperation among multiple insulator proteins, often zinc finger-containing factors53,68–73. LIN-13, observed at the TADL boundary, co-occupies the genome with condensin I (ref. 52), and is also a binding partner of HPL-2/HP1 (refs. 74,75), raising the possibility that LIN-13 functions as both a boundary factor and heterochromatin organizer. Our motif analysis found a modest enrichment of a single factor, ZTF-6, near the elCID boundary. Whether any of these factors directly contribute to boundary formation remains to be determined.

One elCID domain encompassed the 5S and SL leader repeat locus. At the sequence level, these repeats are flanked by H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin, potentially analogous to the heterochromatin surrounding the nucleolus. The 5SL elCID differs from the nucleolus, however, in that the two loci remain spatially separated within nuclei. An intriguing possibility is that the 5S locus is packaged within a domain, analogous to other ribosomal genes.

Aside from elCID and TADL, most traces displayed less defined structures, consistent with reports that TADs account for only a small fraction of chromosome conformations in mammalian cells in vivo (≤3%)21,37. Such configurations are poorly captured by Hi-C but accessible through single-molecule imaging. Our statistical polymer baselines show that generic fluctuations can produce apparent boundaries but fail to account for measured boundary probabilities and excess variance. This discrepancy was genotype-dependent, with condensin loss reducing heterogeneity, unlike H3K9me loss, identifying condensin-mediated interactions as a major source of structured variability.

Our tracing focused on heterochromatic regions of chromosome V, providing a foundation for future genome-wide comparisons. However, a limitation of the study is the 100 kb probe spacing, which resolves megabase-scale architecture but not the finer features that coexist within these domains. Our statistical polymer framework offers a non-mechanistic baseline for detecting structural variability, and our TADL terminology similarly captures structural and genetic dependence on condensin I, but future studies are needed to model chromosome organization and to uncover their functions.

Methods

Worm strains

C. elegans strains used in this study included the N2 Bristol strain (wild type), GW638 met-2(n4256) set-25(n5021) III and TY4381 dpy-28(s939)/qC1 [dpy-19(e1259) glp-1(q339) qIs26] III. All strains were cultured at 20 °C on nematode growth medium plates seeded with OP50.

Genomic data

Hi-C data

Hi-C data for C. elegans embryos (GSE206065)31 were used to generate contact matrices at 100 kb resolution, normalized using the iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition method; regions excluded from tracing were similarly omitted from Hi-C plots. Pearson correlation confirmed strong agreement between averaged tracing distances and normalized Hi-C contact frequencies (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Hi-C boundary information for Fig. 1a was taken from prior work (GSM1556154)22.

To evaluate cohesin and condensin perturbation, Hi-C datasets from GSE199723 (ref. 23) were binned at 100 kb resolution to match the tracing scale. Distance-dependent contact probability and log2(fold change) relative to wild type were computed for genomic separations of ~100 kb–2 Mb on chromosome V and the X chromosome.

Circos plot

To visualize interchromosomal contacts for the region of interest (ROI) on chromosome V, we generated circular plots using the pyCircos Python library. Hi-C balanced contact matrices from GEO accession GSE168803 (ref. 31) were loaded at 10 kb resolution with the Cooler Python library. Interchromosomal contacts were identified and statistically evaluated using FitHiC2 (ref. 78), which estimates expected contact probabilities and assigns significance (q values) to each interaction. Significant interactions were defined as those with q < 10⁻3 and were retained for downstream visualization. Analyses were restricted to the ROI on chromosome V. Autosomes and the X chromosome were included in the circular layout, each represented as a distinct segment. Significant interchromosomal interactions were displayed as curved links colored by the target chromosome. Insulation scores were computed from the same Hi-C data and shown as an additional track together with genomic features annotated from the WormBase WBPS18 canonical gene set (PRJNA13758) to provide contextual information for the observed contacts.

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

Raw single-cell RNA-seq counts (GSE83523)54 were aggregated into pseudobulk samples by developmental range (0–1, 2–4, 5–8, 9–40, 41–80 and 81–140 cells) and replicate, matching the ranges used for chromatin tracing. Differential expression was assessed in DESeq2, using the 0–1-cell stage as reference to minimize maternal transcript influence; log2(fold changes) were shrunken with lfcShrink79, and likelihood ratio tests identified genes with significant expression changes across stages. Resulting log2(fold changes), normalized expression values and adjusted P values were used for downstream analyses.

CHIP–seq data analysis

ChIP–seq data for H3K9me3 (GSE2272039)76 were downloaded and visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). To characterize chromatin state across the chromosome V ROI, histone ChIP–seq bedGraph tracks for H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 (modENCODE_5163) and H3K36me3 (modENCODE_5165) were obtained from WormBase and smoothed using a 2 kb rolling window before visualization. Chromatin domain annotations were incorporated from a previous publication77. Active and regulated domains were displayed as semi-transparent genomic spans over regions enriched for H3K36me3 and H3K9me3/H3K27me3, respectively.

Transcription factors enriched at condensin I-IDC binding sites in early embryos were selected based on the prior analysis52. The five most enriched transcription factors were CEH-30, LIN-13, MEP-1, CEH-26 and HLH-1. Processed ChIP–seq signal and peak tracks for these factors were downloaded from WormBase, corresponding to the following GEO datasets: CEH-30 (GSE25808), LIN-13 (GSE25804), MEP-1 (GSE25800), CEH-26 (GSE37798) and HLH-1 (GSE25784). MACS-called peak files for condensin subunits CAPG-1, DPY-26 (kleisin), DPY-28 (hCAPD2), DPY-27 (SMC4) and MIX-1 (SMC2) were downloaded from GEO (GSE45678) and used for visualization52. In addition, ZTF-6 ChIP–seq signal (fold change over control) was obtained from GSE257069 and used for plotting. Given that chromatin tracing was performed at 100 kb resolution, whereas the ChIP–seq datasets have kilobase-scale resolution (2 kb), these comparisons allow assessment of relative enrichment within traced regions.

Repeat and zinc finger motif analysis

Repeat annotations for chromosome V (UCSC RepeatMasker, ce11, Repbase80) were used to calculate per-1 kb-bin repeat density (fraction of bases overlapping annotated repeats) for each repeat class and combined. Rolling mean densities were computed over 150 kb windows; densities within ±75 kb of each ROI center (19.0 Mb, 19.75 Mb) were compared against background density estimated from 10,000 randomly sampled genomic positions.

For motif analysis, ±75 kb windows around each ROI were subdivided into 2 kb segments and scanned for 18 C2H2 zinc finger motifs (JASPAR Core) using the MEME Suite, against 450 random 2 kb background regions. The ZTF-6 motif was further scanned genome-wide across chromosome V using FIMO.

Proteomics sample preparation and LC–MS/MS analysis

Embryos were dissected as for tracing experiments, flash-frozen and lysed (5% SDS, 10 mM TCEP, 0.1 M TEAB) by sonication (PIXUL, Active Motif), heated (95 °C, 10 min) and alkylated (20 mM iodoacetamide). Proteins were digested with S-Trap micro columns (Protifi) using sequencing-grade trypsin (1:25 w/w, Promega; 1 h, 47 °C), and eluted peptides were dried under vacuum.

Peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid/0.02% DDM and analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a timsTOF Ultra 2 (Bruker, CaptiveSpray source) coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC (Thermo), separated on a C18 column (100 µm × 30 cm, 1.5 µm ReproSil Saphir; 60 °C, 0.4 µl min−1) over a 2–95% acetonitrile gradient (~35 min). The mass spectrometer ran in dia-PASEF mode (100–1,700 m/z; eight scans × three ion-mobility windows, 400–1,000 m/z, 25 Da isolation; 1,600 V capillary voltage, 200 °C dry temperature), with collision energy ramped from 59 eV to 20 eV across the ion-mobility range.

Raw data were processed in Spectronaut (v.20.3, directDIA) against the C. elegans UniProt proteome (26,696 sequences) plus contaminants. Fragment-group intensities were analyzed in ProteoFlux v.1.8.0 (log-transformed, median-normalized, missing values imputed with lc_conmed), and differential abundance was tested using limma with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (Supplementary Table 1).

Chromatin tracing

Tracing was conducted with probe sets at 100 kb resolution, using chromatin tracing as previously described33,34.

Sample preparation

Gravid young adult worms were dissected in double-distilled water to isolate embryos. The embryos were then transferred onto round cover slips pre-coated with poly-L-lysine for better adhesion. Embryos were fixed for 5 min in a solution of 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 0.05% Triton X-100. The slides were placed on dry ice and freeze-cracked35.

For dpy-28(s939), homozygous adults were identified by selecting worms with a wild-type, non-roller phenotype and confirming the absence of GFP signal. Embryos were collected from these verified homozygous mutant mothers, thus ensuring the absence of detectable maternal contribution (Extended Data Fig. 2a)40. The dpy-28(s939) allele has been previously characterized as a strong loss-of-function mutation that impairs condensin I activity40,42, although residual condensin activity may be partially retained.

Primary probe design

Probe design and synthesis methods were as previously described35. DNA sequence was extracted from the C. elegans genome assembly (ce11). Primary probes, each 102 nt in length, target consecutive 100 kb regions covering the distal right arm of chromosome V. Each primary probe sequence contains four parts: 5′ PCR-priming sequence (20 nt), genome homologous sequence (42 nt), readout sequence (20 nt) and 3′ PCR-priming sequence (20 nt) from prior work81. The OligoArray 2.1 (ref. 82) parameters used were: targeting region length, 42 nt; melting temperature, 78–100 °C; no internal secondary structure with melting temperature greater than 76 °C; no cross-hybridization with melting temperature greater than 76 °C. GC content 30–90% and sequences do not contain consecutive repeats of seven or more identical nucleotides. OligoArray v.2.1 was run on sciCORE for high-performance computing at the University of Basel. The 42 nt homologous sequences were further blasted by NCBI BLAST+ v.2.9.0 for specificity over the C. elegans genome.

Hybridization protocol

After freeze-crack fixation, samples were rinsed in PBS, washed three times in PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 and treated with RNase A (0.05 mg ml−1, 37 °C, 30 min) to digest RNA. Samples were then pre-hybridized (2× SSC, 10% dextran sulfate, 0.1% Tween-20, 50% formamide; 47 °C, 1 h). Primary probes, diluted 1:100 in hybridization buffer, were added and incubated for 10 min, followed by denaturation on a hot metal surface (85 °C, 10 min) and hybridization in a humid chamber for ≥16 h at 47 °C. Post-hybridization washes consisted of 1 h in 2× SSC/50% formamide, followed by two 30 min washes in 2× SSC and two in 0.5× SSC, all at 47 °C. Secondary probes (8 nM in 2× SSC/25% ethylene carbonate) were hybridized for 30 min, washed and imaged (561 nm or 647 nm). Across sequential rounds, displacement oligonucleotides removed bound secondary probes while new secondary probes for subsequent regions were introduced concurrently33,36.

Microscope imaging pipeline

Imaging used a widefield microscope (Ti2-Crest, Nikon) with a custom microfluidics system for secondary probe delivery36,43. Embryos were stained with DAPI, and fiducial beads (488 nm) were added for alignment. Primary probe and DAPI signals were captured per field of view, followed by primary probe photobleaching and sequential secondary probe imaging rounds. Tetraspeck beads were imaged each round for channel alignment. Average localization precision was ~50 nm33,34.

Imaging analysis

After tracing, the acquired images underwent foci fitting as previously described33,34. For image segmentation and chromatin tracing, MATLAB was used, implementing modified methods outlined in prior studies33,36. Initially, DAPI fluorescence was used to identify nuclei within each embryo. Background signals were subtracted, and noise was removed. The images were then converted to binary format, distance-transformed and processed using watershed segmentation, resulting in volumetric masks that delineated individual nuclei. Chromosome territories were segmented in a similar fashion, with the nuclear mask excluding signals outside the nuclei. This process produced a volumetric representation in which each volume corresponded to a distinct chromosome. Chromatin tracing was subsequently executed within these defined volumes using the nearest-neighbor approach as previously described33.

Median distance matrices used traces with ≥7 detected regions, averaged across replicates (which showed strong correlation). Regions 19.4, 19.5 and 20.6 Mb were excluded owing to undetectable hybridization signal; in H3K9me(−) datasets, region 19.0 Mb showed reduced coverage from a hybridization artifact and is shown as NaN where median distance could not be computed. After filtering, 165 wild-type (4,022 nuclei), 89 H3K9me(−) (3,547 nuclei) and 154 condensin(−) (5,715 nuclei) embryos were analyzed.

Detection efficiency was quantified from a binary detection matrix, which denotes successful detection of a locus in a chromosome trace. Average probe recovery was defined as the mean fraction of traces in which each locus was detected. Trace completeness was defined as the fraction of loci detected per trace, and the median trace completeness was reported for each dataset. After exclusion of low-quality loci, average probe recovery was 53.7% for wild type, 55.1% for H3K9me(−) and 44.9% for condensin(−). Median trace completeness was 51.9%, 55.6% and 40.7% for wild type, H3K9me(−) and condensin(−), respectively.

Chromosome conformation analysis

Pearson correlation between replicates

For each genotype, a Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the mean pairwise spatial distance consistency between regions and across replicates. The MATLAB function corrplot was used for these analyses.

Visualization of individual traces

To visualize individual genomic region traces in three dimensions, we used the scatter3 function in MATLAB. Each trace was represented in a 3D space, with the X, Y and Z parameters corresponding to the coordinates of each point in the trace.

Insulation analysis

Insulation scores were calculated following previous work22, with modifications for tracing data. Mean distances were first normalized for genomic distance—accounting for non-consecutive hybridization regions—by fitting the data to a power-law function reflecting the expected relationship between spatial proximity and genomic distance34. A 200 kb sliding window was applied along the interaction matrix diagonal to average interactions across bins, with the mean signal assigned to the corresponding tracing region; edge bins were excluded, as no data were available for the first and last regions owing to insulation box constraints.

Scores were normalized per chromosome as the log2 ratio of each bin’s score to the chromosome-wide mean, allowing comparison of local insulation relative to overall organization. Local minima (‘valleys’), representing high insulation, were identified with a delta vector (the difference between mean insulation values 100 kb upstream and downstream of each bin), with valleys detected at zero-crossings. Boundary strength was quantified as the difference in delta values between the local maximum (left) and minimum (right), following prior work22; bins with boundary strength of ≥0.1 were classified as boundaries.

Boundary probability

Traces with ≥25 regions were used to generate a mean distance matrix, with linear interpolation across single missing points. Insulation analysis (300 kb insulation box, 200 kb delta box; boundary strength of ≥0.5) was applied per trace, and boundary probability was calculated across all traces, following previous publications47,55. Boundary strength and probability distributions were compared between genotypes using the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Clustering

Clustering was conducted as previously described33,36. Pairwise distance vectors from each trace were assembled into a matrix; regions 2, 11, 17, 18 and 29 were excluded genome-wide owing to low detection. Matrices were normalized (LogNormalize, ScaleData; in Seurat) before dimensionality reduction to remove global compaction differences while preserving relative folding topology.

Clustering used Louvain community detection (in Seurat). Resolution was selected by systematically sweeping the Louvain resolution parameter and evaluating clustering quality using graph modularity and subsampling stability (adjusted Rand index); both metrics exhibited a broad optimum across intermediate resolutions (~0.8–1.2), with higher resolutions primarily subdividing clusters with similar average structures or generating small fragmentary clusters. A conservative resolution of 0.8 was therefore selected for wild-type analyses, preserving major structural states while avoiding over-partitioning and enabling comparison across datasets. Robustness was confirmed on two independent random 50% subsamples of wild-type traces, without replacement, clustered at resolution 1.0 (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Clusters within each dataset were ordered by boundary strength; resolution-sweep statistics are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

Median matrix comparison

Matrices from different genotypes were subtracted from one another. To test for significance, we performed a one-tailed Welch’s t-test on all pairwise distance measurements for each interaction. Unless otherwise noted, all mutant comparisons were performed relative to the wild-type dataset shown in Fig. 1.

Downsampling the chromosome between genotypes

To compare traces across genotypes (wild-type, H3K9me mutant, condensin mutant) without confounding by trace count or age distribution, chromosome ages were binned into five-age intervals, retaining only bins common to all three conditions. For each shared bin, traces were randomly downsampled to match the minimum count across genotypes (deterministic where the limiting genotype had all available traces); conclusions were unchanged across independent resampling replicates.

Iterative downsampling and Wilcoxon testing of spatial distances

To assess differences in spatial distance distributions between conditions, we performed repeated random downsampling followed by statistical testing using chromosome traces derived from the age-matched downsampling described above. Distances were computed across all loci within the specified elCID or TADL domains without restricting traces based on cluster assignment. For each genotype, distances were randomly subsampled (n = 1,000 per iteration), and the procedure was repeated across 100 independent iterations. In each iteration, we computed the median pairwise distance for loci within the TADL or elCID domains and evaluated pairwise differences between datasets using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

For each comparison, we quantified the fraction of iterations yielding P < 0.05 (reported as significance), which reflects the robustness of the observed differences across random subsets of the data.

RoG

The RoG was calculated for chromosome traces containing a minimum of 25 regions, as previously described33,47, with genotype differences assessed by t-test.

Co-clustering and chi-squared test for co-clusters

Age-matched, downsampled traces from N2, condensin(−) and H3K9me(−) embryos were pooled and clustered jointly (see ‘Clustering’) to identify structural states shared across genotypes as well as genotype-specific states. Resolution was selected by sweeping values and evaluating modularity and subsampling stability (adjusted Rand index); clustering quality plateaued at a resolution of 0.5, as higher resolutions mainly fragmented similar structural classes without improving modularity or stability, so 0.5 was used for downstream analyses. Traces were then separated by genotype to visualize cluster distributions. For the 5SL elCID domain, traces were filtered to ≥5 detected regions and clustered at a resolution of 0.35, yielding four clusters.

Differences in cluster distribution across genotypes were assessed using Pearson’s chi-squared test of independence on genotype–cluster contingency tables (chi-squared statistic was computed following a previous publication36; all expected cell counts >5), with Cramér’s V quantifying effect size. Standardized residuals identified genotype–cluster combinations contributing most to the global association, indicating both direction and magnitude of enrichment or depletion; per-cluster enrichment relative to wild type (N2) was further tested by a 2 × 2 chi-squared test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Enrichment scores for visualization were calculated as signed −log10(FDR), signed by the standardized residual direction (positive, enrichment; negative, depletion relative to N2).

Nuclear fractions were calculated by first assigning each traced allele to its parent nucleus, identified by mapping the allele’s coordinates onto the DAPI-based nuclear segmentation mask. Traces were grouped by nucleus identity (sample, field of view and nucleus label); nuclei with more than two associated territories were excluded as probable segmentation artifacts. The fraction of nuclei in each state, and the fraction showing mixed conformations, was calculated separately for each genotype.

Power-law fitting

To compare scaling parameters across datasets, we performed log–log linear regressions (log(D) = log(a) + s × log(G), where D is mean spatial distance and G is genomic distance) to estimate the scaling exponent s and step size a at 1 Mb and 100 kb. Standard errors from regression confidence intervals were propagated to step-size uncertainties (100 kb value computed as a × (0.1)s), and pairwise z-tests compared parameters between datasets using their estimated values and standard errors, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (significance threshold of P < 0.05 after correction).

To assess variability across traces, scaling exponents were also estimated independently per trace by log–log regression, with mean and 95% confidence intervals calculated across traces per condition (two-sided t-distribution; Supplementary Table 5).

Microscopy experiments

Antibody staining

Samples were fixed with 1% PFA (5 min), followed by cold methanol (5 min, –20 °C), then washed three times in PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 (10 min each) and twice in PBS, all at 22 °C room temperature. Samples were blocked in 4% BSA/PBS (30 min, room temperature), then incubated with ChromoTek GFP-Booster (gb2AF488) or anti-H3K9me3 (ab8898), diluted 1:500 in blocking buffer, for 1 h at room temperature. After three PBS washes (5–10 min each), secondary antibody was applied for 1 h at room temperature. A second fixation (1% PFA, 5 min) stabilized the signal before downstream DNA or RNA FISH.

FLAP-Y smFISH probe preparation and hybridization

Equimolar 5S rDNA probes (5′-ACAACATCCAGGATTCCCAGGCCGTC-3′ and 5′-AACTTGCCAGATCGGACGGGATGGCGT-3′) were pooled and diluted fivefold in IDTE buffer (pH 8.0; final concentration, 0.833 µM per probe). For pre-hybridization, a 20 µl reaction (4 µl primary probe pool, 1 µl secondary FLAP-Y probe, 2 µl 10× NEBuffer 3, 13 µl nuclease-free water) was thermocycled (85 °C, 3 min; 65 °C, 3 min; 25 °C, 5 min) and stored at 4 °C (ref. 83).

For hybridization, 100 µl hybridization solution (10% dextran sulfate in 1:1:8 formamide:20× SSC:water) was applied and incubated (37 °C, 1 h), then replaced with 50 µl fresh solution containing 1 µl pre-hybridized probes (37 °C, 4 h, dark). Samples were washed twice in wash buffer (1:1:8 formamide:20× SSC:water), then incubated in 100 µl wash buffer (37 °C, 1 h, dark), washed twice more and mounted in Vectashield with DAPI (H-1200, Vector Laboratories).

Imaging acquisition

Imaging used a spinning-disk confocal system (Olympus IX83, Yokogawa CSU-W1, 50 µm disk; cellSens software), with a Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion sCMOS camera and ×60 oil-immersion objective (NA, 1.5).

smFISH count and distance pipeline

Embryos and nuclei were segmented using Cellpose-SAM, applied to a Gaussian-blurred FISH channel (embryos) and raw DAPI images (nuclei). FISH dot detection used an in-house Python pipeline: images were deconvolved with a microscope-specific point spread function (pycudadecon), then filtered with a Difference of Gaussians filter to enhance FISH puncta, and dots were segmented by dataset-specific intensity thresholding of the Difference of Gaussians-filtered images. Semantic masks were converted to instance masks (SciPy or CuPy label function), and small artifacts (<100 pixels) were removed. Dots were assigned to nuclei by mask overlap, with centroids computed from voxel-based masks; dot count was the total detections per nucleus or embryo. For distance measurements, dots from paired FISH channels were matched per nucleus by nearest-neighbor centroid distance to avoid cross-nucleus assignments.

H3K9me3 enrichment surrounding Pol III foci

To analyze H3K9 methylation enrichment around Pol III foci, we segmented foci as for FISH detection and generated two concentric 200 nm rings extending outward from each focus, yielding three zones: the core focus and two peripheral rings. A low-threshold mask restricted the H3K9me3 signal to regions of detectable fluorescence, avoiding skew from its concentration in discrete foci. Median H3K9me3 intensity in each zone was normalized to mean nuclear intensity and zone area, and fold enrichment was calculated by comparing each ring’s normalized intensity to that of the core focus, with significance assessed using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Statistical polymer baselines

Multivariate normal sampling

To provide a minimal statistical baseline to distinguish biologically regulated from intrinsic polymer fluctuations, we used a Gaussian framework in which polymer configurations are drawn from a multivariate normal (MVN) distribution. Pairwise interactions between loci are encoded in an interaction matrix, which specifies the covariance matrix that fully constrains the MVN distribution. Specifically, for a polymer with n monomers, configurations are sampled from the following distribution:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P({x})={(2\pi )}^{-3n/2}{\det ({\Sigma })}^{-1/2}\exp \left(-\frac{1}{2}{x}^{T}{{\Sigma}}^{-1}x\right)$$\end{document}P(x)=(2π)−3n/2det(Σ)−1/2exp(−12xTΣ−1x)

In the interaction matrix, attractive interactions were assigned negative values, while repulsive interactions were positive. The interaction matrix was constructed as a Kirchhoff matrix by setting each diagonal entry such that its corresponding row sums to zero. Given that the Kirchhoff interaction matrix has zero determinant owing to translational symmetry, we removed the last row and column and fixed the corresponding monomer at the origin (0,0,0). This eliminates the global translational degree of freedom and ensures the matrix can be used for MVN sampling. To generate three-dimensional structures, the interaction matrix was replicated across each of the three diagonal blocks of the full precision matrix. Chromatin conformations were sampled from an MVN distribution using PyTorch’s torch.distributions.MultivariateNormal, with the interaction matrix provided as the precision matrix.

In both the free polymer and uniformly attractive polymer (UAP) baselines, five additional monomers were appended to the 5′ end to eliminate the terminus effect, as only the 3′ end corresponds to the actual chromatin terminus. These extra monomers were excluded from the final output to ensure alignment with the experimental dataset.

Parameters

Free polymer

In the free polymer baseline, we only included uniform nearest-neighbor interactions. The value of the interaction coefficient was set to 45.6 µm−2 so that the mean RoG of the free polymer configurations matched the wild-type experimental dataset at 0.55 µm. Pairs with longer distances were set to zero interaction strength (Extended Data Fig. 9).

UAP

In the UAP baseline, we set interaction strength based on the distance between the pair of regions. The values were adjusted to fit the folding relationship (Extended Data Fig. 9). Four different values were used: 7.098 µm−2 for nearest neighbors (0.1 Mb), 0.098 µm−2 for pairs 0.2–0.9 Mb apart, 0.049 µm−2 for pairs 1–1.9 Mb apart and 0.0147 µm−2 for pairs ≥2 Mb apart (Extended Data Fig. 9). We note that these coefficients are not uniquely determined, as different sets of values might similarly reproduce the observed distance relationship.

Domain-forming polymers

To investigate whether localized interaction strengthening can generate domain-like structures, we constructed domain-forming models as perturbations of the UAP baseline. In single-domain models, interaction strengths within a contiguous region of five consecutive nearest-neighbor pairs were increased by 3×, 10× or 100× relative to baseline, then re-optimized using the UAP fitting procedure to preserve global scaling while maintaining the relative enhancement within the domain. Double-domain models introduced two such 100×-enhanced regions, separated by one, five or nine regions, with interactions similarly re-optimized to preserve global scaling. All matrices were constructed in Kirchhoff form and used as precision matrices for MVN sampling; the full set is provided in the associated GitHub repository (https://github.com/dcpb0/Chromosome-domains.git).

DIMES

We inferred effective interaction matrices from experimental data using the DIMES framework according to a previous publication58, which implements a maximum entropy approach to determine the interaction matrix that reproduces the observed mean pairwise distances under a Gaussian polymer model.

The mean distance matrix derived from experimental data was used as input (input-type: dmap; input-format: text). The resulting connectivity matrix represents an effective interaction matrix whose inverse corresponds to the covariance structure of an equivalent MVN polymer model.

To ensure compatibility with our MVN sampling framework, the inferred matrix was symmetrized to correct for numerical asymmetries. Given that DIMES defines interactions with an opposite sign convention, we used the negative of the connectivity matrix as the precision matrix Λ for MVN sampling.

As with other models, the translational degree of freedom was removed by excluding the last row and column, and the corresponding monomer was fixed at the origin (0,0,0). The resulting matrix was then used to generate polymer conformations by MVN sampling.
