De Novo Long-Read Genome Assembly and Annotation of the Mosquito Gut-Dwelling Fungus, Smittium minutisporum
Authors: Anusha Prakash, Yan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMosquito guts host a variety of microbes, yet fungi are often overlooked. Smittium (Harpellales, Zoopagomycota) comprises numerous species that are obligate symbionts residing in the hindgut of mosquito larvae. Despite their association with pathogen-bearing vectors, these fungal symbionts remain understudied, largely due to the lack of high-quality genome resources. This limitation has impeded a deeper understanding of their genome biology and adaptive strategies in relation to their mosquito hosts, which may hold significant epidemiological implications. To address this gap, we generated the first reference-quality genome assembly for this group of fungi, using PacBio HiFi long-reads for an axenic culture of Smittium minutisporum, originally isolated from the eastern treehole mosquito, Aedes triseriatus. The genome assembly consists of 53 contigs, spanning a total length of 32.5 Mb, and is predicted to encode 8,254 protein-coding genes, with repetitive regions constituting 25.22% of the genome. Notably, despite being highly contiguous and gap free, the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Ortholog analysis suggests a completeness score of 71.8%, implying unusual genome features, possibly shaped by adaptation and specialization within the mosquito gut. This high-quality genome resource will be invaluable for advancing our understanding of mosquito gut-dwelling fungi, their natural history, and their cryptic symbiosis with insect hosts.

Significance

Mosquito gut-dwelling fungi hold significant potential to enhance our understanding of host–microbe interactions and support the development of disease biocontrol strategies, given their close association with insect vectors. However, these endosymbiotic fungi remain understudied, primarily due to the absence of high-quality genome resources. In this study, we present the first reference-quality genome assembly of Smittium minutisporum, an obligate mosquito gut-dwelling fungus, using PacBio High-Fidelity sequencing. This assembly is the first to achieve a highly contiguous and gap-free status for insect gut-dwelling fungi, capturing repetitive regions with unprecedented detail and uncovering potential gene loss events, likely driven by long-term interactions with mosquitoes. This genome resource opens new avenues for future research on genome evolution in microbial fungi inhabiting extreme environments, such as the mosquito gut, and will help illuminate the symbiotic mechanisms between mosquitoes and their gut symbionts.

Introduction

Mosquito guts host a diverse community of microbes, including bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and fungi, which play important roles and often influence host phenotypes (Cansado-Utrilla et al. 2021). Sequencing technology has greatly advanced our understanding of these microbes and their functions in relation to the hosts. For example, the bacterial endosymbiont, Wolbachia, can modulate the lifespan of mosquitoes by interfering with host microRNAs (Hussain et al. 2011). Additionally, the genome sequence of the human malaria parasite transmitted by mosquitoes has revealed an expanded capacity for immune evasion and host–parasite interactions (Gardner et al. 2002). However, mosquito gut-dwelling fungi are often overlooked, and their interactions with mosquito hosts remain less understood.

Harpellales (Kickxellomycotina, Zoopagomycota), commonly known as insect gut-dwelling fungi, include several species that inhabit the hindgut of mosquito larvae, such as Legeriodes culiciola, Smittium culicis, S. insulare, S. morbosum, S. minutisporum, S. simulii, and Zancudomyces culisetae (Sweeney 1981; White et al 2006b; Wang et al. 2013; Strongman and Wang 2022). These fungi can act as mosquito pathogens (e.g., S. morbosum) or exhibit relationships ranging from commensal to parasitic or mutualistic, depending on environmental conditions (Horn and Lichtwardt 1981; White et al 2006a; Wang 2016). Previous genome sequencing efforts on mosquito gut-dwelling fungi have been instrumental in uncovering cryptic interactions and hidden aspects of their natural history. For example, the horizontal gene transfer of a polyubiquitin gene from mosquitoes to their gut fungus Z. culisetae was discovered using Illumina sequencing (Wang et al. 2016a). As more genomes became available, they helped to identify a universal gene toolbox involved in insect–fungus interactions (Wang et al. 2018). To date, three genome assemblies have been constructed for mosquito gut-dwelling fungi in this clade, specifically for S. culicis and Z. culisetae. However, these genomes were sequenced using Illumina short-reads, leaving significant portions of the genome unassembled due to complex structures.

As a step toward further understanding this enigmatic group of microbial fungi, we completed a highly contiguous genome assembly of S. minutisporum, marking it as the third sequenced species in this clade of mosquito gut-dwelling fungi and the first using PacBio High-Fidelity long-read sequencing data. Smittium minutisporum was originally isolated from Aedes triseriatus, the primary vector of the La Crosse virus, which causes pediatric encephalitis (Calisher 1994; USDA-ARS 2016). Like other mosquito gut-dwelling fungi, this species resides in the hindgut of mosquito larvae by attaching to the chitinous gut lining (White et al. 2006b). Notably, the two previously sequenced fungi, S. culicis and Z. culisetae, were isolated from different mosquito hosts, Culex sp. and Culiseta impatiens, respectively. The genome resource produced from S. minutisporum will enable comparative genomic analyses to expand our understanding of host specificity and identify host-associated genetic components. Additionally, as the first reference-quality genome of insect gut-dwelling fungi assembled to near-chromosome level, this resource will support future investigations on noncoding regions, including transposons and complex repetitive structures, with the potential to uncover insights into genome evolution and the mechanisms by which these fungi interact with their mosquito hosts and possibly coexisting microbes.

Results and Discussion

Genome Statistics

PacBio long-read sequencing of S. minutisporum resulted in 53 contigs with a total length of 32.5 Mb and an average coverage of 60× (Fig. 1a). Notably, 23 contigs accounted for 97% of the genome, with the largest contig measuring 2.8 Mb and the assembly's overall guanine-cytosine (GC) content at 37% (Fig. 1a). The N50 score of 1.9 Mb indicates a highly contiguous genome assembly (Fig. 1a). Compared with other mosquito gut-dwelling fungi, S. minutisporum exhibits the highest assembly quality to date, outperforming Z. culisetae (N50 = 26.4 Kb, 1,954 scaffolds) and S. culicis (N50 = 15.2 Kb, 7,749 scaffolds; N50 = 20.2 Kb, 6,137 scaffolds; Table 1). Additionally, the S. minutisporum genome assembly surpasses the previously best-assembled Harpellales genome, Capniomyces stellatus (N50 = 595.9 Kb, 72 scaffolds), which was generated using multiple polymerase chain reaction–free libraries of varying insert sizes (Table 1; Wang et al. 2016b).

The assembly has a Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Ortholog (BUSCO) completeness score of 71.8% using the “fungi_odb10”? dataset (Fig. 1; Manni et al. 2021), which highlight a notable 21.0% of missing and 7.3% of fragmented genes typically found in fungi. As an obligate insect gut-dwelling fungus, S. minutisporum has likely undergone significant genomic changes through its 200 My of coevolution with mosquito hosts (Wang et al. 2019). Consequently, many conserved genes shared among other fungal lineages, and used as benchmarks in the BUSCO “fungi_odb10”? dataset, may have been lost or disrupted, as their functions are no longer essential for a symbiotic lifestyle within insect digestive tracts. This phenomenon, commonly observed in highly diverged eukaryotes like protists, often results in lower BUSCO completeness scores when using broad datasets (de Paula Baptista et al. 2024). For this reason, some groups have developed custom BUSCO datasets, such as those for Microsporidia (Cormier et al. 2021; Angst et al. 2023). However, a dataset specifically for insect gut-dwelling fungi is not yet available and could be developed for future studies. In 2018, a comparative genomic study proposed a Harpellales core gene dataset consisting of 1,241 genes, of which 1,204 (97%) were successfully identified in the S. minutisporum genome and were further used to confirm its phylogenetic placement in Harpellales (Fig. 1b; Wang et al. 2018).

We also examined the previously sequenced genomes of insect gut-dwelling fungi using Illumina short-reads, finding that they consistently exhibit missing scores ranging from 16.7% to 23.9% and fragmentation scores between 6.6% and 9.2%, with total completeness ranging from 67.3% to 76.4% (Fig. 1b and Table 1). Notably, the outgroup Kickxella alabastrina, a soil saprobic fungus phylogenetically related to these insect symbionts, shows a higher completeness score of 83.3% and a much lower missing score of 11.3% (Fig. 1b; Chang et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023). Our results find that improved genome assembly using long-read data does not necessarily lead to higher BUSCO scores, which suggest that gene loss events may have occurred during coevolution with insect hosts. Additionally, the similarity in BUSCO metrics among Harpellales fungi, combined with their shared adaptation to insect guts, supports the idea that evolutionary genomic divergence from the most recent common ancestor of fungi was likely driven by collective specialization to a novel environmental niche.

Genome Annotation

A total of 98.6% of the RNAseq data successfully mapped back to the genome assembly. The assembly is predicted to contain 8,254 protein-coding genes, with 444 alternatively spliced gene products. Additionally, the annotation predicts 9,792 protein family (Pfam) domains, corresponding to 3,086 distinct Pfam types. This reference-quality genome assembly demonstrates the highest completeness in recovering repetitive elements among Harpellales. RepeatMasker analysis estimated that repetitive elements make up 25.22% of the genome, with nearly half being retroelements (11.40%). The proportion of repetitive sequences in this genome is approximately five times higher than in Illumina-based assemblies, including those of other mosquito-associated fungi, such as S. culicis strains GSMNP (3.34%), ID-206-W2 (3.64%), and Z. culisetae (4.29%; Wang et al. 2018; Table 1). These findings suggest that the complex genome structure of insect gut-dwelling fungi, characterized by extensive repetitive regions, can be successfully resolved using long-read sequencing technology. This underscores the potential of long-read sequencing to resolve repetitive regions and their role in long-term association and coevolution with insect hosts.

Implications and Future Directions

Historically, there have been significant identity-related misunderstandings among mosquito gut-associated fungi. These misidentifications arose from limited knowledge of fungal diversity and over-reliance on species identification based primarily on morphological features. Such methods often fail to capture the full complexity of this group, as morphological traits can show homoplasy due to similar environments, and the same species may exhibit variable characteristics in different hosts (Strongman 2005; Vojvodic and McCreadie 2009; Wang et al. 2013). For example, despite the utility of morphological classification, reliance on this approach alone led to the misidentification of S. minutisporum strains WKRa and WKRb as S. morbosum, a confusion that was resolved through a combination of morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses (Wang et al. 2014). Similarly, Z. culisetae was initially classified as a Smittium species based on morphological data until molecular phylogenetics clarified its misplacement, which highlights the advantages of molecular data for species differentiation (Wang et al. 2013). These gaps in essential knowledge have hindered progress in understanding the unique nature of these fungi, including the development of hypotheses regarding their adaptation, interactions, and coevolution with their hosts (Tawidian et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019).

We believe this genome resource will significantly contribute to our understanding of the genomic diversity of mosquito gut-associated fungi. Alongside the three previously sequenced genomes of mosquito gut-dwelling fungi, this resource will help shed light on important aspects of genome evolution in these fungi and the molecular mechanisms of host specificity. Future studies should leverage comparative genomics among mosquito gut-dwelling fungi to identify key genomic elements that define this group. Given the notable gaps in BUSCO scores for S. minutisporum and other Harpellales fungi, this high-quality genome will provide insights into the loss of functional genes through coevolution with mosquitoes and may reveal cryptic interactions or shared pathways with their mosquito hosts. Such findings could pave the way for the development of host-specific and environmentally safe biocontrol strategies.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation and DNA Extraction

Smittium minutisporum WKRb was originally isolated from A. triseriatus (=Ochlerotatus triseriatus) in South Carolina, USA. A culture was obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture - Agricultural Research Service Collection of Entomopathogenic Fungal Cultures (ARSEF 9061). The fungus was cultured in Brain Heart Infusion Glucose Tryptone vitamins liquid broth at room temperature for 1 week prior to DNA and RNA extractions. Fungal tissues were filtered through Miracloth (MilliporeSigma, Canada) and ground into fine powder using liquid nitrogen. High-molecular-weight DNA (>20 kb) and RNA were extracted using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Toronto, ON, Canada) and the MagMax plant RNA isolation kit (Thermo Fisher), respectively, following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Genome Sequencing and Assembly

DNA libraries were prepared and sequenced using the PacBio Sequel II platform at the Centre for Applied Genomics (Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, ON, Canada). Computations were performed on the Niagara supercomputer at the SciNet HPC Consortium, University of Toronto (Loken et al. 2010; Ponce et al. 2019). PacBio HiFi reads were adapter trimmed using HiFiAdapterFilt and then assembled into contigs with HiFiASM v0.16.1 (Cheng et al. 2021; Sim et al. 2022). To further enhance assembly quality, the repeat-aware tool finisherSC was applied, utilizing both the primary assembly from the previous step and the trimmed long-read data (Lam et al. 2015). The completeness of the genome assembly was evaluated with Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v5.1.2, using the “fungi_odb10”? database (Manni et al. 2021). Harpellales core genes, identified from previously sequenced genomes, were used as an alternative approach to evaluate the completeness of the S. minutisporum genome. A total of 1,280 protein-coding genes conserved in Harpellales genomes were identified (Wang et al. 2018). Of these, 1,241 protein products (with sequence lengths >50 amino acids) were selected to build Profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) using HMMER v3.3.2 (Eddy 2011). These Harpellales HMMs were then used to search for orthologous sequences in the S. minutisporum genome via hmmsearch, with an E-value cutoff of 1e−10. Genome assembly statistics were visualized using a snail plot generated with BlobToolKit v.2.6.5 (Challis et al. 2020). A bar chart comparing BUSCO statistics of Harpellales genomes was created using the R package “ggplot2”? (Wickham 2016).

Genome Annotation and RNA Sequencing

Interspersed repeats and low-complexity DNA sequences in the assembly were soft-masked using RepeatModeler version 2.0.2a and Repeatmasker version 4.1.0 (https://www.repeatmasker.org/RepeatModeler/). RNA library construction (2 × 150 bp) and sequencing were performed at the Novogene UC Davis Sequencing Center using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Raw RNAseq data were adapter trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.36 and mapped to the genome assembly using HISAT2 v2.2.1 and samtools v1.12 (Li et al. 2009; Bolger et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2019). Genome annotation was conducted on the soft-masked assembly using RNA evidence with the Braker pipeline v1.9 (parameters –fungus –softmasking 1), which employs both Augustus (Stanke and Waack 2003; Stanke et al. 2008) and GeneMark (Lomsadze et al. 2014). Gene functions and protein domain architectures were inferred by using PfamScan to search predicted proteins against the Pfam v35.0 HMM database (Finn et al. 2016).

Phylogenetic Analyses

The dataset of 1,241 conserved Harpellales genes was used to reconstruct the phylogenetic tree. Each HMM alignment was concatenated into a super-alignment file using FASconCAT-G v1.02, which was used as the input for subsequent phylogenetic analyses (Kück and Longo 2014). The maximum-likelihood tree was inferred using IQ-TREE v.1.6.12, with the best-fit model determined for each partition/HMM (Nguyen et al. 2015; Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017). Branch support values were calculated using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Hoang et al. 2017).
