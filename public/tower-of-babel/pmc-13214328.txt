Correlation between fine needle aspiration cytology with histopathology of non-thyroidal head and neck lesions: first report from Somalia
Authors: Ismail Mohamud Abdullahi, Nor Abdi Yasin, Abdulkamil Abdullahi Adani, Ismail Mohamed Ali, Nuh Mohamed Yahye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a widely recognized, cost-effective, and minimally invasive diagnostic technique employed for the assessment of head and neck masses. Despite its worldwide utilization, no prior research from Somalia has evaluated the diagnostic precision of FNAC in non-thyroidal head and neck lesions.ObjectiveTo assess the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive characteristics of fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) by matching cytological results with histological diagnoses in non-thyroidal head and neck masses.MethodsThis retrospective study encompassed 265 patients with non-thyroidal head and neck lesions who had fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) followed by histological analysis at a tertiary hospital in Somalia during a five-year period. FNAC outcomes had been compared with the relevant biopsy data. The sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were determined utilizing histology as the gold standard.ResultsA total of 265 patients underwent both fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) and histological assessment for non-thyroidal head and neck lesions. Among these, 145 (54.7%) were men and 120 (45.3%) were females, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.2:1. Lymph node lesions constituted the predominant group of cases (60.8%), followed by salivary gland lesions (36.2%) and other soft tissue/cystic masses (3.0%). FNAC demonstrated the highest sensitivity for lymph node lesions (99%) and the highest specificity and accuracy for salivary gland lesions (98.8% and 98%, respectively). The overall sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were 97.1%, 96.7%, and 98.5%, respectively. Lymphomas constituted the predominant malignancy identified, while the majority of salivary gland lesions were benign, with pleomorphic adenoma being the most prevalent.ConclusionFNAC exhibits superior diagnostic efficacy in assessing non-thyroidal head and neck lesions, affirming its position as a primary diagnostic instrument, particularly in resource-limited environments such as Somalia.

Introduction

Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a widely recognized, affordable, and minimally invasive diagnostic method for assessing different lesions in the head and neck area. It enables quick preliminary diagnosis, promoting timely and suitable clinical therapy without necessitating more invasive procedures [1, 2].

In most circumstances, FNAC serves a vital role in the preoperative assessment of cervical lymphadenopathy, salivary gland tumors, and other neck lesions. Its capacity to identify benign from malignant processes has made it an essential tool in both resource-rich and resource-limited instances [3, 4]. However, in Somalia, information on FNAC utilization and its diagnostic performance are lacking despite a growing prevalence of cancer and neck lesions [5, 6].

Over the past two decades, FNAC has become crucial to head and neck lesion diagnosis globally, although no cytopathological correlation studies have yet been reported from Somalia. A recent analysis by Tahtabasi et al. noted lymphomas, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and salivary gland tumors as the most frequent head and neck cancers in the country [6], further underscoring the need for trustworthy diagnostic techniques.

This study is the first report from Somalia comparing FNAC and histopathology in non-thyroidal head and neck lesions. This study intends to examine the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive qualities of FNAC, stressing its diagnostic utility in low-resource settings where advanced diagnostics like immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow cytometry are often unavailable.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective investigation was performed at the pathology laboratory of Mogadishu Somalia Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary care facility. The study duration spanned from January 2018 to December 2022, including all patients who received both fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) and subsequent histological analysis of non-thyroidal head and neck lesions. Data has been collected over a five year duration from the hospital’s electronic health information system (HIS). Ethical permission was granted by the hospital’s Ethical Research Committee (Ref No: 10520), and informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. A total of 265 cases were found and incorporated into the analysis.

Criteria for inclusion and exclusion

Patients of all age groups and genders with head and neck masses (excluding thyroid lesions) who underwent both fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) and histological diagnosis were included. Cases without further histological validation or with insufficient FNAC samples were removed.

Fine needle aspiration cytology and histopathological procedures

Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration cytology was conducted utilizing 23-gauge needles and 5 mL disposable syringes. Six glass slides were prepared for each case. Air-dried smears were stained with May-Grünwald-Giemsa, whereas alcohol fixed smears were stained with Papanicolaou or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). All FNAC procedures were performed by an interventional radiologist.

Histopathological specimens were acquired either surgical excision or core needle biopsies and subsequently fixed in 10% buffered formalin. Histological assessment was conducted using standard H&E staining. No ancillary techniques, including immunocytochemistry or flow cytometry, were available at the institutional laboratory.

Pathological assessment

Cytopathology slides were initially evaluated independently by one pathologist, whilst histopathology slides were reviewed independently by another pathologist on a monthly schedule. Cases classified as malignant, suspect, or diagnostically challenging were later assessed collaboratively by both pathologists. In cases of diagnostic uncertainty or discordance, a third senior pathologist conducted a final diagnosis. The inter-observer agreement was systematically evaluated to assess diagnostic concordance.

Tuberculosis was diagnosed using classical cytomorphologic characteristics, including granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis (Fig. 1), and supported by robust clinical and radiological correlation. Ziehl-Neelsen staining was not available during the study duration. Without diagnostic methods like microbiological confirmation (e.g., Ziehl-Neelsen staining), the accuracy of these diagnoses is constrained. Rare disorders like Rosai-Dorfman disease; were diagnosed using classical cytohistomorphologic features (Fig. 2) and clinical correlation, with the lack of immunohistochemistry recognized as a constraint. Initially, Castleman disease; was regarded as benign lymphoid proliferations based on cytology; however, further histopathological analysis validated the diagnosis of Castleman disease, revealing cytohistological concordance.

Surgical excision in lymphoma patients was mostly conducted for diagnostic excision biopsy rather than therapeutic intervention, as fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was inadequate for conclusive subtyping due to the absence of immunohistochemistry (IHC). In nasopharyngeal cancer, surgical samples were performed for diagnostic confirmation rather than excision.

The diagnostic precision of FNAC was evaluated using histology as the gold standard. FNACs deemed suspicious or malignant were classified as true positives (TP) if histological analysis verified malignancy, and as false positives (FP) if histology indicated no cancer. Benign FNAC results were classified as true negatives (TN) when histology confirmed benign findings, and as false negatives (FN) when histological analysis indicated cancer despite benign FNAC results.

The FNAC diagnosis of lymph nodes were classified into eight categories: (I) benign, (II) reactive follicular hyperplasia, (III) suppurative inflammation, (IV) TB lymphadenitis, (V) suspicious for lymphoma/lymphoma, (VI) malignant, and (VII) carcinoma metastasis.

The results of lymph node FNAC were classified according to a local system based on morphological patterns. The Sydney System and WHO recommendations for lymph node cytopathology were not utilized, as they were released after the study period and were only commonly adopted in our context on mid 2024. This limitation has been acknowledged accordingly.

Salivary gland FNAC diagnoses were classified into six categories based on the Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology: (I) non-diagnostic, (II) non-neoplastic, (III) atypia of undetermined significance, (IV) neoplasm, further divided into: (IVA) benign neoplasm, (IVB) salivary gland neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential (SUMP), (V) suspicious for malignancy, and (VI) malignant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables, including age, were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical characteristics, such as sex and diagnostic categories, were presented as frequencies and percentages. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study comprised 265 patients with non-thyroidal head and neck tumors who underwent fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) and subsequent histological investigation over a five year period. The ages of patients varied from 1 month to 90 years, with a median age of 41 years. Lymph node and salivary gland lesions were more commonly identified in individuals aged 10 to 25 years, while soft tissue masses and cystic lesions were primarily noted in patients aged 25 to 40 years.

The study demonstrated a male predominance, with 145 males (54.7%) and 120 females (45.3%), generating a male-to-female ratio of 1.2:1. The most significant gender disparity occurred in lymph node lesions, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.5:1, whereas salivary gland lesions had a female predominance, reflected in a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.2.

A total of 161 cases (60.8%) were derived from lymph nodes (including anterior/posterior cervical, submental, and supraclavicular regions), followed by 96 cases (36.2%) involving salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, and minor salivary glands), and 8 cases (3.0%) comprising cystic and soft tissue lesions. Of the 161 lymph node lesions, lymphoma was the predominant histological diagnosis, identified in 46.6% (n = 75) of cases. Following this, the occurrences included tuberculosis (24.2%, n = 39), metastatic cancer (15.5%, n = 25), reactive lymphadenitis (9.9%, n = 16), and suppurative inflammation (2.5%, n = 4). Two uncommon instances were identified as Rosai-Dorfman disease and Castleman’s disease. (Figures 1 and 2).

Hodgkin lymphoma constituted 36% (n = 27) of all lymphomas, whereas non-Hodgkin lymphoma comprised 34.6% (n = 26). The remaining 29.3% (n = 22) could not be subclassified due to the absence of immunohistochemistry markers.

In the group of 25 metastatic carcinoma patients, the predominant primary was undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (64%, n = 16), followed by thyroid carcinoma (16%, n = 4), tongue carcinoma (8%, n = 2), and three cases of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma, and undifferentiated carcinoma respectively.

The diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for lymph node FNAC were 99%, 91.3%, and 97.6%, respectively (Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

Head and neck masses constitute one of the most prevalent clinical manifestations observed in everyday medical practice. An accurate and timely diagnosis is vital for guiding suitable therapeutic methods due to the extensive differential diagnoses, which encompass congenital cysts, inflammatory diseases, primary neoplasms, and metastatic tumors [5]. Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a fundamental diagnostic method for assessing such lesions, primarily owing to its minimally invasive characteristics, cost efficiency, and swift turnaround time [1, 5].

As of now, no prior studies have been published from Somalia investigating the cytohistological correlation of non-thyroidal head and neck lesions. This study is the inaugural report from the region assessing the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC in these lesions. Our results highlight the efficacy of FNAC by exhibiting high sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic precision.

According to affected organs, an initial report on 1306 cancer cases in Somalia indicated that lymph node cancer was the 11th most prevalent, while nasopharyngeal and salivary gland cancers were 13th and 18th, respectively. Lymphomas ranked as the sixth most prevalent malignancy among males in that report [6]. Our study contributes to the epidemiological background by demonstrating that lymph node lesions were the most commonly sampled non-thyroidal head and neck masses in our population.

Patel et al. conducted a study in India revealing that head and neck cancers constitute a significant percentage of all malignancies, comprising roughly 23% in males, 6% in females, and 5% of all pediatric cancers [7, 9, 10]. These percentages significantly contrast from those documented in Somalia by Tahtabasi et al., where head and neck cancers constituted 11.4% of all cancers in males, 9% in females, and 9.7% among pediatric malignancies [6].

The gender distribution indicated a male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.2:1, aligning with previous investigations [8, 9, 11, 12]. Lymph node lesions were more prevalent in males (1.5:1), while salivary gland lesions displayed a modest prevalence in females. The predominant diagnosis for lymph node lesions was lymphoma (46.6%), followed by TB (24.2%) and metastatic cancer (15.5%).

Our lymphoma findings match what was published by Nasreen et al., who identified that 49.7% of malignant lymphadenopathies were lymphomas [11, 13]. In their study, non-Hodgkin lymphoma was more prevalent than Hodgkin lymphoma (38.4% vs. 11.5%), however our analysis revealed a comparable distribution with Hodgkin lymphoma at 36% and non-Hodgkin lymphoma at 34.6%. Conversely, research conducted by Shahid et al. and Pathak et al. indicated much decreased lymphoma rates (below 10%) and elevated instances of reactive lymphadenitis, followed by metastatic carcinoma and TB [12–15]. Agrawal et al. observed that 25% of lymph node lesions were indicative of tuberculous lymphadenitis, a statistic consistent with our findings [14, 16].

In this investigation, FNAC demonstrated good diagnostic precision in lymph node lesions, with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy recorded at 99%, 91.3%, and 97.6%, respectively. These rates exceed those documented by Nasreen et al., who showed a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 67.2% [8, 11]. FNAC exhibited significant accuracy, with sensitivity varying from 92.3% for salivary glands to 99% for lymph nodes. The diagnostic constraints in lymphomas were primarily attributable to the lack of immunohistochemistry, which is crucial for accurate subtyping.

In salivary gland lesions, over 85% were benign, with pleomorphic adenoma constituting the predominant diagnosis at 73%. This aligns with the findings of Khetrapal et al., who similarly recognized pleomorphic adenoma and chronic sialadenitis as the primary diagnosis [10, 15]. The performance of FNAC in salivary gland lesions in our study shown a sensitivity of 92.3%, specificity of 98.8%, and accuracy of 98%.

Cystic and soft tissue lesions constituted merely 3.0% of cases, a percentage consistent with the findings of Bhasker et al., who documented miscellaneous neck lesions in 5.1% of cases [8, 10, 16].

FNAC had a sensitivity of 97.1%, specificity of 96.7%, and diagnostic accuracy of 98.5% for all non-thyroidal head and neck lesions. Tandon et al. documented a specificity of 98.5%, although Poorey and Rammeh recorded lower specificity values of 95% and 94.4%, respectively [7, 12, 17]. In assessing FNAC’s diagnostic efficacy, sensitivity is paramount, as a false-negative outcome may postpone essential therapy or additional investigations.

In our cohort, 151 out of 265 lesions were benign, underscoring the pivotal role of FNAC in preventing unnecessary surgical procedures. Our findings substantiate FNAC’s efficacy as a secure, cost-effective, fast, and precise initial diagnostic instrument in the assessment of head and neck lesions. Although operator-dependent, FNAC is becoming recognized for its diagnostic reliability and clinical value.

Study limitations

This research possesses multiple limitations. Firstly, Several FNAC patients lacking comparable histopathological data were eliminated. The retrospective and single-center methodology, along with the limited sample size, may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, the dataset was deficient in information regarding risk variables, including occupational exposures and radiation history. Thirdly, the classification of lymph node cytology did not adhere to conventional methods, such as the Sydney or WHO recommendations, which were established post-study time and are just now being implemented in our context. Fourthly, The lack of further diagnostic methods, such as immunohistochemistry and Ziehl-Neelsen staining, limited definitive subtyping and microbiological confirmation, especially in cases of lymphoma and tuberculosis. Ultimately, long-term clinical follow-up and outcome data were inaccessible owing to logistical and geographical obstacles preventing patient tracking.

Nonetheless, This is the initial Somali study assessing the cytohistological correlation and diagnostic efficacy of FNAC in non-thyroidal head and neck diseases. It illustrates the utility of FNAC in a resource-limited environment and lays the groundwork for future diagnostic standardization and enhancement of cytopathology services in Somalia.

Conclusion

This study presents a significant level of diagnostic accuracy and consistency between fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) and histopathology for non-thyroidal head and neck lesions in a resource-limited environment. FNAC has demonstrated reliability, quickness, along with minimal invasiveness as a diagnostic technique, particularly in the identification of benign and malignant tumors in salivary glands and lymph nodes. However, its limitations in subtyping lymphomas and identifying rare entities without immunohistochemistry underscore the necessity for expanded pathology infrastructure in Somalia.
