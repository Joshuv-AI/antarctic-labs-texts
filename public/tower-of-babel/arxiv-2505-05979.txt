Mixtures of multivariate linear asymmetric Laplace regressions with multiple asymmetric Laplace covariates
Otto, Arnoldus F., Bekker, Andriëtte, Punzo, Antonio, Ferreira, Johannes T., Tortora, Cristina

Abstract
In response to the challenge of accommodating non-Gaussian behaviour in data, the shifted asymmetric Laplace (SAL) cluster-weighted model (SALCWM) is introduced as a model-based method for jointly clustering responses and random covariates that exhibit skewness. Within each cluster, the multivariate SAL distribution is assumed for both the covariates and the responses given the covariates. To mitigate the effect of possible atypical observations, a heavy-tailed extension, the contaminated SALCWM (cSALCWM), is also proposed. In addition to the SALCWM parameters, each mixture component has a parameter controlling the proportion of outliers, one controlling the proportion of leverage points, one specifying the degree of outlierness, and another specifying the degree of leverage. The cSALCWM has the added benefit that once the model parameters are estimated and the observations are assigned to components, a more refined intra-group classification in typical points, (mild) outliers, good leverage, and bad leverage points can be directly obtained. An expectation-conditional maximization algorithm is developed for efficient maximum likelihood parameter estimation under this framework. Theoretical identifiability conditions are established, and empirical results from simulation studies and validation via real-world applications demonstrate that the cSALCWM not only preserves the modelling strengths of the SALCWM but also significantly enhances outlier detection and overall inference reliability. The methodology proposed in this paper has been implemented in an R package, which is publicly available at https://github.com/arnootto/ALCWM.

Introduction

Clustering and regression are fundamental tools in statistical modelling, often employed to uncover patterns and relationships within data.
Traditional finite mixture models have been widely used for clustering, yet they typically assume normality and fail to incorporate dependencies between variables.
When a clear regression relationship exists among variables, it becomes crucial to account for these dependencies to extract meaningful insights.

Finite Mixture of Regressions Model (MRM;
DeSarbo and
Cron (1988)
) is a well-established approach that models such dependencies while assuming fixed covariates.
However, a key limitation of MRM is that it does not consider the distribution of covariates themselves, making it less flexible in scenarios where covariates contribute to the group structure.

To overcome these constraints, the Cluster-Weighted Model (CWM), first introduced by
Gershenfeld (1997)
, provides a more comprehensive approach by explicitly modelling the distribution of covariates.
This model, also known as a finite mixture of regressions with random covariates, offers enhanced flexibility by jointly modelling both response variables and covariates within a clustering framework.
Over the years, CWMs have been extensively studied and extended in various directions; examples are
Ingrassia et al. (2015)
,
Ingrassia and
Punzo (2016)
,
Galimberti and
Soffritti (2020)
,
Počuča et al. (2020)
,
Punzo
et al. (2021)
,
and
Perrone and
Soffritti (2024)
.
However, as noted by
Gallaugher et al. (2022)
, relatively little attention has been devoted to incorporating skewed distributions, limiting their ability to model skewness often present in real-world data.

Herein, we extend this branch of literature by considering the multivariate shifted asymmetric Laplace (SAL) distribution
(
Franczak
et al., 2013
)
to model scenarios where, in each cluster, both the responses given the covariates and random covariates exhibit skewness, respectively.
The SAL has gained popularity as a viable alternative model for data exhibiting non-Gaussian behaviour, offering a peaked, skewed distribution, with tails heavier than the multivariate normal distribution.
These properties make it particularly well-suited for applications across multiple disciplines
(
Morris and
McNicholas, 2013
;
Tortora
et al., 2024
;
McLaughlin
et al., 2024
)
.

Real data are, however, often contaminated by atypical values that affect the estimation of the model parameters or, in a regression context, the regression coefficients.
Improper imposition of CWMs in the presence of these atypical values can underestimate standard errors and overstate the significance of the regression coefficients, which could lead to misleading inference.

This raises the question: how should these atypical observations be handled?
To answer this, it is important to note that atypical values are generally divided into two broad categories:

1.

Mild atypical values: observations sampled from some population different or even far from the assumed model.

2.

Gross atypical values: observations that cannot be modelled by a distribution as they are unpredictable.

In the presence of gross atypical values, the recommended approach is to eliminate the observations or choose a suitable method for suppressing them
(
Barnett and
Lewis, 1994
)
.
For mild atypical values, which is of specific interest in this paper, it is usually recommended to use a heavy-tailed model that is flexible enough to accommodate the atypical data points
(
Ritter, 2014
)
.
However, as emphasized by
Davies and
Gather (1993)
, atypical observations should be defined with respect to a reference distribution.
That is, the shape of the good (typical or non-atypical) points has to be assumed to define what a bad point is, and the region of bad (atypical) points can be defined, e.g., as a region where the density of the reference distribution is low.

Punzo and
McNicholas (2017)
introduced the contaminated Gaussian CWM (CGCWM), which provides some resistance to atypical values but is limited by its assumption that all components are symmetric.
Although incorporating contamination can help mitigate the influence of atypical values, it may misclassify observations near the tails of asymmetric components as atypical.
Additionally, the CGCWM (and other symmetric CWMs) may overfit the data by assigning multiple symmetric components to represent a single asymmetric one.

Our proposal is twofold: first, we introduce the SAL cluster-weighted model (SALCWM) to jointly model responses and random covariates that exhibit skewness cluster-wise; second, we propose a heavy-tailed extension, the contaminated SALCWM (cSALCWM), which offers greater robustness to atypical values while preserving the ability to capture skewness in the clusters.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, some key results are introduced that are used to develop the SALCWM and cSALCWM, which are presented in Section
3
.
There, the proposed CWMs are also compared with mixtures of Laplace regressions.
Sufficient conditions for identifiability are given in Section
4
, while an expectation-conditional maximization algorithm for parameter estimation is outlined in Section
5
, along with additional operational details.
A sensitivity analysis demonstrating the effectiveness of the cSALCWM in the presence of mild atypical values is presented in Section
6
.
Finally, Section
7
provides a real-world application of the SALCWM and cSALCWM, and Section
8
offers some concluding remarks.

2.1
Shifted asymmetric Laplace distribution

In this paper, we define
\bm{W}\sim\text{SAL}_{p}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})
to be a
p
-variate random vector following a multivariate SAL distribution with location parameter
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, skewness parameter
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
p\times p
non-negative definite matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
.
It follows from
Franczak
et al. (2013)
that the density of
\bm{W}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{w};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\exp\{(\bm{w}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\alpha}\}}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\bm{\Sigma}|^{1/2}}\left(\frac{(\bm{w}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{w}-\bm{\mu})}{2+\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{\nu/2}K_{\nu}(u),

(1)

where

\nu=\frac{2-p}{2}
,

u=\sqrt{(2+\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\alpha})(\bm{w}-\bm{\mu})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{w}-\bm{\mu})}
, and

K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the third kind with index
\nu
. The random vector
\bm{W}\sim\text{SAL}_{p}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})
can be written using the following stochastic representation

\displaystyle\bm{W}=\bm{\mu}+V\bm{\alpha}+\sqrt{V}\bm{N},

(2)

where
V
follows an exponential distribution with rate 1, i.e.,
V\sim\text{Exp}(1)
, and
\bm{N}
follows a
p
-variate Gaussian (normal) distribution with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, i.e.,
\bm{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
; consequently,
\bm{W}|V=v\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(\bm{\mu}+v\bm{\alpha},v\bm{\Sigma}\right)
. It follows from (
2
) that
\bm{W}
belongs to the class of multivariate normal variance-mean mixtures and that the expected value and covariance of
\bm{W}
are given by, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\bm{W})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}+\bm{\alpha},

(3)

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\bm{W})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\alpha}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}.

(4)

If
p=1
, then the characteristic function of
W
corresponds to a univariate asymmetric Laplace distribution, i.e.,
W\sim\text{SAL}_{1}(\mu,\phi,\alpha)
, where
\nu=1/2
and the Bessel function can be written as
K_{1/2}(u)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2u}}\exp\{-u\}
. As a result, the density of
W\sim\text{SAL}_{1}(\mu,\phi,\alpha)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{\text{SAL}}(w;\mu,\phi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\exp\left\{-\frac{|w-\mu|}{\phi^{2}}-\gamma\,\alpha\,\text{sign}(w-\mu)\right\},

(5)

where
\gamma=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+2\phi^{2}}
and all other terms are as previously defined (see
Kotz
et al., 2001
, for details).

2.2
Contaminated shifted asymmetric Laplace distribution

Morris
et al. (2019)
employed the approach adopted by
Punzo and
McNicholas (2016)
to define multivariate contaminated normal mixtures, and introduced the multivariate contaminated SAL (cSAL) distribution with density

\displaystyle f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{w};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha},\delta,\eta)=(1-\delta)\underbrace{f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{w};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})}_{\text{reference}}+\delta\underbrace{f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{w};\bm{\mu},\eta{\bm{\Sigma}},\sqrt{\eta}\bm{\alpha})}_{\text{contaminant}},

(6)

where
\delta\in(0,1)
denotes the proportion of bad points and
\eta>1
denotes the degree of contamination.
Because of the assumption
\eta>1
, it can be interpreted as the increase in variability due to the bad observations.
The mode and covariance matrix of the contaminant component
f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{w};\bm{\mu},\eta{\bm{\Sigma}},\sqrt{\eta}\bm{\alpha})
of the cSAL distribution in (
6
) are
\bm{\mu}
and
\eta\left({\bm{\Sigma}}+\bm{\alpha}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\right)
, respectively.
Hence, the contaminant component has the same mode
\bm{\mu}
and inflated covariance matrix (recall that
\eta>1
) with respect to the good component; moreover,
\bm{\mu}
is also the mode of the cSAL distribution.

The SAL distribution can be obtained as a limiting case when
\delta\to 1^{-}
or
\delta\to 0^{+}
, and
\eta\to 1^{+}
.
Another advantage is that once the parameters are estimated, say
\hat{\bm{\mu}},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}},\hat{\delta}
, and
\hat{\eta}
, we can determine whether an observation
\bm{w}
is good or bad with respect to the reference SAL distribution, using the
a posteriori
probability

\displaystyle P(\bm{w}\text{ is good}\mid\hat{\bm{\mu}},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}},\hat{\delta},\hat{\eta})=\frac{(1-\hat{\delta})f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{w};\hat{\bm{\mu}},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}})}{f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{w};\hat{\bm{\mu}},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}},\hat{\delta},\hat{\eta})},

(7)

and
\bm{w}
will be considered good if
P(\bm{w}\text{ is good}\mid\hat{\bm{\mu}},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}},\hat{\bm{\alpha}},\hat{\delta},\hat{\eta})\geq 0.5
, and bad otherwise.

2.3
The general cluster-weighted model

In many applied problems, the random vector
\bm{W}
is composed of a
d_{\bm{Y}}
variate response vector
\bm{Y}
and a random vector of covariates
\bm{X}
of dimension
d_{\bm{X}}
, with
d_{\bm{X}}+d_{\bm{Y}}=d_{\bm{W}}
, i.e.,
\bm{W}=(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
.
In general, a CWM represents the joint distribution of a response variable
\bm{Y}
and a (random) covariate
\bm{X}
as

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{\theta})=\sum^{G}_{g=1}\pi_{g}f(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{Y}|g})f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{X}|g}),

(8)

where
\bm{\theta}=\{\pi_{g},\bm{\theta}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\theta}_{\bm{Y}|g};g=1,\dots,G\}
represents the set of all parameters, and
\pi_{g}
are positive weights, with
\sum^{G}_{g=1}\pi_{g}=1
.
Hereafter, we will add a subscript to all parameters to distinguish those related to
\bm{X}
from those referring to
\bm{Y}
.

Unlike classical mixture models, the CWM factorizes the joint distribution of
p(\bm{x},\bm{y})
in the
g
-th mixture component into the product of the conditional distribution of
\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}
and the marginal distribution of
\bm{X}
, by assuming a parametric functional relationship for the expectation of
\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x}
.
This approach accounts for the dependency of
\bm{Y}
in
\bm{X}
within each mixture component by allowing the mean, or more generally, some location parameter to depend on
\bm{x}
via some linear or nonlinear functional relationship.
The possibility to specify different models for either
f(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{Y}|g})
or
f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{X}|g})
, as well as different linear/nonlinear dependencies for
\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}
in each mixture component makes the CWM a very flexible modelling approach.
The CWM is particularly useful in this context as part of the family of mixture models with random covariates
(
Hennig, 2000
)
.

3
Methodological proposal

In this paper, we focus on (
8
) by assuming that both
f(\bm{y}|\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{Y}|g})
and
f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{\bm{X}|g})
are either the SAL or cSAL in (
1
) and (
6
), respectively.
Thus, the SALCWM can be written as

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g})f_{\text{SAL}}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}),

(9)

where
\pi_{g}
are positive weights,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g})=\mathrm{E}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},\bm{\beta}_{g})=\bm{\beta}_{g}^{\top}\bm{x}^{*}
denotes the local conditional mean of
\bm{Y}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}
, with
\bm{\beta}_{g}
being a vector of regression coefficients of dimension
(1+d_{\bm{X}})\times d_{\bm{Y}}
and
\bm{x}^{*}=(1,\bm{x})
to account for the intercepts.
By considering the cSAL distribution in (
6
) instead, the cSALCWM can be written as

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\delta},\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g})f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}).

(10)

Just as the cSAL distribution can be seen as a heavy-tailed extension of the SAL distribution, the cSALCWM similarly extends the SALCWM.
As a result, the SALCWM emerges a limiting case of the cSALCWM if both
\delta_{\bm{X}|g}
and
\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}
tend to
0^{+}
or
1^{-}
, and
\eta_{\bm{X}|g}
and
\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}
tend to
1^{+}
.

In regression analysis, atypical values can be distinguished between two types.
Atypical observations in
\bm{Y}|\bm{x}
indicate model failure and are referred to as (vertical) outliers.
Meanwhile, atypical observations with respect to
\bm{X}
are called leverage points.
It is useful to distinguish between the two types of leverage points: good and bad.
A bad leverage point is a regression outlier that has an
\bm{x}
value that is atypical among the values of
\bm{X}
as well.
In contrast, a good leverage point is a point that is unusually large or small among the
\bm{X}
values but is not a regression outlier, i.e.
\bm{x}
is atypical, but the corresponding
\bm{y}
fits the model quite well.
A point like this is considered “good" because it improves the precision of the regression coefficients.
The cSALCWM allows us to classify each point
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
into one of the four categories presented in Table

, and we can do this separately for each cluster.

3.1
Comparison with mixtures of Laplace regressions

The comparison involves mixtures of regression models with SAL errors (SALMRM) and cSAL errors (cSALMRM), which are respectively defined as

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x};\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha})=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)

(11)

and

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x};\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha},\delta,\eta)=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right).

(12)

Both models in (
11
) and (
12
) belong to the class of mixtures of regression models with fixed covariates.
Consequently, they suffer from the assignment independence property – that is, the group assignment probabilities do not depend on the covariates
\bm{x}
.
Moreover, these models cannot be used to detect local leverage points.

It is important to note that comparisons between (
11
) and (
9
), or between (
12
) and (
10
), are not direct.
This stems from the fact that the SAL and cSAL regression mixtures model the conditional distribution
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
, whereas the CWMs model the joint distribution
p(\bm{x},\bm{y})
.
While the fixed-covariate regression models do not provide a model for the marginal distribution
p(\bm{x})
— and thus cannot yield
p(\bm{x},\bm{y})
— it is still possible to derive the conditional distributions
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
from the SALCWM and cSALCWM frameworks.
For the cSALCWM, for example, by integrating out
\bm{y}
from model (
10
) we obtain

\displaystyle p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}\right),

(13)

which is a mixture of cSAL distributions for the
\bm{X}
only. The ratio of (
10
) over (
13
) yields

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}|\bm{x};\bm{\theta})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{G}}\pi_{j}f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|j},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|j},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|j},\delta_{\bm{X}|j},\eta_{\bm{X}|j}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}\right)f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}\left(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right),

(14)

which is the conditional distribution of
\bm{Y}|\bm{x}
from the cSALCWM and can be seen as a mixture of cSAL regression models with (dynamic) weights depending on
\bm{x}
.
The following proposition shows that the family of mixtures of cSAL regression models can be seen as nested in the family of cSALCWM, as defined by (
14
).

Proposition 1
.

If, in (
14
),
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|1}=\dots=\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|G}=\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|1}=\dots=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|G}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}
,
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|1}=\dots=\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|G}=\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}}
,

\delta_{\bm{X}|1}=\dots=\delta_{\bm{X}|G}=\delta_{\bm{X}}
, and

\eta_{\bm{X}|1}=\dots=\eta_{\bm{X}|G}=\eta_{\bm{X}}
, then mixtures of the cSAL regression models as defined in (
12
), can be seen as a particular case of the cSALCWM in (

).

Identifiability

An important point in dealing with the proposed SALCWM and cSALCWM is establishing their identifiability.
Without identifiability, the parameters might not be estimated and interpreted, and, more generally, the inference might be meaningless.
The nontrivial concept of identifiability for mixture models is defined by
Titterington
et al. (1985)
, which is used in Proposition
2
to show that the cSALCWM is identifiable (up to the label switching problem).

Proposition 2
.

Let

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\bm{\theta})=\sum_{g=1}^{G}\pi_{g}f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g})f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g})

and

\displaystyle p(\bm{x},\bm{y};\tilde{\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{g=1}^{\tilde{G}}\pi_{j}f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{y};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x};\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j}),\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Y}|j},\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{\bm{Y}|j},\tilde{\delta}_{\bm{Y}|j},\tilde{\eta}_{\bm{Y}|j})f_{\text{cSAL}}(\bm{x};\tilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\bm{X}|j},\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{X}|j},\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{\bm{X}|j},\tilde{\delta}_{\bm{X}|j},\tilde{\eta}_{\bm{X}|j}).

be two different parameterizations of the cSALCWM given in (
10
), then the equality
p(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{\theta})\equiv p(\bm{x},\bm{y},\tilde{\bm{\theta}})
implies that
G=\tilde{G}
and also implies that for each

there exists an

such that
\pi_{g}=\tilde{\pi}_{j},

\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}=\tilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\bm{X}|j},

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}=\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{X}|j},

\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}=\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{\bm{X}|j},

\delta_{\bm{X}|g}=\tilde{\delta}_{\bm{X}|j},

\eta_{\bm{X}|g}=\tilde{\eta}_{\bm{X}|j}
,
\bm{\beta}_{g}=\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{j},

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}=\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{Y}|j},

\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}=\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{\bm{Y}|j},

for maximum likelihood (ML) estimation of the parameters of the more general cSALCWM in (

), and an expectation maximization (EM) algorithm

)

5.1
An ECM algorithm for the cSALCWM

Let
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),\dots,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n})
be an observed sample from the cSALCWM (
10
).
For the application of the ECM algorithm, it is convenient to view the observed data as incomplete.
The sources of incompleteness in this context can be categorized into three distinct types.
The first source, which is a classical issue in the use of mixture models, arises from the uncertainty regarding the component membership of each observation.
This uncertainty is represented by an indicator vector
\bm{z}_{i}=(z_{i1},\dots,z_{iG})
, where
z_{ig}=1
if the observation
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
belongs to component
g
, and
z_{ig}=0
otherwise.

The second and third sources of incompleteness are specific to the model under consideration.
These arise from the fact that, for each observation, it is unknown whether it is an outlier and/or a leverage point with respect to a given component
g
.
This is denoted by the indicator vector
\bm{u}_{i}=(u_{i1},\dots,u_{iG})
, where
u_{ig}=0
if
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
is not an outlier in component
g
, and
u_{ig}=1
otherwise.
Similarly, the indicator vector
\bm{v}_{i}=(v_{i1},\dots,v_{iG})
is defined, where
v_{ig}=0
if
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
is not a leverage point in component
g
, and
v_{ig}=1
otherwise.

The complete-data are thus given by
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1},\bm{z}_{1},\bm{u}_{1},\bm{v}_{1}),\dots,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n},\bm{z}_{n},\bm{u}_{n},\bm{v}_{n})
and, from (
10
) (and, inherently, (
6
)), the complete-data likelihood function can be written as

\displaystyle L_{c}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\delta},\bm{\eta})=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{g=1}^{G}\left\{\pi_{g}\left[\left(1-\delta_{\bm{X}|g}\right)f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}\right)\right]^{1-v_{ig}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left[\delta_{\bm{X}|g}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{X}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}\right)\right]^{v_{ig}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left[\left(1-\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)\right]^{1-u_{ig}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left[\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)\right]^{u_{ig}}\right\}^{z_{ig}}.

(15)

The complete-data log-likelihood then follows from (
15
) as

\displaystyle l_{c}(\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\alpha},\bm{\delta},\bm{\eta})=

\displaystyle l_{1c}(\bm{\pi})+l_{2c}(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{X}})+l_{3c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}})+l_{3c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}})+l_{4c}(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle+l_{5c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}})+l_{5c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}}),

(16)

where
\bm{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{G})
,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}}=\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|1},\dots,\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|G}\right)
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}}=\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|1},\dots,\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}}=\left(\alpha_{\bm{X}|1},\dots,\alpha_{\bm{X}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\delta}_{\bm{X}}=\left(\delta_{\bm{X}|1},\dots,\delta_{\bm{X}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}}=\left(\eta_{\bm{X}|1},\dots,\eta_{\bm{X}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\beta}=\left(\bm{\beta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{G}\right)
,

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}}=\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|1},\dots,\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}}=\left(\alpha_{\bm{Y}|1},\dots,\alpha_{\bm{Y}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\delta}_{\bm{Y}}=\left(\delta_{\bm{Y}|1},\dots,\delta_{\bm{Y}|G}\right)
,

\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}}=\left(\eta_{\bm{Y}|1},\dots,\eta_{\bm{Y}|G}\right)
,

\displaystyle l_{1c}(\bm{\pi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\mathrm{ln}\pi_{g},

\displaystyle l_{2c}(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left[\left(1-v_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left(1-\delta_{\bm{X}|g}\right)+v_{ig}\mathrm{ln}\delta_{\bm{X}|g}\right],

\displaystyle l_{3c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left(1-v_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}\right),

\displaystyle l_{3c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}v_{ig}\mathrm{ln}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g},\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{X}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}\right),

\displaystyle l_{4c}(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left[\left(1-u_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left(1-\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)+u_{ig}\mathrm{ln}\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right],

\displaystyle l_{5c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left(1-u_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right),

\displaystyle l_{5c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}u_{ig}\mathrm{ln}f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}),\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g},\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right).

Computationally, it is more efficient to use the relationship between the SAL and the multivariate normal distribution outlined in (
2
) to rewrite
l_{c3}^{\text{good}}
,
l_{c3}^{\text{bad}}
,
l_{c5}^{\text{good}}
, and
l_{c5}^{\text{bad}}

as

\displaystyle l_{3c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left(1-v_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left[f_{\text{N}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}+w_{ig}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},w_{ig}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}\right)f_{\text{Exp}}(w_{ig};1)\right],

(17)

\displaystyle l_{3c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}v_{ig}\mathrm{ln}\left[f_{\text{N}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}+w_{ig}\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{X}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g},w_{ig}\eta_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}\right)f_{\text{Exp}}(w_{ig};1)\right],

(18)

\displaystyle l_{5c}^{\text{good}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}\left(1-u_{ig}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left[f_{\text{N}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g})+w_{ig}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},w_{ig}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)f_{\text{Exp}}(w_{ig};1)\right],

(19)

\displaystyle l_{5c}^{\text{bad}}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}u_{ig}\mathrm{ln}\left[f_{\text{N}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g})+w_{ig}\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g},w_{ig}\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)f_{\text{Exp}}(w_{ig};1)\right].

(20)

where
f_{\text{Exp}}(\cdot,1)
denotes the PDF of an exponential distribution with rate 1, i.e.,
f_{\text{Exp}}(\cdot,1)=e^{-w_{ig}},w_{ig}>0
.

The ECM algorithm iterates between three steps, an E step and two CM steps, until convergence.
The only difference from the EM algorithm is that each M-step is replaced by two simpler CM-steps.
They arise from the partition
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
, where
\bm{\theta}_{1}=(\bm{\pi},\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}})
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}=(\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}})
.

E-step

In the E-step, we compute the conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood function, denoted as

Q\left(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)

for the
(r+1)
-th iteration, where

\displaystyle Q\left(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=

\displaystyle Q_{1}\left(\bm{\pi}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)+Q_{2}\left(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{X}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)+Q_{3}^{\text{good}}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)+Q_{3}^{\text{bad}}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)

\displaystyle+Q_{4}\left(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{Y}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)+Q_{5}^{\text{good}}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)+Q_{5}^{\text{bad}}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)

(21)

which is in the same order as (
16
), where

\displaystyle Q_{1}\left(\bm{\pi}|\theta^{(r)}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\pi_{g},

(22)

\displaystyle Q_{2}\left(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{X}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left[\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left(1-\delta_{\bm{X}|g}\right)+v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\delta_{\bm{X}|g}\right],

(23)

\displaystyle Q_{3}^{\text{good}}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}}\right)=

\displaystyle-\frac{nd_{\bm{X}}}{2}\mathrm{ln}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}\right|-\frac{d_{\bm{X}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{{3_{\bm{X}|ig}}}^{(r)}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)}\left[(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\left[(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)},

(24)

\displaystyle Q_{3}^{\text{bad}}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{X}}\right)=

\displaystyle-\frac{nd_{\bm{X}}}{2}\mathrm{ln}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\left|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}\right|-\frac{d_{\bm{X}}}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\eta_{\bm{X}|g}

\displaystyle-\frac{d_{\bm{X}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{3_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{{(r)}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)}\frac{1}{\eta_{\bm{X}|g}}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{X}|g}}}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{X}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}^{(r)},

(25)

\displaystyle Q_{4}\left(\bm{\delta}_{\bm{Y}}|\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left[\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left(1-\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right)+u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\right],

(26)

\displaystyle Q_{5}^{\text{good}}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{nd_{\bm{Y}}}{2}\mathrm{ln}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{ln}\left|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right|-\frac{d_{\bm{Y}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{3_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{{(r)}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)}(\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}))^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}\left(\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}))^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-u_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}\left(1-v_{ig}^{(r)}\right)\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)},

(27)

\displaystyle Q_{5}^{\text{bad}}\left(\bm{\beta},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}},\bm{\eta}_{\bm{Y}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{nd_{\bm{Y}}}{2}\mathrm{ln}(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\left|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}\right|-\frac{d_{\bm{Y}}}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{G}_{g=1}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{ln}\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}

\displaystyle-\frac{d_{\bm{X}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}v_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{3_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{{(r)}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)}\frac{1}{\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}}(\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}))^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}}}(\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}))^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{Y}|g}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g=1}^{G}z_{ig}^{(r)}u_{ig}^{(r)}\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}^{(r)}.

(28)

To do this we need to calculate
\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(Z_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\right)
,
\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(V_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}_{i}\right)
,
\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(U_{ig}|\bm{y}_{i},\bm{z}_{i}\right)
,
\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,

\mathrm{E}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{1_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
,

i=1,\dots,n
and
g=1,\dots,G
. Note that the terms on the
Q
-function where
\mathrm{E}_{3_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{3_{\bm{X}|ig}}
,

\mathrm{E}_{3_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
,
and
\mathrm{\tilde{E}}_{3_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
are constant with respect to the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
; therefore, they are not required in our calculations but we provide them nevertheless for completeness. The updates are calculated as

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(Z_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{g}^{(r)}f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}^{(r)}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)}\right)f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\delta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)}{p\left(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)}

\displaystyle:=z_{ig}^{(r)},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(V_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-\delta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)f_{\text{SAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)}{f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\delta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)}:=v_{ig}^{(r)},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{\bm{\theta}^{(r)}}\left(U_{ig}|\bm{y}_{i},\bm{z}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)}\right)f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}^{(r)}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)}\right)}{f_{\text{cSAL}}\left(\bm{y}_{i};\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}^{(r)}\right),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(r)}\right)}:=u_{ig}^{(r)}.

(31)

Since
W_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},z_{ig}=1\sim\mathcal{GIG}(a_{g},b_{ig},v)
and
\tilde{W}_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},z_{ig}=1\sim\mathcal{GIG}(a_{g},c_{ig},v)
where
\mathcal{GIG}(a,b,v)
denotes the generalized inverse Gaussian (GIG) distribution with parameters
a>0,b>0
and
v\in\mathcal{R}
,
a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}=2+\left(\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{-1}\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}
,
b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}=\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)

and

c_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}=\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\left(\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}\right)
and
{v_{\bm{X}}=(2-d_{\bm{X}})/2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}^{(r)}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}\left(W_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}+1}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)}{\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)},

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}^{(r)}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}\left(W_{ig}^{-1}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}+1}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)}{\sqrt{b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)}-\frac{2v_{\bm{X}}}{b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}^{(r)}_{3_{\bm{X}|ig}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}\left(\mathrm{ln}W_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=\mathrm{ln}\frac{\sqrt{b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}}{\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}K_{v_{\bm{X}}}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}b_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{E}}^{(r)}_{1_{\bm{X}|ig}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}\left(\tilde{W}_{ig}|\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{(r)}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{c_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}+1}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}c_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)}{\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}}K_{v_{\bm{X}}}\left(\sqrt{a_{\bm{X}|g}^{(r)}c_{\bm{X}|ig}^{(r)}}\right)},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{E}}^{(r)}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}

Notes on an EM algorithm for the SALCWM

Let

The ML estimates for parameters of the SALCWM follow easily from the ECM described in Section

In this case, we employ an EM algorithm instead of an ECM algorithm, as there is no need to estimate the contamination parameters (i.e.,

Consequently, the EM algorithm only consists of a single M-step, instead of the two CM-steps required in the ECM algorithm.

This uncertainty is represented by an indicator vector

if the observation
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
belongs to component

, and

otherwise, as in the case of the SALCWM.
Since we do not account for additional sources of uncertainty in this case of whether a point
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
is an outlier or a leverage point, we set
\bm{v}_{i}
and
\bm{u}_{i}
in the ECM algorithm equal to
\bm{0}
.

Thus, in the M-step on the
(r+1)-th
iteration, the EM algorithm updates for

,

,
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}

,

5.3
Initialization

The choice of the starting values for EM-based algorithms constitutes an important issue
(
Biernacki
et al., 2003
;
Punzo and
McNicholas, 2017
)

In the first iteration of the EM algorithm for the SALCWM,
we follow the initialization approach used in the
G
-component
MixSAL
package
(
Franczak
et al., 2018
)
, where initial cluster labels are assigned using k-means clustering.

\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)}
is then computed as the weighted mean of
\bm{X}
of the points assigned to the
g
-th cluster, while
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)}
is the weighted covariance matrix.
The asymmetry parameter,
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)}
, is initially set to
\bm{0}
, ensuring a symmetric starting point before the EM algorithm iteratively adjusts it to account for skewness.
Similarly,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(0)}
is computed as the weighted mean of
\bm{Y}
of the points assigned to the
g
-th cluster, while
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(0)}
is the weighted covariance matrix.
The mixing proportions are initialized as the mean of the cluster assignment matrix.

For the
G
-component cSALCWM, we adopt the approach used by
Punzo and
McNicholas (2017)
.
The
G
-component SALCWM in (
9
) can be seen as nested in the
G
-component cSALCWM in (
10
) when
\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\to 0^{+}
or
\delta_{\bm{X}|g},\delta_{\bm{Y}|g}\to 1^{-}
, and
\eta_{\bm{X}|g},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}\to 1^{+}
, for
g=1,\dots,G
.
Under these conditions,
u_{ig},v_{ig}\to 0^{+}
,
i=1,\dots,n
and
g=1,\dots,G
, causing model (
10
) to tend to (
9
).
Consequently, the posterior probabilities, say
z_{ig}^{(0)}
, obtained from the EM algorithm for the SALCWM – along with the constraints
u_{ig}^{(0)},v_{ig}^{(0)}\to 0^{+}
, and
\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)},\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)}\to 1^{+}
– can be used to initialize the first CM-step of our ECM algorithm in Section
5.1
.
From an operational point of view, due to the monotonicity property of the ECM algorithm, this also guarantees that the observed-data log-likelihood of the cSALCWM will always be greater than, or equal to, the observed-data log-likelihood of the initial SALCWM.
In our analysis, we set
u_{ig}^{(0)},v_{ig}^{(0)}=0.001
and
\eta_{\bm{X}|g}^{(0)},\eta_{\bm{Y}|g}^{(0)}=1.001
. These values are not set equal to 0 and 1, respectively, to avoid singularity issues within the first iteration
(
Punzo
et al., 2020
)
.

5.4
Convergence

The Aitken acceleration
(
Aitken, 1926
)
is used to assess the convergence of our EM algorithms described in Section
5.1
and
5.2
.
With this estimate, we can determine whether the algorithm has reached convergence, meaning the log-likelihood is sufficiently close to its estimated asymptotic value.
The Aitken acceleration at iteration
r+1
is calculated as

\displaystyle a^{(r+1)}=\frac{l^{(r+2)}-l^{(r+1)}}{l^{(r+1)}-l^{(r)}},

where
l^{(r)}
represents the observed-data log-likelihood at iteration
r
. Based on this, the asymptotic estimate of the log-likelihood at iteration
r+2
is given by

\displaystyle l^{(r+2)}_{\infty}=l^{(r+1)}+\frac{1}{1-a^{(r+1)}}\left(l^{(r+2)}-l^{(r+1)}\right).

Following
Böhning et al. (1994)
, the EM-based algorithms are considered to have converged when the difference

\displaystyle l^{(r+2)}_{\infty}-l^{(r+1)}<\epsilon

is both positive and smaller than a predefined threshold
\epsilon
. In our analysis, we set
\epsilon=0.00001
.

5.5
Dealing with infinite log-likelihood values

As documented in
Morris
et al. (2019)
and
McLaughlin
et al. (2024)
, complications can arise when estimating the location parameter
\bm{\mu}_{g}
of a SAL and cSAL distribution, and consequently, the SALCWM and cSALCWM. Computational singularities occur when the parameter
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}
or
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}|g}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}\right)
is equal, or very close, to some observation
\bm{x}_{i}
or
\bm{y}_{i}
, respectively, in the data. In our CWM family, such singularities manifest when calculating the expected values
\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}
or
\mathrm{E}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
(and
\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{2_{\bm{X}|ig}}
or
\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{2_{\bm{Y}|ig}}
in the case of the cSALCWM). To remedy this issue, we stop updating
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}
and
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}|g}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}_{g}\right)
if the Euclidean distance between
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{X}|g}
and
\bm{x}_{i}
, or between
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{Y}|g}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\beta}\right)
and
\bm{y}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
g=1,\dots,G
, is less than a user-specified value that is sufficiently small. In practice, we found that
10^{-5}
was an effective value for our purposes. As noted by
Franczak
et al. (2013)
, this is a simple but effective solution to dealing with the problem at hand. Other methods for dealing with infinite likelihoods can be found in
Fang
et al. (2023)
.

6
Sensitivity analysis

In this study, we perform a sensitivity analysis to investigate the impact of atypical observations on the SALCWM and cSALCWM.
To generate the data, we follow the simulation study performed by
Punzo and
McNicholas (2017)
and we consider the following data generation processes with univariate conditional/marginal distributions (i.e.,
d_{\bm{X}}=d_{\bm{Y}}=1
) and
G=2
mixture components:

1.

SALCWM with 1% of points randomly substituted by outliers with coordinates
(\mu_{X|2},y^{*})
, where
\mu_{X|2}
is the mean of the second mixture component, and
y^{*}
is generated from a uniform distribution over the interval
(8,10)
;

2.

SALCWM with 1% of points randomly substituted by good leverage points with coordinates
(x^{*},y^{*})
, where
x^{*}
is generated from a uniform distribution over the interval
(8,10)
and
y^{*}=\beta_{02}+\beta_{12}x^{*}
lies on the straight line on the second mixture component;

3.

SALCWM with 1% of points randomly substituted by bad leverage points with coordinates
(x^{*},y^{*})

where both
x^{*}
and
y^{*}
are generated from a uniform distribution over the interval
(8,10)
;

4.

SALCWM with 1% of points randomly substituted by noise points generated from a uniform distribution over the interval
(-8,8)
.

All of these data generation processes share the following parameters

\displaystyle\pi=0.4,\quad\bm{\beta}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\
-0.2\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\beta}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\
0.2\end{pmatrix},\quad\Sigma_{Y|1}=\Sigma_{Y|1}=0.5,\quad\alpha_{Y|1}=-0.2,\quad\alpha_{Y|2}=0.2.

The parameters relating to
X
will be specified depending on either assignment independence or assignment dependence.
The four scenarios described cover various situations involving possible atypical values in real-world data, as outlined in Table
1
. Under each scenario, we generate 100 samples considering two different sample sizes,
n=250
and
n=500
. The models are directly fitted with
G=2
.
For comparison, we report the bias and the mean squared error (MSE) of the estimates for the mixture weight
\pi_{1}
and all regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{1}=(\beta_{01},\beta_{11})
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}=(\beta_{02},\beta_{12})
. Additionally, we assess the cSALCWM’s ability to effectively classify atypical points according to Table
2
. Specifically, we report the true positive rate (TPR), which measures the proportion of atypical observations that are correctly identified as atypical, and the false positive rate (FPR), corresponding to the proportion of typical points incorrectly classified as atypical.

\displaystyle\mu_{X|1}=\mu_{X|2}=0,\quad\Sigma_{X|1}=\Sigma_{X|2}=1,\quad\alpha_{X|1}=\alpha_{X|2}=0.2.

;
Li

;

. We focus on a subset of six variables, which include red cell count (RCC), white cell count (WCC), body mass index (BMI), sum of skin folds (SSF), body fat percentage (BFT), and lean body mass (LBM). The blood-related variables (RCC and WCC) are treated as responses (
\bm{Y}

Australian Institute of Sport data

Discussion

In this paper, we introduced the shifted asymmetric Laplace (SAL) cluster-weighted model (SALCWM) as a mixture of multivariate linear regression models with multiple random covariates that allows for clustering of responses and covariates exhibiting varying degrees of skewness, which can vary across clusters. To address the impact of atypical observations, we further propose the contaminated SALCWM (cSALCWM), which simultaneously mitigates the influence of atypical values and facilitates their detection, distinguishing specifically between typical points, mild outliers, good leverage points, and bad leverage points.

An expectation-conditional maximization algorithm is discussed for maximum likelihood parameter estimation, supported by identifiability conditions. Simulation studies also illustrate the effectiveness of the cSALCWM in both mitigating the impact of atypical points and accurately identifying them. The proposed CWMs are also compared to their corresponding finite mixture of regression models (MRMs), where covariates are treated as fixed. Specifically, it is shown that by ignoring the distribution of the covariates, MRMs may fail to recognize the underlying group structure in the data. A real-world application to sports data further illustrates the potential of the proposed models. Importantly, the models are applicable across a wide range of fields, without being restricted to any specific domain.

Possible extensions of this work could include exploring parsimonious structures for the scale matrices for the SAL and cSAL distribution, as suggested by
Tortora
et al. (2024)
, and incorporating them into a cluster-weighted modelling framework, similar to the approach of
Dang et al. (2017)
for the Gaussian CWM.
