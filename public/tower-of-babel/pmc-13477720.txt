A Cross-Sectional Study to Evaluate the Economic Impact on Caregivers of Patients Undergoing Treatment for Cerebral Palsy and Disabilities at AIIMS Deoghar
Authors: Vasanth Chellamuthu, S Sharanabasappa, Amisha Chowdhury, Anasua Paria, Teena Khuriwal, Patade Vidula Rajesh, Raj Nandani Sah, Khimchali Hasnu, Manisha Meena, Anjali Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cerebral Palsy (CP) is a non-progressive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by impairments in movement and posture resulting from early brain injury. In India, the estimated incidence is approximately 3 per 1,000 live births, accounting for 15–20% of childhood physical disabilities. The condition imposes substantial psychosocial and economic burdens on families, highlighting the need for effective support systems.Methodology:A quantitative, cross-sectional study was conducted at All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Deoghar. A total of 250 caregivers were selected using a purposive sampling technique. Data were collected using structured tools assessing socio-demographic characteristics, medical expenditure, and non-medical expenditure.Results:The findings revealed a considerable financial burden among caregivers, with median medical and non-medical expenditures indicating substantial out-of-pocket costs. Individuals from lower socio-economic groups constituted the largest proportion of the sample (32%). Statistically significant associations were observed between age and non-medical expenditure (p = 0.01); caregiver education and medical (p = 0.01) as well as total expenditure (p = 0.02); occupation and total expenditure (p = 0.01); type of CP and medical expenditure (p = 0.04); and distance from healthcare facility and non-medical expenditure (p = 0.03).Conclusions:The study highlights the significant economic burden experienced by caregivers of children with Cerebral Palsy, particularly in rural and low-income settings. The most common clinical type identified was spastic CP. The mean medical and non-medical expenditures were ₹8,339.4 and ₹6,776.4, respectively. Key caregiver and contextual factors were significantly associated with these costs. The findings underscore the need for subsidized rehabilitation services, caregiver education, and improved accessibility to healthcare in order to reduce financial strain and enhance the quality of disability care

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of permanent, non-progressive disorders of movement, posture, and motor function caused by disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain. These disturbances may occur prenatally, perinatally, or in early postnatal life and are often associated with impaired sensation, cognition, communication, perception, and behaviour, as well as secondary musculoskeletal problems (1,2). CP remains the most common cause of childhood physical disability worldwide and represents a significant public health concern due to its lifelong impact on functional independence and quality of life (3).

The global prevalence of cerebral palsy is estimated to be approximately 2–3 per 1,000 live births (4,5). However, studies consistently demonstrate higher prevalence rates in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where estimates may reach up to 3.5–4 per 1,000 live births or higher in certain settings (6,7). This variation is largely attributed to disparities in perinatal care, higher rates of preterm birth, low birth weight, birth asphyxia, neonatal jaundice, and limited access to timely obstetric and neonatal interventions (8–(10).. In India, hospital-based and community-based studies have also reported a relatively higher burden of CP, reflecting ongoing challenges in maternal and child health services (11,12).

Cerebral palsy is associated with a wide range of lifelong disabilities that require continuous multidisciplinary management. Children with CP often need physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, orthopaedic care, antispasticity medications, surgical procedures, and assistive devices such as wheelchairs or orthoses (13,14). The complexity and chronicity of care make CP one of the most resource-intensive paediatric neurological conditions, placing sustained demand on both healthcare systems and families.

The economic burden of cerebral palsy is substantial and multidimensional. It includes direct medical costs such as hospital admissions, outpatient consultations, rehabilitation services, medications, and surgical interventions, as well as direct non-medical costs such as transportation, special nutrition, and caregiving-related expenses (15,16). Additionally, indirect costs, including loss of parental income, reduced workforce participation, and productivity loss, significantly contribute to the overall financial strain on households (17,18). Evidence suggests that the lifetime cost of care for a child with CP is several times higher than that of a child without disability, with costs escalating in cases of severe functional impairment and associated comorbidities such as epilepsy or intellectual disability (19,20).

In LMICs, the economic burden is further intensified by the predominance of out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure, as health insurance coverage for long-term rehabilitation and disability-related services remains limited or fragmented (21,22). This often leads to catastrophic health expenditure, forcing families to borrow money, sell assets, or compromise on other essential needs such as education and nutrition. Such financial vulnerability perpetuates a cycle of poverty and disability, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations (23,24).

In addition to financial challenges, caregiving for children with CP imposes a profound psychosocial burden. Caregivers, predominantly mothers, often experience chronic stress, anxiety, depression, physical exhaustion, and social isolation due to the demanding nature of long-term care responsibilities (25–(27).. The need for constant supervision, frequent healthcare visits, and therapy sessions disrupts family routines and limits opportunities for employment and social participation. Studies have also highlighted reduced quality of life among caregivers, particularly in settings where formal support systems and respite care services are inadequate (28,29).

Despite increasing global attention to childhood disability, there remains limited comprehensive evidence from India that simultaneously evaluates direct medical costs, indirect costs, and caregiver burden in cerebral palsy. Most available studies are either facility-based, focus on clinical outcomes, or assess only one dimension of burden, thereby underestimating the true socioeconomic impact of the condition.

Therefore, the present study aims to assess the economic burden on caregivers of children with cerebral palsy, including both medical and non-medical costs, and to identify the associated factors influencing this burden. The findings are expected to provide evidence for strengthening rehabilitation services, improving financial protection mechanisms, and guiding policy decisions for families affected by cerebral palsy.

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was estimated using the standard formula for prevalence studies: N=4PQ/D2

Where:

P = expected prevalence (assumed as 50% due to lack of prior similar studies, which provides the maximum sample size)

Q = 100 − P = 50%

D = allowable error (5%)

Substituting the values: The sample size was estimated using the standard formula for prevalence studies:

N=4x50x50/52

N=1000/25=400

Thus, the initial estimated sample size was 400.

However, considering feasibility constraints and study setting limitations, the final calculated and feasible sample size was adjusted to 250 participants.

To account for possible non-response or incomplete data, an additional 10% was added:

10% of 250=25

Therefore, the final sample size required for the study was 275 participants (250 + 25 = 275).

Accordingly, a total of 275 caregivers of children with cerebral palsy were targeted for recruitment; however, 250 complete responses were included in the final analysis after excluding incomplete or non-respondent cases.

The study adopted a purposive sampling technique to recruit eligible participants based on predefined inclusion criteria from selected healthcare settings.

Ethical consideration:

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) [IEC code-2025-03-MScN-05], AIIMS Deoghar, along with permission from the Nursing Research Monitoring Committee before initiating the study. The investigator introduced himself to participants, explained the study in their local language, and obtained informed consent. Data collection was conducted with respect to participants' privacy and confidentiality. Participants were free to withdraw at any time without providing a reason.

Data collection tool and technique:

A structured instrument created and approved by Maurya et al. (2022) was used to gather the study's data. There were three parts to the tool. The caregiver's age, gender, religion, place of residence, education, occupation, family type, socioeconomic status, financial aid, number of hospital visits, distance to the healthcare facility, number of earning members, mode of transportation, presence of other children with chronic conditions, use of health insurance, type of cerebral palsy, and patient's age were all collected in Section A, which concentrated on sociodemographic variables. This part was used for descriptive analysis and lacked a rating system. Medical expenses, including registration, tests, prescription drugs, PMR treatment, operations, and special aids, were evaluated in Section B. Non-medical costs like transportation, food, lodging, personal care products, and at-home treatments were assessed under Section C.

Statistical Analysis

The data were entered and analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Data cleaning and validation were performed prior to analysis to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables such as medical expenditure, non-medical expenditure, and total expenditure were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR), due to the presence of skewed distribution.

The distribution of cost variables was assessed using histogram plots, skewness values, and Shapiro–Wilk test of normality. Since the expenditure data were not normally distributed and showed positive skewness (right-skewed distribution), non-parametric statistical tests were applied.

For comparison of expenditure across more than two independent groups, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used. When significant differences were identified, post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were performed to determine specific group differences. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To identify factors independently associated with economic burden, bivariate analysis was first performed, followed by multivariable regression analysis for variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate testing. This was done to adjust for potential confounding variables such as age of the child, caregiver education, occupation, socioeconomic status, and distance from the healthcare facility. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and model fitness was evaluated using appropriate regression diagnostics.

Time frame for cost Estimation

The cost estimation was carried out based on recall of expenditures incurred over the preceding 12 months from the date of data collection. This included both:

➢Direct medical costs (consultation, investigations, medications, therapy, hospital visits, surgical care, and assistive devices), and

Direct medical costs (consultation, investigations, medications, therapy, hospital visits, surgical care, and assistive devices), and

➢Non-medical costs (transportation, food, accommodation, utilities, and caregiving-related expenses).

Non-medical costs (transportation, food, accommodation, utilities, and caregiving-related expenses).

Participants were asked to report average monthly or episodic expenditures, which were subsequently converted into annual cost estimates to ensure uniformity of analysis and comparability across participants.

Discussion

The present study assessed the demographic profile, cost distribution, and determinants of economic burden among caregivers of children with cerebral palsy. The findings demonstrate that cerebral palsy imposes a substantial financial burden, with both medical and non-medical expenditures contributing significantly to overall costs.

In the present study, most caregivers were in the age group of 31–39 years, predominantly male, and from rural backgrounds with lower socioeconomic status. Similar demographic trends have been reported by Vadivelan et al. (2020), who observed that caregivers of children with cerebral palsy in India are mostly young to middle-aged adults from rural and economically disadvantaged households, reflecting structural inequities in access to rehabilitation services (30). Khandaker et al. (2019) also emphasized that cerebral palsy disproportionately affects families in LMICs due to limited perinatal care and delayed early intervention services (31).

The predominance of spastic cerebral palsy (74.8%) in this study is consistent with global literature. Colver et al. (2014) reported that spastic CP accounts for nearly 70–80% of all cases worldwide (32). Gulati and Sondhi (2018) further confirmed that the spastic subtype is the most common clinical presentation in Indian settings due to perinatal hypoxic events and neonatal complications (33).

The economic analysis in the present study revealed that both medical and non-medical expenditures contribute substantially to caregiver burden. The high utilization of diagnostic tests such as MRI, CT scans, and laboratory investigations reflects dependence on hospital-based diagnostic evaluation. Novak et al. (2020) reported similar patterns, noting that in LMICs, diagnostic and pharmacological interventions often dominate care pathways, while structured rehabilitation services remain underutilized due to accessibility and affordability barriers (34).

A major finding of this study was that non-medical expenditure, particularly transport, food, and accommodation, formed a significant proportion of total costs. Tonmukayakul et al. (2018) reported that indirect costs in cerebral palsy often exceed direct medical expenses, especially in long-term care scenarios (35). Brehaut et al. (2004) also highlighted that caregiver-related costs, including travel and productivity loss, represent a hidden but substantial component of economic burden in chronic pediatric conditions (36).

The mean medical and non-medical expenditures observed in the present study were ₹8,339.4 and ₹6,776.4, respectively, indicating considerable financial strain. These findings are comparable to Arneson et al. (2015), who demonstrated that lifetime costs of cerebral palsy are several times higher than in unaffected children, with severity significantly influencing expenditure levels (37). Fairhurst (2016) similarly described cerebral palsy as an “iceberg of need,” where visible medical costs represent only a portion of the overall burden (38).

Significant associations were observed between caregiver education, occupation, family type, and distance from healthcare facilities with total expenditure. Higher education levels were associated with increased expenditure, possibly due to greater awareness and utilization of specialized services. Al-Gamal and Long (2013) reported similar findings, stating that caregiver education and socioeconomic status significantly influence healthcare-seeking behaviour and expenditure patterns in chronic paediatric conditions (39).

In this study, nuclear families incurred higher costs compared to joint families. Ketelaar et al. (2008) reported that reduced family support increases caregiver stress and financial burden, as joint families often share caregiving responsibilities and reduce indirect costs (40). Additionally, caregivers residing more than 50 km from healthcare facilities incurred significantly higher non-medical expenditure, particularly transport costs. Khandaker et al. (2019) also emphasized geographic inaccessibility as a major determinant of delayed and costly care in LMICs (31).

Interestingly, socioeconomic status and type of cerebral palsy were not significantly associated with total expenditure, although slightly higher costs were observed in more severe forms. Arneson et al. (2015) reported that the severity of disability is positively correlated with lifetime costs, although variability exists depending on access to services and family coping capacity (37).

Overall, the findings of this study reinforce global evidence that cerebral palsy imposes a multidimensional economic burden on families, particularly in LMIC settings where out-of-pocket expenditure is high, and rehabilitation services are limited. The study highlights that non-medical costs and accessibility barriers are major contributors to financial strain, often exceeding direct medical expenses.

Results

The study included caregivers of children with cerebral palsy, with the majority belonging to the age group of 31–39 years (44.4%), and males constituted 61.6% of the study population. Regarding religion, most participants were Hindus (78.4%), followed by Muslims (20.4%). A higher proportion of caregivers resided in rural areas (60%), while 13.6% were from semi-rural areas. In terms of educational status, 25.6% had primary education and 36.8% had secondary education. The majority of caregivers were daily wage workers (40.8%) and private employees (35.6%). Most families were joint families (57.6%), and a significant proportion belonged to lower socioeconomic status (32%) and lower-middle socioeconomic status (23.2%). Among the children with cerebral palsy, the spastic type was the most common (74.8%), followed by the ataxic type (14.4%). More than half of the families (56.8%) did not receive any financial assistance. Nearly half of the participants (48.8%) reported two hospital visits. Most families (66.8%) resided within 50 km of healthcare facilities, and public transport was the most commonly used mode of travel (82.8%). Health insurance coverage was very low (10%), indicating a high dependence on out-of-pocket expenditure. The majority of children were aged below 5 years (57.2%), followed by 6–15 years (28%).

Regarding direct medical expenditure, most participants incurred relatively low costs for diagnostic and treatment services. A majority spent ₹0–200 on registration fees (58.8%), while 74.4% spent ₹0–999 on blood investigations. Metabolic screening costs were within ₹0–999 for 82.4% of participants. Most children (98.4%) had not undergone an ECG. MRI spine costs were mostly within ₹0–1999 (94.8%), X-rays within ₹0–250 (77.6%), MRI brain within ₹0–1999 (61.6%), and CT brain within ₹0–1999 (72.4%).

In terms of treatment costs, 54% of participants reported spending ₹0–1999 on regular medications, while 90.8% did not incur any additional medication expenses. Occupational therapy costs were low, with 96.8% spending ₹0–199. A majority of participants (97.6%) had not undergone any surgical procedures. Regarding assistive devices, most families spent within low-cost ranges for walkers (99.2% ₹0–2999), wheelchairs (94.8% ₹0–999), braces (98.4% ₹0–1999), canes or crutches (98.4% ₹0–999), and hearing aids (99.6% ₹0– 999), indicating limited utilization of high-cost rehabilitation aids.

Non-medical expenditure was mainly related to transport, food, accommodation, utilities, and personal care. For transport, 98.8% of participants spent ₹0–999 on taxi services, while 84.8% spent ₹0–599 on public transport. Grocery expenses were mostly within ₹0–3999 (88.8%), and 99.6% of participants spent ₹0– 1499 on restaurant food. Electricity expenses were predominantly ₹0–999 (79.2%), and internet expenses were ₹0–499 in 74.8% of households.

Among personal care items, 97.6% spent ₹0–249 on hair care, 96% spent ₹0–2499 on clothing, and 72.8% spent ₹0–499 on diapers. A majority of families (72%) reported no expenditure on home care therapies, while the remaining participants incurred varying costs ranging from ₹300 to ₹4000, depending on therapy type and frequency.

The mean medical expenditure per participant was ₹8,339.4, while the mean non-medical expenditure was ₹6,776.4, indicating a considerable financial burden on families. Significant associations were observed between several variables and expenditure patterns. Age of the child was significantly associated with non-medical expenditure (p = 0.01), while religion showed an association with medical expenditure (p = 0.05). Caregiver education was significantly associated with both medical (p < 0.001) and total expenditure (p = 0.02). Occupation also showed a significant association with total expenditure (p = 0.01). The type of cerebral palsy was associated with medical expenditure (p = 0.04), and distance from healthcare facilities was significantly associated with non-medical expenditure (p = 0.03). Family type also influenced expenditure, with nuclear families incurring higher costs compared to joint families (p = 0.05).
