Diagnostic accuracy of Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin (NGAL) for inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review and bivariate diagnostic meta-analysis
Authors: Ting Yu, Fanyu Meng, Kai Ma, Chen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically assess the diagnostic accuracy of neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and to compare its performance with fecal calprotectin (FC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) using marker-specific diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched for diagnostic studies using endoscopy and/or histopathology as the reference standard. The primary synthesis used directly reported complete four-cell table data and marker-specific bivariate random-effects models (Reitsma). Exploratory analyses using sensitivity/specificity records were performed only as supplementary checks because patient populations, thresholds, sample types, and reference standards were not sufficiently exchangeable for a formal diagnostic network meta-analysis. Subgroup feasibility, direct-data sensitivity checks, continuity correction use, and Deeks funnel plot asymmetry were assessed.ResultsSeventy-eight studies with 148 diagnostic records were included. The primary bivariate analysis was based on 27 directly reported complete four-cell records, including 11 FC, 7 CRP, 5 ESR, and 4 NGAL records; 85 sensitivity/specificity records were used only for supplementary exploratory analyses. NGAL showed a pooled sensitivity of 90.9% (95% CI: 66.1%-98.1%) and specificity of 76.1% (95% CI: 42.4%-93.2%), but the estimate was imprecise and underpowered because only 13 NGAL records and 4 complete four-cell records were available. Exploratory network diagnostics did not support clinically interpretable ranking.ConclusionNGAL may have high sensitivity for IBD diagnosis, but current evidence is preliminary, heterogeneous, and based on very limited complete four-cell data. NGAL should not be interpreted as a replacement for FC or as a stand-alone diagnostic test. Larger prospective studies with standardized thresholds, sample types, and reference standards are needed.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, NGAL, Diagnostic accuracy, Bivariate meta-analysis, Fecal calprotectin

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) mainly includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) are a class of autoimmune diseases characterized by chronic, recurrent inflammation of the intestine [1]. Since the mid-20th century, the incidence of IBD has continued to rise in Western countries such as North America, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand, and its prevalence continues to rise despite stabilizing in recent years [2]. At the same time, the incidence of IBD in developing countries such as Asia and South America is rapidly increasing, and IBD has evolved into a global public health problem that puts a heavy burden on national health systems [3].

The pathophysiological mechanism of IBD is complex, involving the interaction of multiple factors such as genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and intestinal microecological disorders [4]. At present, the diagnosis of IBD is still based on colonoscopy combined with histopathological examination as the gold standard. However, endoscopy has limitations such as invasiveness, high cost, complex operation, and poor patient compliance, especially in pediatric patients, dynamic monitoring of disease activity, and emergency screening [5]. Historically, the diagnosis of IBD relied primarily on symptom-based assessments, which often led to diagnostic delays and misclassification. The widespread adoption of ileocolonoscopy with histopathological examination since the late 20th century has substantially improved diagnostic accuracy, enabling direct visualization and biopsy of intestinal mucosa. However, this invasive approach is ill-suited for repeated monitoring, population screening, or emergency settings, prompting a sustained search for reliable non-invasive alternatives.Therefore, finding non-invasive biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity is an important direction for IBD diagnostic research.

Currently, commonly used noninvasive markers include fecal calprotectin (FC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). FC, as a direct indicator of intestinal inflammation, is mainly derived from activated neutrophils and has been widely validated in the diagnosis and activity assessment of IBD [6, 7]. However, FC testing relies on the collection of stool samples, and there are limited accessibility issues in some clinical scenarios (e.g., emergency, intensive care, poor patient compliance). CRP and ESR, as classic markers of systemic inflammation, are readily available and inexpensive, but they lack specificity for local inflammation in the intestine and may be false negative in patients with isolated small bowel CD or disease remission [8, 9]. Current treatment strategies for IBD have evolved from symptom control to objective targets, including endoscopic remission and mucosal healing, as recommended by the Selecting Therapeutic Targets in Inflammatory Bowel Disease (STRIDE) initiative. In this paradigm, biomarkers play an increasingly critical role—not only in initial diagnosis but also in dynamic monitoring of disease activity, prediction of relapse, and assessment of treatment response. Non-invasive markers are particularly valuable for guiding therapeutic escalation or de-escalation decisions without repeated endoscopic procedures, thereby improving patient adherence and reducing healthcare costs [6–8].

Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), also known as lipocalin-2 (LCN2), is a neutrophil-associated inflammatory marker that may be measured in blood, fecal, or urine samples [10–15]. In recent years, novel biomarkers have emerged to address unmet clinical needs, particularly for patients in whom stool collection is challenging—such as those in emergency departments, intensive care units, or pediatric settings. Among these candidates, NGAL has attracted attention because it can be measured in blood, urine, or feces, offering flexible sampling options compared with FC, which requires stool collection. In IBD, NGAL expression is related to epithelial and neutrophil-driven inflammation. Nevertheless, available NGAL studies differ in sample type, assay platform, threshold, patient spectrum, and reference-standard implementation. NGAL should therefore be regarded as a candidate marker whose clinical role remains uncertain rather than as an established substitute for FC.

Previous studies have evaluated FC, CRP, and ESR in IBD diagnosis [16–18], and several studies have explored NGAL [19–21]. The evidence base for NGAL remains limited and heterogeneous. This study primarily aimed to estimate marker-specific diagnostic accuracy using bivariate diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis and to compare NGAL descriptively with FC, CRP, and ESR. Network-based comparisons were treated as exploratory only because the included studies differed in patient spectrum, thresholds, sample types, and reference-standard implementation.

Methods

Protocol and reporting

The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420261306386). During revision, the analytic framework was clarified: marker-specific bivariate diagnostic meta-analysis was used as the primary synthesis, whereas network meta-analysis was downgraded to an exploratory supplementary analysis because the exchangeability assumption was not adequately supported by the included evidence.

Literature search strategy

This study adhered to PRISMA-DTA principles. PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched from January 1, 2010, to February 9, 2026. The search combined IBD terms (inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis), biomarker terms (NGAL, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, lipocalin-2, fecal calprotectin, CRP, C-reactive protein, ESR, erythrocyte sedimentation rate), and diagnostic accuracy terms (sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic accuracy, ROC, AUC). Equivalent controlled-vocabulary and free-text strategies were adapted for each database, and reference lists of included studies were manually screened.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with confirmed or suspected IBD, including CD and UC; (2) evaluation of at least one marker among NGAL, FC, CRP, and ESR; (3) use of endoscopy, histopathology, or a clinically defined reference standard; and (4) availability of diagnostic accuracy data. The primary bivariate meta-analysis required directly reported complete four-cell table data (TP, FP, FN, TN). Records reporting sensitivity and specificity without complete four-cell data were retained for descriptive or exploratory supplementary analyses only, and were not used as the primary evidence base.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) reviews, case reports, animal studies, and conference abstracts without extractable data; (2) studies without extractable diagnostic accuracy data; and (3) non-English literature.

Data extraction

The extracted variables included first author, year, country, study design, sample size, age range, marker type, sample type when identifiable, detection method, cut-off value, sensitivity, specificity, and four-cell table data (TP, FP, FN, TN). For the primary analysis, only directly reported complete four-cell tables were used. Records based only on sensitivity/specificity and sample size were flagged separately for supplementary description or exploratory checks. When a complete four-cell table contained a zero cell, 0.5 was added to each cell for continuity correction, and the number of corrected records was reported.

Quality evaluation

The quality of the included studies was evaluated using QUADAS-2 across patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow/timing domains. The risk of bias in each domain was rated as low, unclear, or high. QUADAS-2 findings were incorporated into interpretation, especially when assessing heterogeneity, applicability, and the strength of NGAL-related conclusions.

Statistical analysis

Bivariate meta-analysis

A bivariate random-effects model was used to estimate pooled sensitivity and specificity for each marker. The Reitsma model was selected as the primary model because the objective was to obtain marker-specific pooled sensitivity and specificity from complete four-cell data while accounting for their correlation and between-study heterogeneity. HSROC was considered conceptually relevant because thresholds varied across studies; however, the small number of complete four-cell records for several markers, especially NGAL, limited stable threshold-parameter estimation. Therefore, threshold heterogeneity was addressed through cautious interpretation and subgroup feasibility assessment rather than formal HSROC modelling.

Exploratory network analysis

Diagnostic network meta-analysis was not used as the primary analysis because such comparisons require reasonable exchangeability across studies, including comparable patient spectrum, disease severity, reference standards, sample types, and threshold context. These assumptions were not adequately met. An exploratory network check was retained only to audit the feasibility of indirect comparisons. Pairwise logit contrasts for sensitivity and false-positive rate were examined using the netmeta package in R, but ranking results were not used for clinical inference.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses were planned by sample type, pediatric versus adult population, IBD subtype, and diagnostic purpose. Subgroup pooling was performed only when enough complete four-cell records were available within a clinically coherent subgroup. Otherwise, subgroup feasibility was reported descriptively. Direct-data sensitivity analysis and leave-one-out checks were interpreted cautiously because several marker-specific evidence sets, especially NGAL, contained very few records.

Publication bias

The Deeks funnel plot asymmetry test was used to evaluate publication bias when enough records were available, with P < 0.05 indicating significant asymmetry. Results were interpreted cautiously because small marker-specific datasets have low power for detecting asymmetry.

Results

Literature search and screening

The search identified 7,241 records, of which 5,443 remained after duplicates were removed. After screening and full-text review, 78 studies covering 148 diagnostic biomarker records were included. The analysis denominators were clarified as follows: 148 records formed the full extraction set, 27 directly reported complete four-cell records entered the primary bivariate meta-analysis, 85 sensitivity/specificity records were retained for supplementary exploratory analyses, and 74 records belonged to the previously connected exploratory network subset. Thus, the 27-record and 85-record counts refer to different analysis sets rather than inconsistent study selection. The literature screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was above moderate, with major uncertainties concentrated in the patient selection domain.

Exploratory network analysis

Network-based comparison was retained only as an exploratory feasibility audit rather than as the primary synthesis. Across the sensitivity/specificity database, 85 records were potentially eligible for exploratory checks, and the previously connected subset contained 74 records. However, the included studies differed substantially in patient spectrum, disease severity, thresholds, sample type, and reference-standard implementation; therefore, the exchangeability assumption required for diagnostic network meta-analysis was not met.

The statistical revision code generated 68 within-study pairwise logit contrasts from the exploratory network-eligible records. All 68 contrasts were zero or near-zero because several multi-marker records contained identical sensitivity/specificity values across biomarkers within the same study. Formal network diagnostics were run after installing the required R packages; the exploratory network Q statistic was 0 for both sensitivity and specificity, reflecting the zero-contrast structure rather than clinically meaningful consistency. Therefore, P-score rankings were not used for clinical superiority claims or recommendations. The exploratory network feasibility audit is summarized in Table 9 and illustrated in Figs. 11 and 12. These exploratory materials were used only to assess the feasibility of indirect comparison and were not used to rank markers or support clinical superiority claims.

Subgroup analysis

The planned subgroup analyses were limited by sparse complete four-cell data, particularly for NGAL. NGAL had 13 total records, but only 4 complete four-cell records for primary pooling. By sample type, blood-based NGAL contributed 2 complete four-cell records, fecal NGAL contributed no complete four-cell record, and the remaining complete records had unclear sample type. Pediatric/adult subgroup pooling was also not feasible because all four NGAL four-cell records were adult or unclear.

Disease-subtype and activity-stratified analyses were also audited. For NGAL, no CD-only, UC-only, mixed CD/UC, activity-assessment, or diagnosis stratum had at least four complete four-cell records. Therefore, formal NGAL subgroup meta-analyses by sample type, pediatric versus adult population, IBD subtype, or disease activity were not performed. These limitations support interpreting the pooled NGAL estimate as preliminary rather than stable across subgroups. Sensitivity analysis

Direct-data sensitivity analysis confirmed that the primary bivariate estimates were based on directly reported complete four-cell records. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis should be interpreted cautiously because marker-specific evidence sets differed substantially in size. FC, CRP, and ESR showed no obvious single-study dominance. For NGAL, however, only 4 complete four-cell records were available in the primary analysis, making leave-one-out estimates unstable and strongly influenced by individual studies. The sensitivity check therefore supports the conclusion that NGAL findings are preliminary rather than robustly established. Time trends in diagnostic accuracy

Meta-regression by publication year was explored descriptively. Apparent time trends should be interpreted cautiously because the marker-specific datasets were small and heterogeneous, especially for NGAL. These exploratory findings do not change the primary conclusion that standardized prospective studies are needed before NGAL can be assigned a firm clinical role.

Discussion

This study evaluated NGAL together with FC, CRP, and ESR using a systematic review and marker-specific bivariate diagnostic meta-analysis. The main revision is interpretive: the available data do not support a primary diagnostic network meta-analysis for clinical ranking. Included studies varied by patient population, disease severity, sample type, threshold, assay, and reference-standard implementation. A marker-specific bivariate meta-analysis is therefore the more appropriate primary synthesis.

NGAL showed high pooled sensitivity, but this finding was based on only 4 complete four-cell records and should therefore be considered preliminary and underpowered. The evidence is insufficient to conclude that NGAL has superior clinical utility for ruling out IBD, and the manuscript should avoid presenting NGAL as a replacement for FC or as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Diagnostic value and clinical positioning of NGAL

The potential strength of NGAL lies in its high sensitivity, but the certainty of this estimate is low. In a typical clinical scenario with a pre-test probability of 50%, the probability transformation in Table 13 suggests that a negative NGAL result may reduce post-test probability. However, because NGAL evidence is sparse and heterogeneous, this should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as proof of a reliable exclusion strategy.

NGAL can be measured in blood or other biological samples and may be useful where stool sampling is difficult. Nevertheless, the current evidence does not support presenting NGAL as an alternative or replacement for FC. FC remains the better-established intestinal inflammatory marker, whereas NGAL should be regarded as an investigational adjunct requiring further validation.

NGAL’s low specificity (0.761, 95% CI: 0.424–0.932) and wide confidence interval suggest limited reliability for positive diagnosis. Heterogeneity analysis showed that tauSens and tauSpec for NGAL were the highest among markers, indicating substantial variation between studies. This was closely related to the limited number of NGAL-related studies, the 4 complete four-cell records, and the lack of standardization in detection methods, sample types, and cut-off values. Therefore, NGAL is not currently recommended as an independent diagnostic indicator.

Comparative positioning of traditional markers

The marker-specific bivariate results suggest that ESR, FC, CRP, and NGAL each have distinct diagnostic profiles, but the present evidence does not support a formal clinical hierarchy across markers. ESR showed high pooled sensitivity and specificity in the complete four-cell dataset, but this estimate may partly reflect the clinical spectrum of the included studies, including patients with more active systemic inflammation. Its performance may be lower in remission, mild disease, or disease phenotypes with weaker systemic inflammatory responses [22].

FC remains the best-established intestinal inflammatory marker [6, 7]. Unlike systemic markers, FC directly reflects neutrophil-related intestinal inflammation [16] and has stronger clinical validation than NGAL. Although stool collection may be difficult in some settings, the current NGAL evidence is too sparse to justify describing NGAL as a substitute for FC. Fecal NGAL and combined biomarker strategies remain biologically plausible, but they require prospective validation with standardized sample types, thresholds, and reference standards before clinical recommendations can be made.

CRP is widely available and may provide complementary information, particularly when systemic inflammation is present. However, CRP may be normal in some patients with endoscopically active IBD, especially in isolated small-bowel CD or milder disease. In the present review, NGAL should be discussed only as a potential adjunctive marker rather than as a tool that can reliably capture suspected cases or replace established diagnostic pathways.

Analysis of heterogeneity sources

The inter-study heterogeneity observed in this study (Table 8) may stem from the following factors.

First, the heterogeneity of cut-off values is the main source. As shown in Table 3, the cut-off values of FC were 1.5–782.0 µg/g, and the cut-off values of NGAL were 5.0 –220.5 ng/mL. There is no accepted standard for the NGAL cut-off value, and differences between studies are particularly significant, which is one of the key bottlenecks hindering its clinical adoption.

Second, technical-methodological differences also affect the results. NGAL’s detection platform has not been standardized, and different ELISA kits and sample types (serum/plasma/urine) have been used across studies, limiting comparability of test results [23]. FC’s assay kits cover multiple platforms, such as PhiCal, EliA, and Quantum Blue, and calibration differences across platforms can also introduce system bias.

Third, the heterogeneity of the patient population cannot be ignored. Some studies included initially screened patients, and some referred patients with high suspicion of IBD, and the disease spectrum effect may affect estimates [24]. In addition, differences in IBD subtypes (CD vs. UC) may affect the diagnostic presentation of markers, but because the original study presented data in a way that did not allow separate subtype analysis, this analysis could not be performed.

Fourth, the difference between geographical and temporal dimensions. Limitations of this study

The following limitations exist in this study. First, NGAL evidence was very limited: only 13 NGAL records were extracted, and only 4 directly reported complete four-cell records were available for the primary bivariate meta-analysis. Therefore, NGAL estimates are preliminary, underpowered, and associated with wide confidence intervals. Second, although subgroup analyses by sample type and pediatric versus adult population were planned, formal NGAL subgroup pooling was not feasible because the available complete four-cell records were too sparse; no fecal NGAL record had complete four-cell data, and all four complete records were adult or unclear. Third, substantial heterogeneity was present, especially for NGAL, with high tau values for both sensitivity and specificity. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in sample type, assay platform, cut-off value, patient spectrum, disease severity, and reference-standard implementation. Fourth, some records without complete four-cell tables could only be retained for supplementary sensitivity/specificity description and exploratory checks, so the 27-record primary dataset and the 85-record supplementary dataset should not be interpreted as the same evidence set. Fifth, exploratory network analysis was not used for clinical ranking because exchangeability across patient populations, thresholds, sample types, and reference standards was not adequately supported.

Clinical implications

Based on the marker-specific bivariate analysis and illustrative probability transformation (Table 13), NGAL may be considered a candidate adjunctive marker in settings where stool sampling is difficult. However, these clinical implications should be framed cautiously. The low negative likelihood ratio is hypothesis-generating rather than definitive because it is derived from only 4 complete four-cell records.

The current evidence does not justify recommending NGAL as a stand-alone screening test, a replacement for FC, or a marker that can independently rule out IBD. If NGAL is used clinically, it should be interpreted together with symptoms, FC when available, CRP or ESR, and confirmatory endoscopic or histopathological assessment. Combined biomarker strategies are promising but require direct prospective evaluation rather than inference from the present exploratory comparisons.

Future studies should use standardized NGAL assays, prespecified thresholds, clearly defined sample types, comparable reference standards, and sufficient pediatric and adult subgroups. Such studies are needed before NGAL can be assigned a stable role in IBD diagnosis or disease-activity stratification.

Conclusion

This systematic review found that NGAL may show high sensitivity for IBD diagnosis, but the current evidence is preliminary, heterogeneous, and underpowered. The primary synthesis should be interpreted as marker-specific bivariate diagnostic meta-analysis rather than diagnostic network meta-analysis. NGAL should not be presented as a replacement for FC or as a stand-alone diagnostic test. Larger prospective studies with standardized thresholds, sample types, assay platforms, and reference standards are needed before NGAL can be assigned a firm clinical role.
