The Effects of Nursing Care Applied in the Intensive Care Unit on a Case of Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis: A Case Report
Authors: Zuhal Gulsoy, İclal Özdemir Kol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis are rare mucocutaneous diseases characterized by widespread epidermal necrosis and a high mortality rate. These conditions are often associated with drug exposure and require complex treatment and nursing care because they cause diverse symptoms in patients. Meticulous implementation of nursing protocols, particularly minimizing wound infections and bedsores that can lead to sepsis, is crucial to reducing mortality by reducing complications. This study aimed to emphasize the importance of nursing care by drawing attention to the treatment and care services provided to patients with a diagnosis of toxic epidermal necrolysis in collaboration with multidisciplinary healthcare professionals. We present a case of toxic epidermal necrolysis following ciprofloxacin treatment in a 32‐year‐old male patient. The patient, who had a high mortality risk, had 100% involvement. The patient had bullous lesions in the mouth, nose, perineum, scalp and entire body. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and presented with agitation, pain, burning sensation, tachycardia and hyperthermia. Thanks to multidisciplinary collaboration and nursing care, the patient recovered and was transferred to the internal medicine ward. Multidisciplinary collaboration and nursing care reduce the development of complications in patients with toxic epidermal necrolysis, thus decreasing mortality and increasing survival. The most important approaches in the care of these patients are infection prevention, wound care and the implementation of supportive care strategies to prevent long‐term complications. In this case, early admission of the patient to the intensive care unit and meticulous implementation of nursing care with a specially prepared sterile care bundle were crucial for reducing complications, ensuring wound healing and improving survival. Hospitals should develop standard care protocols that include rapid triage and specialized care requirements for patients diagnosed with toxic epidermal necrolysis.

Introduction

Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) is an acute hypersensitivity reaction characterized by widespread necrosis and desquamation of the epidermis, usually occurring in response to drugs [1, 2]. In this condition from the dermis in more than 30% of the body surface. If the involvement is less than 10%, it is considered Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), and if it is greater than 30%, it is considered TEN [1, 3, 4]. SJS and TEN are very serious clinical conditions with a high mortality rate and are often associated with certain drugs [5, 6, 7, 8]. Although it is thought that some drugs, infectious agents, malignancies, connective tissue diseases, immune system‐related conditions such as vaccination and radiation therapy play a role in the development of the disease, its pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated [9, 10, 11, 12].

Although numerous drugs have been reported as causative agents for TEN, the most common reactions are reported to be sulfonamides, penicillin and beta‐lactam antibiotics, carbamazepine‐containing antiepileptics, allopurinol‐containing drugs used in gout treatment and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) [5]. Some genetic predispositions, such as HIV, active malignancy, collagen diseases, bacteria such as herpes simplex virus and mycoplasma, and measles vaccination, have been identified as risk factors for SJS [8, 9].

Despite the wide variety of local and systemic treatment options available in recent years, optimal supportive care remains the most important and effective treatment approach [3]. The SCORTEN scale, developed for TEN, is widely used to estimate disease severity and, consequently, mortality. The SCORTEN scale calculates mortality by patient age (> 40), heart rate (> 120/min), serum urea levels (> 28 mg/dL), serum glucose (> 252 mg/dL), bicarbonate levels (< 20 mEq/L), the presence of malignancy and the total body surface area affected (> 10%) (score/mortality ratio %; 0–1/3.2, 2/12.2, 3/35.3, 4/58.3, ≥ 5/90) [13]. Rapid transfer of patients with a SCORTEN score of 3 or higher to a burn unit or intensive care unit is crucial [5]. These patients experience numerous and very serious health problems simultaneously. The addition of factors such as fear of death, loneliness and intense pain to the physiological health problems makes the treatment process more complex and difficult. Patients require close monitoring and treatment in a multifaceted way, addressing aspects such as nutrition, wound care, infection prevention, pain relief, fear and feelings of loneliness. This study aims to emphasize the importance of collaborative treatment and nursing care provided by multidisciplinary healthcare professionals in the management of TEN patients. In this context, it highlights the positive impact of nursing interventions and interdisciplinary collaboration on survival rates in critically ill patients with high mortality.

Case Report

This case presents a 32‐year‐old male patient diagnosed with SJS and TEN. The patient had no comorbidities and was not taking any regular medications. He had used paracetamol for 3–4 days due to an upper respiratory tract infection. As his symptoms worsened, he experienced a burning sensation during urination and presented to the emergency department at another hospital. Ciprofloxacin was prescribed despite the patient's history of penicillin allergy. After two doses of ciprofloxacin, he developed a rash, erythema, dry mouth and throat swelling. The patient was then referred to a university hospital. Upon evaluation in the emergency department, his SCORTEN score was calculated as 2, corresponding to a mortality risk of 12.2%. He was subsequently admitted to the Anaesthesia Intensive Care Unit (AICU) with diagnoses of SJS and TEN on 29 December 2021.

The patient had no significant past or family history. Physical examination revealed a moderate‐to‐poor general condition, consciousness, orientation and cooperation. Spo2: 90%, BP: 134/90 mmHg, pulse: 93/min and respiratory rate: 31/min. There was widespread epidermal separation on the oral and orbital regions, trunk and back, as well as extensive erythematous lesions that had opened in some places. The genital area was erythematous, and there were widespread lesions in the mouth and nose. There was vision loss in both eyes. He was in pain and agitated. The patient was monitored. An arterial catheter and a Foley catheter were inserted. A nasogastric (NG) tube could not be inserted due to bleeding lesions in the mouth and nose. Low‐level oxygen therapy was initiated via mask. A central venous catheter (CVC) was inserted at the site with the least lesions (left jugular vein) for parenteral nutrition (TPN), medical and fluid therapy. After CVC insertion, fluid replacement therapy, TPN and medication administration began effectively. Consultation with relevant departments was requested, and the patient's treatment plan was planned.

Medication Administration

The patient was started on antihistamine, analgesic, cortisone, proton pump inhibitor, low molecular weight heparin, antipsychotic and calcium therapy. Intravenous immunoglobulin therapy was administered for 3 days. Calcium, bicarbonate, magnesium and erythrocyte replacements were administered based on blood values. N‐acetyl cysteine, ascorbic acid and intravenous glutamine treatments were administered to accelerate wound healing. Additionally, the patient with low albumin levels received albumin supplementation for 13 days. The patient with ocular involvement was evaluated daily by an ophthalmologist. The patient was treated with benzalkonium chloride eye drops, drops containing a combination of hyaluronic acid and trehalose, cyclosporine eye drops, carbomer (polyacrylic acid) artificial tears and sterile dexpanthenol eye drops. On the 5th day of his admission to the AICU, the patient began to experience hyperthermia and an increase in his lesions, agitation and pain was observed. The patient had a pulse rate of 145/min, a NaHCO3 of 16.6 mEq/L, a fever of 38.8°C, and a 100% TEN involvement percentage (Figure 1). His SCORTEN score increased to 4, and his mortality rate increased to 58.3%. During the care of the patient's hands and feet, numerous bullous ruptures (Figure 2) and the skin peeled, resulting in the patient losing all her fingernails and toenails (Figure 3). Physical contact increased the number and depth of lesions on the patient's body. Antibiotic therapy was initiated based on the recommendation of the infectious diseases specialist.

Isolation Precautions

The patient was extremely vulnerable to sepsis due to extensive epidermal loss. The patient, who was at high risk of cross‐contamination and nosocomial infection from healthcare workers, was transferred to a single room and a team of experienced nurses and support staff was formed in the intensive care unit and assigned to this team the responsibility for follow‐up care across all shifts. Wound care was provided using sterile technique and appropriately frequent aseptic dressings. The use of protective equipment was made mandatory upon entering and exiting the patient's room. Visitors were admitted to the patient twice daily, under the supervision of a healthcare professional, unless otherwise necessary (e.g., the patient's request for their father to be with them at night). The bedding was sterilized and sterile care bundles containing all the medical supplies needed for the patient's care were created. These bundles included sheets, a bedcover, pillowcases, an apron, liquid vaseline, gauze, a scalpel for debridement of necrotic tissue, a clamp, bandages, materials for oral care (chlorhexidine‐based oral care solution and oral care swabs), and pads for hygiene. Records of lesioned areas were kept daily, and changes in the wounds were monitored and recorded daily.

Wound Care

During the 08:00–16:00 shift, the same three experienced nurses and one auxiliary staff member performed the patient's wound care. Thus, unless an additional situation occurred (such as contamination or displacement of the dressing), wound care was performed once daily. According to the physician's instructions, sedation and additional analgesic medications were administered during wound care to prevent the patient from experiencing pain. While one nurse participated in a non‐sterile manner to organize the procedure, two nurses and the auxiliary personnel performed the wound care under sterile conditions. Central Catheter Care and Nutritional Support

Due to the absence of suitable tissue for suturing at the patient's central catheter site, a dressing could not be applied. The catheter insertion site was rinsed with sterile distilled water and then covered with a sterile gauze for protection. Serum sets and other medical supplies were replaced according to the infection control committee's recommendations.

Nutritional support was provided to support the immune system and accelerate wound healing, and fluid and electrolyte balance was maintained. For nutritional support, protein and calorie needs were calculated daily and planned accordingly. Once the nasal mucosa healed, TPN was discontinued to reduce the risk of infection. Given the patient's difficulty swallowing, a NG tube was inserted, and enteral feeding was initiated. The patient was assessed daily for swallowing difficulties. As these issues improved, oral water was introduced while continuing enteral feeding. Once the patient could swallow water comfortably, different foods were gradually included in his diet. When he tolerated solid foods, the NG tube was removed.

Fluid Replacement

Due to the loss of the skin barrier which caused significant insensible fluid loss, fluid intake and output were monitored, and fluid replacement was ordered based on central venous pressure. Because the patient had draining wounds all over his body, bed sheets were placed under these areas. These protective sheets were weighed and added to the amount of fluid output, allowing for more accurate fluid intake and output monitoring and fluid replacement. Furthermore, the necessity of catheters and all invasive materials was assessed daily. As the patient began to heal, the need for catheters decreased, and catheterization was discontinued.

Pain Management

The patient experienced severe pain due to mucosal and skin lesions. To assess the presence and severity of pain, evaluations were made hourly. In collaboration with the physician, additional analgesic medication was administered as needed. The patient was unable to sleep because of the pain, resulting in adjustments to their medication schedule, which took into account dressing times and sleep patterns.

Oral and Ocular Care

Oral and eye care were performed using sterile distilled water and sterile supplies as often as necessary. Initially, oral care was not very effective because the patient had difficulty opening his mouth due to pain from the lesions. However, as the pain and burning sensation subsided, oral care became more effective and was conducted three times a day. To ensure the patient's cooperation with these procedures, we timed painful care activities with the administration of analgesic medication.

Psychological Support

To facilitate psychosocial recovery, communication was prioritized to help the patient cope with anxiety, depression and body image issues. Given that the patient's vision problems and pain might be contributing to agitation, after the administration of analgesic medication, the patient was contacted to allow him to speak and express his wishes and thoughts. Furthermore, the patient's availability for communication was constantly considered. Intermittent attempts were made to listen to music of her choice to encourage relaxation. Information was provided before her care and treatment, and any questions he had were answered, providing opportunities for her to express her wishes and thoughts. Because the patient particularly desired his father's presence at night, his father was contacted to ensure that he spent time with him at specific times.

Following supportive care measures such as care of the lesioned areas, position changes and other supportive care, the patient's general condition improved. The patient was subsequently transferred to the internal medicine ward on the 29th day of her admission to the AICU.

Discussion

Steven‐Johnson syndrome and TEN are severe, life‐threatening clinical conditions that often result from adverse drug reactions. Our literature review revealed no other case reports in which symptoms began within 24 h of drug ingestion. Notably, despite 100% skin involvement, our patient survived. Some sources indicate that TEN typically develops 1 to 3 weeks after the administration of a suspected drug, while others suggest a timeframe of approximately 4–28 days [8, 14]. The most commonly implicated drugs include penicillin antibiotics, carbamazepine (an antiepileptic), quinolones and allopurinol (used for gout treatment). In our case, ciprofloxacin was identified as the suspected drug. The first step in treatment is to identify and discontinue the suspected drug [8]. Subsequent steps focus on providing symptomatic care, such as ensuring airway and hemodynamic stability, wound care and pain management [8]. In this instance, ciprofloxacin was thought to be the cause of TEN, and it was discontinued before the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. Literature suggests that TEN can manifest within 48 to 72 h, often presenting with early prodromal symptoms that mimic an upper respiratory tract infection; these include fever, sore throat, cough, vomiting, diarrhoea, muscle pain, lethargy and fatigue [3]. In our case, the initial symptoms resembled those of an upper respiratory infection, aligning with existing literature. However, the absence of fever during the initial days of the illness, along with its later onset following the rash, is not consistent with typical findings. This atypical presentation may be related to paracetamol treatment initiated after the patient was diagnosed with an upper respiratory tract infection.

Our patient received treatment and nursing care through an approach driven by multidisciplinary collaboration. According to the literature, medical treatment for conditions such as SJS and TEN includes corticosteroids, cyclosporine, intravenous immunoglobulin and TNF‐α inhibitors [8]. Similar treatments were administered to our patient. SJS and TEN are conditions that have a high mortality rate, making early diagnosis and a comprehensive treatment approach essential [15]. Despite 100% skin involvement, the patient survived with the support of the implemented treatment protocol and meticulous nursing care addressing the patient's clinical, physiological and psychological needs. A SCORTEN score above 5 in TEN is associated with a 90% mortality rate [13]. In the case of our patient, the SCORTEN score increased to 4, resulting in a 58.3% mortality rate. The literature recommends that patients with a SCORTEN score of 3 or higher should be promptly transferred to a burn unit or intensive care unit [5]. Our patient was admitted to the ICU earlier, with a SCORTEN score of 2, which is earlier than the clinical level recommended in the literature. We believe that earlier admission to the intensive care unit had a significant impact on our patient's survival. Some studies in the literature suggest that the burn unit is the most appropriate place for treating patients with TEN due to its specialized facilities, including isolation capabilities, appropriate wound management, continuous nursing care and advanced technology such as air‐flow mattresses. Additionally, these studies indicate that early admission to a burn unit can reduce the risk of infection and infection‐related death, as well as shorten the length of hospital stays [16, 17]. Given that many of the advanced technological devices and features available in burn units at the time of these studies are also found in today's ICUs, we believe this conclusion remains applicable to ICUs as well.

The intensive care unit environment and its noise, light and equipment can negatively affect the patient [18]. Separation from family and social environment in the intensive care unit is a significant source of stress for the patient [19]. In critical illness, many patients begin to contemplate issues such as the meaning of life, death, faith and hope ([20]). In addition, the loss of organ function increases the stress and fear experienced by the patient. Ocular involvement and vision loss can occur in TEN cases. The psychology of a patient who is blind and experiencing intense pain is usually a complex state where intense feelings of uncertainty, fear, and loss of control are experienced together [21, 22]. Vision loss can create a psychological burden, uncertainty, and a feeling of loss of control over life in the patient [21, 22]. In our case, there was involvement and vision loss in both eyes. The patient's loss of sight, intense pain throughout the body, the fear of not knowing if their life was in danger, and the feeling of dependence on others for their needs caused the patient to experience both anxiety and helplessness. Therefore, empathetic communication, continuous information and a reassuring approach are of great importance in healthcare. During their care, the nurses tried to convey to the patient the message that they were constantly there for them. The patient's wishes were taken seriously, and they were given the opportunity to spend time with their father and pray with him. In this way, an environment was created that would make the patient feel safe and comfortable, reducing their stress and fears.

The careful implementation of nursing protocols is essential for reducing complications and mortality rates, particularly by minimizing wound infections and bedsores that can lead to sepsis. In this context, long‐term complications such as blindness, dysphagia and urinary tract issues can be prevented through thorough care of mucous membranes, including oral, ocular and genital areas. In this case, close monitoring of the patient's nutritional status and daily protein intake by nurses, along with meticulous wound, catheter and physical care using patient‐specific care kits to prevent secondary infections, likely contributed significantly to the patient's outcome.

Conclusion

It is essential to recognize the vital role of nursing interventions and to enhance standards of care for rare and life‐threatening conditions like SJS and TEN. Although the care for patients with SJS/TEN shares similarities with approaches used in burn units, it is distinct due to the underlying immunological processes involved, necessitating specialized protocols.

Upon admission to the AICU, the patient presented with burn‐like lesions covering his entire body, including the scalp. Managing this condition, which is characteristic of TEN, requires intensive nursing care due to the risks of infection, dehydration and multiple organ failure. Nursing care serves as a crucial support system for patients with TEN and significantly affects their quality of life and chances of survival. Effective and multidisciplinary nursing approaches have been shown to reduce mortality rates and lower the risk of complications in these patients. The early admission of this patient to the AICU and the careful implementation of nursing care, using a specially prepared sterile care bundle, were essential in minimizing complications and promoting wound healing. It is vital for hospitals to develop specialized education programmes for nurses, equipping them with advanced training in wound care, mucosal care and the administration of pharmacological treatments for TEN patients. Furthermore, hospitals should establish standardized care protocols that include rapid triage and specific care requirements for individuals diagnosed with TEN.
