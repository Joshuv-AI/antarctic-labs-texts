Background

In Denmark, women between the ages of 50 and 69 are offered breast cancer screening free of charge biennially as part of a centrally organized national breast cancer screening programme, which has been gradually implemented since the 1990es to become national in 2008. The overall participation rate is 83 pct. showing high trust and confidence in the programme [1].

Among countries with organized screening programmes, mammography is most often recommended for women aged 50 to 69 years, gender and age being the only criterions for eligibility though optimal screening intervals are discussed. There is widespread agreement that time has come for an update and a modernization of the programmes as discussed at a European consensus conference held in 2019 [2] and supported by the Council of the European Union in an updated recommendation on cancer screening [3]. Additionally, these thoughts are further motivated by the need to use the scarce health care resources more intelligently.

Today the prerequisites exist to initiate risk-stratified screening according to a woman’s individual risk of breast cancer as opposed to the existing one-size-fits-all age-based programme [4]. A personal risk-stratified programme screens intelligently by intensifying the screening effort for women at high risk and reducing it for women at low risk. It is expected that some of the disadvantages inherent in any existing screening programme such as false positives and unnecessary radiation exposure can be reduced by distributing the screening effort according to risk and thereby accelerate earlier diagnosis for women at high risk [5].

Risk-stratified breast cancer screening has not yet been implemented in any country, but Denmark and other European countries will in the future very likely move towards more personalized screening [2]. Several studies have shown high interest among women for risk-stratified breast cancer screening [6–10], although they express hesitancy about extending screening intervals if found to be at low risk [8, 10–12]. However, this has not previously been studied in Denmark before this qualitative study and a Danish survey among 5.001 women made in 2023 by colleagues at the Danish Cancer Society as a follow-up on our study [13].

Two large ongoing studies, MyPebs funded by the European Union, and the American WISDOM study will investigate long-term outcomes such as compliance to personalised breast cancer screening, frequency of advanced breast cancer tumours, and on even longer term breast cancer mortality [14, 15].

Introducing risk-stratified breast cancer screening requires that the women invited understand the concept and agree to have their breast cancer risk estimated and accept a possible change in their screening interval. In our study the women had to imagine a hypothetical scenario of being invited to a randomized trial and have their breast cancer risk assessed.

The aim of this study was to explore Danish women’s understanding of breast cancer risk and their attitudes towards the concept of risk-stratified breast cancer screening.

Methods

Setting

In the Capital Region of Denmark an ongoing randomized trial “Population-based Randomized Study Of a Novel breast cancer risk ALgorithm and stratified screening” (PRSONAL) will assess risk-stratified breast cancer screening among 1.000 women aged 50–67 [16]. As background for the information strategy of PRSONAL it was essential to qualitatively explore how women felt about having their risk score estimated and their screening intervals adjusted accordingly.

In the PRSONAL study a risk score is estimated according to the BOADICEA model (the CE marked CanRisk model PMID: 3,335,023) predicting the individual woman’s risk of breast cancer [17]. The model considers the BIRADS density from a mammogram, measurement of height and weight, collection of a blood sample for a genetic analysis of common genetic variants associated with risk of breast cancer and self-reported information about life-style factors, family history of breast cancer and reproductive history.

Half of the women will be randomized to the intervention group, have their risk estimated and will be offered screening according to their risk being low, intermediate, elevated, or high. Women with low risk will be advised to have a screening mammography performed every fourth year, women with intermediate risk will be offered screening biennially and women with elevated or high risk will be offered screening every year. The other half, the control group, will continue screening every two years.

Some studies include preventative measures, like lifestyle changes or chemo prevention for women at high risk [7, 18, 19]. Some studies suggest no screening for women at low risk [6, 18, 20]. In PRSONAL women at low risk will be offered screening every fourth year, but they do not waive their right to biennial screening if they prefer to continue in the national programme. Therefore, the low-risk group has a truly free choice, and the primary outcome of the trial will be their adherence to the longer screening intervals.

Study design

This is a qualitative study using an anthropological approach [21, 22] and interpretive description [23]. The anthropological approach focuses on understanding the women’s world view i.e. including other aspects of health than those related to personalized screening and also letting the women’s voices be represented by quotes. Data was collected using semi-structured focus group interviews (FGIs) and telephone interviews.

Three FGIs with women aged 50-69 were planned in March 2020. One FGI was conducted in the beginning of March, but COVID-19 lock-down made us convert two FGIs into 15 individual interviews. To ensure data saturation, we held three additional FGIs in May 2022. For each FGI we recruited women at same age range and with similar educational background level. Thus, we were able to explore topics with women from a shared background, enhancing the quality of the insights gathered. See Table 1 for the composition of the FGIs. We used the same interview guide for both types of interviews. First author, a trained anthropologist, moderated all focus groups and second author, also a skilled interviewer acted as assistant moderator. The FGIs leveraged group dynamics to generate diverse perspectives. All FGIs were recorded and transcribed.

Composition of focus groups

First and second author supplied by a colleague conducted the telephone interviews. These interviews allowed for an in-depth exploration of each participant’s views and helped us explore findings from the initial FGI. During the telephone interviews we took thorough notes and used the notes as our study data. The approach to notetaking was principally determined in the moment, tailored to the interviewer and the woman interviewed, reflecting the anthropological approach.

Before each interview, participants were briefed, and informed consent was obtained. This involved providing the women with detailed information about the study, including its objectives and the voluntary nature of participation. At the beginning of each interview, we gained insight into women’s knowledge about breast cancer and their self-perceived risk prior to a presentation of the concept of risk-stratified screening. The interview guide also consisted of questions regarding screening experience, understanding of risk-stratified screening, and possible incentives and barriers towards participating in a randomized trial. We presented the women with the premises of PRSONAL and the four risk-groups: high, elevated, intermediate, and low. After each interview, participants were debriefed to address any questions or concerns they might have had.

The interviews were conducted in Danish. The semi-structured interview guide, as well as the quotes used to illustrate the findings, have been translated into English. For confidentiality, pseudonyms are used to present quotes. See additional file 1 for the interview guide.

Participants and recruitment

In total 46 women participated in the study. The recruitment was undertaken by an external agency according to the following selection criteria:

1) Women residing in the Capital Region of Denmark, 2) aged 50-69 (with some age distribution), 3) representing all educational levels, 4) women already attending the screening programme, 5) women not yet attending, 6) and women having no history of breast cancer. 18 of the interviewed women aged 50-51 still awaited their first screening invitation due to lack of staff in the current programme. All data including screening status were self-reported with no external validation, see Table 2.

Analysis

In our analysis we refer to the COREQ checklist [24] for reporting qualitative studies, see additional file 2. Transcripts and notes were discussed and coded according to the most salient themes emerging across the interviews using systematic text condensation [25]. First and second author cooperated in performing the analysis by both repeatedly reading the transcriptions and made an ongoing comparison of different views on data. We used open coding, made notes and headings in the transcripts to obtain a comprehensive view of data and looked for traces and patterns.

Results

In the present study we explored women’s views of the current screening programme, their understanding of breast cancer risk and the premises of risk-stratified screening exemplified by the PRSONAL trial, and their approach to screening based on an individual risk calculation. Our findings are presented by four themes: 1) High appreciation of the current breast screening programme 2) Good knowledge about risk factors but little concern about own breast cancer risk 3) Strong interest in being invited to risk-stratified screening and 4) Trust and distrust of changed screening intervals.

To clarify the terms used in the following: “one” precisely refers to one, “very few” indicates approximately 1–5, “few” refers to 6–10, “some” indicates 11–15 and “many” defines more than half of the women.

High appreciation of the current breast screening programme

Generally, the women highly appreciated the current screening programme, whether already attending or still longingly awaiting their first invitation. Women already attending described breast screening as highly professional and efficient and valued the staff at the screening clinics for being very nice, competent and professional. Having regular mammograms was seen as important and made them feel secure. Breast screening appeared to be a natural part of the women’s health prevention behavior in line with physical exercise and a healthy diet. It was also described as a kind of defense mechanism preventing them from thinking too much about breast cancer. Screening provided reassurance because early detection was seen as the best chance of surviving breast cancer.

All research, all information in this area points out that the earlier cancer is found, the better the chance of survival. You don’t need to read a lot about this before you know how important screening is. Jane, 50, not yet screened.

Screening was felt to have far more advantages than disadvantages. Many considered the mammography unpleasant or even painful:

‘I think it hurts a lot and you think ‘will my boobs ever be normal again’? Iben, 50, screened once.

But they described this as an acceptable inconvenience not least because they felt so well taken care of. Another disadvantage mentioned by a few women was that waiting for the result of their mammogram made them worry about having cancer. Some mentioned the limitation of mammograms by not detecting breast cancer with a 100 percent certainty. A few worried about false negative results referring to recent media coverage about women who had received a normal test result but later turned out to have breast cancer. But none of this hindered their adherence to breast screening and they generally trusted the answer they got. Very few worried about the exposure to X-rays and only one woman mentioned overdiagnosis while reading about this in a brochure during the FGI.

Good knowledge about risk factors but little concern about own breast cancer risk

We wanted to explore what the women knew about the causes of breast cancer and how they perceived their own breast cancer risk, before being introduced to the premises of the trial, PRSONAL. None of the women had ever had their risk score estimated. The women reeled off the common causes of breast cancer and expressed an awareness that lifestyle changes may reduce their risk. Only one woman said she knew nothing about the causes of breast cancer.

Despite a high level of knowledge about breast cancer risk factors, most women had not given much thought to their own risk and were not too worried about it. However, breast cancer was an issue lingering in the back of their minds. The thought of their own risk mostly popped up when awaiting the result of their mammogram or when someone close was diagnosed.

We got it [breast cancer] in our family – my mom and her mum. I worry, I would say continuously, not in an uncontrolled way, but it is in the back of my mind. I don’t have my mum anymore. She died from breast cancer. /…/ It’s an ongoing reminder and an important reason [for being screened]. Lise, 64, screened regularly.

Most women had friends, colleagues, or family with a history of breast cancer. For some women having family members with breast cancer this did not affect their perception of own risk, while others worried. Not having a family history of breast cancer led others to believe they were at low risk.

The women discussed whether and how they could change their risk group. A few women suggested they would stop drinking wine on weekdays if that could reduce their risk. They also wondered how the different risk factors interacted whether for example genes counted more than lifestyle factors.

Strong interest in being invited to risk-stratified screening

The women were introduced to the PRSONAL trial setup, and despite a high confidence in the current screening programme they generally considered a screening concept including personal factors to be more reliable than screening based on age alone. They did not mind having a blood test taken and would gladly contribute more personal information than today like age at menarche, age at menopause, family history of cancer etc. to have their risk estimated.

I think that personalized breast cancer screening will be more trustworthy than the current screening programme. It makes good sense that screening is based on the circumstances of the individual. Stine, 51, not yet screened.

A reason for wanting to participate in a trial if possible was a wish to help improve the existing screening programme, but the women also wanted to know more about the scientific reasoning behind personalized screening and whether other countries were also preparing to change their programmes. Some wondered if risk-stratified screening was yet another example of cost reduction in health care expenses. Others regarded it as a way of prioritizing health care resources:

It's great that it [PRSONAL] is tailored to each woman and that women with a particular risk of getting breast cancer are the primary focus. This way resources are spent right. We don’t have endless resources, so we need to use them the best way we can. Anne, 52, screened once.

Asked whether they wanted health prevention advice as part of their risk allocation and how this should be done, they declined, arguing that if ending up being at high risk they would know where and how to find advice about preventative measures that could reduce their risk of breast cancer.

Trust and distrust of changed screening intervals

The women reflected upon how they might react to being told their personal breast cancer risk. A few women were hesitant about knowing their risk, but no one said upfront that they did not want to know. They furthermore understood that low risk is not the same as no risk just as high risk is not equal to ever developing breast cancer. But they worried whether women with high risk would experience increased anxiety and considered it to be an ethical dilemma that some women would needlessly worry about a cancer that might never develop.

This is part of the dilemma, isn’t it, because it isn’t certain that the women in the high-risk group will ever develop cancer. It’s always a balance between making people feel ill or, really, it’s a balance. It’s interesting. It’s an ethical dilemma, I think. Theresa, 50, not yet screened.

On the other hand, being allocated to the high-risk group with the ensured increased screening intensity, would make them feel better taken care of.

Being at high-risk is a pity, but it’s fine to have more frequent screenings then. There are women dying from breast cancer and I don’t want to be included in the statistics, whether my risk is high or low. This program (risk-stratified screening) takes your risk of getting breast cancer into account and I’d feel relieved if told that my risk is low and should my risk be high, I’d feel well taken care of. Mona, 51, screened once.

In general, the women said they would trust the risk score calculated by the BOADICEA model, although some felt more trust in a high-risk score than a low-risk score. Even though no one preferred to be in the high-risk group, they were positive about the possibility of knowing their risk and consequently be offered more frequent screenings. The idea of knowing one’s risk also triggered a feeling of assurance and security, whether imagining it to be high or low:

If I was told I only needed a screening every fourth year, I would confidently lean back. But if I had been told that I maybe was in one of the other risk groups, I would be happy to be screened more often. I’m like, I want to know precisely where I am. Insecurity eats me up. Laila, 60, screened regularly.

The thought of prolonging the screening intervals for low-risk women was an important barrier of their potential participation in personalized screening. Some proposed that intervals should only change for high-risk women.

The disadvantage [of personalized screening] is perhaps that you'll overlook some [cancers] in the low-risk group because they are screened less often. I think about whether the offer may be inferior to the existing [screening programme]. I think it would be nice to be checked every year, and if there's something it will be discovered in time. Maybe I'll be more concerned if I only have a check every fourth year. It would be fine if everybody with low risk will be screened like today. Lone, 50 years, screened once.

There was a tendency to expect wanting to opt out of a trial if being labelled low risk because they would worry about interval cancers and therefore would demand thorough information about possible effects of prolonged screening intervals before entering a trial:

If you're in the low-risk group there's probably no need to worry. Because you're a little worried every time [you get a screening mammogram]. If you're in the low-risk group, it must be made clear before participating, that you will be screened every fourth year instead of every second year. Karina, 51, not yet screened.

Despite skepticism about prolonged screening intervals for women at low risk, about half the women agreed they would accept screening every fourth year if they received reassuring information that changes were based on solid scientific grounds and that the doubling of intervals did not jeopardise their breast cancer survival. The women feeling comfortable with screening every fourth year further pinpointed that they would not hesitate to see their GP if finding a lump or change in their breast in between screenings.

I would feel comfortable with the results. Also, if I was at low risk, I could always see my GP and get a referral to a mammogram if I experienced a lump. I am not a sceptic. I have contributed to the information given [blood test, reproduction history etc.], so I'm sure that it'll give me the right result. Caroline, 51, not screened yet.

Discussion

The women in our study demonstrated high interest in screening based on the estimation of their individual breast cancer risk and showed prospective willingness to take part in risk-stratified screening if available. Especially if being allocated to the high-risk group. This indicates that risk-stratified breast cancer screening might be feasible and acceptable in Denmark among women in the target group for breast cancer screening. But it also stresses the need for thorough risk communication that reassures women at low risk that longer screening intervals are not a deterioration and still secures the aim of screening, namely early detection of breast cancer leading to reduced breast cancer mortality. A willingness among women to accept changes is crucial for the success of risk-stratified screening.

Comparison with other studies

Like other studies we found screening to be an inherent part of the women’s self-caring, a routine expressing responsibility for taking care of their own health [10, 26]. They had strong positive and emotional ties to breast cancer screening, feeling cared for by the national screening programme and the staff at the mammography clinics as also shown in an Australian study [27].

Many women in our study still awaited their first screening mammography due to postponed screening invitations because of lack of radiology staff resources. The medias have covered stories about women being diagnosed in late stages due to delays which consequently means more treatment, risk of late effects and worse prognosis. However, neither stories about delays nor disadvantages such as false positive findings made them less trustful of the mammography screening. They were acutely aware that breast cancer is a very common cancer among women in Denmark. Instead of this making them more fearsome about breast cancer, they only showed moderate or low levels of breast cancer worry also found in other studies [13, 18].

The women knew the most common causes of breast cancer except for dense breast tissue on the mammogram consistent with American women [28]. They discussed lifestyle changes that could prevent breast cancer, such as losing weight which some found difficult because of menopause or reduce alcohol intake but screening seemingly was their number one remedy against breast cancer. Their knowledge stems from stories of breast cancer among family, friends, and colleagues, but also from the medias with women telling their personal breast cancer stories. A phenomenon found in other European studies showing how women are influenced by a high and constant media coverage [10, 29, 30], in which breast cancer seems to be a disease with “a ubiquitous nature” [31] touching the deepest of women’s feelings and thus attracting much media attention [29]. In a Danish study, even young women in their twenties interviewed about their non-participation in cervical cancer screening, had more knowledge about breast cancer than cervical cancer [32].

Like other studies taking place at the pre-implementation stage of a randomized trial for risk-stratified screening [20, 33], the women had to imagine a hypothetical scenario of being part of a new screening concept and responding to being allocated to a risk-group. The women’s motivation for wanting to participate was essentially altruistic, they wanted to take part in research for the sake of science and future generations of women consistent with a British study with women having had their risk estimated as part of the NHS Breast Screening Programme [26].

A Danish survey among 5,001 women followed up our qualitative findings and showed that 74% of the respondents were positive towards being offered mammography more or less frequently than today based on estimates of personal risk and backed up risk-stratified screening to reduce their risk (39%) or feel safer (25%) and only be examined when necessary (22%)[13]. Other studies confirm this especially for respondents imagining themselves to be in the high-risk group with the possibility of more frequent screenings [8, 9, 34]. The hesitation among Danish women towards prolonging well-known screening intervals is characteristic for women in countries with long-standing and well-established screening programmes, like Australia, the UK and Spain [10, 26, 30].

The women were basically positive towards having their personal risk score estimated as found in other studies [18, 30]. Only a few women were hesitant about knowing their risk. In a Danish survey however, 20% did not want to have their personal risk estimated mainly because they did not want to worry (64%) or feel sick (14%) [13]. In a Spanish study more than three out of four women agreed that information about individual breast cancer risk provided reassurance while the remaining agreed that it would lead to anxiety or worry [30].

To receive a personal risk score makes women more aware that they are at risk even if low [35] and this is a potential ethical dilemma like the dilemma of women with high risk worrying about breast cancer that might never occur. Our study found that the offer of screening in itself does make women more aware of a possible risk even though most women in the age-based screening programmes are never diagnosed with cancer [36]. It is important to offer women with high risk the possibility of a consultation with a doctor if needed, which was also shown in a Dutch study [8]. But it is just as important or even more so to offer adequate information to women at low risk that will make them feel comfortable and secure with a doubling of the current screening intervals.

As pointed out in a Dutch study risk-based screening would have to respect a woman’s right not to know her risk and still ensue equitable access to breast cancer screening [37].

Even though the women in our study expressed trust in the risk prediction model behind the calculating of risk scores they hesitated to accept prolonged intervals for low-risk women like other studies [8, 13, 26, 30, 34, 38]. Also, half the women questioned the value of a low risk-score as it would make them worry about interval cancers as found in other studies [10, 30]. Less frequent screening intervals for low-risk women was an important barrier towards participating in risk-stratified screening later confirmed in a Danish survey [13]. Our findings highlight the importance of making women trust the accuracy of their individual risk measure and the need of low-risk women to receive targeted information to reassure them about the reliability of their allocated risk score.

It seems to be a contradiction in terms that the women tended to trust a high-risk score more than a low-risk score and therefore showed reluctance to accept a doubling of the screening intervals if being at low risk. We need to secure that women being invited to take part in personalized screening are well-informed and trust the information provided. But the information about any new personalized screening program must compete with the women’s existing knowledge of breast cancer and their ingrained and very strong wish to avoid it. The women know that early detection means less invasive treatment and better survival rates and therefrom their conclude that more screening must be better than less screening. On the other hand, half the women in our study anticipated that they would accept a possible doubling of screening intervals. Either way this pinpoints the importance of testing information material to ensure that the women get the thorough information they ask for.

Risk-stratified screening is quite different from the existing age-based programme with a higher degree of complexity making it more difficult to understand for lay people. Interviews with Dutch and Swedish women reported a great need to understand the scientific basis of risk-stratified screening and the subsequent screening and prevention recommendations [31, 39]. In our further work with information and website of the PRSONAL trial we have seen the importance of testing and adjusting and testing again before reaching the point where women invited to risk-stratified screening can be said to be well-informed.

As risk-stratified breast cancer screening has not yet been implemented in any country, we must await the results of the European MyPeBS and the American WISDOM study to learn about real time results and whether women accept the risk-reduction recommendations that are part of some of trials. The results of MyPeBS are planned to be used for proposing “general recommendations for more effective breast cancer screening in Europe” [14].

Methodological considerations

We had to deviate from our initial plan of exclusively using focus groups as our study method. Due to the COVID-19 lock-down we were unable to conduct physical FGIs and switched to individual interviews. This introduced a different dynamic to the data collection process and furthermore enriched our findings by providing deeper, more personal insights that usually do not emerge in a group setting.

Our dual-researcher approach ensured great reliability and validity of the findings. By independently analyzing the data and then comparing our interpretations, we minimized individual biases and enhanced the credibility of our results. This method allowed for cross-verification and a more nuanced understanding of the data, ensuring that our conclusions were well-supported and thoroughly derived.

Our analysis revealed no significant differences in attitudes towards the concept of risk-stratified breast cancer screening based on age groups or educational levels among the women in our study. However other factors, such as income level, employment status, geographic location and ethnicity could play a significant role in shaping attitudes towards screening.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first qualitative study of Danish women’s understanding and attitudes towards risk-stratified screening. It has given us valuable insights regarding factors that might influence women’s acceptance of partaking in risk-stratified screening. It has also provided us with insights into the kind of information women need to make an informed decision to participate or not considering the pros and cons of a new programme. Our findings were used for developing and testing elaborated communication on the PRSONAL website and the invitations to the trial. It is crucial that the women understand their personal risk score and that the majority of the women adhere to subsequent screening recommendations.

Limitations can be that we only included Danish-speaking women already participating or wanting to participate in screening. We cannot tell whether risk-stratified screening would potentially attract or further alienate non-participants, screening sceptics or women with minority background, or whether inequality in participation will grow due to the complexity that is inherent in a risk-stratified – and more complex—screening concept.

Conclusion

In this study we explored Danish women’s understanding of breast cancer risk and factors motivating or discouraging their participation in risk-stratified screening. In general, the women showed acceptance of a new breast cancer screening concept calculating their personal risk score. Our findings show that due to concerns about interval cancers and the changes to screening intervals, offering more intensified screening for high-risk women may be less challenging than deescalating screening for low-risk women. Whether the positive attitude and curious interest in the concept expressed in this study will translate into actual participation if implemented needs to be investigated in the ongoing Danish randomized trial, PRSONAL.
