Revisiting Diffusion Q-Learning: From Iterative Denoising to One-Step Action Generation
Thanh Nguyen, Chang D. Yoo

Abstract
The generative power of diffusion models (DMs) has recently enabled high-performing decision-making algorithms in offline reinforcement learning (RL), achieving state-of-the-art results across standard benchmarks. Among them, Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL) stands out as a leading method for its consistently strong performance. Nevertheless, DQL remains limited in practice due to its reliance on multi-step denoising for action generation during both training and inference. Although one-step denoising is desirable, simply applying it to DQL leads to a drastic performance drop. In this work, we revisit DQL and identify its core limitations. We then propose One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), a novel framework that enables efficient one-step action generation during both training and inference, without requiring auxiliary models, distillation, or multi-phase training. Specifically, OFQL reformulates DQL within the sample-efficient Flow Matching (FM) framework. While conventional FM induces curved generative trajectories that impede one-step generation, OFQL instead learns an average velocity field that facilitates direct, accurate action generation. Collectively, OFQL eliminates the need for multi-step sampling and recursive gradient updates in DQL, resulting in faster and more robust training and inference. Extensive experiments on the D4RL benchmark demonstrate that OFQL outperforms DQL and other diffusion-based baselines, while substantially reducing both training and inference time compared to DQL.



Introduction

Performance vs. Decision Frequency.
Performance (i.e., normalized score) and Decision frequency are measured on an A100 GPU and averaged across MuJoCo tasks from D4RL. Our OFQL achieves both high inference speed and strong performance, significantly outperforming prior baselines.

In recent years,
offline reinforcement learning
(Offline RL) has achieved impressive progress through the integration of diffusion models, which produce many state-of-the-art algorithms. A prominent example is Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL)
(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
, which replaces the conventional diagonal Gaussian policy in TD3-BC
(
Fujimoto and Gu 2021
)
with a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
. This approach has demonstrated substantial performance gains and has spurred widespread interest in leveraging generative models for policy learning. Notably, DQL remains competitive with or outperforms many more recent methods in both diffusion-based planning and policy optimization
(
Lu et al. 2025a
;
Dong et al. 2024
)
.

Despite its strong empirical results, DQL faces key practical limitations, including high computational demands during training and inference
(
Kang et al. 2023
;
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
, as well as susceptible training instability
(
Park, Li, and Levine 2025
)
. Upon closer analysis, we identify the key bottleneck lies in its use of DDPM-style diffusion models
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
, which involve multiple denoising steps per action, leading to slow inference. Furthermore, the training speed is doubly affected: beyond the diffusion loss, DQL requires two policy sampling per iteration—one for the current action and another for the next—to compute all loss components. In addition, DQL leverages the reparameterization trick to backpropagate through the entire denoising chain, amplifying computational load and contributing to unstable optimization. These characteristics collectively hinder DQL’s efficiency and robustness.

It is worth noting that several recent approaches have partially addressed these limitations—through improved denoising solvers
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
, avoiding backpropagation through time (BPTT)
(
Hansen-Estruch et al. 2023
)
, and the use of auxiliary models and policy distillation strategies
(
Park, Li, and Levine 2025
;
Lu et al. 2025b
)
. Nevertheless, such solutions typically introduce additional complexity, multi-phase training procedures, or undesirable trade-offs in scalability and policy quality.

As all the problems arise from the diffusion model, developing a policy capable of generating high-quality actions in a single denoising step could eliminate these issues entirely. Thus, we introduce One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), a novel framework specifically designed to enable efficient one-step action generation during both training and inference, without the need for auxiliary models, policy distillation, or multi-stage training. At the heart of OFQL is the elimination of DDPM’s computationally intensive multi-step denoising and associated reparameterization trick. By recasting DQL under the Flow Matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
paradigm, we facilitate its efficient few-step action sampling. However, conventional Flow Matching frequently yields curved trajectories, limiting one-step inference accuracy—an issue rooted in the intrinsic properties of the marginal velocity field it models. We address this by learning an average velocity field instead, enabling accurate direct action prediction from a single step. As a result, OFQL eliminates the necessity of iterative denoising and recursive gradient propagation, providing a faster, more stable, one-step training-inference pipeline. Extensive empirical evaluations on the D4RL benchmark demonstrate that OFQL achieves superior performance relative to DQL and other approaches, while significantly increase inference and training speed, as well as simplifying the policy learning pipeline.

Preliminaries

Offline RL.
A decision-making problem in reinforcement learning (RL) is typically formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma\}
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
denote the state and action spaces,
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
is the transition probability from state
s
to
s^{\prime}
after taking action
a
, and
R(s,a,s^{\prime})
is the corresponding reward. The discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
governs the trade-off between immediate and future rewards. The goal of RL is to learn a policy
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)
, parameterized by
\theta
, that maximizes the expected discounted return
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}r(s_{h},a_{h})\right].

To support policy learning, the action-value (Q) function is defined:

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}r(s_{h},a_{h})\mid s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right],

(1)

which measures the expected cumulative return starting from state
s
and action
a
under policy
\pi
.

In the offline RL setting, the agent does not interact with the environment but instead learns from a fixed dataset of transitions
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{h},a_{h},s_{h+1},r_{h})\}
. The challenge lies in learning an optimal policy solely from this static dataset that often contains suboptimal behavior, without any further exploration.

Diffusion Models.
Diffusion models are a high-performing class of generative models that learn to sample from an unknown data distribution
q(x^{0})
using a dataset drawn from it
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
;
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2020
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
)
. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
, the canonical diffusion model used in DQL, define a forward diffusion process
q(x^{1:K}\mid x^{0})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(x^{k}\mid x^{k-1})
as a fixed Markov chain that gradually corrupts data with Gaussian noise over
K
steps, where
q(x^{k}\mid x^{k-1})=\mathcal{N}\left(x^{k};\sqrt{1-\beta^{k}}x^{k-1},\beta^{k}\bm{I}\right),
and the variance schedule
\{\beta^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is predefined such that as
K\to\infty
,
x^{K}
approaches an isotropic Gaussian.

The corresponding reverse process, enables generating data from pure noise, is parameterized by
\psi
and defined as:

p_{\psi}(x^{0:K})=\mathcal{N}(x^{K};\mathbf{0},\bm{I})\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{\psi}(x^{k-1}\mid x^{k}),

(2)

which is learned by maximizing the variational lower bound

\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\frac{p_{\psi}(x^{0:K})}{q(x^{1:K}\mid x^{0})}\right]

(
Blei, Kucukelbir, and McAuliffe 2017
;
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
.

After training, sampling from
q(x^{0})
is approximated by drawing
x^{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I})
and applying the reverse Markov chain from
k=K
to
k=1
via the learn model
p_{\psi}
. Conditional generation is straightforwardly supported via
p_{\psi}(x^{k-1}\mid x^{k},c)
.

Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL)

(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
leverages the expressiveness of diffusion models by treating the reverse process of a conditional DDPM as a parametric policy:

\pi_{\theta}(\bm{a}\mid\bm{s})=p_{\theta}\left(\bm{a}^{0:K}\mid\bm{s}\right)=p(\bm{a}^{K})\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{\theta}\left(\bm{a}^{k-1}\mid\bm{a}^{k},\bm{s}\right),

(3)

where
\bm{a}^{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I})
. More specifically, following DDPM
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
)
, DQL chooses to parameterize
p_{\theta}(a^{k-1}|a^{k},s)
with the variance fixed as
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{a}^{k},k;\bm{s})=\beta^{k}\bm{I}
and the mean is defined via a noise prediction model:

\bm{\mu}_{\theta}(\bm{a}^{k},k;\bm{s})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{k}}}\left(\bm{a}^{k}-\frac{\beta^{k}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}^{k}}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{a}^{k},k;\bm{s})\right),

(4)

where
\alpha^{k}=1-\beta^{k}
,
\bar{\alpha}^{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\alpha^{i}
, and
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
is a neural network predicting Gaussian noise. To generate an action, the model sequentially samples from
K
conditional Gaussians:

\bm{a}^{k-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{k}}}\left(\bm{a}^{k}-\frac{\beta^{k}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}^{k}}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{a}^{k},k;\bm{s})\right)+\sqrt{\beta^{k}}\bm{\epsilon},

(5)

where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{I})
. Training is performed using a simplified ELBO that resembles behavior cloning. Specifically, the model minimizes the score matching loss:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DBC}}(\theta)=\\
&\mathbb{E}_{k,\bm{\epsilon},(\bm{a}^{0},\bm{s})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\big\|\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}\big(\quad\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}^{k}}\bm{a}^{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}^{k}}\bm{\epsilon},k;\bm{s}\big)\big\|^{2}\Big],\end{split}

(6)

where
k\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
(\bm{a}^{0},\bm{s})
are sampled from the offline dataset
\mathcal{D}
.

Q-Value Learning and Policy Optimization.

To incorporate RL objectives, DQL uses the Bellman backup to train a Q-network:

\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\,\bm{a}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}}\left[\left(r+\gamma\min_{i}Q_{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}(\bm{s}^{\prime},\bm{a}^{\prime})-Q_{\phi_{i}}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\right)^{2}\right],

(7)

where
i\in{1,2}
indexes the Q-networks for double Q-learning, and
(\phi^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
denote target network parameters.

The policy is then trained to maximize Q-values while staying close to the behavior policy:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DBC}}(\theta)-\alpha\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim\mathcal{D},\bm{a}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[Q_{\phi}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\right],

(8)

where
\alpha
controls the trade-off between behavior cloning and value maximization. To normalize for dataset-specific Q-value scales,
\alpha
is adapted as
\alpha=\frac{\eta}{\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s},\bm{a})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left\|Q_{\phi}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\right\|\right]},

with
\eta
being a tunable hyperparameter. The denominator is treated as a constant for optimization.

Despite being implemented with a relatively simple MLP architecture for both the policy and Q-functions, Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL) has demonstrated remarkably strong performance across standard offline RL benchmarks such as D4RL
(
Fu et al. 2020
)
, outperforming many recent diffusion-based policies and planners. Subsequent reproductions have consistently confirmed DQL’s effectiveness
(
Dong et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025a
;
Lu et al. 2025b
)
.

Nevertheless, DQL exhibits two notable limitations: (1) slow training and inference process
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
, and (2) susceptibility to instability during optimization
(
Park, Li, and Levine 2025
)
.

Rationale and methodology

The limitation of DQL at inference time stems from its reliance on a denoising diffusion process, where actions are sampled through reverse chain of
K
Gaussian transitions (Eq.
5
). Due to the Markovian nature of the DDPM framework, sampling an action during inference requires the same number of denoising steps
K
as used during training. Additionally, a large
K
is typically necessary to ensure that
\bm{a}^{K}
approximates an isotropic Gaussian. Reducing
K
breaks this assumption, often resulting in significant performance degradation. Consequently, inference in DQL is inherently tied to a large number of denoising step, leading to inefficiencies in runtime and memory usage.

In training, DQL also exhibits compounding inefficiencies. First, the critic loss (Eq.
7
) requires sampling target actions
\bm{a}^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}
, each of which must be generated via the full
K
-step denoising, introducing considerable computational overhead. With a smaller
K
, especially in the single-step regime, training can become unstable due to deviations from the intended policy caused by approximation error or fluctuation in actor updates. Furthermore, the ciric loss uses the target policy
\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}
, defined as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the actor parameters, for stability purposes. This target policy, however, introduces additional memory and computation burdens. Second, the actor loss (Eq.
8
) requires sampling actions from
\pi_{\theta}
and performing backpropagation through all
K
denoising steps (i.e., BPTT) using the reparameterization trick (Eq.
5
). Although this enables end-to-end training, the recursive gradient flow through a long stochastic computation graph becomes increasingly prone to numerical instability as
K
grows—an inherent requirement of the DDPM framework to approximate the Gaussian prior at terminal steps.

At first glance, one might assume that simply applying more efficient diffusion methods could resolve DQL’s limitations. However, this is far from straightforward. For example, efficient solver DDIM
(
Song, Meng, and Ermon 2020
)
reduces inference steps via a non-Markovian sampler, yet retains a sequential denoising process. In our experiments, applying DDIM for one-step action generation significantly degraded policy performance, underscoring its limitations in this regime.

Likewise, distillation-based approaches
(
Salimans and Ho 2022
;
Lu et al. 2025b
)
offer faster inference through student policies but introduce an additional training phase and leave core inefficiencies in training untouched.

More broadly, one-step training methods like Consistency Training
(
Song et al. 2023
)
have shown instability when used alone
(
Song and Dhariwal 2023
;
Lu and Song 2024
)
. These issues may worsen when integrated into reinforcement learning, where optimization is already fragile.

Casting to Flow Matching.

Diffusion models generate samples via stochastic and inherently curved trajectories, making direct one-step sampling challenging. Flow Matching (FM)
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
provides a principled alternative that maps noise to data through smoother and more directed paths. This facilitates both efficient sampling and improved stability
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
. As illustrated in Fig.
2
(c,d), the generation trajectories under FM differ substantially from those of diffusion models, highlighting the contrast between curved stochastic paths and direct deterministic flows. Motivated by these advantages, integrating FM into Q-learning frameworks such as DQL presents a promising direction for improving both training and inference efficiency.

Given data
x\sim q(x)
and noise
\epsilon\sim p_{\text{prior}}(\epsilon)
, FM defines a linear flow path:

z_{t}=\alpha_{t}x+\beta_{t}\epsilon,\quad v_{t}=\frac{dz_{t}}{dt}=\dot{\alpha}_{t}x+\dot{\beta}_{t}\epsilon,

(9)

where
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}
are predefined schedules (e.g.,
\alpha_{t}=1-t
,
\beta_{t}=t
), and the dot notation (e.g.,
\dot{\alpha}_{t}
) denotes the time derivative with respect to the continuous flow step
t\in[0,1]
. The
conditional velocity

v_{t}(z_{t}\mid x)
captures the direction of flow for a specific sample, and the
marginal velocity
field is defined as the expectation:

v(z_{t},t)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}(v_{t}\mid z_{t})}[v_{t}].

(10)

FM essentially models the marginal velocity, as it is feasible to approximate this field– parametrized by the neural network
v_{\theta}(z_{t},t)
–using the Conditional Flow Matching loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x,\epsilon}\left\|v_{\theta}(z_{t},t)-v_{t}(z_{t}\mid x)\right\|^{2},

(11)

where, under the commonly used schedule
\alpha_{t}=1-t
,
\beta_{t}=t
, the conditional velocity simplifies to
v_{t}(z_{t}\mid x)=\epsilon-x
.

In inference, sampling is performed by solving the ODE in reverse time:

\frac{dz_{t}}{dt}=v(z_{t},t),\quad\text{starting from }z_{1}=\epsilon\sim p_{\text{prior}}(\epsilon),

(12)

where the solution is approximated using a numerical solver, such as Euler’s method:
z_{t-\Delta t}=z_{t}-\Delta t\cdot v(z_{t},t).

Intuitively, since flows are designed to follow straight trajectories (i.e., ”rectified”)
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
, modeling the policy via FM should, in principle, enable one-step generation. However, in practice, the marginal velocity field typically induces a curved overall trajectory. Importantly, the curvature is not simply a consequence of imperfect neural approximation, but rather an inherent property of the ground-truth marginal velocity field. This phenomenon is visualized in Fig.
2
(a,b,c).

To enable one-step generation, we reinterpret the velocity field
v(z_{t},t)
in FM as the
instantaneous velocity
, which describes the local direction tangent to the trajectory at each time step. In contrast, if we can model the
average velocity
, which directly points from the noisy input to the target (i.e, the clean action), we can bypass iterative integration and enable one-step sampling. This is achievable by defining the average velocity over an interval
[r,t]
as:

u(z_{t},r,t)\triangleq\frac{1}{t-r}\int_{r}^{t}v(z_{\tau},\tau)\,d\tau,

(13)

which represents the total displacement over the interval
[r,t]
divided by its duration. The variables
r
and
t
denote the target and current time, respectively. This average velocity formulation is key to bypassing iterative integration, as it directly parameterizes the generative flow across the entire interval.

Importantly, this definition admits a mathematically equivalent form , known as the
MeanFlow Identity

(
Geng et al. 2025
)
:

u(z_{t},r,t)=v(z_{t},t)-(t-r)\frac{d}{dt}u(z_{t},r,t),

(14)

where the total derivative expands as
\frac{d}{dt}u(z_{t},r,t)=v(z_{t},t)\cdot\partial_{z}u+\partial_{t}u
. This identity enables a tractable training objective through a simple surrogate loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,r\leq t,x,\epsilon}\left\|u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t)-\text{sg}(u_{\text{tgt}})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(15)

where the target velocity is defined as:

u_{\text{tgt}}=v_{t}-(t-r)\left(v_{t}\cdot\partial_{z}u_{\theta}+\partial_{t}u_{\theta}\right),

(16)

with
v_{t}=\epsilon-x
is the condition velocity and
\text{sg}(\cdot)
denotes a stop-gradient operator to detach the target from gradient flow during optimization. The
u_{\text{tgt}}
can be compute efficiently with Jacobian-vector product
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
.

Mapping to the RL Framework.

To form OFQL, we alternate the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DBC}}
loss to train the average velocity field
u_{\theta}
conditioned on the state
s
. The resulting loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FBC}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,r\leq t,\,(a,s)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\epsilon}\left\|u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,s)-\text{sg}(u_{\text{tgt}})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(17)

where
z_{t}=(1-t)a+t\epsilon
and the target velocity
u_{\text{tgt}}
(Eq.
16
) is computed with
v_{t}=\epsilon-a

Given a trained
u_{\theta}
, sampling an action becomes a differentiable one-step operation:

a\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\quad\Rightarrow\quad a=\epsilon-u_{\theta}(\epsilon,r=0,t=1,s).

(18)

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The actor loss is updated with:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FBC}}(\theta)-\alpha\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim\mathcal{D},\bm{a}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[Q_{\phi}(\bm{s},\bm{a})\right],

(19)

The critic loss (Eq.
7
) is reused with the key difference being that actions are now generated in one-step and we also omit the target policy, as the one-step formulation yields stable training with a single policy network.

Related Work

Diffusion Models in Offline Reinforcement Learning.
Offline RL aims to learn effective policies from fixed datasets without environment interaction
(
Levine et al. 2020
)
, with early methods addressing distributional shift via conservative objectives—e.g., CQL
(
Kumar et al. 2020
)
, TD3+BC
(
Fujimoto and Gu 2021
)
, and IQL
(
Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine 2021
)
. However, these approaches often rely on unimodal Gaussian policies, which struggle to model complex, multi-modal action distributions. To address this, recent approaches have adopted diffusion model
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
;
Song et al. 2020
)
for offline RL. These models excel in representing complex distributions and have been applied in various forms: as planners for trajectory generation
(
Janner et al. 2022
;
Ajay et al. 2022
)
, as expressive policy networks
(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
;
Hansen-Estruch et al. 2023
)
, and as data synthesizers to augment training
(
Zhu et al. 2023
)
.

Among methods, Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL)
(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
stands out as a strong baseline, replacing Gaussian policies in TD3+BC with a diffusion model to better capture multi-modal actions. Follow-up evaluations, including those by Clean Diffuser
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
and recent empirical studies
(
Lu et al. 2025a
;
Lu et al. 2025b
)
, confirm DQL’s superiority over policy-based and planner-based methods. Despite its effectiveness, DQL is known to incur significant computational overhead during both training and inference
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
, and often exhibits training instability due to the challenges of learning accurate denoising policies
(
Park, Li, and Levine 2025
)
.

Subsequent works have attempted to mitigate these issues. For example,
Kang et al. 2023
uses an efficient solver that reduces the denoising steps. IDQL
(
Hansen-Estruch et al. 2023
)
sidesteps BPTT by training a diffusion policy to clone the behavior policy only, action then weighted by a separately learned IQL-based value function. However, both training value function and inference still require to query the multiple denoising steps diffusion policy. Similarly,
Park, Li, and Levine 2025
adopts a FM model to clone the behavior policy, but relies on distillation into a unimodal policy for actor and critic updates, avoiding BPTT. Although inference is performed in one step using the distilled unimodal policy, the distillation itself requires multi-step sampling from the flow model. While these methods partially address DQL’s limitations, they introduce additional components or multi-phase training schemes, increasing system complexity and limiting practical generality.

In contrast, our method departs from prior work by identifying the diffusion model itself as the root cause of inefficiency and instability in DQL. We introduce a one-step alternative, offering a fundamentally simpler and more stable solution without compromising policy expressivity.

Toward Efficient One-Step Diffusion.
Our work is inspired by advances in efficient generative modeling with diffusion and flow-based models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
;
Song and Ermon 2019
)
. A key limitation of diffusion model is their reliance on long sampling chains, which has motivated the development of faster alternatives. One line of work focuses on distillation techniques that compress many-step models into fewer steps
(
Salimans and Ho 2022
;
Sauer et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2024
)
, while another pursues flow matching approaches that learn time-dependent velocity fields for straight-through sampling
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
. Consistency Models
(
Song et al. 2023
)
offer another path to one-step generation, but suffer from training instabilities
(
Song and Dhariwal 2023
;
Lu and Song 2024
)
. More recent methods
(
Frans et al. 2024
;
Geng et al. 2025
;
Zhou, Ermon, and Song 2025
)
address these limitations by exploiting different physical parametrization using dual time variables, showing improved stability and performance.

Inspired by the consistent and stable average velocity field modeling of
Geng et al. 2025
, our method leverages this framework to realize a one-step flow-based policy. This design eliminates the need for iterative sampling, distillation, or multi-phase training, while preserving the expressiveness of non-Gaussian policies.

Experiment Setting

Benchmarks.
We evaluate OFQL on a diverse set of tasks from the D4RL benchmark suite
(
Fu et al. 2020
)
, a widely adopted standard for offline reinforcement learning. Our evaluation spans various domains, including locomotion, navigation and manipulation tasks to demonstrate the method’s generalizability. Detailed task descriptions and experimental protocols are provided in Appendix A.

Baselines.
To rigorously assess OFQL’s performance, we compare it against a broad spectrum of representative baselines, categorized as follows: (1) Non-Diffusion policies: Behavior Cloning (BC), TD3-BC
(
Fujimoto and Gu 2021
)
, and IQL
(
Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine 2021
)
; (2) Diffusion-based policies: IDQL
(
Hansen-Estruch et al. 2023
)
, DQL
(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
, and EDP
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
; and (3) Diffusion-based planners: Diffuser
(
Janner et al. 2022
)
, Decision Diffuser (DD)
(
Ajay et al. 2022
)
, and Diffusion Veteran (DV)
(
Lu et al. 2025a
)
.

Implementation Details.
Our approach builds directly on DQL
(
Wang, Hunt, and Zhou 2022
)
, inheriting its training and inference procedures to ensure a fair comparison. We adopt the original DQL architecture for both the Q-function and policy networks. The only minor modification lies in the policy input, which is augmented by concatenating an additional positional embedding corresponding to the target step
r
, in addition to the standard timestep embedding
t
. All hyperparameters and training protocols remain consistent with DQL. For reproducibility and reliability, each reported
normalized score

(
Fu et al. 2020
)
is averaged over 150 evaluation episodes per task. Further architectural and implementation details are included in the appendix B.

Experimental result

Benchmark results are summarized in Table
1
, with details discussed below.

Locomotion Domain (MuJoCo).
OFQL achieves strong performance in locomotion tasks, surpassing competitive diffusion-based baselines such as DQL and DV. In particular, OFQL improves the average performance of DQL from 88.0 to 92.5, with notable gains on medium and medium-replay tasks, which are known to contain suboptimal and noisy trajectories. These tasks likely give rise to complex, multi-modal action distributions, making them more challenging for standard policy learning. Robust action modeling and stable value learning are important when dealing with this kind of diverse or imperfect datasets. This result strongly suggest that OFQL’s improvements stem from two factors: (1) its policy modelling may offer great capacity for capturing complex action distributions, and (2) a Q-learning objective that avoids BPTT, potentially leading to more stable value estimation in offline settings.

Furthermore, we note that while EDP and IDQL prioritize training efficiency and stability, they exhibit a trade-off in final performance. In contrast, OFQL appears to offer better efficiency and effectiveness, showing consistent improvements across varying data conditions.

AntMaze Domain.
AntMaze tasks are notably difficult due to sparse rewards and suboptimal demonstrations, under which many prior approaches such as BC and Diffuser struggle. Interestingly, we observe that methods incorporating Q-learning signals, such as DV and DQL, consistently perform better in this domain, hinting at the importance of value-guided learning in sparse-reward environments.

OFQL, which builds on Q-learning framework, achieves a non-trivial improvement over DQL from 64.55 to 96.6, outperforming all diffusion-based baselines, including DV (83.2). This result aligns with the broader pattern we observe in locomotion and further strengthen the factors behind the improvement:
(1) Improved Q-learning appears crucial in this domain, and OFQL may benefit from its avoidance of BPTT.
(2) Expressive action modeling, enabled by denoising-based diffusion policies, likely complements the Q-learning objective, contributing to more effective learning from diverse and suboptimal trajectories.

While EDP and IDQL continue to underperform relative to DQL, OFQL consistently surpasses it, indicating robustness to both reward sparsity and data quality issues, without compromising scalability or efficiency

Kitchen Domain.
In Kitchen tasks, OFQL consistently improves upon DQL (61.6 → 67.0), achieving the best performance among diffusion-based policies. Interestingly, behavior cloning (BC) remains competitive, suggesting that accurate action modeling may outweigh the contribution of Q-learning in this domain. The Kitchen datasets exhibit low-entropy, narrowly distributed behaviors
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
, which may amplify the influence of the behavioral component during training.

While OFQL performs strongly, it slightly lags behind DV, which leverages planning-time exploration with long planning horizon and best-of-
N
action selection—though at the expense of significantly higher computational cost due to its Transformer-based architecture
(
Lu et al. 2025a
)
. In contrast, OFQL offers a more favorable efficiency-performance trade-off, making it a promising candidate for practical and scalable offline RL applications.

Comparison of Strategies Toward One-Step Prediction.
To explore how to effectively adapt DQL for one-step prediction, we evaluate several improvement strategies: (1) DQL: The base model, trained and evaluated with 5 denoising steps. (2) DQL+DDIM: Applies a one-step DDIM sampler to a pretrained DQL model at inference time only. (3) FQL: DQL but using a Flow Matching policy, trained with 5-step sampling but evaluated with one-step. (4) OFQL (Ours): Trained and evaluated fully in the one-step regime.

presents the average performance across 9 MuJoCo tasks, each evaluated with 150 random seeds.

We observe that DQL+DDIM suffers from severe degradation (

FQL provides a more promising outcome, showing improved compatibility with one-step inference, though it still underperforms relative to DQL (

OFQL sets a new performance standard, outperforming DQL by +4.5 points while enabling consistent one-step sampling during both training and inference. Although the diffusion policies used in DQL theoretically provide expressive action distributions under a multi-step regime, the observed improvement suggests that critic training stability potential plays a more critical role. OFQL enables this improvement by eliminating backpropagation through extended denoising steps. This further suggests that bypassing or optimizing BPTT is key to efficient, high-performing RL framework.

Training and Inference Efficiency Comparison.

We compare OFQL and DQL in terms of training time and decision frequency
(
Lu et al. 2025b
)
. Both methods need two policy sampling per training step, but OFQL avoids the use of target policy and EMA updates, reducing memory and computation. While DQL requires K denoising steps per action, OFQL performs generation in a single step.

Figure
4
shows the wall-clock training time (1 million steps) and decision frequency measured on an A100 GPU (Appendix D provides detail experiment protocol). DQL’s training time scales nearly linearly with step count—from 11.7 hours (5 steps) to 49.5 hours (50 steps)—whereas OFQL completes training in just 6.3 hours. At inference, OFQL achieves 496.1 Hz versus 238.7 Hz for 5-step DQL and drops to 35.5 Hz at 50 steps.

Overall, OFQL offers significantly faster training and higher decision frequency without sacrificing model expressivity.

Conclusion

We presented One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), a novel policy learning framework that overcomes key limitations of Diffusion Q-Learning by enabling efficient, single-step action generation during both training and inference. By reformulating DQL within the Flow Matching framework and learning an average velocity field rather than a marginal one, OFQL eliminates the need for multi-step denoising, recursive gradient propagation. This leads to faster training and inference, while surpassing the performance of state-of-the-art diffusion-based offline RL methods. Our empirical results on the D4RL benchmark validate the effectiveness and efficiency of OFQL, highlighting the potential of flow-based generative policies as a scalable alternative to diffusion-based approaches in offline reinforcement learning.
