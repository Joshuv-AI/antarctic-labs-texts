Guiding a diffusion model with itself using sliding windows
Nikolas Adaloglou, Tim Kaiser, Damir Iagudin, Markus Kollmann

Abstract
Guidance is a widely used technique for diffusion models to enhance sample quality. Technically, guidance is realised by using an auxiliary model that generalises more broadly than the primary model. Using a 2D toy example, we first show that it is highly beneficial when the auxiliary model exhibits similar but stronger generalisation errors than the primary model. Based on this insight, we introduce masked sliding window guidance (M-SWG) , a novel, training-free method. M-SWG upweights long-range spatial dependencies by guiding the primary model with itself by selectively restricting its receptive field. M-SWG requires neither access to model weights from previous iterations, additional training, nor class conditioning. M-SWG achieves a superior Inception score (IS) compared to previous state-of-the-art training-free approaches, without introducing sample oversaturation. In conjunction with existing guidance methods, M-SWG reaches state-of-the-art Frechet DINOv2 distance on ImageNet using EDM2-XXL and DiT-XL. The code is available at https://github.com/HHU-MMBS/swg_bmvc2025_official .

Introduction

Diffusion models (DMs) have emerged as a powerful approach for generative tasks, achieving remarkable success in areas such as image synthesis and text-to-image generation
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Karras et al., 2022
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Kingma et al., 2023
;
Saharia et al., 2022
;
Adaloglou et al., 2025
)
. Despite their success, DMs often fail to generate high-quality samples
Ahsan et al. (2024)
and require guidance techniques to improve visual fidelity (
section

1
). The currently most popular method, classifier-free guidance (CFG), pushes the sample towards regions with higher posterior class probability
Tang et al. (2025)
. Unlike its predecessor, classifier guidance
Dhariwal and Nichol (2021)
, which relies on training an external classifier on labeled noisy images, CFG combines conditional and unconditional denoisers, which can be trained jointly
Ho and Salimans (2022)
.

In the following, we denote by
\bm{x}
a noisy image and by
\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t;c)
and
\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
the class conditional and unconditional noise predictors at timestep
t
of the denoising process
Dhariwal and Nichol (2021)
. CFG combines the two noise predictions during sampling using the linear extrapolation scheme

\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{x},t;c)=\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t;c)+w[\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t;c)-\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)]\ ,\quad\text{with guidance weight }w>0.

(1)

Therefore, CFG can be viewed as a linear extrapolation method
Song et al. (2021)
;
Bradley and Nakkiran (2024)
. Equivalent extrapolation schemes can be found for all diffusion model formulations, such as target prediction or flow matching
Karras et al. (2022)
;
Salimans and Ho (2022)
.

Limitations of CFG.
Despite the widespread use of CFG in conditional synthesis
Peebles and Xie (2023)
, it comes with notable limitations. First, it increases the training budget. When trained jointly, the unconditional task can consume up to 20% of the computational cost
Ho and Salimans (2022)
. Additionally, while CFG reduces class mismatch between samples and conditions of the noise predictor
Saharia et al. (2022)
, this benefit comes at the expense of sample diversity, as sampling focuses on regions with high class probability
Karras et al. (2024a)
. Since guidance is a linear extrapolation scheme, the sampling trajectory can overshoot the desired distribution, leading to oversaturated or oversimplified images
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024)
. Finally, CFG sampling is restricted to class-conditional generation by design.

Weak model guidance.
Recently, a new class of guidance methods has been developed that addresses some of the limitations of CFG. These methods utilize the linear extrapolation scheme of CFG, given by

\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)+w[\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)],

(2)

in a more generic way. Here, the subscripts
pos
for positive and
neg
for negative refer to the sign of the noise predictors. The idea behind
Equation

2
is to extrapolate into high likelihood regions by designing a negative model
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
that has an inferior performance compared to a well-performing DM
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
. Typically,
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
is derived from
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
by re-training using fewer parameters, architecture-based heuristic manipulations, or shorter training times
Ahn et al. (2024)
;
Karras et al. (2024a)
. We refer to this class of guidance methods as
weak model guidance (WMG)
. While WMG methods seem promising, they often require training additional models, or careful manual selection of specific layers to impair
Ahn et al. (2024)
.

In this paper, we first introduce a toy example to show that (i) WMG samples closer to high likelihood regions than CFG and (ii) guidance works best if
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
makes similar errors as
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
but stronger. A similar effect can be achieved under increased weight regularization in high-resolution image synthesis. Additionally, we introduce masked sliding window guidance (M-SWG), a novel guidance method designed to upweight long-range dependencies (
section

1
). In contrast to existing guidance techniques
Saharia et al. (2022)
;
Ho and Salimans (2022)
;
Ahn et al. (2024)
;
Karras et al. (2024a)
;
Sadat et al. (2024a)
;
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
, M-SWG requires neither training nor class conditioning and can be applied to any DM that can process multiple image resolutions. M-SWG achieves a superior IS score without causing oversaturation, while maintaining competitive Frechet distances. Finally, by combining M-SWG with existing guidance techniques
Ho and Salimans (2022)
;
Karras et al. (2024a)
, a state-of-the-art Frechet DINOv2 distance (FDD) on ImageNet is achieved using EDM2-XXL and DiT-XL
Peebles and Xie (2023)
;
Karras et al. (2024b)
.

Related work

Diffusion sampling can be coarsely divided into CFG-based variations and alternative methods. Vanilla CFG can be improved by adding a noise schedule to the condition
Sadat et al. (2024a)
, introducing monotonically increasing guidance weight schedules
Wang et al. (2024)
, stepwise intensity thresholding
Saharia et al. (2022)
, or applying CFG only at an interval in the intermediate denoising steps
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024)
. Despite recent advancements, CFG cannot be used with unlabelled datasets or conditional-only trained denoisers by design. While several sampling methods focus on modifying CFG
Sadat et al. (2024a)
;
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024)
;
Saharia et al. (2022)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
;
Sadat et al. ()
, training- and condition-free approaches remain an open quest
Sadat et al. (2024b)
.

Alternative guidance methods can be roughly grouped into three categories: i)
architecture-based impairments
, ii)
image-level manipulations

Hong et al. (2023)
, and iii) inferior capacity models, which we refer to as
weak models
. Architectural impairments typically leverage self-attention maps
Ahn et al. (2024)
;
Hong (2024)
that are known to capture structure-related information
Balaji et al. (2022)
;
Hertz et al. (2022)
;
Nam et al. (2024)
;
Ahn et al. (2024)
. For instance, a handful of manually picked attention maps can be replaced with an identity matrix
Ahn et al. (2024)
or filtered using Gaussian smoothing
Hong (2024)
. Nonetheless, the choice of attention maps depends on the model architecture, which limits the applicability of the method. Finally, architecture-specific impairments can create significant side effects, such as deteriorating the visual structure
Hong (2024)
. Image-level manipulations such as frequency filtering have been attempted with limited success
Hong et al. (2023)
. Hong et al.
Hong et al. (2023)
restrict high-frequency filtering to image patches corresponding to high activation areas, achieved by upsampling self-attention maps. However, similar to architecture-based impairments, it requires cherry-picking while being prone to artifacts
Hong (2024)
.

Weak models of inferior capacity compared to the positive model can be constructed by limiting the network size (i.e. number of parameters) or its training time
Karras et al. (2024a)
. However, such approaches require additional training from scratch or various weight instances, which are not always available. Concurrent work
Alemohammad et al. (2024)
deteriorates the positive model by fine-tuning with its own generated samples to derive the negative model. Different from prior works
Karras et al. (2024a)
;
Alemohammad et al. (2024)
, we aim to systematically study WMG methods, starting with a toy example. Our analysis reveals that the negative model needs to exhibit
a similar modeling error as the positive model, but stronger.
The latter statement has not been explicitly formulated in prior works
Ahn et al. (2024)
;
Hong et al. (2023)
;
Karras et al. (2024a)
.

In the following, we introduce a two-dimensional toy example
(
Fig.

5
) to visualize the conceptual differences between CFG and WMG. The data distribution of our toy example consists of three data points
{\cal D}=\{\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2},\bm{y}_{3}\}
, where each data point is assigned a different class label
c\in\{1,2,3\}
.
We can generate trajectories
\bm{x}(t)
by numerically solving the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x}(t),\sigma(t))d\sigma(t)
, with
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
the optimal noise predictor in the Bayesian sense, and
\sigma(t)\in[0,\sigma_{max}]
the current noise level.

Note that
d\sigma(t)<0
along a denoising trajectory. It can be shown that for a finite set of
N
data points, the optimal noise predictor takes the explicit form
Karras et al. (2022)

\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma(t))=\frac{1}{\sigma(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{i})p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x},t),\quad p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x},t)=\frac{{\cal N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y}_{i},\sigma(t)^{2})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\cal N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y}_{i},\sigma(t)^{2})},

(3)

where
{\cal N}(.|.)
denotes an isotropic normal distribution. That is, the optimal noise predictor is the normalised residual between
\bm{x}
and the posterior-mean
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{y}_{i}p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x},t)
as an estimator for a data point. The optimal noise predictor generates trajectories with endpoints arbitrarily close to one of the data points if the ODE is initialized with
\bm{x}_{init}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{x}|\bm{0},\sigma_{max}^{2})
and
\sigma_{max}
is sufficiently large.

Assume we are given an error-prone noise predictor,
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}
, whose endpoints are distributed as a superposition of three normal distributions, all with variance
\delta^{2}
, and each normal distribution centered around a different data point (
Fig.

5
a). For this special case it can be shown that
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},\sigma(t))=\sigma(t)/\tilde{\sigma}(t)\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x},\tilde{\sigma}(t))
, with
\tilde{\sigma}(t)^{2}=\sigma(t)^{2}+\delta^{2}
. The question is now, to what extent CFG and WMG can help to shift the trajectory endpoints of the error-prone predictor closer to the data distribution? To answer this question, we introduce the error-prone predictors
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
,
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
, and adjust the guidance weight
w
of
Equation

2
such that the average endpoint error is minimized.
Using
\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t):=\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{x}(t),\sigma(t))
for brevity, we recover the CFG setting by introducing the class-conditional noise predictor
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},t;c)
and the unconditional noise predictor
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},t)
. Likewise we recover the WMG setting by
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},t)
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{err}^{\prime}(\bm{x},t)
, where
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}^{\prime}
differs from
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}
by a larger endpoint variance
\delta^{\prime}>\delta
. The denoising trajectories shown in
Fig.

5
follow from numerically solving the ODE for
\bm{x}(t)
, using
Equation

2
instead of the optimal noise predictor.

CFG.
As we use just one datapoint per class, the class conditional noise predictor is given by
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)=\sigma(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{i_{c}})
, with
i_{c}
the data point index belonging to class
c
, resulting in the special case of straight lines as denoising trajectories (
Fig.

5
a, CFG). For small guidance weights, the trajectory endpoints match slightly better to the data distribution (
Fig.

5
b, CFG), but for larger guidance weights, they move away from the data distribution (
Fig.

5
c, CFG). This behavior is a consequence of the fact that
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
drives the trajectory towards the data point with the corresponding class label, whereas
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
denoises in the direction of a weighted superposition of all data points. As a result, CFG tends to shift the trajectories away from the center of mass of the data distribution (
Fig.

5
d, CFG), favoring classifiable endpoints over a fit to the data distribution. This behavior has been described in previous works
Ho and Salimans (2022)
and scales to higher dimensions, e.g. images, where a better distribution overlap between generated samples and training samples in feature space is only observed for sufficiently small guidance weights.

WMG.
In contrast to CFG, increasing the guidance weight for WMG drives trajectory endpoints closer to the data distribution (
Fig.

5
a-c, WMG), up to the point where trajectories become unstable. The crucial condition for WMG to work well is that, in each step, the prediction of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
is closer to
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
than the prediction of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
, causing the extrapolation to correct the prediction of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
towards the optimal prediction of
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
(
Fig.

5
d, WMG).

w^{*}(\bm{x},t)=\frac{\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x},t)\|}{\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)\|}

(4)

. We use
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
to independently process

overlapping image crops.

is generated by superimposing the processed crops and averaging the overlapping pixels.

For the special case that
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
exhibit the same error up to a multiplicative factor, it is possible for WMG to closely recover the prediction of the optimal denoiser
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
, under the optimal guidance weight
w^{*}(\bm{x},t)
(
Equation

4
and supplementary material). The optimal weight is obviously of little practical use as it requires knowledge about the optimal noise predictor
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
. However, it illustrates that the practical choice of a constant guidance weight is, in general, an
ad hoc
simplification. Surprisingly, we observe excellent, albeit imperfect, matching between endpoints and datapoints for constant but sufficiently large weights (
Fig.

5
).

Sliding window guidance (SWG)

Assuming that a significant source of the predictor’s error can be attributed to long-range dependencies, we introduce a new guidance method that aims to correct such errors. The idea is to generate
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
by restricting the spatial input size (
H\times W
) of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
to a smaller size
k\times l
, with
k<H
and
l<W
, which induces a defined cut-off for long-range dependencies. We use sliding windows to generate
N
crops of size
k\times l
, using a fixed stride
s
per dimension. These crops are independently processed (without rescaling) by
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
as illustrated in
section

3
. The
N
predictions of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
are superimposed in the same order and at the same positions, which results in an
H\times W
output for
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
. Overlapping pixels are averaged. A pseudo-code for SWG is provided in the supplementary material. We emphasize that using sliding windows is a common technique in high-resolution 2D/3D image processing
Cardoso et al. (2022)
;
Jiang et al. (2023)
;
Shao et al. (2024)
;
Ruhe et al. (2024)
;
Bar-Tal et al. (2023)
.

Hyperparameters and limitations.
One limitation of SWG is that
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
must process inputs of varying image resolution. Nonetheless, current architectures, such as Unets
Ho et al. (2020)
or (diffusion) image transformers
Peebles and Xie (2023)
, generally satisfy this requirement. SWG can be implemented in a few lines of code and requires no training-related modifications or class conditioning. Unless otherwise specified, we assume
H=W
and use crop size
k=l\approx 5/8\times H
and
N=4
, which translates into
k=40,s=24
at
64\times 64
resolution. We denote the overlap ratio per dimension as
r=1-\frac{s}{k}=0.4
.

SWG in latent DMs.
We keep the same design choices for latent DMs
Rombach et al. (2022)
that operate in
\frac{H}{8}\times\frac{H}{8}
feature dimensions, where
8
is the upscaling/downscaling factor. More specifically, SWG is performed in each denoising step in the low-dimensional latent space (i.e.
64\times 64
), and the final output is passed to the decoder. The decoder handles the upscaling to the image dimensions, i.e., from
64\times 64
to
512\times 512
. The rationale behind SWG operating in lower feature dimensions is that latent representations, i.e., of a VAE, preserve to a significant extent the spatial structure of the image
Kouzelis et al. (2025)
;
Dieleman (2025)
. To make SWG compatible with DiT, we add positional encoding interpolation to process the crops. The choice of
k\approx\frac{5}{8}H
is determined by practical considerations of Unets. Specifically,
k
must satisfy
\frac{k}{2^{n}}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
n
is the number of downsampling steps of the Unet, and the diffusion model
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
must be able to process multiple image resolutions.

Computational overhead.
The computational overhead of SWG compared to CFG is determined by the overlap ratio
r
and the architecture. For EDM-S and
r=0.4
, we measured an overhead of less than 30% compared to CFG based on a naive implementation of SWG. More details are provided in the supplementary material.

Masked SWG (M-SWG).
We experimentally observe that applying guidance only to overlapping regions of the sliding windows shows better performance than applying guidance to the whole image. We therefore introduce a binary mask
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}
to filter for overlapping regions when applying guidance,
\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)+w\mathbf{M}\odot[\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)]
.

Combining SWG with existing methods.
Since SWG and M-SWG are conceptually different from existing techniques, we investigate whether they can enhance existing techniques as a linear combination of their stepwise outcomes
Liu et al. (2022)
. Mathematically,

\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)+\sum_{i}w_{i}[\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\epsilon}^{(i)}_{\text{neg}}(\bm{x},t)],

(5)

where
i
indicates different guidance methods. Quantitative improvements would suggest a complementary relationship between methods. For instance, combining CFG with SWG would increase noise-condition alignment whilst conforming to long-range dependencies.

Experimental evaluation and discussion

Diffusion guidance baselines.

We divide the evaluation into training-free methods (
Table

3
) and state-of-the-art comparisons (
Table

3
), where M-SWG is used in conjunction with existing methods (
Equation

5
). Regarding training-free methods, we consider reduced training (RT) as a state-of-the-art baseline
Karras et al. (2024a)
, along with sampling without guidance (
w=0
). Nonetheless, publicly available DMs do
not
always offer earlier weight instances such as DiT-XL
Peebles and Xie (2023)
and StableDiffusion
Rombach et al. (2022)
, and thus RT cannot be applied. When combining M-SWG with other non-training-free state-of-the-art methods (
Equation

5
), we adopt CFG and the recent approach of reducing training time and learning capacity (
RCT
), also known as
“autoguidance”

Karras et al. (2024a)
. We emphasize that CFG requires jointly or independently training a conditional and an unconditional denoiser, and it is thus not training-free.

Implementation details.
The experiments were conducted on 4 Nvidia A100 GPUs with 40GB of VRAM. Following recent works
Stein et al. (2023)
;
Karras et al. (2024b)
;
Jayasumana et al. (2024)
;
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024)
;
Karras et al. (2024a)
;
Chong and Forsyth (2020)
, we produce 50K samples for the evaluations and measure the Frechet distance
Heusel et al. (2018)
using features from InceptionV3 and DINOv2 ViT-L
Oquab et al. (2023)
, denoted by FID and FDD, respectively. We use FDD as our primary metric since it has shown better alignment with human judgment
Parmar et al. (2022)
;
Morozov et al. (2021)
;
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2023)
;
Stein et al. (2023)
. We also report the inception score (IS) as a measure of visual quality. We apply SWG and M-SWG to state-of-the-art models, such as DiT-XL
Peebles and Xie (2023)
, and EDMv2

.

M-SWG versus training-free methods.
In
Table

3
, we observe significant gains compared to the unguided generation using SWG and M-SWG. M-SWG provides a competitive alternative when previous weight instances, such as with DiT-XL, are unavailable. We believe that the inferior Frechet distances from SWG/M-SWG are attributed to background simplification, which strongly affects the statistics of the image distribution. This is a shared limitation with CFG
Ho and Salimans (2022)
;
Chung et al. (2024)
and can be further investigated in future work. Crucially, by combining RT with M-SWG, we achieve a new state-of-the-art FDD for training-free image synthesis using EDM2-XXL (32.9), which also outperforms CFG (36.7). The gains in FDD do not translate to FID. We hypothesize that the less-trained negative model (RT) makes more errors related to local image details, complementing SWG, and it is best captured in the DINOv2 feature space. Finally, no training-free approach can significantly outperform the unguided EDM2-XXL FID score. This is linked to existing concerns that FID does not reflect gradual improvements
Parmar et al. (2022)
;
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2023)
;
Stein et al. (2023)
;
Jayasumana et al. (2024)
;
Adaloglou et al. (2025)
, as well as that the EDM2-XXL makes fewer errors that can be corrected with guidance techniques.

Similar to EDM2, applying M-SWG to DiT is highly beneficial in terms of FID/FDD. To the best of our knowledge, early training weight instances are not publicly available for DiT to benchmark against RT or other WMG variants. The poor performance of the unguided DiT-XL is mainly attributed to the joint training on both conditional and unconditional generation, which may reduce network capabilities and lead to skewed sampling distributions
Karras et al. (2024a)

Same error but stronger in high dimensions.
Motivated by the toy model, we verify its implication in higher dimensions by constructing
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
with the same network architecture as
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
, but with significantly increased weight regularization. This can be implemented by either fine-tuning

Karras et al. (2024a)

\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}

6
Conclusion

In this work, we identified the general concept of “same error but stronger” for designing diffusion guidance methods. We validated this concept using a toy example and experimentally in high dimensions using a negative model with increased weight regularization. Additionally, we presented a novel training-free and condition-free guidance method (M-SWG) that enhances global coherence and consistency of objects. M-SWG can be used as a standalone method or combined with state-of-the-art sampling methods, such as CFG, to yield visually appealing and state-of-the-art results in image synthesis.

Appendix B
Toy example details

This section presents the derivation of the toy example from the main text. The code for computing the trajectories will be released and allows for the visualization of guidance effects for different combinations of
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{pos}}
,
\bm{\epsilon}_{\text{neg}}
, and datasets. We introduce with time
t\in[0,1]
a variable that denotes the progress of denoising, which starts from a normally distributed sample,
\bm{x}(1)
, and ends at a fully denoised sample,
\bm{x}(0)
. In the following, we denote by
\bm{y}
a sample from the data distribution
P(\bm{y})
, by
\sigma:=\sigma(t)
the time-dependent noise level, and by
\bm{x}:=\bm{x}(t)
a point along a denoising trajectory. We set
\sigma(0)=0
and choose with
\sigma(1)=80
a value such that
\sigma(t)\gg std(y_{i})
, for all dimensions
i\in\{1,2,..,d\}
. We assume
\sigma(t)
to be a strictly monotone increasing function in time
t
. To derive the optimal denoiser, we make use of the ordinary differential equation (ODE) that describes the field lines of probability flow (e.g. Eq. (1) of Ref.
Karras et al. (2022)
). This ODE allows for the generation of sampling trajectories

d\bm{x}=-\dot{\sigma}\sigma\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})dt,

(7)

when initialized with a noise sample
\bm{x}(1)\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{x}|0,\sigma(1)^{2})
. Here and in following we use the definition
\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}):=\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
to denote an isotropic normal distribution. Note that
dt<0
for inference, by definition.
Futhermore,
\dot{\sigma}\sigma
can be interpreted as a diffusion coefficient,
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})
as a score function, and
P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P(\bm{y})d\bm{y}
as the distribution of noisy samples, which results from adding Gaussian noise to data samples. To derive the optimal denoiser, we rewrite the score function as

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}{P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P(\bm{y})d\bm{y}}{P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{y}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P(\bm{y})d\bm{y}}{P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})P(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{x}P(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})d\bm{y}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}P(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})d\bm{y}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma)}{\sigma^{2}},

(8)

where the optimal denoiser is given by the posterior mean

\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}P(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})d\bm{y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}\frac{\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P(\bm{y})}{P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}d\bm{y}.

(9)

To construct an error-prone denoiser, we substitute the true data distribution with a “broader” data distribution
P(\bm{y})\rightarrow P_{\delta}(\bm{y})
, with
P_{\delta}(\bm{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}
, where
\delta
is the standard deviation of noise that randomly shifts the true data points. The corresponding posterior mean is given by

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\delta}(\bm{x},\sigma):=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}\frac{\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P_{\delta}(\bm{y})}{P_{\delta}(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})}d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}d\bm{y}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}d\bm{y}}\quad,

(10)

with
P_{\delta}(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P_{\delta}(\bm{y})d\bm{y}
.
To find an explicit expression for
\bm{y}_{\delta}
, we utilize known integrals
Owen (1980)
.
For the denominator, we use the expression

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\bm{x})\phi({\bm{a}}+b\bm{x})d\bm{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\phi\bigg(\frac{{\bm{a}}}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\bigg).

(11)

with
\phi(\bm{x}):={\cal N}(\bm{x}|0,1)
. However, we have to reformulate this expression for the product of two Gaussians with different means and variances. To this end, consider the substitution
\bm{x}=\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d},c,d\in\mathbb{R}
and adjust the LHS.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\bm{x})\phi({\bm{a}}+b\bm{x})d\bm{x}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\bigg(\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d}\bigg)\phi\bigg({\bm{a}}+b\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d}\bigg)\frac{1}{d}d\bm{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\bigg(\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d}\bigg)\phi\bigg(\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\sigma}\bigg)\frac{1}{d}d\bm{z}\qquad,\ {\bm{\mu}}={\bm{c}}-{\bm{a}}\frac{d}{b},\sigma=\frac{d}{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}.

Combining with the RHS of
Equation

11
yields the desired integration formula

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\phi\bigg(\frac{{\bm{a}}}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}}\phi\bigg(\frac{{\bm{c}}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}}\bigg)=\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2}).

(12)

For the numerator, we use the expression
Owen (1980)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{x}\phi(\bm{x})\phi({\bm{a}}+b\bm{x})d\bm{x}=-\frac{{\bm{a}}b}{(1+b^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\phi\bigg(\frac{{\bm{a}}}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\bigg).

(13)

Using the same substitutions as before yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{x}\phi(\bm{x})\phi({\bm{a}}+b\bm{x})d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d}\phi\bigg(\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{c}}}{d}\bigg)\phi\bigg(\frac{\bm{z}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\sigma}\bigg)\frac{1}{d}d\bm{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{z}\sigma\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}-\frac{c}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:denominator})}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{z}\sigma\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}-\frac{c}{d}\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2}).

Combining with the RHS of
Equation

13
yields the second integration formula

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{z}\sigma\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{c}},d^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2})d\bm{z}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\sigma}\Bigg(-\frac{{\bm{c}}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\sigma}\frac{d}{\sigma}\bigg(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}}\bigg)^{3}\phi\bigg(\frac{{\bm{c}}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}}\bigg)+\frac{\sigma{\bm{c}}}{d}\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\sigma}\Bigg(-\frac{({\bm{c}}-{\bm{\mu}})d}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\sigma^{3}}{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2})+\frac{\sigma{\bm{c}}}{d}\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2})\bigg({\bm{c}}-\frac{({\bm{c}}-{\bm{\mu}})d^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}({\bm{c}}|{\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}+d^{2})\frac{{\bm{c}}\sigma^{2}+{\bm{\mu}}d^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+d^{2}}.

(15)

For the numerator, of Eq. (
10
) we now get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}d\bm{y}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})d\bm{y}P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:numerator})}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2})\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}}P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{\prime}\sigma^{2})P(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\prime}|\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2})d\bm{y}^{\prime}.

(17)

For the denominator of Eq. (
10
), we get

\displaystyle P_{\delta}(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})P_{\delta}(\bm{y})d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y},\sigma^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{x},\sigma^{2})\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\delta^{2})d\bm{y}P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:denominator})}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}|\bm{y}^{\prime},\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2})P(\bm{y}^{\prime})d\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}).

(18)

Finally,

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\delta}(\bm{x},\sigma)\overset{(\ref{eq:sol-numerator})+(\ref{eq:sol-denominator})}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{\prime}\sigma^{2})P(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\prime}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})d\bm{y}^{\prime}}{P(\bm{x}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})}\frac{1}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\prime}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})d\bm{y}^{\prime}+\sigma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}^{\prime}P(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\prime}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})d\bm{y}^{\prime}}{P(\bm{x}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})}\frac{1}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\sigma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{y}^{\prime}\frac{P(\bm{x},\bm{y}^{\prime}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})}{P(\bm{x}|{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2})}d\bm{y}^{\prime}}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},{\tilde{\sigma}})\sigma^{2}}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}.

(19)

with
{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}
. Using the optimal denoiser
\bm{y}^{*}
, we define the optimal noise predictor

\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma):=-\sigma\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log P(\bm{x}|\sigma^{2})\overset{(\ref{eq:score-transform})}{=}\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma)}{\sigma},

(20)

and similarly, we define the error-prone noise predictor as

\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},\sigma):=\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{\delta}(\bm{x},\sigma)}{\sigma}.

(21)

Inserting the explict expression for
\bm{y}_{\delta}
in
Equation

21
yields the expression for
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}
used in the main text

\bm{\epsilon}_{err}(\bm{x},\sigma)=\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{y}_{\delta}(\bm{x},\sigma)}{\sigma}=\frac{\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},{\tilde{\sigma}})\sigma^{2}}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}}{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},{\tilde{\sigma}}))}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}}=\frac{\sigma}{{\tilde{\sigma}}}\bm{\epsilon}^{*}(\bm{x},{\tilde{\sigma}}).

We can now generate trajectories
\bm{x}(t)
from
Equation

7
by numerically solving the ODE with
\bm{y}^{*}
or
\bm{y}_{\delta}
, corresponding to
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
or
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}
. In practice, we consider a finite set of data-points
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, in which case
Equation

9
and
19
simplify to

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}\bm{y}_{i}N(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{y}_{i},\sigma^{2})}{\sum_{i}N(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{y}_{i},\sigma^{2})}

(22)

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\delta}(\bm{x},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{x}\delta^{2}+\bm{y}^{*}(\bm{x},{\tilde{\sigma}})\sigma^{2}}{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{2}},

(23)

which can be directly used to simulate trajectories. To do so, we use the Euler method shown in
Algorithm

1
. We found that higher-order integration methods (e.g., Alg. 1 of Ref.
Karras et al. (2022)
) can be used but are not necessary to achieve sufficient accuracy. This framework can be used for simulations of different configurations of
\bm{\epsilon}^{*}
,
\bm{\epsilon}_{err}
, conditional and unconditional models, as well as arbitrary finite datasets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Figures

16
and
17
show a detailed analysis of WMG on two datasets, the triangle formation used in the main text, and a cloud of normally distributed data samples.
