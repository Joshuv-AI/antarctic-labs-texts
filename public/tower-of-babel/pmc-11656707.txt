Direct Monomer Recovery from Ring-Closing Depolymerization of Thermosets
Authors: Chihui Zheng, Gadi Slor, Youwei Ma, Francesco Stellacci
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recovering monomers from the depolymerization of thermosets presents a significant challenge, which becomes even more daunting if one sets the goal of doing it directly, i.e., without complex chemical separation steps. To this end, we have synthesized a new type of polycarbonate thermoset by first copolymerizing alkyl cyclic carbonates (ACCs) with small amounts of allyloxy cyclic carbonates (AoCCs), followed by cross-linking the resulting allyloxy polycarbonate with excess tetrathiol compounds under UV irradiation. These cross-linked polycarbonates demonstrate enhanced thermal and mechanical properties compared to their linear analogues, while maintaining the linear polymers’ capacity for ring-closing depolymerization. The depolymerization process enables the direct recovery of ACC and its dimer, bypassing complex chemical separation steps that are commonly employed in the recycling of conventional chemically recyclable thermosets. The yields range from 74.7% to 91.7% depending on the ratios of AoCC to ACC in the thermosets. Furthermore, the recovered compounds can be repolymerized with AoCCs leading to polycarbonate of the same quality to the initially synthesized one.

Thermosets, which comprise nearly one-third of the synthetic polymer market, demonstrate superior thermal stability and mechanical properties in comparison to thermoplastics, primarily due to their cross-linked molecular architecture.1−4 However, this feature poses significant challenges for recycling postconsumer thermosets, with over 90% being discarded in landfills or incinerated, leading to substantial economic losses and environmental pollution.5,6 Therefore, it is of crucial importance to develop efficient recycling methods for thermosets.

Recent advancements in dynamic covalent chemistries offer a promising approach to addressing this challenge by incorporating labile chemical motifs—such as cleavable groups7−10 or dynamic covalent bonds11−18—either in the backbone or as cross-links of thermosets. Unlike conventional thermosets, the polymers featuring these labile bonds can undergo controlled degradation and/or depolymerization when exposed to specific agents, facilitating the subsequent recovery or reuse of their constituent components.3,19−21

While these approaches have been proven effective in recycling thermosets, they usually require chemical separation processes following degradation or depolymerization to isolate and then recover the individual starting materials. The separation primarily hinges on differences in polarity,11,22 solubility,23 molecular weight,7 or acid–base properties11,20,24,25 of the constituent components, requiring specific techniques such as organic solvent extraction, dialysis, precipitation, neutralization or column chromatography to process the depolymerized products. These steps can be time-consuming, inefficient, and heavily dependent on large quantities of organic solvents, thus complicating the process and increasing the costs of thermosets recycling. For example, in our previous case of recycling thermosetting PVA, after the decross-linking of the thermoset, dialysis or precipitation is required to recover the starting PVA materials, involving significant use of water or ethanol, respectively.7 Furthermore, these chemical separation techniques may not be applicable to thermosets composed of chemically similar building blocks. Therefore, recovering monomers directly after depolymerization, bypassing extensive chemical separation, becomes essential to streamline the recycling of thermosets.

Recent progress in chemical recycling of thermoplastics to monomers through ring-closing depolymerization have garnered considerable attention, which consists of depolymerization of the polymers into their constituent monomers upon thermal treatment above their ceiling temperatures.26−28 Examples of ring-closing depolymerizable (RCD) thermoplastics include those polyesters,29−31 polyamides,32 polythioesters,33,34 polycarbonates,35−39 and polyolefins,40 which are mainly synthesized by ring-opening polymerization of five-,41,42 six-,43,44 seven-,45,46 or even eight-membered cyclic monomers.47,48 Of particular interest are these cyclic monomers, typically featuring a low-boiling point, thereby allowing their in situ and continuous recovery through vacuum distillation or sublimation during the heat-induced depolymerization of thermoplastics.49 Drawing inspiration from these,50,51 we anticipate that thermosets, formed by cross-linking RCD thermoplastics, could allow direct monomer recovery following their depolymerization.

To test this, we select RCD polycarbonates as the testbed, also on account of their extensive investigation, spanning from structure–property relationships to diverse applications.52,53 First, as shown in Scheme 1(i), small amounts of allyloxy cyclic carbonates (AoCCs) are copolymerized with alkyl cyclic carbonates (ACCs) through a ring-opening mechanism, initiated by 1,4-benzenedimethanol (BDM) in the presence of 1,5,7-triazabicyclo[4.4.0]dec-5-ene (TBD) catalyst, leading to the formation of allyloxy polycarbonates (AoPC-x). Here we use the letter x to represent the incorporation ratio of AoCC into the final polymer. The engineered allyloxy functionality enables the conversion of AoPC-x into cross-linked polycarbonates (AoPC-x-C) through a thiol–ene reaction with an excess of pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP) upon UV irradiation at a wavelength of 365 nm (Scheme 1(ii)). The resulting AoPC-x-C exhibits enhanced thermal stability and mechanical properties in comparison to its linear counterpart, while still capable of undergoing ring-closing depolymerization (Scheme 1(iii)), which allows the excellent recovery of ACCs without the requirement for chemical separation processes.

Results and Discussion

We set out to synthesize two cyclic carbonates, ACC and AoCC, by reacting 2.1 equiv of ethyl chloroformate and 2-butyl-2-ethyl-1,3-propanediol or 2-(allyloxymethyl)-2-ethyl-1,3-propanediol, respectively (details on pages S3–4 of the Supporting Informaiton). The carbonylation reactions proceeded in the presence of 2.2 equiv of triethylamine for 18 h. Following workup and purification via column chromatography, ACC and AoCC were obtained in yields of 90.3% and 85.2%, respectively, and also at high purity, as confirmed by 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectroscopy, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Figures S3–8).

The as-synthesized ACC and AoCC were then copolymerized using 0.1 mol % of the initiator BDM and 1 mol % of the catalyst TBD. The copolymerization afforded linear polycarbonates (AoPC-x) as a colorless liquid, with yields ranging from 72.7% to 93.6% depending on x (Figure 1a, left). The incorporation ratio of AoCC (i.e., x) in AoPC-x was controlled by adjusting the feeding ratio of AoCC to ACC during the synthesis, and determined as 0%, 3%, 6%, and 12% by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S10–13). They exhibited number-average molecular weights of 12.9–16.5 kg mol–1, with the dispersity (Đ) varied between 1.1 and 1.2 (Figure S14; Table S2). In addition, the AoPC-x (x > 0%) possesses allyloxy functionalities on their side chains, which were subsequently converted into AoPC-x-C via a thiol–ene reaction with an excess of PETMP (200 mol % thiol groups relative to ene bonds) under UV irradiation at 365 nm for 30 min. After UV irradiation, liquid AoPC-6% transformed into a solid film (Figure 1b). Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis indicates the disappearance of the peak at 1672 cm–1, attributed to the stretching vibration of ene bonds in the spectrum of AoPC-12%, and the emergence of a new peak at 711 cm–1, ascribed to the vibration of C–S bonds, in that of AoPC-12%-C (Figure 1c). These changes in both the polymer appearance and structure provide clear evidence of successful cross-linking between AoPC-x and PETMP.

The chemical resistance of AoPC-x-C was evaluated by immersing AoPC-6%-C in various organic solvents including acetone, chloroform, dichloromethane, toluene, ethanol, and methanol at room temperature for 24 h (Figure S15). In all of these cases, AoPC-6%-C exhibited only swelling rather than dissolution, further corroborating the presence of cross-linking structure. We next examined the swelling capability of AoPC-x-C films by putting them into THF for 24 h with solvent renewal every 6 h (Figure S16a). Upon increasing the value of x, the swelling ratio decreased from 850% to 470%, while the gel fraction increased from 60% to 91% (Figure S16b), reflecting the increased cross-link density.

We then investigated the thermal properties of AoPC-x and AoPC-x-C by thermal gravity analysis (TGA) and Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC). TGA curves show a stark contrast (Figure 1d); AoPC-x exhibited a low 5% decomposition temperature (T5%) of 135–170 °C, while upon cross-linking, the T5% increased to a range of 213–257 °C (Figures 1d and S17). Moreover, incorporation of more cross-links further improved the thermal stability of the samples. For example, T5% of AoPC-3%-C was 213 °C, and it significantly increased to 224 °C for AoPC-6%-C, and further to 257 °C for AoPC-12%-C (Figure 1d). DSC traces reveal that the linear polycarbonates (i.e., AoPC-x) have a glass transition temperature (Tg) at approximately −30 °C (Figure S18). The degree of cross-linking had a direct impact on Tg, with higher cross-link densities leading to increased Tg values. This was evidenced by the increases from −11 °C of AoPC-3%-C to −6 °C of AoPC-6%-C and then to −2 °C of AoPC-12%-C (Figure 1e). The enhanced Tg is primarily attributed to the restricted chain mobility in the AoPC-x-C networks, imposed by the cross-links.54

The thermomechanical properties of the AoPC-x-C (x > 0%) films were then studied by Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA). All AoPC-x-C samples exhibited a discernible drop in storage modulus (E′) between 6 and 20 °C (Figure 1f), corresponding to the peak observed in the tan δ plots, indicative of their Tg. This transition was followed by the onset of a rubbery regime, with E′ values ranging from 0.1 to 0.8 MPa, confirming the presence of a cross-linking architecture in these polymers. Within this regime, AoPC-3%-C showed the lowest E′ value of 0.1 MPa, which steadily decreased with increasing temperature, suggesting a low cross-link density; whereas AoPC-6%-C and AoPC-12%-C reached a plateau and exhibited values of 0.4 and 0.8 MPa, respectively (Figure 1f). This suggests that higher cross-link densities are presented in the latter two, which is the highest in AoPC-12%-C. As the cross-link density increased, the peak in the tan δ plots shifted to a higher temperature (Figure 1f), reflecting an increase in Tg, consistent with the trend observed in the DSC (Figure 1e). However, the Tg values obtained by these two techniques differ by ca. 20 °C (Figure 1e–f), which is mainly attributed to the 1 Hz of oscillating force employed in the DMA measurement.55

The mechanical properties of the AoPC-x-C (x > 0%) films were further evaluated by tensile testing (Figure 1g). AoPC-3%-C was considerably soft, with a stress at break of only 0.015 MPa, which was increased by over 50-fold to 0.8 MPa upon incorporation of a 100 mol % excess of cross-links. However, subsequent doubling of the cross-link density, as seen in AoPC-12%-C, led to a reduction in stress at break to 0.4 MPa. A similar trend was observed for strain at break, with AoPC-3%-C exhibiting a value of 120%, followed by an increase to 130% AoPC-6%-C, and a subsequent drop to 40% for AoPC-12%-C. The decreases are mainly attributed to the restricted chain movement induced by the excessive cross-links.56,57 The Young’s modulus steadily increased from 0.07 MPa of AoPC-3%-C to 2.6 MPa of AoPC-12%-C, marking an over 37-fold increase, upon increasing the cross-link density (Figure 1g). These findings reflect that the presence of cross-links can improve the mechanical properties of AoPC-x-C distinctly.

Having established the role of cross-links in enhancing both the thermal and mechanical properties of our polymer films, we next investigated their impact on the depolymerization capability of AoPC-x-C (Figure 2a, left). The depolymerization process was adapted from a procedure recently reported by the Williams group.37,44 Specifically, 1 g of AoPC-x-C shreds were mixed with 20 wt % of glycerol ethoxylate (GEO; Mn = 1000 g mol–1), serving as both initiator and solvent, together with 1 wt % of Sn(Oct)2 as a catalyst. The mixture was connected with a vacuum distillation apparatus and heated at 200 °C for 20 h under a vacuum pressure of 0.1–1 mbar (Figure 2b). The setup allowed us to directly recover liquid products in yields of 74.7–91.7% (Figure 2d), without the need for further separation. Linear polycarbonate AoPC-0% (only consisting of ACC-derived repeating units) had the highest recovery yield of 91.7%, and such a value saw no discernible change upon incorporation of 3 mol % cross-links, as evidenced by the 91.4% recovery yield of AoPC-3%-C. However, higher cross-link densities resulted in a reduction in the depolymerization efficiency, with recovery yields decreasing to 87.9% for AoPC-6%-C and 74.7% for AoPC-12%-C. The results show that the incorporation of small amounts of cross-links in RCD polycarbonates maintains their depolymerization capability, while excessive cross-linking negatively impacts this property. Copolymer AoPC-12% was also subjected to the same depolymerization procedures, and allowed us to recover the mixture of ACC and AoCC successfully (Figure S19).

The recovered compounds were then subjected to 1H NMR spectroscopy and HRMS for analysis (Figures 2c, S20–23). 1H NMR spectra suggest the recovery of ACC in high quality from the depolymerization of AoPC-0% and AoPC-x-C (Figures 2c, S20–22). However, mass spectra reveal the presence of the ACC dimer in the depolymerized compounds (Figure S23). We attribute the formation of the dimer to the high depolymerization temperature of 200 °C that is able to overcome the energy barrier for the dimerization of ACC, a phenomenon also seen in previous studies.58,59 Our hypothesis for the depolymerization mechanism, involving the activation of carbonate motifs in the AoPC-x-C through coordination with Sn(Oct)2 and subsequent intramolecular insertion, is given in Figure S24.44,60

A subsequent repolymerization of the recovered compounds and 6 mol % of fresh AoCC (Figure 2a, right) yielded a linear polycarbonate with a chemical composition and molecular weight comparable to the original AoPC-6%, as evidenced by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures 2e, S25–26). These findings indicate that the presence of the ACC dimer does not significantly affect the repolymerization capability of the recovered compounds.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have presented a type of chemically recyclable thermosetting polycarbonate that exhibits improved thermal and mechanical properties compared to their linear counterparts, while also enabling direct monomer recovery following depolymerization. The thermosets are synthesized by the copolymerization of alkyl cyclic carbonates (ACCs) and allyloxy cyclic carbonates (AoCCs), with the AoCCs comprising allyloxy groups, which then enable cross-linking of the polymerized polycarbonates through a thiol–ene reaction with a tetrathiol compound upon UV irradiation at 365 nm. The resulting polycarbonate thermosets exhibit higher 5% decomposition temperatures, reaching up to 257 °C, in contrast to 135–170 °C for their linear counterparts. Such a topological transformation also leads to improved mechanical properties. Most importantly, we reveal here that the thermosets are still capable of undergoing depolymerization, which unlike conventional recycling scenarios for thermosets—mostly involving complex chemical separation processes—instead allows for direct monomer recovery right after depolymerization. Incorporating small amounts of cross-links (3 mol %) preserves a depolymerization capability similar to the linear version, while higher cross-link densities (above 6 mol %) reduce this capability. Nonetheless, this work marks a significant step toward more convenient and efficient recycling strategies for thermosets, allowing the direct recovery of valuable compounds.
