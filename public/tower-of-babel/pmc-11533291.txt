Reliable change indices for the Italian version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) in non-demented Parkinson’s disease patients
Authors: Edoardo Nicolò Aiello, Federica Solca, Silvia Torre, Beatrice Curti, Giulia De Luca, Ruggero Bonetti, Francesco Scheveger, Eleonora Colombo, Alessio Maranzano, Marco Olivero, Claudia Morelli, Alberto Doretti, Luca Maderna, Federico Verde, Roberta Ferrucci, Sergio Barbieri, Fabiana Ruggiero, Denise Mellace, Angelica Marfoli, Angelica De Sandi, Alberto Priori, Gabriella Pravettoni, Vincenzo Silani, Nicola Ticozzi, Andrea Ciammola, Barbara Poletti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background. The present study aimed at deriving regression-based reliable change indices (RCIs) for the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) in an Italian cohort of non-demented Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients.MethodsN = 33 consecutive, non-demented PD patients were followed-up at a 5-to-8-month interval (M = 6.6; SD = 0.6) with the MoCA. Practice effects and test-retest reliability were assessed via dependent-sample t-tests and intra-class correlation (ICC) coefficients, respectively. RCIs were derived separately for raw and demographically adjusted MoCA scores according to a standardized regression-based approach by accounting for both baseline confounders (i.e., demographics, disease duration and Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale scores) and retest interval.ResultsNo practice effects were found (t(32) = 0.29; p = .778), with acceptable test-retest reliability being detected (ICC = 0.67). MoCA scores at T0 proved to be the only significant predictor of T1 MoCA performances within both the model addressing raw scores and that addressing adjusted scores (ps < 0.001).ConclusionsThe present study provides Italian practitioners and researchers with regression-based RCIs for the MoCA in non-demented PD patients, which can be reliably adopted for retest interval ≥ 5 and ≤ 8 months without encountering any practice effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-024-03920-9.

Introduction

Up to 40% of patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) present with cognitive deficits involving executive and attentive processes, visuo-spatial abilities, language and memory [1]. Given the detrimental impact of such dysfunctions towards patients’ functional outcomes [2], their early detection and longitudinal monitoring via clinimetrically sound cognitive screeners is clinically pivotal [3]. In this respect, the Movement Disorders Society (MDS) has recommended the use of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [3] for cognitive screening aims in clinical and research practice addressing PD patients [4]. Indeed, the MoCA has shown optimal clinimetric properties and feasibility for detecting cognitive impairment in this population [4]. However, the vast majority of these reports are cross-sectional [4], with the number of studies focusing on the longitudinal feasibility of the MoCA in this population being unfortunately scarce [5; 6]. Investigations on the performance of the MoCA in tracking PD patients’ cognitive trends over time would indeed be of major practical relevance – since changes in cognition do occur longitudinally in this population [7], and the onset of cognitive impairment does not necessarily corresponds to the onset of PD (i.e., it might also become manifest later over the course of the disease) [8].

The abovementioned scarceness of longitudinal studies on the MoCA in PD particularly applies to the Italian scenario [9]. Indeed, in Italy, the clinimetric properties of the MoCA in PD patients have been solely explored cross-sectionally up to now [10–13]. Country-specific studies on cognitive screening tests are fundamental [9], given that language and cultural differences between Countries are known to possibly influence the psychometric and diagnostic performance, as well as the clinical usability, of the tests themselves [14]. Accordingly, a previous systematic review has highlighted the need for Country-specific clinimetric studies also with regard to the MoCA [15], and the call for Country-specific investigations of such a kind appears to be largely answered to – as testified by a recent work showing that more than 80 different versions of the MoCA exist worldwide [16].

When examining the longitudinal feasibility of cognitive screeners, the derivation of thresholds for defining clinically meaningful changes in cognition net of test-, context- or examinee-related confounders – i.e., of reliable change indices (RCIs) – is pivotal [17]. To this aim, the standardized regression-based (SRB) approach is currently regarded as the gold-standard statistical method [17]. This approaches indeed aims to test whether an over-time discrepancy in test scores is actually underpinned by a systematic variation in cognition that is not confounded by test-retest reliability, practice effects, ceiling/floor effects, retest interval, regression-to-the-mean phenomena or patients’ baseline features [17]. In this view, SRB RCIs allow to confidently identify actual changes in cognitive status over time at a single patient-level by taking into account relevant intervening variables [17].

Given the above premises, the present study aimed at deriving regression-based RCIs for the MoCA in an Italian cohort of non-demented PD patients.

Methods

Participants

Data on N = 33 PD patients consecutively referred to IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano, Milano, Italy and having been followed-up at a 5-to-8-month interval (M = 6.6; SD = 0.6) with the MoCA [3; 18] were retrospectively retrieved. Patients were free of (1) further neurological/psychiatric diagnoses, (2) severe/unstable general-medical conditions and (3) uncorrected hearing/vision deficits. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano (I.D.: 23C923_2019) and participants provided their informed consent.

Materials

Patients were administered the Italian version of the MoCA (v. 7.1) [18]. The MoCA is a 30-point, performance-based cognitive screener including items assessing both instrumental domains (i.e., language, memory, visuo-spatial abilities and orientation to time and space) and non-instrumental functions (i.e., attention and executive functioning). Additionally, functional outcomes were assessed via the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) [19], whilst disease stage via the Modified Hoehn-Yahr’s (H-Y) system [20].

Statistics

For the purposes of this report, total MoCA scores were addressed. Raw MoCA scores were free of ceiling/floor effects at both time-points (i.e., skewness values <|1|) [21]. Thus, the occurrence of practice effects was assessed, by addressing raw scores, via dependent-sample t-tests [22] and test-retest reliability via intra-class correlation (ICC) coefficients [23]. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.5 → unacceptable test-retest reliability; ≥0.5 and 0.75< → acceptable test-retest reliability; ≥0.75 and 0.9< → optimal test-retest reliability; ≥0.9 → excellent test-retes reliability [24].

RCIs were derived separately for raw and adjusted [18] MoCA scores according to an SRB approach [17]. Adjusted scores on the MoCA were computed according to current regression-based norms, where both age and education proved to be significant predictors of the performance on this test [18]. Accordingly, raw MoCA scores at follow-up (T1) were first regressed by stepwise-entering as predictors raw MoCA scores at baseline (T0), age at T0, education, retest interval (in months), disease duration (in months) at T0 and UPDRS and H-Y scores at T0. The same model was run when addressing adjusted MoCA scores – by nonetheless dropping age and education from the range of predictors. Subsequently, predicted MoCA scores at T1 were estimated based on the regression equations yielding from the best models. Finally, RCIs were computed by weighting the difference between predicted and observed MoCA scores at T1 on the standard error of the estimate of the model. Resulting z-deviates were deemed as suggestive of clinically meaningful changes if >|1.645|.

Analyses were run via IBM® SPSS® Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., 2021) and jamovi 2.3 (the jamovi project, 2022).

Results

Table 1 summarizes patients’ features at both time-points. No practice effects yielded (t(32) = 0.29; p = .778; Cohen’s d = 0.05), with acceptable test-retest reliability being detected (ICC = 0.67; p = .001; CI 95% [0.34, 0.84]). MoCA scores at T0 proved to be the only significant predictor of T1 MoCA scores within both the model addressing raw scores and the one addressing adjusted scores (ps < 0.001) (Table 2). Discussion

The present study provides practitioners and researchers with regression-based RCIs for the Italian MoCA in non-demented PD patients, thus adding up to previous, Country-specific evidence supporting its feasibility in this population [11–13]. Furthermore, findings herewith reported demonstrate that, in non-demented PD patients, the Italian MoCA is not subjected to practice effects, reliable and independent of baseline confounders (i.e., demographics and disease-related features) at a retest interval of 5-to-8 months.

The fact that no significant improvements in MoCA scores were herewith found suggests that this screener, even if administered at a relatively short distance, yields a score that is not biased by possible learning effects. As to the result of test-retest reliability being acceptable, but not optimal, it might be hypothesized that some changes in cognition occurred – albeit subtly. Interestingly, these findings align with a previous Italian report by Biundo et al. [10], where the MoCA yielded overall stable scores in a cohort of non-demented PD patients followed-up at a 12-month distance.

In this respect, Biundo et al. [10] also showed that the MoCA performed better than the Mini-Mental State Examination [25] in detecting cognitive decline in this population. Taken together, and even in spite of the fact that the current study is not directly comparable to Biundo et al.’s [10] one due to the difference in retest intervals, these findings point towards the suitability of the Italian MoCA to the aim of longitudinally monitor cognition over time in PD patients.

At the same time, it has to be noted that other, non-Italian reports addressing longer retest intervals did not support the capability of the MoCA to effectively track cognitive changes over time in this population [6; 26]. Whilst the differences in both the retest interval and the Countries where these study took place do not allow for a proper comparison, the heterogeneity of available evidence on the topic highlights the need for further investigations.

The above being said, this study still delivers valuable and practical information for Italian clinicians and applied researchers – as providing them with straightforward algorithms to determine whether Italian, non-demented PD patients present with clinically meaningful changes on the MoCA or not at a 5-to-8 retest interval.

Finally, it should be borne in mind that this study is not devoid of limitation. First and foremost, the retest interval herewith addressed is much shorter than the ones typically encountered within the concerning literature (i.e., ∼ 12 months) [8]. This of course does not allow for the application of the current RCIs to longer retest intervals and raises the need of further studies embracing different time spans. Second, the current sample is relatively restricted in size only include patients without dementia. Hence, further investigations are needed that address larger cohorts of PD patients stratified according to their cognitive phenotypes (i.e., normal cognition vs. mild cognitive impairment vs. dementia). Third, this study did not explore the longitudinal feasibility of each MoCA subscales/items: it is advisable that future reports aim at testing whether individual MoCA subscales/items are featured by different measurement properties over time in this population.

Future studies are then advisable that test the feasibility of currently available alternate forms of the Italian MoCA [27] for the longitudinal assessment of cognition in this population.

Conclusions

The present study provides Italian practitioners and researchers with SRB RCIs for the MoCA in non-demented PD patients, which can be reliably adopted for retest interval ≥ 5 and ≤ 8 months without encountering any practice effect.
