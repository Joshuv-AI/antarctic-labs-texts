Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the uniqueness problem of entire functions that share an entire function with their higher-order difference operators. We obtain two results that confirm the conjectures posed by Liu and Laine [ 25 ] and by Zhang et al. [ 39 ] , respectively. In addition, we present several relevant examples to further illustrate and support our findings

1.
Introduction and main results

In this paper, a meromorphic function
f
always means a function that is meromorphic in the entire complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. We assume that the reader is familiar with the standard notation and main results of Nevanlinna theory (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
38
]
).

We define the linear measure by

m(E):=\int_{E}dt

and the logarithmic measure by

l(E):=\int_{E\cap[1,\infty)}\frac{dt}{t}.

By
S(r,f)
, we denote any quantity satisfying
S(r,f)=o(T(r,f))
as
r\to\infty
possibly outside of an exceptional set of finite logarithmic measure. A meromorphic function
a
is said to be a
small function
of
f
if
T(r,a)=S(r,f)
.

Moreover, we use the notations
\rho(f)
,
\rho_{1}(f)
,
\mu(f)
and
\lambda(f)
to denote, respectively, the
order
,
hyper-order
, emphlower order, and
the exponent of convergence of zeros
of a meromorphic function
f
.
If
\mu(f)=\rho(f)
, we say that
f
is of
regular growth
.

It is well known that if
f=e^{g}
, where
g
is a polynomial, then
\rho(f)=\mu(f)=\deg(g)
. Furthermore, if
f
and
g
are two non-constant meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\rho(f)<\mu(g)
, then

T(r,f)=o(T(r,g)\;\;(r\to\infty)

(see Theorem 1.18
[
38
]
).

The
Borel exceptional
small function
a
of
f
is defined by

\lambda(f-a)=\limsup\limits_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log^{+}N(r,0;f-a)}{\log r}<\rho(f)

where
\lambda(f-a)
is the exponent of convergence of zeros of
f(z)-a(z)
. Also we know that if
f
is a transcendental meromorphic function with finite positive order
\rho(f)
, then
f
has at most two
Borel exceptional
values (see
[
38
, Theorem 2.10]
).

As usual, the abbreviations CM and IM stand for counting multiplicities and ignoring multiplicities, respectively.

Let
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Then the forward difference
\Delta^{n}_{c}f
for each integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
is defined in the standard way by

\Delta^{1}_{c}f(z)=\Delta_{c}f(z)=f(z+c)-f(z)

\Delta_{c}^{n}f(z)=\Delta_{c}\left(\Delta_{c}^{n-1}f(z)\right)=\Delta_{c}^{n-1}f(z+c)-\Delta_{c}^{n-1}f(z),\;\;n\geq 2.

Moreover

\Delta_{c}^{n}f(z)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-j}C^{j}_{n}f(z+jc),

where
C^{j}_{n}
is a combinatorial number. If an equation includes shifts or differences of
f
, then the equation is called difference equation.

We recall the following conjecture proposed by Brück
[
9
]
.

Conjecture A
.

Let
f
be a non-constant entire function such that
\rho_{1}(f)\not\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
If
f
and
f^{\prime}
share one finite value
a
CM, then
f^{\prime}-a=c(f-a),\;\text{where}\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

While the conjecture remains unsettled in its full generality, it has generated a substantial body of research concerning the uniqueness of entire and meromorphic functions sharing a single value with their derivatives.

Theorem A
.

[
18

Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function with
\rho(f)<2
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
. If
f(z)
and
f(z+c)
share the values
a\in\mathbb{C}
and
\infty

f(z+c)-a=\tau(f(z)-a)
holds for some constant
\tau
.

In the same paper, they also provided the following example to demonstrate that the condition “
\rho(f)<2
” cannot be relaxed to “
\rho(f)\leq 2
”.

Example A
.

Let
f(z)=e^{z^{2}}+1
and
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Clearly
f(z)
and
f(z+c)
share
1
and
\infty
CM,

\frac{f(z+c)-1}{f(z)-1}=e^{2cz+c^{2}}\neq\;\text{constant}.

For a transcendental entire function of order
\rho(f)<2
, Liu and Laine
[
25
]
obtained the following result
by replacing
f(z+c)
by
\Delta^{n}_{c}f
in
Theorem A
.

Theorem B
.

[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function of order
\rho(f)<2
not
having period
c
. If
f
and
\Delta^{n}_{c}f
share the value
0
CM, then
\Delta^{n}_{c}f=\tau f
, where
\tau\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

Next we consider the following example.

Example B
.

[
25
]
Let

f(z)=Ae^{z\log(c+1)}-((1-c)/c),

where
c\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},c>-1
and
A
is an arbitrary constant. Then
(\Delta_{1}f(z)-1)=c(f(z)-1)
.

Therefore
Example A
suggests that

\frac{\Delta^{n}f-a}{f-a}

may reduce to a non-zero constant, at least if
\rho(f)=1
and
N(r,1/f)\neq S(r,f)
.

Motivated by
Example B
, Liu and Laine
[
25
]
proposed the following conjecture (see Remark 1.4
[
25
]
).

Conjecture B
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function of order
1<\rho(f)<\infty
not
having period
c
. If
f
and
\Delta^{n}_{c}f
share the value
a\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
CM, then
\Delta^{n}_{c}f-a=\tau(f-a)
, where
\tau\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

In 2015, Zhang et al.
[
39
]
partially resolved Conjecture B under the assumptions “
\rho(f)<2
” and “
\lambda(f-a)<\rho(f)
”
In fact, they established the following result.

Theorem C
.

[
39
, Theorem 1.2]
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function such that
\rho(f)<2
and
\alpha(\not\equiv 0)
be an entire function such that
\rho(\alpha)<\rho(f)
and
\lambda(f-\alpha)<\rho(f)
. If
f-\alpha
and
\Delta^{n}_{1}f-\alpha
share
0
CM, then
\alpha
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
and
f
must be of form
f(z)=\alpha(z)+H(z)e^{dz}
, where
H
is a polynomial such that
cH=-\alpha
and
c,d\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
c^{d}=1
.

In the same paper, Zhang et al.
[
39
]
made the following remark.

“We are not sure whether the assumption
\alpha\not\equiv 0
in Theorem 1.2
[
39
]
is necessary or not.”

Consequently, Zhang et al.
[
39
]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture C
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function such that
\rho(f)<2
and
\lambda(f)<\rho(f)
. If
f
and
\Delta^{n}f
share
0
CM, then
f
must be of the form
f(z)=ce^{dz}
, where
c,\;d\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

To the best of our knowledge,
Conjectures
B and C remain unconfirmed. In this paper, we confirm both conjectures and further improve
Theorems
B and C by eliminating the condition “
\rho(f)<2
”

1.1.
In connection with Conjecture B

Concerning
Conjecture B
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function such that
\rho(f)<+\infty
,
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\not\equiv 0
and
\lambda(f)<\rho(f)
. If
f
and
\Delta^{n}_{c}f
share
0
CM, then
f
must be of form
f(z)=c_{0}e^{dz}
, where
c_{0},d\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

The following example demonstrates that the condition “
\lambda(f)<\rho(f)
” in Theorem
1.1
cannot be weakened.

Example 1.1
.

Let
f(z)=(\exp z-1)\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+\tau)}{c}z\right)
, where
\log
denotes the principal branch of the logarithm and
c=2\pi i
such that
\log(1+\tau)\neq c
. Note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\exp z-1)\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+\tau)}{c}(z+c)\right)-(\exp z-1)\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+\tau)}{c}z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\exp z-1)\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+\tau)}{c}z\right)\left(\exp(\log(1+\tau))-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau(\exp z-1)\exp\left(\frac{\log(1+\tau)}{c}z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau f(z).

Clearly
f
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
0
CM. On the other hand, we see that
\rho(f)\leq 1
and
\lambda(f)=\lambda(\exp z-1)=1
. Since
\lambda(f)\leq\rho(f)
, it follows that
\lambda(f)=\rho(f)
. Also it is clear that
f
does not satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
.

The following example indicates that the condition “
\rho(f)<+\infty
” in Theorem
1.1
is the best possible.

Example 1.2
.

Let
f(z)=e^{z}e^{s(z)}
, where
s(z)
is a periodic function with period
c=\log 2
. Clearly
\rho(f)=+\infty
. Note that
\Delta_{c}f=f
and so
f
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
0
CM. On the other hand, we see that
\lambda(f)=0<\rho(f)
, but
f
does not satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
.

In connection with Conjecture C

Concerning
Conjecture C
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function such that
\rho(f)<+\infty
,
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\not\equiv 0
and
\alpha(\not\equiv 0)
be an entire function such that
\rho(\alpha)<\rho(f)
and
\lambda(f-\alpha)<\rho(f)
. If
f-\alpha
and
\Delta^{n}_{c}f-\alpha
share
0
CM, then

\alpha
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
and
f
must be of form
f(z)=\alpha(z)+H(z)e^{dz}
, where
H
is a polynomial such that
c_{0}H=-\alpha
and
c_{0},d\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
c^{d}=1
.

The following examples verify the sharpness of the condition ‘
\lambda(f-\alpha)<\rho(f)
” in Theorem
1.2
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
f(z)=e^{z}
and
c=\log 2
. Note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f(z)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}C^{j}_{n}f(z+(n-j)c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{z}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}C^{j}_{n}e^{(n-j)c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(e^{nc}-C^{1}_{n}e^{(n-1)c}+\ldots+(-1)^{n}\right)e^{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(e^{c}-1\right)^{n}e^{z}=e^{z}.

Therefore
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\equiv f
and so
f
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f
share
1
CM. Since
0
and
\infty
are the Borel exceptional values of
f
, it follows that
1
can not be a Borel exceptional value of
f
and so
\lambda(f-1)=\rho(f)
. Clearly
f
does not satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
.

Example 1.4
.

Let
f(z)=\sin z
. Note that
\lambda(f)=\rho(f)
and
\Delta_{\pi}(f)
and
f
share
0
CM, but
f
does not satisfy Theorem
1.1
.

It is easy to see that condition “
\rho(\alpha)<\rho(f)
” in Theorem
1.2
is sharp.

Example 1.5
.

Let
f(z)=e^{z}+1
,
\alpha(z)=e^{z}-1
and
c=\log 2
. Note that
\rho(\alpha)=\rho(f)
and
\Delta_{c}f(z)=e^{z}
. Clearly
\lambda(f-\alpha)=0<\rho(f)
and
f-\alpha
and
\Delta_{c}f-\alpha
share
0
, but
f
does not satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
.

Following example shows that condition “
\rho(f)<+\infty
” in Theorem
1.2
is necessary.

Example 1.6
.

Let
f(z)=e^{z}\left(e^{s(z)}-1\right)
, where
s(z)
is a periodic function with period
c=\log 2
and
\alpha(z)=-e^{z}
. Clearly
\rho(f)=+\infty
. Note that
\Delta_{c}f=f
and so
f-\alpha
and
\Delta_{c}f-\alpha
share
0
CM. On the other hand, we see that
\lambda(f-\alpha)=0<\rho(f)
, but
f
does not satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Auxiliary lemmas

For the proof of our main results, we make use of the following five key lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
13
, Corollary 2.6]
Let
f
be a meromorphic function of finite order
\rho
and
\eta_{1},\;\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{f(z+\eta_{1})}{f(z+\eta_{2})}\right)=O(r^{\rho-1+\varepsilon}).

Lemma 2.2
.

[
13
, Corollary 8.3]
Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
be two arbitrary complex numbers and
f
be a meromorphic function of finite order
\sigma
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Then there exists a subset
E\subset(0,\infty)
with finite logarithmic measure such that for all
r\not\in E\cup[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\exp(-r^{\sigma-1+\varepsilon})\leq\bigg|\frac{f(z+\eta_{1})}{f(z+\eta_{2})}\bigg|\leq\exp(r^{\sigma-1+\varepsilon}).

Now we recall the definition of difference polynomial (see
[
20
, pp. 557]
). Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function,
\alpha_{\lambda}\in S(f)
, and let
\mu_{\lambda,j}\geq 0
and
\tau_{\lambda}\geq 1
be integers, where
j=1,\ldots,\tau_{\lambda}
and
\lambda\in J
, a finite index set. We always refer to an expression of the following form when we say “difference product”

M_{\lambda}(z,f)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\tau_{\lambda}}\left(f(z+c_{\lambda,j})\right)^{\mu_{\lambda,j}},

where
c_{\lambda,1},\ldots,c_{\lambda,\tau_{\lambda}}
are finite complex numbers such that at least one of
c_{\lambda,1},\ldots,c_{\lambda,\tau_{\lambda}}
is non-zero. Then
M(z,f)=\sum\limits_{\lambda\in J}a_{\lambda}M_{\lambda}(z,f)
is called a
difference polynomial in
f
. The maximal total degree of
M(z,f)
in
f
and the shifts of
f
is defined by

\deg(M(z,f))=\max\limits_{\lambda\in J}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\tau_{\lambda}}\mu_{\lambda,j}.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
20
, Theorem 2.3]

Let
f
be a transcendental meromorphic solution of finite order
\rho
of a difference equation of the form

U(z,f)P(z,f)=Q(z,f)
,
where
U(z,f),P(z,f),Q(z,f)
are difference polynomials such that the total degree
\deg\left(U(z,f)\right)=n
in
f
and its shifts and
\deg\left(Q(z,f)\right)\leq n
. Moreover we assume that
U(z,f)
contains just one term of maximal total degree in
f
and its shifts. Then for each
\varepsilon>0
we have

m(r,P(z,f))=O(r^{\rho-1+\varepsilon})+S(r,f)

possible outside of an exceptional set of finite logarithmic measure.

Remark 2.1
.

Also from the proof of Lemma
2.3
, we can say that if the coefficients of

U(z,f)
,
P(z,f)
and
Q(z,f)
, say
a_{\lambda}
satisfy
m(r,a_{\lambda})=S(r,f)
, then the same conclusion still holds.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
27
, Corollary 3.2]
Let
g
be a non-constant meromorphic solution of the linear difference equation

b_{k}g(z+kc)+b_{k-1}g(z+(k-1)c)+\ldots+b_{0}g(z)=R(z),

where
R
is polynomial and
b_{i}
’s for
i=0,1,\ldots,k
are complex constants with
b_{k}b_{0}\neq 0
,
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then either
\rho(g)\geq 1
or
g
is a polynomial. In particular if
b_{k}\neq\pm b_{0}
, then
\rho(g)\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
38
, Theorem 1.51]
Suppose that
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\;(n\geq 2)
are meromorphic functions and
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}
are entire functions satisfying the following conditions

(i)

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}e^{g_{j}}=0

(ii)

g_{i}-g_{j}
is non-constant for
1\leq i<j\leq n
;

(iii)

T(r,f_{j})=o\left(T(r,e^{g_{h}-g_{k}})\right)

(r\rightarrow\infty,r\not\in E)
for
1\leq j\leq n
,
1\leq h<k\leq n
.

Then
f_{j}\equiv 0
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.

3.
Proofs of main results

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By the given conditions, we have
\lambda(f-\alpha)<\rho(f)
. By Hadamard factorization theorem, there exist an entire function
H(\not\equiv 0)
and a polynomial
P
such that

\displaystyle f=\alpha+He^{P},

(3.1)

where
\rho(H)<\rho(f-\alpha)
and
\deg(P)=\rho(f-\alpha)
. Since
\rho(\alpha)<\rho(f)
, we have

\rho(H)<\rho(f)
and
\deg(P)=\rho(f)
. Let

\displaystyle P(z)=a_{s}z^{s}+a_{s-1}z^{s-1}+\cdots+a_{0},

(3.2)

where
a_{s}(\neq 0),a_{s-1},\ldots,a_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Also from (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f=\Delta_{c}^{n}\alpha+H_{n}e^{P},

(3.3)

where

\displaystyle H_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}H(z+ic)e^{P(z+ic)-P(z)}\;\;\text{and}\;\;c_{i}=(-1)^{n-i}C^{i}_{n}.

(3.4)

First we suppose that
\rho(f)<2
. Then by
Theorem C
, we conclude that
\alpha
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
and
f
must be of the form
f(z)=\alpha(z)+H(z)e^{dz}
, where
H
is a polynomial such that
c_{0}H=-\alpha
and
c_{0},d\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
c^{d}=1
.

Next we suppose that
\rho(f)\geq 2
. Since
f-\alpha
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f-\alpha
share
0
CM, then there exists a polynomial function
Q
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f-\alpha=(f-\alpha)e^{Q}.

(3.5)

We know that
\rho(H)<\rho(f)
and so
\rho(H(z+ic))<\rho(f)
for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
. Note that
\deg\left(P(z+ic)-P(z)\right)\leq s-1=\rho(f)-1
. Then from (
3.4
), we get
\rho(H_{n})<\rho(f)
. Also we know that
\rho(\Delta^{n}_{c}\alpha)\leq\rho(\alpha)
. Consequently from (
3.3
), we get
\rho\left(\Delta^{n}_{c}f\right)\leq\rho(f)
. Now from (
3.5
), we have
\deg(Q)\leq\rho(f)=s
.

We now consider following two cases.

Case 1.
Let
\deg(Q)=0
. Then from (
3.5
), we may assume that

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f-\alpha=c_{0}(f-\alpha),

(3.6)

where
c_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Now from (
3.1
), (
3.3
) and (
3.6
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}_{c}\alpha-\alpha=(c_{0}H-H_{n})e^{P}.

(3.7)

Note that
\rho\left(\Delta^{n}_{c}\alpha-\alpha\right)<\rho(f)=\rho\left(e^{P}\right)=\mu\left(e^{P}\right)

and
\rho\left(c_{0}H-H_{n}\right)<\rho(f)=\mu\left(e^{P}\right)
. Consequently
T\left(r,\Delta^{n}_{c}\alpha-\alpha\right)=S\left(r,e^{P}\right)
and
T\left(r,c_{0}H-H_{n}\right)=S\left(r,e^{P}\right)
. Now using Lemma
2.5
to (
3.7
), we have

\Delta^{n}_{c}\alpha\equiv\alpha\;\;\text{and}\;\;c_{0}H\equiv H_{n}.

Let
h(z)=e^{sca_{s}z^{s-1}}
. Then
\rho(h)=\mu(h)
and

\displaystyle T(r,h)=m(r,h)=\frac{|s||c||a_{s}|}{\pi}(1+o(1))r^{s-1}.

(3.8)

Now for
0\leq j\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle e^{P(z+jc)-P(z)}=e^{jsca_{s}z^{s-1}}e^{P_{j}(z)}=h^{j}(z)e^{P_{j}(z)},

where
P_{j}
is polynomial with degree at most
s-2
.
Note that

\rho\left(e^{P_{j}}\right)=\deg(P_{j})\leq s-2<s-1=\rho(h)=\mu(h)

and so
T\left(r,e^{P_{j}}\right)=S(r,h)
and so
T\left(r,e^{P_{j}}\right)=S(r,h^{j})
for

\displaystyle\exp\left(-r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}\right)\leq\bigg|\frac{H(z+jc)}{H(z+nc)}\bigg|\leq\exp\left(r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}\right)\;(j=0,1,\ldots,n-1).

(3.46)

We easily get

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left(jsa_{s}cz^{s-1}\right)\right|=\exp\left(js|a_{s}c|r^{s-1}\right),\;j=0,1,\ldots,n

(3.47)

and

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left(d_{s-1}z^{s-1}\right)\right|=\left|\exp\left(|d_{s-1}|r^{s-1}\cos((s-1)\theta_{0}+\theta_{2})\right)\right|\leq\left|\exp\left(|d_{s-1}|r^{s-1}\right)\right|.

(3.48)

Also we easily obtain

\displaystyle\left|\exp(\pm P_{s-2,j}(z))\right|<\exp\Big(\frac{3|C_{0}|}{2}r^{s-2}\Big)\;\text{and}\;\left|\exp(\tilde{P}_{s-2}(z))\right|<\exp\Big(\frac{3|d_{s-2}|}{2}r^{s-2}\Big)

(3.49)

for sufficiently large values of
r
.

Then from (
3.46
), (
3.47
) and (
3.49
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{H(z+jc)}{H(z+nc)}e^{jsa_{s}cz^{s-1}}e^{P_{s-2,j}(z)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(js|a_{s}c|r^{s-1}+r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}+\frac{3|C_{0}|}{2}r^{s-2}\right)

(3.50)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left((n-1)s|a_{s}c|r^{s-1}+r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}+\frac{3|C_{0}|}{2}r^{s-2}\right),

for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
.

Again from (
3.46
), (
3.48
) and (
3.49
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{H(z)}{H(z+nc)}e^{d_{s-1}z^{s-1}}e^{\tilde{P}_{s-2}(z)}\right|\leq\exp\left(|d_{s-1}|r^{s-1}+r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}+\frac{3|d_{s-2}|}{2}r^{s-2}\right).

(3.51)

Now from (
3.45
), (
3.47
), (
3.49
)-(
3.51
), we obtain

\displaystyle\exp\left(ns|a_{s}c|r^{s-1}\right)

(3.52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{n}}\frac{H(z+jc)}{H(z+nc)}e^{jsa_{s}cz^{s-1}}e^{P_{s-2,j}(z)}-\frac{1}{c_{n}}\frac{H(z)}{H(z+nc)}e^{d_{s-1}z^{s-1}}e^{\tilde{P}_{s-2}(z)}\right|\left|e^{P_{s-2,n}(z)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B\exp\left(x_{2}r^{s-1}+r^{\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon}+\frac{6|C_{0}|+3|d_{s-2}|}{2}r^{s-2}\right),

where

B=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}|c_{j}|\;\;\text{and}\;\;x_{2}=\max\{(n-1)s|a_{s}c|,|d_{s-1}|\}.

Since
|d_{s-1}|<ns|a_{s}c|
, we have
x_{2}<ns|a_{s}c|
. Also we have
\rho(H)-1+\varepsilon<s-1
. Consequently from (
3.52
), we get a contradiction.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By the given conditions, we have
\lambda(f)<\rho(f)
. Then there exist an entire function
H(\not\equiv 0)
and a polynomial
P
such that
f=He^{P}
, where
\rho(H)<\rho(f)
and
\deg(P)=\rho(f)
.

Now we divide the following two cases.

Case 1.
Let
\rho(f)<2
. Since
\rho(f)=\deg(P)<2
, it follows that
\rho(f)=\deg(P)=1
. Again since
\lambda(H)<\rho(f)
, we have
\lambda(H)<1
. Therefore we may assume that

\displaystyle f(z)=H(z)e^{dz},

(3.53)

where

and
\lambda(H)<1
. Now from (
3.53
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f(z)=H_{n}(z)e^{dz},

(3.54)

where

\displaystyle H_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}H(z+ic)e^{icd}\;\;\text{and}\;\;c_{i}=(-1)^{n-i}C^{i}_{n}.

(3.55)

Since
f
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f
share
0
CM, then by
Theorem B
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{n}f=af,

(3.56)

where
a\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Then from (
3.53
)-(
3.56
), we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}e^{icd}H(z+ic)+((-1)^{n}-a)H(z)=0.

(3.57)

Now we divide the following two sub-cases.

Sub-case 1.1.
Let
a\neq(-1)^{n}
. Now using Lemma
2.4
to (
3.57
), we conclude that

H
is a non-zero polynomial. We claim that
H
is a non-zero constant. If not, suppose
\deg(H)=k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
H(z)=a_{k}z^{k}+a_{k-1}z^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}z+a_{0}\;(a_{k}\neq 0)

and

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}e^{icd}+((-1)^{n}-a)=(e^{cd}-1)^{n}-a=0

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}e^{icd}(ika_{k}+a_{k-1})+((-1)^{n}-a)a_{k-1}=0.

(3.59)

Now from (
3.58
) and (
3.59

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}ie^{icd}=0.

(3.60)

Again from (
3.58
), we have
a=(e^{cd}-1)^{n}
. Since
a\neq 0
, we have
e^{cd}\neq 1
.
Note that

\displaystyle C_{n}^{i}i=\frac{n!i}{(n-i)!i!}=\frac{n(n-1)!}{((n-1)-(i-1))!(i-1)!}=nC_{n-1}^{i-1}

and so

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}ie^{icd}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}C^{i}_{n}ie^{icd}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ne^{cd}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}C^{i-1}_{n-1}e^{(i-1)cd}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ne^{cd}(e^{cd}-1)^{n-1}\neq 0.

Therefore from (
3.60
) and (
3.61
), we get a contradiction.
Hence
H
is a non-zero constant. Then from (
3.53
), we may assume that
