Trends in Stunting Prevalence and Sex-based Disparities among Children under Five in Somalia, 2000–2024: A Secondary Analysis of WHO Model-based Estimates
Authors: Abdirasak Sharif Ali, Augustus Osborne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to describe the 24-year trajectory (2000–2024) of stunting prevalence among children under five years of age and quantify the magnitude and temporal stability of sex-based disparities in chronic malnutrition using WHO model-based estimates.MethodsA cross-sectional trend analysis was performed using model-based estimates extracted from the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) version 7.0. This study analysed annual stunting prevalence disaggregated by sex and utilized inequality metrics, including the difference (D), ratio (R), Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), and Population Attributable Risk (PAR), to quantify health inequities over time.ResultsStunting prevalence in Somalia decreased from 33.6% in 2000 to 23.9% in 2024, reflecting an overall reduction of 9.7 percentage points. However, sex-based disparities persisted across the study period, with males consistently experiencing higher stunting prevalence than females. In 2024, the prevalence was 25.7% (95% CI: 15.8–39.1) among males and 22.0% (95% CI: 13.0–34.8) among females. The inequality ratio remained nearly unchanged at 0.86–0.87, suggesting persistent relative inequality despite improvements in the national prevalence of stunting.ConclusionSomalia has achieved notable reductions in chronic malnutrition over the last two decades; however, the prevalence of stunting remains a medium-to-high public health concern. The enduring vulnerability of male children to stunting highlights the need for targeted sex-sensitive nutritional interventions. To meet international health targets, policymakers must implement multisectoral strategies that address modifiable risk factors, such as maternal education, household wealth, and climate resilience.

Introduction

Childhood malnutrition remains a critical public health challenge globally, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which continues to report the highest regional burden of growth failure as of 2024 (The UNICEF-WHO-World Bank Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates (JME) Standard Methodology [23]. Stunting, defined as a height-for-age z-score (HAZ) more than two standard deviations below the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards median, remains the foundational metric for identifying chronic undernutrition resulting from the prolonged deprivation of basic needs [10]. The consequences of stunting are severe and often irreversible, leading to compromised cognitive potential, reduced physical growth capacity, and a heightened vulnerability to infectious diseases across the life course (Home—Global Nutrition Report [12, 21]. Despite global efforts to achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2.2, which aims to end all forms of malnutrition by 2030, progress in SSA has been uneven, with the region often reporting the highest absolute number of stunted children [1, 7].

Somalia represents a unique and complex environment for addressing childhood stunting because of its history of protracted conflict, recurring natural disasters such as droughts and floods, and widespread food insecurity [7, 13]. In resource-limited and unstable settings, traditional cross-sectional surveys often face logistical and security barriers, leading to significant data gaps. To address these challenges, researchers have increasingly utilized model-based estimates and geostatistical forecasting to assess the nutritional status. For instance, geostatistical modelling has demonstrated that stunting in Somalia is spatially and temporally heterogeneous, with environmental factors such as rainfall and vegetation cover serving as major drivers of this variation. Based on these models, the national stunting prevalence in Somalia was estimated to be approximately 30% in 2008 and 28% in 2010 [13].

More recent evidence from the 2019 Somalia Micronutrient Survey indicates a stunting prevalence of 17.2% among children aged 0–59 months, although this remains a medium-level public health concern according to WHO standards [7]. Research across the broader SSA region suggests that stunting is driven by several population-modifiable risk factors, including maternal education, household wealth, access to clean water and sanitation, and child health indicators such as iron deficiency and a history of diarrhoea [1, 22]. In Somalia, children residing in severely food-insecure households and those with systemic inflammation face a significantly higher risk of stunting [7].

Given the high burden of stunting and the regional disparities observed within Somalia ranging from hotspots in the Central and Eastern regions to lower rates in parts of the South there is an urgent need for targeted, evidence-based interventions [9]. Understanding the determinants of stunting through advanced modelling allows policymakers to prioritize resources for vulnerable populations. The study described temporal trends in the prevalence of stunting in Somalia using WHO model-based estimates, with a focus on quantifying sex-based disparities, to inform evidence-based public health strategies aimed at reducing chronic malnutrition in the region.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a secondary descriptive trend analysis of repeated cross-sectional model-based estimates to assess sex-based disparities in stunting prevalence among children under five years of age in Somalia. Using model-based estimates spanning 24 years (2000–2024), we examined the progression of chronic malnutrition using sex-disaggregated data sourced from the World Health Organization (WHO).

Data Source

This study utilized data extracted from the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), a publicly accessible online software designed to facilitate the exploration and comparison of health inequalities across countries. Specifically, we accessed the built-in database edition of the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), Version 7.0, and extracted Somalia-specific estimates for the period 2000–2024. The data were retrieved from the WHO Health Inequality Monitor repository through the HEAT platform in January 2026. The HEAT database used in this analysis draws from the WHO Health Inequality Monitor repository, which compiles health inequality indicators from global health surveys and model-based estimates. It should be noted that the repository may have been revised or updated since the time of data extraction, and the most recent version of the database is available on the WHO website [5]. The HEAT software provides disaggregated data by dimensions, such as sex, enabling the assessment of inequities in health outcomes, such as stunting, which is a key indicator of chronic malnutrition.

To provide clarity on the data utilized in this study, it should be noted that the model-based estimates from the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) are generated through a synthesis of global health surveys, national datasets, and advanced statistical modelling approaches as outlined by the WHO. These estimates often employ Bayesian hierarchical models to address data gaps and variability, particularly in conflict-affected regions such as Somalia, by incorporating covariates like demographic and environmental factors when direct survey data are limited (WHO 2025). However, comprehensive details regarding the specific modelling assumptions, data weighting techniques, or the exact sources underpinning the estimates for Somalia over the 2000–2024 period were not fully accessible during this analysis. This limitation in detailed methodological documentation is recognized as a potential constraint in evaluating the precision and reliability of the stunting prevalence estimates presented here.

Data Extraction and Indicators

Stunting, defined as a height-for-age z-score (HAZ) less than -2 standard deviations below the median of the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards, based on the WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study (MGRS) 2006 standards, remains a key indicator of chronic undernutrition resulting from prolonged deprivation of essential nutritional and health needs. The dataset included annual estimates from 2000 to 2024, disaggregated by sex (male and female), and provided point estimates of prevalence along with 95% confidence intervals (lower and upper bounds), and national averages (referred to as the 'setting average' in the dataset). Additionally, we retrieved inequality measures computed by HEAT, including the difference (D), ratio (R), Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), and Population Attributable Risk (PAR), to quantify sex-based disparities in stunting prevalence over the same period. Following the HEAT framework, the Difference (D) and Ratio (R) summarize absolute and relative subgroup inequality, respectively, whereas Population Attributable Risk (PAR) and Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) compare the national estimate with the subgroup having the most favourable outcome. All data were accessed and downloaded as comma-separated value (CSV) files directly from the HEAT interface to ensure consistency with the software’s predefined calculations and outputs. No primary data collection was conducted for this analysis, as it relied entirely on secondary data provided by the WHO.

Data Analysis

The analysis was performed using pre-calculated estimates and inequality metrics generated by HEAT software without additional statistical processing or modification. For stunting prevalence, we organized the data into a tabular format to present annual estimates by sex, including point estimates, confidence intervals, and national averages, to illustrate trends over time and sex-based differences in the prevalence of stunting. The national average (setting average) was interpreted as the overall prevalence of stunting in Somalia for each year, allowing for an assessment of temporal trends at the country level. For inequality measures, we compiled the HEAT-generated metrics: Difference (D), representing the absolute difference in prevalence between females and males; Ratio (R), indicating the relative disparity as a proportion of female to male prevalence; and PAF and PAR, assessing the population-level impact of sex-based inequality, into a separate table to highlight the extent and nature of the disparities. The descriptive interpretation of these results focused on identifying patterns, trends, and implications of the prevalence of stunting and inequities over the 24-year period. No external statistical software was used because the HEAT platform provided all necessary computations internally.

Ethical Considerations

This study relied on publicly available anonymized secondary data from the WHO HEAT database; therefore, ethical approval or informed consent was not required. The data did not include individual-level information, ensuring compliance with data protection and privacy standards. The use of HEAT and the associated data adhered to the terms and conditions outlined by the WHO for non-commercial research purposes.

Limitations of Data and Software

The reliance on model-based estimates from the HEAT database introduces several limitations that must be carefully considered when interpreting the results of this study. Primarily, the wide confidence intervals (CIs) associated with the prevalence figures indicate substantial uncertainty, likely arising from sparse data availability, inherent assumptions in the modelling process, or the logistical difficulties of collecting accurate information in a conflict-affected setting like Somalia. Such uncertainties could impact the precision of the observed trends and the reliability of comparisons across years. The widening confidence intervals in later years, especially after 2015, indicate increasing uncertainty in the model-based estimates. This may reflect greater reliance on modelled inputs where recent direct survey data were sparse or difficult to obtain in conflict-affected and insecure settings. Additionally, the use of secondary data means that the underlying sources and specific statistical techniques employed by WHO are not entirely transparent within the HEAT platform, which restricts our capacity to fully evaluate potential biases or inaccuracies in the estimates. For example, if the models do not adequately account for localized issues such as seasonal food shortages or population displacement due to ongoing conflict, the resulting estimates might misrepresent the actual stunting prevalence. Furthermore, periodic updates to the WHO Health Inequality Monitor repository may alter the data extracted for this analysis, and we advise readers to consult the most current database version for verification of these results. These constraints emphasize the need for cautious interpretation and highlight the importance of future studies utilizing primary data collection to corroborate these model-based findings in Somalia’s unique context. Moreover, the scope of the HEAT dataset precluded a direct examination of modifiable risk factors such as maternal education, household wealth, or access to clean water and sanitation, which are widely recognized as key determinants of stunting in Sub-Saharan Africa, thereby limiting the comprehensiveness of our analysis in identifying underlying causes of the observed trends.

Sex-based Disparities in Stunting Prevalence

A striking feature of the data in Table 1 is the persistent sex-based disparity in the prevalence of stunting across all years. In the HEAT database, the “setting average” refers to the overall national model-based estimate for Somalia for a given year. It is presented separately from subgroup-specific prevalence estimates and should not be interpreted as the average of the subgroup confidence interval bounds.Males consistently exhibit higher stunting rates than females, with the gap ranging from 3.6 to 4.5 percentage points. For instance, in 2024, the prevalence was 25.7% for males (95% CI: 15.8–39.1) compared to 22.0% for females (95% CI: 13.0–34.8), while in 2000, the figures were 35.8% for males (95% CI: 32.2–39.6) and 31.3% for females (95% CI: 28.0–34.9). This disparity is evident every year, with males showing a higher point estimate of stunting prevalence, suggesting a greater vulnerability to chronic malnutrition. These wide confidence intervals underscore considerable uncertainty in the prevalence estimates, likely attributable to limited data availability and the inherent difficulties of gathering reliable information in Somalia’s conflict-affected environment, which may compromise the precision of the observed sex-based differences and the overall trend analysis. The uncertainty becomes more pronounced in the later years of the series, particularly from approximately 2015 onward, when the confidence intervals widen substantially for both sexes. This pattern suggests reduced precision in recent model-based estimates and should be considered when interpreting the apparent year-to-year changes in subgroup prevalence.

The Ratio (R) measure, which reflects relative inequality by comparing stunting prevalence in females to that in males, remained stable at approximately 0.86 to 0.87 across the study period. This indicates that females consistently experience approximately 86–87% of the stunting prevalence observed in males, underscoring a persistent relative inequity. The stability of this ratio over 24 years suggests that, despite overall reductions in stunting prevalence, the proportional difference between the sexes has not significantly changed, highlighting a systemic challenge in addressing sex-based disparities in nutritional outcomes.

Population Attributable Risk (PAR) and Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) were reported as 0.00 across all years in the HEAT output. According to the HEAT framework, these summary measures compare the national estimate with the subgroup experiencing the most favourable outcome. For the sex dimension in this dataset, females consistently had the lower stunting prevalence and therefore constituted the reference subgroup. The resulting PAR and PAF values of 0.00 should therefore be interpreted as a feature of the HEAT summary measure framework for this dimension, rather than as evidence that male–female differences in stunting were absent.

Discussion

The findings of this analysis indicate a notable reduction in Somalia's national average stunting prevalence from 33.6% in 2000 to 23.9% in 2024. This 9.7 pp decrease is consistent with recent data from neighbouring countries, such as Ethiopia, where the prevalence of stunting decreased from 47 to 37% over a comparable time frame [6]. Despite these advancements, the prevalence of stunting in Somalia in 2024 remains a significant public-health concern. Previous regional evaluations by [16] suggested that stunting in Africa had plateaued at approximately 40%,however, the current findings suggest that Somalia has achieved a more substantial decline than previously expected, which may be attributable to the expansion of nutrition-specific interventions [7].

Examining Somalia’s stunting trends alongside those of other conflict-affected countries in Sub-Saharan Africa offers additional perspective on the role of contextual factors in shaping nutritional outcomes. For example, South Sudan, which similarly endures prolonged conflict and mass displacement, reported a stunting prevalence of approximately 31% in recent estimates, though data reliability is compromised by security constraints [14]. Unlike Somalia, where a gradual decline has been observed, South Sudan’s rates exhibit less consistent improvement, potentially due to more severe interruptions in humanitarian aid distribution. Conversely, Ethiopia, despite facing historical conflict and environmental challenges, has seen a more pronounced reduction in stunting from 47 to 37% over a comparable period, likely aided by structured national nutrition initiatives [6]. Somalia’s distinct combination of enduring conflict, frequent natural disasters like droughts and floods, and limited governmental capacity likely impedes similar progress, as these conditions disrupt food supply chains, displace communities, and hinder access to health services. Additionally, the accuracy of model-based estimates in Somalia may be affected by these challenges, as data collection often remains incomplete or skewed toward more accessible regions, potentially underestimating the true burden in remote or conflict-intensive areas. This suggests that the reported 9.7 percentage point decline in stunting prevalence may not fully capture the severity in underserved zones. Policymakers must account for these contextual barriers when designing interventions, prioritizing flexible and mobile strategies that can operate amidst insecurity and displacement. Future research should aim to validate these model-based estimates through localized surveys in Somalia to better understand regional disparities driven by conflict and environmental instability.

The persistent higher prevalence of stunting among male children in Somalia, observed consistently across the 24-year study period, necessitates a closer examination of potential contributing factors, particularly those rooted in the Somali context. Biologically, male children may require higher caloric and nutritional intake during critical growth periods, rendering them more susceptible to deficiencies in environments marked by food scarcity [17]. In Somalia, where recurring droughts, floods, and conflict frequently disrupt food availability, this biological vulnerability could be exacerbated if household resources fail to meet these elevated needs. Furthermore, cultural dynamics may contribute,in some Sub-Saharan African settings, gender norms might prioritize female child survival under harsh conditions, potentially leading to differential caregiving or health-seeking behaviours that disadvantage male children [22]. In rural Somali communities, young boys may also face greater exposure to environmental risks, such as waterborne infections during outdoor activities or early labour roles, which could increase their likelihood of illness and subsequent stunting. Regrettably, specific data on feeding practices, caregiving patterns, or gender-based health access in Somalia are limited, hindering a definitive identification of the mechanisms driving these disparities. This data gap highlights an urgent need for qualitative research involving Somali caregivers to uncover cultural and social determinants of sex differences in nutritional outcomes. Until such evidence is available, nutritional interventions in Somalia should adopt a sex-sensitive lens, ensuring that programs are designed to address potential differences in vulnerability between male and female children, particularly in the most affected regions.

Although this study focused on sex-disaggregated trends in stunting prevalence, it did not incorporate an analysis of modifiable risk factors due to the limitations of the HEAT dataset, which primarily offers prevalence estimates and inequality metrics without detailed covariate data. Extensive research from Sub-Saharan Africa identifies factors such as maternal education, household economic status, access to safe water, and child health conditions like diarrhoea as critical drivers of stunting [1]. In Somalia, where severe food insecurity and systemic inflammation are prevalent challenges, these factors are likely to exert significant influence, as highlighted by findings from the 2019 Somalia Micronutrient Survey [7]. The exclusion of these determinants from our analysis restricts our ability to fully elucidate the root causes behind the observed trends and sex disparities. To address this gap, future studies should utilize datasets such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) or undertake primary data collection to investigate how these risk factors interact with sex differences and environmental stressors in Somalia. Such comprehensive analyses are essential for developing targeted interventions that address the multifaceted nature of stunting in this context.

Inequality measures further highlight the systemic nature of these gaps. Although the absolute difference (D) narrowed slightly from -4.50 to -3.70, the ratio (R) remained remarkably stable at 0.86 to 0.87. This stability indicates that despite the overall reduction in stunting, the relative disadvantage for male children has not been mitigated by public health improvements. This suggests that the drivers of stunting in Somalia may be rooted in modifiable factors that affect both sexes but impact biological development differently, such as the high prevalence of systemic inflammation and iron deficiency reported in recent Somali micronutrient surveys [7].

An intriguing aspect of this study is the consistent reporting of zero values for Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) and Population Attributable Risk (PAR) over the 24-year period, despite evident differences in stunting prevalence between males and females. This may imply that the HEAT toolkit’s algorithms do not attribute population-level excess stunting to sex disparities, possibly because sex is not categorized as a modifiable determinant within their modelling framework. Alternatively, it could suggest that in Somalia, systemic challenges such as pervasive food insecurity, inadequate sanitation, or conflict-related disruptions overshadow sex-specific vulnerabilities at the aggregate level, rendering sex disparities less significant in population metrics. This finding diverges from studies in other Sub-Saharan African contexts where PAF and PAR have been effectively used to quantify the burden of modifiable risk factors like maternal education or household wealth [1] Due to the absence of detailed documentation on how HEAT calculates these metrics, it remains unclear whether this result reflects a genuine lack of population-level impact or a methodological artifact. Future research should consider employing alternative inequality measures or collecting primary data to better understand the population-level significance of sex disparities in stunting within Somalia’s unique socio-political landscape.

Policy Implications

The persistence of high stunting rates despite a two-decade decline underscores the need to prioritize interventions that have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing child undernutrition in low-resource settings, while adapting their delivery to Somalia’s conflict-affected and climate-vulnerable context. A key priority is strengthening maternal education and caregiver nutrition knowledge, given the consistent association between low maternal education and childhood stunting across Sub-Saharan Africa [1, 20]. Evidence syntheses on maternal and child nutrition also support the role of maternal education, counselling, and community-based nutrition interventions in improving child nutrition outcomes [3, 4, 18]. In Somalia, these strategies may be adapted through collaboration with local NGOs and community health workers, including outreach in internally displaced persons (IDP) camps and other underserved settings. Given Somalia’s vulnerability to droughts, floods, and food-system shocks, nutrition programming should be integrated with broader resilience-oriented actions that protect household food access and essential child health services during environmental crises. This recommendation is supported by evidence that environmental conditions are closely linked to nutritional vulnerability in Somalia [13], although the effectiveness of specific climate-adaptation interventions for reducing stunting should be evaluated further in this setting. Social protection approaches, including cash transfers and related safety-net mechanisms, should also be considered because household poverty is a major determinant of childhood stunting [1, 8]. Evidence from low- and middle-income settings suggests that such approaches can improve food security and care practices, particularly when linked with health and nutrition services [4, 18]. In Somalia, delivery through mobile money platforms may offer a feasible implementation pathway in insecure or hard-to-reach areas, although local effectiveness should be assessed through operational research.

Future Directions

Future research should utilize high-resolution geospatial and multi-class classification models to pinpoint stunting hotspots at the district level, particularly in Central and Eastern Somalia, where historical data suggest higher prevalence [9, 19]. Considering Somalia’s logistical constraints, such studies could employ satellite imagery and mobile data collection tools to assess nutritional outcomes in remote or conflict-affected areas where traditional surveys are infeasible. While this study focused on sex as a primary stratifier, future analyses should incorporate additional variables like birth interval, type of cooking fuel, and maternal BMI, which have been shown to significantly affect stunting severity in neighbouring Ethiopia [2, 11]. Moreover, longitudinal research is needed to investigate whether the zero PAF/PAR values observed here result from methodological limitations or indicate that gender-neutral systemic improvements (e.g., sanitation or water access) offer the most effective intervention pathway [15]. In Somalia’s unique setting, mixed methods approaches, including qualitative interviews with caregivers in displacement camps, could reveal critical barriers to nutritional equity, providing insights for intervention design. These focused research efforts are crucial for addressing Somalia’s specific challenges and building on the trends identified in this study to inform effective public health strategies.

Study Limitations

This study used secondary model-based estimates from WHO HEAT rather than primary anthropometric data, and limited access to Somalia-specific modelling inputs restricted full assessment of bias and precision. Wide confidence intervals suggest substantial uncertainty, particularly in a conflict-affected setting where underlying data may be incomplete or unevenly distributed. The descriptive analysis, based on pre-calculated outputs, could not assess causal determinants of stunting, while national averages may have masked important subnational disparities. Comparable annual data on maternal formal education and low birth weight were also unavailable, preventing assessment of their trends and contribution to observed stunting patterns. In addition, the zero PAF/PAR values may reflect methodological constraints rather than a true absence of population-level inequality. Although the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards are sex-specific, the observed male–female difference may still be partly sensitive to the anthropometric reference or metric used; this should be interpreted as a methodological caveat, not a complete explanation of the Somali findings.

Conclusions

Somalia has achieved a notable reduction in childhood stunting since 2000, reaching a 24-year low of 23.9% by 2024. However, the persistence of sex-based disparities, with males bearing a significantly higher burden, highlights the enduring inequities in nutritional outcomes. The stability of the inequality ratio (0.86–0.87) suggests that current public health efforts, while effective at the national level, have not yet addressed the specific biological or cultural factors that place Somali boys at a higher risk of death. To meet international benchmarks, Somalia must implement multisectoral policies that combine poverty mitigation, maternal education, and environmental forecasting to target the most vulnerable children across all geopolitical zones.
