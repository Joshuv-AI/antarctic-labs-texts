Clinical features and outcomes of nocardiosis in Thai patients with childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Sirikarn Tangcheewinsirikul, Sirin Nuntasri, Supattra Rungmaitree, Nuntawan Piyaphanee, Sirirat Charuvanij, Maynart Sukharomana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNocardiosis is a rare but life-threatening opportunistic infection in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), although data on childhood-onset SLE remain scarce. The objectives of this study were to determine the prevalence, clinical features, associated factors, and outcomes of nocardiosis in cSLE.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted in a tertiary academic center including cSLE patients followed between January 2009 and August 2024. Culture-confirmed nocardiosis cases were identified through systematic review of electronic medical records. Firth penalized logistic regression was used to explore baseline factors associated with the development of nocardiosis.ResultsSeven out of 323 patients (2.2%) developed culture-confirmed nocardiosis. All were female with a mean age at diagnosis of 11.9 ± 3.1 years. The median (interquartile range) duration from cSLE diagnosis to nocardiosis diagnosis was 4.3 (2.1–13.4) months, with 71.4% occurring within the first year. All patients were receiving corticosteroids at a mean prednisolone-equivalent dose of 1.7 ± 1.3 mg/kg/day at the time of infection. The respiratory tract involvement manifesting as pneumonia was the most common infection site (71.4%). Proliferative lupus nephritis had an exploratory association with nocardiosis (OR 5.03, 95% CI 1.19–28.36, p = 0.028). Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis at initial SLE diagnosis was significantly more frequent in the nocardiosis group (28.6% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.014). Carbapenem-based induction followed by trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX) consolidation was used in the majority of patients. The in-hospital mortality rate from the index nocardiosis episode was 0%, and the 12-month all-cause mortality rate was 14.3%, attributable to secondary Corynebacterium septicemia in the patient with the most multiple immunosuppressive drug therapy with cumulative burden.ConclusionNocardiosis affects 2.2% of cSLE patients and occurs predominantly early in the disease course. Proliferative lupus nephritis had an exploratory association with nocardiosis, highlighting the need for heightened clinical vigilance and the use of antimicrobial susceptibility testing to guide management in high-risk patients.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus, Nocardiosis, Opportunistic infection, Mortality

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by immune dysregulation and autoantibody production. Childhood-onset SLE (cSLE), defined as disease onset before the age of 18, accounts for approximately 15–20% of SLE cases [1, 2]. Compared to adult-onset SLE, cSLE is associated with higher proportion of major organ involvement, greater cumulative organ damage and increased morbidity and mortality [3–7].

The management of SLE necessitates prolonged use of corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, and immunosuppressive drugs which are essential to achieve low disease activity and minimize organ damage [4, 8]. The inherent immune dysfunction of SLE compounded by the immunosuppressive burden of therapy renders cSLE patients profoundly susceptible to a broad spectrum of infections [9, 10]. Infections are one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality of SLE, with opportunistic pathogens accounting for a disproportionate burden of fatal outcomes [11, 12].

Among the opportunistic infections reported in SLE patients, nocardiosis is a rare but potentially life-threatening bacterial infection caused by an aerobic, filamentous, gram-positive organism of the genus Nocardia. Given the indolent and often atypical clinical presentation of nocardiosis, diagnostic delay is common and can result in disseminated disease with significant morbidity. This underscores the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for Nocardia infection in any SLE patient. Although nocardiosis has been well-described in immunocompromised adult SLE [13], data pertaining to cSLE remain exceedingly limited. In this paper, we describe nocardiosis in cSLE, aiming to characterize the clinical features, microbiological findings, treatment strategies, and outcomes in cSLE patients.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University. Our center is a super-tertiary university hospital located in Bangkok, Thailand. Electronic medical records of all patients with cSLE (International Classification of Diseases-10 codes M32, M321, M329) who were followed in our hospital between January 2009 and August 2024 were reviewed. The protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (COA no. Si 717/2024), which granted waiver for the requirement for informed consent given the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the classification criteria for SLE by the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) [14], the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) [15], or the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/ACR classification criteria [16] before the age of 18 years with follow-up at our institution during the study period. Patients with incomplete medical records or those lost to follow-up were excluded.

Within the cSLE cohort, all cases of nocardiosis were identified through a systematic review of electronic medical records, microbiology laboratory databases, and inpatient discharge summaries using the International Classification of Diseases-10 codes for nocardiosis (A43, A430, A431, A438, A439). A confirmed case of nocardiosis was defined as the isolation of Nocardia species from any clinical specimen (e.g., blood, sputum, pleural fluid, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, tissue biopsy, cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, or wound swab) by culture on appropriate microbiological media, including blood agar and Löwenstein-Jensen medium. Identification was based on colony morphology, Gram staining, modified acid-fast staining, and biochemical testing, with molecular identification performed when available. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted by broth microdilution to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations for amikacin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, ciprofloxacin, imipenem, linezolid, moxifloxacin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX), where available. Cases of probable nocardiosis, defined by compatible histopathological findings in the absence of culture confirmation, were excluded from the cohort. Demographic and SLE-related data included sex, age at the time of SLE diagnosis, disease duration at the time of nocardiosis diagnosis, SLE manifestations at initial presentation (renal, hematologic, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous, and musculoskeletal involvement), and relevant immune profiles, complete blood count, and renal function parameters. Disease activity was assessed using the SLE disease activity index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) [17] at the time of diagnosis and at the time of diagnosis of nocardiosis. Cumulative organ damage was scored using the SLICC/ACR Damage Index (SDI) [18]. Attainment of Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) [19, 20] was also recorded. The reviewed immunosuppressive therapy data included all medications received prior to the diagnosis of nocardiosis. High-dose corticosteroids were defined as a patient receiving prednisolone > 0.4 mg/kg/day or > 30 mg/day for at least 4 weeks [21, 22].

Data related to nocardiosis included clinical presentation, duration from SLE onset, site(s) of infection (pulmonary, cutaneous, kidney, joint, blood, central nervous system), Nocardia species identified, antimicrobial susceptibility testing results when available, imaging findings, treatment regimen and duration, clinical response to therapy, length of hospital stay, required admission to intensive care, and outcome. Disseminated nocardiosis in our study was defined as the involvement of two or more non-contiguous anatomical sites consistent with previously proposed definitions [13].

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze all variables. Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage. To explore the baseline factors associated with the development of nocardiosis, Firth penalized logistic regression was used to reduce small-sample bias for binary outcome with rare events [23]. Variables for inclusion in the Firth regression model were selected based on clinical plausibility and prior literature rather than stepwise selection to avoid overfitting. Three variables were entered into the model: proliferative lupus nephritis, leukopenia (WBC < 4,000 cells/mm³), and active disease at SLE diagnosis (SLEDAI-2 K ≥ 4), with inactive disease (SLEDAI-2 K < 4) as the reference category. The results of Firth regression were reported as odds ratios with 95% penalized profile likelihood confidence intervals and were presented as a forest plot. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 2024.12.0 + 467.

Results

A total of 323 cSLE patients were included in the study. Seven patients (2.2%) who developed culture-confirmed nocardiosis were female (100%) with the mean age at nocardiosis diagnosis was 11.9 ± 3.1 years. The median (interquartile range) duration from cSLE diagnosis to nocardiosis diagnosis was 4.3 (2.1–13.4) months. Clinical characteristics of the overall cohort with comparison of patients with and without nocardiosis were reported in Table 1. Nocardiosis patients had lower mean age at SLE diagnosis (11.0 ± 3.6 year) than non-infected patients (11.9 ± 3.0 years). The mean baseline SLEDAI-2 K score was higher in the nocardiosis group (17.6 ± 5.7) compared with non-infected patients (13.4 ± 6.2). At the time of nocardiosis, the median (IQR) SLEDAI-2 K score was 12 (7–11). Notably, the median (IQR) SLEDAI-2 K at the most recent clinic visit prior to infection was similar at 12 (6–13). The SDI score was 0 in five patients (patients 1–5), and 1 from cataract in two patients (patients 6 and 7).

Among the clinical manifestations of SLE, hematologic manifestations were present in all seven nocardiosis patients (100%) compared to 75.9% of patients in the non-infected group. Neurological involvement was more frequent in the nocardiosis group (28.6% vs. 8.5%) while musculoskeletal manifestations were higher in non-infected group (14.3 vs. 28.8%). Mucocutaneous involvement was similarly distributed between groups (71.4% vs. 72.2%). Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) was absent in all nocardiosis patients at initial cSLE diagnosis, compared with 3.2% of the non-infected group. Only patient 1 developed MAS concurrently with nocardiosis approximately 4 weeks after the diagnosis of SLE. Nocardiosis patients had more renal involvement (85.7% vs. 62.7%), with proliferative lupus nephritis present in 71.4% of nocardiosis patients compared with 30.4% of non-infected group. Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis at initial SLE diagnosis was significantly more frequent in the nocardiosis group (28.6% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.014). Antiphospholipid antibody positivity and mean complement levels (C3 and C4) were similar between two groups. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Firth penalized logistic regression was performed to explore factors associated with nocardiosis development. Proliferative lupus nephritis was the only factor having exploratory association with nocardiosis, with statistical significance (OR 5.03, 95% CI 1.19–28.36; p = 0.028). Leukopenia showed a numerically elevated but non-significant association with nocardiosis (OR 2.18, 95% CI 0.45–10.43; p = 0.315). Similarly, active disease at SLE diagnosis (SLEDAI-2 K ≥ 4) was not associated with nocardiosis development when adjusted for the other covariates (Fig. 1).

The clinical characteristics, microbiological findings, treatment, and outcomes of nocardiosis patients are shown in Table 2. The predominant SLE manifestations at the time of or prior to nocardiosis were autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), present in six out of seven patients (85.7%), followed by mucocutaneous involvement in five (71.4%), lupus nephritis in five (71.4%), thrombocytopenia in two (28.6%), and neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) in two (28.6%). Among patients with lupus nephritis, class IV proliferative nephritis was the most common class (patients 3, 6, 7), followed by class III (patient 4) and one patient (patient 2) who at first had class II nephritis and subsequently progressed to a mixed class III/V pattern in three years. Patient 5 had refractory AIHA and had neither lupus nephritis nor NPSLE but presented with disseminated nocardiosis and organs involving the central nervous system (CNS), kidney, and respiratory tract.

All seven patients were receiving systemic corticosteroids at the time of diagnosis of nocardiosis with high-dose prednisolone combined with intravenous cyclophosphamide (IVCY) and hydroxychloroquine reflecting ongoing induction therapy for active major-organ SLE. The mean cumulative prednisolone-equivalent dose during the 4 weeks prior to nocardiosis diagnosis was 1362.4 ± 348.8 mg (1.7 ± 1.3 mg/kg/day). No nocardiosis patient achieved LLDAS. Four out of seven patients (57.1%) had also received recent pulse intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) (patients 1, 3, 4, 7), consistent with escalation of immunosuppression for flare in the period preceding infection. Patient 1 received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and dexamethasone in the context of MAS. Notably, none of the patients with cyclophosphamide induction were receiving TMP/SMX prophylaxis at the time of nocardiosis diagnosis.

The respiratory tract was the most common site of nocardiosis, manifesting as pneumonia, in five of the seven patients (71.4%). One patient (patient 5) had disseminated nocardiosis with simultaneous involvement of the CNS, respiratory tract and kidney abscess, with imaging shown in Fig. 2. One patient (patient 2) presented with nocardial skin infection/abscess.

Species identification was achieved in three of the seven patients (42.9%) using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Nocardia cyriacigeorgica was isolated from patient 3 (lung biopsy), a polymicrobial co-infection with both Nocardia farcinica and Nocardia Kroppenstedtii was identified in patient 4 (lung biopsy and pleural biopsy), and Nocardia farcinica was identified in patient 5 (blood culture). The remaining four patients had Nocardia spp. without species-level identification. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed in four of seven patients (57.1%) which all isolated demonstrated susceptibility to amikacin and linezolid. The Nocardia farcinica and Nocardia Kroppenstedtii coinfection in patient 4 was susceptible to a broad panel including amikacin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, ciprofloxacin, imipenem, linezolid, moxifloxacin and TMP/SMX (Table 1).

All patients received intravenous induction antibiotic therapy. Carbapenem-based induction was used in five of the seven patients detailed in Table 2. Patient 4 received intravenous ceftriaxone as induction and patient 2 received intravenous amoxicillin/clavulanate. The duration of induction treatment ranged from 7 to 30 days according to the clinical and serological response of the patient. TMP/SMX consolidation therapy was used in six out of seven patients (85.7%). The duration of oral consolidation therapy ranged from 6 to 12 months. Patient 4 received an extended sequential regimen of ciprofloxacin (5 months) followed by TMP/SMX (12 months), guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing results demonstrating susceptibility to fluoroquinolones. Patient 5 received a prolonged dual oral consolidation with amoxicillin/clavulanate (12 months) plus TMP/SMX (12 months), reflecting the severity of disseminated CNS/renal/pulmonary disease. Patient 3, whose N. cyriacigeorgica infection showed susceptibility to ceftriaxone, received ceftriaxone with netilmicin followed by oral cefixime for total 12 months as the patient developed pancytopenia.

The median (IQR) total length of stay was 40.5 (38.5–50) days. Four patients required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU): patient 4 (4 days ICU), patient 5 (1 day ICU), patient 6 (13 days ICU), and patient 7 (6 days ICU). The in-hospital mortality rate from the index nocardiosis episode was 0%, and the 12-month all-cause mortality rate was 14.3%. Mortality in one of the 7 patients occurred in patient 7, who survived the index hospitalization but was readmitted two months later and died from Corynebacterium septicemia complicated by supraventricular tachycardia and refractory septic shock; note that this episode of Corynebacterium septicemia was not a catheter-related bloodstream infection. This case had the highest immunosuppressive burden at the time of nocardiosis as detailed in Table 2, the SLEDAI-2 K of 17, and the SDI of 1 during the index nocardiosis admission.

Discussion

Our study identified 7 culture-confirmed cases of nocardiosis among 323 patients with cSLE in a single tertiary-care cohort. Nocardiosis occurred predominantly during the early induction phase of therapy. All affected patients were female, and most had AIHA, mucocutaneous manifestations, and proliferative lupus nephritis. Furthermore, proliferative lupus nephritis at cSLE diagnosis was associated with nocardiosis.

The prevalence of nocardiosis in our cohort was 2.2% similar to the 2.8% (6/215) prevalence reported in an adult SLE cohort in Hong Kong, the largest previously published series [13]. Case report/series of nocardiosis in adult SLE patients have been reported from various countries and but rarely in cSLE [13, 24–33]. Proliferative lupus nephritis represents the immunologically severe renal manifestation of SLE and requires intensive induction therapy, typically combining high-dose corticosteroids with intravenous cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate mofetil and/or biologics [2, 34]. The potential association with proliferative lupus nephritis at baseline in our cohort therefore likely functions as a composite proxy for both the severity of immune dysregulation in cSLE patients with class III/IV nephritis and the immunosuppressive treatment burden attributed by disease severity that may require more dosage and duration.

Systemic corticosteroids treatment has been reported as the dominant independent risk factor for nocardiosis in immunocompromised patients [35]. In our cohort, all seven nocardiosis patients were receiving corticosteroids to treat active SLE disease, and 57% (4/7) received recent pulse intravenous methylprednisolone for the treatment of proliferative lupus nephritis and RPGN in the period preceding infection. In patients with rheumatic diseases, high-dose corticosteroids and intensive immunosuppressive treatment including cyclophosphamide were reported as possible predisposing factors for nocardiosis [36], consistent with findings in our cohort.

Nocardia is an intracellular pathogen whose clearance is critically dependent on T-cell mediated immunity and macrophage activity [11]. There was a positive trend toward leukopenia for nocardiosis. Leukopenia and lymphopenia due to disease-mediated bone marrow suppression and the side effects of cyclophosphamide create the cellular immune deficit that underpins susceptibility. Active disease at SLE diagnosis (SLEDAI-2 K ≥ 4) was not independently associated with nocardiosis after covariate adjustment, although higher baseline SLEDAI-2 K scores in affected SLE patients may reflect impaired host defense from disease-related immune dysregulation and immunosuppressive therapy [11, 12]. None of the seven nocardiosis patients achieved LLDAS [19, 20] at the time of infection. This likely reflects the fact that the patients were receiving induction therapy, high-dose glucocorticoids, or had active disease, all of which precluded LLDAS by definition [19, 20]. Whether sustained disease control and reduced immunosuppressive burden are associated with a lower risk of opportunistic infection warrants further study.

Respiratory involvement manifesting as pneumonia predominated (71.4%), consistent with prior reports in SLE and rheumatic disease in adults [13, 36]. In rheumatic disease patients with nocardiosis, all patients presented with pulmonary disease and 60% had disseminated nocardiosis [36]. In our cohort, one case (patient 5) developed disseminated nocardiosis involving the CNS, renal, lungs, and blood which identified N.farcinica at 16 months after SLE diagnosis, highlighting that dissemination can occur beyond the initial phase of intensive immunosuppressive therapy. Similarly, disseminated N. farcinica infection was reported in an adult female SLE patient from India, involving the lungs, brain and subcutaneous tissue/lymphatics [29] and one female adult SLE from China, involving brain, heart, and blood [33]. Among patients with CNS nocardiosis, overall mortality has been reported at 22.8% with N.farcinica identified as the most common causative species and corticosteroids use as the associated risk factor [37]. The identification of N.farcinica in patients 4 and 5 is therefore clinically significant. Patient 4 developed NPSLE symptoms and N.farcinica may predispose to aggressive infection and worse outcomes, with species-specific mortality risk persisting in multivariable analyses [38, 39]. Despite the risk factor, patient 4 had a favorable outcome following 17 months of susceptibility-guided antimicrobial therapy. It is also noteworthy for clinicians that the initial presentation of disseminated nocardiosis may mimic the flare-up of SLE [26], as both diseases can have multiple organ involvement. Moreover, concomitant infections of nocardiosis and other pathogens should also be taken into account when present with mimicking features [40]. This underscores the importance of molecular speciation and antimicrobial susceptibility test-directed treatment in all immunocompromised patients with confirmed nocardiosis.

Treatment with carbapenem-based induction followed by TMP/SMX consolidation used in 85.7% of patients is consistent with previous reported and published outcomes in rheumatic disease cohorts [13, 35, 38]. A duration of 12 months of consolidation phase is recommended for immunocompromised patients with CNS involvement [41, 42] while the 6-month consolidation used in the remaining cases with no relapse documented at 12-month follow-up. None of the seven nocardiosis patient were receiving TMP/SMX prophylaxis at the time of diagnosis. To date, data concerning prophylaxis of nocardiosis specifically in cSLE remains scarce. In adults with SLE, TMP/SMX prophylaxis has been considered for prophylaxis of major infective episodes and opportunistic infections especially Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, particularly patients on high dose corticosteroids in combinations with immunosuppressive therapy [43–45]. Munthananuchat et al. reported that among SLE patients receiving low-level immunosuppressive therapy with prophylaxis of TMP/SMX, there were no occurrence of TMP/SMX opportunistic infections (Nocardia spp., Toxoplasma spp., Pneumocystis jirovecii) during the 12-month follow-up period; nevertheless, there was a high rate of adverse drug reactions observed [45]. Prior studies in transplant populations have shown that TMP/SMX prophylaxis is independently associated with a significantly decreased risk of nocardiosis [46, 47]. However, TMP/SMX does not confer complete protection and the mechanism by which its reduced nocardiosis infection risk remains incompletely understood, as breakthrough nocardiosis occurs in 12–40% of immunocompromised patients [35, 38, 48]. The risks of bone marrow suppression, nephrotoxicity, and drug interactions with azathioprine in patients with active lupus nephritis and cyclophosphamide-induced cytopenias require careful individual risk-benefit evaluation, and the optimal prophylactic dose, timing, and duration in cSLE remain to be prospectively defined. The potential role of TMP/SMX prophylaxis in SLE patients therefore warrants further investigation, particularly given the limited and inconsistent evidence [49]. Therefore, when considering TMP/SMX prophylaxis in cSLE, the risks and benefits of TMP/SMX in SLE should be taken into account.

From our study, the in-hospital mortality rate from the index nocardiosis episode was 0%, and the 12-month all-cause mortality rate was 14.3%. The reported one-year all-cause mortality from nocardiosis across immunocompetent and immunocompromised populations is 19-56.7%, with CNS involvement, solid-organ transplantation, and premature treatment discontinuation identified as poor prognostic factors [13, 36, 38, 50]. Our favorable infection-specific outcomes likely reflect early recognition, prompt carbapenem-based induction, and sustained consolidation. The 12-month all-cause mortality of 14.3% was attributable entirely to patient 7, who survived the index nocardiosis episode but died two months post-discharge from Corynebacterium septicemia with septic shock, suggesting profoundly impaired host defenses that predisposed to subsequent lethal secondary infection. This patient also carried the most multiple immunosuppressive drug therapy with cumulative burden in the cohort, suggesting that vulnerability to subsequent severe infections may persist despite completion of nocardiosis [51, 52]. From our study, the onset of nocardiosis early in the cSLE disease course could be attributed by the complex immune dysregulation, that the immunodeficiency inherent in the SLE disease itself may potentially be associated with increased risk of nocardiosis, in addition to the secondary immunodeficiency due to treatment by corticosteroids and immunosuppressive drugs.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. The small event count constrains statistical power, and true risk factors of moderate effect size may have been missed, suggesting that statistically significant results should be interpreted with caution to avoid over-conclusion. The regression model explored the factors associated with nocardiosis, of which the independent variables assessed were baseline clinical characteristics at initial cSLE diagnosis. Therefore, variables concerning treatment including corticosteroid exposure and immunosuppressive agents were not included in the regression model. The cumulative dose of corticosteroids, arguably the most directly modifiable predictor, was not available for formal modeling. These factors are biologically and clinically central risk factors for nocardiosis, hence the current model cannot determine whether proliferative lupus nephritis itself is associated with nocardiosis independently of treatment burden. Findings from the regression analysis therefore suggest an exploratory association rather than an independent predictor. Species-level identification was achieved in 42.9% of the cases. Despite these limitations, this study provides the first regression-based risk factor analysis for nocardiosis in a cSLE cohort with 12-month outcome data, and identifies proliferative lupus nephritis as a clinically relevant risk factor to be validated in multicenter registries.

Conclusions

Nocardiosis occurred in 2.2% of our cSLE patients, predominantly during early intensive immunosuppression, in females, with major organ involvement presenting as AIHA, mucocutaneous manifestations, and proliferative lupus nephritis. Interestingly, proliferative lupus nephritis at the time of SLE diagnosis was an exploratory associated factor, potentially reflecting both disease severity and treatment burden. Outcomes were favorable with timely diagnosis and appropriate therapy. Heightened clinical suspicion in high-risk patients, along with early microbiological confirmation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, is crucial to guide management. Further studies are warranted to validate the findings and inform prevention and treatment strategies.
