Single-cell transcriptome analysis defines novel molecular subtypes and reveals therapeutic implications of T/myeloid mixed-phenotype acute leukemia
Authors: Bin Huang, Wenjie Liu, Yuxin Du, Ping Liu, Zixing Lu, Shiyang Zhong, Xingfei Hu, Wanting Zhou, Yuzhu Shi, Runheng Huang, Xian Zhang, Jinning Shi, Chuanyang Lu, Chunling Wang, Liang Yu, Lingxiang Wu, Wei Wu, Peng Xia, Qian Sun, Liwei Zhu, Zhi Wang, Ruohan Zhang, Xinrui Lin, Sali Lv, Qianghu Wang, Sixuan Qian, Kening Li, Ming Hong

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundT/myeloid mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (T/My MPAL) is a malignant disease characterized by co-expression of lymphoid and myeloid features. The lack of molecular classification of T/My MPAL results in highly heterogeneity in treatment responses and clinical outcomes. Identifying molecular subtypes and developing subtype-specific treatment strategies are crucial for improving prognosis and enabling personalized therapies.MethodsWe constructed a single-cell transcriptomic landscape of T/My MPAL, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), T-cell acute lymphoid leukemia (T-ALL), and normal donors by analyzing nearly 275,000 cells. Malignant cells were identified using lineage-specific markers and healthy reference datasets. By comparing the whole transcriptomic profiles of T/My MPAL malignant cells with those of AML and T-ALL, we defined three distinct subpopulations and uncovered both intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity. Subpopulation-specific molecular markers were identified and validated using immunohistochemistry and independent datasets. These markers were further linked to clinical outcomes. Finally, potential subpopulation-specific therapeutic drugs were identified by correlating gene signatures with IC50 values.ResultsMalignant cells in T/My MPAL display distinct lineage characteristics and experience differentiation arrest at a more primitive stage compared to other leukemias. Biphenotypic and bilineal MPAL subtypes defined by flow cytometry exhibit similar transcriptomic profiles, indicating the traditional classification based on a limited set of lineage markers is insufficient. Instead, we define three subpopulations of malignant cells in T/My MPAL, including AML-like, T-ALL-like, and a unique subpopulation that shows distinct transcriptional characteristics neither similar to AML nor to ALL. Markers for each subpopulation are identified and further validated by independent datasets and immunohistochemistry. The unique subpopulation exhibits higher stemness and quiescence, with elevated HOPX expression. Notably, patients with higher levels of the unique subpopulation have significantly poorer prognoses. We further computationally screen potential drugs targeting each subpopulation and indicate that Venetoclax could effectively inhibit the unique MPAL subpopulation and help patient achieve complete remission.ConclusionsOur study provides new insights into the molecular heterogeneity and offers personalized diagnostic and therapeutic targets for T/My MPAL patients. Background

Mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) is a rare and diverse group of malignant diseases, accounting for 2%−5% of acute leukemia cases [1]. Despite significant advances in the treatment of acute leukemia over the past three decades, the prognosis for MPAL have not improved substantially. It is the lack of standardized treatment guidance within current diagnostic frameworks that drives clinical uncertainty and contributes to the substantial variability in reported 5-year survival rates, from 47–75% in children to 20–40% in adults [2–5]. Current treatment regimens for MPAL include acute myeloid leukemia (AML)-based therapy, acute lymphoid leukemia (ALL)-based therapy, or a combination of both, known as “hybrid” therapy [6–9]. However, these regimens have not significantly improved patient prognosis, primarily due to an insufficient understanding of the most effective therapeutic strategies for distinct patient subgroups. Therefore, addressing the underlying heterogeneity in MPAL cases and identifying subtypes that guide treatment decisions is crucial.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [4, 7, 10], MPAL is classified by immunophenotype into B/myeloid (B/My), T/myeloid (T/My), and T/B/myeloid (T/B/My) or T/B. Additionally, MPAL can be categorized into biphenotypic and bilineal subtypes. Biphenotypic blasts exhibit coexpression of lymphoid and myeloid markers within a single blast population, while bilineal blasts consist of two distinct subpopulations, each expressing either lymphoid or myeloid features [11, 12]. Previous studies showed no significant differences in mutation patterns between biphenotypic and bilineal leukemia subclones, suggesting similar genomic characteristics [13]. Moreover, Bachir reported minimal clinical differences between biphenotypic and bilineal patients [14]. These findings underscore that cellular-level classification alone is insufficient to reveal the underlying biological differences between the two subtypes or to guide treatment planning.

The cytogenetic classification identifies t(9;22)(q34;q22)/BCR-ABL1 and KMT2A-rearrangements (KMT2Ar) as markers for MPAL subtyping [12, 15], marking the beginning of precision medicine for MPAL. Advances in sequencing technologies have enabled the discovery of novel molecular subtyping methods at both genome and transcriptome levels [5, 16–20]. For example, WT1 mutations and ZNF384 rearrangements (ZNF384r) have been identified in T/My MPAL and B/My MPAL, respectively [5, 20]. In ZNF384r B/My MPAL, FLT3 overexpression suggests that FLT3 inhibitors could serve as potential treatments [21]. Similarly, BCL11B rearrangements in T/My MPAL indicate that targeted suppression of BCL11B may benefit these patients [16, 22, 23]. Transcriptome-based classification by Wang et al. identified eight molecular subgroups with distinct genomic and transcriptomic features [18]. However, the varying proportions of malignant and normal-like cells can obscure disease-specific transcriptional patterns and genomic events, complicating molecular subtyping. Bulk analyses lack the resolution to capture intra-tumoral heterogeneity, highlighting the need for single-cell transcriptome profiling to enable accurate subtyping for MPAL.

Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology offer new possibilities to identify leukemia malignant cells and comprehensively decode the intra-tumoral heterogeneity of MPAL. Granja et al. employed single-cell multiomic analyses to deconvolve features of MPAL malignant cells using healthy blood development as a reference, identifying shared disease-specific peak-to-gene linkages across MPAL patients [19]. Comparative studies with AML, ALL, and healthy controls have identified distinct transcriptional patterns unique to MPAL, providing valuable insights for diagnosis and treatment [24]. Additionally, recent research has highlighted a shared stem cell-like transcriptional profile in MPAL blasts, which is associated with poor prognosis [25]. However, these studies primarily focus on the common features of malignant cells within MPAL individuals, rather than investigating the variety among different cell subpopulations. Given the fact that subpopulations with different molecular features may cause variations in stages of differentiation arrest and drug sensitivity [26, 27], it is crucial to deconstruct the transcriptional heterogeneity of malignant MPAL cells to facilitate the accurate molecular subtyping and provide tailored treatment recommendations.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of nearly 275,000 single-cell transcriptome data from patients with AML, T-ALL, T/My MPAL, and normal donors. We identified three distinct subpopulations of MPAL malignant cells: AML-like T/My MPAL (aT/My MPAL), T-ALL-like T/My MPAL (tT/My MPAL), and unique T/My MPAL (uT/My MPAL) that exhibits higher stemness, quiescence, and elevated HOPX expression. Patients with a higher proportion of unique cell subpopulation have significantly worse prognoses. We identified specific molecular markers and potential therapeutic drugs for each subpopulation and further validated them in both internal and external clinical cohorts. These findings provide new insights into the molecular heterogeneity and offer personalized diagnostic and therapeutic targets for T/My MPAL patients.

Methods

Patients and cohorts

This study included 17 human bone marrow samples at our institution, comprising five T/My MPAL, six AML, three T-ALL, and three normal samples. The six diagnostic AML samples were sourced from a previously published study by our group [26]. Bone marrow mononuclear cells were isolated by density gradient centrifugation, and scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform. To validate subpopulation‐specific signatures, fourteen formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) bone marrow biopsy specimens were collected, including one AML, one T-ALL, and twelve T/My MPAL samples. To assess the depletion of HOPX-positive leukemia cells, three paired samples obtained before and after Venetoclax-based treatment were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC). All samples were collected at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between 2021 and 2025, following approval from the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Publicly available single-cell transcriptome datasets were integrated to enhance cohort diversity and statistical robustness, including 20 normal bone marrow samples [28] and 10 T/My MPAL samples from Granja et al. [19], Montefiori et al. [16], and Mumme et al. [24]. Bulk RNA-seq datasets were retrieved from the TARGET consortium [5], GSE113601 [20] and Wang et al. [18] for validation and survival analysis. Chromatin accessibility datasets (GSE184851 [29], GSE162949 [30]) and transcriptomic profiles of hematopoietic cells (E-MTAB-9067 [31]) were used to assess whether HOPX expression reflects hematopoietic progenitor identity and stem-like characteristics.

Different cohorts were used for specific analytical purposes. The in-house and public scRNA-seq datasets were used for discovery, subpopulation identification, and cross-cohort validation, whereas bulk RNA-seq datasets were used for deconvolution, drug response correlation, and survival analyses. The clinical and molecular characteristics of all samples are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Human bone marrow single-cell suspensions preparation

Fresh human bone marrow samples from leukemia patients and normal donors were subjected to density gradient centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C to selectively isolate mononuclear cells. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 5 ml of RPMI-1640 containing 5% FBS and 2-Mercaptoethanol, then filtered through a 40 µm nylon mesh (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove residual cell aggregates. After staining with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) to identify live cells, cell numbers were quantified using the Countess II Automated Cell Counter, whenever feasible.

Library construction for scRNA-seq

Overall, cell viability exceeded 90% across all samples. Subsequently, 10,000 cells from each sample were loaded onto a Chromium Single-Cell 3′ Chip Kit v2 and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the Chromium single-cell 3′ Reagent Kits (v2 and v3) from 10 × Genomics. Library construction was performed using the Single 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit v2 (PN-120237) and the Chromium i7 Multiplex Kit v2 (PN-120236).

Single-cell data processing

cDNA libraries were sequenced using the HiSeq Xten platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw BCL files were converted to FASTQ format using Illumina's bcl2fastq software. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the CellRanger (v3.0.2) STAR alignment algorithm, producing gene expression matrices. Quality control and single-cell analyses were performed using Seurat (v4.0.0) [32]. To ensure robust downstream analyses, we applied stringent quality control criteria to exclude low-quality or stressed cells. Specifically, cells were retained only if they had between 200 and 4000 detected genes, more than 1000 total UMIs, and less than 30% of UMIs derived from mitochondrial transcripts. Additionally, we removed genes corresponding to mitochondrial and ribosomal RNA prior to normalization to avoid bias from highly abundant housekeeping transcripts and technical noise.

Integration and annotation

Single-cell sequencing data were integrated and annotated using Seurat. Data normalization was initially performed with a logarithmic transformation, and variable features were identified through variance-stabilizing transformation. Integration was achieved using the ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ and ‘IntegrateData’ functions in Seurat, followed by further normalization with the ‘ScaleData’ function. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using principal component analysis (PCA), with significant components determined through the ‘JackStraw’ method. The top 30 principal components were selected for subsequent analysis. Data visualization was performed using the UMAP algorithm, and cell clusters were identified with the ‘FindNeighbors’ and ‘FindClusters’ functions at a resolution of 5. Cell type annotation was refined using the SingleR (v1.4.0) BlueprintEncodeData library [33], supplemented with established marker features to enhance classification accuracy. Markers included CD34 for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, CD19 for B cells, and CD3E for T cells.

Identification and validation of malignant cells

Using our previously established algorithm [26], malignant cells were identified in the integrated single-cell dataset. The prediction stabilized when cells from leukemia samples constituted at least 80% of a cluster (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). This threshold was determined by calculating the ratio of cells from normal donors to those from leukemia patients within each cluster. Cells from leukemia samples in clusters exceeding the 80% threshold were classified as leukemia-like cells, while those in other clusters were categorized as normal-like cells. To validate this classification, we applied infercnvpy (v0.4.5) with normal cells as the reference to assess CNV levels, computed enrichment scores for four LSC signatures (DeJONGE_LSC_TOP50_genes, EPPERT_CE_HSC_LSC.v2025.1.Hs, EPPERT_LSC_R.v2025.1.Hs) using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, and employed the Galen random forest model [34], trained on AML scRNA-seq data with matched mutation profiles, to predict AML leukemia-like cells.

Lineage characterization of T/My MPAL leukemia cells

To evaluate lineage bias in T/My MPAL malignant cells, a reference dataset was constructed from single-cell RNA-seq profiles of bone marrow cells from 23 normal donors. Cells annotated as HSCs, MPPs, or ambiguous populations were excluded. Remaining cells were grouped into three major hematopoietic lineages: lymphoid (B cells, T cells, CLPs, NK cells, and plasma cells), myeloid (CMPs, GMPs, granulocytes, and monocytes), and megakaryoerythroid (erythrocytes, megakaryocytes, and MEPs). The reference dataset was normalized, followed by variable feature selection using FindVariableFeatures() (method = "vst", nfeatures = 2000), scaling with ScaleData(), and dimensionality reduction with RunPCA(). Label transfer was performed using Seurat’s FindTransferAnchors() and TransferData() functions (dims = 1:30, reference.reduction = "pca") to project T/My MPAL malignant cells onto the reference space. For each query cell, prediction scores for each hematopoietic lineage were calculated and cells were assigned to the lineage with the highest prediction score. Cells with an absolute difference of less than 0.2 between predicted lymphoid and myeloid scores were classified as “both”, indicating a mixed lymphoid-myeloid transcriptional phenotype (Additional file 2: Fig. S5C).

T/My MPAL molecular subtyping

To construct a reference framework for identifying AML- or T-ALL-enriched transcriptional states, we integrated 74,918 leukemia-like cells from AML and T-ALL samples and performed unsupervised clustering using Seurat (v4.0.0). Clustering was performed at a resolution of 1.2, with parameter selection informed by Clustree (v0.4.3) [35], yielding 25 distinct clusters (Additional file 2: Fig. S6A). Cluster-level enrichment was evaluated using Cacoa (v0.4.0) [36], which accounts for inter-sample variability via isometric log-ratio transformation and linear discriminant analysis. Clusters were classified as AML-enriched, T-ALL-enriched, or non-biased based on adjusted p-values. T/My MPAL leukemia-like cells were then projected onto the AML and T-ALL reference using Seurat’s TransferData function. For each T/My MPAL cell, the closest matching reference cluster was assigned, and the proportion of cells aligning with each cluster was calculated. Low-abundance clusters (< 5% of T/My MPAL cells) were excluded from downstream analyses.

Pseudotime analysis

To reconstruct leukemia cell trajectories, we performed pseudotime analysis using Monocle2 (v2.18.0) [37] on 30,000 randomly sampled leukemia-like cells from AML, T-ALL, and T/My MPAL samples. Ordering genes were defined as the top 2,000 highly variable genes among all cells using the “vst” method in Seurat. Trajectory inference was performed using the DDRTree algorithm in an unsupervised manner. The root state was determined based on pseudotime scores and the enrichment of CD34 + leukemia cells, representing transcriptionally primitive, stem-like populations.

Bulk data validation

To evaluate the presence of T/My MPAL subpopulations at the bulk transcriptome level, we identified signature genes for the uT/My, aT/My, and tT/My MPAL subpopulations using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat (v4.0.0) with the following parameters: only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25, and pct.1—pct.2 > 0.1 (Additional file 1: Table S4). The top eight marker genes for each subpopulation were used for hierarchical clustering, which stratified patients into different sample groups. These groups were then utilized for survival comparisons.

For validation, we analyzed two bulk RNA-seq datasets: (1) adult T/My MPAL samples from GSE113601 [20], and (2) pediatric T/My MPAL samples from the TARGET initiative, as defined by Alexander et al. [5]. To further support these findings, we performed bulk deconvolution analysis using CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [38]. A reference signature matrix was constructed from single-cell transcriptomic profiles of the three subpopulations and applied to both bulk cohorts. The estimated subpopulation proportions were used for clustering and survival analysis.

Survival analysis was conducted using the survival (v3.2.7) and survminer (v0.4.9) R packages, with stratification based on transcriptional clusters, inferred uT/My MPAL fractions, or HOPX expression. External survival validation was performed using the independent dataset reported by Wang et al. [18].

Functional analysis of T/My MPAL subpopulations

To investigate the functional characteristics of the aT/My, tT/My, and uT/My MPAL subpopulations, we selected the top 30 genes from each subpopulation and performed functional enrichment analysis for the GO BP module using the clusterProfiler (v3.18.1) R package. Stemness scores were calculated using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat, with the GMT file DeJONGE_LSC_TOP50_genes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA function in clusterProfiler. GMT files used for GSEA included HP_ACUTE_LYMPHOBLASTIC_LEUKEMIA.v2022.1.Hs, HP_ACUTE_MYELOID_LEUKEMIA.v2022.1.Hs, GRAHAM_NORMAL_QUIESCENT_VS_NORMAL_DIVIDING_UP.v7.5.1, and Hallmark gene sets. ATAC-seq data (GSE184851; GSE162949) were used to assess chromatin accessibility across hematopoietic stages, processed with the ENCODE pipeline and visualized via PCA/UMAP and UCSC Genome Browser. For transcription factor (TF) activity, we employed two approaches. First, DoRothEA (v1.2.2) [39] regulons with high-confidence interactions (levels A-C) were used as input for VIPER (v1.24.0) [40], which infers TF activity from the expression of target genes rather than the TF itself. TF activity was calculated using the run_viper function with the "scale" method (Z-score standardization, enrichment by the aREA algorithm), and results were reported as normalized enrichment scores (NES) for cross-sample comparison. In parallel, pySCENIC (v0.11.0) [41] was applied to independently validate TF regulatory programs: co-expression modules were inferred from log-normalized data, motif enrichment was evaluated using curated human motif databases, and AUCell was used to score regulon activity at the single-cell level.

Identification of IHC markers in T/My MPAL subpopulations

Candidate IHC markers for each T/My MPAL subpopulation were identified using sc2marker (v1.0.3) [42] on the scRNA-seq dataset with parameters category = "IHC" and org = "human". Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function, excluding genes with min.pct2 > 40% to remove candidates with high background expression in non-target subpopulations. For candidate genes, sc2marker determined the optimal expression threshold (split.value) that maximized classification performance between the target and non-target subpopulations. Cells above the threshold were labeled as “target” and those below as “non-target,” generating true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). Marker performance was assessed by accuracy, calculated as (TP + TN)/(TP + FP + TN + FN), and only genes with sensitivity > 50% and accuracy > 60% were retained. Candidate markers were then ranked by log2 fold change from the DEG analysis, and the top-ranked marker for each subpopulation was selected for IHC validation. Full evaluation metrics, including confusion matrices, accuracy, marker directionality, and false-positive rates, are provided in Additional file 1: Table S5. In addition, the expression levels of CD7, S100A9, and HOPX were used to construct binary classifiers for each subpopulation (C24, TEC, and AEC) against all other cells. Model fitting was performed with the glmnet (v4.1.6) R package using family = "binomial" and alpha = 0. The regularization parameter (λ) was optimized by tenfold cross-validation (type.measure = "auc"), and the final models were refitted at λ.min. Model performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and quantified by the area under the curve (AUC) using the pROC (v1.18.0) package.

Immunohistochemical staining

Bone marrow trephine biopsy samples underwent formalin fixation and paraffin embedding. Sections were stained with anti-CD7 (Cell Signaling Technology, #94,135), anti-S100A9 (Cell Signaling Technology, #34,425) and anti-HOPX antibody (Merck, HPA030180). Antibody binding was visualized by incubation with secondary antibody and development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The whole section on each slide was imaged and analyzed with an Aperio CS2 Digital Pathology Slide Scanner (Leica Biosystems).

Drug sensitivity prediction and validation

To investigate subpopulation-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities, we integrated pharmacogenomic data from the GDSC1.0 and GDSC2.0 projects (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) [43]. The collected datasets included pre-treatment gene expression profiles and LN-transformed IC50 values of 51 leukemia cell lines, including 27 AML, 24 ALL cell lines (Additional file 1: Table S8). For each drug tested in ≥ 5 cell lines, we performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using the top 10 genes for aT/My, tT/My, and uT/My MPAL subpopulations derived from our single-cell dataset to calculate the signature scores for each cell line. Spearman correlations between ssGSEA scores and IC50 values were used to evaluate the drug sensitivity of each cell line. Drugs whose IC50 values showed significant negative correlations with the signature scores of specific subpopulations were prioritized as candidate therapies for targeting these MPAL subtypes (Additional file 1: Tables S8, S9). To validate these associations in patient samples, we incorporated single-cell transcriptomic data from five untreated T/My MPAL patients and two patients treated with Venetoclax. Following the same methodology, tumor cells were identified, subpopulation assignment was performed, and HOPX expression was assessed. Additionally, we analyzed paired IHC samples from three patients before and after Venetoclax treatment to assess whether HOPX positive cells were reduced, indicating their potential sensitivity to the drug.

Results

T/My MPAL malignant cells showed differentiation arrest at more primitive stage compared to AML and T-ALL

To investigate the heterogeneity of leukemia cells in T/My MPAL and develop tailored therapeutic strategies for distinct subpopulations, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis on samples from eight T/My MPAL patients, six AML patients, three T-ALL patients, and 23 normal donors using the 10X Genomics platform [16, 19, 28]. The discovery set results were validated using 89 bulk RNA samples from T/My MPAL and seven single-cell transcriptome datasets [18, 20, 24] (Fig. 1A). After quality control, 274,944 high-quality cells were retained, with per-patient cell counts detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1. Distinct positioning of cells from different sample types on the UMAP plot highlighted their transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 1B). We then performed unsupervised clustering of filtered cells at high resolution, identifying 103 transcriptional cell clusters (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Using SingleR [33] analysis and established markers, we categorized the cells into four main groups, including hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and megakaryocyte/erythroid cells, with detailed cell types presented in Fig. 1C and Additional file 2: Fig. S2. Each group exhibited distinct gene expression profiles consistent with known cell type-specific markers, further supporting the accuracy of the classification (Fig. 1D; Additional file 1: Table S2).

Next, we identified leukemia cells using our previous algorithm [26] by comparing the ratio of patient-derived cells to normal control-derived cells in each cluster (Fig. 1E; Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). A significant positive correlation was observed between the predicted percentage of leukemia-like cells and the percentage of clinical morphological blasts (Spearman’s rho = 0.51, p = 0.042; Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). Copy number variation (CNV)-based clustering clearly separated normal-derived and leukemia-derived cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S3C), with leukemia-like cells showing significantly higher CNV burden than normal-like cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S3D). Additionally, leukemia-like cells across all three leukemia subtypes enhanced activation of multiple LSC-associated transcriptional programs (Additional file 2: Fig. S3E). To further confirm malignant cell identification, we applied the Galen a random forest model [34], trained on integrated single-cell transcriptomic and mutational profiles from AML patients. In our AML dataset, the malignant fractions inferred by this model closely matched our annotations, supporting the robustness of our malignancy assignment (Additional file 2: Fig. S3F). Lineage analysis revealed that T-ALL cells expressed lymphoid markers (CD7, CD3E), AML cells expressed myeloid markers (MPO, CD33), and T/My MPAL cells expressed both (Fig. 1F). UMAP clustering showed clear spatial patterns, with AML cells positioned in the myeloid branch, T-ALL cells in the lymphoid branch, and T/My MPAL cells in the stem/progenitor branch (Additional file 2: Fig. S4A).

The proportion of HSPCs in leukemia patients was significantly higher compared to that in normal honors (Fig. 1G; Additional file 2: Fig. S4B). Among HSPCs, multipotent progenitor cells (MPPs) were more abundant in T/My MPAL (3.3%) than in AML (1.6%) or T-ALL (0.1%). Similarly, granulocyte/monocyte progenitor cells (GMPs) and common myeloid progenitor cells (CMPs) were most abundant in AML (72.6%), followed by T/My MPAL (42.9%) and T-ALL (5.4%). In contrast, common lymphoid progenitor cells (CLPs) were most enriched in T/My MPAL (32.4%), compared to T-ALL (20.3%) and AML (1.7%) (Fig. 1H). These findings suggested that malignant cell differentiation in T/My MPAL was arrested at a more primitive stage.

Biphenotypic and bilineal MPAL subtypes exhibit similar expression profiles at the whole transcriptomic level

Leukemia subtyping is clinically performed using flow cytometry to evaluate lineage-specific markers, including myeloid markers (e.g., MPO, CD33), T-lymphoid markers (e.g., CD7), and B-lymphoid markers (e.g., CD19) [11]. Based on these markers, MPAL is classified into T/My MPAL and B/My MPAL. T/My MPAL is further divided into biphenotypic MPAL, where tumor cells co-express markers of both lineages, and bilineal MPAL, where distinct cell populations express lineage-specific markers [11]. However, the limited availability of lineage-specific markers poses challenges for precise MPAL subtyping.

To investigate the transcriptional heterogeneity between biphenotypic and bilineal subtypes at the single-cell level, we analyzed eight T/My MPAL samples classified by flow cytometry (three biphenotypic, four bilineal, and one unclassified) [19]. Lineage assessment based on a defined panel of myeloid and lymphoid markers (Fig. 2A) showed that bilineal cases contained smaller fraction of malignant cells co-expressing both lineage programs compared with biphenotypic cases, consistent with their immunophenotypic classification.

Although these differences in lineage marker co-expression were apparent, global transcriptomic analysis of ~ 40,000 single cells revealed that leukemic cells from biphenotypic and bilineal cases were largely intermixed on the UMAP projection, without distinct segregation by immunophenotypic subtype (Fig. 2B). This indicated that marker panel-based classification did not capture key molecular differences, which may explain why this approach may fail to in predicting prognosis and guiding treatment outcomes in clinical practice [14]. Hierarchical clustering of whole-transcriptome data further supported the absence of transcriptional distinction, revealing highly similar gene expression profiles between the two subtypes (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A, B). Both biphenotypic and bilineal samples contained leukemia cells expressing exclusively myeloid markers, exclusively lymphoid markers, or markers of both lineages (Additional file 2: Fig. S5C). Dual-lineage malignant cells were evenly distributed on the UMAP plot across both subtypes, and their proportions were comparable (Fig. 2C, D). In brief, our single-cell transcriptome analysis demonstrated that classification based on a limited set of lineage-specific markers was insufficient to identify transcriptionally distinct subtypes, highlighting the need for comprehensive whole-transcriptome profiling for accurate subtyping.

Identification of three subpopulations in T/My MPAL malignant cells with distinct developmental trajectories and clinical outcomes

To explore the intra-tumoral heterogeneity of T/My MPAL, we integrated 74,918 leukemia-like cells from AML and T-ALL as a reference dataset (Fig. 3A, B). This analysis defined 25 distinct clusters (Additional file 2: Fig. S6), with UMAP visualization showing clear separation between AML-derived and T-ALL-derived leukemia cells. Of these, six clusters were classified as AML-enriched (AECs), 13 as T-ALL-enriched (TECs), and six clusters (representing 9.7% of total cells) were designated as non-biased, showing no significant overrepresentation in either group (see "Methods" section; Fig. 3C; Additional file 2: Fig. S7A). Importantly, these six non-biased clusters exhibited a high proportion of cells in the G1 phase and a very low proportion of MKI67 + cells, indicating that they did not arise from cell cycle effects (Additional file 2: Fig. S8). We then applied the label transfer algorithm to assess the transcriptional similarity between T/My MPAL leukemia cells and the clusters identified in Fig. 3C. Our analysis showed that 41.6% of T/My MPAL cells aligned with TECs, defined as T-ALL-like T/My MPAL (tT/My MPAL); 6.4% aligned with AECs, defined as AML-like T/My MPAL (aT/My MPAL); and 46.9% aligned with C24, defined as C24-like T/My MPAL. Four low-abundance clusters (C13-like, C14-like, C16-like, and C19-like), collectively comprising 5.1% of T/My MPAL cells, were excluded from further analysis due to insufficient representation. Notably, although C24 cells are present at very low frequencies (mean percentage: 0.37%) in AML and ALL samples and lack of statistical enrichment (adjusted p > 0.05), the C24-like cells were markedly enriched in T/My MPAL samples (mean percentage: 49.9%; Additional file 2: Fig. S7E; Additional file 1: Table S3). Therefore, we designated C24-like cells as unique T/My MPAL (uT/My MPAL) cells.

Next, we performed pseudotime analysis to investigate the trajectories of AML, T-ALL and T/My MPAL leukemia cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). Root states were defined as those enriched for CD34 + cells, representing transcriptionally primitive populations (Additional file 2: Fig. S9A-C). uT/My MPAL cells were mainly localized within root-associated states and displayed significantly lower pseudotime values than other subpopulations, except for the infrequent C14-like group (Additional file 2: Fig. S9D-F), indicating that they occupy the most transcriptionally primitive position within the leukemia hierarchy. In contrast, tT/My MPAL cells were positioned along the left branch downstream of T-ALL, whereas aT/My MPAL cells localized to the right branch downstream of AML (Fig. 3E, F). To further explore the transcriptional heterogeneity among T/My MPAL subpopulations, we identified differentially expressed genes among tT/My, uT/My, and aT/My MPAL leukemia cells (Fig. 3G). The results showed that tT/My MPAL leukemia cells had transcriptional patterns similar to T-ALL leukemia cells, and aT/My MPAL leukemia cells resembled AML leukemia cells. In contrast, uT/My MPAL leukemia cells displayed distinct transcriptional features.

Specifically, tT/My MPAL leukemia cells exhibited high expression of CD7 and IGLL1 (Additional file 1: Table S4). CD7, a marker of early T cell development, was present in almost all T-ALL cases, and CD34 + T-ALL cells relied on CD7 expression for self-renewal and differentiation [44]. IGLL1, activated by the super-enhancer regulatory gene FYB1 [45], promoted T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia progression. In contrast, aT/My MPAL leukemia cells showed high expression of S100A8 and S100A9, both associated with poor prognosis in AML [46]. uT/My MPAL leukemia cells exhibited elevated expression of HOPX and HSPB1. HOPX maintained stemness and quiescence in AML leukemia cells and was linked to poor prognosis [47]. HSPB1 encoded the chaperone protein Hsp27, which regulated cell development, differentiation, and resistance to apoptosis [48].

We observed variation in the proportions of leukemia cell subpopulations across T/My MPAL samples (Fig. 3H; Additional file 1: Table S3). In samples #2, #5, and #8, uT/My MPAL leukemia cells accounted for over 70%. Similarly, tT/My MPAL leukemia cells made up more than 70% of samples #4 and #6. Sample #3 showed comparable proportions of tT/My MPAL and uT/My MPAL cells, while samples #1 and #7 contained all three subpopulations, with aT/My MPAL cells comprising 24% of sample #7. These results highlighted the complexity of malignant cell lineage features in T/My MPAL and reveal substantial intra-sample and inter-sample heterogeneity.

To validate the presence of distinct subpopulations, we examined the expression of subpopulation-specific genes in external T/My MPAL samples. Two independent bulk transcriptome datasets were collected and analyzed by hierarchical clustering (Fig. 3I, J). The results showed that signatures exhibited specific high expression across samples, providing evidence for the existence of these three malignant subpopulations in the T/My MPAL samples. To further assess heterogeneity, we performed deconvolution analysis on the TARGET dataset using CIBERSORTx [38]. The estimated proportions of the three subpopulations varied among samples (Additional file 2: Fig. S10A). Notably, hierarchical clustering identified a subset of samples (Group 3) with higher uT/My MPAL fractions and a distinct transcriptomic profile (Additional file 2: Fig. S10B). A similar pattern was observed in the GSE113601 dataset, where subpopulation compositions were heterogeneous across samples and Group 1 showed enrichment for uT/My MPAL cells with a similarly unique transcriptional signature (Additional file 2: Fig. S10C, D). In the TARGET cohort, survival analysis suggested that patients in Group 3, or those with higher uT/My MPAL proportions, tended to have poorer outcomes (Additional file 2: Fig. S10E, F), indicating the potential clinical relevance of this subtyping. This adverse trend may be related to the distinct molecular features of uT/My MPAL cells, which are transcriptionally less similar to canonical AML or ALL blasts and may therefore exhibit reduced sensitivity to standard lineage-specific therapies.

Identification and validation of distinct markers for T/My MPAL subtypes

To support the clinical application of this molecular subtyping, we identified markers for each subpopulation based on scRNA-seq analysis to facilitate the detection using immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 4A; Additional file 1: Table S5). Based on gene expression levels, detection accuracy, positive detection rates, and fold-changes relative to other subpopulations, we selected HOPX to distinguish uT/My MPAL, CD7 for tT/My MPAL, and S100A9 for aT/My MPAL subpopulations. Threshold-based analyses showed that HOPX identified uT/My MPAL leukemia cells with 68.7% accuracy, CD7 identified tT/My MPAL cells with 73.7% accuracy, and S100A9 identified aT/My MPAL cells with 95.7% accuracy. The majority of cells in each subpopulation were positive for their respective markers, with HOPX in uT/My MPAL, CD7 in tT/My MPAL, and S100A9 in aT/My MPAL (Fig. 4B). Logistic regression models using these three markers achieved AUCs of 0.768 for uT/My MPAL, 0.784 for tT/My MPAL, and 0.971 for aT/My MPAL, demonstrating strong discriminatory performance (Fig. 4C).

To further validate the robustness of these markers, we performed IHC experiments on bone marrow samples from patients diagnosed with AML, T-ALL, and T/My MPAL (Fig. 4D, E; Additional file 1: Table S6). The results revealed dominant leukemia cell subpopulations within patients. In AML#7, there were more S100A9 + cells compared to CD7 + and HOPX + cells. Similarly, leukemia cells in aT/My MPAL#7 and AML#7 displayed intense S100A9 staining and weaker CD7 and HOPX staining. In T-ALL#4, CD7 + cells were more abundant than S100A9 + or HOPX + cells. Leukemia cells in tT/My MPAL#6 and T-ALL#4 also showed intense CD7 staining and lighter S100A9 and HOPX staining. In uT/My MPAL#5, HOPX staining was more intense compared to the other markers, aligning with single-cell transcriptional results. Although derived from a limited number of paired samples, the IHC-based proportions of CD7 +, HOPX +, and S100A9 + cells showed a consistent positive trend with both marker expression levels and the relative abundance of the corresponding subpopulations in scRNA data, with Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from 0.71 to 1 (Additional file 2: Fig. S11). These findings support the reliability of these subpopulation-specific markers for clinical subtyping of T/My MPAL.

Enhanced stemness and quiescence in uT/My MPAL malignant cells

To elucidate the biological functions of the three T/My MPAL subpopulations, we identified gene sets with progressively increased expression along the aT/My MPAL and tT/My MPAL branches of the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 5A, B). At the root of the trajectory, the uT/My MPAL subpopulation exhibited high expression of HOPX and CD34, with enrichment in pathways related to myeloid leukocyte differentiation, lymphoid proliferation, and leukocyte homeostasis. HOPX is known to maintain the quiescence and stemness of hematopoietic stem cells [47], while CD34 is a well-established marker of stem and progenitor cells [26, 49]. Given previous findings that quiescent leukemia stem cells (LSCs) contribute to chemotherapy resistance [26, 50], this suggested that the stemness and undifferentiated properties of uT/My MPAL malignant cells may allow them to evade conventional chemotherapy, potentially contributing to disease relapse. In contrast, S100A9 and S100A8 showed increased expression along the trajectory from uT/My MPAL to aT/My MPAL and subsequently to AML, with enrichment in pathways related to neutrophil degranulation, antigen processing and presentation, and neutrophil activation. Similarly, CD7 and DNTT exhibited increased expression along the trajectory from uT/My MPAL to tT/My MPAL and then to T-ALL, with enrichment in pathways involved in lymphocyte differentiation, leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, and positive regulation of lymphocyte activation. Importantly, these observations were further validated by repeating the pseudotime and pathway enrichment analyses using all significantly upregulated genes, which reproduced the same expression modules and lineage-biased programs, thereby supporting the robustness of our conclusions (Additional file 2: Fig. S12A, B).

As the root of the trajectory, the uT/My MPAL subpopulation contained a higher proportion of CD34 + cells (Fig. 5C), with 46.9% of cells expressing CD34 compared to 34.8% in tT/My MPAL and 7.4% in aT/My MPAL. Consistently, the uT/My MPAL subpopulation displayed a higher stemness score (Fig. 5D). The uT/My MPAL signature gene Hopx has been identified as a regulator of primitive hematopoiesis [51]. In our single-cell dataset, HOPX expression was highest in HSCs and MPPs (Fig. 5E). A similar pattern was observed in an external dataset of flow-sorted mouse bone marrow hematopoietic populations, where Hopx was also predominantly expressed in MPPs (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, analysis of publicly available ATAC-seq datasets of flow-sorted mice hematopoietic populations revealed that high chromatin accessibility at the Hopx promoter was restricted to HSCs and MPPs (Fig. 5G). Collectively, these findings identified HOPX as a key regulator required for maintaining stem cell properties.

Next, we compared the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) results of these subpopulations (Fig. 5H). The uT/My MPAL subpopulation exhibited stronger quiescence, potentially contributing to the poor prognosis. In contrast, the aT/My MPAL subpopulation displayed features typical of AML, while the tT/My MPAL subpopulation showed characteristics of T-ALL. These distinct gene expression profiles and pathway enrichments highlighted the intra-sample heterogeneity within T/My MPAL leukemia cells, suggesting that these three subpopulations may respond differently to treatments. These findings underscored the need for subtype-specific therapeutic strategies to improve outcomes for T/My MPAL patients.

To further characterize the uT/My MPAL subpopulation, hallmark pathway enrichment analysis revealed strong activation of stemness-associated signaling, notably the WNT pathway [52, 53], alongside downregulation of cell cycle-related pathways, including the E2F and G2M checkpoints [54] (Fig. 5I). Transcription factor activity analysis showed increased activity of E2F5, associated with G0/G1 accumulation [55], and GATA2, a regulator of hematopoietic stemness [56, 57], whereas cell cycle-promoting factors including E2F1, E2F3, and E2F4 were suppressed [54]. To validate these observations, we further examined TF activity using an independent regulon-based approach. This analysis confirmed strong activation of stemness- and quiescence-associated TFs (TAL1 [58], MEIS1 [59]) and reduced activity of proliferation-related TFs (E2F2, SP1 [60]) in uT/My MPAL cells (Fig. 5J; Fig. 12C-D; Additional file 1: Table S7). Together, these complementary analyses consistently indicate that uT/My MPAL cells adopt a quiescent, stem-like transcriptional program, potentially conferring resistance to therapies that primarily target proliferative leukemia cells.

Additionally, we analyzed RNA-seq data from hematopoietic-specific Hopx knockout mice aged 18 months, a model of aged Hopx-deficient hematopoiesis. Phenotypic analysis revealed characteristics of stem cell exhaustion, including increased myeloid differentiation, reduced progenitor and lymphoid populations, and evidence of extramedullary hematopoiesis [47]. ssGSEA demonstrated a significant reduction in the uT/My MPAL subpopulation signature score and Hopx expression in Hopx −/− long-term HSCs (Fig. 5K-L), indicating compromised stemness and quiescence. Consistent with this global shift, transcriptomic profiling showed downregulation of stemness-associated genes (Cd34, Gata2, Tal1, Meis1) [57–59] and quiescence-maintaining genes (Foxo1, Cxcr4, Cxcl12) [61, 62], together with increased expression of the proliferation marker Mki67 (Fig. 5M-N). Collectively, these data support a key role for HOPX in maintaining stem-like and quiescent properties in hematopoietic cells.

In summary, the uT/My MPAL subpopulation exhibited enhanced stemness and quiescence, with HOPX identified as a key regulator of these properties. High HOPX expression was associated with poor prognosis in both the pediatric TARGET cohort and the adult cohort from Wang et al., and this association was robust across different cutoff strategies (Fig. 5O, P; Additional file 2: Fig. S12E, F), emphasizing the need for subtype-specific therapeutic strategies.

Venetoclax shows therapeutic efficacy for patients enriched by the uT/My MPAL subpopulation

To identify potential drugs for targeting the three T/My MPAL subpopulations, we analyzed the correlation between subpopulation-specific signature scores and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of various drugs in acute leukemia cell lines (Fig. 6A; Additional file 1: Table S8, S9). This analysis revealed specific vulnerabilities in each subpopulation, providing a basis for tailored therapies. Venetoclax emerged as the most effective drug for uT/My MPAL leukemia cells, attributed to its inhibition of the BCL-2 protein, which is essential for the survival of leukemia cells [63]. For tT/My MPAL leukemia cells, nelarabine, a commonly used treatment for T-ALL, was identified as the optimal option. Nelarabine, an antimetabolite prodrug, is an approved monotherapy for relapsed and refractory T-ALL and lymphoma in both children and adults [64, 65]. Similarly, 5-azacytidine was identified as the most suitable drug for aT/My MPAL leukemia cells [66, 67]. This traditional AML therapy is a viable option for patients with relapsed or refractory AML following intensive chemotherapy.

Notably, uT/My MPAL signature scores showed a strong inverse correlation with Venetoclax IC50 values in acute leukemia cell lines. HOPX expression, a defining gene of the uT/My MPAL subpopulation, also inversely correlated with IC50, suggesting that patients with high HOPX expression may be more responsive to Venetoclax (Fig. 6B). Previous studies have demonstrated that Venetoclax improved clinical outcomes in AML patients by targeting cells with high oxidative phosphorylation activity [68]. Primitive AML cells and leukemia stem cells (LSCs), which are critical for AML persistence and relapse, rely on BCL2 and oxidative phosphorylation for survival [63, 69]. Consistent with this, uT/My MPAL cells exhibited higher expression of oxidative phosphorylation and BCL2 target genes compared with tT/My MPAL and aT/My MPAL cells, potentially explaining their heightened sensitivity to Venetoclax (Fig. 6C, D).

We analyzed single-cell data from five T/My MPAL patients who received ALL- or AML-based induction therapy without Venetoclax, as reported by Mumme et al. [24]. Patients with higher baseline proportions of HOPX + leukemia cells tended to relapse following induction therapy (Fig. 7A). For example, patient #17 (72% HOPX +) was MRD + after induction and subsequently relapsed, whereas patient #16 (2% HOPX +) achieved MRD negativity and remained relapse-free. Patients with intermediate fractions showed more variable outcomes. Patient #21 (6%) was refractory to induction and later relapsed, and patient #18 (5%) was also refractory at induction but ultimately achieved durable remission without relapse. In addition, patient #20, who relapsed after combined AML and ALL therapies, showed 59% HOPX + cells at relapse. Consistent with these observations, bulk RNA-seq data from Wang et al.’s T/My MPAL cohort revealed a trend toward increased relapse risk in patients with elevated baseline HOPX expression (Fig. 7C). Together, these findings suggest that T/My MPAL patients with elevated HOPX expression may respond poorly to standard ALL- or AML-based induction regimens in the absence of Venetoclax.

To further clarify the cytotoxic effect of Venetoclax on the uT/My MPAL subpopulation, we treated five T/My MPAL patients with a combined Venetoclax-based therapy regimen (Additional file 1: Table S10). Among them, T/My MPAL#9 and T/My MPAL#10, with initial HOPX + cell proportions of 2% and 95%, respectively, both achieved complete remission with no relapse after Venetoclax treatment (Fig. 7B). Additionally, three other patients (T/My MPAL#19, #13, #7), who showed a marked decrease in HOPX + cell proportions compared to baseline, also achieved complete remission (Fig. 6E). The treatment resulted in a 100% MRD-negative rate, supporting Venetoclax's effectiveness in eliminating uT/My MPAL leukemia cells. Furthermore, building on these findings, our recent retrospective clinical study involving 13 patients with acute leukemia of ambiguous lineage (ALAL) demonstrated a 100% remission rate after one Venetoclax-based therapy cycle [70], suggesting broader applicability of Venetoclax across MPAL subtypes. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic characterization of T/My MPAL, a rare and clinically challenging leukemia, and uncovers pronounced molecular heterogeneity. We delineated three malignant subpopulations: AML-like, T-ALL-like, and a distinct HOPX + population characterized by enhanced stemness and quiescence. Enrichment of this transcriptionally primitive HOPX + subpopulation was associated with adverse clinical outcomes, underscoring its prognostic relevance. Integrated single-cell and clinical analyses further indicated that HOPX + cells may be less responsive to conventional ALL- or AML-based induction therapies, yet appear selectively vulnerable to Venetoclax. Together, these findings refine the biological understanding of T/My MPAL and provide a rationale for exploring Venetoclax-based therapeutic strategies in this rare leukemia.

The clonal origin and mechanisms underlying the mixed-phenotype presentation of T/My MPAL have been debated since the 1980 s [71–73]. One theory, known as “lineage infidelity,” suggests that subclonal genomic variation drives clonal evolution during disease progression in ALL or AML [20, 74]. Alternatively, Greaves’ hypothesis of “lineage promiscuity” proposes that leukemic transformation occurs at the stage of early hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSC/HSPC) with multi-lineage potential, allowing for the simultaneous expression of myeloid and lymphoid lineage markers [75, 76]. Our study indicated that malignant cell differentiation in T/My MPAL was arrested at a more primitive stage compared to AML and T-ALL. This suggested that the lineage diversification in MPAL may result from alterations in immature hematopoietic progenitor cells. This primitive differentiation arrest also contributes to the poor prognosis of MPAL.

T/My MPAL was previously classified into biphenotypic and bilineal phenotypes based on immunophenotype. Kotrova divided leukemia cells into biphenotypic and bilineal cell subpopulations and found no significant differences in mutation patterns [13]. Similarly, Bachir reported no significant differences in clinical outcomes between biphenotypic and bilineal patients [11]. These findings suggest that cellular-level classification alone is insufficient to reveal underlying biological differences. Our results aligned with these observations, showing no major differences between these subtypes at the single-cell transcriptomic level, consistent with clinical outcomes. In contrast, Takahashi et al. categorized MPAL into AML-type and ALL-type based on genome-wide methylation profiles, supporting lineage-matched therapy [20]. Wang et al. identified eight molecular subgroups through bulk transcriptome analysis, highlighting the heterogeneity among MPAL samples, with some resembling AML and others resembling T-ALL [18]. However, bulk-level studies may overlook intra-tumoral heterogeneity and are influenced by non-tumor cells, potentially obscuring the distinct intra-sample features of T/My MPAL patients. Importantly, we found that immunophenotype-based lineage composition did not align with transcriptionally defined AML-like or T-ALL-like subpopulations (Additional file 2: 8C-E). Patients with a higher fraction of myeloid lineage cells did not necessarily harbor more AML-like leukemia cells. This discrepancy may stem from the frequent presence of myeloid transcriptional programs in early T-cell precursor ALL (ETP-ALL) [77], as well as TEC clusters that represent malignant rather than conventional lymphoid cells. These findings highlight the limitations of lineage-based classification and emphasize the need for integrative transcriptomic approaches to more accurately define malignant programs in T/My MPAL.

Using single-cell transcriptomics, we identified three malignant subpopulations of T/My MPAL: AML-like, T-ALL-like, and a unique subpopulation enriched for HOPX + and CD34 + cells. Patients with higher fractions of this uT/My MPAL subpopulation had a poorer prognosis. These cells were marked by strong stemness and quiescence signatures. HOPX, the smallest homeodomain protein, is a recognized stem cell marker in multiple tumors and a regulator of hematopoietic stem cell quiescence via the CXCL12 pathway [47]. Evidence from developmental hematopoiesis further highlights its primitive role: during embryogenesis, Hopx marks hemogenic mesoderm-derived endothelial cells (H-ECs), precursors of definitive hematopoietic cells [51]. In bone marrow, Hopx shows high promoter accessibility in HSCs and MPPs, with peak transcript levels at the MPP stage, consistent with normal donor single-cell data. Functional studies in hematopoietic-specific Hopx knockout mice demonstrated stem cell exhaustion, reduced progenitor output, myeloid skewing, and increased proliferation, accompanied by downregulation of stemness/quiescence programs and upregulation of Mki67 [47]. Consistent with these observations, uT/My MPAL cells in our dataset showed elevated stemness and quiescence scores, suggesting that HOPX not only marks a primitive leukemic state but may also sustain therapy-resistant properties. Supporting this, both single-cell (n = 4) and bulk RNA-seq datasets (n = 40) indicated that higher HOPX + proportions at diagnosis were associated with increased relapse risk, and the only available relapse sample showed a high HOPX + fraction. Given the rarity of T/My MPAL and the limited availability of paired diagnosis-relapse samples, these findings remain preliminary and require further validation. In summary, the enhanced stemness and primitive nature of HOPX + uT/My MPAL cells likely contribute to their poor clinical response, underscoring the need for subtype-specific therapeutic strategies.

Building on this rationale, we next sought to identify clinically applicable markers for distinguishing subpopulation. Using scRNA-seq data and sc2marker analysis, we identified S100A9 for aT/My MPAL cells, CD7 for tT/My MPAL cells, and HOPX for uT/My MPAL cells. The biological plausibility of these markers was supported by lineage context: S100A9, highly expressed in AML cells, regulates myeloid differentiation and has therapeutic relevance in myelomonocytic AMLs [46]. Similarly, genes such as CD7 and DNTT increased expression during the trajectory process from uT/My MPAL to tT/My MPAL, and subsequently to T-ALL subpopulations. The uT/My MPAL subpopulation, as the root of trajectory, exhibited more HOPX + cells compared to the other subpopulations. These markers were validated through independent public datasets and IHC experiments on patient bone marrow samples, demonstrating high specificity and sensitivity in distinguishing the three T/My MPAL subpopulations. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.

However, defining subtypes is only the first step-identifying effective, targeted treatments for each remains a critical challenge. Previous research on T/My MPAL treatment has focused on comparing the efficacy among AML-like, ALL-like, or “hybrid” therapy regimens [78, 79]. However, the pronounced intra-tumoral and inter-tumoral heterogeneity in T/My MPAL has resulted in inconsistent patient outcomes and widely varying overall survival rates, with no consensus on the optimal approach. We found that patients with AML-like subtypes are more likely to benefit from AML-specific therapies, while those with T-ALL-like subtypes may respond better to T-ALL-targeted treatments. Notably, we identified the uT/My MPAL subtype, which is strongly associated with poor prognosis and appears less responsive to traditional AML- or ALL-targeted therapies. This finding highlights the critical need for subtype-specific treatment strategies to improve clinical outcomes in T/My MPAL patients.

Against this backdrop, we performed drug-sensitivity prediction analyses to search for agents that could effectively target uT/My MPAL. Through correlation analysis between subpopulation-specific gene signature scores and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for various drugs on acute leukemia cell lines, we found Venetoclax to be the most potentially effective drug against uT/My MPAL leukemia cells. Venetoclax, a BCL-2 inhibitor, significantly improves the prognosis of AML and ALL patients by selectively targeting the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 [68, 80, 81]. Our results indicated higher levels of oxidative phosphorylation and increased BCL2 activity in uT/My MPAL malignant cells, possibly explaining the efficacy of Venetoclax in this subtype. Most functionally characterized LSCs exhibit relatively low levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS-low LSCs abnormally overexpress BCL-2, and BCL-2 inhibitors can reduce oxidative phosphorylation and selectively eliminate quiescent LSCs [82]. In our study, we also found that uT/My MPAL subpopulation had a higher LSC score. Additionally, Venetoclax’s effectiveness likely stems from inhibition of BCL-2, which is crucial for the survival of certain leukemia cells. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the BCL-2 inhibitor Venetoclax significantly improves clinical outcomes in AML patients, with response rates ranging from 34 to 90% [63]. Additionally, primitive AML is highly sensitive to Venetoclax, while monocytic AML is more resistant and relies on MCL1 for survival due to distinct transcriptomic features. Therefore, targeting AML subclones at different disease stages is crucial for overcoming drug resistance[69]. The primitive stage is characterized by higher oxidative phosphorylation activity, making these cells more dependent on BCL-2 for survival. uT/My MPAL leukemia cells are at a more primitive stage. These findings suggest that Venetoclax was effective for patients with the uT/My MPAL subtype.

Several case reports have reported the effectiveness of combined Venetoclax regimens in MPAL, in line with our drug-sensitivity predictions [83, 84]. uT/My MPAL signature scores and HOPX expression both showed robust negative correlations with Venetoclax IC50 values, consistent with this subtype’s elevated oxidative phosphorylation and BCL2 target gene expression, features linked to Venetoclax sensitivity in AML and leukemia stem cells. Clinically, in the Mumme et al. cohort [24], patients with high baseline HOPX + blasts (6–72%) tended to remain MRD + or relapsed after standard AML/ALL therapy, whereas those with < 5% remained relapse-free. Wang et al.’s cohort showed a similar trend. In our Venetoclax-treated series, all five T/My MPAL patients achieved MRD-negative remission without relapse. Extending these observations, in our broader cohort of 13 MPAL/ALAL patients [70], one-cycle Venetoclax-based therapy yielded 100% remission. Although these findings require validation in larger prospective cohorts, they collectively highlight Venetoclax as a promising therapeutic option for HOPX-high uT/My MPAL, with potential applicability to other T/My MPAL subtypes. Importantly, one patient (Pt #9) with a low HOPX + fraction died from post-transplant complications rather than treatment resistance, underscoring the difficulty of interpreting outcomes in small cohorts where transplant-related mortality may confound assessments of drug efficacy. Beyond Venetoclax, our drug-sensitivity predictions also nominated Nelarabine and 5-azacytidine as candidate selective agents for tT/My and aT/My MPAL, respectively, pointing toward opportunities for subtype-tailored therapies.

By integrating single-cell transcriptomics with functional and clinical data, we defined three transcriptionally and clinically distinct T/My MPAL subpopulations. Among them, the uT/My MPAL subpopulation represented a primitive, stem-like state associated with poor outcomes, for which Venetoclax emerged as a rational targeted option. These findings provide a molecular framework for subtype-specific therapeutic strategies in MPAL and emphasize the potential of precision medicine in rare leukemias.

Conclusions

This study unveiled the molecular heterogeneity of T/My MPAL, identifying three distinct malignant subpopulations: AML-like, T-ALL-like, and a unique subtype characterized by elevated HOPX expression, which was associated with poor prognosis. These findings emphasize the limitations of traditional classification methods and highlight the importance of whole transcriptome-based subtyping. Moreover, by demonstrating the therapeutic potential of Venetoclax for the unique HOPX-high subpopulation, the study lays a foundation for the development of personalized treatment strategies that aim to improve outcomes for T/My MPAL patients.
