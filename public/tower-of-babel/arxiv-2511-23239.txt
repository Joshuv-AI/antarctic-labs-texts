Towards Understanding Transformers in Learning Random Walks
Wei Shi, Yuan Cao

Abstract
Transformers have proven highly effective across various applications, especially in handling sequential data such as natural languages and time series. However, transformer models often lack clear interpretability, and the success of transformers has not been well understood in theory. In this paper, we study the capability and interpretability of transformers in learning a family of classic statistical models, namely random walks on circles. We theoretically demonstrate that, after training with gradient descent, a one-layer transformer model can achieve optimal accuracy in predicting random walks. Importantly, our analysis reveals that the trained model is interpretable: the trained softmax attention serves as a token selector, focusing on the direct parent state; subsequently, the value matrix executes a one-step probability transition to predict the location of the next state based on this parent state. We also show that certain edge cases not covered by our theory are indeed failure cases, demonstrating that our theoretical conditions are tight. By investigating these success and failure cases, it is revealed that gradient descent with small initialization may fail or struggle to converge to a good solution in certain simple tasks even beyond random walks. Experiments are conducted to support our theoretical findings.

Introduction

In recent years, transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
have revolutionized many fields such as natural language processing
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Radford et al. 2019
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, computer vision
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
;
Rao et al. 2021
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Janner et al. 2021
;
Jumper et al. 2021
)
, and have rapidly emerged as a key component in state-of-the-art deep learning models due to their ability to capture complex dependencies in data. While transformers exhibit remarkable practical performance, the underlying mechanisms of transformers are still not well understood due to their complex architecture.

In order to theoretically understand transformers, a number of recent works have investigated their capability in learning from sequential data that follows certain classic statistical models.
Specifically,
Cao et al. 2025
;
Nichani et al. 2024
studied sequential data with an underlying causal graph, and theoretically showed how transformers can encode the causal structure with the self-attention mechanism for in-context learning.
Chen et al. 2024
;
Edelman et al. 2024
considered the task of in-context learning of Markov chains, and investigated how two-layer transformers can make predictions for Markov chains according to the context.
Ildiz et al. 2024
considered Markov chain forecasting tasks and reveals a connection between a context-conditioned Markov chain and the self-attention mechanism.
Makkuva et al. 2025
characterized the loss landscape of a one-layer transformer and demonstrated the existence of global minima and bad local minima in learning Markovian data with vocabularies of size two. Although these works provided valuable insights, there remain many open questions that require further exploration. Notably, the Markov chain data studied in
Makkuva et al. 2025
can be essentially understood as a random walk over a space of only two states. Therefore, given the results in
Makkuva et al. 2025
, a natural question is when and how transformers can learn more general random walks over a larger state space.

In this paper, we consider a classic statistical model for random sequences, namely random walks on circles, and study the capability of one-layer transformers to learn from such data and make predictions. We consider a general setting that allows a large number of nodes (possible locations of the walker) on the circle, which is an extension to the two-state setting studied in
Makkuva et al. 2025
. We also consider the general setting where a walker moves on the circle clockwisely with probability
p
, and counter-clockwisely with probability
1-p
, and we build theories that cover the full range of
p\in[0,1]
. For such a classic, yet relatively general class of random sequences, our goal is to theoretically study the performance of a one-layer transformer trained by gradient descent in predicting the next location, and reveal the interpretability of the obtained transformer model.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We theoretically demonstrate that a one-layer transformer can be trained to optimally predict the next location of a random walk with
p\in(0,1)
. Despite non-convexity, we prove that the trained model converges to the optimal prediction function with a rate
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
, where
T
is the iteration number of gradient descent. Furthermore, we show that the trained transformer achieves optimal prediction accuracy
\max\{p,1-p\}
after a constant number of iterations.

•

Our analysis reveals that the trained transformer model is interpretable, as we precisely delineate the role of each component in the model. First, the trained softmax attention can select the “direct parent” token by assigning it a near-one score. Second, the trained value matrix serves as a one-step transition model that recovers the ground-truth probability transition matrix, which is applied to the “direct parent” token to make an optimal prediction.

•

We also identify failure cases when
p=0\text{ or }1
. In these cases, we show that starting from zero initialization, the training of the one-layer transformer model with any loss function and any learning rate will always fail, resulting in a transformer model whose performance is no better than a random guess. This negative result is complementary to our positive guarantees for learning random walks with
p\in(0,1)
, and they together give a comprehensive characterization of the capability of transformers in learning random walks with
p\in[0,1]
.

•

We provide intuitive explanations that the failure cases with
p=0\text{ or }1
are optimization failures caused by zero initialization. Notably, similar optimization failures may also happen beyond the cases of random walks, as we can construct simple question answering tasks that also suffer from similar issues in optimization. We also empirically demonstrate that although training may still take longer, these failure cases can be resolved to a certain extent with random initialization.

2
Problem Setup

In this section, we present our problem formulations, including the random walk prediction task, the one-layer transformer model, and the training algorithm.

We study random walks on circles. Specifically, consider
K
nodes (possible locations) that are arranged on a circle so that each node has two neighbors. Without loss of generality, we suppose that the nodes are assigned with
node IDs
1,2,\ldots,K
in a clockwise manner.
A “walk” on the circle refers to the process where a “walker” moves step-by-step among the nodes of the circle. We suppose that, starting from a random initial location, at each step, the walker moves either clockwise with probability
p
or counterclockwise with probability
1-p
, to a neighboring node of its current position, where
p\in(0,1)
is a fixed probability. In this way, a random walk of length
N
generates a sequence of “states”
s_{1},\ldots s_{N}
, where
s_{i}\in[K]
denotes the location (node ID) of the walker at the
i
-th step. We aim to address the problem of predicting the walker’s next location
s_{N}
based on the historical locations
s_{1},\ldots s_{N-1}
.

To better formulate this random walk prediction task, we map
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N-1}
to embeddings
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Our goal is then to train a model to predict the target
y=s_{N}
based on
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}
. In the following, we give a detailed definition and discuss some basic properties.

Random walk on circles.
Suppose that there are
K
nodes on a circle and the transition probability is
p
.
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1},y
are generated as follows:

1.

Draw
s_{1}\sim\text{Unif}([K])
.

2.

For
i=2,\ldots,N
, sample either
s_{i}=\langle s_{i-1}+1\rangle_{K}
with probability
p
or
s_{i}=\langle s_{i-1}-1\rangle_{K}
with probability
1-p
.

3.

Set
\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{e}_{s_{i}}
,
i\in[N-1]
, and
y=s_{N}
.

Here,
\langle s\rangle_{K}
is defined as the integer satisfying
\langle s\rangle_{K}\in[K]
and
\langle s\rangle_{K}\equiv s\pmod{K}
.
It is clear that the sequence
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1},\bm{e}_{y}
form a Markov chain, and
\mathbb{P}(y|\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1})=\mathbb{P}(y|\bm{x}_{N-1})
. Moreover, the transition matrix of the Markov model is
\bm{\Pi}=(\pi_{i,j})_{K\times K}
, where
\pi_{i,j}=p\cdot\ind\{i\equiv j-1(\text{mod }K)\}+(1-p)\cdot\ind\{i\equiv j+1(\text{mod }K)\}
.
A visualization of
\bm{\Pi}
is given in Figure
2
. The Markov property indicates that the optimal predictor of
y
is given by

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1})=\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1},

and the optimal prediction accuracy any predictor can achieve is
\mathrm{OPT}=\max\{p,1-p\}
. Based on the formulation of
f^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\cdot)
, it is clear that a simple autoregressive model
f(\bm{X})=\bm{V}\bm{x}_{N-1}
can already solve this random walk prediction task. However, the goal of this work is not to identify the optimal model for solving random walk tasks. Instead, we aim to understand and analyze the capability and interpretability of transformers when learning such classic statistical tasks from scratch. Therefore, in the following, we introduce a simple transformer model.

with
p=0.5

p=0.7

We consider learning the random walk prediction task with a simple one-layer transformer model.
By naturally treating the one-hot vectors
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}
as token embeddings, the task to predict the next position
y
is a problem of next token prediction. Therefore, we define the data matrix
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1},\textbf{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
.
We also employ a positional embedding matrix
\bm{P}=[\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
, where
M
is the embedding dimension with
M=\Omega(N^{3/2})
and
\bm{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{i}=\left[\sin\left(\frac{i\pi}{M+1}\right),\sin\left(\frac{2i\pi}{M+1}\right),\ldots,\sin\left(\frac{Mi\pi}{M+1}\right)\right]^{\top}

for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. The positional embeddings above are inspired by the fact that
\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{p}_{j}\rangle=0
for all
i\neq j
, which significantly helps to simplify our theoretical analysis (see Lemma
E.5
in the appendix). Additionally, orthogonal positional embeddings are commonly considered in existing theoretical studies
(
Bietti et al. 2024
;
Nichani et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
Then, we define the matrix
\tilde{\bm{X}}
by concatenating the input matrix
\bm{X}
and the positional embedding matrix
\bm{P}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\bm{P}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}&\cdots&\bm{x}_{N-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\bm{p}_{1}&\bm{p}_{2}&\cdots&\bm{p}_{N-1}&\bm{p}_{N}\end{bmatrix}:=[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{1},\tilde{\bm{x}}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+M)\times N}.

We consider a one-layer transformer model to make a prediction on a given input matrix
\bm{X}
. The transformer is defined as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(\bm{X})=\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N}),

(2.1)

where
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
,
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
are the trainable parameter matrices,
{\mathcal{S}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the softmax function defined by
[{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{z})]_{i}=\frac{\exp(z_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(z_{j})}
, and
\theta=(\bm{V},\bm{W})
denotes the collection of all the trainable parameters. In this definition, we consider a reparameterization where we use a single matrix
\bm{W}
to denote the product of the key and query parameter matrices in self-attention. Such kind of reparameterizations are widely considered in theoretical studies of transformer models
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Ildiz et al. 2024
;
Jelassi et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Nichani et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
. In addition, we omit the softmax scaling factor, which is mainly for simplicity. Such omission has also been considered in most of the theoretical studies
(
D’Angelo et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Nichani et al. 2024
;
Svete and Cotterell 2024
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023a
)
.

Note that by (
2.1
), given any input matrix
\bm{X}
, the transformer model outputs a
K
-dimensional vector. This follows the standard practice of
K
-class classification: for
i\in[K]
,
[f_{\theta}(\bm{X})]_{i}
can be treated as a predicted “score” of the
i
-th class. More specifically, we define the prediction rule as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

For any predictor
f(\bm{X}):\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
, the predicted label is given as

\displaystyle\text{Pred}(f(\bm{X})):=\min\left\{j\in[K]:[f(\bm{X})]_{j}=\max_{i\in[K]}\{[f(\bm{X})]_{i}\}\right\}.

The definition above matches the common practice to predict the label that corresponds to the entry in
f(\bm{X})
with the maximum function value. It also gives a naive way to handle ties – when
f(\bm{X})
contains multiple dimensions with the same (and maximum) function value, we always predict the dimension corresponding to the smallest label. We remark that this definition to handle ties is just to exclude ambiguity, and the detailed rule to handle ties is not essential. Our result works for all reasonable methods to handle ties.

We train the transformer model defined in (
2.1
) by gradient descent, minimizing the loss function

\displaystyle L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{X},y)}\left[\ell\left(\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}f_{\theta}(\bm{X})\right)\right],

(2.2)

where
\ell(\cdot)
is a loss function. In terms of the specific choice of
\ell(\cdot)
, our analysis will cover learning random walks on a circle by minimizing the log-loss
\ell(z)=-\log(z+\epsilon)
, which has been considered in a series of recent works
(
Ildiz et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Makkuva et al. 2025
;
Thrampoulidis 2024
)
.

We consider gradient descent with zero initialization
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}^{K\times K}
,
\bm{W}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}^{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
to train the model. The update rule for the parameter matrices
\bm{W},\bm{V}
are as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{(t+1)}=\bm{W}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{W}}L(\theta^{(t)}),\quad\bm{V}^{(t+1)}=\bm{V}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{V}}L(\theta^{(t)}),

(2.3)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate and
t\geq 0
is the iteration number. Note that the log-loss
\ell(z)=-\log(z+\epsilon)
is well-defined and does not blow up at zero initialization due to the stability constant
\epsilon>0
. Gradient descent with appropriate learning rate further ensures that it does not blow up during training.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our main theoretical results on learning random walks with a self-attention layer.
In our result, we can choose any
T^{*}=\text{poly}(\eta,\epsilon^{-1},K,N,M)
as the maximum admissible number of iterations, and only consider the training period
0\leq t\leq T^{*}
. This technical assumption regarding a polynomially large maximum admissible number of iterations prevents training from becoming exponentially long and is a mild assumption since exponentially long training is impractical.

Our main results are given in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
0<p<1
,
K>4
, and
N\geq C_{p}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(K)
for some constant
C_{p}
that only depends on
p
. Further suppose that the transformer is trained by gradient descent (
2.3
) to minimize the loss (
2.2
) with
\ell(z)=-\log(z+\epsilon)
, and
\eta,\epsilon=\Theta(1)
. Then there exists
T_{0}=\Theta(1)
, such that for all
T_{0}\leq T\leq T^{*}
, it holds that:

1.

The trained transformer achieves optimal prediction accuracy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[\text{Pred}(f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X}))=y\big]=\mathrm{OPT}=\max\{p,1-p\}.

2.

The transformer converges to the optimal predictor:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})}{\|f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})\|_{2}}-f^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\bm{X})\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

3.

The value matrix converges to the true transition matrix in direction:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\bm{V}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{\bm{\Pi}^{\top}}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

4.

Softmax attention selects the “direct parent” of
y
:

\displaystyle\big[{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})\big]_{N-1}\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(N)),

\displaystyle\big[{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})\big]_{j}\leq\exp(-\Omega(N))\text{ for all }j\neq N-1.

The first result in Theorem
3.1
states that the transformer trained by gradient descent for a constant number of iterations can achieve a prediction accuracy
\max\{p,1-p\}
, which matches the optimal accuracy
\mathrm{OPT}
. The second result in Theorem
3.1
further gives a more detailed characterization of the trained transformer, and demonstrates that the normalized model converges to the optimal prediction model
f^{\mathrm{OPT}}(\bm{X})=\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1}
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of how the trained one-layer transformer performs optimal prediction in an example with
p=1/2
,
K=6
. Here we denote
\bm{S}={\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})
.

The third and fourth results in Theorem
3.1
further back up the first two results by a precise characterization on how the self-attention mechanism works in predicting random walks. Specifically, the third result demonstrates that in direction,
the value matrix
\bm{V}^{(T)}
serves as a one-step transition model
by recovering the ground-truth one-step transition matrix, and the last result indicates that
softmax attention performs optimal token selection
by assigning a near-one score to the
(N-1)
-th token (the direct parent of
y
). These two results show that, when learning to predict random walks, the one-layer transformer obtained through gradient descent is
interpretable
: the trained one-layer transformer model makes predictions by (i) selecting the correct parent token
\bm{x}_{N-1}
of
y
by assigning a softmax weighting close to 1 to it, and (ii) predicting
y
by applying a one-step transition model to
\bm{x}_{N-1}
through the linear mapping defined by
\bm{V}
. An illustration is given in Figure
3
.

A recent related work
(
Makkuva et al. 2025
)
studied how one-layer transformers can learn Markov chains with a state space of size two, and analyzed the loss landscape by identifying global minima and bad local minima. We remark that we consider a different parameterization of one-layer transformers, which makes our results not rigorously comparable. However, by studying random walks over state spaces of arbitrary sizes and establishing theoretical guarantees directly on transformers trained by gradient descent, our work explores a setting that is not covered in
Makkuva et al. 2025
.

Notably, Theorem
3.1
is based on the assumption that the transition probability satisfies
0<p<1
, excluding the edge cases when
p=0,1
. In Section
4
, we will show a negative result showing that when
p=0,1
, gradient descent with zero initialization fails to achieve good prediction accuracy. Therefore, the assumption
0<p<1
in Theorem
3.1
is inevitable.

Here we informally explain how Theorem
3.1
is proved. The idea of the proof can be shown by investigating the first several gradient descent steps:

Step 1.

After the first gradient descent step, due to the zero initialization and the Toeplitz property of the transition matrix
\bm{\Pi}
, it can be shown that
\bm{V}^{(1)}
is also a Toeplitz matrix whose largest entries appear exactly on the locations of the largest entries of
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
.
\bm{W}^{(1)}
is still a zero matrix due to the fact that
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
.

Step 2.

With the same analysis, we can also show that
\bm{V}^{(2)}
is a Toeplitz matrix whose largest entries appear exactly on the locations of the largest entries of
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
. Moreover, the locations of the largest entries in
\bm{V}^{(1)}
encourage
\bm{W}^{(2)}
to be updated so that higher softmax weightings are put upon the “direct parent” token
\bm{x}_{N-1}
(see Lemma
C.5
in the appendix).

Step 3.

The higher weighting on
\bm{x}_{N-1}
given by
\bm{W}^{(2)}
further encourages
\bm{V}^{(3)}
to be updated towards
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
in direction. And
\bm{V}^{(2)}
obtained in Step 2 continues to encourage
\bm{W}^{(3)}
to place a even higher weighting on
\bm{x}_{N-1}
(see Lemma
C.7
in the appendix).

From the three gradient descent steps discussed above, it is clear that
\bm{V}^{(t)}
will converge to the direction of
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
, and
\bm{W}^{(t)}
will consistently place a high weighting on the direct parent token
\bm{x}_{N-1}
. This is our key intuition for proving Theorem
3.1
, and in our formal proof (given in the appendix), we use an induction to characterize the whole training procedure.

4
“Deterministic Walks” with
p=0,1

In Section
3
, we demonstrate that a one-layer transformer can be trained to optimally predict random walks under the assumption
0<p<1
. In this section, we justify ‌the necessity of this assumption and analyze the edge cases
p=0,1
, which lead to a failure for learning random walks. Random walks with
p=0,1
are essentially “deterministic walks”, and for them we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
N=rK+1
with
r\geq 1
, and
p=0\text{ or }1
. Further suppose that the transformer is trained by gradient descent (
2.3
) to minimize the loss (
2.2
). Then for any loss function
\ell(\cdot)
, any learning rate
\eta>0
, and any
T\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\text{Pred}(f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X}))=y\big)=\frac{1}{K}.

Moreover, with probability 1, for all
T\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(T)}\propto\mathbf{1}_{K\times K},~~\left[{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})\right]_{1}=\cdots=\left[{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})\right]_{N-1}.

Theorem
4.1
shows that the prediction accuracy of the trained transformer on deterministic walks is
1/K
, equal to the accuracy of a random guess. Moreover, the characterizations of the softmax scores and the value matrix
\bm{V}^{(T)}
further demonstrate that the transformer takes average over all tokens, and then gives the same prediction scores for all possible values of
y
. Notably, these results hold for any choice of the loss function and any learning rate, indicating that this failure case of the transformer cannot be resolved by simply adjusting these training setups.

Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
together provide a comprehensive characterization of the transformer’s performance when learning random walks with transition probabilities
p\in[0,1]
. Based on Theorem
4.1
, it is clear that the assumption in Theorem
3.1
that
0<p<1
is necessary and cannot be further relaxed. Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
also point out an interesting fact, that compared to random walks with
0<p<1
, the seemingly easier task of predicting deterministic walks with
p=0,1
may be more challenging for a transformer to learn. Here, we would like to remark that the failure of learning deterministic walks is an optimization failure, and is highly due to zero initialization. To see the reason, we can consider the two initial gradient descent steps:

Step 1.

Since the initial softmax weightings on all tokens are the same,
\bm{V}^{(1)}
is essentially trained based on the averaged token
\overline{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}
. Importantly, we can see that

\overline{\bm{x}}
is a constant vector that does not depend on
y
.

This means that
\overline{\bm{x}}
does not provide any helpful information in predicting
y
, and is therefore “uninformative”.
As a result, it can be shown that all entries in
\bm{V}^{(1)}
are equal (see Lemma
D.1
in the appendix). Additionally,
\bm{W}^{(1)}
is still a zero matrix since
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
.

Step 2.

With the same analysis as Step 1, we can show that all entries in
\bm{V}^{(2)}
are equal. Moreover, due to the fact that
\bm{V}^{(1)}
is proportional to the all-one matrix, it can be further shown that
\bm{W}^{(2)}
is updated so that the softmax weightings on all tokens
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N-1}
remain equal.

In our formal proof, we inductively show that throughout training, the value matrix
\bm{V}^{(t)}
is always proportional to the all-one matrix, and the softmax weights on all tokens are always the same.

From the discussion above, we can observe that transformers struggle to learn deterministic walks due to the unbreakable symmetry in the training dynamics, arising from the “uninformative” token average
\overline{\bm{x}}
given by the zero initialization. We remark that if random initialization is used, the symmetry will be broken, and transformers may succeed in learning the optimal predictor. However, if the random initialization is too small in scale, we can still expect that the optimization for learning deterministic walks to be more challenging compared to that for random walks. While our theoretical analysis does not cover random initialization, we present empirical studies in Sections
5
and
6
to verify this claim. We believe that theoretically analyzing the impact of random initializations can be an important future work direction.

5
Experiment

In this section, we present experimental results to support our theoretical analysis. We consider two cases: the first one is the zero initialization case that aligns with the setting used in our theoretical analysis, and the second one is the random initialization case, which helps verify our discussion about the optimization failure caused by zero initialization. In all experiments introduced in this section, we set the number of nodes
K=6
and the length of each sequence
N=97
. We utilize the transformer model introduced in Section
2
and utilize the gradient method to train the model. The prediction accuracy is calculated based on 1000 test data.

Zero initialization.
In this case, we set the length of the positional embedding
M=1000
, the initialization
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{K\times K},\bm{W}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
, and the learning rate
\eta=1

6
Beyond Random Walks

In Section
4
, we intuitively explain that one-layer transformers may suffer from optimization issues when learning deterministic walks with
p=0,1
due to the fact that zero initialization produces a token average which is “uninformative”.
In this section, we briefly discuss other tasks beyond random walks where transformers with zero/small initialization may face similar optimization challenges.

We construct two question answering tasks. The detailed descriptions are given as follows.

Task 1.
We consider a simple question answering task, where possible input questions are of the form:

Based on the list ‘apple, orange, apple, apple, orange’, which type of fruit appears most frequently?

Here, the list stated in the question can be any combination of ‘apple’ and ‘orange’ with a fixed length of 5. Therefore, there are a total of
32
possible questions the model may see, and each of these questions occurs with probability
1/32
. Ignoring punctuation marks, each input sample is assumed to be 16 words involving the list and other words in the inquiry sentence. The correct response (the ’label’ for classification) is the fruit that appears most frequently in the list. For example, for the question
“Based on the list ‘apple, orange, apple, apple, orange’, which type of fruit appears most frequently?”
, the correct response is
apple
.

Task 2.
We again consider a simple question answering task with only two possible questions:

Based on the sentence ‘I prefer an apple to an orange’, which type of fruit do I prefer?

Based on the sentence ‘I prefer an orange to an apple’, which type of fruit do I prefer?

Here, each of the two questions above occurs with probability
1/2
. Similar to Task 1, we ignore the punctuation marks, and the input is the 18 words in the sentence. The correct response (the “label” for classification) is
apple
for the first question above, and
orange
for the second question above.

Combining all the words appearing in two tasks, we attain a vocabulary with a length of 19: {‘apple’, ‘orange’, ‘Based’, ‘on’, ‘the’, ‘which’, ‘type’, ‘of’, ‘fruit’, ‘list’, ‘appears’, ‘most’, ‘frequently’, ‘sentence’, ‘I’, ‘prefer’, ‘an’, ‘to’, ‘do’}. We embed this sequence as a matrix

K
and the length of each input sequence

are set as
(K,N)=(19,17),(19,19)
for Task 1 and Task 2 respectively.

In the experiments, we consider a similar transformer model as we introduced in our theoretical analysis. To train the model, we consider Gaussian random initialization

with

to train the model. The constant

in the log-loss is set as

7
Conclusion

This paper investigates the capability of a one-layer transformer model to learn random walks on circles. We demonstrate that transformers successfully learn such walks for the transition probability
0<p<1
, achieving the optimal prediction accuracy. We also show that the trained model is interpretable: the softmax attention mechanism effectively selects the correct “parent” token, while the value matrix recovers a one-step transition model that applies to the selected “parent” token for optimal prediction. In addition, we identify that the edge cases (
p=0,1
) are failure cases, thereby proving the necessity of the assumption
0<p<1
. Motivated by the analysis of success and failure in learning random walks, we design simple question answering tasks that exhibit similar optimization challenges, showing the broader applicability of our analysis to other tasks beyond random walks. We also provide experimental results to validate our theoretical findings.

In future works, it is important to theoretically study the impact of random initialization in learning random walks. Moreover, an interesting future work direction is to extend the results and study the performance of deeper transformer architectures, which may require more advanced theoretical tools. Moreover, extending the finding to more complicated learning tasks, such as random sequences generated by general Markov chains or Bayesian networks, is also an important future work direction.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

In this section, we give an overview of some additional related works.

Token selection.
Our work reveals that a one-layer transformer can learn to perform the optimal token selection by focusing on the direct parent in a random walk.
A line of recent works has studied the token selection of the self-attention mechanism from different perspectives.
Tarzanagh et al. 2023a
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023b
propose an equivalence between the optimization dynamics of one self-attention layer and an SVM problem and prove the global convergence under certain assumptions.
Li et al. 2024a
shows that when training a self-attention layer, the priority in token selection is determined by a directed graph extracted from the training data.

Wang et al. 2024
demonstrates that transformer models can learn the sparse token selection task effectively while fully connected networks fail in the worst case.
Li et al. 2024b
shows that a self-attention layer can be trained to perform proper token selection so that the model acts as a one-nearest neighbor classifier in context. More recently,
Zhang et al. 2025
shows that one-layer transformer can learn to perform group-sparse linear classification.

Next-token prediction.

Thrampoulidis 2024
explores the implicit bias of next-token prediction employing a related SVM formulation.
Lu et al. 2024
demonstrates that transformers fail to solve the Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes problem (POMDP) even with sufficient data.
He and Su 2025
observes a phenomenon of next-token prediction in LLM that each layer contributes equally to enhancing the prediction accuracy.
Tian et al. 2023
studies the SGD training dynamics of a transformer with one self-attention layer and one decoder layer for next-token prediction, restricted to some specific assumptions like no positional encoding, long input sequences, and the fact that the decoder layer learns faster than the self-attention layer.

Training dynamics of transformers.

Mahankali et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
investigate the training dynamics of in-context learning in transformers with a single self-attention layer trained through gradient flow on linear regression tasks.
Huang et al. 2024
solves in-context linear regression with the orthogonal input data by gradient descent on a single softmax attention layer.
Jelassi et al. 2022
demonstrates that the position-position block of a single attention layer in a vision transformer can encode spatial structure by dealing with a binary classification task.
Tian et al. 2024
delves into the training process of transformers with multi-layers by analyzing the dynamics of the MLP layers.
Bietti et al. 2024
analyzes a synthetic in-context learning task and emphasizes the significance of weight matrices as associative memories.
Abbe et al. 2024
shows incremental learning dynamics in transformers with diagonal attention matrices.

Appendix B
Gradient Calculation

Recall that the population loss is

\displaystyle L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[-\log(\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}f_{\theta}(\bm{X})+\epsilon)]=\mathbb{E}[-\log(\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N})+\epsilon)].

The following lemma calculates the gradients of the population function.

Lemma B.1
.

The gradients regarding
\bm{V}
and
\bm{W}
are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta)

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}\cdot\bm{e}_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\bm{e}_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta)

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\\
\textbf{0}&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\\
\textbf{0}&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N})
, and
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

For
\bm{V}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}f_{\theta}(\bm{X})+\epsilon}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}}{\partial\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\bm{e}_{y}{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\bm{e}_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}.

For
\bm{W}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}f_{\theta}(\bm{X})+\epsilon}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N})}{\partial\bm{W}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{X}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N})\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{X}}[\text{diag}({\mathcal{S}})-{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}]\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top})\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\\
\textbf{0}&(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top})\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where we use the fact that
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N})=[\text{diag}({\mathcal{S}})-{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}]
and

\text{diag}({\mathcal{S}}):=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathcal{S}}_{1}&&&\\
&{\mathcal{S}}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&{\mathcal{S}}_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

∎

To simplify the notation, we denote

\displaystyle\bm{A}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top})\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{B}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top})\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}.

With this notations, by Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta)=-\frac{1}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}&\bm{A}\\
\textbf{0}&\bm{B}\end{bmatrix}.

(B.1)

We also denote
\bm{A}^{(t)},\bm{B}^{(t)}
the corresponding matrices at the
t
-th iteration of gradient descent.
Moreover, we can observe that
\bm{W}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{W}_{11}&\bm{W}_{12}\\
\bm{W}_{21}&\bm{W}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\bm{W}_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
,
\bm{W}_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M}
,
\bm{W}_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}
, and
\bm{W}_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
. By (
B.1
), we know that
\bm{W}_{11}^{(t)}=\textbf{0}_{K\times K}
and
\bm{W}_{21}^{(t)}=\textbf{0}_{M\times K}
for all
t\geq 1
.

By the definition of the random walks on circles, we can write the transition matrix
\bm{\Pi}
as
\bm{\Pi}=p\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{\top}+(1-p)\bm{\Pi}_{0}
, where

\displaystyle\bm{\Pi}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&&&1\\
1&0&&&\\
&1&0&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

In this section, we analyze random walks with the transition probability

satisfying
0<p<1
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\frac{1}{2}\leq p<1
. We consider gradient descent starting from zero initialization. The following lemma presents the result of the first iteration.

Lemma C.1
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(1)}=\frac{\eta}{\epsilon NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}~\text{and}~\bm{W}^{(1)}=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}.

Proof of Lemma
C.1
.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{y}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\epsilon NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}

where the first equation is by the initialization of
\bm{V}^{(0)}
and
\bm{W}^{(0)}
, the second equation is by the sampling method, and the third equation is by
\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{I}_{K}
for
i\in[N-1]
since
\bm{x}_{i}
is uniformly distributed in
\bm{E}
.
Thus, by the update, we can get

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{V}^{(0)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{\epsilon NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}.

Since
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{K\times K}
and
\bm{W}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
, we can get
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{(1)}=\bm{W}^{(0)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}.

∎

Lemma
C.1
gives explicit calculations of
\bm{V}^{(1)}
. Based on these, we can further derive some properties of
\bm{V}^{(1)}
, given in Lemma
C.2
below. In this lemma, for all matrix indices, we consider simplified notations following the rule that if an index
i
is not in the set
\{1,\ldots,K\}

i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
with
i^{\prime}\equiv i\pmod{K}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
, it holds that
[\bm{V}^{(1)}]_{i_{1},j_{2}}=[\bm{V}^{(1)}]_{i_{2},j_{2}}
for

.

Proof of Lemma
C.2
.

We use induction to prove that for any
R\in\mathbb{N}
,
[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{1},j_{2}}=[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{2},j_{2}}
for
i_{1}-j_{1}\equiv i_{2}-j_{2}\pmod{K}
. The result is obvious at
R=1
. Suppose that the results hold for
\bm{\Pi}^{R}
. We aim to prove the results hold for
\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}
. By the definition of
\bm{\Pi}
, for any

, we obtain that

\displaystyle[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{i,j}=p\cdot[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j-1}+(1-p)\cdot[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j+1}.

(C.1)

By induction, for any
i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}
satisfying
i_{1}-j_{1}\equiv i_{2}-j_{2}\pmod{K}
, we have
[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{1},j_{1}-1}=[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{2},j_{2}-1}
and
[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{1},j_{1}+1}=[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i_{2},j_{2}+1}
. Thus, by (
C.1
), we can easily get
[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{i_{1},j_{1}}=[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{i_{2},j_{2}}
, which completes the induction.

From Lemma
C.1
, we know
(\bm{V}^{(1)})^{\top}=\frac{\eta}{\epsilon NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{N-i}
holds the result. Therefore,
\bm{V}^{(1)}
also has the same property.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
, it holds that
[\bm{V}^{(1)}]_{2,1}=\|\bm{V}^{(1)}\|_{\max}
.

Proof of Lemma
C.3
.

Without loss of generality, we assume
\frac{1}{2}\leq p<1
. From Lemma
C.1
, we know
\bm{V}^{(1)}=\frac{\eta}{\epsilon NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}
. We can observe that since
p<1
, with increasing
R
,
(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{R}
will be closer to
\frac{1}{K}\textbf{1}\textbf{1}^{\top}
, as stated in Lemma
E.2
and
E.3
. Thus, the location of the largest entries in
\bm{V}^{(1)}
is mainly determined by the first several terms. In
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
, there are two locations with non-zero values
p,1-p

matrix of interest:

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{i}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{\Pi}\bm{e}_{1}).

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{i}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{\Pi}^{j}\bm{e}_{1}).

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)=\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)+\bm{I}.

And, we can get

\displaystyle\gamma^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{(t)}+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\eta\bm{e}_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]\right\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{(t)}+2\epsilon K^{2}N\exp(-\Omega(N))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon K}+2t\epsilon K^{2}N\exp(-\Omega(N)),

where the second inequality is by (
C.3
), and the third inequality is by induction.

Next, we consider
{\mathcal{S}}^{(t+1)}
. Recall that

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}=\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{A}^{(t)}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\right]~\text{and}~\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}=\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{B}^{(t)}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}+\epsilon}\right],

where

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{(t)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{1}}^{(t)}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{(t)}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}.

We also have
\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{N}=\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}
and
\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{j}=\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
.
Then, for
j=N
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{B}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\eta M{\mathcal{S}}_{N}^{(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}.

For
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N-2\}
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{A}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\sqrt{M}{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\sqrt{M}{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(t)}\frac{\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}}{\min_{i,j}[\bm{V}^{(t)}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\sqrt{M}\exp(-\Omega(N))\left(2K+\frac{4(t-2)\epsilon^{2}K^{4}}{\eta}\right),

where the first inequality is by
\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{N-1}=\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}
, and the second inequality is by induction and Lemma
C.6
.
And,

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{B}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta M{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta M{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(t)}\frac{\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}}{\min_{i,j}[\bm{V}^{(t)}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta M\exp(-\Omega(N))\left(2K+\frac{4(t-2)\epsilon^{2}K^{4}}{\eta}\right),

where the first inequality is by
\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{N-1}=\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}
, and the second inequality is by induction and Lemma
C.6
.
For
j=N-1
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{A}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\sqrt{M}{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\sqrt{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{-\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\eta\sqrt{M}\frac{\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}}{\min_{i,j}[\bm{V}^{(t)}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\eta\sqrt{M}N\exp(-\Omega(N))\left(2K+\frac{4(t-2)\epsilon^{2}K^{4}}{\eta}\right),

where the second inequality is by
\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{N-1}=\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}
, and the third inequality is by induction and Lemma
C.6
.
And,

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{B}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta M{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\eta M\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{-\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{e}_{y}}{\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}+\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\eta M\frac{\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t)}\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}}{\min_{i,j}[\bm{V}^{(t)}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\eta MN\exp(-\Omega(N))\left(2K+\frac{4(t-2)\epsilon^{2}K^{4}}{\eta}\right),

where the second inequality is by
\bm{e}_{y}^{\top}\bm{V}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{N-1}=\|\bm{V}^{(t)}\|_{\max}
, and the third inequality is by induction and Lemma
C.6
.
Therefore, we can get that for
j\neq N-1
,

\displaystyle\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t+1)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{N-1}-\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t+1)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{j}

\displaystyle\quad=\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}-\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}-\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t+1)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\bm{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}-\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}

\displaystyle\qquad-4\eta MN\exp(-\Omega(N))\left(2K+\frac{4(t-2)\epsilon^{2}K^{4}}{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{N-1}-\left[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}\right]_{j}-\exp(-\Omega(N))

\displaystyle\quad\geq\Omega(N),

where the last inequality is by induction. Thus,
{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t+1)}/{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(t+1)}\geq\exp(\Omega(N))
for
j\neq N-1
, which implies that
{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t+1)}\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(N))
. Therefore, we prove that the results hold for
t+1
, which completes the proof.
∎

The next two lemmas show the convergence rates of
\bm{V}^{(T)}/\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}
and
f_{\theta^{T}}(\bm{X})/\|f_{\theta^{T}}(\bm{X})\|_{2}
.

Lemma C.8
.

Assume the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
. For
\Omega(\eta\epsilon^{-2}K^{-2})\leq T\leq T^{*}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\bm{V}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{\bm{\Pi}^{\top}}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
C.8
.

By Lemma
C.7
, we can get that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\bm{V}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{\bm{\Pi}^{\top}}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\beta^{(T)}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}+\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{\bm{\Pi}^{\top}}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\frac{\beta^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{1}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right)\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\frac{\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}.

For the first part, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\frac{\beta^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{1}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right)\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\beta^{(T)}\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}{\|\beta^{(T)}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}+\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{\beta^{(T)}\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}{\beta^{(T)}\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{K(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})}\beta^{(T)}+\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K\gamma^{(T)}}{\sqrt{K(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})}\beta^{(T)}+K\gamma^{(T)}}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\frac{\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon}+2T\epsilon K^{3}N\exp(-\Omega(N))}{\frac{\sqrt{2K}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\eta T}-\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon K}\right)+\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon}+2T\epsilon K^{3}N\exp(-\Omega(N))}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right),

where
(i)
is by
\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}=\sqrt{K(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})}
, and
(ii)
is by Lemma
C.7
.
For the second part, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}{\|\beta^{(T)}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}+\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}{\|\beta^{(T)}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{\bm{V}}^{(T)}\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{K\gamma^{(T)}}{\frac{\sqrt{2K}}{2}\beta^{(T)}+K\gamma^{(T)}}

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\frac{\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon}+2T\epsilon K^{3}N\exp(-\Omega(N))}{\frac{\sqrt{2K}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\eta T}-\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon K}\right)+\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon}+2T\epsilon K^{3}N\exp(-\Omega(N))}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right),

where
(i)
is by
\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}=\sqrt{K(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})}\geq\sqrt{2K}/2
, and
(ii)
is by Lemma
C.7
.
Therefore, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\bm{V}^{(T)}}{\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\|_{F}}-\frac{\bm{\Pi}^{\top}}{\|\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\|_{F}}\right\|_{F}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

∎

Lemma C.9
.

Assume the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
. For
\Omega(\eta\epsilon^{-2}K^{-2})\leq T\leq T^{*}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})}{\|f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})\|_{2}}-\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
C.9
.

The output with

is
f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})=\bm{V}^{(T)}\bm{X}{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\bm{X}}^{\top}\bm{W}^{(T)}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{N})=\bm{V}^{(T)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(T)}\bm{x}_{i}

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})}{\|f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})\|_{2}}-\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\frac{\beta^{(T)}{\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(T)}}{\left\|\bm{V}^{(T)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(T)}\bm{x}_{i}\right\|_{2}}-1\right)\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1}\right\|_{2}

C.7

C.7

C.7

C.7

, and the third inequality is by Lemma
C.7
.
Therefore, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})}{\|f_{\theta^{(T)}}(\bm{X})\|_{2}}-\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{x}_{N-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4.1

In this section, we analyze “deterministic walks” with
p=0,1
. Without loss of generality, we set
p=1
, which means
\bm{\Pi}=\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{\top}
. The following lemma shows the results of the first iteration.

Lemma D.1
.

Under the same condition as Theorem
4.1
, for any loss function
\ell(\cdot)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(1)}=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{\eta r}{NK}\mathbf{1}_{K\times K}~\text{and}~\bm{W}^{(1)}=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}.

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{y}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}_{0})^{N-i}

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K},

where the first equation is by the initialization of
\bm{V}^{(0)}
and
\bm{W}^{(0)}
, the second equation is by the sampling method, the third equation is by
\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{I}_{K}
for
i\in[N-1]
, and the last equation is by Lemma
E.1
.
Thus, by the update, we can get

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(1)}=\bm{V}^{(0)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{\eta r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K}.

Since
\bm{V}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{K\times K}
and
\bm{W}^{(0)}=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
, we can get
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{(1)}=\bm{W}^{(0)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(0)})]=\textbf{0}_{(K+M)\times(K+M)}.

∎

The following lemma states the results of the second iteration.

Lemma D.2
.

If
\bm{\Pi}=\bm{\Pi}_{2}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(2)}=-(\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})+\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)}))\cdot\frac{\eta r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{12}^{(2)}=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\frac{\eta^{2}r^{2}}{N^{3}K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{22}^{(2)}=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta^{2}r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\frac{\eta^{2}r^{2}}{N^{3}}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}.

Proof of Lemma
D.2
.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(1)})]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{y}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}_{0})^{N-i}

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\frac{r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K},

where the second equation is by the sampling method, the third equation is by
\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{I}_{K}
for
i\in[N-1]
, and the last equation is by Lemma
E.1
.
Thus, we can get

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\bm{V}^{(1)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(1)})]

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\cdot\frac{\eta r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K}-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\frac{\eta r}{NK}\textbf{1}_{K\times K}.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(1)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(1)}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(1)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r}{N^{2}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{K}-\frac{\eta r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r}{N^{2}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}-\frac{\eta r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r^{2}}{N^{2}K}\mathbf{1}_{K}-\frac{\eta r^{2}(N-1)}{N^{3}K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\frac{\eta r^{2}}{N^{3}K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

where the second equation is by Lemma
D.1
, and the fourth equation is by the fact that all the
\bm{x}_{i}
is uniformly distributed in
\bm{E}
.
We also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{B}^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(1)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(1)}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(1)})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r}{N^{2}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{K}-\frac{\eta r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r}{N^{2}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\frac{\eta r(N-1)}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{i}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\frac{\eta r^{2}}{N^{3}}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

where the second equation is by Lemma
D.1
. Thus, we can get that

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{12}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{12}^{(1)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(1)})]_{12}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\bm{A}^{(1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\frac{\eta^{2}r^{2}}{N^{3}K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

and

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{22}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{22}^{(1)}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(1)})]_{22}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\cdot\bm{B}^{(1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(1)})\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(0)})\left(\frac{\eta^{2}r}{N^{3}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\frac{\eta^{2}r^{2}}{N^{3}}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}.

∎

Next, we can analyze the gradient descent dynamics over multiple iterations.

Lemma D.3
.

If
\bm{\Pi}=\bm{\Pi}_{2}
, then for any
t\geq 0
and any sequence of learning rates
\{\eta_{t}\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{(t)}\propto\mathbf{1}_{K\times K},~\text{ and }~{\mathcal{S}}_{1}^{(t)}={\mathcal{S}}_{2}^{(t)}=\cdots={\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t)}.

Proof of Lemma
D.3
.

We use induction to prove that for some scalar
\alpha_{1}^{(t)},\alpha_{2}^{(t)},\alpha_{3}^{(t)},\alpha_{4}^{(t)}
, it holds that for
t\geq 2
,
\bm{V}^{(t)}=\alpha_{1}^{(t)}\textbf{1}_{K\times K}
,
\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}=\alpha_{2}^{(t)}\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}
, and
\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}=\left(\alpha_{3}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{4}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}
. By Lemma
D.2
, we know that the hypothesis holds for
t=2
. Suppose that the hypothesis holds for
t=t^{\prime}
. We aim to prove that the hypothesis holds for
t=t^{\prime}+1
. We have

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t^{\prime})}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\\
\bm{x}_{2}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{2}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{x}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{12}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}+\bm{p}_{N-1}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\\
\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{2}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{p}_{N}+\alpha_{3}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{1}^{\top}\bm{p}_{1}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{p}_{N}\\

\vdots\\

-\alpha_{4}^{(t^{\prime})}(\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{p}_{N})^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(D.1)

Since
\bm{p}_{1}^{\top}\bm{p}_{1}=\bm{p}_{2}^{\top}\bm{p}_{2}=\cdots=\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\bm{p}_{N}
, we have
[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t^{\prime})}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{1}=[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t^{\prime})}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{2}=\cdots=[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t^{\prime})}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{N-1}
. Thus, we can get that
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}={\mathcal{S}}_{2}^{(t^{\prime})}=\cdots={\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t^{\prime})}:=s^{(t^{\prime})}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\cdot\bm{e}_{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[\bm{e}_{y}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{N-i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\frac{s^{(t^{\prime})}}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\bm{\Pi}_{0})^{N-i}

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\frac{s^{(t^{\prime})}r}{K}\textbf{1}_{K\times K},

where the second equation is by the induction, the third equation is by the sampling method, the fourth equation is by the fact that
\bm{x}_{i}
is uniformly distributed in
\bm{E}
, and the last equation is by Lemma
E.1
. Thus, we can get
\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}=\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime})}-\eta^{(t^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{V}}\ell(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})]\propto\textbf{1}_{K\times K}
.
We also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}^{(t^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime})})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{1}}^{(t^{\prime})}{\mathcal{S}}_{i_{2}}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime})})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{K}-\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\bm{x}_{i_{2}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}-\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i_{1}}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}(N-1)}{K}\mathbf{1}_{K}-\frac{\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}(N-1)^{2}}{K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

where the second equation is by the induction, and the fourth equation is by the fact that all the
\bm{x}_{i}
is uniformly distributed in
\bm{E}
. And,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{B}^{(t^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime})})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{V}^{(t^{\prime})})^{\top}\bm{e}_{y}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{K}-\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{i}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{K}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{i}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

where the second equation is by the induction. Therefore, we can get

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{12}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{12}^{(t^{\prime})}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})]_{12}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}^{(t^{\prime})}\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}-\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}^{(t^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}^{(t^{\prime})}\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}-\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}(N-1)}{K}-\frac{\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}(N-1)^{2}}{K}\right)\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{2}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top},

and

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{22}^{(t^{\prime})}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})]_{22}

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{3}^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{4}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}-\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\mathbb{E}[\bm{B}^{(t^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{3}^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{4}^{(t^{\prime})}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad-\eta\ell^{\prime}(\theta^{(t^{\prime})})\left(\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}s^{(t^{\prime})}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{1}^{(t^{\prime})}(s^{(t^{\prime})})^{2}(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{i}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}

\displaystyle:=\left(\alpha_{3}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{4}^{(t^{\prime}+1)}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}.

Therefore, by induction, we can conclude that for all
t\geq 2
,
\bm{V}^{(t)}=\alpha_{1}^{(t)}\textbf{1}_{K\times K}
,
\bm{W}_{12}^{(t)}=\alpha_{2}^{(t)}\textbf{1}_{K}\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}
, and
\bm{W}_{22}^{(t)}=\left(\alpha_{3}^{(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{p}_{i}-\alpha_{4}^{(t)}\bm{p}_{N}\right)\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}
. Similar to (
D
), we have
[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{1}=[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{2}=\cdots=[\tilde{\bm{X}}\bm{W}^{(t)}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{N}]_{N-1}
, which implies that
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}^{(t)}={\mathcal{S}}_{2}^{(t)}=\cdots={\mathcal{S}}_{N-1}^{(t)}
.
∎

In this section, we present some auxiliary lemmas. The following lemma states the properties of
\bm{\Pi}_{0}
.

Lemma E.1
.

By the definition of
\bm{\Pi}_{0}
, it holds that
\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{K}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
,
\bm{\Pi}_{0}\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{k}=\textbf{1}_{K\times K}

Proof of Lemma
E.1
.

In this proof, the index
i
larger than
K
represents
i-K
. For
\bm{\Pi}_{0}
, only
[\bm{\Pi}_{0}]_{i+1,i}=1
for
i\in[K]
and other elements are 0. We can get that for
\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{k}
, only
[\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{k}]_{i+k,i}=1
for
i\in[K]
and other elements are 0. By this observation, we can derive that
\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{K}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{k}=\textbf{1}_{K\times K}
. Also, we have
\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{\top}=\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{K-1}

\bm{\Pi}_{0}\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{\top}=\bm{\Pi}_{0}^{K}=\mathbf{I}_{K}
.
∎

Further, the following three lemmas show some properties of
\bm{\Pi}
with transition probability
p

Lemma E.2
.

Assume that
K
is odd. It holds that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\bm{\Pi}^{R}\right]_{i,j}-\frac{1}{K}\right|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{8p(1-p)R}{K^{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
E.2
.

\bm{\Pi}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{0},...,\lambda_{K-1}
, where

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=pe^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}k}{K}}+(1-p)e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}k}{K}}=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{K}\right)+\mathrm{i}(1-2p)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{K}\right)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{k}|=\sqrt{1-4p(1-p)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi k}{K}\right)}\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{16p(1-p)}{K^{2}}}\leq 1-\frac{8p(1-p)}{K^{2}}

for

, where the first inequality is by

. The eigendecomposition of each entry in

\displaystyle[\bm{\Pi}]_{i,j}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}c_{k,i,j}\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\lambda_{0}+\sum_{k\not=0}c_{k,i,j}\lambda_{k},

where

.
Then, we can get that

\displaystyle[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}c_{k,i,j}\lambda_{k}^{R}=\frac{1}{K}+\sum_{k\not=0}c_{k,i,j}\lambda_{k}^{R}

Thus,

\displaystyle\left|[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j}-\frac{1}{K}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k\not=0}c_{k,i,j}\lambda_{k}^{R}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\not=0}|c_{k,i,j}||\lambda_{k}|^{R}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{8p(1-p)}{K^{2}}\right)^{R}\sum_{k\not=0}|c_{k,i,j}|

where the second inequality is by the bound of the absolute values of the eigenvalues, and the last inequality is by
(1-t)^{R}\leq e^{-Rt}
for any
0<t<1
and
|c_{k,i,j}|=\frac{1}{K}
for all
k,i,j
.
∎

Lemma E.4
.

For
\bm{\Pi}
with

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\right]_{1,1}\geq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\right]_{1,1}+C_{p},

Proof of Lemma
E.4
.

Without loss of generality, we assume

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\right]_{1,1}=\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{e}_{1}=\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot\bm{\Pi}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{e}_{1},

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\right]_{1,1}=\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\bm{e}_{1}=\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot\bm{\Pi}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\bm{e}_{1}.

\bm{\Gamma}(N)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\bm{\Pi}^{i}

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}\geq\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}+(1-p)^{N-2}\text{ for }i\neq j.

\bm{\Gamma}(2)_{1,1}=\bm{\Gamma}(2)_{i,j}+1

\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)

\bm{\Gamma}(N)
, by the definition of
\bm{\Gamma}(N)
, we can get
\bm{\Gamma}(N)=\bm{\Pi}\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)+\mathbf{I}
. Thus, based on the definition of
\bm{\Pi}
, we obtain that for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}=p\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j-1}+(1-p)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j+1}.

When
|i-j|\geq 2
, by induction, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=p\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j-1}+(1-p)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle\leq p(\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-3})+(1-p)(\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-3})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-3}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-2}.

When
i=j+1
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=p\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j-1}+(1-p)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle\leq p(\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-3})+(1-p)\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-p(1-p)^{N-3}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-2}.

When
i=j-1
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=p\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j-1}+(1-p)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{i,j+1}

\displaystyle\leq p\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}+(1-p)(\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-3})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}-(1-p)^{N-2}.

Obviously,
\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)_{1,1}\leq\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}
as
\bm{\Gamma}(N)=\bm{\Gamma}(N-1)+\bm{\Pi}^{N-2}
. Therefore, we prove that the induction hypothesis holds for
\bm{\Gamma}(N)
, which completes the induction. Thus, for all
N\geq 2
, the diagonal element is the largest entry in
\bm{\Gamma}(N)
. We analyze this result from the perspective of random walks. Given that
\bm{\Pi}^{k}
is the
k
-step transition matrix in the random walk task, the entry
\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{N-2}\bm{\Pi}^{l}
can be regarded as the expected number of visits to state
j
within
N-1
steps, starting from state
i
. Since the largest entry in
\bm{\Gamma}(N)
is found at the diagonal, we can conclude that the expected number of visits back to state
i
within
N-1
steps, starting from state
i
, is the largest.
From Lemma
E.2
and
E.3
, we know that for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\left|[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j}+[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{i,j}-\frac{2}{K}\right|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{8p(1-p)R}{K^{2}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{8p(1-p)(R+1)}{K^{2}}\right).

Hence, we get that for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\left|[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{1,1}-[\bm{\Pi}^{R}]_{i,j}-[\bm{\Pi}^{R+1}]_{i,j}\right|\leq 2\left[\exp\left(-\frac{8p(1-p)R}{K^{2}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{8p(1-p)(R+1)}{K^{2}}\right)\right].

Thus, for a constant integer
N_{0}=\omega(p^{-1}(1-p)^{-1})
and
N>N_{0}
, we can get for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{i,j}\geq(1-p)^{N_{0}-2}-c_{p0}\exp(-N_{0}),

where
c_{p0}
are constants related to
p
. In addition, we can get
|\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,j_{1}}-\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,j_{2}}|\leq c_{p0}\exp(-N_{0})
for any
j_{1}\neq 1,j_{2}\neq 1
.

Next, we focus on
\bm{\Pi}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}
with
k=1,2,...,N-1
. Denote
\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)=\bm{\Pi}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}
. Similar to the analysis of induction above, we can use induction to prove that for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)_{1,1}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)_{1,1}\geq\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)_{i,j}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)_{i,j}-(1-p^{2})^{k}.

The proof can be directly extended from the proof for
\bm{\Gamma}(N)
above. Then, we use this result to prove

\displaystyle[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2)]_{1,1}\geq[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)]_{1,1}

for any
k\geq 2
. We also use induction to prove this. It is easily checked that
[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2)]_{1,1}=p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}\geq 3p^{3}(1-p)+3p(1-p)^{3}=[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(3)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(4)]_{1,1}
. Suppose that
[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)]_{1,1}\geq[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+1)]_{1,1}+[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+2)]_{1,1}
. Then, by
\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+1)+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+2)=(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k))(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{2}
, we can get

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+1)_{1,1}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+2)_{1,1}

\displaystyle=p^{2}[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)]_{1,K-1}

\displaystyle\quad+2p(1-p)[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)]_{1,1}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-p)^{2}[\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)]_{1,3}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k-1)_{1,1}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k)_{1,1},

where the inequality is by the result demonstrated before. Therefore, we complete the induction and get that for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+1)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2k+2)_{1,1}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}+\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(3)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(4)_{1,1}

\displaystyle=(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})(1-3p(1-p)).

Since
\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(2)_{1,1}=0
, we obtain for

k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1}\geq(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})(1-3p(1-p)):=c_{p1}.

We provide an intuitive explanation from the perspective of random walks.
\bm{\Pi}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}
represents first taking a step according to the transition
\bm{\Pi}
, followed by
k
steps according to the transition
\bm{\Pi}^{\top}
. With increasing
k
, the distribution of the possible state after
k+1
steps is closer to uniform across all states, regardless of the starting state chosen, which can also be shown by Lemma
E.2
and
E.3
. Thus,
\bm{\Pi}(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\bm{e}_{1}
will converge to a vector corresponding uniform distribution, and
\bm{\Pi}\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\bm{e}_{1}
represents the most sparse case with the largest value concentrating on the first entry.

Combining all the results obtained above, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{\top}\right]-\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\bm{\Pi}^{i}\cdot(\bm{\Pi}^{\top})^{k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}(N)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)\bm{e}_{1}-\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}(N)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)\bm{e}_{1}

\displaystyle\quad=\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}+\sum_{i=2}^{K}\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{i,1}-\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1}-\sum_{i=2}^{K}\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{i,1}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1})+\min_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}(1-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1})-\max_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}(1-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1})

\displaystyle\quad=\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}(\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}-\min_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\})-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1}(\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}-\max_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\})

\displaystyle\qquad+\min_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}-\max_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}

\displaystyle\quad\geq(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(1)_{1,1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{2}(k)_{1,1})(\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,1}-\min_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\})+\min_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}-\max_{2\leq i\leq K}\{\bm{\Gamma}(N)_{1,i}\}

\displaystyle\quad\geq c_{p1}\left((1-p)^{N_{0}-2}-c_{p0}\exp(-N_{0})\right)-c_{p0}\exp(-N_{0})

\displaystyle\quad\geq C_{p},

where
C_{p}
is a positive constant related to
p
.
∎
