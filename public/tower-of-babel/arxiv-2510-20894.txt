Abstract
We compute thermal holographic correlators by combining their analytic structure with the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) condition and a finite set of multi-stress tensor OPE coefficients determined from the dual AdS description. We focus on two-point functions of identical scalar operators with integer conformal dimensions at zero spatial separation. For correlators holographically dual to black brane backgrounds, we show that our results satisfy all relevant consistency conditions and agree with numerical solutions of the bulk wave equation. We further demonstrate that the expansion in generalized free field correlators proposed in Barrat:2025nvu admits a natural interpretation in terms of Witten diagrams. Finally, we extend our analysis to spherically symmetric black hole backgrounds and show that higher-curvature corrections can be incorporated within the same framework.

In this work, we employ analytic bootstrap techniques to determine two-point functions of scalar operators with integer scaling dimensions
\Delta_{\phi}
in AdS black brane and spherical black hole backgrounds.
Specializing to the zero spatial separation limit shown in Figure
1
, we demonstrate that the correlators can be reconstructed from two simple ingredients: (1) the knowledge of the spectrum, and (2) a finite set of multi-stress tensor OPE coefficients.
Within this framework, the double-trace contributions arise automatically and cancel the poles that appear in multi-stress tensor coefficients.
For each correlator, the required OPE data do not mix with the double-trace sector and remain finite even at integer
\Delta_{\phi}
, thereby resolving the long-standing obstruction encountered in earlier approaches.
The two-point functions can be determined exactly for arbitrary integer
\Delta_{\phi}
using the following expansion in GFF correlators:

\langle\phi(\tau)\phi(0)\rangle_{\beta}=\sum_{\Delta<2\Delta_{\phi}}\frac{a_{\Delta}}{\beta^{\Delta}}g_{\text{GFF}}\left(\Delta_{\phi}-\frac{\Delta}{2},\tau\right)+\text{const}.\,.

(1)

Importantly, the sum is finite and thus, as mentioned above, only a finite set of OPE coefficients – which all belong to the multi-stress tensor sector – are needed to fully fix the correlators.
We argue that this expression is valid both for the black brane and black hole backgrounds.
In the first case, we compare our results to numerical solutions of the scalar wave equation and find very good agreement.
Moreover, we show that higher-curvature corrections also fit into our framework by appropriately modifying the multi-stress tensor OPE coefficients.
Finally, we present evidence that the analytic bootstrap admits a natural AdS interpretation as an expansion in graviton modes around the thermal AdS background.

2
Black brane background

This section is dedicated to the analysis of holographic two-point functions in the presence of a black brane background.
We start by briefly reviewing the analytic bootstrap framework for thermal correlators established in
Barrat:2025nvu
from the CFT side, as well as the specific features of the holographic setup arising on the weakly-coupled gravity side, such as the spectrum of operators and the OPE coefficients of the stress-tensor sector.
Specializing to the four-dimensional case, we then use these techniques to derive two-point functions of scalar operators with integer scaling dimension
\Delta_{\phi}
in the zero spatial separation limit, using a finite set of dynamical input from holography to fully fix the correlator.
We show that our results satisfy all the consistency conditions expected to hold for holographic two-point functions.
Moreover, we compare them to a numerical solution of the wave equation, following the method of
Parisini:2023nbd
, and find very good agreement for
\Delta_{\phi}=3
.
We then provide an interpretation of our correlators in terms of Witten diagrams, performing a small temperature expansion of the bulk metric.
In this context, we find a natural splitting between perturbative and non-perturbative sectors of the correlator, with the perturbative part matching our bootstrap results for integer
\Delta_{\phi}
.
The framework of
Barrat:2025nvu
can also be used to study the bouncing singularities of the multi-stress tensor sector, for which we find a perfect match with previous studies
Fidkowski:2003nf
;
Ceplak:2024bja
.
We conclude by showing that higher-curvature corrections can be incorporated in the bootstrap framework and provide explicit correlators for this case as well.

2.1
Thermal two-point functions

In this paper we study two-point functions of identical scalar operators at finite temperature.
We express the correlators in the Matsubara (or imaginary time) formalism
Matsubara:1955ws
, which amounts to considering the CFT correlator on
S^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
In this formalism, the signature is Euclidean and
\beta=1/T
is both the inverse temperature of the system and the length of the thermal circle.
It is convenient to define

g(\tau,x)=\langle\,\phi(0,0)\,\phi(\tau,x)\,\rangle_{S^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\,,

(2)

with
x\equiv|\vec{x}|
.
In this work we mainly focus on the case where the two operators are inserted at zero spatial separation, i.e.,
x=0
.
To streamline the notation we define
g(\tau)\equiv g(\tau,0)
.

Thermal two-point functions satisfy a number of structural properties, which we use as
bootstrap axioms
of the problem. We summarize them in the following.

OPE.

The OPE remains valid on the geometry
S^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with the limitation that it converges only within a ball of maximal radius, i.e.,
\tau^{2}+x^{2}<\beta^{2}
.
Provided that these conditions are satisfied, we can expand the two-point function in thermal OPE blocks
Iliesiu:2018fao
:

g(\tau,x)=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{a_{\mathcal{O}}}{\beta^{\Delta}}C_{J}^{(\nu)}\!\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}+x^{2}}}\right)\left(\tau^{2}+x^{2}\right)^{\Delta/2-\Delta_{\phi}}\,,

(3)

where
\Delta
and
J
are respectively the scaling dimension and spin of the exchanged operators
{\mathcal{O}}
, while
\nu=\frac{d-2}{2}
and
a_{\mathcal{O}}=\frac{J!}{2^{J}(\nu)_{J}}f_{\mathcal{O}\phi\phi}b_{\mathcal{O}}
.
Here
f_{\mathcal{O}\phi\phi}
is the zero temperature three-point function and
b_{\mathcal{O}}
is defined as the thermal one-point function coefficient via
Iliesiu:2018fao
;
Marchetto:2023fcw

\langle\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{J}}\rangle_{S^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\equiv\langle\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{J}}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{b_{\mathcal{O}}}{\beta^{\Delta}}\left(e^{\mu_{1}}\ldots e^{\mu_{J}}-\text{traces}\right)\,,

(4)

with
e^{\mu}
the unit vector whose only non-zero component is at
\mu=0
, and
C_{J}^{(\nu)}
is a Gegenbauer polynomial.
For
x=0
, the OPE reduces to

g(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}\left(\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)^{\Delta}\,,\qquad\text{with }a_{\Delta}=\sum_{\mathcal{O}:\,\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}=\Delta}a_{\mathcal{O}}\,C_{J}^{(\nu)}(1)\,.

(5)

KMS condition.

The two-point function of identical scalars satisfies periodicity (or KMS condition) around the thermal circle
Kubo:1957mj
;
Martin:1959jp
.
Combining the KMS condition with the transformation
\tau\to-\tau
, which is always a symmetry in a CFT,

If the CFT is not parity-invariant, it may be necessary to accompany this transformation with a reflection in some spatial coordinates

.
one finds that the two-point function satisfies

g(\tau)=g(\beta-\tau)\,.

(6)

Analytic structure and GFF expansion.

The analytic structure of thermal correlation functions is a crucial ingredient that enabled the derivation of a thermal inversion formula
Iliesiu:2018fao
and the crossing-symmetric inversion of single operators in the OPE
Alday:2020eua
;
Barrat:2025nvu

In this paper we focus on two-point functions of identical scalars at zero spatial separation and with a spectrum consisting only of operators with integer conformal dimensions.
Here we spell out the analytic structure and its consequences in the complex
\tau
-plane for this special case.

The fact that all conformal dimensions are integers implies that the correlator
g(\tau)
is analytic except for the Matsubara poles, i.e., poles that are located at
\tau=\beta k
,

This situation is a special case of the general analysis in

g(\tau)=\sum_{\Delta}\frac{a_{\Delta}}{\beta^{\Delta}}g_{\text{GFF}}\left(\Delta_{\phi}-\frac{\Delta}{2},\tau\right)+g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)\,,

(7)

where
g_{\text{GFF}}
corresponds to the GFF correlator of a scalar of conformal dimension
\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta/2
,
9
9

9

We note that the convention adopted in this paper differs slightly from that of
Barrat:2025nvu
, where the GFF expansion is denoted as
g_{\text{GFF}}\!\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta,\tau\right)
instead of
g_{\text{GFF}}\!\left(\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta/2,\tau\right)

defined as

g_{\text{GFF}}(\Delta,\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2\Delta}}\left[\zeta_{H}\!\left(2\Delta,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\!\left(2\Delta,1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\;,

(8)

and where
\zeta_{H}
denotes the Hurwitz-

As already noticed in

In this paper we apply (

) to holographic setups, showing that it can predict exact correlators as functions of a

Boundedness conditions.

The correlator is bounded in the complex
\tau
-plane by its value on the real axis

,

|g(\tau)|\;\geq\;|g(\tau+i\eta)|\,,\qquad\tau,\eta\in\mathbb{R}\,,

(9)

and also by its value on the imaginary axis

|g(i\eta)|\;\geq\;|g(\tau+i\eta)|\,,\qquad\tau,\eta\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(10)

Clustering condition.

At infinite volume, the two-point function is expected to satisfy the clustering conditions

g(\tau,x)\;\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\;\langle\phi\rangle_{\beta}^{2}\,,\qquad g(t=i\tau,x)\;\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\;\langle\phi\rangle_{\beta}^{2}\,.

(11)

In
Barrat:2025nvu
it was shown that, if all the consistency conditions listed above are satisfied by the expansion in GFF correlators, then
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)=0
.
Clustering – and in particular the second condition – plays an important role in fixing the arcs to zero.

2.2
Holographic thermal correlators

We specialize our study to the case of holographic two-point functions in the presence of a black brane background in asymptotically AdS
d+1
spacetime.
The gravity dual is given by a free massive scalar field in the bulk, which amounts to studying the (Euclidean) action

S=\int\text{d}^{d+1}y\,\sqrt{g}\,\left(\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{grav}}\right)+S_{\text{bdry}}\,,

(12)

with

\mathcal{L}_{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}g^{MN}\partial_{M}\Phi\partial_{N}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\Phi^{2}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{grav}}=R+\Lambda+\ldots\,,

(13)

where the ellipsis denotes possible higher-derivative curvature corrections and
S_{\text{bdry}}
denotes the Gibbons-Hawking boundary term.
Here
y^{M=0,1,\ldots,d}=\{z,\tau,\vec{x}\}
.
The bulk field
\phi
is dual to the scalar operator in the boundary theory.
The holographic dictionary relates the mass of the scalar field in AdS to the conformal dimension of the scalar field on the boundary
\phi(\tau,x)
through

m^{2}=\Delta_{\phi}(\Delta_{\phi}-d)\,.

(14)

Here we set the AdS radius
\ell
to unity.
The rotationally invariant and stationary metric in Euclidean signature corresponding to a black brane in AdS can be written as

\text{d}s^{2}=\frac{1}{z^{2}}\left(\frac{\text{d}z^{2}}{h(z)}+f(z)\,\text{d}\tau^{2}+\text{d}x^{2}\right)\,.

(15)

The asymptotic AdS boundary conditions further constrain the form of the functions
f(z)
and
h(z)
, in particular

f(z)=1-f_{0}z^{d}+\ldots\ ,\hskip 28.45274pth(z)=1-h_{0}z^{d}+\ldots\ .

(16)

The ellipsis are related to higher-derivative curvature corrections and are discussed in Section
2.7
.
In
Fitzpatrick:2019zqz
it was shown that conformal invariance on the boundary necessarily imposes
f_{0}=h_{0}
.
Restricting ourselves to
f_{0}
corresponds to the case of the AdS-Schwarzschild black brane, for which the horizon
z_{\text{h}}
is related to
f_{0}
and to the inverse temperature of the black brane via

f_{0}=z_{\text{h}}^{d}=\left(\frac{d\beta}{4\pi}\right)^{d}\,.

(17)

The two-point functions in the boundary theory can be computed by solving the equations of motion for the scalar propagator
\Phi(z,\tau,x)
in the AdS black brane background, namely,

\left(\Box-m^{2}\right)\Phi(z,\tau,x)=0\,,

(18)

and taking the limit

g(\tau,x)=\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{\Phi(z,\tau,x)}{z^{\Delta_{\phi}}}\,.

(19)

Solving this equation analytically in the presence of a black hole background is challenging; We refer to
Aminov:2020yma
;
Dodelson:2022yvn
;
Dodelson:2023vrw
and references therein for further discussions and results.
In the following we discuss the spectrum of the OPE
\phi\times\phi
in the black brane background, as it plays an essential role in our bootstrap method.

Black brane spectrum from gravity.

At leading order in
1/C_{T}
(with
C_{T}
the central charge of the CFT), the gravity action (
12
) is effectively classical. This means that quantum gravity effects are absent and the only fields appearing in the bulk are bound states of two scalar fields and multi-particle states of gravitons.
On the dual CFT side, the former correspond to the sector of operators appearing in a (generalized) free scalar OPE – namely operators with conformal dimensions
\Delta=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+J
, corresponding to double-twist (or
double-trace
) operators; the latter to multi-stress tensor operators in the CFT at finite temperature.
The expected spectrum appearing in the OPE can therefore be summarized to:

\displaystyle\mathds{1}:

\displaystyle\Delta=0,\ J=0\,,

\displaystyle[\phi\phi]_{n,J}:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,J}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+J\,,

(20)

\displaystyle[T^{n}]:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=dn,\quad J_{n}=0,2,\ldots,dn/2\qquad(n\geq 1)\,.

It was pointed out in
Fitzpatrick:2019zqz
that, from the bulk point of view, the equation of motion (
18
) can be solved at small
\tau,x
by inputting the form of the OPE (
3
) and in particular the spectrum (
20
).
This is, however, only possible in the case where double-trace and multi-stress tensor operators do not mix, i.e., when
\Delta_{\phi}
is not an integer.
This method was applied to obtain the following multi-stress tensor OPE coefficients in four dimensions, assuming no higher-order curvature corrections, i.e.,
f_{k}=h_{k}=0
except

\displaystyle a_{4,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{f}_{0}\Delta_{\phi}}{120}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle a_{8,4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{f}_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\phi}\left(7\Delta_{\phi}^{2}+6\Delta_{\phi}+4\right)}{201600(\Delta_{\phi}-2)}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle a_{8,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{f}_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\phi}\left(7\Delta_{\phi}^{3}-23\Delta_{\phi}^{2}+22\Delta_{\phi}+12\right)}{201600(\Delta_{\phi}-3)(\Delta_{\phi}-2)}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle a_{8,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{f}_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\phi}\left(7\Delta_{\phi}^{4}-45\Delta_{\phi}^{3}+100\Delta_{\phi}^{2}-80\Delta_{\phi}+48\right)}{201600(\Delta_{\phi}-4)(\Delta_{\phi}-3)(\Delta_{\phi}-2)}\,,

where the coefficients are labeled by the conformal dimension and the spin of the operator contributing in the OPE as

We have also defined

These coefficients do not admit a known closed form in terms of the scaling dimensions and spins of the multi-stress tensor operators.
The OPE coefficients associated with double-trace operators remain elusive, as one needs to impose additional boundary conditions on the bulk propagator
\Phi(\tau,x,z)

These results are consistent with the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH), which states that a thermal two-point function can be computed as a zero-temperature heavy-light-light-heavy correlator

Exact two-point functions for integer
\Delta_{\phi}

We now study correlators in the black brane background for the case

According to the AdS/CFT dictionary, the parameters of the
4d
CFT (the coupling constant
\lambda=g_{\text{YM}}^{2}N

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\ell^{4}}{2\ell_{s}^{4}}\,,\qquad\qquad N=\frac{\ell^{4}}{4\pi g_{s}\ell_{s}^{4}}\,,

Keeping this map and the OPE data derived from the gravity side in mind, we now turn to the finite temperature CFT perspective.
Our approach consists in applying the formula in (

Two distinct cases arise:

\star

Non-integer external dimension
\Delta_{\phi}

In this case the expansion in (

Since we do not have analytic control over all the (thermal) OPE coefficients of these operators, exact results are not available.
Nevertheless, one can approximate their contributions by using the asymptotic values at large dimensions, as first shown in

Integer external dimension

In this case the expansion in (

To make this point more explicit, consider a strongly-coupled theory such as the

In this case, one could still expand the correlator in terms of GFF blocks, as we do here; one would however immediately encounter spurious contributions associated with the classical, i.e., without anomalous dimensions, double-trace operators.

An even clearer illustration arises in the

\phi^{2}

\Delta_{\phi^{2}}=1

\Delta_{\sigma}=2

m_{\text{th}}
– cancels the
\phi^{2}

In contrast, for the holographic correlators studied in this work, the situation is different: The GFF expansion already reproduces all the physical operators in the theory, so there are no spurious terms to cancel.
While there exist specific values of the coefficients
a_{[T^{n}]}
corresponding to the holographic setup, setting
a_{[T^{n}]}=0
still yields a consistent (pure GFF) solution.
Hence, within the present framework it is not possible to further constrain the thermal OPE coefficients.
However, additional constraints might emerge by considering correlators at non-zero spatial separation, or by including
1/N

2.5
Bulk interpretation through Witten diagrams

We now discuss an interpretation of our bootstrap results in terms of Witten diagrams.
In particular, we show a one-to-one correspondence between our expansion in GFF correlators and an expansion of the action around the thermal AdS background for the case of the Schwarzschild black brane.

Perturbative expansion around thermal AdS.

Inspired by the form of our correlators, we perform an expansion of the action around thermal AdS.
We define the expansion parameter

\varepsilon=\frac{\ell}{\beta}\,,

(37)

with
\ell
the AdS radius, which we keep explicit to allow dimensional analysis.
For small
\varepsilon
, the black brane metric can be expanded in powers of
\varepsilon^{d}
around the thermal AdS metric as follows:

15

15

Note that
h^{(d)}
is traceless.

\displaystyle g_{MN}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{AdS}\,MN}+\varepsilon^{d}h_{MN}^{(d)}+\varepsilon^{2d}h_{MN}^{(2d)}+\ldots\,,

(38)

\displaystyle g^{MN}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{AdS}}^{MN}+\varepsilon^{d}h^{(d)\,MN}+\varepsilon^{2d}\left(h^{(d)\,M}_{\phantom{(d)\,M}P}h^{(d)\,PN}-h^{(2d)\,MN}\right)+\ldots\,.

(39)

12

Non-perturbative effects and arcs.

It is well known that the OPE in momentum space needs to be corrected with non-perturbative contributions, which become relevant at low frequencies while being exponentially suppressed at high frequencies
Caron-Huot:2009ypo
;
Manenti:2019wxs
.
Since the connection between the momentum space OPE and the dispersion relation is pointed out in
Barrat:2025nvu
, here we focus on the relation between the arc contributions and the non-perturbative corrections at small frequencies.

Since the expansion in thermal AdS discussed above admits the interpretation of a high-frequency expansion, we expect these contributions to correspond to non-perturbative effects in that setup.
Indeed, during the calculation we made the crucial approximation of extending the integration region from
z_{\text{h}}
to
\infty
in the Witten diagrams.
If
z_{\text{h}}
is large but finite, this approximation is justified because the corrections are exponentially suppressed.
More precisely, consider the full action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z_{\text{h}}}\!\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d-1}x\,\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d-1}x\,\left(\mathcal{L}_{\text{thAdS}}+\ldots\right)-\int_{z_{\text{h}}}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d-1}x\,\mathcal{L}\,.

(56)

The first term corresponds to our calculation, while the second term is difficult to evaluate exactly and encodes the non-perturbative effects at high frequencies.
It is possible however to give an estimation of its contribution.
The simplest way is to observe that

\int\mathcal{D}[\phi]\exp\left(-\int_{z_{\text{h}}}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d-1}x\,\mathcal{L}\right)\;\sim\;e^{-\left(F_{\text{thAdS}}-F\right)}\;\sim\;e^{-\mathcal{S}}\;\sim\;\mathcal{O}\bigl(e^{-\varepsilon^{d-1}}\bigr)\,,

(57)

where here
\mathcal{S}
is the entropy of the black brane.
This estimation highlights the fact that this contribution is non-perturbative in
\varepsilon
.
In different words, the expansion around thermal AdS that reproduces the GFF expansion (
7
) does not (perturbatively) take into account horizon effects.

A yet more accurate estimation of the non-perturbative contributions can be obtained by considering

\int_{z_{\text{h}}}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d-1}x\,\mathcal{L}\,,

(58)

expanded near the horizon, i.e., at
z\sim z_{\text{h}}
.
It is straightforward to estimate the leading order contribution.
At this order, the equation of motion reduces to

\psi_{\omega,k}^{\prime}(z)+\frac{\ell^{2}}{4z_{\text{h}}}\left(\frac{m^{2}\ell^{2}}{z_{\text{h}}^{2}}+k^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)\psi_{\omega,k}(z)=0\,,

(59)

where we have defined

\Phi(z,\tau,x)=T\sum_{n}\int\frac{\text{d}^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}e^{\omega_{n}\tau+ikx}\psi_{\omega,k}(z)\,,\quad\omega_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}\,.

(60)

The solution of (
59
) is

\psi_{\omega,k}(z)=\psi_{c}\exp\!\left[-\frac{z\ell^{2}}{4z_{\text{h}}}\left(\frac{m^{2}\ell^{2}}{z_{\text{h}}^{2}}+k^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)\right]\,,

(61)

with
\psi_{c}
a constant fixed by boundary conditions.
The contribution of the bulk-to-boundary propagator given above to the thermal two-point function of the boundary CFT is therefore

g_{\text{n.p.}}(k,\omega)\sim e^{-(k^{2}+\omega^{2})}\,.

(62)

As a consequence,
g_{\text{n.p.}}
precisely captures the non-perturbative terms in momentum space as expected from the arguments above.
Since the perturbative expansion in
\varepsilon
matches the GFF expansion in (
7
), we identify

g_{\text{n.p.}}(\tau,x)\;=\;g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau,x)\,.

(63)

This gives a novel interpretation of
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau,x)
as encoding horizon effects, which are not captured by the GFF expansion.

In the correlators considered in this paper we fix
\Delta_{\phi}
to be integer; we have already noted that in this case only a finite number of contributions appear in the expansion (
7
).
At the same time, we also argued that
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)=0
.
This, however, does not imply that horizon effects vanish.
Rather, the vanishing of
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)
is due to the fact that we are integrating
g_{\text{n.p.}}(k,\omega)
over spatial momenta: The prediction is that this integral vanishes when the external operator has integer dimension (i.e., at zero spatial separation), but
g_{\text{n.p.}}(k,\omega)
itself is non-zero.
An interesting direction, currently under investigation, is the extension of the present analysis to non-zero spatial separation,
x\neq 0
, as proposed in
Barrat:2025nvu
.
In this case, we expect
g_{\text{arcs}}(x,\tau)
to be non-vanishing.
18
18

18

A simple way to see this is that only a finite number of operators contribute in the generalized method of images, which is insufficient to reproduce the expected exponential decay at large

This expectation can in principle be tested by employing exact momentum-space formulae
Aminov:2020yma
;
Dodelson:2022yvn
;
Dodelson:2023vrw
and/or by developing a more precise version of the near-horizon expansion, which we have only sketched here.
We hope to report our results in future work.

2.6
Bouncing singularities for integer
\Delta_{\phi}

In
Ceplak:2024bja
the authors showed that summing over multi-stress tensor operators in a holographic model of the type studied in this paper produces poles in the complex
\tau
-plane (see
Valach:2025saf
and references therein for a complete introduction).
These poles correspond precisely to the so-called bouncing singularities in AdS
Fidkowski:2003nf
;
Festuccia:2005pi
, which were first identified through the geodesic approximation.
Such singularities are unphysical, in the sense that a generic two-point function should only exhibit the Matsubara poles.
In this paper we discuss how this observation is consistent with (and in fact directly related to) the analytic formulas and expansions provided in
Barrat:2025nvu
(see also Figure
2
).
For half-integer dimensions we show in Appendix
C
that the only operators contributing to the dispersion relations are the multi-stress tensor operators.
Summing over them generates the extra poles, whose presence implies that the contribution from the arcs is no longer constant:
19
19

19

The appearance of this pole violates the assumption, made in
Barrat:2025nvu
, that the analytic structure of the dispersion relation matches that of the full correlator; without this hypothesis, the theorem predicting constant arc terms does not hold.
instead, it must cancel the poles, as explicitly shown for the asymptotic model in
Ceplak:2024bja
;
Buric:2025anb
and using the general formulas of
Barrat:2025nvu
in Appendix
C
.

When the conformal dimension takes integer values, the situation changes qualitatively. In this case, some multi-stress tensor operators and double-twist operators become indistinguishable in the OPE, as they share the same conformal dimensions and this degeneracy obscures the application of methods analogous to those employed in
Buric:2025anb
. The dispersion relation, however, circumvents this difficulty by effectively eliminating the contributions of double-twist operators and isolating those of the multi-stress tensors. Consequently, only a finite number of multi-stress tensor operators contribute to the dispersion relation, which is precisely what enables the analytic results derived in this work.

However, the distinction between integer and non-integer conformal dimensions does not appear in the geodesic calculation.
In this section, we show that the bouncing singularity persists in the integer dimension case when we neglect double-trace operators, and that it can be seen directly from the expansion

g(\tau)=\sum_{\Delta}\frac{a_{\Delta}}{\beta^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\left[\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta,1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]+\kappa\,,

(64)

where the sum includes only the multi-stress tensor operators.
When
\Delta_{\phi}
is an integer, one observes that for
4n>2\Delta_{\phi}

\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-4n,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-4n,1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)=0\,,

(65)

with
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It would however in this context not be correct to conclude that their contribution vanishes, since the corresponding OPE coefficients diverge for
\Delta_{\phi}\in\mathbb{N}
.
This can be seen by considering the large
\Delta
asymptotics of the OPE coefficients associated with multi-stress tensors, as derived in
Ceplak:2024bja
:

a_{4n}=\frac{\pi}{20}(-4)^{n}n^{2\Delta_{\phi}-3}\left(\frac{4^{2\Delta_{\phi}}\Delta_{\phi}^{2}(\Delta_{\phi}-1)}{\Gamma\left(2\Delta_{\phi}+\tfrac{3}{2}\right)}\right)\csc(\pi\Delta_{\phi})\,,

(66)

Note that
\csc(\pi\Delta_{\phi})
diverges whenever
\Delta_{\phi}\in\mathbb{N}
.
More precisely, the function behaves as

\csc(\pi x)\sim\frac{(-1)^{\Delta_{\phi}}}{\pi(x-\Delta_{\phi})}+\ldots\,,\qquad\forall\Delta_{\phi}\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(67)

while the Hurwitz
\zeta
function behaves as follows when
x
is a positive integer

\zeta_{H}\left(2x-\Delta,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\approx\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)-2(x-\Delta_{\phi})\left.\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\zeta_{H}\left(y,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\right|_{y=2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta}+\ldots\,.

(68)

Therefore, since the conformal dimension
\Delta
is always even for a multi-stress tensor in
d=4
, the product of OPE coefficients and
\zeta_{H}
functions produces a
finite
contribution in the multi-stress tensor sector.
This finite piece is precisely responsible for the pole corresponding to the bouncing singularity. To make this concrete, we consider
\Delta_{\phi}=2
in the following.
Taking the correct limit and using the definition of the Hurwitz
\zeta
function, one finds

\sum_{n}\frac{a_{4n}}{\beta^{4}}\left[\zeta_{H}\left(4-4n,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\left(4-4n,1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\sim\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\beta^{4}\log\,\bigl(|\tau/\beta+m|\bigr)}{\left[4(\tau+m\beta)^{4}+\beta^{4}\right]^{2}}\,.

(69)

This expression exhibits poles at the locations of the bouncing singularities.
Their position depends as expected only on the number of spacetime dimensions and not on the conformal dimension of the external operators.
Furthermore, around
\tau_{c}=\pm\frac{\beta}{2}\pm i\frac{\beta}{2}
, we have

\sum_{n}\frac{a_{4n}}{\beta^{4}}\left[\zeta_{H}\left(4-4n,\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\left(4-4n,1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\sim\frac{1}{(\tau-\tau_{c})^{2}}\,,

(70)

where the power
2\Delta_{\phi}-2=2
matches the prediction of
Ceplak:2024bja
.
This observation is consistent with the formula given in (
27
) since there we do not neglect the double-trace contributions and work with finite OPE coefficients.

2.7
Higher-curvature corrections

We now conclude our study of the black brane background by considering higher-order curvature corrections.
Such extensions may correspond to different types of gravitational theories
Lovelock:1971yv
;
Myers:2009ij
;
Camanho:2009vw
;
deBoer:2009gx
as well as stringy corrections with
\alpha^{\prime}\sim 1/\sqrt{\lambda}
as a parameter.
In order to preserve conformal symmetry, they can only modify the functions
f(z)
and
h(z)
presented in (
16
).
We parameterize them as an expansion around the black brane metric,

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=1-f_{0}z^{4}-f_{4}z^{8}-f_{8}z^{12}-\ldots\,,

(71)

\displaystyle h(z)

\displaystyle=1-h_{0}z^{4}-h_{4}z^{8}-h_{8}z^{12}-\ldots\,,

(72)

with
f_{0}=h_{0}
needed as before for consistency with conformal symmetry.
There is no such constraint, however, on
f_{4}
,
h_{4}
, and higher-order terms.
It is also important to note that, from the perspective of the thermal CFT, those corrections do not induce anomalous dimensions.
Thus only the thermal OPE coefficients are expected to be modified.

In order to obtain the correlators with modified thermal OPE coefficients, it is sufficient to consider (
30
)–(

3.1
Thermal two-point functions

We study two-point functions of identical scalar operators at finite temperature and finite volume.
More precisely, we consider correlators on the geometry
S^{1}_{\beta}\times S^{d-1}_{R}
.
We denote by
R
the radius of the
(d-1)
-dimensional sphere.
As in the infinite volume case, i.e.
S^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

g_{{\beta/R}}(\tau,x)=\langle\,\phi(0,0)\,\phi(\tau,x)\,\rangle_{\beta/R}\equiv\langle\,\phi(0,0)\,\phi(\tau,x)\,\rangle_{S_{\beta}^{1}\times S_{R}^{d-1}}\,.

(75)

As for the infinite volume case, we focus on the limit in which the two operators have zero spatial separation and denote the correlator by
g_{{\beta/R}}(\tau)\equiv g_{{\beta/R}}(\tau,0)

In this section we discuss the adaptation of the bootstrap axioms of Section
2.1
to the finite volume case.

OPE.

The OPE remains valid on the geometry
S^{1}_{\beta}\times S^{d-1}_{R}
but converges only when
\tau^{2}+x^{2}<\min\!\left(\beta^{2},\,4\pi^{2}R^{2}\right)
.
In the limit
x=0
, the two-point function admits the following expansion in thermal blocks:

g_{{\beta/R}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\sum_{\Delta}a_{\Delta}(\beta/R)\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{R}\right)\text{csch}\left(\frac{\tau}{2R}\right)\right]^{2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta}\,,

(76)

with

a_{\Delta}(\beta/R)=\sum_{{\mathcal{O}}:\,\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}=\Delta}f_{\phi\phi{\mathcal{O}}}b_{\mathcal{O}}(\beta/R)\left(\frac{J!}{2^{J}(\nu)_{J}}\right)C^{(\nu)}_{J}(1)\,.

(77)

The decomposition is justified by the fact that the equation (

Iliesiu:2018fao

\langle\,\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{J}}\,\rangle_{\beta/R}=\frac{b(\beta/R)}{\beta^{\Delta}}\left(e^{\mu_{1}}\ldots e^{\mu_{J}}-\text{traces}\right)\,.

(78)

Starting from this expression, it is possible to express the OPE similarly to the infinite volume case. Then, using finite volume variables leads to the expansion (

KMS condition.

The two-point function of identical scalars satisfies the KMS condition which, associated with time reversal symmetry, reads

;

g_{\beta/R}(\tau)=g_{\beta/R}(\beta-\tau)\,.

(79)

At finite volume there is an additional periodicity coming from the sphere geometry
S_{R}^{d-1}

In this work we focus on correlators at zero-spatial coordinates and thus leave the implication of this condition for future studies.

As in the infinite volume case, the correlator is bounded in the complex

At finite volume, the clustering condition no longer holds.

Fixing the constant arcs is a very interesting problem on its own and we leave this to future work.

Correlators in the presence of a black hole.

We consider, as before, a free massive scalar field in the bulk, described by the action (

3.3
Checks and applications

Note that unlike in the infinite volume case, finite volume correlators do not satisfy the clustering property (

As a result, the arc contributions

These arc terms are moreover compatible with the infinite volume limit since they are functions of
\beta/R
and vanish smoothly as

We can verify that our correlators satisfy the two boundedness conditions spelled out in (

In Figure

A final non-trivial check is the fact that the two-point function at finite volume should reproduce the one at infinite volume, i.e., the black brane, in the infinite volume limit.
Since our thermal two-point functions are made by a finite sum over GFF correlators it is enough to show that this is the case for the GFF correlator.

In the case of the black brane, we successfully compared our analytic solution with the numerical solution of the wave equation.
However, for the spherical black hole case, there are additional limitations which we discuss now.
First, obtaining an accurate numerical solution is technically more challenging due to the increased complexity of the wave equation.
Second, the analytic solutions for the black hole background are exact only up to an arbitrary function
\kappa_{\Delta_{\phi}}(\beta/R)
.
By fixing
\beta
and
R
, one could in principle tune this function to match the numerical result; however, such procedure reduces the discrepancy in an (almost) arbitrary way.
For this reason, we do not consider the numerical solution of the wave equation as a meaningful consistency check in this case.
24
24

24

It would be interesting to develop a more stable and precise way to solve the wave equation numerically.
Note that the fact that the solution is unique up to a constant in the kinematics is already a check of our correlators.

The Witten diagram interpretation discussed in Section
2.5
also differs in the black hole case.
The expansion proposed in Section
2.5
effectively corresponds to a large
\beta
expansion, which is less meaningful in the black hole background since solutions exist only for
\beta^{2}<\pi^{2}R^{2}/2
.
25
25

25

This can be seen from the fact that the square root in Equation (

\beta/R
.

Whether an alternative bulk interpretation of these blocks exists remains an open question, which we plan to revisit in future work.

Let us also comment that, according to the ETH, the thermal two-point function is related to a zero-temperature four-point function of the form

g_{{\beta/R}}(\tau,x)\sim\langle\,\mathcal{O}_{H}(\infty)\phi(z,{\bar{z}})\phi(1)\mathcal{O}_{H}(0)\,\rangle\,,

(102)

where the dimension of the heavy operator
\Delta_{H}
is taken to be large and

The holographic realization of this correspondence has been extensively studied in

;
Karlsson:2019dbd
;

;
Karlsson:2019qfi
;
Parnachev:2020fna
;

;
Karlsson:2020ghx
;
Karlsson:2021duj
;

However, the case in which the dimension of the light scalar operator

;
Li:2020dqm

Having analytic expressions for thermal two-point functions may thus prove useful, as they can be directly compared with heavy–heavy–light–light correlator data.
This provides an additional motivation to further investigate the thermal two-point function at non-zero spatial separation, where the degeneracy in spin is lifted.
In this regime, one could also identify which contributions are perturbative and which are non-perturbative in the large-spin expansion, potentially allowing new predictions for these correlation functions.

Finally, as discussed in Section
2.7

As in the black brane case, the operator spectrum remains unchanged; the only difference is that the OPE coefficients of the multi-stress tensor operators now depend on the Wilson coefficients multiplying the higher-curvature terms, or equivalently on the parameters

4
Conclusions

In this work we analyzed thermal two-point functions of scalar operators with integer scaling dimension
\Delta_{\phi}
, both at infinite volume and at finite volume on a sphere.
These two settings are holographically dual to AdS black brane and spherical black hole backgrounds, respectively.
Our approach is based on expanding thermal correlators at zero spatial separation in terms of GFF correlators,

g(\tau)=\sum_{\Delta}\frac{a_{\Delta}}{\beta^{\Delta}}g_{\text{GFF}}\left(\Delta_{\phi}-\frac{\Delta}{2},\tau\right)+g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)\,,

(103)

a representation that was rigorously established for finite temperature CFTs at infinite volume in
Barrat:2025nvu
.
In the present work we extended its validity to finite volume systems, under the condition that both external and exchanged operator dimensions are integers.

For holographic correlators, the expansion (
103
) truncates and only a finite set of multi-stress tensor operators contributes.
Furthermore, the bootstrap conditions uniquely fix the solution up to an additive constant, which is determined to vanish, thereby eliminating the arc contributions.
This simplification is a special feature of the integer
\Delta_{\phi}
case, whereas for non-integer
\Delta_{\phi}
the arcs remain non-trivial, as discussed in Appendix
C
.
We present explicit results for several low values of
\Delta_{\phi}
, though the method applies equally well to arbitrary integer dimensions.
To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first exact expressions for thermal holographic correlators in
d>2
as a function of the imaginary time.
We corroborate these results by solving the scalar wave equation numerically and find convincing agreement for the black brane case.
We also provide an AdS interpretation of (
103
) as an expansion of the metric in graviton modes around thermal AdS, and we verify this picture through explicit Witten diagram computations up to second order in the expansion parameter.
Finally, curvature corrections to the thermal correlators are also included in (
103
), as anomalous dimensions are suppressed with respect to
N
, i.e., only the OPE coefficients
a_{\Delta}
need to be corrected.

This work opens several directions for future research, which we list here:

\star

The results obtained in this work admit several natural extensions.
Beyond the kinematic regime
|\vec{x}|=0
, arc contributions are expected to play a non-trivial role, and the OPE contributions become disentangled in spin.
As noted in
Ceplak:2024bja
, the large
\Delta
behavior of the multi-stress tensor sector involves alternating signs that spoil analyticity in
\Delta
.
It would be interesting to explore the analyticity in the spin
J
, which would imply to study the correlator at non-vanishing spatial separation.
Additionally, it might be possible to determine the arc contributions for integer
\Delta_{\phi}
, in analogy with the procedure outlined in Appendix
C
;

\star

An important check for the non-zero spatial separation correlator would be to compare it with the exact momentum-space correlators computed in
Dodelson:2022yvn
.
The
\tau

Nevertheless, it is worth noting that integrating out the momentum modes reduces the correlator of

to the Fourier transform of GFF blocks, as discussed in
Barrat:2025nvu

The relation between these two exact results should be valuable and deserve to be explored more deeply;

As mentioned in Section

For holographic theories, this correspondence was partially verified perturbatively (and at large spin) in

The case in which

A deeper understanding of non-integer operator dimensions in the geometry

The main difficulty in this context is that, once CFT operators acquire anomalous dimensions, additional operators – potentially an infinite number – contribute to the dispersion relation. Nevertheless, it was shown in

Incorporating

From the bootstrap perspective, the two phases differ in their spectra: in the thermal AdS phase scalar two-point functions do not involve multi-stress tensor exchanges.

An interesting direction would be to devise bootstrap approaches that are sensitive to the phase transition itself.

Appendix A
GFF thermal correlators at finite volume

We provide here the derivation of the two-point function of a generalized free field of integer dimension
\Delta_{\phi}
as given in Equation (
86
).
It can obtained via the method of images from the correlator on the geometry
\mathbb{R}\times S^{d-1}_{R}
:

g_{\text{GFF},{\beta/R}}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau,\vec{\theta})=\frac{1}{2^{\Delta_{\phi}}R^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{\cosh((\tau+m\beta)/R)-\cos|\vec{\theta}|}\right]^{\Delta_{\phi}}\,.

(104)

We will set zero spatial separation
\vec{\theta}=0
to reduce to the problem treated in this work.

In what follows it will be convenient to introduce the ratio
\xi=\frac{\beta}{2\pi R}
: after setting
\beta=1
, we can rewrite the correlator as

g_{\text{GFF},\xi}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau)=\left(\pi\xi\right)^{2\Delta_{\phi}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\text{csch}\left[\pi\xi(\tau+m)\right]^{2\Delta_{\phi}}+(\tau\to 1-\tau)\ .

(105)

At this stage, we can make progress by choosing an integer external dimension
\Delta_{\phi}\in\mathbb{N}
. In terms of the parameter
q=e^{-2\pi\xi}
, standard computations allow to reshape the series (
105
) as

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\text{csch}\left[\pi\xi(\tau+m)\right]^{2\Delta_{\phi}}=\frac{4^{\Delta_{\phi}}}{\Gamma(2\Delta_{\phi})}\sum_{s=0}^{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}\frac{c(\Delta_{\phi},s)}{\log(q)^{s}}\frac{\text{d}^{s}}{\text{d}\tau^{s}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\ell\tau}}{1-q^{\ell}}\ ,

(106)

where we defined the
c(\Delta_{\phi},s)
coefficients, involving the Stirling numbers of the first kind
S_{n}^{(m)}
,

c(\Delta_{\phi},s)=\sum_{r=0}^{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}\binom{r}{s}S_{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}^{(r)}(\Delta_{\phi}-1)^{r-s}\ .

(107)

The only thing left to do is to evaluate the generalized Lambert series appearing in the right hand side of Equation (
106
). It can easily be reshaped as follows, where
q
-Polygamma function
\psi_{q}
is employed:

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\ell\tau}}{1-q^{\ell}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{\ell(\tau+j)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{\tau+j}}{1-q^{\tau+j}}=\frac{\psi_{q}(\tau)+\log(1-q)}{\log(q)}\ .

(108)

The action of the derivatives on the generalized Lambert series is then given by

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\text{csch}\left[\pi\xi(\tau+m)\right]^{2\Delta_{\phi}}=\frac{2^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}{\Gamma(2\Delta_{\phi})}\sum_{s=1}^{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}(-1)^{s+1}\frac{c(\Delta_{\phi},s)}{(2\pi\xi)^{s+1}}\psi_{e^{-2\pi\xi}}^{(s)}(\tau)\ ,

(109)

where we used the fact that the coefficient
c(\Delta_{\phi},0)
vanishes identically; this retroactively changes the definition of the coefficients to

c(\Delta_{\phi},s)=\sum_{r=1}^{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}\binom{r}{s}S_{2\Delta_{\phi}-1}^{(r)}(\Delta_{\phi}-1)^{r-s}\ .

(110)

We conclude by evaluating the sum appearing in the coefficients (
110
), which can be reshaped in terms of the derivatives of a falling factorial
26
26

26

The falling factorial
a^{(b)}
is defined as
a^{(b)}=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(a-b+1)}
; the falling factorial is implemented in
Mathematica
as the function
FactorialPower
[a,b]
. Notice the difference with the Pochhammer symbol
(a)_{b}=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)}
.

c(\Delta_{\phi},s)=\frac{1}{s!}\left[\frac{\text{d}^{s}}{\text{d}x^{s}}x^{(y)}\right]_{x=\Delta_{\phi}-1,y=2\Delta_{\phi}-1}\ .

(111)

The falling factorial can be shown to be a odd function of

\Delta_{\phi}-1

c(\Delta_{\phi},\ell)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\ell)}\left[\frac{\text{d}^{2\ell-1}}{\text{d}x^{2\ell-1}}x^{(y)}\right]_{x=\Delta_{\phi}-1,y=2\Delta_{\phi}-1}\ .

(112)

We are ready to write down the final result:

g_{\text{GFF},\xi}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\Delta_{\phi})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\Delta_{\phi}}\left(4\pi^{2}\xi^{2}\right)^{\Delta_{\phi}-\ell}c(\Delta_{\phi},\ell)\left[\psi_{e^{-2\pi\xi}}^{(2\ell-1)}(\tau)+\psi_{e^{-2\pi\xi}}^{(2\ell-1)}(1-\tau)\right]\ .

(113)

We reintroduce the dependence on
\beta
and

g_{\text{GFF},\beta/R}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2\Delta_{\phi}}\Gamma(2\Delta_{\phi})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\Delta_{\phi}}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)^{\Delta_{\phi}-\ell}c(\Delta_{\phi},\ell)\left[\psi_{e^{-\beta/R}}^{(2\ell-1)}\left(\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\psi_{e^{-\beta/R}}^{(2\ell-1)}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\ .

(114)

The infinite temperature limit.

We now consider the infinite temperature limit, equivalent to
\xi\to 0
in the chosen conventions. By reversing the definition (
108
) of the
q

\psi_{e^{-2\pi\xi}}^{(s)}(\tau)=-2\pi\xi\frac{\text{d}^{s}}{\text{d}\tau^{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-2\pi\xi(n+\tau)}}{1-e^{-2\pi\xi(n+\tau)}}\ .

(115)

In the limit, this function reduces to

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\psi_{e^{-2\pi\xi}}^{(s)}(\tau)=(-1)^{s+1}s!\,\zeta_{H}(s+1,\tau)\ .

(116)

In the formula (
114
), the only term in the sum over

\lim_{\xi\to 0}g_{\text{GFF},\xi}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau)=c(n_{\phi},n_{\phi})\left[\zeta_{H}(2\Delta_{\phi},\tau)+\zeta_{H}(2\Delta_{\phi},1-\tau)\right]\ .

g_{\text{GFF}}(\Delta_{\phi},\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2\Delta_{\phi}}}\left[\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi},\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)+\zeta_{H}\left(2\Delta_{\phi},1-\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)\right]\ .

Appendix B
3d

N

The goal of this appendix is to show that the expansion in (

\mathrm{O}(N)

N

d=3

After performing the Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation, the Lagrangian of the model can be written as

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\,\phi_{i}\phi_{i}\,,

(119)

where
\sigma
is the Hubbard–Stratonovich field.
The corresponding thermal two-point function
\langle\,\phi\phi\,\rangle_{\beta/R}

g_{\beta/R}(\tau)=\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2l+1)\,e^{\frac{2\pi in\tau}{\beta}}}{\frac{(2\pi n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{l(l+1)}{R^{2}}+m_{\text{th}}^{2}(\beta/R)}\,,

where

We now establish two facts:
(i) the two-point function in (

(ii) the coefficients of such an expansion can be interpreted as thermal OPE coefficients (at finite volume, thus depending on the dimensionless ratio
\beta/R
) corresponding to the operators appearing in the
\phi\times\phi

GFF decomposition.

Starting from (
120
), one can observe – by analogy with the infinite volume case – that the expansion of the correlator in powers of
m_{\text{th}}^{2}
is equivalent to an OPE expansion.
Indeed,

g_{{\beta/R}}(\omega_{n})=\sum_{m}\frac{m_{\text{th}}^{2m}(\beta/R)}{\Gamma(2m+1)}\,g_{\text{GFF},{\beta/R}}(1-2m,\omega_{n})\,,

(121)

where

g_{\text{GFF},{\beta/R}}(1-2m,\omega_{n})=(-1)^{m}\Gamma(2m+1)\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{l(l+1/2)}{R^{2}}\right)^{m+1}}\,,

(122)

following the general result obtained in Section 2.4.2 of
Barrat:2025nvu

Interpretation of the coefficients.

The interpretation of the coefficients in (
121
) follows as in the infinite volume case.
Since the spectrum does not change, the operators contributing to the OPE remain the same:

\displaystyle\text{Double-twist operators }[\phi\phi]_{n,J}:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,J}=2\Delta_{\phi}+2n+J\,,

\displaystyle\text{Scalar operators }\sigma^{m}:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma^{m}}=2m\,.

(123)

The identity operator is of course also present.
Other operators, related to those above by the equations of motion, may also appear.
For example,
\phi\sigma\phi
is equivalent to
\phi\Box\phi=[\phi\phi]_{1,0}
, since the equations of motion imply
\Box\phi\sim\sigma\phi
.
Operators such as
\phi\sigma\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\phi
have dimension
\Delta=2\Delta_{\phi}+2
and are degenerate with
\phi\Box\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\phi=[\phi\phi]_{1,2}
; even reinstating spatial dependence (and hence spin) does not lift this degeneracy.
Therefore, the relevant operators can all be recast as those in (
B
) (plus degenerate descendants).

Since the double-twist operators do not contribute, since their momentum space blocks vanish, the operators appearing in (
121

\sigma^{m}
.
The corresponding OPE coefficients are directly read from the expansion:

a_{\sigma^{m}}(\beta/R)=\frac{m_{\text{th}}^{2m}(\beta/R)}{\Gamma(2m+1)}\,.

(124)

The dependence of these one-point functions on the dimensionless ratio
\beta/R
is encoded in the thermal mass
m_{\text{th}}
, which acquires a finite volume correction depending on the sphere radius.
For large
R

m_{\text{th}}(\beta/R)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\log\varphi^{2}+\frac{1}{24\log\varphi^{2}}\,\frac{\beta^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{55+32\sqrt{5}\log\varphi^{2}}{28800\log^{3}\varphi^{2}}\frac{\beta^{4}}{R^{4}}+\ldots\right)\,.

(125)

It would be interesting to recover the OPE coefficients (
124

David:2024pir
for the lowest-lying operators.

For completeness, we note that obtaining a closed-form expression for (
120

However, when the correlator is restricted to the thermal circle, a closed form for
g_{\beta/R}(\omega_{n})
can be derived.
Finally, we can perform the sum over the spherical modes labelled by

\tilde{m}_{\text{th}}^{2}=m_{\text{th}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}
to rewrite the series as follows

\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{\frac{(2\pi n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{(l+1/2)^{2}}{R^{2}}+\tilde{m}_{\text{th}}^{2}}=\frac{1}{16\pi\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}\left[\frac{(2\pi n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\tilde{m}_{\text{th}}^{2}\right]+(2l+1)^{2}}

(126)

\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{\frac{(2\pi n)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{(l+1/2)^{2}}{R^{2}}+\tilde{m}_{\text{th}}^{2}}=\frac{1}{16\pi\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{a+(2l+1)^{2}}\,.

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{a+(2l+1)^{2}}

(129)

Appendix C
The asymptotic model

In this appendix, we reformulate the content of
Buric:2025anb
in the language of Section
2
.

Spectrum.

We focus on the case where the external operators have dimension
\Delta_{\phi}=3/2
and
d=4
.
In this case, the spectrum of the correlator is

\displaystyle\mathds{1}:

\displaystyle\Delta=0,\quad J=0\,,

\displaystyle[T^{n}]:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=4n\,,\quad J=0,2,\ldots,2n\,,

(131)

\displaystyle[\phi\phi]_{n,J}:

\displaystyle\Delta=3+2n+J\,.

The multi-stress tensor and double-twist operators’ conformal dimensions are never equal in this scenario, hence their OPE coefficients are never degenerate in the sense determined by (
5
). The OPE breaks down into three separate contributions

g(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau^{3}}\left[1+\sum_{n}a_{[T^{n}]}\left(\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)^{4n}+\sum_{\Delta(n,J)}a_{[\phi\phi]_{\Delta}}\left(\frac{\tau}{\beta}\right)^{\Delta}\right]\,.

(132)

The model is called asymptotic because it assumes this formula for the OPE coefficients of all the multi-stress tensor operators
Ceplak:2024bja
;
Buric:2025anb

a_{[T^{n}]}=-\alpha_{0}\frac{96\sqrt{\pi}}{175}(-4)^{n}\,,

(133)

with
\alpha_{0}\sim 1
.
27
27

27

The value of
\alpha_{0}
is not known but it was shown in
Ceplak:2024bja
to be close to
1
at large
\Delta
.

In a generic holographic model, this formula corresponds to the leading asymptotic for
a_{[T^{n}]}
at large
n
.

Expansion into GFF blocks.

We can apply the dispersion relation to the spectrum in the form of the GFF expansion in Equation (
7
).
We find that the identity and all multi-stress tensor operators contribute to the discontinuity, while the double-trace operators drop, explicitly

g(\tau)=g_{\text{GFF}}(3,\tau)+\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{[T^{n}]}\beta^{4n}g_{\text{GFF}}(3-4n,\tau)+g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)\,.

(134)

It is important to understand that this expression is expected to nevertheless recreate the spectrum of double-twist operators in the OPE (
3
).
Explicitly, the multi-stress tensor sector is given by

\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{[T^{n}]}\beta^{4n}g_{\text{GFF}}(3-4n,\tau)=-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\beta^{3}}\frac{96i\sqrt{\pi}}{175}\left(\frac{1}{\frac{\tau}{\beta}-\frac{1+i}{2}}-\frac{1}{\frac{\tau}{\beta}-\frac{1-i}{2}}\right)\,,

(135)

which is also found in
Buric:2025anb
taking images over the single blocks.

Unphysical pole, arcs, and uniqueness.

The first two terms in the right-hand side of Equation (
134
) cannot be the final answer, since they present poles located at

\tau=\frac{\beta\pm i\beta}{2}\,.

(136)

These poles are unphysical, as discussed in
Buric:2025anb
.
In fact, their presence violates our assumption of boundedness in Equation (
9
).
Notice that in
Barrat:2025nvu
the authors proved that
g_{\text{arcs}}
is a single constant if the function is bounded and periodic on the real axis (see Appendix A of
Barrat:2025nvu
).
However, as observed in
Buric:2025anb
;
Ceplak:2024bja
, the sum over multi-stress tensor operators produces an additional pole which prevents
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)
from being an entire function.
This is a violation of the assumption of
Barrat:2025nvu
and, as a consequence, the arcs cannot be only a constant.
Since the arcs have to present a pole themselves to compensate the unphysical poles, an infinite number of operators has to contribute to
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)
: in accordance with the principle of OPE compatibility, the arc contribution has to allow for an OPE decomposition; however, only the sum of an infinite amount of OPE terms can produce a divergence.
In holographic theories this extra, unphysical pole, is associated with the bouncing singularity in the holographic dual.

It was noted in
Buric:2025anb
that the problem of determining the arc contribution can be solved by subtracting the spurious pole and summing over images, which results in

g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)=\alpha_{0}\frac{192\pi^{3/2}}{175\beta^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{\pi(2i\tau/\beta+1)}}-\frac{1}{1+e^{\pi(2i\tau/\beta-1)}}\right)\,.

(137)

This procedure produces a consistent correlator as it is now free of poles and bounded in the sense of (
9
).
Moreover, the new correlator satisfies all the properties required and, by using the same argument as in Appendix A of
Barrat:2025nvu
, one can show that additional arcs are at most constant.
This constant can be fixed to zero by using clustering in real time, thus this correlator is
the only one
that satisfies our consistency conditions, given the input.

It is worth emphasizing a major advantage of the method presented here.
Each multi-stress tensor coefficient corresponds to a distinct block in the GFF expansion.
Since the arc contributions are associated with the poles generated by the infinite sum over multi-stress tensor coefficients, they depend only on the asymptotic behavior of the OPE data, not on their detailed low-lying structure.
Consequently, modifying a finite number of OPE coefficients does not affect any fundamental property of the two-point function.
Indeed, the GFF blocks are intrinsically KMS invariant, their analytic structure is independent of the OPE coefficients, and we have just argued that
g_{\text{arcs}}(\tau)
depends solely on the asymptotic behavior of
a_{[T^{n}]}
.
This implies that the asymptotic model can, in principle, be systematically improved by replacing the lowest-lying
a_{[T^{n}]}
with their exact values, which can be computed using the techniques of
Ceplak:2024bja
;
Fitzpatrick:2019zqz
.
Such refinements can also be incorporated following the method of
Buric:2025fye
; in fact, a similar strategy (without the asymptotic tail) was successfully implemented in
Buric:2025anb
.

Implications for asymptotic approximations.

It was observed in
Buric:2025anb
that the OPE coefficients associated with the correlator (
135
) do not exhibit the analytic behavior at large conformal dimension
\Delta\to\infty
predicted in
Marchetto:2023xap
.
This discrepancy can already be traced back to the input data: the multi-stress tensor OPE coefficients themselves display alternating signs, in contrast with the monotonic behavior expected from the analysis of
Marchetto:2023xap
.

To understand the origin of this difference, it is instructive to revisit the proof of the asymptotic prediction given in
Barrat:2025nvu
.
A key assumption in that argument is that the sum over GFF correlators yields a function with the correct analytic structure, so that the arc contributions are at most constant.
However, this assumption is violated in the asymptotic model of
Buric:2025anb
, due to the presence of the so-called
bouncing singularities
: in essence, the expression (
134
) implicitly includes an infinite number of operators in the arc contribution.

Nevertheless, the asymptotic behavior derived in
Marchetto:2023xap
still holds
on average
:
if one averages the thermal OPE density over a finite window in conformal dimension (of width
\delta\Delta\sim 2
, in the language of
Marchetto:2023xap
), the result continues to satisfy the expected asymptotic form.
From the perspective of
Barrat:2025nvu
, this can be understood as follows: the contribution of double-trace operators also effectively captures the next multi-stress tensor correction, approximating the gap in conformal dimension between the two as negligible.

On the other hand, the approximation of
Marchetto:2023xap
coincides – at least in some examples, such as the three-dimensional Ising model – with the large-spin perturbation theory of
Iliesiu:2018fao
(for a detailed explanation of this correspondence, see
Miscioscia:2025pjh
).
A breakdown of large-spin perturbation theory could imply that the correlator is not Regge-bounded, and consequently that the OPE coefficients fail to be analytic in spin.
The alternating sign pattern observed in (
133
) could be a manifestation of this phenomenon, although a more detailed analysis at non-zero spatial separation is required to determine whether the dominant contribution to the alternation arises from large or small spin.
We plan to return to this question in future work.

Appendix D
Numerical solution of the wave equation

In this section we review the solution of the wave equation in AdS, with a focus on the thermal scalar two-point function for an integer external dimension.
The AdS
d+1
black brane metric is given in (
15
) where we set the AdS radius to one. The wave equation for a massive field in this background takes the form (
18
).

D.1
Method

Near the boundary, the field behaves as

\displaystyle\Phi(z,\tau,x)=z^{d-\Delta_{\phi}}\phi_{1}(\tau,x)+\cdots+z^{\Delta_{\phi}}\phi_{2}(\tau,x)+\cdots\,,

(138)

and depending on the external dimension and the chosen boundary conditions, the boundary two-point function can be extracted from the ratio of the two leading terms.
The thermal correlator at zero spatial separation is then given by

\langle\,\phi(0)\phi(\tau)\,\rangle_{\beta}=\lim_{z\to 0}z^{-\min(\Delta_{\phi},\,d-\Delta_{\phi})}\Phi(z,\tau,0)\,.

(139)

Following
Parisini:2023nbd
;
Buric:2025fye
;
Buric:2025anb
, we decompose the scalar field as

\displaystyle\Phi(z,\tau,x)=\Psi(z,\tau,x)+\tilde{G}(z,\tau,x)\,,

(140)

where
\tilde{G}
is built from the bulk-to-boundary propagator in pure AdS,

\displaystyle G_{\text{AdS}}(z,\tau,x)=\frac{z^{\Delta_{\phi}}}{\big(\tau^{2}+x^{2}+z^{2}\big)^{\Delta_{\phi}}}\ ,

(141)

modified by replacing
\tau^{2}
with a periodic function
f(\tau)
. For the purposes of this numerical evaluation any periodic function of period
\beta
and reproducing the correct singularities would work. We used the one also employed in
Buric:2025anb
, which already proved to be reliable in extracting leading OPE coefficients.
This ensures that
\tilde{G}
reproduces the correct short-distance divergence. On the boundary (
z=0
) and at zero spatial separation (
x=0
), the contribution to the two-point function contains only the expected UV divergence at small
\tau
, corrected by higher-order terms as proposed in
Buric:2025fye
.

The remaining piece
\Psi
has the same asymptotics as the full field

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\tau,x)=z^{d-\Delta_{\phi}}\psi_{1}(\tau,x)+\cdots+z^{\Delta_{\phi}}\psi_{2}(\tau,x)+\cdots\,.

(142)

Thus, the full field can be written as

\displaystyle\Phi(z,\tau,x)=z^{d-\Delta_{\phi}}\psi_{1}(\tau,x)+\cdots+z^{\Delta_{\phi}}\left[\psi_{2}(\tau,x)+\left.\left(z^{-\Delta_{\phi}}\tilde{G}(z,\tau,x)\right)\right|_{z\to 0}\right]+\cdots\,.

(143)

We further define the auxiliary function
H(z,\tau,x)

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\tau,x)=z^{d-\Delta_{\phi}}H(z,\tau,x)\,.

(144)

For
0\leq d-\Delta_{\phi}<\Delta_{\phi}
, the boundary conditions are
Parisini:2023nbd
:

•

H|_{z=0}=0
, which enforces
\psi_{1}=0
;

•

\partial_{z}H|_{z=1}=0
, corresponding to regularity at the tip of the black brane cigar geometry;

•

\partial_{x}H|_{x=0}=0
, imposing regularity at the boundary origin;

•

H|_{x\to\infty}=0
, ensuring that the
\delta
-function source vanishes at infinity.

For
0\leq\Delta_{\phi}<d-\Delta_{\phi}
,
H(z,\tau,x)
is instead defined as follows

\displaystyle\Psi(z,\tau,x)=z^{\Delta_{\phi}}H(z,\tau,x)\,,

(145)

with boundary conditions

•

\partial_{z}H|_{z=0}=0
, enforcing
\psi_{2}=0
;

•

\partial_{z}H|_{z=1}=0
, regularity at the tip of the cigar;

•

\partial_{x}H|_{x=0}=0
, regularity at the boundary origin;

•

H|_{x\to\infty}=0
, vanishing of the
\delta
-function source at infinity.

In both cases, periodic boundary conditions along the
\tau
direction must also be imposed.

These ingredients allow one to numerically solve the wave equation.

D.3
Comments on the numerical accuracy

Even though a detailed analysis of the numerical errors in solving the wave equation is beyond the scope of this paper, it is still useful to gain some intuition and check that the numerical solution indeed reproduces the expected behaviour.
A validation case was discussed above (
d=4
,
\Delta_{\phi}=1
), but the lightest non-trivial correlator we are ultimately interested in is
d=4
,
\Delta_{\phi}=3
.
29

29

The case
\Delta_{\phi}=2

Therefore we consider
\Delta_{\phi}=3
to be a stronger check.

For this correlator, knowledge of
H
alone is not sufficient: its first and second derivatives are also required to compute the thermal two-point function.

A simple way to estimate the accuracy of the numerical solution is to quantify how well it satisfies the original differential equation.
Since derivatives of
H
enter into the computation, we also examine the behaviour of the first two derivatives of this numerical discrepancy.
The quality of the solution is relatively good, especially away from the singular points of the correlator.
However, the error increases significantly with the number of derivatives.
This is expected: even functions that are numerically small may exhibit large derivatives.

In particular, since the correlator in the case

in

is a linear combination of the first and second derivatives of

\Delta_{\phi}=1
, which does not require derivatives of

This also suggests that the discrepancy of order

shown in the main text is plausibly due to the numerical accuracy of the solution of the wave equation, rather than to any intrinsic inconsistency.

D.4
The spherical black hole case

The same procedure can also be applied to the case of the spherical black hole.
However, in this case
\tilde{f}_{0}
must be given by (
92
), which expresses it as a function of the ratio
\beta/R
, and the metric must be modified accordingly to describe a spherical horizon.
Moreover, the boundary conditions require an additional adjustment: for
d=4
and
\Delta_{\phi}=3
, the boundary condition at
R
must be replaced by the requirement of periodicity on the sphere.
The resulting problem is a well-defined partial differential equation and can, in principle, be solved numerically.
Nevertheless, the reality condition on
\tilde{f}_{0}
constrains the ratio
\beta/R
to be bounded from above.
In addition, numerical analysis indicates that the solution is less accurate than in the planar case – likely due to the more intricate structure of the differential equation and the subtleties of the boundary conditions – which results in a poorer overall numerical convergence.

In addition, we must recall that our analytical solution is defined up to an additive constant that depends on the ratio
\beta/R
.
This constant can, in principle, be determined by matching the analytic result to the numerical solution.
However, one can always adjust its value to minimize the discrepancy between the two results, which – combined with the limited numerical accuracy – makes it difficult to regard this comparison as a stringent quantitative test.
Nonetheless, we provide a possible comparison in which the unknown constant is fixed by fitting the numerical data: in this case, the maximal relative error is approximately
4\%
.

It would be interesting to obtain more precise numerical estimates in order to perform a stronger quantitative comparison and provide an independent confirmation of the results presented in this work. Indicatively, the procedure will be improved by understanding how to fix the arc contribution; another solution could be to numerically solve the equation in momentum space.

E.1
Metric identities

We present in this section useful results and identities for the expanded metric in the FG gauge.
In general, the determinant of the metric takes the following form:

\sqrt{g}=\sqrt{g_{\text{AdS}}}\left[1+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2d}\left(h^{(2d)}-\frac{1}{2}h^{(d)}_{MN}h^{(d)\,MN}\right)+\ldots\right]\,,

(157)

where we have not used an explicit form for
h

h^{(d)}

The determinant of the AdS metric is given by

\sqrt{g_{\text{AdS}}}=\left(\frac{\ell}{\rho}\right)^{d+1}\,.

(158)

The first-order correction to the AdS metric can be determined by expanding the metric at small temperature.
We find for the first-order correction and its inverse

\displaystyle h^{(d)\,MN}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{d+2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{d}\frac{1}{d}\ \text{diag}\,\left(0,-(d-1),1,\ldots,1)\right)^{MN}\,,

(159)

\displaystyle h^{(d)}_{MN}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{d-2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{d}\frac{1}{d}\ \text{diag}\,\left(0,-(d-1),1,\ldots,1)\right)_{MN}\,.

(160)

Note that, as mentioned above, it is manifest that

h^{(d)}=0\,.

(161)

It is also useful for our purposes to calculate the product of two

h^{(d)\,M}_{\phantom{(d)\,M}P}\,h^{(d)\,PN}=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{2d+2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{2d}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\ \text{diag}\,\left(0,(d-1)^{2},1,\ldots,1\right)^{MN}\,.

(162)

The second-order correction is given by

\displaystyle h^{(2d)\,MN}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{2d+2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{2d}\frac{1}{8d^{2}}\ \text{diag}\,\left(0,4(d^{2}+1)-9d,4-d,\ldots,4-d)\right)^{MN}\,,

(163)

\displaystyle h^{(2d)}_{MN}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{2d-2}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{2d}\frac{1}{8d^{2}}\ \text{diag}\,\left(0,4(d^{2}+1)-9d,4-d,\ldots,4-d)\right)_{MN}\,.

(164)

This order is not traceless:

h^{(2d)}=\left(\frac{\rho}{\ell}\right)^{2d}\left(\frac{4\pi}{d}\right)^{2d}\frac{3d-4}{8d}\,.

(165)

Additional useful identities involving

are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(h^{(2d)}-\frac{1}{2}h^{(d)}_{MN}h^{(d)\,MN}\right)

E.2
Integrals

We now give the integrals useful for calculating the diagrams.
To compute the contact diagrams, we need

I_{k}(\tau_{12},x_{12})=\int_{\beta}\frac{\text{d}^{d+1}y_{3}}{\rho_{3}^{d+1}}\left(\frac{\rho_{3}}{\ell}\right)^{k}\,K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{\phi}}(\rho_{3},\tau_{13},x_{13})K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{\phi}}(\rho_{3},\tau_{23},x_{23})\,.

(168)

This is a standard AdS integral
Freedman:1998tz
.
It takes the kinematic form

I_{k}(\tau_{12},x_{12})=\frac{\alpha(d,\Delta_{\phi},k)}{\ell^{k}(\tau_{12}^{2}+x_{12}^{2})^{(2\Delta_{\phi}-k)/2}}\,,

(169)

where the coefficient is found to be

\alpha(d,\Delta_{\phi},k)=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta_{\phi}-k/2)\Gamma^{2}(k/2)\Gamma(\Delta_{\phi}+(k-d)/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(k)\Gamma^{2}(\Delta_{\phi}-d/2)}\,.

(170)

In the exchange diagram we encounter a more general version of the same integral:

I_{k}^{(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})}=\int_{\beta}\frac{\text{d}^{d+1}y_{3}}{\rho_{3}^{d+1}}\left(\frac{\rho_{3}}{\ell}\right)^{k}\,K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{1}}(\rho_{3},\tau_{13},x_{13})K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{2}}(\rho_{3},\tau_{23},x_{23})\,.

(171)

This is also a well-known integral
Freedman:1998tz
, for which the result is

I^{(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})}_{k}(\tau_{12},x_{12})=\frac{\alpha(d,\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},k)}{\ell^{k}(\tau_{12}^{2}+x_{12}^{2})^{(2\Delta_{\phi}-k)/2}}\,.

(172)

with

\alpha(d,\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},k)=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta_{123}/2)\Gamma(\Delta_{231}/2)\Gamma(\Delta_{312}/2)\Gamma((\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}-d)/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\Delta_{1}-d/2)\Gamma(\Delta_{2}-d/2)\Gamma(\Delta_{3})}\,.

(173)

and

\Delta_{ijk}=\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{k}\,.

(174)

We now move our attention to the most challenging integral of this work, which is the exchange integral defined as

\begin{split}J_{k}(\tau_{12},x_{12})&=\int_{\beta}\frac{\text{d}^{d+1}y_{3}}{\rho_{3}^{d+1}}\left(\frac{\rho_{3}}{\ell}\right)^{k}K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{\phi}}(\rho_{3},\tau_{13},x_{13})\\

(175)

It can be seen to take the kinematic form

J_{k}(\tau_{12},x_{12})=\frac{\beta(d,\Delta_{\phi},k)}{\ell^{2k}(\tau_{12}^{2}+x_{12}^{2})^{\Delta_{\phi}-k}}\,.

(176)

\begin{split}G^{\text{(AdS)}}_{\Delta_{\phi}}(\rho_{34},\tau_{34},x_{34})&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}\nu}{\nu^{2}+(\Delta-d/2)^{2}}\\
&\phantom{=\ }\times\int_{\beta}\text{d}^{d}x_{0}\,K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{d/2+i\nu}(\rho_{3},\tau_{03},x_{03})K^{\text{(AdS)}}_{d/2-i\nu}(\rho_{4},\tau_{04},x_{04})\,.\end{split}

(177)

We can then use (
172
).
The next step is to solve the standard massless Feynman integral

\begin{split}\int\frac{\text{d}^{d}x_{0}}{(\tau_{03}^{2}+x_{03}^{2})^{A}(\tau_{03}^{2}+x_{03}^{2})^{B}}&=\frac{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(A+B-d/2)\Gamma(d/2-A)\Gamma(d/2-B)}{\Gamma(A)\Gamma(B)\Gamma(d-A-B)}\\
&\phantom{=\ }\times\frac{1}{(\tau_{12}^{2}+x_{12}^{2})^{A+B-d/2}}\,.\end{split}

(178)

Specializing the calculation to our case of interest (
d=4

\begin{split}\beta(4,\Delta_{\phi},6)&=\frac{\Gamma(\Delta_{\phi}-6)}{460800\pi^{2}\Gamma(8-\Delta_{\phi})\Gamma^{2}(\Delta_{\phi}-2)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}\nu}{\nu^{2}+(\Delta_{\phi}-2)^{2}}\frac{\nu(\nu^{2}+(\Delta_{\phi}-6)^{2})\sinh(\pi\nu)}{\cosh(\pi\nu)-\cos(\pi\Delta_{\phi})}\\
&\phantom{=\ }\times\frac{\Gamma(4-(\Delta_{\phi}+i\nu)/2)\Gamma(4-(\Delta_{\phi}-i\nu)/2)\Gamma^{2}((\Delta_{\phi}+i\nu+4)/2)\Gamma^{2}((\Delta_{\phi}-i\nu+4)/2)}{\Gamma((\Delta_{\phi}+i\nu-4)/2)\Gamma((\Delta_{\phi}-i\nu-4)/2)}\,.\end{split}

(179)

(180)
