Information Theory of Meaningful Communication
Doron Sivan, Misha Tsodyks

Abstract
In Shannon’s seminal paper, entropy of printed English, treated as a stationary stochastic process, was estimated to be roughly 1 bit per character. However, considered as a means of communication, language differs considerably from its printed form: (i) the units of information are not characters or even words but clauses, i.e. shortest meaningful parts of speech; and (ii) what is transmitted is principally the meaning of what is being said or written, while the precise phrasing that was used to communicate the meaning is typically ignored. In this study, we show that one can leverage recently developed large language models to quantify information communicated in meaningful narratives in terms of bits of meaning per clause.

Introduction

Natural language typically occurs during dialogues, where people talk (or write) to each other by exchanging ’messages’, which are understood in the context of the previous conversation.
These messages could be very short, like ’yes’ or ’really?’, or long, like e.g. somebody recollecting a recent trip to you.
In this study, we focus on the latter case by analyzing personal experience spoken narratives collected by Labov for his pioneering studies of narrative structures
[
1
]
.
In collecting these narratives, Labov asked his subjects a few introductory questions, like ’Was there any time when your life was in danger?’, and when answered ’Yes’, he further asked, ’What happened?’.
The set of 20 narratives analyzed for our study span a large range of lengths, from 12 to 191 clauses.
They were annotated and segmented into clauses by Labov
[
2
]
, and we are using these segmented annotations for our analysis
1
1

1

The segmentation is for each independent clause and its dependent clauses
[
2
]
.
For simplicity, we refer to it in this paper as a ”clause” (see the Supplementary Information, section
S.1
, for elaboration).
.

The goal of the study is to develop information theory of language as a means of communication, by quantifying information communicated by the narrator with each clause.
Unlike the case of printed English considered by Shannon
[
3
]
, we are interested in the information contained in the
meaning
of each clause rather than in the exact phrasing used (see e.g.
[
4
]
). The rationale for this approach is that when exposed to meaningful language, people typically focus on the meaning of what is communicated to them, at the expense of the exact wording that is quickly forgotten, see e.g.
[
5
]
.

2
Information theory of meaningful communicative English

We first develop our formulation in terms of exact phrasing of narratives and get back to the issue of meaning later.
We consider a narrative
N
as a finite sequence of clauses:

N={C_{1},C_{2},...,C_{L}}

(1)

where
C_{i}
stands for the clause with position
i
, and
L
is the narrative length in terms of the number of clauses.
We can define
P(N|c)
as the probability of sampling a narrative
N
from the ensemble of narratives given a particular context,
c
(e.g., spoken narratives that describe life-threatening events of narrators coming from a particular socioeconomic group).
This probability is used to define the information content of the narrative:

\displaystyle I_{N}=-\log P(N|c)\,\,.

(2)

Alternatively,
I_{N}
is the smallest number of bits needed to encode it (in an optimal encoding of the ensemble of narratives)
[
6
]
.

Since the probability of a narrative is given by the product of conditional probabilities for all clauses, one can also define the information in each clause as

\displaystyle I_{i}

\displaystyle=-\log P(C_{i}|c_{<i})\,\,,

(3)

where
c_{<i}
includes
c
and the part of the narrative that precedes the clause
C_{i}
, so that
I_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}I_{i}
.

In his pioneering work, Shannon estimated the entropy of printed English using a human subject who was trying to guess the next character given the previous ones
[
3
]
, with the result of about 1 bit per character (bpc).
This result was corroborated by similar, but more controlled, experiments, e.g.
[
7
]
.
Albeit considerable efforts, until the turn of the century, language models and compression methods were still behind at about 2 bpc
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
Only recently, with the rise of Transformers and large language models (LLMs), 1 bpc was achieved
[
11
,
12
]
(along with impressive results in many other natural language tasks, e.g.
[
13
]
).
Thus, in this study, we use LLMs to estimate
P(C_{i}|c_{<i})
, as they are specifically trained to approximate the conditional probabilities of each new token (usually word) given the context.
In particular, we use Llama3.1-70B-Instruct open-source LLM
[
14
]
.

In Fig.
1

I_{i}
for the 20 Labov narratives (for details about these narratives, see the Supplementary Information (SI), section
S.1
).
The average slope of these curves is approximately 40 bits/clause.
Given that the average length of a clause in our dataset is close to 45 characters, we see that this results in information per letter of about 0.9 bits, which is compatible with Shannon’s estimate (note that Shannon’s values were obtained with 27 letter alphabet, a-z and space, while our narratives contain 72 characters, including capital letters, digits, and other characters.
Reducing the alphabet to 27 letters does not significantly change the result).

We now turn to the central point of our study, namely the information contained in the
meaning
of a given clause (
M_{i}
) rather than in its exact phrasing (
C_{i}
).
In general, the meaning of a particular clause is not unambiguous (e.g. saying ’I hate you’ can mean anything from hate to love), but we assume that when the context of a clause is known, its meaning is uniquely determined ("single meaning approximation", see e.g.
[
15
,
16
]
).
We then need to estimate the conditional probability
P(M_{i}|c_{<i})
.
Since there is no way to directly calculate this probability using LLMs (because they don’t deal with meaning), one could decompose it into a sum of all possible phrasings that preserve the meaning of a given clause:

\displaystyle P(M_{i}|c_{<i})=\sum_{C^{\prime}_{i}}P(M_{i}|C^{\prime}_{i},c_{<i})P(C^{\prime}_{i}|c_{<i})=\sum_{\text{$C^{\prime}_{i}$ means $M_{i}$}}P(C^{\prime}_{i}|c_{<i})\,\,,

(4)

where the second equality is due to the "single meaning approximation" -
P(M_{i}|C^{\prime}_{i},c_{<i})=1
if
C^{\prime}_{i}
means
M_{i}
(and
0
otherwise).
In a recent paper
[
15
]
, this equation was used together with a sampling-based approach (with only ten samples) for improved prediction of accuracy in the question-answering task.
In a more general setting, however, using the above definition is challenging as it requires the whole set of possible rephrasings of a given clause that preserves its meaning.

Thus, we use a different approach by decomposing
P(C_{i}|c_{<i})
as a sum over all possible meanings:

\displaystyle P(C_{i}|c_{<i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M^{\prime}_{i}}P(M^{\prime}_{i}|c_{<i})P(C_{i}|M^{\prime}_{i},c_{<i})=P(M_{i}|c_{<i})P(C_{i}|M_{i},c_{<i})\,\,.

(5)

where the second equation follows again from the single meaning approximation, so that
P(C_{i}|M^{\prime}_{i},c_{<i})\neq 0
only for
M^{\prime}_{i}=M_{i}
.
We can interpret Eq. (
5
) intuitively: when a speaker composes a narrative, first they come up with an idea for the next clause (
M_{i}
) and then decide on its exact wording (
C_{i}
).
The implicit assumption that we make here is that there are potentially multiple different wordings that communicate the same (or similar enough) meaning, otherwise the above equation would be simply reduced to
P(C_{i}|c_{<i})=P(M_{i}|c_{<i})
.

Using Eq. (
5
), we get the desired expression for semantic information of clause
i
,
I_{M,i}
:

\displaystyle I_{M,i}=-\log P(M_{i}|c_{<i})=I_{i}-I_{W,i}\,\,,

(6)

where
I_{i}=-\log P(C_{i}|C_{<i})
is estimated directly by the token conditional probabilities from the LLM (see Eq. (
3
)), and
I_{W,i}=-\log P(C_{i}|M_{i},c_{<i})
, the wording information of the clause, is calculated with
P(C_{i}|M_{i},c_{<i})
.
This probability cannot be directly calculated with an LLM (again, as it does not handle meanings explicitly), however we designed the following procedure that allowed us to estimate it.

First, we generated a rephrasing of a narrative that closely preserves its meaning.
For this purpose, we used ChatGPT (gpt-4)
[
17
]
, gave it the narrative segmented into clauses, and asked for a rephrased segmented narrative where the corresponding clauses retain the same meaning (the prompt is provided in the SI, section
S.2
).
After obtaining the rephrased version of the narrative (N’), we went back to Llama and presented the following prompt to it: "The following two texts tell the same narrative but with different wording: N’… N" (precise prompt is given in the SI, section
S.2
).
Finally, given the above context, we computed the conditional probabilities for each clause in N.
The rationale for this approach is that in the context of this prompt, the meaning of each clause of the narrative N is fixed by the previous context, and hence, the conditional probability for this clause should be equal to
P(C_{i}|M_{i},c_{<i})
(see Fig.
2
for the illustration of our method.)

3
Predictable and surprising clauses

Curiously, some of the clauses contain very little information, see Fig.
1
B, indicating that the meaning of these clauses is strongly constrained by context and could, therefore, be reasonably guessed.
As a way of validating our method, we randomly sampled 543 clauses with different values of
I_{M}
and asked ChatGPT to guess their meaning from the preceding part of the narrative (the prompt is given in the SI, section
S.3
).
ChatGPT correctly predicted the meaning of 31 clauses on the first attempt.
Some of them appeared to be impossible to guess for the authors, which, we reasoned, might result from narratives potentially appearing in the training set of the LLM.
We therefore performed a human guessing experiment on these clauses and found that they could predict the meaning of 23 of them with a single try (see the SI, section
S.3
, for elaboration on our methodology).
Semantic information of these clauses is shown in Fig.
3
A.
One can see that most of the predictable clauses indeed have small values of
I_{M}
.
Note however that there are three predictable clauses with semantic information between 10 and 12 bits, which indicates that our method sometimes results in wrong estimates of information.
The same can be said about clauses with negative semantic information (see Fig.
1
A).

On the opposite side of the spectrum, clauses containing the highest information should be the most difficult to guess.
We cannot check this conjecture directly, however, because a vast majority of clauses cannot be guessed by humans in any reasonable number of attempts; in other words, we cannot directly apply Shannon’s experimental method to clauses.
One could assume, however, that the beginning of a narrative should overall be more informative than the later parts, at least if the general context of the narrative is not known in advance.
Indeed, we found that, on average, the first clause in the narrative has the highest information, with information plateauing after around ten clauses, as shown by the blue curve of Fig.
3
B.
However, when we include initial context as the guiding question that initiated the narrative, the first clauses are no longer more informative on average, as shown by the orange curve (for elaboration, see SI, section
S.4
).

In our approach presented above, we used a rephrasing of the whole narrative to constrain the meaning of clause
C_{i}
, but we found that one can use only a partial rephrasing up to (and including) clause
C_{i}
and get similar results, as detailed in the SI, section
S.5
.
Moreover, in SI, section
S.6
, we showed that using another rephrasing of a narrative, obtained with the same prompt on the rephrased version, also results in similar values of semantic information.

All the results presented above were obtained with Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct language model, which is a model post-trained for chat (chosen for its accessibility compared to the pre-trained version).
In the Supplementary Information, section
S.7
, we show that using other LLMs, both pre- and post-trained, give similar results.

4
Summary and future directions

We developed a technique for estimating the information communicated by the meaning of clauses while ignoring their precise phrasing.
We believe this better describes human communication than the total information contained in each clause.
We applied this procedure to a set of spoken narratives and found that, on average, meaningful information is about
20
bits for a clause, or about one-half of the total.
We also found that different clauses contain vastly different semantic information, from
0
to
100
bits per clause.
In particular, clauses that can be guessed by human subjects based on the previous context usually communicate a small amount of semantic information.
We also found that clauses at the beginning of a narrative, without initial context, on average, convey more semantic information than subsequent clauses.

It will be interesting to apply our technique to other forms of verbal communication, such as dialogues.
With some modifications, one could extend this approach to quantitatively evaluating summaries, which could be used for the development of summarization programs.

S.2
Estimation of semantic information

The semantic information
I_{M}
was estimated using the following procedure. We generated a narrative rephrasing using ChatGPT, and then calculated the conditional probabilities of each clause in the "wording information prompt" that includes the rephrased narrative followed by the original, obtaining the wording information
I_{W,i}
for each clause
C_{i}
. We estimate the total information,
I_{i}
, by the conditional probabilities of each clause
C_{i}
in the context of the previous clauses in the narrative. Finally, the semantic information was estimated from Eq. (
6
):
I_{M,i}=I_{i}-I_{W,i}
. Below, we give a detailed account of this procedure.

S.2.1
Conditional probabilities

First, we briefly overview how conditional probabilities are calculated in auto-regressive (decoder-only) LLMs, see e.g.
[
12
,
14
]
. Given some text
T
, the LLM first segments its input text into tokens (tokenization),
T=\boxed{x_{0}}\boxed{x_{1}}\cdots\boxed{x_{N}}
,
where usually tokens correspond to words, and the first token (
x_{0}
) is a begin-of-text token. Then, in a single forward pass, it calculates the conditional log probability of each token (except the first) given the preceding tokens,
\log P(x_{i}|x_{<i})
.
In this paper, we use the information content of each token (negative log-likelihood),
I_{x,i}=-\log P(x_{i}|x_{<i})
, and represent the text as
T=\overset{I_{x,1}}{\boxed{x_{1}}}\overset{I_{x,2}}{\boxed{x_{2}}}\cdots\overset{I_{x,N}}{\boxed{x_{N}}}
(removing the first begin-of-text token
x_{0}
, as its probability is not calculated).

For example, the information contents of the tokens (in bits per token) composing the first clause in the narrative of Doug D’Almaggio
[
2
]
are given by:

\displaystyle\overset{\text{9.9}}{\boxed{\text{So}}}\overset{\text{6.7}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace I}}}\overset{\text{3.1}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace was}}}\overset{\text{3.6}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace at}}}\overset{\text{0.8}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace the}}}\overset{\text{7.8}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace bar}}}\overset{\text{9.4}}{\boxed{\text{.}}}\,\,,

while the first clause in the narrative of Harold Shambaugh ("The Norwegian sailor")
[
2
]
, which has a similar meaning, but is spoken with a local dialect’s pronunciation, is given by:

\displaystyle\overset{\text{13}}{\boxed{\text{Oh}}}\overset{\text{9.6}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace I}}}\overset{\text{13}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace w}}}\overset{\text{16}}{\boxed{\text{'s}}}\overset{\text{13}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace sett}}}\overset{\text{0.3}}{\boxed{\text{in}}}\overset{\text{0.5}}{\boxed{\text{'}}}\overset{\text{5.1}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace at}}}\overset{\text{5.4}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace a}}}\overset{\text{1.8}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace table}}}\overset{\text{7.3}}{\boxed{\text{\textvisiblespace drink}}}\overset{\text{0.03}}{\boxed{\text{in}}}\overset{\text{0.2}}{\boxed{\text{'}}}\,\,.

The information values were calculated using Llama3.1-70B-Instruct, which is a state-of-the-art open-source LLM.
We use an open-source LLM to have full access to the conditional probabilities, which is not possible with proprietary LLMs.

Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct is a model fine-tuned (post-trained) for chat, which is more popular and hence accessible than the non-fine-tuned version.
A pre-trained (non-fine-tuned) LLM is trained only on the language modeling task, to predict the next token using different context sizes (different numbers of preceding tokens).
Simply put, the pre-training objective is to minimize the information content of the tokens (
I_{x}
) in natural language texts.
A post-trained LLM is further trained on texts of chat between a user and an AI assistant, where the user instructs the assistant to fulfill various tasks in natural language.
In section
S.7
, we provide results of other LLMs which are non-fine-tuned.
We note that we use Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct without the chat prompt format, as if it were the non-fine-tuned version (the only special token we use is a begin-of-text token).

In

[

In the "Boy scout" we notice a slight shortcoming in conserving the meaning: while the original "Boy scout" narrative is about a swimming race, ChatGPT misinterpreted it as a running race, making clause 13 a narrative inconsistency.

Moreover, in the prompt, we didn’t specifically ask to conserve the narrative’s style; indeed, we noticed a change in style.
The rephrased version is formal and edited (e.g., omits the disfluencies or self-corrections) as opposed to the original, which is casual and spontaneously spoken.
We leave improving the quality of the rephrasings and analyzing the sensitivity of semantic information to lexical, syntactic, and style changes for future studies.

The wording information of clause

\displaystyle I_{W,i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{token $x_{j}$ of clause $C_{i}$}\\
\text{in \fcolorbox{codeframe}{codebg}{\color{black}\!\texttt{original}\!}}\end{subarray}}I_{x,j}\,\,,

where the token information content is
I_{x,j}=-\log P(x_{j}|x_{<j})
, as discussed in section

We call it the wording information, because

​

On the other hand, the total information,

is estimated with Llama3.1-70B
[
14

that appear in the original narrative alone (i.e. without the preceding meaning constraining context). Then, the semantic information,

is estimated from Eq. (

I_{M,i}=I_{i}-I_{W,i}
.

[

S.3
Identification of predictable clauses

The 20 Labov narratives contain 1132 clauses.
Instead of analyzing all of them, we sampled the clauses according to their semantic information.
We sampled 40 clauses per bin, for 14 bins with the following bin boundaries: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 20, 25, 30, 40, 60, and 200 bits.
The bins 0-2, 2-4, and 60-200 bits have 33, 39, and 17 clauses, respectively, so we analyzed all of them.
There were also 14 clauses with negative semantic information (between -3 and 0 bits).
Therefore, we define another bin for these clauses, with a total of 15 bins and 543 clauses.
Eight of the sampled clauses were the first ones in the narrative and thus were omitted, as it is impossible to predict them without context.

S.3.2
Human prediction

Therefore, to further verify our results, we asked subjects to predict the next clause in a paused narrative, similar to the task presented to ChatGPT in
S.3.1
.
We recruited 13 participants online via Prolific (www.prolific.com), with the following screeners: primary language English, 20 or more previous submissions, and an approval rate above 99% (around 100,000 active participants matched these criteria).
Each participant predicted 14 or 17 clauses from 6 different narratives, with a median time to complete of 40 minutes.
Each participant was compensated 8 USD for the task (median rate: 12 USD/hour).

The task was conducted online on the participants’ browsers.
The participants were instructed: "This is a writing task.
You will be presented with true personal experience narratives told by real people.
Each narrative is segmented into clauses, which are simple sentences.
The story is paused at a certain point, and your task is to predict the next clause.
To be correct, you only need your predicted clause to convey essentially the same meaning as the one from the true story; wording or phrasing does not matter.".
These instructions were followed by remarks (in short: the narrative can contain slang, some numbered clauses also contain dependent clauses, take time before you answer, don’t use AI assistants or any other trick), followed by a consent form to participate in this research.

Then, each participant was assigned to one of two groups.
The first group, with 7 participants, predicted 14 clauses from 6 narratives.
The second group, with 6 participants, predicted 17 clauses from 6 different narratives.
The participants predicted in total the 31 clauses that were found predictable, as described in the previous section.
For each prediction, the participant was given the previous numbered clauses in the narrative up to that point, and he entered his prediction for the next clause in a textbox.
When a narrative contained more than a single clause to predict, the already read clauses appeared in a gray background to remind the reader that he had read these clauses.

The responses of the participants were primarily usable.
The only issue was one participant from the first group, halfway through the task, started entering previous predictions (verbatim); these copied predictions were removed from the analysis.
Thus, 24 clauses have predictions from 6 participants, and 7 clauses have predictions from 7 participants.

For each participant’s prediction, we manually judged whether it conveyed essentially the same meaning as the original clause.
We note two cases that required careful judgment.
The first is clause 14 in Knott: "So a’ went over that night the same night they moved in," which was only partially predicted by four participants - e.g. "so i knock on the door and introduce myself" or "So we introduced ourselves and asked ’need anything from the shop?’" - and it was decided to be classified as not predicted.
The second is clause 81 in Norman: "So sure enough he went to Harry Pressger", where one prediction was correct, "My husband went to ask Harry.", and in two others, there was confusion between the narrator (the wife) and her husband - "So I went" and "So, my wife went to 803 South 2nd to meet Harry Pressger" - but otherwise they were correct, so it was decided to classify them as predicted.

There were 8 clauses that no participant could predict.
The 23 other clauses could be predicted usually by 2 to 4 participants (in detail, 1, 8, 6, 5, 2, 1 clauses were predicted by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 participants, respectively).
The fact that ChatGPT could predict these 8 clauses with a single attempt, but 6-7 participants could not, means that ChatGPT has better-than-average-human ability on this task, which can be caused by the memorization effect mentioned above.

It can be seen that the 8 clauses that were not predicted by humans have systematically higher semantic information.

S.5
Effect of partial rephrasing

The method we proposed uses the complete rephrasing in
S.2.3
to estimate the wording information of each clause.
This method has the advantage of estimating the wording information of all the clauses in the narrative with a single prompt.
However, it might not be necessary to use the complete rephrasing to constrain the meaning of clause
C_{i}
in the prompt.

To investigate it, we calculate the wording information of clause
C_{i}
with a prompt similar to
S.2.3
, but

​
rephrased
​
is only a partial rephrasing up to (and including) clause
C_{i}
.
The wording information with a partial rephrasing deviates from the wording information with a complete rephrasing by
(-1.1\pm 2.3)
bits.
For the long stories (Knott, Stein, Norman, and Costa, with more than 65 clauses), we compared only the 30 first and 30 last clauses.
The deviations had no apparent trend with clause number or semantic information.

The above result means that providing rephrasings of the clauses after clause
C_{i}
further constrains the meaning of clause
C_{i}
on average, but only by a tiny amount.
The two methods result in very similar semantic information, and the method we used in the paper is preferred as it uses a single prompt instead of
L
prompts for a narrative with
L
clauses.
