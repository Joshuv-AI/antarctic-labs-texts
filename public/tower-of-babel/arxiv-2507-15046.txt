Volatility Spillovers and Interconnectedness in OPEC Oil Markets: A Network-Based log-ARCH Approach
Fayçal Djebari, Kahina Mehidi, Khelifa Mazouz, Philipp Otto

Abstract
This paper examines several network-based volatility models for oil prices, capturing spillovers among OPEC oil-exporting countries by embedding novel network structures into ARCH-type models. We apply a network-based log-ARCH framework that incorporates weight matrices derived from time-series clustering and model-implied distances into the conditional variance equation. These weight matrices are constructed from return data and standard multivariate GARCH model outputs (CCC, DCC, and GO-GARCH), enabling a comparative analysis of volatility transmission across specifications. Through a rolling-window forecast evaluation, the network-based models demonstrate competitive forecasting performance relative to traditional specifications and uncover intricate spillover effects. These results provide a deeper understanding of the interconnectedness within the OPEC network, with important implications for financial risk assessment, market integration, and coordinated policy among oil-producing economies. Keywords: Oil price volatility, OPEC, Volatility spillovers, Network models, Spatiotemporal econometrics JEL Classifications: C33, C58, D85, Q43, Q47

Introduction

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) plays a central role in stabilizing global oil markets by coordinating production and pricing among its members. Acting as a cartel, OPEC restricts oil supply to support prices
(
Kilian 2009
;
Al Rousan et al. 2018
)
. Despite this coordination, significant differences persist across member countries regarding production behavior and pricing dynamics. Understanding how volatility transmits across these economies remains an important, unresolved question.

Recent studies have increasingly adopted network-based methodologies to explore volatility spillover and interdependencies. These include dynamic production networks
(
Al Rousan et al. 2018
)
, time-varying spillover indices
(
Ji et al. 2018
)
, multilayer spillover structures
(
Dai et al. 2023
)
, and global energy networks
(
Uddin et al. 2023
)
. Many of these rely on the connectedness framework of
Diebold and Yilmaz 2009
;
Diebold and Yilmaz 2012
;
Diebold and Yilmaz 2014
, which measures directional spillovers using forecast error variance decompositions. However, these approaches often depend on external explanatory variables or impose structure through rolling-window VARs, and do not directly model the volatility process or fully account for the network-based structure of conditional heteroskedasticity.

This paper addresses this gap by asking: Can volatility spillovers among OPEC countries be better captured by embedding network structures directly into volatility models? Our motivation stems from the need for models that reflect both the time-varying nature of volatility and the structural interdependence among oil-exporting countries. In particular, the OPEC framework presents a unique case in which policy coordination coexists with heterogeneity in market responses. This calls for a modeling strategy capable of uncovering latent transmission channels that are both temporal and cross-sectional.

To address this, we develop a network-based volatility modeling framework. Building on the spatiotemporal ARCH modeling literature, such as
Otto et al. 2024a
, we introduce a methodology that embeds time-series clustering-derived network structures into the volatility specification. This allows us to model volatility transmission not only over time but also across a network of interdependent economies. Our approach does not rely on external variables; instead, it infers spillover dynamics directly from the historical log return data.

We focus on six OPEC countries, selected based on the completeness of their monthly historical log return data from the official OPEC database. We construct several dissimilarity-based weight matrices using raw data Euclidean distances, correlations, and model-implied metrics from CCC-GARCH
(
Bollerslev 1990
)
, DCC-GARCH
(
Engle 2002
)
, GO-GARCH
(
Van der Weide 2002
)
, and the financial networks considered in the network log-ARCH models of
Mattera and Otto 2024
. These weight matrices define the network structures used in the log-ARCH specification. The model is implemented within a rolling-window forecasting scheme to evaluate out-of-sample performance.

Our empirical results show that network-based log-ARCH models incorporating CCC, DCC, and GO-GARCH-derived weight matrices demonstrate strong forecasting performance relative to standard alternatives and substantially reduce the computational complexity associated with the standard multivariate GARCH models. These models yield lower Root Mean Squared Forecast Errors (RMSFE) and Mean Absolute Forecast Errors (MAFE) than most competing specifications. More importantly, they uncover richer and more detailed spillover patterns across the OPEC network. These patterns often align with known geopolitical and institutional ties, even though no such data are explicitly included in the model.

In addition to improving forecast accuracy, the proposed framework enhances interpretability. This reveals the latent transmission channels of volatility that reflect the evolving relationships between member states. For investors, this provides a tool for improved volatility forecasting and dynamic risk management. For policymakers, the model offers early warning signals of breakdowns in coordination or emerging systemic risks. Since the method relies only on return data, it enables real-time assessment without the need for external indicators.

2
Literature Review

Since its establishment in Baghdad in 1960, OPEC has aimed to coordinate and harmonize the petroleum policies of its member countries to ensure stable oil prices and adequate returns on investment
(
OPEC Statute
)
. Over the decades, the organization has been shaped by a series of major historical and geopolitical events
(
OPEC Brief History
)
, many of which have had far-reaching effects on the global economy
(
Jo 2014
)
. These events have exposed the persistent susceptibility of oil-exporting countries to external shocks, highlighting the complexity of managing coordinated policies in the face of heterogeneous economic responses
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
. Geopolitical risks, particularly in oil-producing regions such as the Middle East and North Africa, have consistently driven oil price volatility
(
Mignon and Saadaoui 2024
)
. For instance, regional tensions and conflicts, along with global crises—such as the Chinese economic slowdown
(
Hamilton 2014
)
—have led to sharp fluctuations in oil prices. More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic amplified this volatility by triggering both supply- and demand-side shocks
(
Bourghelle et al. 2021
;
Le et al. 2023
)
. Such compounded disruptions underscore the importance of understanding how volatility transmits across oil-exporting economies, particularly within policy-coordinated networks such as OPEC, where shocks may propagate unevenly across members.

Economies that are heavily dependent on oil exports face considerable challenges due to persistent shocks and uncertainties in global oil markets. These fluctuations hinder long-term economic planning, disrupt investment strategies, and constrain economic growth
(
Sachs and Warner 1995
)
. To mitigate these adverse effects, it is crucial for such countries to diversify their energy sources and reduce their reliance on oil revenues, thereby limiting their exposure to oil price volatility
(
Kilian 2008
;
Kilian 2014
)
. Diversification enhances economic resilience by insulating national economies from the destabilizing effects of external shocks.

Oil price volatility remains a central concern in economic and financial forecasting, given its influence on key macroeconomic indicators, such as investment, durable goods consumption, and aggregate output. Uncertainty generated by oil price fluctuations can lead to reduced economic activity, particularly in resource-rich exporting nations
(
Finn 2000
;
Kilian 2008
;
Elder and Serletis 2010
;
Vo and Le 2021
)
. These countries, as emphasized by
Cappelli et al. 2023
, are disproportionately affected due to their strong fiscal reliance on oil revenues. Despite decades of research, accurately forecasting oil price movements remains a formidable task due to complex interactions among market agents
(
Baumeister and Kilian 2016a
)
.

Volatility in oil markets also has profound implications for corporate investment behavior. Elevated uncertainty about future energy input costs, as discussed by
Pindyck 1991
, deters firms from undertaking irreversible investment projects. This effect is more pronounced in oil-exporting economies, where future profitability is directly tied to oil price movements. Numerous empirical studies corroborate the negative relationship between oil price uncertainty and investment levels
(
Henriques and Sadorsky 2011
;
Ratti et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2017
)
. The exceptional volatility of oil and gas prices, surpassing that of most commodities, is attributed to supply-demand dynamics, macroeconomic fluctuations, and geopolitical tensions, as evidenced by research papers such as
(
Regnier 2007
)
and
(
Demirbas et al. 2017
)
.

Conventional econometric models often fail to fully capture the complexity of oil price volatility, particularly its dynamic and interconnected nature within coordinated economic blocs. In response,
Engle 1982
and
Bollerslev 1986
developed the ARCH and GARCH models, which model volatility clustering in financial time series. These nonlinear frameworks remain foundational in financial econometrics, especially through multivariate extensions such as CCC-GARCH
(
Bollerslev 1990
)
, DCC-GARCH
(
Engle 2002
)
, and GO-GARCH
(
Van der Weide 2002
)
, which accommodate dynamic correlations in the return series. More recently, attention has shifted to models that incorporate spatial and network-based dependencies to account for cross-country volatility transmission. Spatiotemporal ARCH (spARCH) models incorporate contemporaneous spatial and temporal dependence to better capture volatility interlinkages
(
Otto et al. 2018
)
, while spatial and spatiotemporal log-ARCH models
(
Sato and Matsuda 2021
;
Otto et al. 2024a
)
and network log-ARCH models
(
Mattera and Otto 2024
)
extend this framework to account for multiplicative effects. A comprehensive synthesis of these advanced approaches is presented in
Otto et al. 2024b
.

Empirical studies have shown strong interdependencies between oil prices and financial markets
(
Awartani and Maghyereh 2013
;
Guhathakurta et al. 2020
;
Guan et al. 2024
)
. For instance,
Nguyen et al. 2020
document the short-run global repercussions of oil price shocks, while
Creti et al. 2014
observe heightened oil–stock market co-movements in oil-exporting countries. However, existing studies often rely on models that assume static or exogenous relationships, limiting their ability to reflect the endogenous and evolving nature of the interdependencies among oil-exporting countries. In response, recent advances—such as time-series clustering methods
(
Maharaj et al. 2019
)
embedded into Dynamic Spatiotemporal ARCH models
(
Otto et al. 2024a
)
—offer promising tools for inferring network structures directly from return data. This opens new avenues for modeling volatility transmission in a way that reflects both temporal dynamics and cross-sectional spillovers, which is particularly relevant for OPEC economies, where coordinated production masks heterogeneous national responses. By integrating clustering-derived networks with advanced volatility models, our approach captures the latent and evolving interdependencies that shape volatility spillovers in oil-exporting nations. These methods move beyond traditional frameworks by eliminating the need for external variables, allowing for real-time analysis rooted in the structure of the return data itself. This offers both improved forecast accuracy and deeper insights into the underlying transmission channels relevant for policy coordination and risk management.

4
Overview of Time-Series Volatility Models

This section reviews the univariate ARCH and GARCH models, including their logarithmic variants (log-ARCH and log-GARCH), which provide the foundation for modeling time-varying volatility in financial time series. We then extend to multivariate specifications—CCC, DCC, and GO-GARCH—that capture static and dynamic correlations and volatility spillovers across assets, and are subsequently used to construct model-based weight matrices for the Network log-ARCH model.

4.1
Introduction to Univariate ARCH and GARCH-type Model

This subsection defines the univariate ARCH and GARCH models employed as building blocks in the CCC, DCC, and GO-GARCH frameworks. We also introduce the univariate log-ARCH process, which later serves as the foundation for the network-based log-volatility models.

4.1.1
Univariate ARCH and GARCH process

Since the seminal work of
Engle 1982
, autoregressive conditional heteroscedastic (ARCH) models have become central to econometric forecasting, allowing time-varying volatility to be explicitly modeled in financial time series.
Bollerslev 1986
extended this framework through the Generalized ARCH (GARCH) model, where volatility depends on past squared observations and previous conditional variances. Both the ARCH and GARCH models capture conditional variance dynamics separately from the conditional mean, which may follow temporal trends, seasonal patterns, or autoregressive structures. In contrast, stochastic volatility models introduce a latent process to govern volatility evolution, which is also influenced by lagged squared returns. In what follows, we briefly review key univariate and multivariate versions of both classes. Let
\{Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
denote a discrete-time,
r
-dimensional random process observed at equally spaced intervals.

For a univariate time series (i.e.,
r=1
), the process
Y_{t}
is given by

Y_{t}=h_{t}^{1/2}\varepsilon_{t}\,,

(1)

where
h_{t}^{1/2}
scales the i.i.d. sequence
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
with zero mean and unit variance, and
h_{t}
denotes the conditional variance of
Y_{t}
, interpreted as time-varying volatility. In volatility models,
h_{t}
evolves based on past realizations of the process.

In an ARCH(
p
) model, the volatility dynamics are specified as

h_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}Y_{t-i}^{2},

(2)

while the GARCH(
p,q
) specification is given by

h_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}Y_{t-i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}h_{t-j},

(3)

where
\alpha_{0}>0
,
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
, and
\beta_{j}\geq 0
ensure the positivity of the conditional variance. The process is weakly stationary if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}<1
. For a comprehensive treatment of GARCH-type models, see
Francq and Zakoian 2019
.

4.1.2
Log-ARCH and log-GARCH models

The log-volatility model was first proposed by
Geweke 1986
and later supported by
Pantula 1986
and
Milhøj 1987
. It avoids non-negativity constraints on the coefficients and facilitates the inclusion of regressors in the volatility specification. A log-ARCH model is given by

\log h_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\log Y_{t-i}^{2}.

(4)

This can be extended to a log-GARCH model:

\log h_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\log Y_{t-i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\log h_{t-j}.

(5)

Log-GARCH models exhibit multiplicative volatility dynamics, unlike the additive structure of standard ARCH and GARCH models
(
Francq et al. 2013
, see)
. In these models, volatility is a deterministic function of past observations. In contrast, stochastic volatility (SV) models treat volatility as a latent process. A standard SV model specifies log-volatility as an AR(1) process:

\log h_{t}-\mu_{h}=\phi(\log h_{t-1}-\mu_{h})+u_{t},

(6)

where
|\phi|<1
, and
\varepsilon_{t}
and
u_{t}
are independent shocks. For details on the statistical properties of the SV models, see
Ghysels et al. 1996
and
Shephard 2005
.

4.2
Multivariate GARCH models

While univariate GARCH models capture the conditional volatility of a single financial series, multivariate extensions are required to model the joint dynamics and cross-dependencies across assets.
Bollerslev et al. 1988
introduced the VEC-GARCH model, which generalizes the GARCH framework to the multivariate setting by allowing time-varying covariance and correlation structures. Let
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
be an
r
-dimensional random vector of asset returns. Then:

\mathbf{Y}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{1/2}\varepsilon_{t},

(7)

where
\varepsilon_{t}
is an i.i.d. innovation vector and
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
is the conditional covariance matrix, with:

\text{Var}(\mathbf{Y}_{t}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{v},v<t)=\mathbf{H}_{t}.

(8)

Various multivariate GARCH models address the growing complexity of estimating
\mathbf{H}_{t}
as the number of series increases. In this study, we focus on the CCC-GARCH, DCC-GARCH, and GO-GARCH models, each of which offers distinct features and levels of flexibility. These specifications are particularly suited for constructing model-based weight matrices in our network analysis of monthly log returns from the six OPEC countries. The following subsections discuss these models in increasing order of structural flexibility, highlighting their methodological linkages.

4.2.1
Constant and Dynamic Conditional Correlation GARCH Models

To capture co-movement in financial returns with time-varying volatility,
Bollerslev 1990
proposed the Constant Conditional Correlation (CCC) GARCH model. This framework assumes that, while individual asset volatilities may evolve over time, their correlations remain constant. Formally, the conditional covariance matrix
\mathbf{H}_{t}
is decomposed as:

\mathbf{H}_{t}=\mathbf{D}_{t}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{D}_{t},

(9)

where
\mathbf{R}
is a fixed correlation matrix, and
\mathbf{D}_{t}
is a diagonal matrix containing conditional standard deviations,

\mathbf{D}_{t}=\text{diag}(\sqrt{h_{1t}},\ldots,\sqrt{h_{Nt}}).

(10)

Each
h_{it}
follows a univariate GARCH
(p,q)
process, as defined in (
3
). Its general form is:

h_{it}=\alpha_{0}^{(i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}^{(i)}Y_{i,t-j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}^{(i)}h_{i,t-j},\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

(11)

While CCC-GARCH offers a simple yet effective structure, the assumption of constant correlations can be overly restrictive. To address this,
Engle 2002
introduced the Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC) model, which preserves the same matrix form,

\mathbf{H}_{t}=\mathbf{D}_{t}\mathbf{R}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{t},

(12)

but allows the correlation matrix
\mathbf{R}_{t}
to evolve over time. Correlations are derived from standardized residuals
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\mathbf{D}_{t}^{-1}Y_{t}
, through a dynamic process:

\mathbf{Q}_{t}=(1-a-b)\bar{\mathbf{Q}}+a\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^{\top}+b\,\mathbf{Q}_{t-1},

(13)

where
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
is the unconditional covariance of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, and
a+b<1
ensures the stationarity. The matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
is then rescaled to yield the time-varying correlation matrix:

\mathbf{R}_{t}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{Q}_{t})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{t}\text{diag}(\mathbf{Q}_{t})^{-1/2}.

(14)

By introducing dynamic correlations while preserving a parsimonious structure, the DCC model offers greater flexibility in modeling multivariate volatility dynamics. However, as the number of assets increases, the estimation of the correlation matrices becomes more complex. To circumvent this, the GO-GARCH model adopts a factor-based approach, as outlined below.

4.2.2
The Generalized Orthogonal GARCH Model

The Generalized Orthogonal GARCH (GO-GARCH) model, introduced by
Van der Weide 2002
, extends the Orthogonal GARCH framework by allowing the mixing matrix to be any invertible matrix, thus relaxing the orthogonality constraint. The
r
-dimensional zero-mean return vector
{Y}_{t}
is expressed as a linear combination of uncorrelated latent factors
{f}_{t}
:

{Y}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}{f}_{t},\qquad{f}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{H}_{t}),

(15)

where
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is an invertible matrix and
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\text{diag}(h_{1t},\dots,h_{rt})
is diagonal with each element following a univariate GARCH
(1,1)
process:

h_{it}=\omega_{i}+\alpha_{i}f_{i,t-1}^{2}+\beta_{i}h_{i,t-1},\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,r.

(16)

The implied conditional covariance matrix of
{Y}_{t}
is

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}.

(17)

The estimation proceeds in two steps. First, a spectral decomposition of the unconditional covariance matrix
\mathbf{H}=\mathbb{E}[{Y}_{t}{Y}_{t}^{\top}]
provides initial estimates of the eigenvectors
\mathbf{P}
and the eigenvalues
\mathbf{A}
. Second, a rotation matrix
\mathbf{U}
is estimated via the maximum likelihood method, yielding:

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{U}.

(18)

The time-varying conditional correlation matrix is then recovered as

\mathbf{R}_{t}=\mathbf{D}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{t}^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{D}_{t}=\text{diag}(\sqrt{h_{1t}},\dots,\sqrt{h_{rt}}).

(19)

The GO-GARCH model is particularly suitable for high-dimensional settings. By factorizing returns into latent components, it reduces the parameter space and avoids the direct estimation of full conditional covariance matrices. Each latent process follows a simple GARCH structure, making the model both flexible and parsimonious in capturing complex volatility dynamics, as in our network-based analysis of OPEC countries’ oil return volatilities.

5
Network-based Volatility models

A network is defined as
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, where
V
denotes the set of vertices (e.g., OPEC countries) and
E
the set of weighted edges capturing financial, geographic, or structural interdependencies; this structure underpins network-based log-ARCH models that embed both temporal and cross-sectional volatility dynamics to analyze and forecast spillovers.

5.1
Network log-ARCH models

The network log-ARCH model is an extension of spatiotemporal ARCH models
(
Otto et al. 2018
;
Otto et al. 2024a
, see)
,
specifically designed to handle multivariate time series data in the context of
network structures
(
Mattera and Otto 2024
)
. It captures both temporal and contemporaneous interactions
in the volatility process, similar to the ARCH model, but adapted to a network setting. Unlike the previously reviewed univariate and multivariate GARCH models, these network-based approaches allow for volatility spillovers that occur instantaneously across the network in a GARCH-like manner. That is, the conditional variance in (
8
) also depends on adjacent nodes in
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
(e.g.,
v=t
), instead of strictly conditioning on all
v<t
. Moreover, multivariate GARCH models are typically not suitable for a large number of cross-sections. This is a significant extension,
as it implies that the current volatility is directly influenced by the volatilities of the neighboring nodes.

In a network log-ARCH model, the process is observed at the nodes
V
, with the
edges
E
define the dependence structure in the volatility dynamics. This
implies that the volatility at a given node is influenced not only by its own
past observations but also by the volatility of its adjacent nodes at the same
time point. Formally, the model builds upon the framework proposed by
Otto et al. 2024a

for dynamic spatiotemporal ARCH models. The observed process
Y_{t}(s_{i})
at
time
t
and node
v_{i}
is given by

Y_{t}(v_{i})=\sqrt{h_{t}(v_{i})}\varepsilon_{t}(v_{i}),

(20)

where
h_{t}(v_{i})
represents the volatility, and
\varepsilon_{t}(v_{i})

is a white-noise process. In the network log-ARCH model, the volatility

h_{t}(v_{i})
depends not only on past values of
Y_{t-1}(v_{i})
but also on
the contemporaneous values from adjacent nodes,
\{Y_{t}(v_{j}):j\in E_{i}\}
,
where
E_{i}
is the set of edges connected to node
v_{i}
.

Let

\boldsymbol{h}^{*}_{t}=(\ln h^{2}_{t}(v_{1}),\ldots,\ln h^{2}_{t}(v_{n}))^{\prime}

and

\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t}=(\ln Y^{2}_{t}(v_{1}),\ldots,\ln Y^{2}_{t}(v_{n}))^{\prime}
.
The network log-ARCH process of order one can then be expressed as

\boldsymbol{h}^{*}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\omega}+\mathbf{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t-1}+\rho\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t},

(21)

where
\mathbf{W}
is the matrix of edge weights (adjacency matrix) defining the
degree of volatility spillovers across the network,
\rho
is a parameter
capturing the strength of these contemporaneous network interactions, and
\mathbf{\Gamma}

is a diagonal matrix of node-specific ARCH effects
1
1

1

Note that this is
a specific extension proposed by
Mattera and Otto 2024
, deviating
from the original spatiotemporal log-ARCH model of
Otto et al. 2024a
.
.
The term
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is constant volatility intercepts. The matrix

\mathbf{W}
, analogous to a spatial weight matrix, defines how the volatility
spillovers propagate through the network.

The model can also be represented in an ARMA-like form of the log-squared observation:

\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\rho\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t}+\mathbf{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{u}_{t},

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{t}
are the log-squared errors, and

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
is a constant vector. This formulation highlights the endogenous
nature of the model, as
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{t}=(\ln(Y^{2}_{t}(v_{i})))_{i\in V}
appears on both sides of the
equation, necessitating specialized estimation techniques such as the Generalized
Method of Moments (GMM), as proposed by

Otto et al. 2024a
or quasi-maximum-likelihood
estimators (QMLE) by
Otto et al. 2023
to effectively handle the endogeneity issue.

5.2
Choice of the Network Weight Matrices and Dissimilarity Measures

The choice of weight matrices
\mathbf{W}
is based on the similarity between the log returns of the six OPEC countries and model-implied correlations, measured using temporal distance metrics. We consider six alternative dissimilarity measures: Euclidean distances, correlation-based distances, Piccolo distances, and distances derived from the CCC-GARCH, DCC-GARCH, and GO-GARCH models. Three of these were adopted from
Mattera and Otto 2024
, following the time series clustering methodologies outlined in
Maharaj et al. 2019
.

(1) Euclidean Distance.
A raw-data-based approach computing pairwise
distances between log-return observations:

d_{ij}^{\text{(Euc)}}=\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(y_{t}^{(i)}-y_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{2}},

(23)

where
y_{t}^{(i)}
denotes the oil price log return of country
i
at time
t
.
The corresponding network weight is defined as:

W_{ij}^{\text{(Euc)}}=\frac{1}{d_{ij}^{\text{(Euc)}}},\quad W_{ii}^{\text{(Euc)}}=0,

(24)

Therefore, smaller distances imply stronger connectivity.

(2) Correlation-Based Distance.
This feature-based approach uses the Pearson correlation coefficient
\rho_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}
between countries
i
and
j
, following the methodology of
Mantegna 1999
. The corresponding distance metric is:

d_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}})},

(25)

where
\rho_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}
is computed from the log-return series
\{y_{t}^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
as:

\rho_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}=\frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}^{(i)}y_{t}^{(j)}-\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}^{(i)}\right)\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}^{(j)}\right)}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}^{(i)})^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}^{(i)}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}^{(j)})^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right)}}.

(26)

This transformation maps high correlations (
\rho_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}\approx 1
) to small distances (
d_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}\approx 0
), providing a metric representation of similarity. The corresponding weight matrix is constructed as the inverse of the distance:

W_{ij}^{\text{(Corr)}}=\frac{1}{d_{ij}^{\text{(corr)}}},\qquad W_{ii}^{\text{(Corr)}}=0,

(27)

so that stronger co-movements translate into higher weights in the network structure.

(3) Piccolo-Based Distance.
Following
Piccolo 1990
and
Mattera and Otto 2024
, model-based dissimilarities in volatility dynamics are computed using the log-ARCH coefficient vectors from:

\ln h_{t}^{(i)}=\omega_{i}+\sum_{p=1}^{P_{i}}\gamma_{ip}\ln(y_{t-p}^{(i)})^{2},

(28)

where
\gamma_{ip}
are the autoregressive coefficients for country
i
. Coefficient vectors are zero-padded for comparability when
P_{i}\neq P_{j}
. This corresponds to a truncated AR(
\infty
) representation in the sense of
Piccolo 1990
. The Piccolo distance is defined as:

d_{ij}^{\text{(Pic)}}=\sqrt{\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left(\gamma_{ip}-\gamma_{jp}\right)^{2}},

(29)

with the associated weight matrix:

W_{ij}^{\text{(Pic)}}=\frac{1}{d_{ij}^{\text{(Pic)}}},\quad W_{ii}^{\text{(Pic)}}=0.

(30)

(4) CCC-GARCH Distance.
Constant conditional correlations
\rho_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}}
, extracted from the matrix
\mathbf{R}
in (
9
) as introduced by
Bollerslev 1990
, are used to construct a dissimilarity measure:

d_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}})}.

(31)

To derive bounded and numerically stable network weights, we applied a nonlinear transformation of the squared distance:

w_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}}=\frac{1}{2(1-\rho_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}})+1},\quad w_{ii}^{\text{(CCC)}}=0.

(32)

This formulation ensures finite weights and prevents singularities when
\rho_{ij}^{\text{(CCC)}}\approx 1
, which corresponds to a near-perfect correlation.

(5) DCC-GARCH Distance.
Based on the dynamic conditional correlations
\rho_{ij,t}^{\text{(DCC)}}
, which are elements of the time-varying matrix
\mathbf{R}_{t}
in (
14
)
(
Engle 2002
)
, we compute time-averaged correlations:

\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(DCC)}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{ij,t}^{\text{(DCC)}},\quad d_{ij}^{\text{(DCC)}}=\sqrt{2\left(1-\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(DCC)}}\right)}.

(33)

The corresponding network weights are defined as follows:

w_{ij}^{\text{(DCC)}}=\frac{1}{2\left(1-\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(DCC)}}\right)+1},\quad w_{ii}^{\text{(DCC)}}=0.

(34)

(6) GO-GARCH Distance.
Based on the time-varying correlations
\rho_{ij,t}^{\text{(GO)}}
extracted from the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{t}
in (
19
), as proposed by
Van der Weide 2002
, we compute time-averaged correlations:

\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(GO)}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{ij,t}^{\text{(GO)}},\quad d_{ij}^{\text{(GO)}}=\sqrt{2(1-\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(GO)}})}.

(35)

The associated network weights are as follows:

W_{ij}^{\text{(GO)}}=\frac{1}{2(1-\bar{\rho}_{ij}^{\text{(GO)}})+1},\quad W_{ii}^{\text{(GO)}}=0.

(36)

All raw spatial weight matrices—whether based on Euclidean, correlation, or GARCH-derived dissimilarities—are symmetric by construction, satisfying
W_{ij}=W_{ji}
and
W_{ii}=0
, thereby ensuring well-defined pairwise comparisons. The GO-GARCH-based dissimilarities offer a complementary perspective to CCC and DCC specifications: while the latter directly model pairwise correlations, GO-GARCH captures time-varying dependence through latent factor structures. This factor-based approach enhances estimation stability in high-dimensional settings and yields smoother inter-country correlation dynamics for network construction.

6
Results and interpretation

This section presents the study’s empirical findings in three stages. First, we report and discuss the estimation results of the univariate and multivariate GARCH models, which form the basis for the construction of the network weight matrices. Second, we analyze the structural patterns revealed by the network plots, providing insight into the interdependencies among oil-exporting countries. Finally, we evaluate the out-of-sample forecasting performance of each modeling approach by comparing their root mean squared forecast errors (RMSFE) and mean absolute forecast errors (MAFE).

6.2
Network Construction

Following the estimation of the CCC-GARCH, DCC-GARCH, and GO-GARCH models, we constructed six distance-based matrices to support the network-based analysis. Two raw data–based matrices were computed using Euclidean and correlation-based dissimilarities from monthly log returns. Subsequently, model-based dissimilarity matrices were computed using truncated AR(
\infty
) representations of log-squared returns, following
Piccolo 1990
, and form the conditional correlation structures produced by the CCC, DCC, and GO-GARCH models. All matrices were constructed following the methodology detailed in the previous section, ensuring comparability and coherence in the subsequent network-modeling framework.

The CCC-GARCH (d), DCC-GARCH (e), and GO-GARCH (f) networks exhibit a strong Middle Eastern core, with Saudi Arabia and the UAE emerging as central hubs, particularly Saudi Arabia, which appears as the primary source of risk spillovers to the majority of OPEC members, highlighting its clear capacity to propagate systemic risk among member countries. )
. These networks, constructed solely from statistical features, highlight how spatiotemporal and GARCH-based models capture the latent interdependencies shaped by geopolitical and macroeconomic influences. These interdependencies may also reflect broader macroeconomic shocks and geopolitical frictions, as emphasized by
Kilian 2008
. Moreover, as noted by
Mignon and Saadaoui 2024
, such factors are essential for understanding systemic risk and volatility propagation within the global oil market. These visualizations identify dominant clusters and key players under varying model assumptions and serve as the structural foundation for the forecasting performance evaluation conducted in the next subsection.

Following the estimation of the network-based volatility models, we assess their out-of-sample forecasting performance using two standard loss functions: root mean squared forecast error (RMSFE) and mean absolute forecast error (MAFE). These metrics are computed over the full forecast horizon
T
and across all
N
countries. Let
y_{i,t}
denote the realized log-volatility and
\hat{y}_{i,t}
its one-step-ahead forecast for country
i
at time
t
. The average forecast errors are then given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{RMSFE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{i,t}-\hat{y}_{i,t})^{2}},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MAFE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|y_{i,t}-\hat{y}_{i,t}|.

(38)

To formally test for differences in predictive accuracy, we employ the Diebold–Mariano (DM) test
(
Diebold and Mariano 1995
;
Diebold and Mariano 2002
)
. Let
e^{(m)}_{i,t}=y_{i,t}-\hat{y}^{(m)}_{i,t}
denote the forecast error from model
m
. The loss differential between models
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
is defined as:

d_{i,t}=L(e^{(m_{1})}_{i,t})-L(e^{(m_{2})}_{i,t}),\qquad\text{with }L(e)=e^{2}\text{ or }|e|.

The DM test statistic is computed as:

\mathrm{DM}_{i}=\frac{\bar{d}_{i}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\bar{d}_{i})}},\qquad\bar{d}_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{i,t},

(39)

where
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\bar{d}_{i})
denotes the heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) variance estimator of
Newey and West 1987
, with truncation lag
k-1=0
for one-step-ahead forecasts. Two-sided
p
-values are reported at the 5% significance level.

For comparisons involving nested models, we employ the Clark–West (CW) test
(
Clark and West 2007
)
, which adjusts for the bias introduced by overfitting in the larger model’s mean squared prediction error (MSPE). The adjusted loss differential is defined as:

f_{t}=(y_{t+\tau}-\hat{y}_{1,t+\tau})^{2}-\left[(y_{t+\tau}-\hat{y}_{2,t+\tau})^{2}-(\hat{y}_{1,t+\tau}-\hat{y}_{2,t+\tau})^{2}\right],

where Model 2 nests Model 1. The CW test statistic is then computed as the average of the adjusted loss differentials:

CW=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{t=R}^{T}f_{t},

(40)

where
P=T-R+1
is the number of out-of-sample observations, and
R
denotes the initial estimation window. Under the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy, the test statistic is asymptotically normally distributed. One-sided critical values apply: the null is rejected at the 10% significance level if
CW>1.282
, and at the 5% level if
CW>1.645
.

Their structure allows for a clear inclusion of instantaneous spillover effects, reduces computational burdens, and accounts for the interconnectedness of the OPEC members log returns.

Overall, the results demonstrate that network-based log-ARCH models constructed with GARCH-derived weight matrices offer a viable and interpretable alternative to standard multivariate GARCH specifications. Despite using a time-invariant weight structure, these models remain competitive with the fully dynamic standard DCC-GARCH model. This finding suggests that the static, time-averaged GARCH-based weights effectively capture the persistent volatility relationships among OPEC members. Future research could explore whether incorporating time-varying network structures yields further improvements, particularly during periods of structural change in oil markets.

7
Conclusion and Outlook

This study introduced a novel application of GMM-based Network log-ARCH models to examine volatility spillovers and interconnections among oil-exporting countries within the OPEC framework. By leveraging six symmetric network weight matrices constructed from raw-data log returns, model-based dissimilarities, and time-averaged conditional correlation structures, we demonstrate how network-based volatility models can capture the complex transmission of risk within oil markets. Despite OPEC’s coordination efforts, the analysis reveals persistent heterogeneities across member countries, likely reflecting geopolitical frictions and structural asymmetries, as evidenced by the underlying network structures.

While the Network log-ARCH models incorporating CCC-, DCC-, and GO-GARCH-based distances exhibit robust forecasting performance, consistently outperform several standard multivariate GARCH alternatives, and reduce the computational burdens associated with these models, the standard DCC-GARCH model remains the most accurate overall. Its superiority is plausibly attributed to its ability to capture dynamic correlation structures in real time, whereas the network models rely on static, time-averaged weight matrices. This structural limitation points to a promising avenue for future research: the development of fully dynamic network-based log-ARCH models incorporating time-varying network weight matrices
W_{t}
. Such models could better reflect evolving transmission channels and further enhance forecasting performance in volatile environments. Additionally, ensemble forecasting methods, as proposed by
Becker and Clements 2008
, may complement these approaches and improve predictive robustness.

This study also highlights broader opportunities for extending the analysis. Expanding the framework to include all OPEC+ members—and eventually all major oil-producing and exporting countries—would yield a more comprehensive view of systemic risks in the global oil market. Moreover, incorporating explanatory variables related to geopolitical tensions, sanctions, and macroeconomic fundamentals (e.g., supply-demand imbalances) could enrich the model specification and improve its interpretability. Future extensions of this study may also explore machine learning techniques to enhance adaptability, capture nonlinear dynamics, and exploit historical regularities more effectively. These directions offer a compelling path toward improving volatility forecasting and advancing the understanding of risk transmission mechanisms in the global energy markets.
