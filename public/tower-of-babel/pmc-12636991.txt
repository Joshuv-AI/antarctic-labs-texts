Duffy-null variant and practical implications for patient care: a scoping review
Authors: Edgar Asiimwe, Thye Peng Ngo, Elad Ziv, Andrew D Leavitt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate and map research examining clinical associations with the Duffy-null variant.DesignScoping review of the existing literature.Data sourcesWe conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, CINAHL and Web of Science for studies published in English between 1 January 2000 and 25 June 2024.EligibilityStudies were eligible for inclusion if they examined associations relevant to current standard clinical practice and met our protocol’s inclusion criteria.Data extractionWe extracted the following information from included studies: study year(s), patient population, sample size, study design, primary outcome and primary findings. Studies were grouped by outcome and synthesised in tabular and qualitative formats.ResultsA total of 2737 studies were screened, and 44 met our inclusion criteria. Most studies were observational, and the most common research question examined was the association with resistance to Plasmodium vivax malaria (9/44). Overall, we observed that the association between the Duffy-null variant and asymptomatic lower absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is demonstrated in large prospective cohort studies. The association with resistance to P. vivax malaria is primarily supported by large cross-sectional studies. There were no studies examining the practical applications of these findings, for example, optimal Duffy-genotype adjusted ANC thresholds for clinical decision-making in patients receiving chemotherapy. Finally, we observed that 19 different associations with this trait have been explored, several in conditions with no clear link to the Duffy trait, for example, progression rates in HIV/AIDS, risk of diabetes, etc.ConclusionsWe found established associations between the Duffy-null variant and asymptomatic lower ANC and with resistance to P. vivax malaria but a lack of data for the practical utilisation of these findings in clinical care. Future studies, such as those examining safe ANC values for entry into clinical trials and for ANC nadir for Duffy-null patients receiving medications associated with increased risk of neutropenia, for example, clozapine, are needed. We observed numerous reported associations of unclear clinical utility. Studies investigating associations with the Duffy trait should be guided by biologic plausibility and clinical utility of positive findings.Keywords: HAEMATOLOGY, GENETICS, Malaria, CHEMOTHERAPY, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Review

Introduction

High prevalence of asymptomatic absolute neutrophil counts (ANC) among individuals of African ancestry, formerly called Benign Ethnic Neutropenia, has been linked to a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the promoter region of the ACKR1 gene (rs2814778).1 2 The SNP is biallelic (T/C) and confers three distinct genotypes: TT, TC and CC.2 The CC genotype confers the Duffy-null variant in which carriers lose expression of the ACKR1 receptor (the Duffy antigen) on erythrocytes. 2 This loss confers increased resistance to Plasmodium vivax malaria, as the pathogen invades erythrocytes via the ACKR1 receptor.2 3 Additionally, it causes circulating neutrophils to migrate to extravascular compartments such as the spleen, leading to lower circulating ANCs in peripheral blood.1,3

Approximately 60%–65% of African Americans (AA), a lower fraction of Hispanic Americans and Middle Eastern populations, and less than 1% of individuals with European ancestry carry the Duffy-null variant.4 The higher prevalence of the trait among those with African ancestry is attributed to positive selection for the variant in regions with historically high P. vivax malaria prevalence.2 5 However, the prevalence of the variant varies by geography, even within the same continent, and is largely correlated with P. vivax malaria endemicity.5

Various studies have explored different associations with the variant. The clinical significance of several of these associations is not entirely clear. The main aim of this review was to evaluate and map existing research on associations with the Duffy-null variant. Specifically, we sought to identify associations supported by well-designed studies and to identify potential applications of current studies for patient care. Because current studies have explored a wide range of associations, we determined that a scoping review was the most appropriate approach for our objectives.

Methods

Search strategy

We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guideline extension for Scoping Reviews to delineate inclusion criteria, extract data and display data in flow charts (figure 1).6 We also created a study protocol using recommended guidelines for scoping reviews (online supplement).7 We searched PubMed, Embase, CINAHL and Web of Science for articles published between 1 January 2000 and 25 June 2024. We selected this period to include the period just before fine mapping of rs2814778 to lower ANC was first reported in the literature.1 2 Our search strategy was developed and refined in PubMed before being applied to other databases. To evaluate accuracy, we evaluated our PubMed results for the yield of key papers before expanding our search to the other databases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To enhance the quality, strength and focus of evidence, we only included peer-reviewed studies examining effects of the Duffy-null variant among adults (18 and older) and excluded conference abstracts, case reports and case-series. We also excluded narrative reviews and opinion pieces, because these are ranked lower in the hierarchy of evidence.8 Included studies examined the Duffy antigen in erythrocytes only; studies examining expression in other cell types (eg, in solid tumours) were excluded. We only included studies with clear implications for current guideline-based clinical practice and excluded those examining investigational associations; for example, studies that evaluated associations with chemokine levels or the utility of Duffy genotyping in forensic science were excluded. Our inclusion criteria are described further in the online supplemental materials.

Data screening, extraction and synthesis

The titles and abstracts of all identified studies were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation). After excluding duplicates, two independent reviewers (EA and TPN) screened the titles and abstracts for inclusion using the study protocol. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between EA and TPN and moderated by a third reviewer (ADL) when necessary. The following variables were extracted from eligible studies: authors, study year(s), patient population, sample size, study design, primary outcome and primary findings. Studies were grouped by outcome and synthesised in both tabular and qualitative formats (online supplemental eTable 1).

Definition of terms

For consistency, the term ‘Duffy trait’ in this review refers to the status of the Duffy antigens determined either by genetic (genotype) or serologic (phenotype) technique. ‘Duffy-positive’ refers to individuals expressing at least one antigen (a−b+; a+/b−; a+/b+). ‘Duffy-null’ refers to individuals who express neither antigen (a−/b−).

Results

Characteristics of the Included Studies

Our search identified 2737 studies, 44 of which met our inclusion criteria for abstraction and synthesis (figure 1). Collectively, these studies evaluated 19 different outcomes or associations, with the most common being resistance to P. vivax malaria (9/44) (table 1).

Duffy-null variant association with resistance to P. vivax malaria

Nine studies examined the association between the Duffy-null variant and P. vivax malaria.9,17 The largest, a multisite cross-sectional study in Ethiopia (N=11 000), estimated a 2–22 fold lower risk of P. vivax malaria infection among Duffy-null individuals.13 A moderately sized study in Madagascar (N=1878) estimated reduced risk at the lower end of this range (twofold lower odds): (OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.35 to 0.75) among Duffy-null individuals.14 Similar protective effects of the Duffy-null variant were reported in other cross-sectional case–control studies, including a study in Ethiopia (n=390) that found a lower incidence of P. vivax infections among Duffy-null versus Duffy-positive individuals (9% vs 91%).9 A study in Ethiopia (N=361) and a study in Sudan (n=992) reported similar findings: (4.4% vs 95.6%) 16and (7.9% vs 82.1),17 respectively. A study in Brazil (N=690) estimated a risk reduction of 91% (95% CI 67% to 97%) among Duffy-null individuals relative to Duffy-positive individuals.15 Conversely, three studies reported null effects11 or increased risk of P. vivax malaria10 12 in association with the Duffy-null variant. However, these studies used a case-only design (ie, only those with confirmed P. vivax were included) and did not make comparisons with disease-free controls (online supplemental eTable1).

Duffy-null variant and lower baseline ANC

Four of the identified studies reported descriptive statistics for ANC measurements in asymptomatic Duffy-null individuals.18,21 In these studies, Duffy-null individuals consistently had lower baseline ANC compared with Duffy-positive individuals (figure 2). Of these, the largest was a prospective cohort study of individuals self-reporting as Black African/Caribbean in the UK Biobank (n=7222, 83% of whom were Duffy-null).21 That study found a mean (SD) ANC of 2820 (1200)×109 cells/µL among Duffy-null individuals vs 4430 cells/µL (1410 cells/µL) among Duffy-positive individuals.21 Two other studies, reporting medians, had similar findings, including a median ANC of 2684 cells/µL among 256 Duffy-null women of African ancestry at a clinical site in the USA,19 and a median ANC of 2820 cells/µL in a cross-sectional study of 120 asymptomatic individuals at a separate site in the USA.18 The latter study also reported the mid-95th percentile ANC range for Duffy-null individuals in their sample (1210 cells/µL to 5390 cells/µL).18 The fourth study, a prospective cohort study of Duffy-null individuals referred for asymptomatic lower ANC in Sweden (N=66), reported the lowest ANC (median of 1000 cells/µL).20 Notably, this study differed from the other three as the study sample was comprised of individuals referred from primary care for markedly low ANCs.20

The Duffy-null variant is not associated with increased risk of infection

Two of the identified studies (one in a Swedish cohort and another in the UKB cohort) evaluated whether Duffy-null individuals have increased risk of infection.20 21 In the Swedish cohort study, the authors assessed this risk by comparing antibiotic prescriptions among Duffy-null individuals to those in the general population.20 With an average follow-up of 5.2 years per individual, they observed no significant difference between the two groups.20 In the UK Biobank study, the investigators compared the cumulative incidence of bacterial, viral or other infection between Duffy-null and Duffy-positive individuals over 20 years and observed no statistically significant difference between the two groups: risk ratio (RR) 0.95; (95% CI 0.83 to 1.08).21

Duffy-null variant, asymptomatic lower ANC and bone marrow biopsies

We identified two studies evaluating the yield of additional diagnostic tests such as bone marrow biopsies (BMBs) among Duffy-null patients with isolated lower ANC.22 23 One was a retrospective cohort study among AA patients undergoing BMBs for asymptomatic low ANC at three sites in the USA.22 In that cohort, the authors found that biopsies from AA patients with the Duffy-null variant and isolated lower ANC (n=35) were more likely to yield normal results compared with biopsies from Duffy-null AA individuals with no history of lower ANC (n=243) (97% vs 55%, p<0.001).22 The authors concluded that patients with the Duffy-null variant and isolated lower ANC might be at increased risk of undergoing unnecessary BMBs.22 Notably, the study identified only 35 occurrences of Duffy-null individuals undergoing BMB for asymptomatic lower ANC over a 22-year period after evaluating over 60 000 records at these three institutions.22 A second study (N=163) compared the proportion of haematologic diagnoses such as leukaemia among non-Hispanic white (NHW) patients referred for further evaluation of lower ANC to that of AA patients referred for similar reasons.23 In this cohort, the authors found that NHWs were more likely to be diagnosed with a haematologic disorder (28% vs 8%; p=0.05).23 The authors attributed this difference to the higher prevalence of the Duffy-null variant among AAs in their cohort (93% vs 50%, p=0.04); however, they did not adjust for the Duffy trait in their analyses.23

Duffy-null variant association with medication dose reduction and treatment discontinuation due to lower ANC

Four studies examined the association between the Duffy-null variant and treatment modification due to lower ANC.24,27 Two of these were among individuals with treatment-resistant schizophrenia on clozapine.26 27 Of these, one was a Genome Wide Association Study (N=552) conducted to identify SNPs associated with treatment discontinuation among individuals of African ancestry in a UK cohort.26 In that study, the authors found that compared with Duffy-positive individuals, Duffy-null individuals on clozapine had 20 times higher odds of reaching an ANC <1500 cells/µL during treatment (the level at which guidelines in that setting advise treatment discontinuation): (OR 20.4, 95% CI 5.3 to 314.3).26 The second study, a multisite open-label clinical study in the USA and Nigeria, evaluated clozapine discontinuation rates among 274 individuals (80% of whom were Duffy-null) over a 6-month follow-up period.27 They observed only one case of treatment discontinuation: a Duffy-null individual who developed severe lower ANC (<500 cells/µL) after 6 weeks of treatment.27 The authors concluded that there was no statistically significant difference in discontinuation rates between Duffy-null and Duffy-positive individuals and that the drug was similarly well-tolerated.27 The authors did not state whether Duffy-null patients required dose adjustments and did not provide details of how ANC levels varied with dose in this group.27

Two studies evaluated effects with other medications. One study (N=33) examined dose intensity and clinical benefit rates (CBRs) (a surrogate endpoint for survival) among patients with hormone-receptor positive breast cancer on palbociclib/letrozole.24 The authors observed that Duffy-null individuals were more likely to have their doses reduced due to low ANC (55.6% vs 7.7%; p<0.05) and to experience a reduced CBR (66.7% vs 84.6%).24 The authors did not state whether this difference in CBR was statistically significant.24

The fourth study examined the rate of treatment discontinuation for haematopoietic toxicity (a composite of leucopenia, lower ANC, thrombocytopaenia, pancytopenia and/or anaemia) by Duffy trait among patients taking azathioprine for various inflammatory conditions, for example, lupus and vasculitis.25 In multivariate analysis, the authors found that the Duffy-null variant was associated with increased risk of treatment discontinuation due to haematopoietic toxicity (HR 2.92; 95% CI 1.57 to 5.41).25 The study also evaluated the association with dose intensity using a separate cohort (paediatric patients with acute lymphocytic leukaemia on 6-mercaptopurine) and found significantly lower relative dose intensity among Duffy-null patients (p=0.013).25

Duffy-null variant association with HIV/AIDS

We identified six studies examining the association between the Duffy-null variant and HIV/AIDS.28,33 The outcomes assessed among these studies were infection, seroconversion and disease progression. Three of these studies reported significant associations with at least one of these outcomes. Of the studies, one was a prospective cohort study (N=142) in South Africa reporting nearly threefold higher odds of HIV seroconversion among HIV-negative Duffy-null individuals with ANC < 2.5×10⁹ cells/L relative to Duffy-positive individuals (OR 2.61; 95% CI 1.09 to 6.24).28 The decision to stratify by an ANC level of 2.5×10⁹ cells/L was not clear.28 The second study, a prospective cohort of HIV positive individuals in the USA (N=1132), found decreased risk of disease progression among leukopenic AAs (white cell count, WCC <4×10⁹ cells/L) with the Duffy-null variant: OR 0.20 (95% CI 0.07 to 0.60).29 This protective effect was not observed among non-leukopenic Duffy-null AAs (ie, those with WCC >4×10⁹ cells/L) or among leukopenic (WCC <4×10⁹ cells/L) Duffy-null NHWs.29 The authors concluded that there is a protective interaction (from AIDS progression) between the Duffy-null variant and leucopenia that is unique to AAs.29 The authors did not explain why they chose to examine the interaction with total WCC (leucocyte count) over the interaction with ANC (with which the Duffy-null variant is specifically associated).28 34 Similarly, a third study (N=3484) examined the risk of HIV infection and concluded that the Duffy-null variant increased the risk of HIV infection by about 40% among AAs and by 11% among native Africans.30 The study authors also suggested that the trait was protective from HIV disease progression as it attenuates the effects of CCL5 (a biomarker associated with HIV disease progression).30 Notably, none of these studies adjusted for negative social determinants of health.

Three subsequent studies conducted with data from four cohorts were unable to replicate these prior findings of an association between the Duffy-null variant and HIV/AIDs.31,33 In explaining this discrepancy, the authors observed that prior studies may not have adequately controlled for the effects of population stratification, a common source of bias in genetic association studies.31,33

Duffy-null variant association with complications in sickle cell disease

Three studies examined the impact of the Duffy-null variant among patients with sickle cell disease (SCD)35,37 focusing on SCD complications such as renal disease, ulcers and hospitalisations for vaso-occlusive crises. One study (N=237) among AA adults with SCD in the USA observed increased risk of chronic organ damage (85% vs 65%; p=0.018) and proteinuria (32% vs 12%; p=0.02) among Duffy-null patients versus Duffy-positive patients.35 In addition, the study found that those with the Duffy-null variant were less likely to receive chronic treatment with hydroxyurea (49% of Duffy-null individuals vs 72% of Duffy-positive patients; p=0.002).35 However, these results were not replicated in subsequent studies, including a cohort study in the French West Indies (N=212)36 or a prospective 2-year cohort study in the UK (n=272).37

Duffy-null variant association with outcomes among solid organ transplant patients

We identified two studies investigating the association between the Duffy trait and outcomes among African-American kidney transplant recipients.38 39 One study examined the risk of acute graft rejection and delayed graft function but found no significant difference by recipient Duffy status.38 The second study investigated five different outcomes and found that Duffy-null recipients who experienced delayed graft function following transplantation had higher risk of eventual graft failure compared with Duffy positive controls.39

Duffy-null variant association with other conditions

We identified additional studies examining the association between the Duffy-null variant and outcomes in the following conditions: breast cancer,40 systemic lupus erythematosus,41 SARS-CoV-2 infection,42 acute lung injury,43 asthma,44 triple-negative breast cancer,45 pre-eclampsia,46 chronic periodontitis,47 ocular toxoplasmosis,48 hypertension,49 non-alcoholic steatohepatitis,50 type II diabetes,49 graft vs host disease (GvHD)51 and risk of heart disease.52 The findings of these studies are summarised in online supplemental eTable1.

Discussion

We provide a comprehensive overview of studies that examine clinical associations with the Duffy-null variant. We found various observational studies demonstrating associations between the Duffy-null variant and (1) asymptomatic lower ANC and (2) resistance to P. vivax malaria. The lack of data on practical applications of these associations for patient care (eg, ANC entry criteria for clinical trials, and optimal ANC values for chemotherapy dose adjustments for Duffy-null individuals), and the heterogeneity in effect estimates of resistance to P. vivax malaria, represent significant knowledge gaps. Additional studies, including decision analyses and well-designed prevalence studies, are needed to address these gaps.

Although we did not conduct a formal risk-of-bias assessment, we observed various design limitations that require cautious interpretation of statistically significant findings in some studies. First, we found investigations for 19 different associations with the Duffy-null variant, which increases the risk of false-positive associations from multiple hypothesis testing. Additionally, various positive associations had no clear biologic link to the trait and were of unclear clinical utility, such as associations with HIV/AIDS acquisition or progression to AIDS. Notably, negative social determinants of health, which significantly impact outcomes in several conditions and disproportionately affect those with African ancestry,34 were rarely adjusted for. Future studies assessing additional or novel associations with the Duffy-null variant should be guided by biological plausibility and potential clinical relevance of positive findings.

We observed that some studies used composite or surrogate outcomes, warranting careful interpretation. For example, the azathioprine study reporting increased risk of treatment cessation due to haematopoietic toxicity among Duffy-null individuals defined toxicity broadly, grouping those whose treatment was stopped due to lower ANC with those stopping because of other cytopenias, for example, anaemia and thrombocytopenia.25 On the other hand, the study reporting 20 times higher odds of treatment discontinuation among Duffy-null individuals on clozapine evaluated a surrogate outcome (the odds of reaching an ANC <1500 cells/µL during treatment) and patients were not followed to ascertain whether treatment was in fact stopped.26

We also observed that several highly significant associations may be rare events; for example, the study reporting that 97% of BMBs among Duffy-null individuals were likely unnecessary only identified 33 such occurrences over a 22-year period at three institutions after screening over 60 000 eligible cases for inclusion.22 Additionally, the clinical significance of some associations was at times unclear. For example, the study evaluating palbociclib/letrozole tolerability among Duffy-null individuals reported a reduced CBR from lower dose intensity among Duffy-null patients.24 However, the clinical relevance of CBR is unclear, and the study did not evaluate other clinically important outcomes, for example, progression-free survival.53

Additional well-designed studies to address prior limitations and to quantify these associations with the variant (eg, with unnecessary BMBs and CBR) are likely of low clinical yield. Concentrating efforts on areas of clinical uncertainty, for example, ascertaining safe thresholds for Duffy-null patients for entry to clinical trials or for the use of certain medications such as clozapine might be a more impactful focus.

Limitations

A key strength of this study is that we used a systematic approach to minimise bias and enhance the replication of our findings. Additionally, we tested our search strategy to ensure accuracy and consistency. Finally, we only included peer-reviewed journal articles, enhancing the quality of the evidence included. Our study has several limitations; first, we did not include grey literature, as there is no requirement for peer review for such articles. Furthermore, we did not use a risk-of-bias tool required for formal systematic reviews; nonetheless, we believe that our decision to include only peer-reviewed articles and our rigorous review of articles during extraction and synthesis mitigates this limitation. Finally, we only included studies published after 2000, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our review.

Conclusions

We report, to our knowledge, the first scoping review of studies examining clinical associations with the Duffy-null variant. Overall, we found various observational studies reporting associations between the Duffy-null variant and both asymptomatic lower ANC and resistance to P. vivax malaria. However, we found a lack of data for the practical applications of these associations. Future work should focus on leveraging these observations to address existing uncertainties in patient care, such as how to factor the lower ANC of Duffy-null individuals into clinical trial eligibility and medication dose adjustments in cancer care and elsewhere.
