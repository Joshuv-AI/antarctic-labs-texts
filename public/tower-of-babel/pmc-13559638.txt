Expression of the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS axis in lung tissues across stages of non-small cell lung cancer and its mechanism in mediating immune cell regulation
Authors: Mingqiang Xue, Mingrui Shi, Jialing Chen, Xiaoming Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), face a dismal prognosis. Immune evasion driven by an imbalanced tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and an abnormal enrichment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) is a central pathogenic mechanism, yet the regulatory pathways involved remain elusive. This study aims to investigate the expression profile and immunomodulatory mechanism of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 12 (NLRP12)/nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1 (NF-κB)/inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) axis in LUSC, and to identify potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.MethodsWe integrated bioinformatics analysis from databases such as gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) and Kaplan-Meier Plotter with experimental validation in 48 clinical LUSC samples. Complementary in vitro models, including CD4+ T cell overexpression/silencing, pathway intervention, and co-culture assays, were employed. Mechanisms were verified using western blotting (WB), quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and flow cytometry.ResultsNLRP12 was highly expressed in LUSC and correlated with shortened overall survival (OS). Its expression showed a negative correlation with p-NF-κB and iNOS levels during tumor progression. Functionally, NLRP12 promoted the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Tregs by inhibiting NF-κB phosphorylation and subsequent iNOS expression. The interleukin (IL)-10/transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β) secreted by these NLRP12-driven Tregs enhanced the proliferation and invasion of LUSC cells in vitro, an effect reversed by neutralizing antibodies.ConclusionsNLRP12 serves as a specific prognostic biomarker in LUSC. The NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS/Treg axis remodels the TIME to foster tumor progression, offering a novel target and experimental rationale for precision diagnosis and therapy in LUSC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• NLR family pyrin domain containing 12 (NLRP12) is highly expressed in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and predicts poor prognosis, with stage‑dependent upregulation.

• NLRP12 inhibits nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1 (NF-κB)/inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)/nitric oxide (NO) signaling to drive CD4+ T cell differentiation into regulatory T cells (Tregs).

• NLRP12-induced Tregs secrete transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β)/interleukin (IL)-10 to promote LUSC proliferation and invasion.

What is known and what is new?

• LUSC has poor prognosis linked to immune evasion and Treg enrichment in tumor immune microenvironment. NF-κB/iNOS/NO pathway regulates CD4+ T cell differentiation and Treg induction. NLRP12 acts as a negative regulator of inflammation in some solid tumors.

• We are the first to report that NLRP12 is a LUSC‑specific prognostic biomarker (not lung adenocarcinoma). This study identified a novel NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOSTreg axis driving immune suppression in LUSC. We demonstrated that NLRP12 promotes LUSC progression via Treg‑derived TGF‑β/IL‑10.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• NLRP12 is a promising prognostic stratification marker for LUSC. The NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS axis is a novel immunotherapeutic target for LUSC. Targeting NLRP12 may reverse Treg‑mediated immune suppression and restore anti‑tumor immunity.

• Validate NLRP12 in large multi‑center LUSC cohorts. Develop NLRP12 inhibitors for preclinical testing. Combine NLRP12 targeting with immune checkpoint inhibitors in LUSC.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most prevalent and deadly malignancy worldwide, imposing a substantial disease burden, with annual mortality surpassing that of any other cancer type (1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all cases, yet the overall 5-year survival rate for patients is a mere 25%. Prognosis for patients with advanced-stage disease is particularly poor, often compounded by therapeutic resistance and recurrence, underscoring an urgent clinical need to elucidate novel pathogenic mechanisms and identify interventional targets (2).

Addressing this therapeutic bottleneck, research has identified dysregulation of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) as a core mechanism limiting treatment response in NSCLC, with tumor immune evasion mediated by functional alterations in immune cell subsets being particularly critical (3). As central effectors of adaptive immunity, CD4+ T cells can differentiate into various functional subsets, including regulatory T cells (Tregs). Tregs orchestrate immune evasion by secreting interleukin (IL)-10 and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), which directly inhibit the activation of effector immune cells (4-6). Although an aberrantly high proportion of Tregs within the tumor tissue of NSCLC patients is well-documented, the upstream regulatory pathways and molecular mechanisms governing this phenomenon remain undefined, representing a critical gap in current research.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying aberrant Treg regulation and identify potential therapeutic targets, molecules modulating the immune microenvironment have become a research focal point. Among these, nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 12 (NLRP12) has emerged as a prime candidate due to its unique function as a negative regulator of inflammation (7). In solid tumors such as gastric and lung cancer, NLRP12 has been shown to influence tumor progression by modulating the TIME (7,8). However, its expression pattern, immunomodulatory role, and underlying molecular mechanisms in NSCLC remain largely unexplored, constituting a significant knowledge gap.

The immunomodulatory effects of NLRP12 are primarily mediated through a cascade of core signaling pathways. The nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1 (NF-κB)-inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) axis, a key regulator of immune cell differentiation and functional homeostasis, may potentially mediate these effects (8-10). NF-κB governs the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and immune cell activation, while nitric oxide (NO) generated by iNOS can directly modulate CD4+T cell differentiation (11,12). Furthermore, studies have confirmed that this pathway can influence Treg differentiation by regulating forkhead box p3 (Foxp3) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) signaling (13,14).

Preliminary evidence from non-oncological studies has demonstrated that NLRP12 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by restraining activation of the NF-κB inflammatory pathway (15) and suppressing iNOS expression (16). Mechanistically, NLRP12 binds to NIK and facilitates its proteasome-mediated degradation, thereby negatively regulating the noncanonical NF-κB cascade (NIK-IKKα-p100/p52-RelB). This signaling inhibition represses transcription of downstream proinflammatory chemokines, alleviates chronic inflammation, and reduces inflammation-associated tumor susceptibility (17). Nevertheless, relevant investigations focusing on the NSCLC tumor microenvironment remain scarce to date. It has not yet been reported whether NLRP12 participates in CD4+ T cell recruitment, modulates Treg differentiation, and remodels the TIME via regulating the NF-κB/iNOS axis. Accordingly, systematic dissection of how NLRP12 governs the NF-κB/iNOS signaling axis and balances immune cell subsets is critical to illuminate the pathogenesis of NSCLC and uncover potential targets for immunotherapy. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0951/rc).

Methods

Public database mining for NLRP12 expression and prognostic value

NLRP12 expression data and corresponding overall survival (OS) information for patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) were obtained from the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on NLRP12 levels. Prognostic differences between the groups were analyzed using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated.

Immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) database. Infiltration abundances of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (DCs) in LUSC samples were extracted. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between NLRP12 expression, the infiltration levels of these immune cell types, and tumor purity. The impact of different immune cell infiltration levels on the OS of LUSC patients was also assessed.

Bioinformatic analysis of correlations between NLRP12, Treg infiltration and immunosuppressive genes

In this study, locally curated expression matrices, clinical metadata and immune infiltration datasets of LUSC were utilized. The primary analyses were performed using total_expr_list [“TCGA_LUSC”] and total_clin_list [“TCGA_LUSC”] stored in symbol.rda. Except for the tumor-vs.-normal expression comparison, only TCGA-LUSC tumor samples with the clinical field Tissue == “Tumor” were included in all remaining analyses, totaling 489 cases.

Immune infiltration data were derived from cell_list [“TCGA_LUSC”] within Cell-infiltration-list.rda. This matrix was organized in a sample-by-immune-feature format and contained outputs from multiple widely adopted immune deconvolution algorithms. Treg infiltration metrics estimated by the following algorithms were extracted for subsequent analysis: CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT_ABS, quantiseq and xCell.

The file treg_immunosuppression_genes.csv contained 49 Treg/immunosuppression-associated genes covering multiple functional categories, including Treg core identity, Treg surface markers, the IL-2 consumption axis, Treg suppressive machinery, Treg-derived cytokine-mediated suppression, adenosine/ATP metabolism, metabolic immune suppression, Treg trafficking, and Treg-recruiting ligands.

The procedures for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were as follows: (I) TCGA-LUSC tumor samples were stratified into NLRP12-high and NLRP12-low subgroups according to the median mRNA expression level of NLRP12; (II) the limma package was applied to generate genome-wide gene ranking based on differential expression between the high and low subgroups; (III) moderated t-statistics were adopted as the ranking metric for GSEA; (IV) the fgseaMultilevel function was used to test whether the Treg immunosuppression signature was enriched in the NLRP12-high subgroup.

Eight candidate genes were selected to validate the expression correlation between NLRP12 and Treg/immunosuppressive signaling: FOXP3, CTLA4, TGFB1, IL10, IL2RA, IDO1, CD274, and CXCL12. mRNA expression values of NLRP12 and these target genes were extracted respectively. For each target gene, the Spearman correlation coefficient between NLRP12 and the corresponding gene expression was calculated. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was performed on the eight Spearman-derived P values. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted as supplementary evidence.

All packaged databases and algorithm scripts described above are provided in https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0951-1.zip.

Clinical sample collection and baseline data compilation

Following approval by the Medical Ethics Committee of Henan Chest Hospital (approval No. 2022-06-02) and obtaining informed consent from patients, tumor tissue samples were collected from 48 patients with LUSC for the construction of a tissue microarray (TMA). Peripheral blood samples were also collected. Clinicopathological characteristics, including patient sex, age, tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage (I–IV), and lymph node metastasis status, were recorded to establish a comprehensive clinical information database.

Patients were stratified into an early-stage group (Stage I–II) and an advanced-stage group (Stage III–IV), as well as a metastasis group and a non-metastasis group, based on tumor stage and metastatic status, respectively, to facilitate subsequent subgroup analyses. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Clinical sample analysis

Molecular expression detection: multiplex immunofluorescence staining (triple labeling with four colors) was performed on the LUSC TMA to detect the localization and co-expression of NLRP12 (ER65953, HuaBio), p-NF-κB (Ser468, 82335-1-RR, Proteintech), and iNOS (22226-1-AP, Proteintech). Staining was carried out following the protocol of the BeyoTSA™ triple-color multiplex fluorescence staining kit (Shanghai Beyotime, P1351S). Western blotting (WB) (18) was used to assess the protein expression levels of FOXP3 (1:1000, 22228-1-AP, Proteintech), NLRP12 (1:1000), p-NF-κB (1:1000), and iNOS (1:1000) in LUSC tissues from different stages, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the loading control for densitometric normalization. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to measure the relative mRNA expression of FOXP3, normalized to GAPDH as the internal reference gene, using the ΔΔCt method (19) for calculation.

Immune Cell Proportion Analysis: Flow cytometry was employed to analyze peripheral blood samples from LUSC patients at different stages. Cells were stained with CD4-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, 65603, Proteintech) and CD25-phycoerythrin (PE-98288, Proteintech). Following gating on the lymphocyte population, the proportion of Tregs was quantified as the percentage of CD4+/CD25+ cells in the Q2 quadrant.

Cytokine and NO Level Detection: Concentrations of TGF-β and IL-10 in patient peripheral blood were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, with sample concentrations calculated from standard curves. NO levels in peripheral blood were determined using the Griess reagent method (Shanghai Beyotime, S0021S), with quantification based on a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of a nitrite standard.

CD4+ T cell isolation and generation of NLRP12 overexpression/silencing cell models

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from human peripheral blood. CD4+ T cells were enriched using immunomagnetic bead sorting. Cell purity was verified by flow cytometry detecting CD3-FITC (CL488-17617, Proteintech) and CD4-PE (PE-65603, Proteintech) double positivity, ensuring specificity and reliability for subsequent experiments.

An NLRP12 overexpression vector (OE-NLRP12) and its corresponding empty vector control (OE-NC) were constructed. Separately, three NLRP12 silencing sequences (NLRP12 shRNA#1/shRNA#2/shRNA#3) and a negative control sequence (Scramble) were designed and cloned. These constructs were transfected into CD4+ T cells. The overexpression vector pCMV-NLRP12-3 × FLAG-Neo was purchased from Miao Ling Biological (Cat# P40586). NLRP12 silencing vectors were constructed using pLKO.1-U6-Puro as the backbone. The NLRP12 shRNA primer sequences were:

H-NLRP12-shRNA1-F: CCG GCT GCT TCT TAA CAG CTT AAT TTC TAG AAA TTA AGC TGT TAA GAA GCA GTT TTT TG; H-NLRP12-shRNA1-R: AAT TCA AAA AAC TGC TTC TTA ACA GCT TAA TTT CTA GAA ATT AAG CTG TTA AGA AGC AG. H-NLRP12-shRNA2-F: CCG GCT CCG AGT CCT CTG GTT ATT TTC TAG AAA ATA ACC AGA GGA CTC GGA GTT TTT TG;

H-NLRP12-shRNA2-R: AAT TCA AAA AAC TCC GAG TCC TCT GGT TAT TTT CTA GAA AAT AAC CAG AGG ACT CGG AG; H-NLRP12-shRNA3-F: CCG GAC TGA AGA AGT TCA AGT TAT ATC TAG ATA TAA CTT GAA CTT CTT CAG TTT TTT TG; H-NLRP12-shRNA3-R: AAT TCA AAA AAA CTG AAG AAG TTC AAG TTA TAT CTA GAT ATA ACT TGA ACT TCT TCA GT.

Overexpression and silencing efficiencies were validated at both the protein and mRNA levels using WB and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), respectively. The most effective silencing sequence was selected for subsequent experiments to ensure the stability of the cell models.

Validation of the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS pathway regulatory mechanism

Based on the established cell models, experimental groups were set up, including Ctrl, NC, OE-NLRP12, KD-NLRP12, OE-NLRP12 + LPS (overexpression + NF-κB activator lipopolysaccharide, LPS), and KD-NLRP12 + 1400W (silencing + iNOS inhibitor 1400W). Protein expression levels of key pathway molecules NLRP12, p-NF-κB, and iNOS were detected by WB.

The concentration of NO, the catalytic product of iNOS, in the supernatant of each group was measured using the Griess method to determine the association between NO production and pathway activation status.

Regulatory role of the NLRP12 pathway on CD4+ T cell differentiation into Tregs

Under the grouping conditions described above, CD4+ T cells were cultured in vitro. The proportion of differentiated Tregs, identified as CD25-PE/FOXP3-FITC (A27684, ABclonal) double-positive cells, was quantified by flow cytometry.

Impact of NLRP12-modulated CD4+ T cells on tumor cell killing

A co-culture system was established by incubating CD4+ T cells with the LUSC cell line NCI-H226. Experimental groups included NC, CD4+ T cells alone, CD4+ T cells + OE-NLRP12, and CD4+ T cells + sh-NLRP12. Apoptosis of NCI-H226 cells was assessed using flow cytometry with an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis staining kit (Shanghai Beyotime, C1062S) to evaluate the cytotoxic capacity of the CD4+ T cells.

Following activation of CD4+ T cells with anti-CD3 (Cat# 300301, BioLegend), anti-CD28 (Cat# 377803, BioLegend), and IL-2 (Cat# HY-P704698, MedChemExpress), cells were cultured according to the groups mentioned above. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was measured using an LDH release assay kit (Shanghai Beyotime, C0016) to assess cell damage. The secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, Cat# SEKH-0047, Solarbio), Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, Cat# SEKH-0046, Solarbio), and IL-17A (Cat# SEKH-0026, Solarbio) in the cell supernatant were quantified by ELISA to determine the effect of NLRP12 on the functional phenotype of CD4+ T cells.

Influence of NLRP12-regulated T cells on target cell viability and invasiveness

A conditioned medium (CM) co-culture system was established using supernatants from differently treated CD4+ T cells added to LUSC cells. Groups included: Ctrl (control medium), T cell (CM from control CD4+ T cells), T cell + OE-NLRP12 (CM from NLRP12-overexpressing CD4+ T cells), T cell + KD-NLRP12 (CM from NLRP12-knockdown CD4+ T cells), and T cell + OE-NLRP12 + Ab (CM from NLRP12-overexpressing CD4+ T cells supplemented with a TGF-β neutralizing antibody, sc-130348, Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Cell viability of NCI-H226 cells in each co-culture group was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h using the CCK-8 method to evaluate the impact of T cells on target cell proliferation.

For cell invasion assays, Transwell chambers (coated with Matrigel, Cat# CLS356234, Corning) were used. Conditioned media from the different T cell treatment groups were placed in the lower chamber as chemoattractants. NCI-H226 cells (2.5×105) were seeded in the upper chamber. After 24 h of co-incubation, cells that invaded through the Matrigel were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted microscopically. This analysis assessed the regulatory effect of T cells on target cell invasion and validated the mediating role of TGF-β in this process.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. For data following a normal distribution with homogeneous variance, comparisons between two groups were performed using independent samples t-tests. For data with heterogeneous variance, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the least significant difference (LSD) method. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences between groups assessed by the log-rank test. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05, and P<0.01 indicated highly significant differences. All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Results

Prognostic value of NLRP12 in LUSC and stage-dependent characteristics of TIME-related indicators (Tregs/FOXP3/Cytokines)

To determine the expression and clinical significance of NLRP12 in lung cancer, we first analyzed its prognostic association using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (Figure 1A). In LUSC patients, high NLRP12 expression (n=241) was significantly associated with shorter OS compared to low expression (n=218; log-rank P=0.01; HR =1.4, P=0.01). In contrast, no significant survival difference was observed between high (n=237) and low (n=239) NLRP12 expression groups in LUAD patients (log-rank P=0.69; HR =1.1, P=0.68). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of the TMA revealed distinct co-localization and expression correlation among NLRP12, NF-κB, and iNOS in LUSC tissues (Figure 1B), providing histological evidence for subsequent mechanistic studies (detailed expression patterns are presented in Table 1).

To dissect the association between NLRP12 and the TIME, we first analyzed the impact of immune cell infiltration on LUSC patient prognosis. This analysis revealed that only the infiltration level of CD4+ T cells was significantly correlated with patient OS (log-rank P=0.04), whereas infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and DCs showed no significant prognostic association (Figure 1C). Further analysis using the TIMER database demonstrated that in LUSC, NLRP12 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration levels of B cells (partial.cor =0.196, P=1.86e−05), CD8+ T cells (partial.cor =0.208, P=4.82e−06), and CD4+ T cells (partial.cor =0.361, P=4.72e−16), and negatively correlated with tumor purity (cor =−0.386, P=1.97e−18) (Figure 1D).

To investigate the dynamics of Tregs during LUSC progression, we assessed the proportion of CD4+CD25+ Tregs in the peripheral blood of LUSC patients at different stages (I–IV) using flow cytometry (Figure 1E). The results showed a gradual increase in Treg proportion with advancing tumor stage: 0.26% in Stage I (Q2 quadrant), rising to 2.46% in Stage II, remaining at 2.44% in Stage III, and further increasing to 3.17% in Stage IV. Concurrently, the distribution of the overall CD4+ T cell population also changed markedly with stage (e.g., Q4 proportion decreased from 91.0% in Stage I to 68.0% in Stage III) (Figure 1F). Western blot and qPCR analyses further confirmed that both protein and mRNA expression levels of the Treg-specific transcription factor FOXP3 were significantly elevated with advancing LUSC stage (qPCR: F=5.980, P=0.03) (Figure 1G). ELISA results demonstrated that concentrations of the immunosuppressive cytokines TGF-β (F=3.620, P=0.03) and IL-10 (F=8.066, P=0.001) increased significantly with tumor progression, whereas the level of NO decreased significantly (F=6.989, P=0.001) (Figure 1H-1J).

In summary, NLRP12 is highly expressed in LUSC and is significantly associated with poor patient prognosis, establishing it as a specific prognostic biomarker for LUSC, but not for LUAD. Furthermore, the correlation of NLRP12 expression with immune cell infiltration and Treg enrichment in LUSC suggests its potential involvement in tumor progression through modulation of the TIME.

NLRP12 expression correlates with Treg infiltration and immunosuppressive signatures in TCGA-LUSC cohort

Correlations of NLRP12 expression with Treg infiltration and tumor microenvironmental immunosuppressive phenotypes were analyzed based on the TCGA-LUSC dataset.

Four independent immune deconvolution algorithms were applied to evaluate correlations between NLRP12 and Treg infiltration scores (Figure 2A). NLRP12 mRNA levels exhibited significant positive correlations with Treg infiltration estimated by CIBERSORT (R=0.17, P<0.001), CIBERSORT_ABS (R=0.21, P<0.001), quantiseq (R=0.13, P=0.003) and xCell (R=0.45, P<0.001), indicating greater Treg abundance in NLRP12-high LUSC tumors. Consistent with the above findings, GSEA revealed prominent enrichment of the Treg immunosuppression signature in NLRP12-high tumors [Enrichment Score (ES) =0.80, P=2.4e−14, Figure 2B], substantiating the association between NLRP12 and Treg-mediated immunosuppression at the pathway level. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate relationships between NLRP12 and eight representative genes governing Treg differentiation, recruitment and suppressive function (Figure 2C). NLRP12 expression was positively and significantly correlated with FOXP3 (R=0.41), CTLA4 (R=0.34), TGFB1 (R=0.26), IL10 (R=0.54), IL2RA (R=0.45), IDO1 (R=0.19), CD274 (R=0.11) and CXCL12 (R=0.51) (all FDR <0.05). Notably, CXCL12, a key chemokine mediating Treg recruitment, and IL10, a major Treg-derived inhibitory cytokine, displayed the strongest correlation coefficients. Collectively, these bioinformatic data reveal that elevated NLRP12 is tightly associated with increased intratumoral Treg infiltration and upregulation of Treg-related immunosuppressive genes in human LUSC, implying that NLRP12 may facilitate the formation of a Treg-enriched immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Correlation analysis between core molecular indicators and tumor stage revealed significant associations. NLRP12 expression was significantly higher in Stage III–IV tumors (295.3±120.6) compared to Stage I–II tumors (147.9±55.2) (P<0.001). Regulation of Treg differentiation by the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS pathway

CD4+ T cells were successfully isolated from human PBMCs using magnetic bead sorting (Figure 3A). Flow cytometry confirmed significant enrichment of the CD3+/CD4+ population post-sorting, validating cell purity (Figure 3B). Subsequently, WB and qPCR analyses confirmed the successful generation of the NLRP12 overexpression cell model at both the protein and mRNA levels (Figure 3C,3D). Furthermore, all three NLRP12 silencing sequences (shRNA#1, #2, #3) significantly downregulated NLRP12 mRNA and protein levels; quantitative results confirmed their silencing efficiency (Figure 3E-3G). Western blot analysis confirmed the modulation of pathway molecules (Figure 3H). Changes in NLRP12 protein levels under different intervention conditions validated the stability of the overexpression/silencing models (Figure 3I). The expression trend of p-NF-κB indicated that NLRP12 inhibited the phosphorylation and activation of NF-κB (P<0.01), an effect that was reversed by LPS stimulation (Figure 3J). iNOS, as a downstream target of NF-κB, exhibited expression changes consistent with p-NF-κB, and its upregulation by NLRP12 silencing was blocked by the iNOS inhibitor 1400W (P<0.01, Figure 3K). Finally, NO levels in the cell supernatant correlated precisely with iNOS protein expression, further validating the “NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS” signaling cascade (Figure 3L) and establishing a molecular foundation for subsequent experiments.

NLRP12 mediates polarization of CD4+ T cells towards an immunosuppressive phenotype

To simulate the TIME of LUSC and investigate the regulatory role of NLRP12 on CD4+ T cell function and fate, we established a co-culture system involving CD4+ T cells, Tregs, and the LUSC cell line NCI-H226 (Figure 4A). Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry revealed that, compared to the control group co-cultured with control CD4+ T cells, CD4+ T cells overexpressing NLRP12 significantly inhibited the apoptosis of NCI-H226 cells in the co-culture system. Conversely, CD4+ T cells with NLRP12 knockdown significantly enhanced the induction of apoptosis in NCI-H226 cells. These results suggest that NLRP12 within CD4+ T cells can attenuate their cytotoxic capacity against LUSC cells by modulating their functional state (Figure 4B). Further analysis of functional markers in CD3+/CD28+ IL-2-activated CD4+ T cells with varying NLRP12 expression levels showed that NLRP12 overexpression significantly reduced LDH release, indicating decreased cell damage and enhanced survival stability. Concurrently, it significantly suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, while upregulating IL-17A secretion (P<0.01). NLRP12 knockdown produced entirely opposite effects (Figure 4C-4F). These findings collectively confirm that NLRP12 suppresses the pro-inflammatory effector functions of CD4+ T cells and drives their polarization towards an immunosuppressive phenotype, consistent with the earlier observation that NLRP12 promotes CD4+ T cell differentiation into Tregs.

Impact of NLRP12-regulated T cells on target cell viability and invasiveness

To explore the impact of soluble factors secreted by NLRP12-modulated CD4+ T cells on the biological behavior of LUSC cells, we established a CM co-culture system (Figure 5A). CCK-8 cell viability assays showed that, compared to CM from control CD4+ T cells, CM from NLRP12-overexpressing CD4+ T cells significantly promoted the proliferation of NCI-H226 cells. In contrast, CM from NLRP12-knockdown CD4+ T cells inhibited tumor cell proliferation. Importantly, the pro-proliferative effect mediated by NLRP12 overexpression was significantly reversed upon addition of a TGF-β neutralizing antibody (Figure 5B). Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that CM from NLRP12-overexpressing CD4+ T cells significantly increased the number of invading NCI-H226 cells, whereas CM from NLRP12-knockdown CD4+ T cells markedly reduced the number of invasive cells. The pro-invasive effect was also effectively blocked by the TGF-β neutralizing antibody (Figure 5C,5D). These results indicate that NLRP12 in CD4+ T cells can enhance the proliferation and invasion of LUSC cells, at least in part, by regulating the secretion of soluble factors such as TGF-β, thereby promoting tumor progression.

Discussion

LUSC, a major subtype of NSCLC, is characterized by high heterogeneity and poor prognosis. Immune evasion resulting from a disrupted TIME constitutes a core pathogenic bottleneck, with aberrant enrichment of Tregs being a key feature of this immunosuppressive milieu (20,21). Although the pro-tumorigenic role of Tregs in LUSC progression is well-established, the upstream regulatory pathways and molecular switches governing their accumulation remain undefined, limiting the development of therapeutic strategies targeting the TIME. This study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism by which NLRP12 remodels the LUSC TIME via an NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS/Treg regulatory axis, establishing NLRP12 as a potential LUSC-specific prognostic biomarker and immunotherapeutic target, thereby offering a novel perspective for precision oncology in LUSC.

As a member of the NLR family, NLRP12 exhibits significant tissue-specific functionality in cancer (22,23). Our study found that NLRP12 is highly expressed in LUSC and correlates with poor patient prognosis, whereas it shows no significant prognostic association in LUAD. This phenomenon suggests that NLRP12 may participate in pathogenic mechanisms unique to LUSC. Considering the distinct histological origins, driver gene mutations, and TIME characteristics between LUSC and LUAD (24), LUSC tumors typically display more pronounced immune cell infiltration coupled with a stronger immunosuppressive phenotype (25). This particular microenvironment may provide a specific context for NLRP12 function. Furthermore, the positive correlation of NLRP12 expression with LUSC stage and its strong association with CD4+ T cell infiltration levels imply its sustained role in modulating immune cell function throughout tumor progression, driving the TIME towards a suppressive state. These findings expand the understanding of NLRP12’s role in solid tumors, providing the first evidence for its clinical value as a LUSC-specific prognostic biomarker and offering a new molecular basis for prognostic stratification in LUSC.

The NF-κB/iNOS axis is a central hub regulating immune cell differentiation and function, and its aberrant activation or inhibition is closely linked to tumor immune evasion (26-29). Our study confirms that NLRP12 inhibits the phosphorylation and activation of NF-κB, leading to downregulation of downstream iNOS expression and NO production, thereby promoting CD4+ T cell differentiation into Tregs. Jayaraman et al. confirmed that CD4+ T cell-expressed iNOS drives NO generation. Acting in a cell-autonomous manner, NO represses TGFβ1 transcription, decreases extracellular TGFβ1 secretion, dampens downstream TGFβ-Smad signaling, reduces Foxp3 transcript abundance, and consequently blocks the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells into Foxp3+ Tregs (13). In non-tumor inflammatory models including psoriasis, increased iNOS in CD4+ T cells coincides with STAT3 activation and downregulated FOXP3 expression. However, direct evidence from functional interventions to verify that iNOS modulates FOXP3 via STAT3 remains absent (30). This regulatory logic aligns with NLRP12’s established role as a negative regulator of inflammation (13,31,32), whereby it indirectly enhances the function of immunosuppressive cell subsets by suppressing pro-inflammatory pathway activity. Mechanistically, NO can inhibit Treg differentiation by modulating the STAT5-Foxp3 signaling axis (13,33,34). Consequently, NLRP12-mediated downregulation of iNOS would inevitably reduce NO production, relieving its inhibitory effect on Treg differentiation and ultimately facilitating Treg enrichment. Notably, the ability of LPS (an NF-κB activator) and 1400W to respectively reverse the effects of NLRP12 overexpression or silencing on Treg differentiation further validates the mediating role of the “NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS” pathway in this process. The elucidation of this mechanism reveals a novel regulatory pathway responsible for the aberrant enrichment of Tregs in LUSC, filling a critical gap in the understanding of NLRP12’s role in NSCLC immune microenvironment regulation.

Most current studies on lung cancer center on NLRP3, which upregulates iNOS via triggering the canonical NF-κB pathway and promotes the establishment of a pro-inflammatory, pro-tumor microenvironment (35-37). As a fellow NLR family member, NLRP12 displays completely opposing regulatory properties. Prior work in intestinal models confirmed that NLRP12 targets NIK to block the non-canonical NF-κB cascade. Extending these findings, our study further demonstrates that NLRP12 indirectly represses canonical NF-κB p65 activation within LUSC CD4+ T cells, diminishes iNOS expression, and governs Treg differentiation. The functional divergence of these NLR proteins arises from differences in their target substrates and cellular selectivity. So far, no study in the lung cancer field has delineated the regulatory relationship among NLRP12, the NF-κB-iNOS axis and Tregs.

Moreover, several investigations have characterized the anti-tumor activity of NLRP4 in lung cancer. Tumor cell-intrinsic high NLRP4 expression elicits T cell-independent anti-tumor immune responses dominated by NK cells and M1 macrophages, potently suppressing NSCLC progression. Despite sharing tumor-suppressive characteristics with NLRP12, existing NLRP4 research primarily concentrates on macrophages without exploring Treg cells (38). This represents one of the key novelties of the present work.

Secretion of immunosuppressive factors is a primary mechanism by which Tregs mediate tumor immune evasion (39). Our study demonstrates that NLRP12-overexpressing Tregs enhance the proliferation and invasion of LUSC cells through the secretion of TGF-β/IL-10, an effect reversible by neutralizing antibodies. TGF-β and IL-10 are classic immunosuppressive cytokines that not only directly inhibit effector T cell activity but can also promote malignant phenotypes in tumor cells by modulating their signaling pathways (40). From the perspective of tumor-immune cell crosstalk, NLRP12-mediated Treg activation and cytokine secretion establish a vicious cycle of “immune cell regulation- cytokine secretion- tumor cell malignant progression”, which likely contributes significantly to the poor prognosis of LUSC patients. Furthermore, our study found that NLRP12 inhibits the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α/IFN-γ) by CD4+ T cells, driving their polarization towards an immunosuppressive phenotype. This finding further refines the immunoregulatory network of NLRP12, indicating that it not only promotes Treg differentiation but also directly modulates the functional state of effector T cells, thereby comprehensively shaping a suppressive TIME.

This study has three key strengths. First, it establishes for the first time the clinical value of NLRP12 as a LUSC- specific prognostic biomarker, addressing the lack of specific molecular markers for prognostic stratification in LUSC. Second, it reveals a novel NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS/Treg immunoregulatory axis, providing a new molecular target for understanding immune evasion mechanisms in LUSC. Third, it confirms that NLRP12 mediates tumor-immune cell crosstalk by regulating Treg secretion of TGF-β/IL-10, offering experimental evidence for the development of combination therapeutic strategies targeting the TIME.

Despite these significant findings, this study has limitations. Firstly, the clinical sample size is relatively modest (n=48) and lacks multi-center validation; the prognostic value of NLRP12 requires confirmation in larger, independent cohorts. Secondly, the in vitro cell culture systems may not fully recapitulate the complex in vivo TIME, and the regulatory effects of NLRP12 on other immune cell populations (e.g., CD8+ T cells, macrophages) remain to be clarified. Of note, this study has not performed in vivo animal experiments. The effect of targeted NLRP12 intervention on LUSC progression requires further confirmation in animal models. Conclusions derived from in vitro assays cannot be reliably extrapolated to the complex in vivo immune microenvironment, as simplified single-cell culture systems fail to reconstruct multicellular interactions within tumors, bringing uncertainties to translational inference. Subsequent work will establish an orthotopic syngeneic LUSC model using immunocompetent DBA/2 mice. Through in vivo NLRP12 overexpression and knockdown manipulation, the regulatory roles of the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS axis in tumor progression, Treg differentiation and overall anti-tumor immunity will be validated in immunocompetent mice bearing intact T cell immunity, to complete the in vivo evidence chain. Future research should focus on expanding the clinical sample size through multi-center, large-cohort studies to validate the clinical utility of NLRP12. Concurrently, establishing LUSC animal models to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of NLRP12 inhibitors, potentially in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, will provide more robust pre-clinical evidence for clinical translation.

Collectively, this study systematically characterizes the expression pattern, prognostic significance and immunoregulatory function of NLRP12 in LUSC, revealing the pivotal involvement of the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS/Treg axis in reshaping the LUSC immune microenvironment. Poor immunotherapeutic responses frequently observed in LUSC are strongly linked to massive Treg infiltration and established immunosuppressive niches (20,21). In view of this clinical obstacle and the translational merits of NLRP12, three prospective translational strategies are proposed: developing NLRP12 agonists to suppress NF-κB/iNOS signaling and relieve tumor immunosuppression; combining NLRP12-targeted agents with PD-1 inhibitors to potentiate checkpoint immunotherapy via diminishing Treg accumulation; and employing tumoral NLRP12 levels as a predictive biomarker to stratify candidates for immunotherapy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study systematically characterizes the expression pattern, prognostic significance and immunoregulatory function of NLRP12 in LUSC, revealing the pivotal involvement of the NLRP12/NF-κB/iNOS/Treg axis in reshaping the LUSC immune microenvironment. This signaling axis represents a novel therapeutic target to tackle immunoresistance in lung squamous cell carcinoma.
