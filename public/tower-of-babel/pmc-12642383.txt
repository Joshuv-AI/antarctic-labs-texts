Targeting of specific CCR5-G protein complexes underlies biased signaling by HIV-1 envelope glycoproteins
Authors: Romain Gasser, Philippe Colin, Célien Jacquemard, Théo Montagné, Zhicheng Zhou, Véronique Pons, Evi Kostenis, Céline Galés, Esther Kellenberger, Bernard Lagane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCCR5 is a critical receptor for anti-infectious immunity. It acts by binding chemokines, which activate Gαβγ protein-dependent signaling and ultimately regulate leukocyte recruitment. However, CCR5 is also implicated in numerous pathological conditions, raising interest in its potential as a therapeutic target. Notably, CCR5 serves as a coreceptor for HIV-1 envelope glycoproteins (Envs), allowing viral entry into host cells and initiating infection. Whether and how Envs trigger CCR5 signaling remains debated, with conflicting evidence regarding their ability to mimic chemokine-induced activation of the receptor. This study aims to clarify the nature of Env-induced CCR5 signaling.MethodsWe used a real-time bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based assay in living cells to monitor the activation of ten Gα subunits from all four major G protein families in response to a prototypical CCR5 chemokine or HIV-1 Envs. Functional consequences of receptor activation were assessed via Ca2+ flux and cAMP inhibition assays. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to investigate differences in ligand-induced conformational changes in CCR5. The role of membrane cholesterol in ligand engagement was studied using methyl-β-cyclodextrin treatments and binding assays.ResultsHIV Envs act as biased agonists at CCR5, selectively activating distinct G protein families compared to chemokines. Notably, Env-induced signaling, but not chemokine signaling, was strictly dependent on specific Gα subunit abundance and CCR5/G protein stoichiometry. This dependency likely explains the conflicting data in the literature regarding Env agonism. Molecular modeling revealed that Envs and chemokines stabilize distinct active conformations of CCR5, particularly at the G protein interaction site, likely accounting for their differential signaling behaviors. Additionally, cholesterol depletion impaired chemokine, but not HIV-1 Env, binding to CCR5, suggesting that the two ligand types engage the receptor within distinct membrane microenvironments.ConclusionsThese findings reveal that CCR5 exists in a mosaic of distinct membrane-associated functional states, differentially accessed by viral and endogenous ligands. This spatial and signaling compartmentalization provides a mechanistic framework to understand CCR5’s functional diversity in health and disease and highlights the potential for developing context-specific CCR5-targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02508-1.Keywords: G protein, Functional selectivity, Chemokine receptor, GPCR activation

Background

C-C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) is a seven transmembrane domain (7TM), G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that regulates leucocyte trafficking and activation in immune responses against pathogens and inflammation. It is also involved in autoimmune diseases, cancer, atherosclerosis, graft-versus-host-diseases and neurological disorders [1–6]. Notably, CCR5 plays a critical role in HIV-1 infection. It acts as a CD4 co-receptor for the gp120 subunit of the envelope glycoprotein (Env) of R5-tropic HIV-1 strains, allowing their entry into CD4 + T lymphocytes and thus initiating the infection process [7]. Maraviroc (MVC) is the only marketed anti-HIV-1 drug that specifically targets CCR5 [8], acting by preventing gp120 binding to the receptor [9, 10]. However, research to inhibit CCR5 in HIV-1 infection is still topical [11], boosted by the cases of remission from the infection in individuals transplanted with hematopoietic stem cells homozygous for the CCR5-Δ32 mutation [12]. CCR5 inhibition is also increasingly considered in other clinical contexts [2, 4, 13–15] but its safety is debated as it can have adverse effects in some viral infections [16].

The agonist chemokines CCL3, CCL4 and CCL5 bind and activate CCR5. Activated CCR5 then activates its cognate αβγ G proteins at the plasma membrane, triggering intracellular signaling. G protein activation involves GTP binding to the α subunit, leading to conformational changes within the heterotrimer allowing the Gα-GTP subunit and the βγ dimer to activate secondary effector proteins such as adenylate cyclase and phospholipase C β (PLCβ) [17]. G proteins are classified into four main families based on the α subunit (Gαi/o, Gαs, Gαq/11 and Gα12/13), with different α isoforms within each family as well as various β and γ subunits combining to form a diverse array of heterotrimers [17]. CCR5 can activate Gi/o, Gq and G12 proteins, but how the receptor distinguishes between these proteins remains to be fully understood. As with other GPCRs [18, 19], the signaling pathways of CCR5 may differ from one chemokine to another, a process referred to as biased agonism or functional selectivity [20–22]. This occurs because different chemokines do not stabilize the same active conformations of the receptor, a concept recently supported by high-resolution structures of active CCR5 conformations in complex with CCL3, CCL5 [23] or the N-terminally modified chemokine CCL5-6P4 [24]. Actually, in recent years, it has become increasingly evident that CCR5 can exist as distinct populations exhibiting variations in post-translational modifications and conformational dynamics, which is modulated by interactions with cytosolic or membrane-associated proteins, as well as with membrane lipids (e.g., cholesterol) [21, 25–29]. CCR5 can also form homo- or hetero-oligomeric arrangements, which can also significantly influence its conformational and functional properties [30–33]. Structurally distinct CCR5 ligands often exhibit selective affinity for specific CCR5 populations, resulting in variable binding levels to CCR5-expressing cells [25, 27, 30, 34]. Recently, it has been proposed that chemokine binding to particular CCR5 populations biases the receptor toward activation of specific G protein subtypes [21].

Env signaling through CCR5 has been reported [35–41]. However, for unknown reasons, there are contradictory data from one study to another regarding the nature of the effectors and signaling pathways activated by Envs [35, 37, 39, 42–44] (and for reviews see [45, 46]). The underlying mechanisms of Env signaling also remain unclear. A recent cryo-EM structure (PDB ID: 6MEO) revealed that Env-bound CCR5 adopts an inactive conformation [7], similar to the conformation bound to the inverse agonist MVC [9, 47]. However, this structure did not include the G protein necessary to stabilize the active CCR5 [23], leaving the mechanism of CCR5 activation by Env unresolved [48]. We previously demonstrated that chemokines and Envs differentially bind to distinct CCR5 conformations [27, 49]. CCL3 binds with high-affinity only to CCR5 coupled to pertussis toxin (PTX)-sensitive, Gαi/o proteins, while HIV-1 gp120 can bind to other CCR5 conformations, which may differ depending on the viral strain [30, 48]. Altogether, these findings suggest that HIV-1 Envs can activate subsets of CCR5 conformations distinct from those activated by chemokines, which might explain why Env-induced CCR5 signaling may differ from chemokine signaling [38, 40, 50–53].

To investigate this hypothesis, we used standard receptor signaling assays combined with a bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based assay allowing real time monitoring of the activation of 10 Gα subunits from the four main families in living cells. Our data revealed that HIV-1 Envs bias CCR5 toward unique G protein signaling complexes. Molecular dynamics simulations further indicate that Envs stabilize specific active conformations of CCR5/G protein complexes. These results highlight the complexity of HIV-1 Env signaling, which is intricately linked to CCR5 conformational plasticity, and open up new perspectives in the study of its role in HIV-1/AIDS pathophysiology. They also provide a better understanding of the molecular basis of CCR5’s pleiotropic functions in immunity and disease, paving the way for more targeted strategies in CCR5 inhibition.

Methods

Cells and reagents

Parental HEK 293 T cells and HEK-R5 cells were detailed in our previous articles [27, 30]. In particular, HEK-R5 cells have been well described regarding their binding characteristics (Bmax, Kd) of CCR5 chemokines and HIV-1 gp120s and in terms of CCR5 conformational heterogeneity [10, 27, 30]. CCL4/MIP-1β (R & D Systems), Maraviroc and TAK779 (Sigma-Aldrich) were reconstituted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CCL5 analogs 5P12 and 6P4 were provided by Dr Oliver Hartley (University of Geneva). Soluble CD4 (sCD4) was produced and purified as described previously [54]. Production of monomeric, soluble gp120s #25 and #34 was also described in previous works [30, 48]. These gp120s were chosen here because we have previously shown that they do not bind/stabilize the same conformations of CCR5 and confer the viruses with different cell tropisms [30, 48]. The gp120s were also isolated from different stages of the infection, during the asymptomatic stage (gp120#25) or at the AIDS stage (gp120#34). Recombinant BaL gp120 was obtained from the NIH AIDS Research and Reference Reagent Program. PTX was from Sigma-Aldrich and FR900359 was described previously [55].

BRET assays

BRET assays to measure G protein activation were carried out as previously described [20, 56, 57]. Briefly, twenty-four hours after seeding, HEK-P or HEK-R5 cells were transfected with the mixture of plasmids encoding the different G protein subunits (as described [57]) using PEI as transfection reagent. Forty-eight hours later, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 0.1% glucose (w/v) at a final concentration of 1 µg protein/µL. Then, 80 uL of cell suspensions were distributed into wells of a 96-well microplate (PerkinElmer) and incubated for 1 (CCL4) or 5 min (gp120) in the presence of buffer alone (basal BRET) or ligands. Unless otherwise specified, gp120s at 100 nM were preincubated with 300 nM sCD4 for 10 min before addition to cells. BRET signal between RLuc8 and GFP2 was then measured after the addition of the RLuc8 substrate coelenterazine 400a/DeepBlue C (5 µM, Interchim). BRET signals were collected using a modified Infinite F500 reader (Tecan Group Ltd) and expressed as the ratio of emission of GFP2 (510–540 nm) to that of RLuc8 (370–450 nm).

cAMP production assay

Twenty-four hours after seeding, HEK-R5 cells were transfected with the plasmid encoding the cAMP BRET sensor CAMYEL [58]. Forty-eight hours later, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 0.1% (w/v) glucose at room temperature and distributed (80 µg proteins/well) in a 96-well microplate (PerkinElmer), stimulated with 10 µM forskolin for 10 min in the absence or in the presence of the different ligands and incubated with coelenterazine h (5 µM) two minutes after addition of forskolin. BRET readings were collected using a modified Infinite F500 (Tecan Group Ltd). The BRET signal (BRET ratio) was calculated by the ratio of emission of YFP (520–570 nm) to RLuc (370–480 nm).

Calcium imaging experiments

HEK-R5 or HEK-P cells, treated or not for 16 h with 100 ng/mL PTX or 2 h with 100 nM FR900359 in DMEM, were washed once with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (Gibco) containing 140 mg/L CaCl2 (HBSS-Ca2+), incubated for 30 min at 37◦C with HBSS-Ca2+ containing 20 mM Hepes (HHBSS-Ca2+), 1 mM fluo-4 acetoxymethyl (AM) ester (Invitrogen) and 0.02% pluronic F-17 (Invitrogen) and then with HBSS-Ca2+ for 30 min at room temperature. New HBSS-Ca2+ was then added to cells before ligand stimulation and calcium flux recording. Cells were imaged with a Zeiss ApoTome 2 inverted microscope equipped with a thermostatic chamber set at 37 °C and a 10x/0.45 NA objective. Images were acquired using an AxioCam HRm (Zeiss) and the Zeiss Zen Blue 3.1 software. Kinetic analysis was performed using 150 or 300 recordings (one per second). Baseline fluorescence (F0) was determined for 0–10 s, and then fluorescence was measured from the 11 to 150 or 300 s. The effects of CCL4 or 6P4 were studied under conditions where the chemokines or HBSS-Ca2+ alone as control were added at t = 20–30 s, after preincubation with TAK779 (10µM), 5P12 (100nM) or HBSS-Ca2+ alone (added at t = 10 s). The experiments with sCD4/gp120 complexes (100 nM) were performed after addition of the complexes or HBSS-Ca2+ alone at t = 10 s and then CCL4 or HBSS-Ca2+ alone at t = 70 s and recording of calcium flux up to 300 s. At t = 30 s before the end of recording, ionomycin (10 µg/mL) was added to the cells to induce calcium influx and serve as a positive control of the calcium signal. Agonist-induced changes in calcium flux in individual cells were analyzed using Image J software and expressed as ΔF/F0 ratios, where ΔF is the maximum difference in fluorescence intensity relative to baseline fluorescence.

MβCD treatment and ligand binding assays

HEK-R5 cells at 80% confluence were detached using PBS/EDTA, washed once with PBS and then resuspended in DMEM (w/o SVF nor antibiotics) prewarmed at 37 °C and containing or not serial dilutions of MβCD (Sigma-Aldrich). Treatments were carried out for 30 min at 37 °C under gentle mixing. Cells were then centrifuged and resuspended in buffer for subsequent trypan blue exclusion test of cell viability and ligand binding assays. Expression, purification and metabolic labeling of gp120#34 with 35S was performed as described previously [30, 59]. The protocols for equilibrium binding experiments of 125I-CCL3 (0. 2 nM final concentration) and sCD4-complexed 35S-gp120#34 (10 nM plus 200 nM sCD4) to intact HEK-R5 cells were also detailed in our previous works [10, 30, 59]. Anti-CCR5 mAbs 2D7 (BD Biosciences) or 45531 (R & D Systems) at a final concentration of 1.5–10 µg/mL, respectively, or control isotype at the same concentrations (non-specific binding), were incubated with 5 × 104 HEK-R5 cells at room temperature for 90 min in 200 µL final volume of PBS containing 0.2% BSA (binding buffer, BF). Cells were then centrifuged at low speed (1500 rpm), washed and incubated for 1 h on ice in BF containing goat anti-mouse, AlexaFluor 647-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen) at a 1:500 dilution. After washing of cells in ice-cold BF, mAb binding levels to cells was determined on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and data were analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The four models of the complexes CCL3–CCR5(a)–Gi, CCL3–CCR5(a)–Gi1−mini, CCR5(a)–Gi, and CCR5(a)– Gi1−mini were prepared from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) structure 7F1Q describing active CCR5 in complex with CCL3 and Gi1 [23]. For the two apo models, CCL3 was removed, and for the models with Gi1−mini, only the last 18 residues of the Gαi C-terminus bound to CCR5 were retained. The two models of the complexes CD4–gp120–CCR5(a)– Gi1−mini were prepared using the PDB structures 7F1Q, 6MEO (complex of CD4, gp120 from HIV-1 isolate 92BR020 and inactive CCR5), and 3J70 (trimer of CD4, gp120, gp41 and monoclonal antibody 17b) as detailed in Supplementary Information (Additional file 1). The V1, V2, and V4 loops of gp120 from the 3J70 structure were grafted onto gp120 from structure 6MEO, which initially lacked these loops. Details about gp120 loop modeling are provided in our previous article [48]. Structures 7F1Q and 6MEO were aligned on the transmembrane residues of CCR5 to dock gp120 into the transmembrane cavity of active CCR5 from 7F1Q. The N-terminal tail, extracellular tips of TM1 and TM5, and ECL2 of CCR5 from structure 6MEO were grafted onto the activated CCR5 structure. The sequences of gp120#25 and #34 [30] were used to construct the 3D structures of the two variants by homology modeling. In all models, tyrosines at positions 10 and 14 were mutated to sulfotyrosine. The preparation of all models was done with MOE 2020.09 software. Details of the model preparation are provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S7A, B.

The solvent, ions, and membrane lipids were added using an in-house script, applying the same model for each complex. Briefly, the model containing the membrane (POPC/POPE/CHOL at a 2:2:1 ratio), water molecules, and ions (KCl, 0.15 M) was generated with CHARMM-GUI without any protein. For each CCR5 complex model, CCR5 was inserted into the membrane, and lipids, water molecules, and ions that clashed with the proteins were removed.

Topology and coordinate files were generated with the tleap program from AMBER18 using the ff14SB, lipid14, and TIP3 force fields. The force field for sulfotyrosine was taken from our previous study [32]. Systems were minimized with pmemd from AMBER18 for 5000 cycles using the steepest-descent method for the first 2500 steps and the conjugate gradient method for the next 2500 steps. Systems were heated with pmemd.cuda from AMBER18 from 0 to 300 K over 1 ns using the Langevin thermostat at constant volume with constraints on atomic coordinates. Constraints were gradually released over approximately 5 ns at constant pressure, and equilibration continued for an additional 50 ns without constraint (details about the constraints applied during the minimization, heating and equilibration phases are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1). Then, five independent 100 ns simulations were performed for each system. Details about MD trajectory analysis are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

Results

HIV-1 gp120s activate Gi/o proteins within specific CCR5/Gα stoichiometric ratios

Whether HIV-1 Envs activate PTX-sensitive, Gi/o proteins is unclear [35, 39]. Here, we first measured in CCR5-expressing HEK 293 T (HEK-R5) cells the capacity of CCL4 and two HIV-1 gp120s (#25 and #34 described in Methods) to inhibit cAMP production by adenylyl cyclase, a typical Gi/o effector. We previously showed that CCL4 and HIV-1 gp120s (in complex with soluble CD4) bind specifically to CCR5 on HEK-R5 cells with affinity constant values of 0.3 and 7 nM, respectively [27, 30]. At saturating concentrations, CCL4 (10 nM) effectively decreased cAMP levels, as previously reported [21, 60, 61]. However, gp120s (100 nM) exhibited significantly lower, yet still statistically significant, efficacies (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

To understand these differences between native and viral ligands, we measured their ability to directly activate Gi/o proteins in living HEK-R5 cells using a BRET assay, as previously described [20, 56]. Particularly for the Gi family, which includes numerous different Gα subunits, this BRET assay enables the selective measurement of the activation of each individual Gαi subunit. This distance-proximity assay measures the direct interaction between the Gα-RLuc8 and GFP2-Gγ subunits transiently co-expressed in cells with the complementary subunit Gβ. In the absence of G protein activation, the Gα-RLuc8 and GFP2-Gγ subunits remain tightly associated, resulting in the detection of a basal BRET signal. Upon receptor activation, conformational changes in the G protein results in the dissociation of Gα from Gγ. This separation increases the distance between the RLuc8 energy donor and the GFP2 energy acceptor, leading to a decrease in the BRET signal. Consequently, the ΔBRET value, defined as the difference between the BRET signal induced by the agonist and that under mock conditions (absence of the agonist), is negative. Hence, we measured the activation of Gi/o proteins in living HEK-R5 cells co-expressing Gαi1-RLuc8, Gαi2-RLuc8, Gαi3-RLuc8, GαoA-RLuc8, GαoB-RLuc8 or Gαs-RLuc8 (negative control) in the presence of GFP2-Gγ2 and Gβ1 subunits. Since we previously demonstrated that the receptor/Gα stoichiometry can influence responses to biased agonists [56], experiments were conducted across different levels of Gα-RLuc8 expression, determined by luciferase activity, i.e., at low (RLU range: 20,000–50,000), medium (50,000–200,000) or high (> 200,000) Gαi-RLuc8 expression ranges. CCL4 robustly activated Gi1 (Fig. 1A) as well as the related proteins Gi2, Gi3, GoA and GoB, with similar activation levels (i.e., ΔBRET values) observed across all Gα-RLuc8 expression levels (Fig. 1B). As expected, it did not activate Gs, consistent with previous findings [20]. CCL4-mediated activation of Gi/o was absent in parental HEK 293 T (HEK-P) cells and was inhibited by the CCR5 antagonist MVC in HEK-R5 cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S2), confirming that activation depends on CCL4 binding to CCR5. MVC alone had the opposite effect to CCL4, i.e., it increased BRET signals (Fig. 1A) and this depended on CCR5 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Previous structural analyses showed that MVC stabilizes CCR5 in an inactive state [9, 47], explaining its inverse agonist properties [10]. These results therefore suggest that the way BRET varies within the αβγ trimer is linked to the activation state of ligand-bound CCR5.

HIV-1 gp120s induced Gi/o activation but in a different way compared to CCL4. Initial experiments with Gi1 revealed slower Gi activation (5 vs. 1 min), consistent with previous data showing slower CCR5 signaling kinetics for gp120s compared to chemokines [40]. Another key difference compared to CCL4 is that gp120s activated Gi1 with a maximal efficacy 4–5-fold lower, and only at high Gα expression level (see Fig. 1A for gp120s #25 and #34 and Additional file 1: Fig. S4 for gp120 of the HIV-1 strain BaL). These effects of gp120s on Gi1 activation depended on CCR5, as they were not observed in the presence of MVC (Fig. 1C). They also required addition of soluble CD4 (sCD4) (Fig. 1D) but could not be achieved with sCD4 alone (Fig. 1E). This is in agreement with the fact that Env must interact with CD4 for binding CCR5 [7]. Finally, gp120-induced Gi activation was absent in HEK-P cells that do not express CCR5 (Fig. 1F and G). Gp120s #25 and #34 in complex with sCD4 also significantly activated Gi2 and Gi3, although their efficacies were notably lower than those of CCL4. Furthermore, their effects were highly dependent on the Gα expression levels, again contrasting with the activation observed with CCL4. Interestingly, and in contrast to Gi1, the activation of Gi2 and Gi3 tended to be more efficient at low Gα expression levels. Finally, both gp120s selectively activated GoB, but not GoA, and, similar to CCL4, did not stimulate Gs proteins. Interestingly, GoB activation by gp120 was best observed when GαoB expression levels were high, while conversely, a trend towards decreased GoB activation was observed when GαoB expression levels increased in the case of CCL4 (Fig. 1B, $P < 0.05 using Tukey’s test).

In summary, our results demonstrate that HIV-1 Envs act as weak partial agonists regarding adenylyl cyclase inhibition. This is linked to activation of Gi/o proteins that is of low efficacy, is restrained to specific isoforms (Gi1-3 and GoB but not GoA) and is manifested within narrow ranges of CCR5/Gα stoichiometric ratios in a Gα isoform-dependent way. In contrast, the full native agonist CCL4 activates all members of the Gαi/o family with similarly high efficacies, independent of their expression levels. Altogether, these results suggest that HIV-1 gp120s selectively activate only subsets of Gi/o-coupled CCR5.

HIV-1 gp120s but not chemokines direct CCR5 toward activation of Gq and G13 proteins

Using a similar BRET assay, we measured the ability of gp120s and CCL4 to activate Gq and other PTX-insensitive G proteins (G11/12/13). CCL4 at concentrations of 10 nM (Fig. 2A, B), 100 nM or 300 nM (Additional file 1: Fig. S5) showed no effect on Gαq activation, regardless of its expression level, consistent with previous findings [20]. Similarly, we found no significant activation of the other G proteins by CCL4 (Fig. 2B). Of note, the chemokine analog 6P4-CCL5, recently described as a Gq-biased CCR5 agonist [21], also tended to activate Gq however with much lower apparent efficacy than Gi1 activation (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). In contrast, both gp120s promoted a decrease in the BRET signal using the Gq-BRET biosensor, indicating Gq protein activation (Fig. 2A). This effect was observed in HEK-R5 but not HEK-P cells or in the presence of MVC (Fig. 2C), indicating CCR5 requirement. It was also dependent on sCD4 addition, but was not reproduced by sCD4 alone (Fig. 2D), suggesting that gp120s activate Gq in their CD4-bound form. Similar to Gi, Gq activation by gp120s was dependent on the Gα expression level. In fact, the agonist effect of gp120s was clearly more pronounced at low Gαq expression levels, but gradually diminished and became statistically nonsignificant as protein expression increased (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, BRET signals decreased more for gp120#25 than for gp120#34, while the opposite trend was observed with Gαi1 (Fig. 1A). These data suggest that gp120s can activate G proteins with varying efficiencies depending on the Gα isoform.

Although Gα11 is closely related to Gαq, there was no evidence of its activation by gp120s (Fig. 2E and F). Gp120s also failed to activate G12. However, they did show effects on G13 activation, which increased significantly with higher Gα expression levels.

Collectively, our data indicate that HIV-1 gp120s differ from chemokines in their capacity to activate Gq and G13. However, like Gi activation, this property is observed only at specific CCR5/Gα ratios.

HIV-1 gp120s antagonize CCL4-induced calcium flux via Gi- and Gq-signaling

To further characterize the activation of Gi/o and Gq proteins by CCR5 in response to chemokines and HIV-1 Envs, we next investigated downstream G protein signaling through calcium imaging experiments on fluo-4-loaded HEK-R5 cells. Previous data have shown that CCR5 chemokines induce intracellular Ca2+ flux through PTX-sensitive Gi/o proteins and/or PTX-insensitive proteins, including Gq [21, 42, 61–63]. CCL4 induced a time-dependent increase in intracellular Ca2+ release, which was blocked by the CCR5 inverse agonist TAK779, the antagonist chemokine analog 5P12-CCL5, or was undetectable in HEK-P cells (Fig. 3A). CCL4-induced Ca2+ flux was also inhibited in cells pretreated with PTX or FR900359, a selective inhibitor of Gαq/11/14 proteins [55], indicating that both Gαi/o and Gαq-type proteins are involved in these responses. This aligns with the current view that Ca²⁺ signaling through Gi-coupled GPCRs is mediated by a complex of PLCβ and Gβγ subunits from activated Gαi-Gβγ heterotrimers, however, under the strict dependence on activated Gαq subunits, which relieve PLCβ auto-inhibition [64]. Thus, our results confirm the activation of Gq-type proteins in HEK-R5 cells by CCL4, despite the chemokine’s low affinity for non-Gαi-coupled CCR5 [27] and its failure to activate Gq in our BRET assay (Fig. 2), a discrepancy that may originate from differences in the specificity of Gβγ isoforms. Also, it cannot be ruled out that activated Gq input in HEK-R5 cells comes from a source independent of CCR5. In parallel experiments, we also observed that Ca2+ flux induced by 6P4-CCL5 occurs with faster kinetics and higher efficacy compared to CCL4, in agreement with data showing that it acts as a superagonist for Ca2+ mobilization [21], and is similarly Gαi/o- and Gαq-dependent (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A-C).

Unlike chemokines, gp120s #25 and #34 did not induce intracellular Ca2+ release, even at a high concentration (300 nM) (Fig. 3B). Indeed, some gp120 variants have been reported to induce Ca2+ flux, though not all [40, 65, 66]. Interestingly, some gp120s appear unable to induce Ca2+ flux, while they can activate the focal adhesion tyrosine kinase Pyk2 in a Ca2+-dependent manner [37]. This suggests that these gp120s act as very weak partial agonists whose capacity to induce Ca2+ flux is too subtle to be detected experimentally. Interestingly, however, gp120s, although unable to induce measurable Ca2+ flux, could partially inhibit CCL4-induced Ca2+ flux, in line with our previous data that HIV-1 gp120s can inhibit chemokine binding to CCR5 [10]. Collectively, these data indicate that gp120s #25 and #34 act as CCR5 antagonists or very weak partial agonists regarding Ca2+ activation in HEK-R5 cells.

HIV-1 gp120s stabilize specific conformations of activated CCR5/G protein complexes

The above results show that gp120s and chemokines elicit distinct signaling, potentially because they stabilize different active conformations of CCR5. To test this hypothesis, we used molecular dynamics. Previous simulations of homology models built from the cryo-EM structure of CD4–gp12092BR020–CCR5 (PDB ID: 6MEO) have shown that inactive CCR5 adopts distinct conformations when engaged by CD4-bound gp120s #25 and #34, which differed markedly from the structure of inactive CCR5 bound to MVC [48]. Here, we investigated how gp120s influence the active state of CCR5 in complex with a mini Gi1 (Gi1-mini). The reference framework was based on the cryo-EM structure of CCL3-CCR5-Gi1 (PDB ID: 7F1Q) [23] (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A), after checking that G protein truncation did not alter receptor dynamics during simulations (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). CCL3 showed a stable binding mode to the receptor (Additional file 1: Fig. S9), consistent with typical chemokine – chemokine receptor interface [67]. The N-terminus of the chemokine occupies the minor subpocket of the CCR5 transmembrane cavity (chemokine recognition site 2 or CRS2), while its 30s-loop locates close to extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) and TM5 at the top of CRS2’s major subpocket (chemokine recognition site 3 or CRS3) (Fig. 4A, B). Several residues of CRS2 consistently established hydrogen bonds with CCL3 including Asn2586.58 in TM6 and Asp2767.32 and Glu2837.39 in TM7 and four residues in ECL2, namely Arg168, Gln170, Glu172, and Ser180 (Fig. 4C and Additional file 1: Fig. S10, superscript numbers indicate Ballesteros-Weinstein numbering). The residues Arg168 and Glu2837.39 further contributed to chemokine binding through ionic bonds (Additional file 1: Fig. S11).

The main structural difference between CCL3-bound active [23] and CD4–gp12092BR020-bound inactive [7] CCR5 is an outward tilt of TM6 and an inward tilt of TM7 near the intracellular side, a hallmark of class A GPCRs. Additionally, the CCL3 core also engages interactions with CCR5’s chemokine recognition site 1 (CRS1) (Fig. 4A), modifying the structure of the receptor at the stem of its N-terminus. To study the interaction of CD4-complexed gp120s #25 and #34 with active CCR5 bound to Gi1-mini, we therefore used the CD4–gp12092BR020–CCR5 structure for modeling of CCR5 CRS1 and its surrounding regions, while the CCL3-CCR5-Gi1 structure served as a reference for the rest of the receptor and the positioning of Gi1-mini (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B). Simulations of both complexes, CD4–gp120#25–CCR5–Gi1-mini and CD4–gp120#34–CCR5–Gi1-mini, resulted in stable models (Additional file 1: Fig. S9) that preserved the receptor’s active state signature, with receptor chemokine recognition sites of similar shape, differing from those bound to CCL3 (Fig. 4B). Gp120s and CCL3 also stabilize different conformations of CCR5 extracellular and transmembrane regions (Fig. 4D, left panel, and Additional file 1: Fig. S12). Considering the G protein binding site of the receptor, the conformational landscape defined by the complexes with gp120 #25, gp120 #34 and CCL3 clustered into three different, yet partially overlapping groups, suggesting that the three ligands stabilize closely related but distinct receptor populations (Fig. 4D, right panel). In line with this, the three ligands displayed specific interaction patterns with CCR5 (Fig. 4C and Additional file 1: Fig. S10, S11), suggesting distinct activation pathways that connect the extracellular and intracellular sides of the receptor. For example, hydrogen bonds formed with Gln2807.36 in TM7 (CRS2) and Ser179 in ECL2 (CRS3) are characteristic of the complex with gp120 #25 and gp120 #34, respectively, but none is observed in the complex with CCL3. On the other hand, the three ligands are in close interaction with Glu172, Ser180 and Glu2837.39. It is noteworthy that the structure of the ligand-bound receptor deviates from that of the ligand-free receptor, as shown by the simulation of CCR5-apo bound to Gi1-mini (Fig. 4D and Additional file 1: Fig. S12).

In summary, the modeling of the CCR5 structure suggested that the gp120s #25 and #34 can bind to an active form of the receptor, albeit in slightly different conformations. These conformations are distinct from those stabilized by the chemokine CCL3, particularly at the G protein binding site. These findings provide structural insights into the distinct pharmacological effects of the three ligands studied.

HIV-1 gp120s and chemokines exhibit differential dependence on membrane cholesterol for binding to CCR5

CCR5 conformation and G protein coupling specificity vary with membrane cholesterol content [25, 28, 62, 68, 69]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the distinct CCR5/G protein complexes differentially activated by chemokines and gp120s might localize in membrane regions that differ in cholesterol content. To test this hypothesis, HEK-R5 cells were treated with methyl-β-cyclodextrins (MβCD) to deplete membrane cholesterol. Then, the binding of a radioactive chemokine (125I-CCL3, closely related to CCL4), 35S-gp120 #34 or anti-CCR5 mAbs 45531 and 2D7 as controls was assessed (Fig. 5). Over a MβCD concentration range between 0.75 and 5 mM, which preserved cell viability as assessed by trypan blue exclusion (dotted line, Fig. 5), 2D7 binding remained unaffected, as previously reported [25], while 45531 binding decreased at MβCD concentrations higher than 1.25 mM. Previous data have suggested that 45531 binds to CCR5 localized in cholesterol-enriched raft regions [25]. Our data thus suggest that rafts containing the 45531-binding forms of CCR5 are disrupted only at high MβCD concentrations. This is consistent with the notion that cyclodextrins more efficiently extract cholesterol from outside than within rafts, where cholesterol strongly interacts with sphingolipids [70, 71].

At the lowest MβCD dose that removes cholesterol from outside rafts, half of CCL3 binding was lost, while gp120 binding was only slightly reduced. Increasing MβCD concentration to deplete cholesterol from rafts led to a more moderate decrease in CCL3 binding, with the reduction similar to those observed for gp120 and 45531. This suggests that within rafts, cholesterol plays a role in regulating the receptor-binding properties of a diverse range of ligand structures. Finally, at a 5 mM concentration of MβCD, only 20% of CCL3 binding remained, as opposed to 60% for gp120 binding.

Taken together, these data indicate that most gp120 binding is cholesterol-independent, whereas chemokine binding is not. This suggests that CCR5 is differentially responsive to chemokine and gp120 depending on its surrounding cholesterol content. Given that the coupling specificity of CCR5 to G proteins varies with cholesterol content and plasma membrane organization [62, 72], this could explain why chemokines and gp120s activate distinct CCR5/G protein complexes and signaling pathways.

Discussion

Earlier studies have demonstrated that CCR5 can be biased toward unique signaling pathways depending on the nature of its ligand. This has been reported with native chemokines of the receptor (e.g. CCL3/4/5) [20, 22], non-peptidic small molecules [73], monoclonal antibodies [74] and N-terminally modified CCL5 analogs (e.g., 6P4-CCL5) [21]. Here, we extend these findings to HIV-1 Envs, which we show to behave as CCR5 partial agonists with selectivity for specific G protein subtypes. Envs but not CCL4 activate Gq and G13, consistent with studies reporting PTX-insensitive Env-mediated signaling [37, 39, 42, 75]. Envs can also activate Gi and GoB but not GoA proteins while CCL4 activates all members of the Gi/o family with similar efficacy. From a molecular perspective, biased agonism of gp120s is explained by our molecular dynamics simulations, showing that, compared to CCL3, they differently shape the binding cavity of active CCR5 and its G protein coupling site. More subtle differences in CCR5 active conformations were also observed between the two gp120s, which may account for their functional divergences observed here (e.g., gp120 #25 was more efficient at activating Gq than gp120 #34, and vice versa for Gi1 activation) and are consistent with our previous observations that these gp120s differentially bind to distinct CCR5 dimer conformations [30]. These data also align with previous works showing that CCR5 signaling can differ across HIV strains [50, 65].

Interestingly, the present study shows that gp120 agonism, but not chemokine agonism, depends on CCR5/Gα stoichiometry. Gp120s can activate G proteins only at specific levels of Gα expression. Studies have reported that the repertoire and relative abundance of Gα subunits vary during maturation and differentiation of immune cells, particularly HIV-1 target CD4 + T cells [76, 77]. Differences in Gα expression levels between cells may explain why Env signaling can be cell type-specific, as well as the often-contradictory results observed regarding the signaling properties of Envs [35, 39, 42, 44, 45]. However, our work shows that under experimental conditions where gp120s do not promote an intracellular signaling (calcium), they can still inhibit CCL4-induced effects. This result aligns with our earlier findings, where competition binding assays demonstrated that HIV-1 gp120 can completely displace the high-affinity binding of CCL3 to HEK-R5 cell membranes [10]. Altogether, these data suggest that Envs may function as either CCR5 agonists or antagonists (or very weak partial agonists), depending on the level of Gα expression, thereby clearly acting as biased ligands of CCR5.

We previously demonstrated that the expression levels of Gα can bias the pharmacological properties of β₁ and β₂-adrenergic receptors, because ligands bind to specific subsets of receptor/Gα complexes, which are differentially stabilized depending on Gα expression levels [56]. This was linked to the differential propensity of distinct receptor conformations to interact with Gα subunits [56]. CCR5 at the cell surface exists in multiple conformations that bind distinct ligand types differentially [25, 27, 30, 34, 69]. Here, we propose a model to explain our data, in which these CCR5 conformations exhibit different propensities to interact with Gα subunits (Fig. 6A-D). This could be due to changes in CCR5/Gα affinity and/or accessibility of Gα to different membrane territories where CCR5 is localized, as we have previously shown [56]. According to this model, high-affinity/accessibility Gα/CCR5 interactions can occur at low Gα expression levels, while lower-affinity/accessibility interactions arise at higher Gα expression levels. The fact that gp120s activate G proteins only within a narrow range of CCR5/Gα stoichiometries suggests that they selectively stabilize only specific subsets of CCR5/Gα complex conformations. In cases where gp120s activate Gi1 only at high Gαi1 expression levels (Fig. 1A), the model suggests that gp120s stabilize only CCR5 conformations with weak affinity/accessibility for Gαi1, but not CCR5 conformations interacting more readily with Gαi1 (Fig. 6C). Conversely, our results are compatible with gp120s stabilizing CCR5 conformations with high affinity/accessibility for Gαq, but not those with lower affinity/accessibility. In Fig. 2A, this is reflected by ΔBRET changes, which are maximal at low Gαq expression levels, and progressively decrease as Gαq expression level increases (Fig. 6D). Thus, the Gα subunit expression level dictates the capacity of gp120s to behave as an agonist in a Gα subtype-dependent manner. This suggests that, in HIV-1 target cells, the expression level of different G proteins may not only regulate whether gp120s signal, but also influence the specific signaling pathways they activate. In contrast to gp120s, ΔBRET values for CCL4-mediated Gi/o activation did not vary with Gα expression level, suggesting that the chemokine can activate all types of CCR5/Gαi/o complexes (Fig. 6B). The activation of a broader range of receptors may account for CCL4 full agonism, whereas gp120s activate only a subset of these receptors, resulting in partial agonism.

Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that gp120 and CCL3 imprint distinct but partly overlapping conformational landscapes of CCR5/G protein complexes (Fig. 4). In a previous work on β1/2 adrenergic receptors, we demonstrated that distinct conformations of receptor/G protein complexes differently partition between cholesterol-rich and cholesterol-poor membrane regions [56]. Similarly, CCR5 is known to reside in membrane territories with varying cholesterol content [25, 69]. Previous studies have also shown that cholesterol is required for chemokine binding and chemokine-induced signaling through Gi/o [28, 62], while conversely, it decreases binding of the inverse agonist MVC [68]. These studies suggest that cholesterol stabilizes Gαi/o-coupled, active CCR5 conformations with high-affinity for chemokines. Consistently, we demonstrate here that cholesterol depletion of HEK-R5 cells resulted in an 80% decrease in chemokine binding (Fig. 5), a result comparable to the effect observed when these cells are treated with the GTP analog Gpp(NH)p, which uncouples CCR5 from G proteins and stabilizes it in a low-affinity state for chemokines [27]. Interestingly, previous studies also reported that depletion of membrane cholesterol [62, 78] and change in membrane organization during immune synapse formation [72] can shift CCR5 signaling from Gi- to Gq-dependent in response to chemokines. In another report, chemokine binding to CCR5 was shown to activate Gαi but not Gαq proteins, despite confirming that the receptor forms complexes with both G protein types [79]. Taken together, these studies suggest that the activation of CCR5/Gq complexes by chemokines may depend on the composition of their membrane environment. This is consistent with our data showing that CCL4 induces calcium flux in HEK-R5 cells in a manner dependent on both Gi and Gq or Gq-like proteins (Fig. 3). However, we did not observe detectable activation of Gq/11 proteins by the chemokine in the BRET assays, in accordance with others [20]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the different type of G protein heterotrimers used in the BRET assay, which differs from those involved in Ca2+ flux in HEK-R5 cells. Another possibility is that CCL4 activates only a small subset of Gq/11 proteins, which may be sufficient for Ca2+ flux but not enough to be detected in the BRET assays. Additionally, unlike chemokines, our results show that 60% of gp120 binding is independent of the presence of cholesterol (Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with our previous work showing that half of the receptors in HEK-R5 cells to which gp120s bind are chemokine low-affinity receptors [27]. Binding to receptors located in cholesterol poor regions might explain why HIV-1 gp120s but not CCL4, can activate the PTX-insensitive Gq and G13 proteins (Fig. 2).

In conclusion, our results support a model in which CCR5 segregates into a mosaic of different functional units, each coupled to particular G protein subtypes and differentially engaged by distinct ligands such as gp120s and chemokines. Although the precise molecular nature of the CCR5 populations forming these functional units remains to be determined, several hypotheses can be drawn from the literature and our own previous works. In particular, as for other GPCRs [80, 81], the ability of CCR5 to form homo- or hetero-oligomers has emerged as a central mechanism contributing to the diversity of its conformations and signaling outputs. We and others have identified multiple dimerization interfaces within CCR5 [31, 32], endowing the receptor with the capacity to assemble into distinct dimeric architectures, higher-order oligomers and potentially nanoclusters analogous to those described for other receptors (e.g., the other HIV coreceptor CXCR4 [82]). CCR5 can also form heterodimers with other GPCRs, a process likely to allosterically modulate its ligand binding properties and G protein coupling profile [33, 83]. Of interest in the context of the present study, we have reported that distinct CCR5 ligands (MVC, gp120s, CCL3) exhibit selective affinity for particular oligomeric conformations of the receptor [30, 32, 84]. Furthermore, the L196K mutation, which alters CCR5 dimer organization and stability, markedly reduces the receptor basal coupling to Gi proteins [84]. Altogether, these results are consistent with recent structural data [31] and support the view that CCR5 exists as an array of different dimeric and oligomeric species that differ in their capacity to engage ligands and G proteins [31]. Defining whether these distinct receptor assemblies also discriminate among G-protein subtypes will be an important next step toward elucidating the molecular basis of the functional diversity described here.

Differential activation of the different CCR5 functional units likely contributes to the functional diversity of CCR5 in immunity and diseases, as well as explains the diversity of Env signaling properties reported in the past, which not always recapitulate the signaling properties of chemokines. The insights gained into the mechanisms of Env signaling could also help enhance our understanding of its role in HIV-1 infection. Finally, our results raise the possibility for developing more targeted approaches to inhibit CCR5 functions in disease, while preserving its beneficial role in immunity.
