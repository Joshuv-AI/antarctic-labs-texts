Shotgun metagenomics of the vaginal microbiome in cervical shortening and preterm birth risk
Authors: Keun-Young Lee, Seung-Ho Shin, Goeun Park, Shin-Hae Kang, Hyun-Jung Kang, Jiye Kim, Jae Jun Lee, Ga-Hyun Son, Ji Young Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Preterm birth (PTB), a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality, is frequently associated with premature cervical remodeling and vaginal microbiome dysbiosis. Cervical shortening in mid-pregnancy is a well-established risk factor for spontaneous PTB (sPTB), yet the microbial signatures underlying this condition remain underexplored, especially in Asian populations. In this study, we conducted shotgun metagenomic analysis of vaginal samples from 35 East Asian pregnant women with a short cervix and 12 with normal cervical length. Species-level taxonomic profiling and functional pathway analysis revealed reduced Lactobacillus dominance, increased microbial diversity, and enrichment of non-optimal CST IV species, such as Fannyhessea vaginae, Bifidobacterium breve, and Mycobacterium canetti in the short cervix group. Functional profiling showed group differences in pathways related to folate biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and epithelial barrier regulation. Among women with a short cervix, those who delivered preterm had vaginal microbiomes enriched in opportunistic pathogens, including Peptoniphilus equinus, Treponema spp., and Staphylococcus hominis. Conversely, B. breve, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Lactobacillus paragasseri were associated with full-term delivery. Functions related to glycosylation, structural stability, and degradation of cervical mucin were enriched in the sPTB group. Network analysis identified distinct microbial interactions between Lactobacillus-dominated clusters and CST IV-associated taxa, providing ecological insights that may reflect competitive dynamics and potential influences on cervicovaginal barrier integrity. These findings enhance our understanding of the taxonomic and functional profiles of the vaginal microbiome linked to cervical shortening and sPTB, contributing to improved risk stratification and management strategies for PTB, particularly in women with cervical shortening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23737-x.

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as delivery before 37 w of gestation, affects over one in ten infants worldwide1. In East Asia, its prevalence varies by country, generally ranging from 5% to 10%, and it accounts for over 50% of all neonatal deaths2–4. Neonates born preterm face increased risks of long-term morbidity, including neurodevelopmental impairments, as well as respiratory and gastrointestinal complications5,6. While preterm labor is associated with multiple pathologic mechanisms, such as inflammation, uterine overdistension, uteroplacental hemorrhage, and other immunologically-mediated processes, ascending infection from the vagina is recognized as a major contributor to spontaneous PTB (sPTB)5–7. Moreover, maternal health factors, such as diet, smoking, stress, and metabolic status, may serve as risk factors for sPTB.

Vaginal microbial communities are categorized into community state types (CST), with CST I, II, III, and V predominantly dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus, L. gasseri, L. iners, and L. jensenii, respectively8. CSTs dominated by Lactobacillus species are typically associated with positive health outcomes in women9,10. In contrast, CST IV is characterized by a reduced proportion of Lactobacilli and an increased abundance of diverse anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella vaginalis, Atopobium vaginae, Mobiluncus sp., and Prevotella sp. This composition is commonly observed in bacterial vaginosis and has been linked to an elevated risk of sexually-transmitted infections, urogenital diseases, and adverse pregnancy outcomes10,11.

Emerging evidence suggests that the vaginal microbiome plays a critical role in sPTB. Women with a history of preterm delivery often exhibit reduced microbial stability and increased richness and diversity of vaginal microbiota10,12–16. Additionally, bacterial vaginosis has been associated with a two-fold increase in sPTB risk17. However, the relationship between vaginal microbiota and sPTB varies across racial and ethnic groups, highlighting the need for population-specific research.

The uterine cervix functions as both a mechanical and immunological barrier, preventing the ascending migration of vaginal microbiota into the upper reproductive tract during pregnancy18. To maintain pregnancy, the cervix remains closed but gradually transitions throughout gestation from a rigid, firm structure to a softened and ripened state. As labor begins, it dilates to facilitate fetal delivery. This process, known as cervical remodeling, involves both cervical shortening and dilation. Premature cervical shortening occurring abnormally in mid-pregnancy is a well-established risk factor for sPTB. It results from a complex interplay of factors, including cervical weakness, infection, hormonal influences, and genetic predisposition19,20. Several in vitro studies suggest that cervicovaginal microbiota may influence cervical function by modulating epithelial barrier integrity and promoting local inflammation through the alteration of adherens junction proteins, regulation of proinflammatory immune mediators, and modulation of microRNA expression, thereby contributing to premature cervical remodeling and sPTB21–24. However, there is a significant scarcity of clinical studies analyzing vaginal microbiome profiles specifically in women with a short cervix, particularly in Asian populations22,25. Moreover, not all women with a short cervix ultimately deliver preterm. This highlights the importance of identifying microbiome features that are associated with cervical shortening, as well as predictive of subsequent sPTB.

To date, most studies examining the association between vaginal microbiome communities and the risk of sPTB have primarily utilized on 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, which has limitations in providing detailed species- and strain-level taxonomic precision26. Consequently, subtle microbial variations that may critically influence pregnancy outcomes have remained largely underexplored, and the functional role of microbial communities in pregnancy remains unclear. Shotgun metagenomics presents an opportunity to address these limitations and provides a comprehensive understanding of distinct communities and species associated with diseases, offering insights into their functional potential of the microbiota. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a comparative analysis of the vaginal microbiome at the species level between women with short cervices and those with normal cervical lengths using shotgun metagenomic sequencing, aiming to identifying microbial communities associated with cervical shortening. We further sought to characterize microbial species and functional attributes of the vaginal microbiome that may contribute to the development of sPTB among women with a short cervix in East Asian populations.

Results

Participant characteristics and pregnancy outcomes

A total of 91 pregnant women were diagnosed with a short cervix via initial screening between 16 and 25 weeks of gestation. After excluding patients lacking samples or meeting exclusion criteria (see the Methods section for details), 47 pregnant women participated in the study (Fig. 1). These comprised 35 women with short cervical lengths (short cervix (SC) group) and 12 with normal cervical lengths (normal cervix (NC) group) (Fig. 1).

Vaginal microbial diversity and functional pathways associated with a short cervix

After quality filtering, an average of 665,572 microbial sequencing reads per sample were obtained. Analysis of microbial distribution across 47 samples revealed taxonomic diversity within each cervix group, taxonomic classification indicated that Lactobacillus spp. were dominant in both groups (mean relative abundance = 73.9%). However, the average relative abundance of Lactobacillus spp. in the NC group (89.1%) was significantly higher than that in the SC group (68.6%; p = 0.004; Fig. 2A). Additionally, α-diversity, assessed using the Shannon index, was increased in the SC group compared to the NC group (Fig. 2B, p = 0.006). The β-diversity analysis demonstrated distinct microbial compositions between the NC and SC groups (Fig. 2C, R2 = 0.049, p = 0.032).

Both linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) and comparative analyses indicated an increased abundance of B. breve, F. vaginae, and Mycobacterium canetti, along with a decreased abundance of L. crispatus and L. johnsonii in the SC group compared to the NC group (Fig. 2D and E; Supplementary Fig. S1). The average proportion of L. iners tended to be higher in the SC group than in the NC group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2E, p = 0.110).

Microbiome Multivariable Association with Linear Models (MaAsLiN2) analysis indicated a strong positive correlation between SC group and B. breve, Limosilactobacillus mucosae, Escherichia coli, Prevotella bivia, Bifidobacterium dentium, F. vaginae, Ureaplasma parvum, Finegoldia magna, Ralstonia sp. CP, Ralstonia wenshanensis. Conversely, the SC group showed a negative correlation with Enterococcus faecium, Limosilactobacillus reuteri, L. intestinalis, and L. johnsonii (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table S1). The majority of positively correlated strains in the SC group were classified under CST IV.

Moreover, significant positive associations between F. vaginae, F. magna, R. wenshanensis and a short cervix, as well as significant negative associations between L. reuteri, L. intestinalis, and L. johnsonii were retained even after adjusting for clinically relevant confounding variables such as GDM and obesity, which were more prevalent in the SC group (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

A total of 3,287 gene functions were identified across all samples, each accounting for over 0.1% of the total functional composition. The Shannon diversity index indicated a marked decrease in the diversity of gene functions related to biological process (BP) and molecular function (MF) categories, whereas an increase was observed in the cellular component (CC) category (Supplementary Fig. S3). Functional analysis using the Bray–Curtis measure showed no statistical differences between the NC and SC groups across these categories. However, multivariate analysis identified strong correlations between the SC group and 21 CCs, 86 BPs, and 189 MFs (Fig. 3B and C; Supplementary Table S2; q-value < 0.01 after multiple testing correction).

Within the BP category, the top five gene functions most associated with the SC group included chorismate metabolism, 7,8-dihydroneopterin 3’-triphosphate biosynthesis, folic acid biosynthesis, dephosphorylation, and alanine metabolism. In contrast, gene functions most associated with the NC group included lipoteichoic acid biosynthesis, lactose metabolism, carbohydrate derivative metabolism, positive regulation of transcription from the RNA polymerase II promoter, and glutathione transmembrane transport. The MF category exhibited the most differential features between groups. The top five gene functions associated with the SC group included gluconate transmembrane transporter, aldose 1-epimerase, 4-phosphoerythronate dehydrogenase, small protein activating enzyme, and malate dehydrogenase (decarboxylating) (NAD+) activities. In contrast, peptide-methionine (S)-S-oxide reductase, D-alanine-poly(phosphoribitol) ligase, 6-phospho-beta-glucosidase, organic phosphonate transmembrane transporter, and deoxyadenosine kinase activities were associated with the NC group.

Characteristics of the vaginal microbiome associated with preterm delivery in women with a short cervix

Distinct structural and functional differences were observed in the vaginal microbial composition of women with a short cervix, depending on their preterm delivery outcome. Several bacterial taxa exhibited a strong positive correlation with preterm delivery, including Peptoniphilus equinus, Treponema sp. OMZ 857, Lachnoanaerobaculum umeaense, Staphylococcus hominis, Cutibacterium avidum, S. epidermidis, Fenollaria sporofastidiosus, and Streptococcus sp. oral taxon 431 (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S2B; Supplementary Table S3; coefficient > 3). In contrast, B. breve, L. gasseri, and L. paragasseri were negatively correlated with preterm delivery (MaAsLin2 coefficient < -3; q-value < 0.25).

Functional analysis indicated key gene functions that were negatively associated with preterm compared to full-term delivery. Within the BP category, these included positive regulation of transcription from the RNA polymerase II promoter, threonine metabolic process, telomere maintenance, galactose catabolic process via UDP-galactose, and the branched-chain amino acid metabolic process. In the MF category, gene functions most positively associated with full-term delivery included phosphoribosylanthranilate isomerase, thiaminase, uridylyltransferase, adenine deaminase, and beta-galactosidase activities. Similarly, in the CC category, the beta-galactosidase complex was negatively correlated with preterm delivery (Fig. 4B-C; Supplementary Table S4).

Interaction between species in the vaginal microbiome of participants with short cervices

A correlation-based network analysis was performed to examine co-occurrence patterns among microorganisms associated with short cervix. The analysis identified four major clusters comprising three or more microbial taxa: (1) cluster containing predominantly Lactobacillus spp., (2) cluster incorporating Prevotella spp. and Streptococcus spp., (3) cluster composed exclusively of Gardnerella spp., and (4) cluster include Lactobacillus iners (Fig. 5). Lactobacillus subspecies formed tightly connected clusters, indicating a cooperative strategy. All Lactobacillus spp. within the first cluster group exhibited positive correlations. However, L. iners was distinct from other Lactobacillus species, forming an independent cluster with F. vaginae, Aerococcus christensenii, and others. Among strains associated with CST IV, Gardnerella species were found in isolated clusters. In contrast, close associations were observed among specific microbial clusters, including one composed of Prevotella corporis, B. dentium, and B. breve; another comprising S. agalactiae, S. anginosus, and F. magna; and a cluster containing E. coli and Limosilactobacillus vaginalis. The interactions between CST IV-related clusters and the Lactobacillus-dominated healthy cluster may influence the dynamics of the vaginal microbiome.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the vaginal microbiome associated with cervical shortening and its potential impact on preterm birth risk in an East Asian population using shotgun metagenomics. Our findings reveal distinct taxonomic and functional differences between the vaginal microbiota of women with short cervices and those with normal cervical length, suggesting a potential association between vaginal microbial communities, cervical remodeling, and the risk of sPTB. However, causal relationships cannot be inferred from this study.

In our study, the NC group exhibited a substantially higher proportion of Lactobacillus spp. compared to the SC group. Conversely, the SC group showed an increased abundance of strains associated with CST IV. These findings are consistent with previous research that indicated a correlation between CST IV and increased odds of having a short cervix in the African–American population27. Additionally, a strong association has been reported between Lactobacillus-depleted microbiota and extremely short cervixes in Caucasian women25. Furthermore, L. iners-dominated microbiota (CST III) have been linked to cervical shortening and are considered a risk factor for PTB19. Discrepancies among these studies may be attributed to variations in sample collection timing, microbiota detection techniques, ethnicity/geographical variations, and complex clinical conditions. Our study indicated the increased abundance of specific bacterial taxa in the SC group, particularly B. breve, F. vaginae, and M. canetti, all of which were substantially enriched, compared to the NC group. The presence of F. vaginae, previously known as Atopobium vaginae, is noteworthy, as it has been strongly associated with bacterial vaginosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Fannyhessea vaginae, in combination with G. vaginalis, has been implicated in biofilm formation and increased proinflammatory cytokine production, both of which may contribute to cervical remodeling25,28. Similarly, B. breve, while typically considered a commensal and beneficial bacterium in the gut microbiome, has been linked to increased microbial diversity in the vaginal niche, particularly in dysbiotic states14. A decreased abundance of L. crispatus and L. johnsonii in the SC group suggests that these species may play a protective role in maintaining cervical integrity and preventing premature remodeling. Lactobacillus crispatus has been widely recognized for its ability to produce lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and bacteriocins, contributing to the maintenance of a stable and protective vaginal environment12. A shift away from L. crispatus-dominated microbiota toward more diverse communities has been associated with inflammation, increased vaginal pH, and susceptibility to infection, all of which may contribute to cervical shortening and subsequent increased risk of sPTB12. Recent studies have shown that machine learning based on microbiome distribution profiles can effectively diagnose and classify diseases29,30. Although the limited dataset size presents challenges for training and evaluating diagnostic models, we applied machine learning to identify biomarkers for SC group classification and confirmed that the SC group can be accurately distinguished using taxonomic profiles consistent with the findings of this study. Notably, classification performance improved when SC-specific taxonomic profiles, selected based on our analyses, were used (Supplementary Fig. S4; AUC = 0.914 vs. AUC = 0.836).

The functional analysis of microbial communities provided additional insights into potential functional differences associated with cervical shortening, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the dominance of particular taxa. In the SC group, we observed enrichment in specific gene functions that are associated with metabolic processes, including folic acid biosynthetic pathways. High homocysteine and low folate concentrations in early pregnancy are known to affect pregnancy outcome31. The increase in folate-producing microbiomes, such as Bifidobacterium, may be part of a protection strategy in response to the changed vaginal environment32. Conversely, the NC group exhibited an enrichment of gene functions related to lactose and carbohydrate-derived metabolic processes. This result is consistent with a study showing that lactic acid produced by the vaginal microbiota enhances the integrity of the cervicovaginal epithelial barrier33.

The novel aspect of our study was the identification of microbial signatures associated with preterm birth within the SC group. Specific bacterial taxa, including P. equinus, Treponema sp. OMZ 857, L. umeaense, and S. hominis exhibited strong positive correlations with sPTB, and many of these taxa are known opportunistic pathogens or commensals with pathogenic potential in dysbiotic environments34. Peptoniphilus species are associated with bacterial vaginosis and intrauterine infections, eliciting robust proinflammatory responses in cervical cells35. Treponema sp. OMZ 857, a member of the oral Treponema genus, and Streptococcus sp. oral taxon 431 are linked to periodontal disease36,37. Many studies have focused on the relationship between periodontitis and preterm birth risk. Animal studies and in vitro models have demonstrated that periodontal pathogens can elevate circulating inflammatory cytokines and induce apoptosis in placental tissues38. Additionally, epidemiological evidence supports this association38,39. Mutations and predicted damaging missense variants in genes previously linked to periodontal disease (e.g., DEFB1, MBL2) have been identified in cases of preterm premature rupture of membranes, suggesting a shared genetic susceptibility underlying the connection between periodontal disease and sPTB40. Periodontitis may serve as a distant reservoir of microbial pathogens and inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to the occurrence of sPTB38. Our finding of oral-associated taxa such as Treponema and Streptococcus sp. oral taxon 431 in the vaginal microbiome of sPTB cases may further support the link between oral and vaginal dysbiosis in PTB pathogenesis. In contrast, B. breve, L. gasseri, and L. paragasseri exhibited negative correlations with sPTB, consistent with the findings of Feehily et al. 41.

Functional metagenomic analysis also indicated distinct genetic features associated with preterm delivery within the SC group. Studies of pregnant women suggest that the development of the cervical mucus plug and mesh tightening correlates with improved host defense42,43. Critchfield et al. reported that the critical barrier properties of cervical mucus were impaired in women at high risk for preterm delivery compared to women with healthy pregnancy44. In accordance with previous studies, our data showed that the preterm samples exhibited reduced abundance of functions relating to the threonine metabolic process, galactose catabolic process via UDP-galactose, uridylyltransferase activity, and beta-galactosidase, which are involved in the glycosylation, synthesis, functional stability, and degradation of cervical mucin.

We examined the interaction between bacterial species within the vaginal microbiome. Network analysis indicated whether a short cervix influenced the temporal dynamics of the vaginal microbiome compared to a cervix of normal length. The analysis indicated a contrast between the “healthy microbiome” cluster, composed of Lactobacillus species, and CST IV clusters, which consisted of Prevotella spp., Bifidobacterium spp., E. coli, S. agalactiae, and F. magna in the short cervix microbiome. Lactobacillus and non-Lactobacillus clusters primarily formed positive cooperative relationships within their respective groups; the hub strains, S. anginosus and L. amylolyticus, exhibited a mutually inhibitory interaction. Considering the protective role of L. crispatus and the disruptive potential of CST IV organisms, the directionality of cluster interactions observed in short cervix cases may indicate complex ecological dynamics that could influence cervical epithelial barrier integrity and modulate local immune responses. Lactobacillus iners uniquely formed an independent cluster with species such as F. vaginae and A. christensenii, rather than grouping with other Lactobacillus species.

Our study has several strengths, including the use of shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which provides higher taxonomic resolution compared to 16 S rRNA sequencing. This approach enabled us to identify microbial communities at the species level and explore functional gene pathways associated with cervical shortening and preterm birth. In addition, our study specifically targeted women with a short cervix, an understudied high-risk subgroup in microbiome research, particularly within Asian populations, allowing for the characterization of microbial signatures uniquely associated with this condition. Furthermore, by analyzing microbial taxa associated with sPTB within the SC group, we identified distinct microbiome profiles that may be implicated in the progression to preterm birth in this vulnerable cohort. In addition, the taxonomic profiles identified in this study can be utilized for machine learning applications in future research. However, there are several limitations to consider. First, the small sample size limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions and constrains the generalizability of our findings, as it may not capture the full heterogeneity of the broader population. Therefore, future studies must include larger sample sizes and multi-center collaborations to validate and expand upon our findings. Additionally, interventions such as cerclage operation, antibiotic usage, and progesterone supplementation among patients with a short cervix may have influenced the vaginal microbiome and pregnancy outcomes, potentially introducing bias into the analysis. The vaginal microbiota is known to be dominated by specific microorganisms, which calls for careful interpretation of research findings. Therefore, integrating methodologies that account for this factor in future studies would be beneficial.In conclusion, our findings highlight the association between non-optimal cervicovaginal microbial communities, particularly CST IV, and cervical shortening, underscoring their potential role in maintaining cervical integrity during pregnancy. Functional analysis indicated metabolic pathway alterations that may contribute to cervical remodeling. In women with a short cervix, increased microbial diversity and enrichment of opportunistic pathogens such as P. equinus were associated with an increased risk of sPTB. Also, metagenomic functions associated with cervical mucus properties differed according to preterm delivery status. These findings enhance our understanding of the taxonomic and functional variations in the vaginal microbiome, contributing to improved risk stratification and management strategies for PTB, particularly in women with cervical shortening.

Materials and methods

Study participants and sample collection

This prospective cohort study enrolled asymptomatic singleton pregnant women with cervical shortening between September 2021 and June 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2021-05-003). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Women in the SC group were diagnosed with a cervical length of < 25 mm between 16 and 25 weeks of gestation, as assessed by transvaginal sonography. The NC group comprised women with a cervical length > 30 mm during the same gestational period and were matched to the SC group based on maternal age and gestational age at the time of vaginal swab collection. Eligible participants were singleton pregnant women aged 20–45 years. Exclusion criteria included uterine anomalies, fetal structural abnormalities, regular uterine contractions, heavy vaginal bleeding, suspected rupture of membranes, prior cerclage before the diagnosis of cervical shortening, medically indicated preterm birth, history of cervical conization, or use of antibiotics during pregnancy. At the time of initial diagnosis of cervical shortening, cervicovaginal swabs were collected using a sterile speculum and OMR-130 swab (DNA Genotek Inc., Ottawa, Canada) from the posterior vaginal fornix and the external cervical os. The samples were immediately placed into collection tubes and transported to the laboratory within seven days.

Upon diagnosis of a short cervix, all participants were initiated on daily vaginal progesterone suppositories (200 mg) until 36 weeks of gestation or delivery. Cervical cerclage using the McDonald technique was performed for the prevention of preterm birth in the following cases: (1) history of sPTB with a short cervix; (2) asymptomatic cervical dilation with visible fetal membranes extending to or beyond the external os on physical examination; (3) cervical length < 10 mm without a prior history of sPTB; and (4) progressive cervical shortening despite vaginal progesterone therapy. Patients who underwent cerclage received prophylactic intravenous antibiotics in the operating room, followed by a three-day postoperative antibiotic course. In cases not meeting the above criteria, vaginal progesterone was continued without cerclage. Clinical data, including demographic characteristics and obstetric history, were extracted from electronic medical records. Maternal height and weight were routinely measured at each outpatient visit and during hospitalization, with pre-pregnancy weight documented based on patient self-reports. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters, with obesity defined as pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, aligning with the criteria for diagnosing obesity in adults in the Republic of Korea45. GDM was diagnosed by either employing a two-step procedure (comprising a 50-g glucose challenge test followed by a 100-g, 3-h oral glucose tolerance test) or using a one-step procedure (75 g, 2-h oral glucose tolerance test)46.

Library preparation and sequencing

DNA for shotgun metagenomic sequencing was extracted by CJ Bioscience (Seoul, Republic of Korea) within 30 d of sampling using the Fast DNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA concentration was measured with a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol for inputs ≤ 100 ng. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp, 300 cycles) was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 1000 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and the quality of the sample pool was validated before sequencing.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.12.1) and MultiQC (v1.24.1)47,48. Low-quality reads were removed during filtering steps using fastp (v0.23.4). Specifically, sequencing reads with more than six bases having a phred Q score below 30 and adapter sequences were excluded. Sequencing reads of human origin were removed using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) and SAMtools (v1.21)49. Filtered reads were selected based on sequence alignment tags indicating unmapped reads to the human genome (GRCh38) and the status of primary alignment. After filtering steps, the sequencing reads were classified using the Kraken2 pipeline with the Kraken2 database (https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2)50. Kraken2 database was built on 20 September 2024 using the following command “kraken2-build --standard”. Classification results from Kraken2 were converted into sample-specific species abundances using Bracken (v2.9)51. Functional profiling was conducted for each sample using the HUMAnN3 (v3.9) pipeline52. The human_regroup_table module was used to calculate gene ontology-specific read counts, which were utilized for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Microbial communities were compared utilizing the vegan (v2.6-8) R package53. The alpha-diversity of the microbiota was assessed by computing the Shannon diversity index, whereas the beta‐diversity was determined using principal coordinate analysis based on the Bray–Curtis similarity matrix of the square‐root‐transformed relative abundance of bacterial genera. The beta‐diversity was evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance test using the adonis2 module from the vegan R package.

MaAsLin2 was used to identify microbiome and functional biomarkers in the linear model54. Putative candidates associated with prognostic variables were selected using the default parameters of MaAsLin2 (p < 0.05) and subsequently subjected to detailed analysis. The LEfSe method was performed to evaluate the selection and reproducibility of biomarkers using the microbiome Marker package (v1.0.2)55,56.

To examine microbial co-occurrence, SPRING (v1.0.4) correlation analysis was performed57. The results of the correlation analysis were converted into a network structure using NetCoMi (v1.1.0)58, and network analysis was conducted to identify microbial co-occurrence and co-exclusion observed in this study. The analysis results were exported as a data matrix and visualized using a home-built R script. For network analysis, node and edge information were exported from the NetCoMi framework and visualized using Gephi (v0.10.1)59.

We employed the SIAMCAT R package (v2.12.0) to conduct the statistical association analysis60. Statistical modeling was conducted using species-level relative abundances greater than 0.001, and both model performance and microbial biomarkers were evaluated through cross-validation. To perform cross-validation, we applied five-fold cross-validation repeated twice for model assessment. The default hyperparameters of the package were used for model construction.
