Pre-activation timing determines influenza severity and viral pathogenicity via STING Inhibition
Authors: Tong Zhu, Mengru Zhu, Feiyu Lu, Kuo Qu, Hong Wang, Shengnan Wang, Zhengyi Sun, Yongli Yu, Liying Wang, Chaoying Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere influenza is frequently associated with bacterial or viral co-infections. Although immune-mediated mechanisms have been extensively investigated, the cell-intrinsic responses of respiratory epithelial cells prior to immune engagement remain poorly characterized. To model co-infection in vitro, we pre-activated B16 melanoma cells using the TLR9 agonist CpG ODN, followed by infection with influenza A virus (IAV) strain FM1. Key molecular events, including STING expression and activation, were assessed by Western blotting and RT-qPCR. The in vivo relevance was assessed in a mouse model of intranasal CpG ODN pre-treatment and IAV challenge. RNA-seq was performed as a supplementary approach to explore possible cell death pathways. Pre-activation significantly enhanced IAV-induced cell death within a restricted vulnerability window (≤ 24 h), independent of viral replication or TLR9 signaling. This was associated with inflammatory priming and initial STING activation. However, IAV infection subsequently suppressed STING phosphorylation and elevated inflammatory cell death. Mice pre-treated with CpG ODN exhibited increased mortality and aggravated lung injury following IAV challenge. RNA-seq analysis suggested the potential involvement of pyroptosis. These findings demonstrate that cellular pre-stimulation exacerbates influenza severity by promoting inflammatory cell death, a process mechanistically linked to the subsequent suppression of STING activation. This study reveals a novel pathogenic cell-intrinsic mechanism in co-infection pathogenesis. Introduction

Influenza viruses, particularly influenza A viruses (IAV), remain a major global health threat, capable of causing seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics with high morbidity and mortality1–4. Moreover, influenza is often complicated by bacterial, viral, and fungal co-infections3,4. Such co-infections are associated with increased disease severity, critical illness, and mortality in influenza patients5–8. As we continue to face challenges posed by influenza viruses, identifying host factors that predispose individuals to severe influenza has become a critical research priority.

The severity of influenza depends not only on the virus itself but also on the host immune response and the state of host cells targeted by the virus. However, most studies have focused on the role of the immune system in influenza pathogenesis. Exploring the intrinsic responses of target cells to influenza virus infection prior to immune system involvement may help elucidate the potential initiating factors underlying severe influenza. Under natural conditions, human respiratory epithelial cells serve as the primary target cells for influenza viruses and act as the first line of defense. In artificial settings, sialic acid-expressing tumor cells such as B16 melanoma cells (B16 cells) can act as influenza virus model targets9,10. Studies demonstrate that upon viral infection, both natural and model target cells not only produce type I interferons (IFN-I) and pro-inflammatory cytokines by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in a manner similar to the innate immune system, but also actively initiate cell death events through intracellular signaling, a process distinct from passive virus-induced cell death. For instance, Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathways are involved in mediating antiviral and inflammatory responses in target cells infected with the influenza virus11,12. Additionally, target cells like respiratory/alveolar epithelial cells can undergo various forms of cell death upon influenza virus infection, such as apoptosis, necrosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy13–19. Interestingly, studies on oncolytic viruses have shown that B16 cells are also susceptible to influenza virus infection and subsequently undergo cell death20–22. The death of B16 cells induced by influenza virus may be attributed to the cell intrinsic PAMPs-PRRs recognition system and programmed cell death pathways beyond the virus itself, with the underlying mechanism being that B16 cells possess a complete innate immune network capable of effectively detecting viral invasion. This network encompasses viral RNA sensors like RIG-I and TLR7/8, DNA sensors including the cGAS/STING pathway and ZBP1, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and key kinases such as TBK1, IKK, MAPKs, and components of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway23–33.Importantly, this sensing system is functionally connected to a broad programmed cell death capability. Independent studies confirm that when exposed to chemical or pathogenic stimuli, B16 cells can execute the full range of cell death types triggered by influenza A virus in respiratory epithelial cells, which includes apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy34–41. Therefore, the B16 cell line is proposed to represent a pertinent and tractable model system for elucidating the specific mechanisms of cell death induced by influenza virus. An appropriate target cell response to influenza virus is crucial for combating viral infections and regulating immune reactions. However, dysregulation may lead to immunopathological damage or enhanced virus-induced cell death. Understanding these early cellular events could help uncover key mechanisms driving disease severity and identify potential therapeutic targets for severe influenza.

Studies have shown that mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released onto the surface of respiratory epithelial cells may be associated with severe influenza, as mtDNA contains abundant unmethylated CpG motifs that act as TLR9 agonists, activating innate immune responses via TLR9 signaling42. Elevated TLR9 activity in peripheral blood leukocytes and increased plasma mtDNA levels have been observed in influenza patients, with higher TLR9 activity correlating with more severe disease in hospitalized patients11. Additionally, mtDNA can activate the cGAS-STING signaling pathway43,44, and STING activation has also been linked to aggravated viral pneumonia45. B16 cells also possess TLR9 and STING signaling pathways, enabling them to mimic the DNA-induced responses observed in respiratory epithelial cells25,46. However, it remains unclear whether the activation of TLR9 or STING signaling occurs sequentially or additively during influenza virus infection. STING is a DNA sensor that recognizes cyclic dinucleotides and serves as an adaptor protein for various DNA sensors, including ZBP1 and cGAS47. Upon activation, STING translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, recruiting TBK1 and Interferon Regulatory Factor 3 (IRF3). TBK1 phosphorylates IRF3, promoting the expression of IFN-I and pro-inflammatory cytokines48. Thus, the STING pathway is a crucial signaling cascade in the innate immune system, primarily involved in detecting intracellular DNA and triggering immune responses. Recent studies have shown that STING activation is associated with multiple forms of programmed cell death. Mechanistically, STING activates IRF3, which subsequently interacts with the pro-apoptotic protein Bax to activate the caspase-3/8/9 signaling cascade, thereby inducing apoptosis49. Beyond apoptosis, STING signaling can also trigger necroptosis through the RIPK3-MLKL pathway or promote pyroptosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the maturation and release of IL-1b/IL-1850–52. However STING inhibition has also been linked to cell death through distinct mechanisms. These include the mtDNA accumulation due to impaired clearance or immune evasion that leads to excessive viral replication53,54. Influenza viruses can evade immunity by suppressing STING, allowing infected cells to survive longer and amplify viral replication, which may ultimately lead to cell death through the toxicity of viral proteins such as NS155. Depletion of STING induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, specifically triggering the activation of the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/CHOP pathway and the cleavage activation of caspase 8, thereby inducing Gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated pyroptosis56. Nevertheless, it remains unclear how target cells respond when they are already in an activated state upon influenza virus infection. As evidenced, B16 cells possess both the STING pathway and diverse cell death forms, demonstrating potential as target cells for influenza virus. Although B16 cells are tumor cells derived from skin melanocytes and exhibit significant differences from respiratory epithelial cells in terms of physiological functions, cellular architecture, and natural growth microenvironment and thus have certain limitations as influenza virus target cells, this unconventional model may nevertheless yield unexpected findings.

In this study, we used B16 cells as in vitro model cells and the mouse-adapted influenza A virus (IAV) FM1 strain and TLR9 agonist CpG ODN as research subjects. The selection of FM1 IAV aligns well with the murine origin of both the cell line and the animal models in our experimental system. We found that pre-activation with CpG ODN increased cellular susceptibility to influenza virus-induced cell death. This enhanced cell death was independent of viral replication and TLR9 signaling but exhibited a susceptible time window, during which STING inhibition correlated with increased cell death. Similarly, mice pre-treated with CpG ODN showed increased mortality and aggravated pulmonary inflammation upon influenza virus infection within a specific time window. RNA sequencing analysis suggested that STING suppression in pre-activated cells after influenza virus infection may exacerbate pyroptosis, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses. This study reveals that the pre-activated state of target cells markedly enhances IAV-induced cell death events, potentially serving as a critical link connecting early viral infection to systemic inflammatory responses. These findings may provide essential experimental evidence for elucidating the predisposition to severe influenza in certain patients and for developing preventive strategies against severe influenza progression.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and pre-activation

Murine B16-F10 melanoma cells (B16 cells) and human A549 lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cells (A549 cells) were provided by the Department of Molecular Biology, College of Basic Medical Sciences, Jilin University. The cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% (v/v) Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and antibiotics (100 IU of penicillin/ml and 100 IU of streptomycin/ml). All cells were maintained routinely in 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 ℃.

For the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), crystal violet staining, and flow cytometry assays, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in a 96-well plate. For protein and RNA extraction, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in a 12-well plate.

Cells were pre-treated with CpG ODN (CpG5805)or CCT ODN for various time prior to influenza A virus (FM1 strain) infection. To evaluate the expression level of surface TLR9 (sTLR9) on the surface of B16 cells pretreated with CpG5805, CCT ODN, or their combination, the experiment was conducted as follows: Cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured overnight. Subsequently, quadruplicate wells were set up and treated with either CpG5805 (6 µg/mL), CCT ODN (6 µg/mL), their combination, or PBS control. After 24 h of incubation, single-cell suspensions were prepared using trypsin digestion method for subsequent TLR9 surface staining and flow cytometric detection and analysis.

Oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs)

TLR9 agonist CpG5805 (5′-TCGACGAACGTTCGGTCGCCGCGG-3′)57 and TLR9 inhibitor CCT ODN (5′-CCTCCTCCTCCTCCTCCTCCTCCT-3′)58 were used in this study. All single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs) were synthesized, purified, and fully phosphorothioate-modified by Takara Biotech (Takara Biotech, Dalian, China). The fully phosphorothioate-modified ODNs were diluted in PBS.

Influenza virus

H1N1 influenza A virus (IAV), a mouse-adapted influenza virus strain A/Fort Monmouth/1/1947 virus (FM1), was propagated in the allantoic cavities of 9-day-old embryonated specific pathogen-free chicken eggs cultured at 37 ℃. After 48 h, the allantoic fluid was harvested from the infected eggs and stored at − 70 °C for subsequent experiments.

To determine the optimal viral infection concentration for B16 cells, a titration experiment was performed. Briefly, B16 cells seeded in a 96-well plate were inoculated with serial dilutions of IAV FM1 (ranging from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶) for 1 h. The cells were then washed twice with cold 1× PBS and replaced with RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum. Microscopic observations were conducted every 24 h. Pronounced cytopathic effects and cell mortality were observed in the groups infected with the 10⁻¹ to 10⁻³ dilutions, with severity correlating with the viral concentration. In contrast, cells exposed to 10− 4 or lower virus concentrations exhibited no significant infection, resembling the blank control group. Based on these results, the 10⁻³ dilution, which represented the minimum concentration that produced a clear infectious outcome, was selected for subsequent infections of B16 cells in this study.

Cell viability assessment

Cell viability was assessed using three methods: microscopic observation, crystal violet staining, and CCK-8 assay.

Microscopic observation

Cellular morphology of B16 or A549 cells in 96-well plates was examined under an optical microscope at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-infection. Observations focused on identifying changes indicative of infection or cell death, such as reduced cell density, cell rounding, and fragmentation. Following the observation of these morphological changes, the cells were subsequently subjected to crystal violet staining and CCK-8 assay.

Crystal violet staining

B16 cells seeded in 96-well plates were treated with CpG5805 and infected with IAV FM1. After treatments, the culture supernatant was removed. The cells were then stained with 0.25% crystal violet (100 µL/well) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). After discarding the supernatant, the plate was rinsed several times with distilled water until the blank wells were clear. For destaining, 100 µL of 0.9% sodium citrate buffer was added per well and left at RT for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader, BioTek).

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The effects of CpG5805 and H1N1 IAV on B16 or A549 cell viability were evaluated using the CCK-8 reagent (K1018, APExBIO). After treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was directly added to each well containing 100 µL of the original culture medium.The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h, and the absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader, BioTek).

Immunofluorescence assay

Infection of cells with the FM1 strain of IAV was detected by immunofluorescence. Cells were harvested and centrifuged onto glass slides. Subsequently, the cells were fixed and stained with FITC-labeled anti-IAV antibody from D3 Ultra DFA Respiratory Virus Screen & ID Kit (Diagnostic Hybrids, Athens, Ohio, USA), incubating at 37 ℃ for 20 min. After staining, the slides were washed, mounted, and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Hemagglutination assay

Viral titers in culture supernatants were determined by hemagglutination assay. Briefly, 50 µL of supernatant from infected cells was serially diluted twofold with 1× PBS in a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 50 µL of a 0.5% chicken erythrocyte suspension was added to each well. The plate was gently agitated to mix the contents and incubated at RT for 30 min. The hemagglutination phenomenon in each well of the plate was then observed and recorded.

Western blotting and quantitative analysis of the immunoblot bands

B16 cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (P0013C, Beyotime) containing 0.1 mmol/L PMSF (ST507, Beyotime) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (CW2383S, CWBIO) used at a 1:100 dilution. The protein concentration of the samples was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (CW0014S, CW Biotech). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with Tris buffered saline containing 5% skim milk at RT for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with antibodies against STING (13647, CST), Phospho-STING (AF7416, Affinity), β-Actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) at 4 ℃ overnight. After washing three times with TBST (0.05% Tween 20) for 5 min each, the membranes were incubated at RT for 1 h with either Goat anti-Rabbit (RGAR001, Proteintech) or Goat anti-Mouse (RGAM001, Proteintech) IgG HRP Antibody. To enable the combined analysis of data from different experimental batches, a protein lysate from untreated B16 cells was routinely loaded in one lane on each gel as a reference standard, designated as “Med” in the results.

For quantitative analysis of immunoblot bands, we utilized ImageJ software (version 1.54f) with the following protocol: Converted raw images to 8-bit grayscale mode to reduce computational complexity and improve analytical precision. Applied “Subtract Background” function with a rolling ball radius set at 50 pixels to eliminate nonspecific signal interference. Selected “Set Scale” in the Analyze module to define pixel as the unit length, ensuring subsequent measurements were recorded as absolute pixel values. Manually delineated target protein bands using rectangular selection tools to ensure complete coverage of regions of interest. Generated lane-specific grayscale distribution curves via Analyze > Gels > Select First Lane > Plot Lanes. Peaks were automatically identified using the Magic Wand tool, with system-recorded peak areas (Area). Repeated the above procedures for all target bands (p-STING, STING, and β-Actin). The data were exported to Excel, and the band intensity ratios (p-STING/β-Actin and STING/β-Actin) were calculated. For bands from different membranes, the ratio of the untreated “Med” band was set to 1, and all other lanes on the same membrane were normalized accordingly to enable the pooled analysis of results from independent experiments.

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The mRNA levels of Tnf-α, Il-6, Cxcl-2, Cxcl-10, Irf3, and Ifn-β were quantified by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from B16 cells using Trizol reagent (ET101-01, TransGen Biotech). The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (AE301-03, TransGen Biotech). Quantitative PCR was performed using a SYBR Green kit on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with gene-specific primers (Table 1). The β-actin gene was used as an endogenous reference for normalization. Gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (2^(-ΔΔCt)) method.

Mouse experiments

Female ICR mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were purchased from Yisi Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., in Changchun, China. The mice were raised in an Animal Biosafety Level 2 Laboratory at the Laboratory Animal Center of Jilin University and provided with free access to food and water. At the experimental endpoint, mice were humanely euthanized via CO₂ inhalation. The mouse experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin University College of Basic Medical Sciences (Approval ID: 2024 − 607). The study was conducted in strict accordance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and the Chinese Guidelines for Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892 − 2018). All procedures were performed in compliance with institutional biosafety and animal welfare policies.

To establish the influenza infection model, mice were randomly divided into four main groups based on the pre-treatment time point (1, 6, 24, or 48 h before viral infection). Each main group was then further divided into two subgroups (n = 9): one receiving an intranasal instillation of CpG5805 (10 µg, 50 µL/mouse), and the other receiving an equal volume of PBS as the vehicle control. All mice were subsequently infected intranasally with the FM1 strain of IAV (10⁻², 50 µL/mouse). At 3 days post-infection, three mice from each subgroup were euthanized for sample collection. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), lung tissue, and liver tissue were harvested for subsequent analysis. The remaining mice in each subgroup were monitored daily for 12 days.

Pathological analysis

To prepare tissue paraffin sections, the lungs of mice were fixed in 10% neutral formalin for 48 h, embedded in paraffin and sliced. The sections were stained with haematoxylin & eosin, and observed under the microscope.

These criteria are used to evaluate pathological changes in the lung, based on observations at different magnifications during microscopic examination. To enable accurate assessment of pulmonary pathology in mice, each lung was sectioned into four equal quadrants. Pathological changes within each quadrant were then scored individually. The specific scoring criteria employed are detailed below. (1) 4× macroscopic view: 3 points: Abnormalities affecting > 75% of the lung. 2 points: Abnormalities affecting > 50% but ≤ 75% of the lung. 1 point: Abnormalities affecting > 25% but ≤ 50% of the lung. 0 points: Essentially no abnormalities in the lung. (2) High-magnification microscopic view: 1 point: Inflammatory cell infiltration. 1 point: Abnormal alveolar structure (e.g., structural disorder, interstitial thickening, etc.). 1 point: Presence of significant hemorrhage. The total lung pathology score is the sum of the scores obtained from the 4×macroscopic view and the high-magnification microscopic view of the 4 quadrants.

Flow cytometry

For analyzing the percentage of IAV FM1 positive (FM1+) cells, B16 cells were stained with FITC-labeled anti-IAV antibody from D3 Ultra DFA Respiratory Virus Screen & ID Kit (Diagnostic Hybrids, Athens, Ohio, USA) for flow cytometry analysis. For analyzing the immune cell composition in mouse BALF, fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies of Ly6G-PE (Cat: 551461), CD14-FITC (Cat: 553739), CD3e-APC (Cat: 558257), CD45-Percp (Cat: 550994) from BD Biosciences (NJ, USA) were used for flow cytometry. For analyzing the sTLR9 expression on B16 cells, mouse TLR9 Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated antibody (FAB7960R, R&D Systems) were used. Staining was performed for 30 min on ice in the dark, followed by two washes with PBS. All the stained cells were detected and analyzed using BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), and experimental data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation. An unpaired t-test was used to compare groups. Survival curves of mice were estimated and compared using the Mantel-Cox test and Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test. Differences were considered statistically significant for p values < 0.05.

Results

Pre-activation of B16 melanoma cells enhances influenza virus-induced cell death

To investigate the impact of cellular pre-activation on viral pathogenicity, we first utilized B16 melanoma cells (B16 cells), a model frequently employed in oncolytic virotherapy studies. After determining that influenza A virus (IAV) FM1 could infect B16 cells (Fig. 1A), we selected CpG ODN, a TLR9 agonist, as a pre-activator, and determined that CpG ODN alone could not induce B16 cell death (Fig. 1B). We then pre-treated B16 cells with CpG ODN for 24 h before infecting them with influenza virus. Crystal violet staining and immunofluorescence analysis showed that the viability of CpG ODN-pre-activated B16 cells significantly decreased 48–96 h after influenza virus infection compared to non-pre-activated cells. Additionally, no difference was observed in the response of B16 cells pre-activated with different doses of CpG ODN (Fig. 1C and D). These results suggest that pre-activated B16 cells are more sensitive to death induced by influenza virus. To investigate whether this was due to the higher viral load in pre-activated B16 cells, we measured the percentage of influenza virus-positive (FM1+) cells using flow cytometry. It was found that the percentage of FM1+ cells actually decreased in the pre-activated B16 cells. Moreover, when gated into high and low viral load subpopulations (FM1hi and FM1low), pre-activated B16 cells showed a significantly lower percentage of FM1low cells than their respective subpopulation in control B16 cells, with a reduction of approximately 50% (Fig. 1E). We also performed immunofluorescence, which showed that the total number of pre-activated B16 cells decreased, and the percentage of FM1+ cells among the counted B16 and pre-activated B16 cells was similar (Fig. 1F). This raises the question of whether cells with a high viral load had already died. Therefore, we detected influenza virus titers in the culture supernatants of both B16 and pre-activated B16 cells by hemagglutination assay, and found that they were low and did not differ significantly (Fig. 1G). The low and comparable virus titers suggest that pre-activation does not enhance viral replication in the target cells but may instead alter cellular responses to the infection. These results suggest that pre-activation enhances the susceptibility of B16 cells to influenza virus-induced death through mechanisms other than viral load.

Pre-activation of B16 cells enhances the cytotoxicity of influenza virus within a susceptibility time window and is independent of TLR9

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which CpG ODN pre-activation enhances influenza virus-induced cell death, we first measured the death of B16 cells infected with influenza virus at different pre-activation times using crystal violet staining. We found that pre-activation of B16 cells for 24 h or less increased susceptibility to influenza virus-induced cell death, whereas pre-activation for over 24 h reduced sensitivity to virus-induced death. Specifically, cells pre-activated for 72 h exhibited similar viability to non-pre-activated cells after infection (Fig. 2A). Next, we administered CpG ODN after influenza virus infection to observe its effects on cell viability. The result showed that when CpG ODN was added 1 h post-infection of influenza virus, B16 cells were not induced to die by the virus and instead showed significant proliferation compared to cells infected with influenza virus alone (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we replaced CpG ODN with a TLR9 inhibitory ODN (CCT ODN) and observed the same results (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometric detection demonstrated that CCT ODN markedly enhanced surface TLR9 (sTLR9) MFI on B16 cells and percentage of sTLR9+ B16 cells relative to CpG ODN stimulation, while also blocking CpG ODN-mediated sTLR9 downregulation (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that the pre-activation enhances the cytotoxicity of influenza virus within a specific time window, and this effect is independent of the TLR9 pathway.

Pre-activation enhances influenza virus induced STING suppression in B16 cells

Since pre-activated B16 cells exhibited an increased sensitivity to influenza virus-induced cell death—independent of viral load and the TLR9 pathway—we hypothesized that pre-activation may prime B16 cells into an early inflammatory state, thereby altering their response to subsequent influenza virus infection. Given that the STING pathway can respond to DNA, DNA/RNA, and RNA stimuli, we initially examined the expression levels of STING and its phosphorylated form (p-STING) in untreated B16 cells (Med) and B16 cells pretreated with either PBS or CpG ODN using Western blotting. Quantitative analysis was performed by calculating the ratio of STING and p-STING band intensities to β-actin as an internal control at each time point, followed by normalization using a standard reference sample. Upon integration of the results from three independent experiments, the data demonstrated that the expression levels of both STING and p-STING in PBS-treated B16 cells at all time points remained comparable to the baseline values (Med group). Compared with the PBS group, B16 cells treated with CpG ODN exhibited decreasing trends in STING levels at 1 h, 6 h, and 12 h, along with an increasing trend at 3 h. However, none of these changes reached statistical significance. In contrast, p-STING expression levels in CpG ODN-treated B16 cells showed statistically significant increases at 1 h, 6 h, and 12 h post-treatment, while no significant differences were observed at 3 h and 24 h compared to the PBS group (Fig. 3A). These results indicate that STING in B16 cells can be activated by CpG ODN stimulation, with this significantly activated state appearing at 1 h post-stimulation and persisting for at least 12 h before returning to baseline levels by 24 h. We next assessed the expression of STING and p-STING in B16 cells pretreated with either PBS or CpG ODN at various time points after infection with influenza virus FM1. The results indicated that following FM1 infection, STING expression in both pretreatment groups either remained at levels comparable to the PBS-treated group without FM1 infection at 1, 3, 6, and 18 h, or decreased at 2, 24, and 48 h, with nearly identical trends observed between the two groups. Similarly, p-STING expression exhibited parallel changes, remaining comparable to the PBS-treated group without FM1 infection at 3, 18, and 24 h but decreasing at 1, 2, 6, and 48 h in both groups. Notably, compared to the p-STING levels in FM1-infected PBS-pretreated B16 cells, the CpG ODN-pretreated B16 cells showed significantly reduced p-STING levels at multiple time points post-FM1 infection, including at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 48 h, as shown in Fig. 3B. These findings suggest that influenza virus suppresses STING activation in B16 cells, and this suppression is amplified in pre-activated B16 cells. We further analyzed the mRNA levels of type I interferons (Ifn-α/β) and pro-inflammatory cytokines in PBS-treated and pre-activated B16 cells before and after influenza virus infection using RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 3C, pre-activation induced an early pro-inflammatory state in B16 cells, characterized by significant increases in the mRNA levels of Il-6, Tnf-α, Cxcl-2, and Cxcl-10, but not in Ifn-α/β. Following influenza virus infection, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in pre-activated B16 cells did not exceed that in PBS-treated B16 cells, potentially reflecting a state of fatigue in the re-activation response of pre-activated B16 cells to the virus. Collectively, these results indicate that pre-activation enhances the inhibitory effect of influenza virus on STING activation in B16 cells.

Pre-activation of A549 lung epithelial cells with CpG ODN recapitulates enhanced susceptibility to influenza virus infection

The findings from the study on B16 cells suggest that pre-activation of respiratory epithelial cells may exacerbate the damage caused by influenza virus infection. Therefore, this study required selection of a respiratory system-derived cell line as the validation model. Although A549 cells share tumorigenic properties and similar limitations with B16 cells, they were selected as the validation model for this study because their human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial origin enables better simulation of respiratory epithelial responses to IAV infection, consistent with their widespread use as an in vitro model in IAV research15,59–61. To explore this further, we investigated whether CpG ODN pre-activation similarly enhances the susceptibility of A549 lung epithelial cells to influenza virus-induced cell death. Using the CCK-8 assay, we measured the viability of PBS-treated and pre-activated A549 cells following influenza virus infection (Fig. 4A). Given that A549 cells are human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cells, their susceptibility to infection by the mouse-adapted FM1 IAV needs to be investigated. For this purpose, we selected several viral titers for the 24 h experimental group and adjusted the viral titers for the 48 h experimental group accordingly, ensuring the conditions were suitable for validating our findings in B16 cells. The results showed that when A549 cells were infected with an IAV titer of 1 × 10⁻², CpG ODN pre-activation significantly exacerbated cytopathic effects (p = 0.0497). In contrast, neither a lower (1 × 10⁻³) nor a higher (5 × 10⁻²) viral titer induced differential pathogenicity between preactivated and control cells at 24 h (Fig. 4B). Therefore, when conducting the 48-hour infection experiments, we excluded the lower titer (1 × 10⁻³) viral group. While retaining the original two viral titers (1 × 10⁻² and 5 × 10⁻²), we added two higher titer groups (1 × 10⁻¹ and 5 × 10⁻¹). The results demonstrated that at 48 h post-infection, CpG ODN-preactivated cells infected with either 1 × 10⁻¹ or 5 × 10⁻¹ titer viruses exhibited significantly exacerbated cytopathic effects compared to control cells. In contrast, no significant difference in pathogenicity was observed between preactivated and control cells at the two lower viral titers (Fig. 4C). We subsequently infected both CpG ODN preactivated and control A549 cells with IAV at titers of 1 × 10⁻¹ and 5 × 10⁻¹, with analyses performed at 24 h and 48 h post-infection. The results confirmed that infection with these two IAV titers consistently induced more severe cytopathic effects in preactivated cells than in controls at both time points (Fig. 4D). This conserved response between B16 melanoma cells and A549 lung epithelial cells suggests that the pre-activation mechanism may not be limited to melanoma cells but could also extend to epithelial cells, underscoring its potential relevance to respiratory infections.

Pre-treatment with CpG ODN exacerbates influenza virus pathogenicity in a mouse model

To validate our in vitro findings in a physiological context, we employed a murine model of influenza virus infection. Mice were intranasally treated with CpG ODN for 1 h to 48 h, followed by intranasal infection with influenza virus FM1. The results showed that mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h exhibited lower survival rates compared to PBS-pretreated mice after influenza virus infection. In contrast, mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 48 h showed the opposite trend. Interestingly, PBS pretreatment alone also influenced the pathogenicity of influenza virus in mice. For example, 100% of mice pretreated with PBS for 1–6 h survived after the infection, whereas only 30% of mice pretreated with PBS for 24 h survived. Notably, both CpG ODN and PBS pretreatment for 24 h resulted in high mortality rates (80% and 70%, respectively), but CpG ODN-pretreated mice died significantly earlier (Fig. 5A). Body weight changes did not follow a clear pattern between CpG ODN- and PBS-pretreated mice, but mice exhibiting early weight loss invariably died at later stages (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that mice pretreated with CpG ODN within 24 h experience more severe outcomes following influenza virus infection. This conclusion is further supported by lung/liver index measurements. Specifically, the lung and liver indices of mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 24 h were significantly smaller than those of PBS-pretreated mice after infection, while the lung index of mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 6 h was significantly larger (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that influenza virus infection in CpG ODN-pretreated mice may induce lung lesions of varying severity—ranging from edema to necrosis—with the latter occurring predominantly in mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 24 h. To further investigate these findings, we used flow cytometry to analyze the proportion of immune cells in the alveolar lavage fluid of CpG ODN- or PBS-pretreated mice on day 3 post-infection of influenza virus. The day 3 post-infection time point was chosen based on the observation that mortality in the 48-h CpG ODN-pretreated mice commenced as early as day 4 following IAV challenge. The results revealed a significant increase in the percentage of Ly6G+ neutrophils in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 24 h, while the percentages of CD14+ monocytes and CD3e+ T cells remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). Histopathological analysis demonstrated exacerbated lung injury in mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 1 h, characterized by alveolar wall thickening and increased inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that mice pretreated with CpG ODN for 24 h or less exhibit an enhanced inflammatory response to influenza virus in the respiratory system, likely driven by increased neutrophil infiltration. The in vivo results corroborate our in vitro observations, demonstrating that pre-activation of respiratory epithelial cells exacerbates influenza virus-induced lung injury and mortality in mice. The recruitment of neutrophils, potentially mediated by pre-activation-induced cytokines, further underscores the role of inflammatory priming in disease severity.

Discussion

In this study, we unexpectedly found that B16 cells pre-activated with the TLR9 agonist CpG ODN were more susceptible to cell death induced by IAV infection in vitro. Similarly, mice pretreated with CpG ODN exhibited increased mortality and more severe lung inflammation following IAV infection. These findings suggest that the mechanism underlying the enhanced severity of IAV infections may be related to the activation of target cells during a specific time window.

IAV is an influenza virus subtype that primarily infects respiratory epithelial cells, leading to acute respiratory infections often characterized by severe clinical manifestations and a high risk of acute lung injury. Consequently, IAV has been the subject of extensive research. Recent studies have increasingly focused on the interplay between viral target cells and immunopathological damage. Research indicates that, besides direct cytopathic effects from viral replication, influenza viruses can trigger diverse programmed cell death pathways, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, either through specific viral proteins (e.g., NS1) or by inducing innate immune responses in target cells, such as the production of type I interferons or pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF)13,15–17,62. However, the conditions under which target cells become more susceptible to virus-induced death remain unclear. During our in vitro experiments infecting B16 cells with IAV, we unexpectedly observed that B16 cells pre-activated with CpG ODN were more prone to virus-induced cell death. B16 cells are not respiratory epithelial cells. Although they can serve as an in vitro model for studying IAV without the need to consider the presence of cilia or a mucosal layer, their natural physiological functions, cellular structure, and growth microenvironment differ significantly from those of respiratory epithelial cells due to their origin as skin melanoma-derived cancer cells. Therefore, using them for IAV study does have certain limitations. Notably, B16 cells express high levels of sialic acid (the receptor for influenza virus)9 and possess a well-developed innate immune system for pathogen defense, including various PRRs such as the TLR9 (Fig S1) and cGAS-STING signaling pathways25. Upon CpG ODN stimulation, we also detected upregulated mRNA levels of Ifn-β and inflammatory cytokines (e.g., Il-6, Tnf, Cxcl2/10). Intriguingly, the increased susceptibility of pre-activated B16 cells to virus-induced death was independent of these cytokines or TLR9 signaling, as the cells were infected with IAV only after replacing the culture medium. Moreover, pretreatment with TLR9 inhibitory ODN (CCT ODN) yielded results similar to CpG ODN pre-activation. Previous studies have reported that CCT ODN (also known as SAT05f) exerts an inhibitory effect on TLR9 signaling58,63. In our experiments, we also demonstrated that it can elevate the level of sTLR9 of B16 cells. Moreover, recent studies have reported that sTLR9 on immune cells (e.g., B cells and neutrophils) is a marker of response inhibition57,64, and sTLR9 has been detected in various tumor cells, including hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, colon cancer, and breast cancer65–67. Additionally, pre-activated B16 cells did not support enhanced viral replication, ruling out the possibility that excessive viral replication led to cell lysis. Instead, we speculate that IAV likely induces programmed cell death. Thus, a critical question arises whether sequential stimulation by pre-activation and viral infection trigger the nucleic acid-sensing system in target cells, thereby initiating inflammatory cell death pathways?

Based on literature cues regarding nucleic acid-sensing systems, STING was identified as a unique molecule. As an endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein, STING not only recognizes cyclic dinucleotides but also serves as an adaptor for multiple DNA sensors (e.g., ZBP1, cGAS, etc.)47. Studies have shown that ZBP1 ligands can include both Z-DNA and Z-RNA, such as the Z-RNA of influenza viruses68. In our experimental system, CpG ODN or CCT ODN which are short single-stranded DNAs were used to pre-activate B16 cells, while influenza virus delivers its Z-RNA into these cells during infection. We hypothesized that STING might participate in cell death induction, as its signaling activation has been linked to cell death. However, when we examined B16 cells (both naïve and pre-activated) at different time points post-IAV infection, we observed reduced STING expression and phosphorylation levels, particularly in pre-activated B16 cells, where phosphorylated STING was even lower. This finding suggests that enhanced IAV-induced cell death may correlate with inhibition of the STING pathway—though this suppression occurred only after viral infection, as pre-activated B16 cells initially exhibited elevated STING phosphorylation. Thus, we speculate that the pre-activated STING pathway enters a negative feedback state during influenza virus response. The observed time window between pre-activation and increased virus-induced cell death further supports this hypothesis. But does STING inhibition actually promote cell death? Previous studies reported that STING-deficient mice exhibited increased necroptosis (mediated by the RIPK3-MLKL pathway) in lung macrophages after Staphylococcus aureus infection, with upregulated RIPK3 and MLKL levels47. However, both B16 and A549 cells used in our study lack RIPK369. Could influenza virus induce cell death by suppressing STING independently of RIPK3? Certainly, to conclusively demonstrate that STING pathway suppression mediates the exacerbated IAV-induced cell death, rescue experiments would be the most compelling approach. If successful in murine models, such interventions could potentially provide a preventive strategy against severe influenza. These validations are certainly worth pursuing in future studies.

Inflammatory cell death refers to a category of programmed or regulated cell death accompanied by inflammatory responses, primarily including necroptosis, pyroptosis, and NETosis (specific to neutrophils)70. In the absence of RIPK3, RIPK1 can also mediate apoptosis or trigger pyroptosis via the NLRP3 inflammasome71,72. Inhibition of the STING pathway may indirectly contribute to necroptosis by relieving RIPK1 suppression and downregulating Caspase 8 expression. Alternatively, reduced IFN-β production may feedback to upregulate ZBP1, exacerbating mitochondrial damage and NLRP3 inflammasome formation, thereby promoting pyroptosis. To elucidate the potential cell death pathways in our experimental system, we performed RNA sequencing analysis on both naïve and pre-activated B16 cells 24 h after influenza virus infection. The results revealed elevated mRNA levels of pyroptosis-related signaling molecules in pre-activated B16 cells post-infection. As shown in Fig S2, the mRNA levels of Nlrp3, Il-18, and Caspase-4 were elevated, indicative of pyroptosis pathway engagement. Although we did not further validate the specific cell death type, both our in vitro and in vivo results demonstrate that pre-activation exacerbates influenza virus-induced cell death. The results provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying severe influenza A virus infections and their prevention.
