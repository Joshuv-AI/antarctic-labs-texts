Evolution of Fear and Social Rewards in Prey-Predator Relationship
Yuji Kanagawa, Kenji Doya

Abstract
Fear is a critical brain function for detecting danger and learning to avoid specific stimuli that can lead to danger. While fear is believed to have evolved under pressure from predators, experimentally reproducing the evolution is challenging. To investigate the relationship between environmental conditions, the evolution of fear, and the evolution of other rewards, such as food reward and social reward, we developed a distributed evolutionary simulation. In our simulation, prey and predator agents co-evolve their innate reward functions, including a possibly fear-like term for observing predators, and learn behaviors via reinforcement learning. Surprisingly, our simulation revealed that social reward for observing the same species is more important for prey to survive, and fear-like negative reward for observing predators evolves only after acquiring social reward. We also found that the predator with increased hunting ability (larger mouth) amplified fear emergence, but also that fear evolution is more stable with non-evolving predators that are bad at chasing prey. Additionally, unlike for predators, we found that positive rewards evolve in opposition to fear for stationary threats, as areas with abundant leftover food develop around them. These findings suggest that fear and social reward have had a complex interplay with each other through evolution, along with the nature of predators and threats.



Introduction

Fear is a fundamental brain function that detects and avoids potential dangers
(
Adolphs, 2013
)
. Beyond generating conscious feelings, fear unconsciously reacts to threats and initiates learning to avoid actions associated with adverse outcomes
(
Tovote et al., 2015
)
. This detection of aversive signals, alongside the generation of positive rewards including food reward and social reward, is a fundamental part of our brains’ reward system
(
Schultz, 2015
)
. This system plays a crucial role in survival to tell us the desired behavior, realized by reinforcement learning (RL).

Given the importance of fear and aversive learning, it is natural to ask how these functions have evolved. Because animals show fear-related expression to external predators
(
Apfelbach et al., 2005
;
Yilmaz and Meister, 2013
)
, predators are believed to be influential in fear evolution
(
fM. Marks and Nesse, 1994
;
Öhman and Mineka, 2001
)
, as well as other threats like infection
(
Schaller and Park, 2011
)
. However, it is difficult to experimentally confirm the importance of predators in fear evolution by experiments. Consequently, it remains unclear how different properties of predators, such as their hunting ability, have shaped the evolution of fear. The evolutionary interplay between fear and other rewards, including food reward and social reward, is also not yet fully understood.

To this end, we developed a distributed evolutionary simulation framework to reproduce the evolution of fear by simulating prey and predators, based on the previous work on food reward evolution in RL agents
(
Kanagawa and Doya, 2024
)
. In our simulation, we model fear as a reward for observing predators by prey’s vision-like proximity sensors, where the value of this reward can be both negative and positive. Both prey and predators inherit their reward function as their genotype from their parents, which contains components related to food intake, motor output, predator observation, and prey observation. They learn to act via RL to maximize rewards produced by their innate reward functions. By allowing population fluctuation based on a simple birth and death rule, we expect that prey’s and predators’ reward functions co-evolve in a biologically plausible direction to live longer and reproduce more.

Our simulation results show that fear-like negative rewards for observing predators evolve in prey. However, at the same time, we found that the reward for observing other prey to form groups is more important for survival, and that fear-like negative rewards for observing predators evolved only after this reward had evolved. Furthermore, to investigate what affects the direction of fear evolution, we conducted a series of comparative experiments. First, we varied predators’ hunting ability by manipulating their mouth size, revealing that fear emergence was more significant with larger-mouthed predators. Second, we found that fear evolution is more stable in prey with non-evolving predators because it’s easier to avoid predators. Lastly, we considered environments with stationary harmful pitfalls, instead of predators. Surprisingly, the evolution of fear was not observed in this environment because stationary threats make zones with many uneaten foods, and entering that area is beneficial. Overall, our findings suggest that fear and social reward have had a complex interplay with each other through evolution, along with the nature of predators and threats.

Related Works

The relationship between fear and predators has been investigated in various ways, ranging from field studies to computational modeling. For example, in the field of Ecology, researchers have shown that fear responses, such as the singing of birds, correlate with population declines due to predation
(
Zanette and Clinchy, 2020
;
Allen et al., 2022
)
. This indicates that fear has adaptively evolved for population preservation. In mathematical models using prey-predator dynamics similar to Lotka-Volterra models
(
Wangersky, 1978
)
, it is reported that a strong fear response to predators can stabilize the population
(
Sarkar and Khajanchi, 2020
)
. Conversely, the concept of social reward is frequently examined in the context of family relationships, including kin selection
(
Foster et al., 2006
)
. Still, several works argue that predation risks amplify grouping behavior in animal studies
(
Krams et al., 2009
)
and simulation
(
Olson et al., 2015
)

We employ a more complex agent-based prey-predator simulation incorporating RL, which is suitable for observing the evolution of individual traits like fear circuits.
Olsen and Fraczkowski, 2015
employed an agent-based prey-predator simulation where each species learns its value function using RL with linear function approximation, finding that the co-evolution of learned value functions produces better-performing species. More recently, there have been several studies utilizing RL with an artificial neural network as its function approximator, called deep RL
(
Yamada et al., 2020
)
. While these studies focused on the evolution of agents’ behavioral strategies
(
Olsen and Fraczkowski, 2015
;
Park et al., 2021
)
and bodily characteristics
(
Yamada et al., 2020
)
, we focus on the evolution of the reward system, including fear and social reward.

We built our simulation on a previous work that investigated food and action reward evolution
(
Kanagawa and Doya, 2024
)
. They employed a distributed evolution model inspired by embodied evolution
(
Bredeche et al., 2018
)
literature, where each agent maintains its energy level, and they can die or produce their children based on their energy level. We extend their simulation model to a prey-predator setting.

Evolvable Reward Function and Learning

An agent
i
receives the rewards
r^{i}_{j}
at step
j
, which is the linear sum of four terms:

\displaystyle r^{i}_{j}=

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{food}}^{i}n_{j}^{i}+0.01w_{\mathrm{act}}^{i}|a_{j}^{i}|

\displaystyle+0.1w_{\mathrm{prey}}^{i}\max_{k}{s}_{\mathrm{prey}}^{i,k}+0.1w_{\mathrm{predator}}^{i}\max_{k}{s}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{i,k},

where
n_{j}^{i}
represents the number of food items or prey consumed at this step,
|a_{j}^{i}|
is the magnitude of the normalized motor output.
\max_{k}{s}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{i,k}
is the maximum proximity sensor input for predators, which means that this reward value reacts to the closest predator.
\max_{k}{s}_{\mathrm{prey}}^{i,k}
is the same for preys. The reward weights
w_{\mathrm{food}}
,
w_{\mathrm{act}}
,
w_{\mathrm{prey}}
, and
w_{\mathrm{predator}}
are genetic parameters that gradually evolve by inheriting values from parents with added mutation noise. For example, we expect that
w_{\mathrm{food}}
will have positive values for both prey and predators, and
w_{\mathrm{predator}}
will have negative values for prey so that it corresponds to fear. Because
n_{j}^{i}
is often zero, other reward weights are scaled to take smaller values. The mutation noise is sampled from Student’s t-distribution with
2
degrees of freedom and scale
0.4
. Because this distribution is heavy-tailed, all reward weights are clipped into
[-10,10]
after mutation to avoid too large a jump. In the initial population,
w
is sampled from a normal distribution with center
0.0
and standard deviation
0.1
. In the experiment with stationary pitfalls, we use reward weights
w_{\mathrm{agent}}
for observing other individuals and
w_{\mathrm{pitfall}}
for observing pitfalls, instead of
w_{\mathrm{prey}}
and
w_{\mathrm{predator}}
used in the predator-prey environment.

Figure 7:
Schematic figure shows the learning procedure of prey and predators

Each agent has its own independent three-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with
64
hidden units. As shown in
Figure

7
, the MLP takes touch sensors, proximity sensors, angle, velocity, angular velocity, and energy as input, and outputs policies for two motors as a Gaussian with state-independent variance. Agents act by sampling motor output from this Gaussian policy. Reward is generated from the genetically inherited reward weights combined with food intake, action, and sensor input information. From this reward and the history of actions and observations, they learn actions to maximize the sum of rewards via an RL algorithm called proximal policy optimization (PPO,
Schulman et al., 2017
). The learned policy is not inherited by newborns. Instead, they all need to learn their behavior from scratch with randomly initialized neural networks.

Conclusion

To study the origin of fear evolution, we conducted a series of evolutionary simulations where prey and predator’s reward functions evolve. Notably, we found that the social reward for observing the same species is more important and evolves first, and then fear-like negative reward for observing predators evolves. In comparison with predators that have different mouth sizes, we found that fear is more important when predators have better hunting ability. On the contrary, in experiments with non-evolving predators, fear evolved stably with negative social reward, while predators showed less hunting ability. This indicates that the importance of fear and social rewards changes according to the nature of predators, and for non-smart predators, fear alone can evolve. Finally, we examined whether fear would evolve in response to stationary threats (pitfalls) rather than predators. Surprisingly, in our simulation environment where food is generated uniformly at random, agents actually evolved to prefer pitfalls because food often accumulates abundantly around pitfalls without being consumed. This suggests that the spatial relationship between threats and foods significantly affects fear evolution.

Our results reveal that the evolution of fear is strongly influenced by social rewards and the predation capabilities of predators. It is particularly interesting that, in environments where grouping provides protection, like in our simulation, social rewards evolve first, followed by the evolution of fear. On the other hand, in our simulations, the effectiveness of visual fear was limited against predators approaching from behind, as agents only had forward-facing proximity sensors as their vision. Investigating the impact of different visual systems on the evolution of fear is interesting for future research. Furthermore, considering that mammals like mice exhibit olfaction-related fear, simulating the evolution of fear associated with smell is another intriguing direction.
