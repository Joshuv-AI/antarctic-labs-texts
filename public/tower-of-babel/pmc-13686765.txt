Treatment patterns and outcomes in advanced clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors
Authors: Adel Shahnam, Daniel Reinhorn, Mrinal M Gounder, Rhoena Desir-Camille, Olayode Babatunde, Ciara M Kelly, Lauren B Banks, Viswatej Avutu, Ping Chi, Sujana Movva, Mark A Dickson, Damon R Reed, Mary L Keohan, Cristina R Antonescu, Meera Hameed, Konstantinos Linos, Narasimhan Agaram, Robert G Maki, Sandra P D’Angelo, William D Tap

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor are ultra-rare soft tissue sarcomas with no established standard of care for advanced disease. Data evaluating outcomes of systemic therapy remain limited. The primary objective was to describe patterns of systemic therapy use, and secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS) and real-world progression-free survival (PFS). Median follow-up was 192 months, and patients received a median of 2 treatment lines with heterogeneous sequencing. Median OS was 18 months (95% CI = 13.6 to 28.0). Median first-line real-world PFS was 3.8 months (95% CI = 2.9 to 6.3), with no statistically significant differences between treatment classes. Outcomes remain poor, highlighting the need for novel therapies and collaborative studies.

Clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor are very rare cancers with no established standard treatment once they have spread. Evidence to guide therapy is limited, and treatment is often based on approaches used for other soft-tissue sarcomas. We reviewed the records of 66 patients treated at a specialist cancer center between 2000 and 2025 to describe treatment patterns and outcomes. Treatments varied widely, and patients received a median of 2 lines of therapy. Median overall survival was 18 months, and median progression-free survival for first-line treatment was 3.8 months. Outcomes were poor across chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, with no treatment class showing a consistent advantage. Results were also similar when clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor were examined separately. These findings highlight the absence of an effective standard approach and support referral to expert centers, enrollment in clinical trials, and international collaboration to develop better treatments.

Introduction

Clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor are an ultra-rare, aggressive soft tissue sarcoma, accounting for approximately 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas.1 They are characterized by recurrent gene fusions involving EWSR1 and members of the CREB transcription factor family, including EWSR1::ATF1, EWSR1::CREB1, and less commonly EWSR1::CREM; however, the relative frequency of these fusions differs by entity, with EWSR1::ATF1 predominating in clear cell sarcoma, whereas EWSR1::CREB1 appears relatively more represented in gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor.2-5 Despite this shared molecular signature, clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor diverge in their lines of differentiation. Clear cell sarcoma typically exhibits melanocytic differentiation, with expression of HMB-45, Melan-A, and MITF, whereas gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor demonstrates neural or neuroectodermal differentiation, characterized by S100 and SOX10 positivity and absence of melanocytic marker expression, including HMB-45 and Melan-A.6,7 In addition, clear cell sarcoma predominantly affects adolescents and young adults and typically arises in the extremities, whereas gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor commonly arises from the gastrointestinal tract.2,7 There is no established standard of care for advanced disease, and data evaluating outcomes of systemic therapy remain limited.8

Advanced or metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor are largely resistant to conventional chemotherapy, with retrospective series demonstrating a median progression-free survival (PFS) of approximately 3 months.2,8-12 In the absence of clear cell sarcoma–specific treatment recommendations in current guidelines, systemic therapy is typically extrapolated from broader soft tissue sarcoma approaches, despite limited efficacy.8 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been used as clear cell sarcoma was historically referred to as melanoma of soft parts, raising the possibility of shared biological features with melanoma.2 However, clear cell sarcoma is now understood to be biologically distinct and less immunogenic.2 Emerging strategies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have shown modest activity, with low response rates and occasional disease stabilization.11,13-15 In clinical practice, gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors are also treated similarly to clear cell sarcoma. However, these data are derived from small, heterogeneous cohorts, and there remains limited real-world evidence to guide treatment selection or sequencing in this rare disease.

Given these limitations, there remains a critical gap in the literature describing real-world treatment patterns and outcomes, particularly from high-volume expert centers. We therefore performed a comprehensive evaluation of systemic therapy use in patients with metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor treated at a tertiary referral institution. By characterizing patterns of care alongside key clinical outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and real-world PFS, we aim to better define the effectiveness of contemporary treatment approaches, including chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, in this rare and understudied disease.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, single-institution observational study used electronic health record data from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) to identify patients with metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor. Eligible patients had histologically confirmed disease, as reviewed by expert sarcoma pathologists at MSKCC, and metastatic diagnosis was between January 1, 2000, and May 31, 2025. Patients with localized disease only were excluded. Collected clinical variables included age at metastatic diagnosis, sex, date of metastatic diagnosis, date of initiation of systemic therapies, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, sites of metastases at diagnosis, primary tumor site, primary tumor size, fusion status, details of systemic therapies administered, and site of progression after first-line systemic therapy. Patients were stratified into 2 cohorts based on the date of metastatic diagnosis: an early cohort (January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2013) and a late cohort (January 1, 2014, to May 31, 2025). These periods were selected to correspond with the clinical availability of pazopanib and cabozantinib, respectively. Where available, genomic data were collected from next-generation sequencing (NGS) reports.

Endpoints

The primary objective of this study was to describe patterns of systemic therapy use in patients with metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor. Secondary endpoints included OS and real-world PFS from first-line systemic therapy. Overall survival was defined as the time from diagnosis of metastatic disease to death from any cause. Real-world PFS was defined as the time from initiation of systemic therapy to disease progression, as determined by the treating clinician, or death, whichever occurred first. In the absence of clearly documented progression, treatment discontinuation or change not attributable to toxicity was considered a proxy for progression. Patients without an event were censored at the date of last follow-up. Exploratory endpoints included evaluation of real-world PFS across treatment classes, including immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and chemotherapy, as well as analyses of selected agents within these classes. Given their rarity, overlapping EWSR1–CREB family fusion biology, and similar real-world treatment approaches, clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor were included in the pooled primary analysis. To evaluate potential heterogeneity between these distinct entities, baseline characteristics, treatment patterns, OS, and first-line real-world PFS were additionally examined by diagnosis. Diagnosis-specific univariable analyses were performed separately for clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor, and exploratory survival analyses compared tumors harboring EWSR1::ATF1 with those harboring EWSR1::CREB1/CREM.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and categorical variables using the χ2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. For sparse multicategory treatment comparisons, Fisher exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was used.

Survival analyses of OS and first-line real-world PFS were performed according to treatment received in the first-line setting, with each patient contributing a single observation. For treatment-specific exploratory analyses, outcomes were evaluated based on the first exposure to each treatment class or agent. To ensure independence of observations and avoid double counting, each patient contributed only 1 observation per treatment class, defined by the first-ever use of that therapy. Patients who received multiple agents within a class across different lines were assigned to the agent received at first exposure and were not included multiple times in the same analysis.

Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Multivariable analyses were not performed because of the limited sample size. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All tests were 2-sided, and a P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.3).

Patient consent

The requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the institutional research ethics committee because of the retrospective nature of the study. The study involved analysis of existing clinical, pathological, imaging, and treatment data with no direct patient contact and no impact on patient management. All data were de-identified prior to analysis, and the study posed minimal risk to participants. The waiver of consent was approved by the institutional research ethics committee in accordance with applicable national regulations and institutional policies.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of MSKCC (IRB-16 to 1101, July 25, 2022).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 66 patients were included in the analysis, with a median follow-up of 192 months. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among the patients, 73% had clear cell sarcoma, and 27% had gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor. The median age at metastatic diagnosis was 36.5 years (IQR = 26.0-47.0 years), with the majority (64%) of patients younger than 40 years. Most patients (53%) had an ECOG performance status of 0, presented with recurrent disease (64%), and had an extremity primary tumor (55%). The median primary tumor size was 5.1 cm (IQR = 3.6-7.5 cm). At the time of metastatic diagnosis, the lung was the most common visceral site of metastasis (42%), whereas nodal involvement was the most frequent overall site (59%).

Demographics of the study cohort.

Baseline characteristics differed between clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor. All gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors arose from gastrointestinal sites, whereas 75% of clear cell sarcoma tumors arose in the extremities (P < .01). Lung metastases were more frequent in clear cell sarcoma than gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor (58% vs 0%), as were nodal metastases (71% vs 28%; both P < .01). In contrast, liver metastases were more common in gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor (78% vs 10%; P < .01), as were peritoneal metastases (39% vs 6.3%; P < .03). Fusion profiles also differed, with EWSR1::ATF1 more frequent in clear cell sarcoma (52% vs 33%) and EWSR1::CREB1/CREM more frequent in gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor (28% vs 8%; overall P = .04). Other baseline characteristics were similar between groups.

Patterns of care

Of 66 patients, 55 received systemic therapy. One patient was managed with best supportive care because of patient preference, 6 patients received local therapies only (radiotherapy or metastasectomy), and 4 patients had no available data on systemic treatment. Treatment patterns across lines of therapy are illustrated in Figure 1. Among the 55 patients who received systemic therapy, the median number of lines of treatment was 2. Fourteen patients received 1 line of therapy, 16 received 2 lines, 10 received 3 lines, and 15 received 4 or more lines of treatment. The median time from metastatic diagnosis to starting systemic treatment was 2.1 months (IQR = 0.8-5.8 months).

Patterns of treatment across first to third line of treatment. Abbreviations: ICI = immune checkpoint inhibitor; TKI = tyrosine kinase inhibitor; Tx = treatment.

In the first-line setting, the most common treatment was tyrosine kinase inhibitor or targeted therapy, followed by chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens. In the second-line setting, treatment distribution was more heterogeneous, with tyrosine kinase inhibitor or targeted therapy and chemotherapy remaining the most frequently used approaches, followed by immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens and clinical trial participation; 25.5% of patients did not receive further systemic therapy. In the third-line setting, chemotherapy was the most common treatment modality, followed by immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens and tyrosine kinase inhibitor or targeted therapy, with further attrition observed as 29.1% of patients did not receive third-line therapy. Across all lines, there was substantial variability in treatment sequencing, with frequent switching between therapeutic classes rather than consistent continuation of a single modality. Table S1 describes the full treatment regimen between the first and third line of treatment. There were no statistically significant differences in treatment patterns between clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor (Table S2). Treatment patterns were more diverse during 2014-2025 than during 2000-2013, with greater use of immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens and clinical trials in later lines, although treatment-class distributions did not differ statistically significantly between periods (all P > .05; Table S3).

Overall survival

Among the patients, 52 had a death event corresponding to a median OS of 18 months (95% CI = 13.6 to 28.0 months) (Figure 2). Compared with first-line doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor–based therapy and ICI were associated with a longer survival (tyrosine kinase inhibitor: hazard ratio [HR] = 0.33, 95% CI = 0.14 to 0.79; P = .01; ICI: HR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.10 to 0.94; P = .04) (Figure S1). No statistically significant association with age, primary tumor site, or molecular subtype was seen (Figure S1). Gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor was associated with numerically longer OS than CCS, although the difference was not statistically significant and estimates were limited by the small GNET subgroup (HR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.30 to 1.04; P = .07) (Figure S2). Larger primary tumor size (>5 cm) was associated with a numerically shorter survival (HR = 1.75, 95% CI = 0.92 to 3.32; P = .09) (Figure S1). Among metastatic sites at diagnosis, bone involvement also showed a numerically poorer outcome (HR = 2.06, 95% CI = 0.98 to 4.30; P = .06), whereas other sites showed no statistically significant differences (Figure S1). In diagnosis-specific sensitivity analyses, the direction of associations was generally consistent with the pooled cohort, although confidence intervals widened because of the smaller subgroup sizes (Figures S3 and S4). Overall survival did not differ statistically significantly between patients with EWSR1::CREB1/CREM and EWSR1::ATF1 fusions (HR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.24 to 1.73; P = .38), although the analysis was limited by the small number of patients in each molecular subgroup (Figure S5).

The overall survival (OS) of the study population.

A total of 12 (18%) patients achieved an OS of greater than 60 months. Of these 12 (92%) patients, 11 had recurrent rather than de novo metastatic disease, with solitary recurrences managed with local therapy such as metastasectomy and/or radiotherapy. Compared with patients with OS less than 60 months, long-term survivors tended to have smaller primary tumors, with 38% having primary tumors greater than 5 cm vs 61% in the OS less than 60 months group (Table S4). Median age at metastatic diagnosis was higher in the long-term survivors (45.5, IQR = 37.5-54.0 years) compared with the OS less than 60 months group (34.0, IQR = 25.0-42.0). They also appeared to have more indolent disease biology, reflected by a longer median time from metastatic diagnosis to initiation of first-line systemic therapy (24.6, IQR = 3.4-69.0 months vs 1.8 months IQR = 0.7-3.6 months). At metastatic diagnosis, they also had lower rates of bone, nodal, and lung metastases. In contrast, no clear differences were observed in fusion status.

Real-world PFS

Of the 55 patients who received systemic therapy, 52 experienced a real-world PFS event, with a median first-line real-world PFS of 3.8 months (95% CI = 2.9 to 6.3 months) (Figure 3). No clinical or pathological factors including diagnosis (clear cell sarcoma vs gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor), or systemic treatment class, were statistically significantly associated with first-line real-world PFS (Figures S6 and S7). In diagnosis-specific sensitivity analyses, the direction of associations was generally consistent with the pooled cohort, although estimates were less precise because of the reduced subgroup sizes (Figures S8 and S9). Real-world PFS did not differ statistically significantly between patients with EWSR1::CREB1/CREM and EWSR1::ATF1 fusions (HR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.37 to 2.23; P = .83), although the analysis was limited by the small number of patients in each molecular subgroup (Figure S10).

The real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) for first-line treatment of the study population.

Across all lines of therapy, the median real-world PFS for first exposure to an immune checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimen was 3.1 months (95% CI = 1.4 to 6.6 months; Figure S11), and first exposure to a tyrosine kinase inhibitor was 4.1 months (95% CI = 2.4 to 7.6 months; Figure S12), with no statistically significant difference between cabozantinib and pazopanib (HR = 1.22, 95% CI = 0.51 to 2.88; P = .66; Figure S13). The median real-world PFS for first chemotherapy exposure was 2.8 months (95% CI = 1.7 to 6.4 months; Figure S14). Patients receiving doxorubicin-based chemotherapy as their initial chemotherapy regimen had improved real-world PFS compared with nondoxorubicin-based chemotherapy regimens (median = 6.4 months, 95% CI = 2.8 to not reached, vs 1.4 months, 95% CI = 1.0 to 3.5 months; HR = 4.1, 95% CI = 1.71 to 9.83; P < .001; Figure S15).

Patterns of progression after first-line therapy

Figure 4 describes the pattern of progression by sites of disease after first-line systemic therapy. The most common site of progression was lung (46%) followed by nodal (42%), liver (29%), and peritoneum (15%) disease. Lung was the most frequent first progression site in the doxorubicin-based chemotherapy cohort (71%), whereas liver and nodal progression were most frequent in the tyrosine kinase inhibitor–based cohort (43% each). In the immune checkpoint inhibitor–based cohort, lung and nodal progression were observed in 50% of patients, and bone progression was more frequent than in other groups (38%). In the nondoxorubicin-based chemotherapy cohort, lung and nodal progression were observed in 44% of patients.

Genomic profile

Among patients with available broad-panel tumor next-generation sequencing/comprehensive genomic profiling, 24 had sequencing data suitable for summarizing co-occurring genomic alterations. This analysis was distinct from diagnostic fusion testing, which was available more broadly. Beyond the characteristic fusion events, recurrent alterations were infrequent; CDKN2A and DNMT1 alterations were identified in 8% of sequenced cases, whereas most other alterations were observed in single patients (Figure 5). These findings suggest that clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumors harbor relatively few recurrent co-alterations detectable by broad-panel next-generation sequencing.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of treatment patterns and clinical outcomes in patients with metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor treated at a high-volume tertiary center. We observed substantial heterogeneity in systemic therapy use, with no consistent treatment sequencing and progressive attrition across lines of therapy. Overall outcomes remained poor, with a median OS of 18 months and limited real-world PFS across treatment classes. Tyrosine kinase inhibitor–based therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitor–based therapy, and doxorubicin-based chemotherapy were associated with improved outcomes in exploratory analyses; however, no treatment approach demonstrated consistent efficacy signal.

We observed a median OS of 18 months in patients with metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor, underscoring the aggressive nature of these diseases and the need for more effective therapies. In our cohort, 18% of patients achieved an OS greater than 60 months, which does suggest there may be distinct biological subgroups of clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor with varying prognosis that needs to be further evaluated. Overall, our findings are consistent with prior reports, including a multi-institutional study of 55 patients demonstrating a median OS of 15 months, as well as population-based analyses reporting 5-year survival rates of approximately 15%-25%.9,16,17

Clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor demonstrated distinct clinicopathologic characteristics, including differences in primary site, metastatic distribution, and fusion profile. Despite these differences, treatment-class distributions and survival outcomes were not statistically significantly different between the 2 diagnoses, although subgroup estimates were imprecise because of limited sample size. Similarly, OS and first-line real-world PFS did not differ statistically significantly between EWSR1::ATF1 and EWSR1::CREB1/CREM-positive tumors. These findings support the clinical relevance of reporting the entities separately while suggesting that the poor outcomes observed in the pooled cohort were not driven exclusively by one diagnostic or molecular subgroup. However, clinically meaningful differences cannot be excluded, particularly for treatment-specific effects.

In this study, no systemic treatment class was associated with improved real-world PFS compared with doxorubicin-based regimens, consistent with prior reports demonstrating limited differences in efficacy across treatment modalities.9,18 However, TKI-based therapy in the first-line setting was associated with improved OS compared with chemotherapy. This observation is biologically plausible, as the EWSR1::ATF1 fusion characteristic of clear cell sarcoma leads to activation of microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) and subsequent upregulation of cellular mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (c-MET) signaling.19 Preclinical studies have demonstrated that MET signaling plays a central role in tumor cell invasion, chemotaxis, and survival and that inhibition of this pathway can suppress tumor growth.19 Despite this rationale, clinical data from the CREATE trial demonstrated limited activity of MET inhibition with crizotinib monotherapy, suggesting that clear cell sarcoma tumor biology is likely driven by multiple parallel or compensatory signaling pathways.14 These findings should be interpreted with caution given the potential for confounding by indication in this retrospective cohort.

Immune checkpoint inhibitor–based therapies demonstrated modest activity, with no statistically significant improvement in real-world PFS compared with doxorubicin-based chemotherapy. This is consistent with prior studies showing low response rates with immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy in clear cell sarcoma, although a subset of patients may achieve disease stabilization.13 The limited efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in clear cell sarcoma may reflect a relatively immunologically cold tumor microenvironment and low tumor mutational burden, although this remains incompletely characterized. Combination strategies, including immune checkpoint inhibitor with anti-angiogenic agents, have shown more promising signals in early phase studies, suggesting a potential role for combinatorial approaches in overcoming resistance.11,15

Chemotherapy demonstrated limited efficacy overall, with short real-world PFS across regimens, consistent with the known relative chemoresistance of clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor.8,12,20 However, doxorubicin-based chemotherapy was associated with improved real-world PFS compared with nondoxorubicin regimens. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect treatment selection bias, with doxorubicin more commonly used in earlier lines of therapy or in patients with better performance status.

This study has several strengths. It represents one of the largest single-institution analyses of metastatic clear cell sarcoma from a high-volume sarcoma referral center, with detailed longitudinal clinical data allowing for comprehensive characterization of treatment patterns and outcomes across multiple lines of therapy. The use of real-world data reflects contemporary clinical practice and provides insight into treatment sequencing and effectiveness in a rare disease where prospective trials are limited.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces the potential for residual confounding and confounding by indication, particularly in treatment-specific outcome analyses. Important clinical variables, including performance status, were incomplete for a proportion of patients, and unmeasured factors such as comorbidities and physician or patient treatment preferences may have influenced treatment selection and outcomes. The relatively small sample size, inherent to this ultra-rare disease, limited statistical power and precluded multivariable analyses. Additionally, the definition of real-world PFS, incorporating clinician-assessed progression and treatment changes as a proxy, may introduce misclassification compared with standardized Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors–based endpoints. Finally, the heterogeneity of treatment approaches over the extended study period may limit generalizability to contemporary practice. Prospective, collaborative studies are needed to validate these findings and better define optimal treatment strategies in this population.

Metastatic clear cell sarcoma and gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumor remains a highly aggressive disease with limited effective systemic treatment options. Real-world outcomes remain poor across treatment classes, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies and collaborative prospective studies in this rare population.
