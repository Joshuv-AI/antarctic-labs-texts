Nomogram for predicting axillary pathologic complete response in cN2–3 breast cancer after neoadjuvant chemotherapy
Authors: Jeong Hun Oh, Soyeon Ahn, Mijung Jang, Yena Yoon, Seongjin Jeong, Jongwon Kang, Hyoung Won Koh, Kyung-Hwak Yoon, Hee-Chul Shin, Eun-Kyu Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to predict axillary pathologic complete response (pCR) in patients with clinical N2–3 breast cancer who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) followed by breast and axillary surgeries. These patients were retrospectively analyzed between 2016 and 2020. Imaging metrics (lesion size and reduction ratios measured using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasonography (US)) and clinical and pathological factors were analyzed. Axillary pCR was achieved in 49.3% (n = 136/276) of the patients. Post-NAC significant predictors included breast lesion and lymph node (LN) sizes on MRI and LN cortical thickness on US. Pre-NAC tumor-specific factors were also associated with axillary pCR. In multivariable logistic regression analyses using three models, Model 3 (imaging variables, tumor characteristics, and molecular subtypes) demonstrated the best predictive performance (AUC: 0.9). Integrating molecular subtype-specific imaging and tumor characteristics enabled precise prediction of axillary pCR in patients with cN2–3 breast cancer, supporting tailored surgical approaches based on individual responses.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Surgical oncology

Introduction

Systemic treatments for breast cancer have been increasingly refined, which has enabled the development of personalized therapeutic approaches. Although the efficacy of chemotherapy differs across molecular subtypes, pathologic complete response (pCR) is significantly associated with improved overall and event-free survival1.

Advancements in systemic therapies have driven efforts to minimize surgical intervention for breast cancer, particularly in the axillary region2–5. Axillary staging evolved from extensive axillary lymph node (LN) dissection (ALND) in the 1970s to less invasive sentinel LN biopsy (SLNB) in the 1990s6. Studies have recently explored omitting SLNB entirely in selected populations7,8.

Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend ALND for clinically node-positive patients who remain node-positive after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), while SLNB may be considered for those who convert to node-negative status9. Although molecular subtype influences nodal response, conversion to a node-negative status is more likely to occur after NAC in patients with cN1 disease10. Ongoing studies are therefore exploring SLNB omission in patients with cN0–1 disease and selected subtypes11. However, in patients with more advanced nodal disease (cN2–3), clinicians remain cautious about using SLNB alone for axillary staging because of the higher burden of disease and associated risks. Despite favorable NAC responses, ALND is frequently performed in patients with cN2–3 disease12. However, in cases in which axillary pCR is achieved, ALND may represent an unnecessary surgical burden. Although there are no established guidelines for omitting ALND in these patients, accurate preoperative prediction of axillary pCR is essential for optimizing surgical decision-making and determining the appropriate extent of axillary surgery.

In addition to improving survival, reducing the invasiveness of axillary surgery has become a priority for mitigating complications such as lymphedema13, which can cause physical discomfort and psychological distress14. As cancer survival rates improve, there is a growing emphasis on optimizing post-treatment quality of life, which is an essential component of holistic breast cancer management.

This study aimed to identify predictors of axillary pCR in patients with cN2–3 disease and to develop a nomogram that could be applied in clinical practice to guide axillary surgery.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 276 patients with cN2–3 breast cancer who underwent NAC followed by breast and axillary surgeries were included in the study. Among them, 136 (49.3%) achieved axillary pCR (ypN0), while 140 (50.7%) had residual nodal disease (ypN+). Predictors of axillary pCR

The univariable analysis identified several significant predictors of axillary pCR (Table 2). Breast-related factors, including post-NAC lesion size (US, p = 0.007; MRI, p < 0.001) and reduction ratio (US, p < 0.001; MRI, p < 0.001) measured using US and MRI, were strongly associated with axillary pCR. Axillary nodal factors, such as pre-NAC (US, p = 0.002; MRI, p = 0.010) and post-NAC (US, p < 0.001; MRI, p < 0.001) nodal size, reduction ratio (US, p < 0.001; MRI, p = 0.008), and post-NAC cortical thickness (p < 0.001), were also significant predictors.

Patients with estrogen receptor (ER)-negative (p < 0.001), progesterone receptor (PR)-negative (p < 0.001), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive (p < 0.001) tumors had significantly higher rates of axillary pCR. Molecular subtypes stratified by hormone receptor (HR) and HER2 status revealed significant differences, with HR−/HER2+ patients achieving the highest axillary pCR rates (p < 0.001). Additionally, a higher histological grade (grade III; p = 0.005) and Ki-67 index (p < 0.001) were positively correlated with axillary pCR.

Multivariable analysis

To balance clinical interpretability with model parsimony, we pre-specified a hierarchical modeling strategy. Candidate predictors were first evaluated in univariable logistic regression analyses. From these initial analyses, using a significance threshold of p < 0.05, we confirmed that both breast- and axilla-based imaging predictors were associated with axillary pCR. However, because multiple correlated variables could reduce statistical reliability, we sought to identify only the most informative predictors with the strongest discriminatory power. Given that multiple imaging combinations were possible—(1) domain-specific predictors from US vs MRI, and (2) pre-treatment, post-treatment, and reduction ratios—we compared alternative sets of predictors after assessing linearity and collinearity. Among breast-related variables, the reduction ratio on MRI yielded the highest AUC; among axillary LN (ALN)-related variables, the reduction ratio on MRI and the post-NAC cortical thickness on US showed the strongest performance. Based on these findings, we selected the most informative imaging predictors for Model 1: breast lesion size reduction ratio measured using MRI (OR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04, p < 0.001); ALN size reduction ratio measured using MRI (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.02, p = 0.3), and post-NAC ALN cortical thickness measured using US (OR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10–0.33, p < 0.001). Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analyses were then considered for inclusion in Models 2 and 3.

Three separate multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the predictive performance of different variable combinations (Table 3). Model 1 included only three imaging-derived variables (breast lesion reduction ratio on MRI, ALN reduction ratio on MRI, and post-NAC ALN cortical thickness on US). Model 2 included the variables of Model 1 and tumor characteristics that were identified as significant predictors in the univariable analysis (number of breast lesions, histological grade, and Ki-67 index). Model 3 included the variables of Model 2 and molecular subtype.

Subgroup analysis

Although Model 3 achieved excellent predictive performance, limitations arose when attempting to apply these results to a nomogram. The high OR for specific molecular subtypes, particularly HR−/HER2+, posed challenges in constructing a universally applicable nomogram. To address this, the patients were divided into four subgroups according to the molecular subtype. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for each molecular subtype using Model 2 variables (excluding molecular subtype; Supplementary Table 2).

In HR+/HER2− patients, the ALN reduction ratio (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12, p = 0.045) and number of breast lesions (OR: 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01–0.51, p = 0.006) were predictive. In HR+/HER2+ patients, post-NAC ALN cortical thickness (OR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.07–1.59, p = 0.2) had a weaker predictive value. In HR−/HER2+ patients, histologic grade (OR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.01–1.17, p = 0.072) was strongly associated with axillary pCR. Finally, in HR−/HER2− patients, the breast lesion reduction ratio (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.04, p = 0.2) was less predictive.

The ROC curves are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2, and the corresponding nomograms for each molecular subtype are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. All subtypes demonstrated excellent predictive performance.

Discussion

This study aimed to enhance the prediction of residual ALN metastasis after NAC by incorporating detailed imaging metrics and basic clinical variables. The highest predictive performance was achieved using a combination of the MRI-derived reduction ratio, US-measured LN cortical thickness, and pre-NAC variables, including the number of breast lesions, histologic grade, Ki-67 index, and molecular subtype.

We considered changes in both breast lesions and ALNs using US and MRI to capture pre- and post-NAC metrics and calculate the reduction ratios. Radiologic complete response (rCR) was evaluated for breast but not axillary lesions because of the presence of normal LNs alongside metastatic nodes. Instead, cortical thickness measured using US, with a threshold of 0.3 cm15,16, was adopted as the key criterion for assessing axillary response.

Variability between US and MRI measurements was inevitable, particularly in cases where fused LNs appeared as a single entity on US but were distinguishable on MRI. Additionally, when the longest axis of a lesion was oblique, US probes often captured a longer dimension than MRI. Post-NAC LN boundaries were frequently less distinct on US than on MRI, which made it more challenging to define lesion margins17. A systematic analysis led to the exclusion of redundant metrics. For example, the breast lesion reduction ratio was equally predictive as the combination of the reduction ratio and rCR, likely owing to the equivalence of a 100% reduction ratio and rCR. This minimized the potential bias from initially small metastatic lesions, which, despite small post-NAC sizes, could still show a low reduction ratio, ensuring their inclusion in metastasis prediction. In the axilla, cortical thickness emerged as the strongest predictor, outperforming both post-NAC size and reduction ratio.

This study included patients with cN2–3 disease, regardless of whether metastatic LNs were confirmed through preoperative biopsy. Among them, 35 (12.7%) did not undergo LN biopsy before surgery because clinical LN metastasis was unequivocally evident. In addition, 26 patients (10.8%) had negative biopsy results. During the evaluation period, fine-needle aspiration (FNA) was predominantly used (68.8%) rather than core needle biopsy, potentially contributing to false-negative results due to the lower negative predictive value of FNA18. Although histologic confirmation was not available in all patients, a sensitivity analysis restricted to tissue-confirmed cases yielded results consistent with the primary cohort, suggesting that this limitation is unlikely to have materially biased the findings (Supplementary Table 3).

During the study period, 89 patients (32.2%) underwent SLNB. Although ALND is commonly performed in patients with advanced clinical nodal stages, surgical practices vary. Even when SLNB results are negative, a high preoperative N stage or palpable nodes often lead to an additional ALND procedure. Patients classified under SLNB included those who had multiple nodes removed via targeted “picking” procedures but did not undergo formal ALND.

Among the 56 patients who underwent ALND after negative SLNB results, 10 had metastatic LNs. We have summarized the key clinicopathologic variables and predicted probabilities for these 10 patients in Supplementary Table 4. These findings highlight that SLNs may not always represent the true extent of residual metastasis. Future strategies such as targeting the pre-NAC index mass may help reduce false-negative SLNB results19.

In addition, we examined the distribution of predicted pCR probabilities in the ypN0 and ypN+ groups and present this in Fig. 5. If a patient predicted to have a low likelihood of axillary pCR using the model receives a negative SLNB result, additional ALN excision should be considered to improve treatment outcomes. However, we also analyzed patients for whom the predictions were incorrect. Among the 136 patients who achieved pCR, 33 had predicted probabilities <50% and 6 had probabilities <20%. Among the 140 patients without pCR, 34 had predicted probabilities ≥50% and 6 had probabilities ≥80%. We further summarized the clinical features of the 6 ypN0 patients with predicted probabilities <20% and the 6 ypN+ patients with predicted probabilities ≥80% in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6. These discordant cases indicate that even with a high predicted pCR probability, SLNB cannot yet be safely omitted. However, if the accuracy of SLNB could be maximized in such patients, these mismatched cases may still be appropriately identified and managed.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies that identified factors associated with breast pCR, such as tumor size, ER/HER2 status, and Ki-67 index20. Previous work by Kim et al. demonstrated that axillary response could be predicted using changes in tumor size on MRI together with LN characteristics on US, achieving AUCs of 0.84 in the training set and 0.78 in the validation set21.

More recently, Malhaire et al. showed that pre-treatment axillary US and breast MRI could predict axillary residual disease, with luminal subtype, anterior tumor location, and cortical thickening greater than 7 mm as significant predictors, whereas a high Ki-67 index and the presence of intratumoral high signal intensity on T2-weighted images were associated with axillary complete response. Their model achieved robust performance, with AUCs of 0.860 in the training set and 0.843 in the test set22.

Building on these prior studies, our investigation extends the field by focusing specifically on patients with advanced nodal disease (cN2–3) and incorporating analyses stratified by molecular subtype defined by ER, PR, and HER2 status. Furthermore, by constructing a nomogram to predict axillary pCR, our study underscores the potential for further de-escalation of axillary surgery in this higher-risk population.

In addition, recent efforts have explored radiomics as a promising tool, whereby quantitative imaging features are utilized to predict cancer prognosis and treatment response23. For example, Lin et al. evaluated multiple machine learning algorithms based on pre-treatment MRI radiomics features and reported that the combined radiomics–clinical model achieved the best performance (AUC 0.825 in the testing set), outperforming models that relied on clinical (AUC 0.682) or radiomics features alone (AUC 0.743). A total of 21 radiomics features were identified as associated with axillary pCR in their study. Notably, this model was developed exclusively from pre-treatment MRI data24. Although our analysis did not employ a high-dimensional radiomics framework, we incorporated quantitative imaging features—specifically, changes in breast tumor and LN size observed on pre- and post-NAC MRI—which represent strong and clinically meaningful predictors of axillary response.

A notable aspect of this study was the consideration of size reduction ratios for both breast lesions and ALNs using MRI and US. Although the size reduction ratio measured using US was excluded from the final model, combining the size reduction ratio measured using MRI with the LN cortical thickness measured using US enhanced predictive accuracy.

Compared to previous studies on axillary surgery following NAC, this study has several unique features. For instance, the ACOSOG Z1071 trial included patients with cN1–2 disease, but the majority were cN1 cases2, while the GANEA 2 study involved patients with cN0–2 disease, with a relatively low proportion of cN2 cases3. In contrast, our study exclusively focused on patients with cN2–3 disease, targeting a population of patients with advanced breast cancer who are typically treated with ALND9. This exclusive focus on patients with cN2–3 disease highlights the relevance and applicability of this study in the clinical management of advanced-stage disease.

This study demonstrated an excellent AUC value of 0.9 for the entire cohort, indicating excellent predictive accuracy. When ER, PR, and HER2 were entered individually into Model 2, the AUC decreased marginally compared to Model 3, and no improvement was observed when they were added to Model 3. By stratifying patients based on molecular subtype rather than using HR or HER2 status as standalone independent variables, the analysis allowed for subtype-specific independent variables to be assigned different weights within the nomogram, further enhancing the predictive accuracy of the model when applied to real-world patient populations.

This was a retrospective analysis, which may have introduced gaps in the data. To mitigate this, the study period was limited to 5 years, ensuring consistent imaging and surgical protocols. However, a prospective validation study is necessary to confirm these findings.

A potential limitation of this study is related to the acquisition of LN data in patients with multiple enlarged nodes. In such cases, it was not always possible to confirm whether the node selected for US-guided biopsy corresponded to the same index node used for imaging measurements, or whether the pre- and post-NAC index nodes on US represented the same mass. However, because this study focused on clinically defined cN2–3 patients rather than exclusively on biopsy-confirmed nodes, it was not essential to verify whether the biopsied node and the imaging index node were identical. Moreover, on MRI, we were able to track the pre-NAC index mass in the majority of cases, thereby minimizing this concern. Finally, as the US-derived size decrease ratio variable was ultimately excluded from the final analysis, the limitations of the US in this context were partially mitigated.

Our findings suggest that integrating imaging metrics, particularly MRI-derived reduction ratios and US-measured LN cortical thickness, with several key tumor characteristics can refine axillary response predictions. This approach may reduce unnecessary axillary surgery in patients who achieve near-complete response after NAC. Although SLNB remains the standard treatment for early breast cancer, adapting these insights to advanced nodal disease could optimize treatment and improve patient quality of life.

In conclusion, in patients with cN2–3 breast cancer who underwent NAC, axillary pCR could be predicted with high accuracy using molecular subtype-specific MRI-derived reduction ratios, US-measured LN cortical thickness, number of breast lesions, histologic grade, and the Ki-67 index. These findings support a tailored approach to axillary surgery in advanced breast cancer, allowing for individualized surgical planning based on treatment response.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital and included patients with cN2–3 breast cancer who underwent NAC followed by breast and axillary surgeries between January 2016 and December 2020. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (Approval number: B-2410-931-102; Date of approval: October 4, 2024) and was conducted as a retrospective chart review with anonymized data collection. Given the minimal risk to the participants, the need for informed consent was waived by the approving body.

To minimize confounding factors, patients with metastatic breast cancer, recurrent breast cancer, occult breast cancer, or with a prior or concurrent malignancy other than breast cancer were excluded. Patients who did not respond to NAC, defined as those whose lesions increased in size during treatment (negative reduction ratio), were excluded from the study. Such cases were not considered suitable for inclusion because the aim of this study was to develop a nomogram to predict the likelihood of axillary pCR, and patients with progressive disease are highly unlikely to achieve pCR. Accordingly, the developed nomogram is not intended to apply to this subgroup of patients.

Axillary pCR was defined as ypN0, indicating no invasive or noninvasive residual disease in the LN25, with isolated tumor cells being permissible26. A flow chart summarizing the patient selection process has been provided as Supplementary Fig. 4.

Study design and data collection

The primary endpoint was axillary pCR, which was analyzed as the dependent variable. The independent variables included demographic data (age and menopausal status), tumor characteristics (bilaterality, number of lesions, clinical T and N stage, histologic type and grade, ER, PR, HER2 status, and Ki-67 index), and imaging data (pre- and post-NAC breast and axillary lesion size and reduction ratio measured using US and MRI). Biopsy methods, ALN assessment, surgical techniques, pathological findings, and immunohistochemical results are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

All patients underwent breast MRI and US before and after NAC, following the American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria27. Both US and MRI examinations were performed and interpreted by four experienced breast radiologists. Inter-observer variability among radiologists was not formally evaluated in this study. Imaging interpretation for the status of ALN metastasis was based on the enlarged and morphologically abnormal change, cortical thickening, and loss of fatty hilum that can be seen with metastasis28.

On US, ALN metastasis was suspected if the LN had any of the following morphologic characteristics: loss of fatty hilum, cortical thickening more than 3 mm, round or irregular shape, or increased extrahilar vascularity15,16. On MRI, ALN metastasis was suspected if the LN had any of the following morphologic characteristics: irregular nodal contour, nodal size > 5 mm, or absence of fatty hilum16.

For cases with multiple enlarged ALNs, the same index node was measured on MRI before and after NAC whenever possible. On US, however, it was often not feasible to confirm that the identical node was evaluated at both time points; therefore, the index node at each US time point was used for measurement.

In addition to pathologically confirmed breast masses, Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) category 4C or higher lesions suspicious for malignancy were also included for the total lesion extent. On MRI, when non-mass enhancement was observed adjacent to the enhancing index mass, the total disease extent was determined as the sum of both lesions29.

For ALNs, the short-axis diameter of the largest node was measured on MRI in accordance with the RECIST guideline30, whereas on US, the recorded data at that time were consistently documented based on the long-axis diameter of the largest node; therefore, long-axis measurements were used for US to ensure data accuracy. When nodes were indistinguishable because of conglomeration, the overall mass size was used. Cortical thickness on US was also assessed to determine whether it exceeded the normal threshold of 3 mm31,32. Imaging protocols

MR Imaging was performed in a 3.0-T MRI unit (Ingenia; Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with a dedicated sixteen-channel breast coil. The protocol consisted of an axial T2-weighted fat-suppressed fast spin-echo sequence (repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE], 9400/100; field of view (FOV), 70.0 × 37.0 cm; matrix size, 374 × 374; slice thickness/spacing, 1.0 mm/1.0 mm) and an axial T1-weighted fat-suppressed fast spoiled gradient-echo sequence (TR/TE, 4.68/2.06; flip angle, 12°; FOV, 34.0 × 34.0 cm; matrix size, 340 × 340; slice thickness/spacing, 1.0 mm/1.0 mm), with one precontrast and five postcontrast dynamic series at 90, 180, 270, 360, and 450 s after administration of gadobutrol (Gadovist; Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany) using an automated injector (Spectris Solaris; Medrad Europe, Maastricht, Netherlands) at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg and at a rate of 2 mL/s, followed by a 20 mL saline flush. Postprocessing, including subtraction and maximum intensity projection, was performed.

To further assess nodal status, additional bilateral axial axillary sequences were obtained. A T1-weighted, contrast-enhanced (after a dynamic breast sequence), fat-saturated, spoiled, 3D volumetric-interpolated gradient-echo sequence was performed. Technical parameters were as follows: TR/TE, 5.75/0.00; flip angle, 12°; matrix size, 372 × 370; slice thickness/spacing, 2.0 mm/2.0 mm). The FOV was optimized to cover the bilateral axillae (levels I–III), supraclavicular fossa, and inferior neck (levels IV–VII).

US examinations before and after NAC were carried out using a linear transducer at a bandwidth of 7–15 MHz (iU22 Ultrasound System, Philips, Bothell, WA, USA; SuperSonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/). Normality of continuous variables was formally tested, and as none satisfied the assumption of normality, continuous variables such as tumor size and reduction ratios were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the ORs with 95% CIs.

The final multivariable logistic regression model was used to construct ROC curves and develop a nomogram. Discrimination was assessed using the AUC with 95% CIs, and AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 repetitions, and calibration was examined with bootstrap-corrected calibration curves. The clinical utility of the nomogram was evaluated using decision curve analysis (net benefit analysis). Subgroup analyses were performed based on molecular subtypes, with separate multivariable logistic regression models created to assess predictive accuracy across subgroups.
