Comparative analysis of drug susceptibility among different Mycobacterium abscessus morphotypes
Authors: Kai Cheng, Jiaying Guo, Yuanyuan Shang, Weicong Ren, Weijie Zhao, Shanshan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycobacterium abscessus (MAB), a rapidly growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium, presents severe therapeutic challenges due to its intrinsic and acquired multidrug resistance. Notably, MAB exhibits phenotypic heterogeneity, with smooth (S) and rough (R) morphotypes displaying distinct drug susceptibility phenotypes.ResultsWe systematically examined the phenotypic diversity and drug susceptibility profiles of 100 clinical MAB isolates, including 79 R-type, 13 S-type, and 8 mixed (M) morphotypes. The three morphotypes (R, S, M) exhibited distinct drug resistance profiles. Both R-type and S-type strains showed broad-spectrum resistance to fluoroquinolones, tetracycline derivatives, and carbapenems. In contrast, S-type strains remained fully susceptible to clofazimine (CFZ), linezolid (LNZ), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMS). Notably, significant differences in resistance to capreomycin (CAP) and clofazimine (CFZ) were observed among the three morphotypes. Genotypic analysis revealed a homogeneous distribution of key resistance genes (erm(41), rrl, rrs) across morphotypes.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the relationship between MAB morphotypes and antibiotic resistance is complex and drug specific. It is necessary to integrate morphotype with genotypic profiling and phenotypic characterization to better understand the complex resistance landscape of MAB and to guide more effective therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Mycobacterium abscessus (MAB), a fast-growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM), has emerged as an important pathogen in lung, skin and soft tissue infections in recent years, and poses a serious threat especially to immunodeficient patients and cystic fibrosis populations worldwide [1]. The intrinsic and acquired drug resistance mechanisms of the organism allow it to exhibit significant resistance to most first-line antibiotics such as β-lactams, macrolides and aminoglycosides, resulting in a high rate of clinical treatment failure, prolonged course of therapy and increased risk of relapse [2]. Although amikacin (AMK) and clarithromycin (CLA) remain the core therapeutic agents, their efficacy varies depending on mycobacterial subspecies (particularly M. abscessus subsp. abscessus versus M. abscessus subsp. massiliense) and the presence of resistance-associated genetic determinants such as erm(41) and rrs [3, 4]. More grimly, recent studies have revealed that MAB can dynamically regulate resistance phenotypes under antibiotic pressure through induced resistance mechanisms (e.g., 16 S rRNA methylation) [5], further exacerbating therapeutic difficulties.

MAB exhibits two distinct macroscopic morphologies, referred to as smooth (S) type and rough (R) type, based on the appearance of cultured colonies [6–8]. Smooth-type colonies are moist and smooth, whereas rough-type colonies display dry, rough, and waxy morphology with increased volume and a rugged surface [9]. The S-type expresses high levels of glycopeptidyl lipids (GPLs) in the cell wall, facilitating biofilms formation in vitro [10]. In contrast, the R-type shows reduced GPLs content and a striped structure, a feature linked to enhanced bacterial virulence [10]. Notably, the two morphotypes can undergo dynamic interconversion via environmentally induced, non-mutagenic mechanisms, with the S-type transitioning to the R-type at a low frequency by epigenetic regulation or environmental stresses [11]. Due to its superior biofilm forming capacity, increased antibiotic resistance, and immune evasion properties, the R-type often dominates in chronic infections, contributing to disease progression [11].

Currently, the rising global prevalence of MAB is closely related to regional antibiotic misuse, host immune status and diagnostic limitations [12]. However, systematic studies on resistance heterogeneity among MAB clinical strains with different morphotypes remain scarce. In this study, we assess the in vitro susceptibility of relevant antibiotics for clinical practice across MAB morphological variants to elucidate the correlation between phenotypic variation and drug resistance. Our findings will provide an important basis for the optimization of phenotype-guided treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Bacterial collection and culture

A total of 100 clinically isolated MAB strains and the reference strain ATCC 19,977 from Beijing Chest Hospital were included in this study. The initial assessment was performed visually by two trained technicians. For ambiguous strains, final determination was made through careful microscopic examination. Morphological phenotypes of the strains were classified according to the characteristics of the colonies on solid medium (7H10 agar): smooth (S-type) colonies were round or oval, with moist and smooth surfaces, regular edges, uniform colours (milky white to beige), and soft textures; rough (R-type) colonies were increased in size, with dry and rough surfaces, irregular edges, granular or wrinkled, deepened colours (yellow to brownish-yellow), and hard and decreased growth rates; mixed type (M-type) has both colonies of S-type and R-type. To assess morphotypic stability, representative colonies were serially passaged on fresh 7H10 agar at 30 °C. All the strains were recovered and cultured on Löwenstein-Jensen (L-J) medium for 3ཞ5 days at 30℃ before in vitro drug susceptibility test.

In vitro drug susceptibility test

The drug susceptibility profiles of all strains were determined by the broth microdilution method in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. The antimicrobial panel included both CLSI-recommended agents—clarithromycin (CLA; 0.015–64 µg/mL), amikacin (AMK; 0.06–128 µg/mL), moxifloxacin (MXF; 0.06–8 µg/mL), cefoxitin (CFX; 2–512 µg/mL), imipenem (IMP; 0.25–256 µg/mL), meropenem (MEM; 0.25–512 µg/mL), linezolid (LNZ; 0.25–64 µg/mL), tobramycin (TOB; 0.25–128 µg/mL), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMS; 1–512 µg/mL)—as well as several investigational drugs not routinely recommended for testing against rapidly growing mycobacteria. The latter group, included to provide a broader susceptibility profile, comprised azithromycin (AZM; 0.06–128 µg/mL), capreomycin (CAP; 0.25–64 µg/mL), levofloxacin (LVX; 0.125–32 µg/mL), gatifloxacin (GAF; 0.5–128 µg/mL), tigecycline (TGC; 0.125–128 µg/mL), clofazimine (CFZ; 0.06–512 µg/mL), and minocycline (MIN; 0.25–512 µg/mL); their selection and concentration ranges were based on previously published studies [13, 14] against M. abscessus complex to ensure the detection of all potential MIC values. The drugs were dissolved using the dissolution methods recommended by the CLSI. For drugs not mentioned in the CLSI guidelines, such as the poorly soluble drug AZM, the dissolution method was carried out according to the procedure described in the article by Leo Lin et al. [15]. Briefly, the bacterial suspension was diluted 1:20 in cation-adjusted Muller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) and incubated over a range of drug concentrations. The plates were incubated at 30℃ for 72 h. A freshly prepared diluted Alamar Blue reagent was added to indicate mycobacterial growth. The plate was incubated for another 24 h to read color changes. Pink color indicates mycobacterial growth, and blue color means no detected growth. Minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration of drug that inhibited the growth of MAB. A blank control and a quality control strain (ATCC 19977) were set up in all experiments to ensure the reliability of the method.

DNA extraction and resistance gene sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from freshly cultured colonies using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. Briefly, bacterial cells were lysed with lysozyme and proteinase K, followed by treatment with CTAB and sodium chloride to remove polysaccharides and proteins. DNA was purified by chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction, precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and finally resuspended in nuclease-free water. The resistance-determinant regions of the erm(41), rrl, and rrs genes were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers.The PCR products were purified and bidirectionally sequenced by the Sanger method. We sequenced the erm(41) (primers: F- 5′-GACCGGGGCCTTCTTCGTGAT − 3′ / R-5′-GACTTCCCCGCACCGATTCC-3′), rrs (primers: F-5′-ATGACGTCAAGTCATCATGCC − 3′/ R-5′-AGGTGATCCAGCCGCACCTTC − 3′), and rrl (primers: F-5′-CTTGGGTGAAGCGGAGAAT − 3′/ R-5′-CAACCGCCCCAGTTAAACTAC − 3′) genes for all isolates. The analysis focused on established resistance mutations: the T28 substitution in erm(41), and the A2058G/A2059G (rrl) and A1401G (rrs) mutations. Sequence assembly and alignment against Mycobacterium abscessus ATCC 19,977 reference sequences were performed using SnapGene software. The erm(41) sequences were analyzed to determine sequevar types, focusing on the T28 codon. The rrl and rrs genes were screened for mutations known to confer constitutive macrolide resistance and high-level aminoglycoside resistance, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance of differences in drug resistance rates among phenotypic groups (R-, S-, and M-types) was determined using Chi-square test (with Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies < 5). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with SPSS 2.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Results

In vitro drug susceptibility of MAB isolates

100 clinical MAB isolates were enrolled in our study and the drug susceptibility test showed distinct MIC distributions and resistance profiles (Table 1). CLA and CFZ demonstrated the lowest MIC50 values, with 55% and 75% of isolates exhibiting MICs ≤ 0.06 mg/L and 0.25 mg/L, respectively. The MIC of AZM showed a bimodal distribution, clustering isolates at lower (0.5-4 mg/L; 49%) and higher (≥ 64 mg/L; 14%) concentrations. Among aminoglycosides, AMK inhibited most isolates (81%) growth at 1–4 mg/L, while CAP and TOB exhibited higher MICs (MIC90 ≥ 16 mg/L). Fluoroquinolones exhibited variable activity: MXF demonstrated the highest potency with the MIC of 83% isolates at 1–4 mg/L), followed by LVX, while GAF showed a bimodal distribution. β-Lactams displayed widespread resistance, with CFX, IMP and MEM exhibiting MICs ≥ 8 mg/L in ≥ 85% of isolates. LNZ and TGC showed moderate activity, whereas TMS and MIN displayed elevated MICs. Overall, macrolides and clofazimine remained the most active agents, while β-lactams and sulfamethoxazole demonstrated universal resistance. Bimodal MIC distributions for AZM, GAF, and TOB suggest the emergence of resistant subpopulations.

Comparative analysis of drug susceptibility among different MAB morphotypes

Among 100 clinical M. abscessus isolates, three distinct morphotypes were identified: R-type (79%, 79/100), S-type (13%, 13/100), and M-type (8%, 8/100) (Table 2). The conserved morphology between parental and progeny colonies over multiple generations verified the stability of the identified characteristics. Comparative analysis revealed notable differences in their drug resistance profiles. R-type isolates exhibited broad-spectrum resistance, particularly to fluoroquinolones (LVX 78.5%; GAF 48.1%; MXF 25.3%), tetracycline derivatives (MIN 67.1%; TGC 51.9%), and carbapenems (MEM 82.3%; IMP 40.5%). In contrast, S-type isolates were fully susceptible to CLA, CFZ, LNZ, and TMS but showed significantly higher resistance to CAP (53.9%) than both R-type (13.9%) and M-type (12.5%) isolates (P < 0.01). Furthermore, CFZ resistance was exclusively observed in R-type isolates (17.7%, P < 0.05). For the remaining 14 antimicrobial agents, resistance rates did not differ significantly among morphotypes (P > 0.05), although S-type isolates demonstrated a trend toward higher resistance to GAF (69.2%), MIN (84.6%), and TGC (76.9%).

Heterogeneous drug resistance in M-type subpopulations

To further elucidate the association between MAB morphology and drug resistance, we isolated R-type and S-type subpopulations from 8 M-type isolates. The analysis of eight M-type strains revealed pronounced resistance divergence between their R- and S-subpopulations (Fig. 1). R-type subpopulations exhibited consistently high resistance rates, with 87.5% for MEM and CFX, 62.5% for CAP, CFZ, AMK, and IMP, 50% for TMS, CLA, AZM, TGC, LNZ, and TOB). Resistance was also observed for LVX and MXF (37.5%), and GAF (12.5%). In contrast, S-type subpopulations showed greater susceptibility (≥ 37.5%) for all agents. Notably, S-type exhibit complete susceptibility (100%) to LVX, MXF, and GAF, high susceptibility (87.5%) to CLA, AZM, AMK, LNZ, CFZ, IMP, TGC, and TOB, moderate susceptibility (62.5%) to MIN, and lower susceptibility (50%) to CFX, CAP, and TMS. The results confirmed there were cross-resistance patterns within macrolides (CLA/AZM) and fluoroquinolones (LVX/MXF). Interestingly, MIN showed complete susceptibility in R-type but 37.5% resistance in S-type subpopulations.

Resistance genotype distribution across M. abscessus morphotypes

Genotypic analysis of the 100 clinical M. abscessus isolates revealed a homogeneous distribution of major antibiotic resistance determinants across the three morphotypes (Table 3). The functional erm(41) T28 sequevar, associated with inducible macrolide resistance, was highly prevalent in all morphologic groups: R-type (82.3%, 65/79), S-type (84.6%, 11/13), and M-type (75.0%, 6/8), with an overall prevalence of 82.0% (82/100). In contrast, mutations conferring constitutive macrolide resistance in the rrl gene were rare in any specific morphotype, occurring in 5.1% (4/79) of R-type and 7.7% (1/13) of S-type isolates, with no mutations detected in M-type strains. Similarly, aminoglycoside resistance-associated rrs mutations were identified in 7.6% (6/79) of R-type and 7.7% (1/13) of S-type isolates, with none found in the M-type group. These findings indicate that the genetic determinants underlying resistance to macrolides and amikacin are not associated with specific colony morphologies.

Discussion

Phenotypic susceptibility testing suggested some association between colonial morphology and antibiotic resistance. However, Sanger sequencing of the key resistance determinants erm(41), rrl, and rrs did not reveal a significant association with morphotypes. The resistance-associated genotypes, including the erm(41) T28 allele (82.3% R-type, 84.6% S-type, 75.0% M-type), as well as mutations in rrl and rrs, were detected across all groups without a clear distribution pattern.These results indicate that while phenotypic data hinted at a possible link between morphology and resistance, genotypic evidence(erm(41), rrl, and rrs) demonstrates that gene mutations are independent of morphological variation.

Beyond genetically determined resistances, morphotype remains indicative for other agents, likely reflecting biological changes during morphological transitions. R-type strains, characterized by reduced glycopeptidolipid expression and enhanced biofilm formation, exhibited broad-spectrum resistance potentially due to impaired drug penetration [16–18]. Conversely, S-type strains demonstrated complete susceptibility to CFZ, LNZ, and TMS but showed notably higher resistance to CAP and elevated resistance rates to LVX and TGC, potentially attributable to gyrA/B mutations or efflux pump activation rather than direct morphological effects [19, 20].Notably, CFZ maintained potent activity against most strains across all morphotypes, likely due to its unique mechanism of disrupting membrane integrity that bypasses traditional resistance pathways [21]. According to our results and previous studies [22–26], MAB strains enhance multidrug resistance through an integrated strategy involving genetic background variations (e.g., erm(41) mutations), cell wall remodeling (e.g., GPL deletion), enhanced biofilm formation, and dynamic transitions between morphotypes, all of which contribute to therapeutic complexity.

Our results demonstrated that morphological heterogeneity in MAB (R/S/M phenotypes) significantly influences distinct drug resistance profiles. Meanwhile, the therapeutic regimens should be promptly adjusted in response to dynamic drug resistance patterns. For M-type strains, we should vigilantly monitor their morphologic phenotype due to the high capacity for rapid resistance development through morphological switching. Based on our findings, we recommend phenotype- and subspecies-guided therapeutic strategies: first, implement routine morphological phenotyping and conduct comprehensive drug susceptibility tests; second, adopt chemotherapy strategies based on the results of drug susceptibility tests; third, monitor for morphological shifts and drug susceptibility change during treatment and modify regimens based on phenotypic evolution.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study: (1) The limited sample size of M-type isolates (n = 8) may affect the statistical reliability of subgroups analyses; (2) In vitro susceptibility testing cannot fully simulate clinically relavant resistance mechanisms or host-pathogen interactions in vivo. Subsequent investigations should incorporate (i) multi-omics analyses to dissect drug-resistance related molecule mechanisms, particularly efflux pump regulation during morphological transitions; and (ii) validation in appropriate animal models to develop optimized therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the relationship between MAB morphotype and antibiotic resistance is complex and drug-specific. While the genetic background is the principal determinant of resistance to core drugs like macrolides and AMK, colony morphology provides valuable, actionable information on susceptibility to several other agents. Therefore, an effective precision medicine approach requires the integration of conventional morphology, systematic drug susceptibility profiling, and molecular genetic testing to navigate the complex resistance landscape of MAB and optimize patient outcomes.
