A transformer-based deep learning survival prediction model and an explainable XGBoost anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcome prediction model based on the cGAS-STING-centered pathways in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ren Wang, Qiumei Liu, Wenhua You, Huiyu Wang, Yun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies suggest cGAS-STING pathway may play a crucial role in the genesis and development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), closely associated with classical pathways and tumor immunity. We aimed to develop models predicting survival and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcomes centered on the cGAS-STING pathway in HCC. We identified classical pathways highly correlated with cGAS-STING pathway and constructed transformer survival model preserving raw structure of pathways. We also developed explainable XGBoost model for predicting anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcomes using SHAP algorithm. We trained and validated transformer survival model on pan-cancer cohort and tested it on three independent HCC cohorts. Using 0.5 as threshold across cohorts, we divided each HCC cohort into two groups and calculated P values with log-rank test. TCGA-LIHC: C-index = 0.750, P = 1.52e−11; ICGC-LIRI-JP: C-index = 0.741, P = .00138; GSE144269: C-index = 0.647, P = .0233. We trained and validated [area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) = 0.777] XGBoost model on immunotherapy datasets and tested it on GSE78220 (AUC = 0.789); we also tested XGBoost model on HCC anti-PD-L1 cohort (AUC = 0.719). Our deep learning model and XGBoost model demonstrate potential in predicting survival risks and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcomes in HCC. We deployed these two prediction models to the GitHub repository and provided detailed instructions for their usage: deep learning survival model, https://github.com/mlwalker123/CSP_survival_model; XGBoost immunotherapy model, https://github.com/mlwalker123/CSP_immunotherapy_model.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, cGAS-STING pathway, deep learning, explainable model

Introduction

Liver cancer is a clinically prevalent malignancy, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) being the most common type. HCC ranks as the third most frequent cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1, 2]. In 2020, ~906 000 cases of liver cancer emerged globally; the distribution of HCC cases had obvious regional specificity [1]. Regions such as East Asia, Africa, North America, and Australia had a higher incidence of HCC. The high-risk diagnostic age of HCC was between 60 and 70 years. The number of annual HCC-related deaths reached 830 000 cases; the prognosis of HCC was usually poor, and the 5-year survival rate is only ~18%. The etiology of HCC encompasses infections with hepatitis B virus or hepatitis C virus, long-term alcohol abuse, and other factors potentially leading to chronic liver injury [3–5]. HCC imposes a substantial burden on patients, their families, and society. Clinically, the main strategy for early HCC is surgical resection; for intermediate-stage HCC, transarterial chemoembolization is usually employed; while for advanced HCC, the strategies include conservative treatment and targeted therapy, such as sorafenib [6–8]. With the development of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, initial clinical trials of ICI treatments for HCC have been initiated; the combination of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment with conventional drugs has been regarded as the potential treatment strategy for HCC [9–11].

The cGAS-STING pathway plays a pivotal role in the recognition of abnormally aggregated DNA within the cytoplasm. Upon binding of cGAS to abnormal DNA, a second messenger known as cyclic dinucleotide is produced; this cyclic dinucleotide is capable of activating the downstream STING protein, which in turn triggers the production of type I interferons and other inflammatory cytokines, thereby initiating an immune system response [12, 13]. The cGAS-STING pathway is directly or indirectly associated with numerous classical pathways, suggesting that it may play a central role among many pathways [14–17]. The cGAS-STING pathway is of significant importance in various inflammatory diseases and tumor immunotherapy, having emerged as a critical target for the treatment of related diseases [18–20]. Existing studies have demonstrated the important biological functions of the cGAS-STING pathway in various tumors, such as breast cancer, non–small cell lung cancer, and colon cancer [21–23]. Several studies suggest that the cGAS-STING pathway plays a pivotal role in the immune microenvironment and immunotherapy of various tumors [24–27]. Recent researches have also reported a significant correlation between the cGAS-STING pathway and the genesis/development of HCC, as well as the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy [28–30]. Pu’s work discovered that some genes related to the cGAS-STING pathway had a strong correlation with HCC. The author found that NLRC3, STING1, TBK1, TRIM21, and XRCC6 might be independent prognostic factors in HCC [31]. Qi’s work found that genes related to the cGAS-STING pathway were associated with the prognosis of HCC and immune cell infiltration, and the immune microenvironment was usually considered a key factor affecting the prognosis of immunotherapy [32]. Lv’s work confirmed that the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway mediated by TET2 could recruit lymphocytes in the tumor microenvironment to enhance antitumor immunity [33]. Compared with other classic pathways, the cGAS-STING pathway might be a hub communicating numerous pathways, and current relevant literature suggested that the cGAS-STING pathway might have a strong correlation with the prognosis of HCC and antitumor immunity in HCC. Therefore, we centered on the cGAS-STING pathway to predict the prognosis and immunotherapy of HCC. These evidences suggest that it might be feasible to utilize the cGAS-STING-pathway-related features for predicting the survival prognosis and predicting outcomes of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy in HCC.

In clinical settings, to prevent the recurrence of cancer in patients with HCC, long-term follow-up is often required. However, it is challenging to precisely identify the population that requires long-term follow-up and treatment. Consequently, the prediction of postoperative prognosis in HCC patients is imperative. Moreover, a simple binary predictive method, which categorizes prognosis as either favorable or poor, is typically an efficacious approach. Clinically, various biomarkers or machine learning models (such as Cox regression) are frequently employed for the prediction of HCC prognosis [34–37]. However, continuous indicators might require large-scale cohorts for threshold evaluation. For more convenient risk assessment by clinicians, a robust evaluation threshold is also indispensable.

In recent years, the transformer model has been widely applied in many fields. Initially employed for natural language processing, transformer model has been progressively extended to the domain of computer vision; the unique attention mechanism of the transformer model enables it to focus on word vectors across different dimensions [38, 39]. Considering that the cGAS-STING pathway is closely associated with numerous other pathways, this kind of relationships share certain similarities with the relationships between word vectors. The transformer model has the following advantages compared to convolutional neural networks (CNNs): The transformer can handle long sequence data better because it globally processes all positions of the entire input sequence through the self-attention mechanism, unlike CNNs which are limited by the local receptive field and usually focus more on the contours and local features of the image matrix. This makes the transformer perform better when dealing with long sequence data such as long texts and time series. For our research, we preferred that the deep learning model could pay attention to multiple pathways rather than just certain genes of a certain key pathway. The transformer model has the following significant advantages compared to recurrent neural networks (RNNs): The transformer model has relatively good parallel processing capabilities; the transformer model could process all positions of the input sequence in parallel, while RNNs need to be processed one by one in sequence. This mechanism enables the transformer to greatly improve computational efficiency during training and inference, especially for long sequence data. In our research, we hope that our model could make the best use of the pathways centered on cGAS-STING as much as possible. These pathways essentially have no sequential order. If RNNs are used, perhaps the originally non-fixed sequential pathways are artificially added with an order. CNNs and RNNs still have wide applications in bioinformatics. CNNs have good predictive effects in medical image processing in medical imaging, such as pathological sections, CT, and MRI; RNNs have excellent predictive effects in time-dependent predictions, such as the interpretation of electrocardiograms and brain waves; but in our research, the transformer model might be more suitable. We attempted to combine the transformer model with biological pathways with applying it to the survival analysis of HCC.

The cGAS-STING pathway plays a significant role in the immune microenvironment of tumors. We have also attempted to construct a model capable of predicting the outcomes of ICI therapy based on the cGAS-STING pathway. However, datasets with immunotherapy outcome labels are much smaller in sample size compared to those with survival information. If we still used the transformer model for predicting immunotherapy outcomes, this model might be prone to overfitting and exhibit poor generalizability; consequently, we adopted the XGBoost model, which has a simpler structure and stronger interpretability, for the prediction of immunotherapy outcomes in HCC.

Evaluation of the diagnostic value of the cGAS-STING pathway and screening of pathways based on correlation analysis

We acquired critical genes of the cGAS-STING pathway from previously reported literatures and utilized these genes as a gene set to quantitatively assess the cGAS-STING pathway [40]. In R language (version 4.2.2), we evaluated the ssGSEA scores of normal liver tissue samples and HCC samples in the TCGA-LIHC cohort using the GSVA package (version 1.46.0) [41]. We conducted ssGSEA analysis based on the cGAS-STING pathway for HCC and normal liver tissues and compared the ssGSEA scores of the two using Mann–Whitney U test. We downloaded the hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/collections.jsp#H). We conducted Spearman correlation analysis between the ssGSEA scores of the cGAS-STING pathway and the ssGSEA scores of the 50 hallmark pathways in HCC samples.

Immune microenvironment analysis based on the cGAS-STING pathway

The proportions of immune cells in all HCC samples from TCGA-LIHC were calculated using the CIBERSORT algorithm [42]. CIBERSORT analysis was performed in R using the CIBERSORT package (version 0.1.0). Immune scores for all HCC samples were calculated using the estimate algorithm [43]. We calculated the immune score of each HCC sample based on the estimate algorithm. We divided the HCC samples into a higher-score group and a lower-score group based on the median ssGSEA score of the cGAS-STING pathway, and we compared the immune scores of the two using Mann–Whitney U test. We performed logarithmic transformation (log2(x + 1)) on the expression levels (TPM) of immune exhaustion markers (PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2, and LAG3) and compared the expression levels of immune exhaustion markers between the higher and lower ssGSEA score groups of the cGAS-STING pathway using Mann–Whitney U test.

The construction of deep learning survival model

The transformer model, initially a core model for natural language processing, is distinctly characterized by its unique self-attention mechanism compared to other deep learning models. Given that word vectors and biological pathways share certain underlying attributes, such as internal correlations and extern parallelisms, we introduced the transformer model into the construction of model based on biological pathways. Overall, our model comprises three modules: the data preprocessing module, the transformer module, and the prediction layer module.

The underlying mathematical principle of this concept is the maximum likelihood method (existence implies rationality). The deployment of deep learning model

We conducted the deployment, training, validation, and testing of deep learning models using the PyTorch module (version 2.0.0) in a Python environment (version 3.8.16); the model’s deployment, training, validation, and testing were performed on central processing unit (CPU). In the transformer model, we conducted a hyperparameter grid search based on the dropout rate and the learning rate, and used the C-index of the validation set as the screening criterion; we found that when the dropout rate and the learning rate were set to 0.2 and 0.0001, respectively, the validation set obtained the maximum C-index; we found in the four-fold cross-validation that when the dropout rate and the learning rate were set to 0.2 and 0.0001, respectively, the validation set also obtained the maximum average C-index (Supplementary Fig. S3a, b). The dimensions (number of columns) of the transformer model are 200, with the number of heads being 4, and the dropout probability for all layers is 0.2. During training, the deep learning model utilized a batch size of 2000, for a total of 150 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001. We interpreted the transformer survival model based on the Captum module. We evaluated the feature importance based on the training set and conducted single-sample feature interpretation in three HCC cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S7). We recommend training and running the model in the Linux environment. For clinicians, Linux server resources might be scarce. We suggest that clinicians who need to use this transformer survival prediction model lease an online Linux server. Our model is trained and run on the CPU and does not require the use of a graphics processing unit. When using the model, we recommend using Conda for controlling the Python version and the versions of other required modules.

The evaluation of deep learning survival model

In the Python environment, the lifelines module (version 0.27.4) was utilized for the assessment of deep learning survival model. The log-rank test was performed using the statistics.logrank_test function from the lifelines package. Additionally, the concordance index was calculated with the utils.concordance_index function of the lifelines module. For the calculation of time-dependent AUC, the timeROC package (version 0.3) was employed in the R language. Furthermore, the survival package (version 3.4.0) was used in R for conducting Cox regression analysis.

Deployment, training, validation, and testing of the XGBoost model

We utilized the xgboost package (version 1.6.0.1) in R for the deployment, training, validation, and testing of the XGBoost model. For model interpretation, the shapviz package (version 0.9.3) was employed in R. In the XGBoost model, we conducted hyperparameter selection based on depth and used the AUC of the validation set as the screening criterion; we found that when the depth was set to 6, the validation set obtained the maximum AUC; we found in the four-fold cross-validation that when the depth was set to 6, the validation set also obtained the maximum average AUC (Supplementary Fig. S3c, d). The parameters for the xgboost function were set as follows: “max_depth” to 6, “eta” to 1, “gamma” to 0.5, “nrounds” to 20, and “objective” to “binary:logistic”. SHAP values were employed to quantify the contribution of each variable. The TIDE algorithm, developed by Professor Liu’s team, is a tool for predicting immunotherapy outcomes [45]. We implemented the TIDE algorithm using the tidepy module (version 1.3.8) in Python to compute relevant information for HCC samples from the TCGA-LIHC dataset. We used Mann–Whitney U test to compare the outputs of the XGBoost model for immune therapy responders and non-responders. We used Mann–Whitney U test to compare the TIDE scores of the higher and lower XGBoost model output groups. We performed logarithmic transformation with base 10 for TMB and compared the log-transformed TMB of the higher and lower XGBoost model outputs using Mann–Whitney U test.

Statistics

We utilized ROC curve to assess the diagnostic capability of continuous variable. The method used for testing comparisons between two independent datasets in this study was the Mann–Whitney U test. All correlation analyses mentioned in this study were conducted using Spearman correlation analysis. We processed the output of the deep learning model as a binary variable with a threshold of 0.5 (output values <0.5 were marked as 0, and output values >0.5 were marked as 1). When fitting the Cox proportional hazard models, we conducted the proportional hazards assumption test for these models; in TCGA-LIHC, ICGC-LIRI-JP, GSE144269, and combined data, these models all conformed to the proportional hazard assumption (P > .05). After organizing the survival time and outcome events, we used the coxph function in R language to construct Cox proportional hazard models in the four cohorts and calculate HRs and P values. We performed visualization operations of the four Cox proportional hazard models based on the forestploter package. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were applied to assess the relationship between the grouping of XGBoost model predictions and the outcomes of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 responses. The P values <.05 were considered significant.

The diagnostic value of the cGAS-STING pathway and screening of related classical pathways

Recent studies indicate that the cGAS-STING pathway plays a significant role in the genesis, development, and immunotherapy of various cancers. We defined the gene set of the cGAS-STING pathway based on existing research, which included a total of nine genes (CGAS, STING1, TBK1, IRF3, IFNA1, IFNB1, NFKB1, IL6, and TNF) [40]. We calculated the single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) scores of the cGAS-STING pathway in normal liver tissue samples and HCC samples from the TCGA-LIHC cohort and evaluated the ability of these scores to distinguish HCC from normal liver tissue using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [area under the ROC curve (AUC) = 0.756] (Fig. 2a). We compared the ssGSEA scores of the cGAS-STING pathway between normal liver tissue samples and HCC samples (P value = 4.3e−09) (Fig. 2b). Studies reported that the cGAS-STING pathway might directly or indirectly affect other classical pathways in various cancers, suggesting that it might play a central role in the biological processes of cancer cells. Therefore, we aimed to identify other biological pathways closely associated with the cGAS-STING pathway. We downloaded the hallmark pathway gene sets from the MSigDB database and conducted a correlation analysis between the ssGSEA scores of these pathways and the ssGSEA scores of the cGAS-STING pathway, selecting seven pathways highly correlated with the cGAS-STING pathway (with an absolute correlation coefficient >0.75 and P value <.05) (Fig. 2c, d). Considering that the genes included in the cGAS-STING pathway and the HALLMARK_IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING pathway were fewer compared to the other six pathways, we merged these two pathways (Supplementary Table S2). These seven pathways (one merged pathway and six original hallmark pathways) served as the initial input for a deep learning survival model. These findings suggest that the cGAS pathway has the potential for diagnosing HCC and may play a significant role in numerous biological pathways of HCC.

Immune microenvironment analysis based on the cGAS-STING pathway

To evaluate the relationship between the immune microenvironment and the cGAS-STING pathway in HCC, we employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to calculate the proportions of 22 types of immune cells in all HCC samples from the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. 3a). Using the median ssGSEA score of the cGAS-STING pathway as a threshold, we divided all HCC samples into two groups. We then compared the immune cell proportions between these two groups, identifying significant differences in 10 types of immune cells (Fig. 3b). We conducted pairwise correlation analyses of the proportions of these immune cells and also analyzed the correlation between all immune cell proportions and the ssGSEA scores of the cGAS-STING pathway, discovering significant correlations in 16 immune cell types with the cGAS-STING pathway ssGSEA scores (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, we calculated the immune scores of HCC samples using the estimate algorithm and compared the immune scores (P value <.0001) (Fig. 3d). We also compared the expression of four immune exhaustion markers between the two groups (PDCD1: P value = 1.6e−10, CTLA4: P value = 1.5e−10, HAVCR2: P value <.0001, LAG3: P value = .00052) (Fig. 3e). Construction of the deep learning survival model

We performed flexible modifications on the original transformer structure, replacing the word vectors in the transformer model, originally used for natural language processing, with gene vectors (pathways), and constructed the model’s loss function based on the optimization concept of Cox regression (Fig. 4). We maintained the continuity and integrity of genes within a pathway, ensuring that genes belonging to the same pathway appear only in one gene vector.

The structure of the deep learning survival model. We employed pathways, centered on cGAS-STING pathway, as feature inputs and padded the pathways containing <200 genes with the null genes (with the value of 0). In the first step, we preprocessed the input genes, which included logarithmic transformation, sample expression correction, and row-wise layer normalization. In the second step, we performed self-attention operation on the preprocessed matrix to obtain the query matrix, key matrix, and value matrix; the three matrices were transformed into a new 7 × 200 matrix through matrix multiplication, normalization, and softmax operation. In the third step, we conducted multi-head connection on the matrices after self-attention operation, followed by random dropout with a probability of .2 and layer normalization (this process is similar to the decision integration in the random forest model). In the fourth step, the matrices after multi-head connection sequentially passed through a fully connected layer, ReLU layer, random dropout layer, another fully connected layer, and another random dropout layer, followed by layer normalization at the end. Steps 2 to 4 was a complete transformer model, and we connected two transformer models sequentially to increase the complexity of the model. In the fifth step, we performed average pooling on the obtained 7 × 200 matrix to obtain a one-dimensional vector of length 7; we then passed this vector through a fully connected layer, ReLU layer, and another fully connected layer to change its length to 1. In the sixth step, to ensure that the predicted HCC risk probability output was between 0 and 1, we sequentially applied ReLU transformation, 4Tanh transformation, and sigmoid transformation to the final output value.

The training and testing of the HCC deep learning survival model

We downloaded and organized the GDC Pan-Cancer expression data (TPM) and survival data (OS and OS time). We conducted 150 epochs of training in the GDC Pan-Cancer training set, and the model loss gradually decreased with the increase in the number of training epochs (Fig. 5a). In the GDC Pan-Cancer training set and validation set, there were significant differences in survival prognosis between the groups with risk probability ≤0.5 and the groups with risk probability >0.5 (Fig. 5b, c). Training, validation, and testing of the explainable XGBoost model

Compared to traditional machine learning models, deep learning models require a very large amount of data. However, the current datasets with immunotherapy outcome labels are significantly smaller than the datasets with survival information labels. If we constructed model using deep learning, the limited immunotherapy datasets might lead to overfitting and low generalizability of the immunotherapy prediction model. Therefore, we chose the explainable XGBoost model with simple structure as the immunotherapy prediction model. Studies have suggested that the cGAS-STING pathway may be associated with immunotherapy; thus, we included all genes of the cGAS-STING-centered pathways as the candidate gene set. We combined IMvigor210CoreBiologies and GSE91061 and divided them into training (75%) and test sets (25%) according to the ratio of positive (response) and negative (non-response) cases. We performed feature selection using AUC in IMvigor210CoreBiologies (max AUC = 0.6696) and GSE91061 (max AUC = 0.8824) from the aforementioned candidate gene set, and selected genes with AUC >0.6 in IMvigor210CoreBiologies and AUC >0.7 in GSE91061 as input features for the XGBoost model, ultimately obtaining 11 genes. These 11 genes (BRCA1, HMGB2, TOP2A, CDC6, IL1RL1, POU2F1, PTH1R, MALL, PLEK2, CEBPD, IRS2) were used as input features for the XGBoost model (Fig. 6a). We evaluated the fitting ability of the model on the training set and validation set using ROC curves, with the training set’s AUC being 1 and the validation set’s AUC being 0.777 (Fig. 6b). To make the XGBoost model explainable, we assessed the contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction values using SHAP values in the training set, with IRS2 being the gene with the highest contribution (average SHAP value: 0.765) among these 11 genes (Fig. 6c). We further evaluated the model’s predictive ability on the original dataset (IMvigor210CoreBiologies and GSE91061), with the AUC being 0.955 for IMvigor210CoreBiologies and 1 for GSE91061 (Supplementary Fig. S10a).

Discussion

HCC, as a prevalent type of liver cancer, imposes a significant burden on individuals, families, and society. To mitigate unnecessary medical expenditures, effective prognostication of HCC is imperative. Currently, mainstream predictive approaches encompass measurable biomarkers and machine learning techniques (e.g. Cox regression). However, the values derived from biomarker measurements and machine learning predictions are mostly continuous variables; clinicians typically require large-scale cohort studies to identify reliable thresholds. Consequently, there is a need for more dependable HCC prognostic models and more stable thresholds.

Recent studies suggest that the cGAS-STING pathway may play a significant role in various cancers, and it is closely associated with numerous classical pathways. The relationships between the cGAS-STING pathway and other pathways evoke an analogy to the relationships between word vectors in transformer models; both exhibit internal correlation and external parallelism. So, we attempted to apply the transformer model, based on pathways, to survival prediction in HCC. Research has indicated a strong correlation between the cGAS-STING pathway and the tumor immune microenvironment. Recent studies have shown that the cGAS-STING pathway might play an important role in the occurrence and development of liver cancer; the key molecules in the cGAS-STING pathway might be independent prognostic factors for HCC, and the molecules in the cGAS-STING pathway are likely to have an important relationship with the immune microenvironment and immunotherapy of HCC. Pu’s work proved that the molecules related to the cGAS-STING pathway were likely to be independent prognostic factors for HCC, and these molecules are highly correlated with the immune microenvironment of HCC; the author used bioinformatics analysis in multiple cohorts to prove that the cGAS-STING pathway plays an important role in the prognosis and immune microenvironment of HCC, and the author also verified this view in clinical samples [31]. Qi’s work also confirmed that the cGAS-STING pathway was highly correlated with the prognosis and immune microenvironment of HCC; the author proved that the key molecules in the cGAS-STING pathway were highly correlated with the clinical stage and pathological grade of HCC, and the author identified a series of molecules highly related to the prognosis of HCC based on the cGAS-STING pathway, such as XRCC5, XRCC6, and PRKDC [32]. Lv’s work explained at the mechanism level why the cGAS-STING pathway can activate the immune microenvironment of tumors to activate the antitumor immune response; the author’s work proved that TET2 could mediate tumor cells to produce CGAS and activate STING1 in endothelial cells, and the activation of STING1 on endothelial cells can recruit more lymphocytes in the tumor microenvironment to enhance the antitumor immune response [33]. The work of these researchers confirmed at different levels that the cGAS-STING pathway was highly correlated with the prognosis and immunotherapy of HCC. Considering the gradual expansion of ICI therapy into the treatment of HCC, we also conducted immunotherapy outcome prediction in HCC anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy based on cGAS-STING-centered pathways.

Type I interferons, as downstream molecules of the cGAS-STING pathway, are usually regarded as a bridge communicating adaptive immune responses and innate immune responses [46]. Holzgruber’s work indicated that type I interferon signaling might activate the expression of intrinsic PD-1 in tumor cells [47]. Gong’s work showed that type I interferon receptors might induce exhaustion of effector CD8 T cells while promoting cancer stemness [48]. Zheng’s work suggested that IFNα might mediate the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment by mediating the generation of BST2+ tumor-associated macrophages [49]. The mechanism by which the cGAS-STING pathway leads to the upregulation of immune exhaustion markers remains unclear, but existing works imply that this process might be mediated by type I interferon signaling.

In the study, we constructed a transformer survival model for HCC centered on the cGAS-STING pathway, which was trained, validated, and tested on independent datasets. We also performed variable selection based on the cGAS-STING-centered pathways and conducted the training, validation, and testing of an explainable XGBoost model. When inputting to the transformer model, we corrected the gene expression levels. Although our correction of gene expression might resist the differences in sequencing depth and batch effects to a certain extent, this correction might overly exaggerate or underestimate the expression of genes, which might lead to overestimation or underestimation of the predicted values in deep learning. The strategy we used to avoid overfitting was to use a simpler-structured model (XGBoost) and fewer features. The sample size of the immunotherapy dataset with response labels is much smaller than that of the dataset with survival prognosis labels. The sample size of independent immunotherapy datasets is usually approximately between 30 and 300. If the transformer model is continuously used to predict immunotherapy response labels, it may lead to overfitting; in addition, too many features might make the input of machine learning more redundant and might instead reduce the generalization of the model. In the field of machine learning, using simple-structured models in small samples usually might have better generalization than complex models and could avoid overfitting to a certain extent. Therefore, in order to overcome the problem of the small sample size of the immunotherapy dataset, we adopted the strategy of using a simple model and controlling the number of features.

Some datasets might have missing feature genes, and the proportion of missing genes was directly related to the accuracy of prediction, especially for some datasets with a small sample size. Despite filling in the missing features, the missing features still caused bias in the model prediction to a certain extent; in our study, the missing value estimation method we used was based on average calculation, but the expression quantity of specific genes in the sample was likely to deviate from the estimated gene expression quantity, which might affect the final model output to a certain extent.

We used 0.5 as the threshold and achieved good prediction results in three independent HCC cohorts; however, we needed to note that the heterogeneity of HCC patients is very strong, and a single threshold might not meet the actual application requirements. We also tried other thresholds (including 0.25, 0.75, lower quartile, and upper quartile) in the three HCC cohorts and observed that different thresholds might yield different prediction results (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Fig. S5). During clinical use, we recommend that clinicians select the appropriate threshold based on the prediction results of the transformer survival model in large-scale clinical cohorts.

In our study, cohort-specific bias is a problem that requires high attention in the prediction of immunotherapy outcomes. The datasets we selected objectively cannot fully represent the overall characteristics (especially some samples with a small sample size, such as GSE78220), which may lead to strong limitations in the conclusions or inferences drawn. This bias might stem from multiple factors, such as excessive heterogeneity of samples in the dataset or the inability of the selected samples in the dataset to comprehensively represent the overall population. Cohort-specific bias might make the research results lose general applicability and fail to truly reflect the overall situation, thereby affecting the scientificity and effectiveness of clinicians’ decisions in immunotherapy. Limited by the number of immunotherapy datasets and the sample size contained in the datasets, our study would have cohort-specific bias to a certain extent. However, with the further advancement of immunotherapy-related research, it becomes possible to obtain large sample sizes and more population-representative public datasets, which may alleviate cohort-specific bias to a certain extent and provide more reliable references for clinicians’ immunotherapy decision-making.

Data quality, sample size, and patient heterogeneity have significant impacts on the prognosis prediction of HCC patients. The liver cancer survival analysis datasets we used are sourced from the TCGA database, the ICGC database, and the GEO database; during the sequencing process, batch effects and allowable human errors were objectively present and inevitable, and the differences in data quality might lead to poor prediction accuracy of the transformer model in survival analysis to a certain extent. The sample size is too small (e.g. GSE144269 has only 68 samples), which may not fully represent the characteristics of the overall population, and this may cause sample-specific bias in the prediction effect of the model. Generally speaking, HCC patients have high heterogeneity, which means that there might be obvious differences in gene expression patterns between individuals. The model might have difficulty accurately identifying and adapting to these differences, resulting in deviations in the prediction results in different patient groups, which reduces the generalization of the model to a certain extent. With the expansion of clinical sample numbers and the improvement of sequencing technology, the biases brought by dataset quality, sample size, and patient heterogeneity to machine learning predictions could be mitigated to a certain extent. The heterogeneity of HCC samples is relatively strong, and the potentially optimal thresholds might be different for different cohorts. If the threshold is selected too high, it might cause clinicians to overlook patients with higher risks; if the threshold is selected too low, it may cause patients to receive too frequent follow-ups and also waste medical resources to a certain extent. The selection of the threshold for the survival prediction model should be done cautiously, and a diverse selection of thresholds could overcome the prediction bias caused by sample heterogeneity to a certain extent.

The prediction probability of our transformer survival prediction model was a continuous value. In clinical applications, whether to handle the output value of the model as a continuous variable or a binary variable is a problem that clinicians and researchers need to consider. Both binary classification indicators and continuous indicators have important application values, but they also have their own advantages and disadvantages. Binary classification indicators have the advantage of being simple, intuitive, and easy to understand and statistically analyze. In clinical decision-making, it could quickly give a clear judgment, facilitating doctors to quickly identify patients with high-risk survival. For large-scale clinical studies, the recording of binary classification indicators is relatively simple, and the data processing is not complicated. However, the disadvantage of binary classification indicators lies in the coarseness of information. It simplifies the continuous model output into two extreme cases, which might ignore many intermediate states and subtle changes. This might lead to the loss of some information and could not fully reflect the survival risk of patients. Continuous indicators can reflect the changes of physiological or pathological states more precisely. Its advantage is that it provides richer and more detailed information, which is helpful for a more accurate understanding of the survival risk of patients. Through continuous indicators, more complex statistical analysis can be carried out to discover more subtle correlations and trends. However, continuous indicators also have shortcomings. The data interpretation of continuous indicators may be relatively complex, and there may be certain difficulties in understanding and application for clinicians and patients. Binary classification indicators and continuous indicators have their own advantages and disadvantages in clinical applications, and the appropriate binary classification or continuous indicators should be selected according to the specific clinical scenario.

The HCC samples of TCGA-LIHC are mainly from the North American population, the HCC samples of ICGC-LIRI-JP are mainly from the Japanese population, and the HCC samples of GSE144269 are mainly from the Mongolian population. The test sets of our model are mainly concentrated in the population of one or several specific regions, which might lead to significant differences in the performance of our model in different datasets. For example, our transformer survival prediction model was trained in the pan-cancer cohort (mainly the North American population), which may explain why our model performed well in the TCGA-LIHC cohort but poorly in the GSE144269 cohort. The differences in ethnic groups of the samples led to the different performances of the model in different cohorts to a certain extent. Clinicians and researchers need to pay attention to the bias caused by specific ethnic groups when using our model to improve the accuracy of prediction.

In survival analysis, different follow-up times might have the following several impacts on the training and validation of the model (including data imbalance, information loss, and feature validity). Data imbalance: If the difference in follow-up time is large, it might lead to an unbalanced distribution of the number of survival events (such as disease recurrence, death, etc.) in different time periods. There might be fewer survival events in the shorter follow-up time, while there may be more survival events in the longer follow-up time, which would make the data present an unbalanced characteristic and affect the learning and generalization ability of the model. Information loss: A shorter follow-up time may lead to incomplete survival status information for some samples. For those individuals who have not had a survival event at the end of the follow-up, their true survival time is unknown and may be censored. Improper handling of this part of the censored data may affect the model’s accurate learning of the survival pattern. Feature validity: With the extension of the follow-up time, some features related to long-term survival might become more significant, while the role of these features may not be obvious in the shorter follow-up time. If the follow-up time is inconsistent or unreasonable in model training and validation, it might lead to the model being unable to capture the relationship between key features and survival time.

At the biological mechanism level, we can preliminarily determine whether the role of a certain gene in immunotherapy is positive or negative through the positive or negative sign of the SHAP value of the gene. At the actionable clinical decisions level, clinicians could design targeted agonists or inhibitors for genes with larger absolute SHAP values, and combine the agonists or inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors for the second immunotherapy. This personalized combination therapy might improve the efficacy of immunotherapy to a certain extent. In different samples, our XGBoost model for predicting immunotherapy would use the SHAP algorithm to give the contribution of each feature (gene) to the predicted outcome. Among these features, the larger the absolute value of the SHAP value is, the greater the influence of the gene on immunotherapy is. If the SHAP value of the gene is positive, it indicates that the gene may have played a positive role in the immunotherapy of the patient; if the SHAP value of the gene is negative, it indicates that the gene might have played a negative role in the immunotherapy of the patient.

The current study suggests that the more important genes in the XGBoost model (such as IRS2, BRCA1, and PLEK2) might have potential biological relevance to the outcome of immunotherapy. Tian’s work suggests that the aging-related gene signature (IRS2, PDGFRA, TFAP2A, and SOD2) might be related to the survival prognosis and immunotherapy outcome of melanoma [50]. Zheng identified five gene markers related to the survival prognosis and immunotherapy outcome of triple-negative breast cancer [51]. Zhang’s work suggests that the co-expression of MEST and BRCA1 might affect the outcome of immunotherapy by influencing the immune microenvironment [52]. Liu’s work suggests that PLEK2 and IFI6 might induce the generation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and make patients resistant to immunotherapy [53]. These studies suggest that the relatively important features screened by the XGBoost model might have potential biological significance related to immunotherapy.

In the XGBoost immunotherapy response prediction model, we used a total of 11 genes as feature inputs. However, some of these feature genes might have correlation or collinearity. Correlated features could have a significant impact on the prediction and interpretability of the model. Although we used the SHAP algorithm to estimate the contribution of each gene, related features might have adverse effects on the interpretability of the model. When there are correlations among features, it means that these feature genes provide similar information. This might make it difficult for the model to accurately distinguish the independent contributions of each feature and may compromise the importance of the input features. During the training process, correlated features might lead to overfitting, and the model may be overly sensitive to minor changes in the training data. When facing new data, the prediction performance of the model might decline.

Our transformer-based survival prediction model and XGBoost-based immunotherapy prediction model for HCC might assist clinicians in patient stratification related to HCC prognosis and the selection of immunotherapy. In our work, we set 0.5 as the threshold of the HCC survival risk probability predicted by the transformer survival prediction model. If clinicians have the condition for large-scale HCC sample sequencing, we recommend that clinicians select the risk probability threshold based on large-scale sequencing data; if not, clinicians could use 0.5 as the threshold for the initial prognostic risk stratification of HCC patients. Our XGBoost immunotherapy prediction model provides the prediction of the probability of immunotherapy response. Since the predicted values given by our XGBoost model are continuous variables, when clinicians use our XGBoost model, we suggest that clinicians compare the immunotherapy prediction values of multiple HCC patients. Patients with higher predicted values of immunotherapy response are recommended for immunotherapy, and patients with lower predicted values of immunotherapy response are recommended for other therapies (such as targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy).

Our transformer model and XGBoost model are both interpretable. The interpretability of our transformer model depends on the Captum python module, and the interpretability of our XGBoost model depends on the SHAP algorithm. In the transformer model, we used the integrated gradients algorithm to explain each feature of the model; the larger the absolute value of the feature contribution calculated by the integrated gradients algorithm is, the more important the feature is. A positive contribution value represents that the feature might represent a poor prognosis of HCC and vice versa. Considering that the input of our transformer model was essentially a matrix integrated from multiple pathways, we explained the transformer model in units of pathways (the feature contribution value of each pathway is the average of the contribution values of all genes in that pathway). In the XGBoost model, we used the SHAP algorithm to explain the contribution of each gene to the predicted outcome. The larger the absolute value of the SHAP value, the more important the gene is for predicting the outcome of immunotherapy. If the SHAP value is positive, it represents that the gene may represent a good outcome of immunotherapy and vice versa.

Biomarkers predictive of HCC prognosis have been widely applied in clinical settings, demonstrating reliable predictive power. Clinically, the common biomarkers of HCC include Alpha Fetoprotein (AFP), Glypican-3, and Des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP); the higher the contents of these three biomarkers in the serum are, the more likely the patient is to have HCC; the three biomarkers usually imply a worse prognosis. AFP has a high accuracy rate for the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma, especially in patients with chronic liver diseases (such as cirrhosis) [54, 55]. If the serum AFP continues to increase and pregnancy, active liver disease, and gonadal embryonic tumors could be excluded, it highly suggests the possibility of hepatocellular carcinoma; if the AFP in the serum of HCC patients continues to increase after surgery, this might represent a poor clinical prognosis. In hepatocellular carcinoma tissues, Glypican-3 shows high expression, while it is expressed at a low level or not expressed in normal liver tissues or benign liver diseases; detection of Glypican-3 is helpful for the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma, especially in patients with AFP-negative hepatocellular carcinoma, and can be used as a supplementary diagnostic indicator; too high Glypican-3 also implies a poor prognosis for HCC patients [56]. DCP is an abnormal prothrombin, which is often elevated in the blood of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and has certain value for the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma; it is helpful for the early diagnosis of liver cancer, and DCP detection could improve the accuracy of diagnosis; too high DCP also implies a poor prognosis for HCC patients [57]. When using our deep learning survival model and XGBoost immunotherapy outcome prediction model, clinicians should fully combine the commonly used clinical biomarkers for comprehensive judgment. The Cox regression model has also been employed in numerous studies for HCC prognosis prediction, yielding favorable prediction capacity. However, traditional biomarkers and machine learning outputs are typically continuous; their clinical application necessitates large-scale cohort validation for threshold selection. We utilized a comprehensible numeric value (0.5) as a threshold, and our deep learning model effectively distinguished between high-risk and low-risk HCC populations across different cohorts. In terms of the model itself, we have achieved a certain degree of improvement over existing survival models. Compared to common deep learning survival models (DeepSurv and DeepHIT), we adopted a more complex deep learning structure (transformer model); in addition, our deep learning model’s input data are biological pathways with internal relations rather than unordered gene sequences [44, 58]. The biological biomarkers and numerous machine learning models have also been employed for outcome prediction of cancer immunotherapy [59–62]. Our XGBoost model exhibits greater interpretability compared to other immunotherapy prediction models, rendering its decision-making process more transparent and comprehensible; we have employed the SHAP algorithm to interpret the XGBoost model, which has been widely applied in machine learning models for other diseases [63–66]. In summary, our HCC deep learning survival model and explainable XGBoost anti-PD-1/PD-L1 model can assist clinicians in making clinical decisions.

Our investigation might provide a new perspective for future prediction of survival prognosis and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcomes in HCC. For instance, a transformer-based survival prediction model could be constructed using fundamental information, biochemical markers, and blood routine examination of HCC patients. We could consider interrelated features as a feature vector within the transformer model (e.g. gender, age, and body weight as the first set of feature vectors; liver biochemical indicators as the second set; and the proportions of various immune cells in the blood as the third set). Moreover, the prediction of outcomes from radiotherapy and chemotherapy in HCC could employ an explainable XGBoost model, where common predictive indicators for radiotherapy and chemotherapy could serve as input features to build the explainable XGBoost model. This model not only integrates diverse indicators but also interprets the contribution of each indicator to the final predictive probability for radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

Overall, our study provides a deep learning model for predicting HCC survival prognosis and an explainable predictive model for predicting anti-PD-1/PD-L1 outcomes. Both of our models possess the potential to predict HCC prognosis and HCC anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy outcomes in clinical application.
