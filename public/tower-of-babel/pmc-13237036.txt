Prototype contrastive and adversarial alignment for heterogeneous domain adaptation
Authors: Zhishu Sun, Xinru Wang, Meijing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterogeneous domain adaptation remains challenging due to the inconsistent feature representations and data distributions between the source and target domains. In this paper, we introduce contrastive learning into heterogeneous domain adaptation, which is barely considered in the literature. Specifically, we propose a novel model called Prototype Contrastive and Adversarial Alignment (PCAA), taking both feature- and class-level distribution alignments into account to discover an informative shared feature space for the source and target domains. For feature-level distribution matching, we develop an adversarial network to reduce the marginal distribution difference. Besides, PCAA involves a prototype contrastive schema to minimize the conditional distribution discrepancy for class-level distribution alignment. We further reduce the cross-entropy loss on the labeled samples to achieve informative latent representations. Experiments on several real-world datasets validate the effectiveness of the proposed model for image classification.

Introduction

Domain adaptation1–5, a.k.a., transfer learning, is a promising solution in training labeled data sparsity problems and has been adopted in many real-world fields, such as image recognition6–10, sentiment analysis11,12, and recommendation systems13. Its main idea is to leverage the knowledge extracted from a source domain to help the learning task in a target domain. Although the past decade has witnessed the great development of domain adaptation, plenty of existing models focus on the knowledge transfer between domains with the same feature space and different distributions, i.e. homogeneous domain adaptation12,14. For example, Transfer Component Analysis (TCA)14 is a classical model that learns transfer components across domains where the data are represented by the same features such as text. Zhao et al.15 proposed adaptively aligning representations at both the feature and classifier levels to bridge gaps between two homogeneous domains in few-shot settings. In practice, the features of the source and target domains may be different, e.g. images characterized by different representations such as SURF16 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {DeCAF}_6$$\end{document}17. This situation refers to the knowledge transfer between heterogeneous domains, i.e. heterogeneous domain adaptation18–20. In this learning paradigm, homogeneous domain adaptation models can not function, and specific heterogeneous domain adaptation approaches are required.

One common strategy for heterogeneous domain adaptation is to learn a latent shared feature space for the source and target domains, such as21–24. The main purpose is to ensure the distributions of the source and target domains in this shared feature space are similar, i.e. the domain gap is minimized. For instance, Discriminative Distribution Alignment (DDA)23 employs Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) to estimate the domain discrepancy, thus finding an optimal feature space for the source and target domains. With the development of deep learning, researchers tend to apply deep learning to heterogeneous domain adaptation. For example, Zhao et al. proposed to mitigate the domain discrepancy and transfer the category correlation knowledge from the source domain to the target domain24. In spite of the great performance achieved by these models, they barely consider contrastive learning25, an emerging tool that has been proven to be effective and powerful in knowledge transfer26.

Recent studies showed that contrastive learning has achieved considerable success in many research areas, such as recommendation systems27 and domain adaptation28,29. These achievements attribute to the superior ability of contrastive learning to bring together two views of the same instance while pushing apart two views of different instances. In Ref.28, Kang et al. proposed employing contrastive learning to jointly optimize intra-class and inter-class differences across domains for distribution alignment. As a result, the performance of minimizing domain discrepancy can be significantly improved by applying contrastive learning to domain adaptation. Considering the practicability of heterogeneous domain adaptation and the great power of contrastive learning, in this paper, we propose a novel model named Prototype Contrastive and Adversarial Alignment (PCAA) to handle the knowledge transfer between two heterogeneous feature spaces. The motivation of the proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1. In particular, we seek to find a feature space where three aims can be satisfied: Firstly, the latent feature representations are informative enough for classification, thus we estimate the cross-entropy error over the labeled data. Secondly, the marginal distribution discrepancy between the source and target domains is minimized, which can be achieved by involving adversarial learning for feature-level distribution alignment. Lastly, for the source and target domains, their prototypes (i.e. centres) of the same class are together while those of the different classes are separate, leading to the further reduction of conditional distribution difference. To this end, PCAA adopts the pseudo labels for the unlabeled target data and takes advantage of the label information on the source and target domains. Consequently, we use contrastive learning and perform contrast between the class prototypes of the two domains for class-level distribution alignment.

Related works

Heterogeneous domain adaptation

When the source and target domain data lie in heterogeneous feature spaces, the knowledge transfer from a well-labeled source domain to a poorly-labeled target domain is called heterogeneous domain adaptation. In the literature, heterogeneous domain adaptation methods can be roughly classified into two groups. The first group aims to exploit an intermediate domain to connect the source and target domains for adaptation, such as30. In Ref.30, Wu et al. introduced unlabeled co-occurrence samples which consist of two parts, i.e. one part shares common factors with the source domain and another part has common factors with the target domain, to bridge two heterogeneous feature spaces, thus the knowledge can be transited from the source domain to the target domain.

The second group adopts the feature transformation strategy to align the distributions of the source and target domains, and such transformations can be divided into asymmetric31,32 and symmetric projections19,22,33. Generally, the former one learns the projection from the source domain to the target domain. For instance, Zhou et al.31 proposed estimating a sparse feature mapping by optimizing the weights of multiple binary classifiers in the source and target domains and then transforming the source features to the target domain. In Ref. 32, Gu et al. proposed transferring data from the source domain to the target domain based on an optimal transport plan that preserves the matching of keypoint pairs. The latter projection seeks to map the source and target domain data into a common feature space. For example, Wang et al.19 proposed transforming the instance features from the source and target domains to a shared feature space that preserves the class consistency and aligns the data distributions. In Ref. 33, Tian et al. proposed preserving the local spatial structure data information during projection and aligning both cross-domain classification space and their distributions in a common feature space.

The above-mentioned models are mainly based on mathematical optimization to learn the projection matrices, there are a few studies like11,24,34,35 attempting to use neural networks to promote domain invariance between the source and target domains. For example, in Ref.34, Fang et al. first derived theoretical analysis for semi-supervised heterogeneous domain adaptation and then proposed a fully-connected neural network to obtain a common feature space based on the theory. In addition, Chen et al.35 proposed combining stochastic pruning and embedding layer to adapt representative neurons for heterogeneous data, and optimizing feature mapping, adaptation, and classification in a unified neural network. These models demonstrate the applicability and effectiveness of neural networks in dealing with heterogeneous domain adaptation problems. In this paper, we also employ neural networks to learn a domain-invariant feature space, where the feature- and class-level distribution differences are both minimized. Different from existing heterogeneous domain adaptation methods, we introduce contrastive learning into the proposed model.

Contrastive learning and adversarial learning

Contrastive learning25,36,37 has become an effective and popular self-supervised strategy in recent years. By contrasting positive and negative sample pairs, samples labeled by the same class can be close to each other and samples with different classes are separated, thus learning the important intrinsic characteristics of the samples. Recent literature tends to apply contrastive learning to domain adaptation, such as26,28,29. For instance, Singh proposed using contrastive learning to minimize the inter-domain discrepancy and instance-level contrastive alignment between the original and augmented unlabeled target samples to reduce the intra-domain gap26. In Ref. 29, Wang et al. first formed positive and negative sample pairs in a cross-domain manner and then adopted contrastive learning to learn domain-invariant features. However, these models are proposed for homogeneous domain adaptation and are not applicable to heterogeneous domain adaptation. In this paper, we introduce contrastive learning into heterogeneous domain adaptation. Specifically, we perform prototype contrast across domains, thus minimizing the class-level distribution difference between the source and target domains.

Adversarial learning has become a widely adopted technique for aligning distributions across domains. The core idea, popularized by Ganin et al.38, involves a domain discriminator that competes with a feature encoder to learn domain-invariant representations. Recent advances have extended adversarial learning to complex cross-domain scenarios. For instance, Liu et al.39 proposed integrating a dual-objective discriminator that not only distinguishes between UAV and satellite views but also identifies various environmental styles, encouraging the feature encoder to produce features invariant to both viewpoint and environmental variations. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_t^l$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_t^u$$\end{document} be the numbers of labeled and unlabeled target samples, respectively, then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_t = n_t^l + n_t^u$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_t^l \ll n_t^u$$\end{document}.

Traditional machine learning assumes that the training and test data come from a feature space where data are independent and identically distributed to guarantee that models learned on the training data generalize well to the test data. With this assumption, one strategy to deal with heterogeneous domain adaptation is to reduce the domain discrepancy between the source and target domains, thus ensuring the data distributions across domains are similar. To this end, for the source and target domains, we propose learning a shared feature space, in which the feature- and class-level distribution differences are jointly minimized. Figure 2 shows an overview of the proposed model. Specifically, we formulate the proposed model as a training procedure that utilizes two feature encoders to project the source and target domain data into a common feature space. In this shared space, to reduce the feature-level distribution difference, we develop an adversarial learning network for marginal distribution matching. Besides, to minimize the class-level distribution difference, we propose a prototype contrastive schema to make sure the prototypes of the same class are mixed while those of the different categories are aparted. Below we elaborate on the detailed information of the proposed model.

Heterogeneous feature encoders

It is impractical to use one single feature encoder to transform features with different dimensions from two heterogeneous spaces. Therefore, we develop two feature encoders, i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_s({\bf X}_s; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s):\mathbb {R}^{d_s\times n_s}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{k\times n_s}$$\end{document} for the source domain and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_t({\bf X}_t; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t):\mathbb {R}^{d_t\times n_t}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{k\times n_t}$$\end{document} for the target domain, to project the source and target domain data into a shared k-dimensional feature space by sharing a network parameter at the output layer. Note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t$$\end{document} are the trainable network parameters for the source and target feature encoders, respectively. Specifically, it is expected that the sample representations learned by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_s(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_t(\cdot )$$\end{document} are informative enough for making predictions. Therefore, by defining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_y(\cdot )$$\end{document} as the label predictor with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_y$$\end{document} being its parameters, we evaluate the cross-entropy error over the labeled data, including labeled source and target samples, to train the classifier as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {{\mathcal {L}}}_{ce}({\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_y) = -\frac{1}{n_s} \sum _{i = 1}^{n_s} \sum _{c = 1}^C y_{s, i}^c \log (\hat{y}_{s, i}^c) - \frac{1}{n_t^l} \sum _{i = 1}^{n_t^l} \sum _{c = 1}^C y_{t, i}^c \log (\hat{y}_{t, i}^c), \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{s, i}^c$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{t, i}^c$$\end{document} are produced by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_y(\cdot )$$\end{document} and indicate the predicted probabilities of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_s({\bf x}_{s,i}; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_t({\bf x}_{t,i}; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t)$$\end{document}, respectively, to be labeled with class c.

Feature-level alignment discriminator

We attempt to ensure that the sample representations learned by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_s(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_t(\cdot )$$\end{document} follow a similar distribution, which can be achieved by adversarial learning. Specifically, we adopt the adversarial loss38 that acts as the Jensen-Shannon divergence, which shows the advantages of symmetrization and bound compared with the Kullback-Leibler divergence41. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_d(\cdot )$$\end{document} be the domain discriminator with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_d$$\end{document} being its parameters. As a result, by assigning the domain labels of the source and target domains a label vector of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf y}_d = [{\bf 0}_{n_s}, {\bf 1}_{n_t}] = [y_{d,1}, \ldots , y_{d, n_s+n_t}]$$\end{document}, we develop a feature-level alignment discriminator that reduces the distribution discrepancy between the source and target domains as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {{\mathcal {L}}}_f({\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_d) = -\frac{1}{n_s + n_t} \sum _{i = 1}^{n_s + n_t} (1 - y_{d, i}) \log (1 - \hat{y}_{d, i}) + y_{d, i} \log (\hat{y}_{d, i}), \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{d, i}$$\end{document} means the predicted domain label probability derived from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{G}_d(\cdot )$$\end{document}. Specifically, the domain discriminator and the heterogeneous feature encoders compete against each other in an adversarial manner to make the latent representations domain-invariant. In particular, reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal {L}}}_f}({\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_d)$$\end{document} gives the domain discriminator the ability to distinguish the latent representations of the source domain from those of the target domain, while maximizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal {L}}}_f}({\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t)$$\end{document} enforces the heterogeneous feature encoders to deceive the domain discriminator by producing latent representations which are indistinguishable across domains. To handle such a minimax issue, we introduce a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL)38 between the heterogeneous feature encoders and domain discriminator. With GRL, the minimax issue converts to a minimization problem of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal {L}}}_f$$\end{document}.

Class-level prototype contrastive

It is indicated in the literature like7,22 that utilizing label information for distribution matching can further reduce the domain discrepancy. Existing heterogeneous domain adaptation models barely introduce contrastive learning for prototype matching to align the conditional distribution difference. In this paper, we take advantage of contrastive learning and propose a prototype contrastive schema to match the class-level distributions between the source and target domains. Specifically, a prototype can be generated by calculating the central point of samples with the same class. Therefore, given a source domain with C categories, we obtain the source prototypes as follows:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {\bf p}_s^c = \frac{1}{n_s^c} \sum _{i = 1}^{n_s^c} \textit{G}_s({\bf x}_{s, i}^c; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s),~c \in \{1, \ldots , C\}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf x}_{s, i}^c$$\end{document} is a source sample labeled with category c and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_s^c$$\end{document} is the corresponding number of samples. For the target domain, it is impossible to obtain the target prototypes as the labels of the unlabeled target samples are inaccessible. To address this issue, we adopt the pseudo label updating strategy. Specifically, based on the labeled target samples, we train an SVM42 classifier, which is used to make predictions for the unlabeled target samples. These predictions are treated as the initial pseudo labels and will be updated with the training of PCAA. The update procedure is given in Sect. "The proposed method". In this way, the unlabeled target samples are labeled with pseudo labels, and we can compute the target prototypes as follows:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {\bf p}_t^c = \frac{1}{n_t^c} \sum _{i = 1}^{n_t^c} \textit{G}_t({\bf x}_{t, i}^c; {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t),~c \in \{1, \ldots , C\}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf x}_{t, i}^c$$\end{document} is a target sample labeled with class c and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_t^c$$\end{document} is the corresponding number of samples.

Next, we propose a prototype contrastive schema based on contrastive learning. Specifically, we pick up two prototypes from the source and target domains, respectively, and regard the prototypes of the same class as the positive pairs and those of different categories as the negative pairs. Hence, the learning on positive pairs ensures the consistency between different prototypes of the same class, while the learning on negative pairs guarantees the divergence across different categories, thus fulfilling class-level distribution alignment. In particular, we use the InfoNCE43 for contrastive learning and obtain the following formulation:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} {{\mathcal {L}}}_c({\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_s, {\boldsymbol{\Theta }}_t) = \sum _{i = 1}^C -\log \frac{\exp ( h({\bf p}_s^i, {\bf p}_t^i) / \tau )}{\sum _{j = 1}^C \exp ( h({\bf p}_s^i, {\bf p}_t^j) / \tau )}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where i and j are class indices, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is the temperature parameter, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h(\cdot , \cdot )$$\end{document} is the function calculating the cosine similarity between two vectors. Particularly, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{({\bf p}_s^i, {\bf p}_t^i) | i \in \{1, \ldots , C\}\}$$\end{document} is the positive pairs and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{({\bf p}_s^i, {\bf p}_t^j) | i, j \in \{1, \ldots , C\}, i \ne j\}$$\end{document} is the negative pairs.

Overall joint training

In summary, the proposed model seeks to minimize the following objective function by incorporating the classifier, feature-level alignment discriminator, and class-level prototype contrastive:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {{\mathcal {L}}}= {{\mathcal {L}}}_{ce} + \alpha {{\mathcal {L}}}_{f} + \beta {{\mathcal {L}}}_{c}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} are trade-off parameters. During each iteration of the training of PCAA, we obtain latent representations of the labeled source and target samples, which are used to train the classifier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_y(\cdot )$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_y(\cdot )$$\end{document} will make predictions for the unlabeled target samples. These predictions will be used as the pseudo labels in the next iteration.

Compared methods

For a comprehensive and fair comparison, we adopt several state-of-the-art heterogeneous domain adaptation models, including shallow and neural network approaches. The brief descriptions of the compared methods are listed as follows.SVM42. We apply SVM (provided by49) to the labeled target data to train a classifier and then use it to make predictions on the unlabeled target samples. SVM is the method employed to generate the pseudo labels for the proposed model and is treated as a baseline method without knowledge transfer.SHFA21. Semi-supervised Heterogeneous Feature Augmentation (SHFA) projects the source and target data into a latent space and then augments the transformed data with their original features and zeros. An SVM classifier with hinge loss is trained for making predictions.CDLS50. Cross-Domain Landmark Selection (CDLS) identifies representative cross-domain data to derive a domain-invariant feature space, where a linear SVM classifier is trained.SGW51. Semi-supervised entropic Gromov-Wasserstein (SGW) incorporates label information into optimal transport to make the transported source and labeled target samples follow similar conditional distributions. An SVM classifier is trained based on the transported source and labeled target instances.LPJT22. Locality Preserving Joint Transfer (LPJT) aligns the source and target domains at both feature-level and sample-level to discover domain-invariant feature representations, in which a label propagation method52 is used to train the classifier.DDA23. DDA involves the classifier adaptation, distribution alignment, and discriminative representations in a unified framework. Here, we adopt the cross-entropy loss to train the classifier.ICDM6. Information Capturing and Distribution Matching (ICDM) jointly maximizes the information preservation and minimizes the domain difference to learn an augmented feature space, where an SVM classifier is obtained.CDSPP19. Cross-Domain Structure Preserving Projection (CDSPP) learns domain-specific projections to transform the source and target samples into a common space where the class consistency is preserved and data distributions are aligned.HGSDR20. HDA with Generalized Similarity and Dissimilarity Regularization (HGSDR) exploits the similarity and separability within the cross-domain labeled data to minimize cross-domain bias.SSAN11. Simultaneous Semantic Alignment Network (SSAN) trains two non-linear network encoders and presents the synchronous semantic alignment to reduce the domain discrepancy between the source and target domains.SCT24. Semantic Correlation Transfer (SCT) minimizes the marginal and conditional domain differences and transfers the class correlation knowledge by maximizing the pairwise class similarity across the source and target domains.Among these models, SVM is a method ignoring knowledge transfer, SHFA, CDLS, SGW, LPJT, DDA, ICDM, CDSPP, and HGSDR are shallow heterogeneous domain adaptation approaches, and SSAN and SCT are neural network-based heterogeneous domain adaptation models. Particularly, DDA, ICDM, CDSPP, HGSDR, SSAN, and SCT can be treated as the state-of-the-art heterogeneous domain adaptation models. For the above-mentioned baseline methods, we follow6 and use the parameters suggested by their original papers or tune the parameters to obtain their best results. Conclusion

In this paper, we study the problem of heterogeneous domain adaptation, where the data of two domains are represented by different features. Considering that very few heterogeneous domain adaptation models adopt contrastive learning, a powerful tool in representation learning, we introduce contrastive learning for class-level distribution alignment. Specifically, we propose a novel model named Prototype Contrastive and Adversarial Alignment (PCAA), which reduces both marginal and conditional distribution discrepancy between the source and target domains. PCAA contains a feature-level alignment discriminator based on adversarial learning to match the marginal distributions. Besides, we present a class-level prototype contrastive schema to align the conditional distributions. We perform experiments on several real-world image datasets and make comparisons with state-of-the-art approaches, and the results demonstrate that the performance of the proposed model is better than that of the compared methods.

Limitations and future work

Despite its effectiveness, PCAA has several limitations worth noting. First, the model is specifically designed for the semi-supervised heterogeneous domain adaptation setting where a small amount of labeled target data is available. These labeled target samples are essential for two purposes, i.e. supervised training of the classifier via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}_{ce}$$\end{document} and generating initial pseudo-labels for prototype computation. In a fully unsupervised scenario with no target labels, PCAA cannot be directly applied, as the classifier would lack target supervision and initial reliable pseudo-labels would be non-trivial. Second, the current implementation uses shallow fully-connected networks, and extending PCAA to deep architectures would require addressing scalability challenges in prototype computation. Addressing these limitations represents promising directions for future research.
