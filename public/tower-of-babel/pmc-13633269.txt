Deficiency of protein kinase Cδ reprograms macrophage immunometabolism to suppress pyroptosis and stabilize atherosclerotic plaques
Authors: Chih-Feng Lien, Hsin-Yi Chang, Shu-Han Yu, Rou-Ling Cho, Sy-Jou Chen, Tzu-Ting Kuo, Patrick Chun-Theng Chong, Chih-Hung Ye, Feng-Yen Lin, Wan-Lin Wu, Shih-Hua Lin, Chien-Sung Tsai, Chin-Sheng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability drives acute coronary syndrome, the regulatory mechanisms underlying plaque stability remain unclear. Protein kinase Cδ (PKCδ) has been implicated in atherosclerosis progression, but its specific role in plaque vulnerability and the underlying mechanisms require clarification. We analyzed PKCδ expression in human atheroma datas and investigated its role using low-density lipoprotein receptor-knockout mice with global and myeloid-specific PKCδ deletion. Plaque morphology, necrotic core formation, and fibrous cap thickness were evaluated. Macrophage metabolic profiling, mitochondrial function, inflammatory responses, and pyroptosis markers were assessed using biochemical and molecular approaches. Human atheroma analysis revealed elevated PKCδ expression, particularly in macrophages within ruptured plaques. PKCδ deletion in mice reduced necrotic core formation and increased fibrous cap thickness in both global and myeloid-specific models. Mechanistically, macrophage PKCδ deficiency improved mitochondrial fitness by promoting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and elevating α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) levels. This metabolic shift reduced pro-inflammatory responses through PIK3AP1–mTORC2 activation and downregulated NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. Dimethyl α-ketoglutarate (DKG) supplementation provided similar protective effects. Both human and in vivo analyses revealed PKCδ association with pyroptosis markers, including NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and GSDMD, whereas PKCδ deficiency reduced their co-localization with CD68⁺ macrophages. In conclusion, PKCδ serves as a key regulator of macrophage inflammation, pyroptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction in atherosclerotic plaques. PKCδ deficiency promotes metabolic reprogramming that stabilizes plaques through reduced pyroptosis and enhanced mitochondrial function. These findings highlight PKCδ as a potential therapeutic target to reduce acute cardiovascular events by modulating plaque stability.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, and its pathogenesis is attributed primarily to the rupture of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques1. Inflammation is recognized as a pivotal contributor to the initiation, progression, and destabilization of these plaques, playing a central role in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease. The clinical relevance of inflammation is further supported by accumulating evidence that links inflammatory activity to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, as demonstrated by the CANTOS (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study) trial and subsequent colchicine-based clinical trials2–4. Accordingly, comprehensive analyses of inflammation-driven molecular mechanisms and the development of targeted therapies are crucial for advancing ACS management.

Macrophages contribute to atherosclerotic plaque evolution through key inflammatory processes5. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has identified multiple macrophage subpopulations within atherosclerotic plaques, including metabolically dysfunctional foamy macrophages in human lesions and lipid-metabolic or -catabolic macrophages in murine plaques6,7. Metabolic reprogramming serves as a fundamental determinant of macrophage polarization and subsequently drives the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis. The metabolic shift toward enhanced glycolysis, coupled with impaired tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), orchestrates M1-like polarization and triggers pro-inflammatory activation in macrophages8. Conversely, metabolic profiles favoring OXPHOS activity promote M2 polarization9. The metabolically programmed M1 macrophages perpetuate plaque instability through matrix metalloproteinase secretion, extracellular matrix degradation, and subsequent programmed cell death, ultimately contributing to the development of thin fibrous cap and necrotic core10. These insights highlight that therapeutic strategies specifically targeting the metabolic pathways governing macrophage polarization represent a promising approach for controlling vascular inflammation and achieving plaque stabilization.

Protein kinase Cδ (PKCδ) is a member of the protein kinase C (PKC) family, which consists of serine/threonine kinases involved in various cellular processes, including differentiation, proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis11. Our previous studies have shown that PKCδ regulates the expression of the macrophage scavenger receptors SR-A and CD36, thereby increasing the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein and exacerbating foam cell formation12. In vivo experiments have indicated that PKCδ deficiency enhances vein graft atherosclerosis in mice13, while the absence of macrophage PKCδ increases atherogenic diet-induced atherosclerosis in Apoe-/- mice14. In a human study, compared with normal arteries, the atherosclerotic left internal mammary artery exhibited a significant increase in PKCδ protein expression12. These results indicate that targeting PKCδ may help stabilize atherosclerotic plaques, illustrating the complex and distinct roles of PKCδ in various cell types involved in atherosclerosis, a multifaceted process that remains incompletely understood.

This study investigated the role of PKCδ in M1 macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses and its impact on plaque stability in atherosclerosis. Using in vitro and in vivo models, metabolite analysis, human data analysis, and advanced omics technologies, including transcriptomics and phosphoproteomics, we aimed to validate the effects of macrophage PKCδ on atherosclerotic plaque evolution. Our findings reveal that macrophage PKCδ regulates inflammatory responses, metabolic reprogramming, and cell death, thereby highlighting its therapeutic potential in atherosclerosis.

Methods

An extended description of methods is provided in the Supplementary material.

Animals and in vivo experiments

PKCδ-deficient (PKCδ-/-) mice were obtained from Professor Keiichi I. Nakayama15 and crossbred with low-density lipoprotein receptor-deficient (Ldlr-/-) mice (RRID:IMSR_JAX:002207) to generate Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice. C57BL/6J wild-type (WT; RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) mice were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Taiwan. All animals were bred and maintained under pathogen-free conditions in accordance with a protocol approved by the Laboratory Animal Center at the National Defense Medical University (Protocol No. IACUC-20072). All experimental procedures complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and to use the minimum number of animals required to achieve scientific objectives. A high-cholesterol diet (HCD) comprising 34.1% sucrose, 20% milk fat, and 0.3% cholesterol (D02031507; Research Diets, Inc.) was used in this study. Eight-week-old mice were fed HCD for 12 weeks. At 20 weeks of age, the mice were anesthetized with tiletamine–zolazepam (20 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) injected intraperitoneally before blood collection via cardiac puncture. After perfusion with 20 ml of phosphate-buffered saline, the liver was harvested for quantitative polymerase chain reaction and western blot analysis, and the aorta, heart, and liver were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3,000×g for 15 minutes at 4 °C, and the plasma was collected and stored at −80 °C.

Bone marrow transplantation

Bone marrow (BM) cells were isolated from 8–12-week-old WT and PKCδ-/- mice, after which 2 × 106 cells/100 μl were intravenously injected into lethally irradiated (2 × 500 rad) Ldlr-/- recipient mice. Following bone marrow transplantation (BMT), the mice received antibiotics in their drinking water and were allowed a 4-week recovery period to facilitate BM reconstitution. After 4 weeks, blood was collected from the recipients via the submandibular (facial) vein for genotyping and verification of BM reconstitution. For atherosclerosis experiments, all recipient mice were fed HCD for 12 weeks.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m., as indicated in each graph. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software). Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using a two-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Macrophage PKCδ is associated with the development and progression of human atherosclerotic plaques

To investigate the role of PKCδ in atherosclerosis, we reanalyzed RNA-seq data from human carotid artery plaques and compared them with data from distant, macroscopically intact tissue (GSE43292)16. Our analysis revealed that the gene expression of several PKC family members, including PRKCA, PRKCB, PRKCD, PRKCE, PRKCH, and PRKCQ, was significantly upregulated in atheroma plaques, whereas PRKCI expression was significantly downregulated relative to the adjacent carotid tissue (Fig. 1a). To further elucidate the specific involvement of PRKCD in plaque vulnerability, we examined transcriptional profiles from another dataset of human carotid endarterectomy samples (GSE41571)17. Notably, PRKCD expression was significantly higher in macrophage-rich regions of ruptured atherosclerotic plaques compared with those of stable plaques (Fig. 1b). To confirm the cellular source of PKCδ within human atherosclerotic plaques, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from the GSE131778 dataset18 (Fig. 1c). Within atherosclerotic plaques, PRKCD was expressed in smooth muscle cells, B cells, and T cells, but predominantly in macrophages (Fig. 1d, e). Building on this observation, we examined the expression patterns of key inflammation-related genes within the same scRNA-seq dataset. We found that several pro-inflammatory genes, including CD68, TNFα, TLR2, CXCL3, CCL4, NLRP3, CASPASE1, IL1β, IL18, and gasdermin D (GSDMD),were highly expressed in macrophages (Fig. 1f). Deficiency of PKCδ alleviates atherosclerosis by improving lipid profiles and restraining pro-inflammatory macrophage activity

To elucidate the role of PKCδ in atherosclerosis, Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice were bred (Fig. 2a) and fed HCD. En face and oil red O staining demonstrated that PKCδ deficiency significantly reduced the size of atherosclerotic plaques and lipid accumulation in the aorta (Fig. 2b, c). Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed that PKCδ deficiency significantly decreased the lesion and necrotic core areas in the aortic sinus while increasing fibrous cap thickness on plaques (Fig. 2b, c). Further examination of plaque complexity in Ldlr-/- and Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice revealed no significant differences in macrophage infiltration, collagen content, smooth muscle cell content; however, the vulnerability index showed a trend toward reduction (Fig. 2b, c). Because hyperlipidemia is a major contributor to the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis, we analyzed plasma lipid profiles. PKCδ deficiency considerably reduced plasma total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride levels in HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice, whereas high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and liver function indicators (GPT and GOT) remained unchanged (Fig. 2d). We then examined the impacts of PKCδ deficiency on inflammatory responses in HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice. Plasma cytokine analysis showed that PKCδ deficiency significantly reduced tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) and interleukin-12 (IL-12) levels, whereas IL-18, IL-6, and IL-33 levels remained unaffected (Fig. 2e). Hematopoietic PKCδ deficiency mitigates plaque complexity and inflammatory responses without markedly altering systemic lipid profiles

The above data proposed that systemic PKCδ knockout affects lipid metabolism and reduces plaque burden. To specifically assess the role of hematopoietic PKCδ in atherosclerosis, Ldlr-/- recipient mice underwent BMT from either WT or PKCδ-/- donor mice (Fig. 3a). Hematopoietic PKCδ deficiency significantly reduced the necrotic core area and vulnerability index while increasing the thickness of the fibrous cap on plaques. There were no changes in the lesion area, macrophage infiltration, collagen content, or smooth muscle cell content (Fig. 3b, c). These findings suggest that the deficiency of hematopoietic PKCδ halts plaque evolution and reduces the complexity of atherosclerotic lesions. Furthermore, BMT from either WT or PKCδ-/- mice into Ldlr-/- mice for 4 weeks, followed by HCD feeding for 12 weeks, did not alter the plasma lipid profile or liver function, except for triglyceride levels (Fig. 3d). Interestingly, hematopoietic PKCδ deficiency markedly reduced the levels of the inflammatory cytokine IL-18, whereas TNFα, IL-12, IL-6, and IL-33 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3e). Multiplexed IHC analyses further confirmed that hematopoietic PKCδ deficiency decreased the expression of MHC class II within the aortic sinus lesions (Fig. 3f). PKCδ deficiency modulates macrophage paracrine signaling to enhance fibrous cap thickness

Emerging evidence indicates that macrophage-derived paracrine factors, including transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), critically regulate fibrous cap formation and plaque stability by modulating extracellular matrix synthesis and degradation20. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 1, under basal conditions, PKCδ-/- BMDMs exhibited significantly increased TGFβ secretion compared with WT controls, whereas PDGF-B and MMP9 secretion were not affected. Upon lipopolysaccharide (LPS)/interferon-γ (IFNγ) (co-treatment with LPS and IFNγ) stimulation, TGFβ secretion was markedly suppressed, whereas PDGF-B and MMP9 secretion were increased in both WT and PKCδ-/- BMDMs. Importantly, the induction of MMP9 was significantly attenuated in PKCδ-/- BMDMs compared with WT controls under inflammatory conditions. Overall, these findings support a model in which PKCδ deficiency reprograms the macrophage secretory profile toward a plaque-stabilizing phenotype.

RNA-seq analysis of the pathway by which PKCδ attenuates M1 macrophage polarization in macrophages

To assess the effects of PKCδ on the pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages, primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) isolated from mice were stimulated with LPS and IFNγ to induce pro-inflammatory responses. We found that PKCδ deficiency attenuated the expression of M1 pro-inflammatory mRNAs, including Tnfα, Il6, Il1β, and Nos2 (Fig. 4a), in BMDMs. In addition, the knockdown of PKCδ reduced the expression of TNFα, IL6, and IL1β in human THP-1 cells (Fig. 4b). To investigate the mechanism by which PKCδ attenuates pro-inflammatory responses, RNA-seq was performed to identify potential regulatory pathways. Ingenuity pathway analysis revealed that the OXPHOS pathway was the most significantly upregulated pathway (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis revealed that the expression of genes related to OXPHOS was significantly greater in PKCδ-/- BMDMs than in WT BMDMs in both vehicle and LPS/IFNγ groups (Fig. 4e). Deficiency of PKCδ enhances immunometabolism to prevent macrophage pro-inflammatory responses

During the pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages, mitochondrial fitness was decreased, which involves the suppression of OXPHOS and a shift toward glycolysis for ATP production8. Our RNA-seq analysis indicated that the OXPHOS pathway is enriched in PKCδ-/- BMDMs. These findings prompted us to explore the role of PKCδ in macrophage metabolism. We found that PKCδ deficiency enhanced OXPHOS (Fig. 5a, b) and glycolytic activity (Fig. 5c, d). Under LPS/IFNγ stimulation, compared with WT BMDMs, PKCδ-/- BMDMs tended toward increased OXPHOS (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b) and glycolysis (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). An energy map revealed that LPS/IFNγ stimulation shifted the metabolic phenotype toward glycolysis, but PKCδ-/- BMDMs exhibited a more energetic phenotype in both vehicle-treated and LPS/IFNγ-stimulated groups (Supplementary Fig. 2e). TCA cycle metabolites are crucial for mitochondrial fitness by regulating OXPHOS and play an important role in the pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages. We then investigated the effects of PKCδ on TCA cycle metabolism (Fig. 5e) in WT and PKCδ-/- BMDMs using ultra-performance liquid chromatography triple quadrupole mass spectrometry. Moreover, PKCδ deficiency in BMDMs induced broad metabolic reprogramming, with most metabolites showing an upward trend; notably, citrate and α-KG levels remained persistently elevated compared with WT BMDMs, even under LPS/IFNγ stimulation (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, total isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) activity was significantly increased in PKCδ-/- BMDMs (Fig. 5g). Next, we conducted a phosphoproteomic analysis to evaluate the effects of PKCδ deficiency on the activity of other kinases. Kinase–substrate enrichment analysis based on phosphorylated peptide levels revealed a reduced activity of several PKC family members in PKCδ-/- BMDMs. In addition, inflammation-related kinases such as IRE1, NEK2, PDK1, and RIPK2 showed decreased activity (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Consistently, upon LPS/IFNγ stimulation, the activities of inflammation-related kinases, including HRI, IRAK4, LOK, NEK2, PDK1, and RIPK2, were markedly lower in PKCδ-/- BMDMs than in WT BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Among these, IRAK4 and RIPK2 are pivotal in TLR/IL-1R and NOD-mediated NF‑κB activation, driving pro‑inflammatory cytokine production22,23. Phosphoproteomic analysis of BMDMs revealed significant changes in phosphorylation across ten distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Interestingly, clusters 1 and 2 contained 121 peptides with higher phosphorylation levels in PKCδ-/- BMDMs than in WT BMDMs. Enrichment analysis of phosphoproteomic data from these clusters highlighted pathways such as “mTOR Signaling Activation by Fatty Acids and Glucose,” and “Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer: Overview.” The enrichment of mTOR signaling and cancer-related metabolic reprogramming pathways indicates that PKCδ deficiency promotes lipid- and glucose-driven metabolic reprogramming, shifting macrophages toward a glycolytic and metabolically active state that may influence inflammatory responses. These findings suggest that mTOR signaling acts as a central regulator of metabolic remodeling in PKCδ-/- BMDMs (Fig. 5h). To elucidate how mTORC controls mitochondrial function in PKCδ-deficient macrophages, we examined phosphorylation patterns and found that PKCδ deficiency increased Akt S473 phosphorylation but not p70 S6 kinase (S6K) T389 phosphorylation, indicating the selective activation of mTORC2 rather than mTORC1 (Fig. 5i). To further investigate the potential upstream regulators of mTORC2, we examined targets enriched in clusters 1 and 2 by phosphoproteomic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3c) and demonstrated that phosphoinositide-3-kinase adaptor protein 1 (PIK3AP1), a previously reported upstream regulator of Akt Ser473 phosphorylation24, was significantly upregulated in the absence of PKCδ. Moreover, our data confirmed that Pik3ap1 mRNA expression was significantly increased in PKCδ-/- BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 3d), suggesting that the upregulation of PIK3AP1 was regulated at the transcriptional level. We further showed that PIK3AP1 expression was enhanced in PKCδ-/- BMDMs via the Akt-independent pathway (Fig. 5j). Treatment with the mTORC2 inhibitor JR-AB2-011 (JR) eliminated the phosphorylation of Akt S473 (Fig. 5i) and normalized both OXPHOS and glycolysis in PKCδ-/- and WT BMDMs (Fig. 5k, l) but did not affect the increased protein expression of PIK3AP1 (Fig. 5j). Similarly, JR treatment reduced α-KG levels in PKCδ-/- BMDMs (Fig. 5m). Furthermore, mTORC2 inhibition reversed the suppression of the pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages caused by PKCδ deficiency (Fig. 5n). These findings establish mTORC2 as the key mediator of PKCδ-dependent metabolic reprogramming during the pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages. Our findings indicate that PKCδ deficiency enhances mitochondrial fitness, promoting OXPHOS and significantly altering TCA cycle metabolism while increasing glycolytic capacity. This metabolic reprogramming occurs through PIK3AP1–mTORC2 activation, positioning PKCδ as a potential therapeutic target for modulating macrophage function under inflammatory conditions.

PKCδ deficiency attenuates the inflammasome and pyroptosis in macrophages

Mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolites activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering pyroptotic cell death that contributes to necrotic core formation in atherosclerotic plaques25. Our RNA-seq data revealed that PKCδ deficiency suppresses pyroptosis pathway activation, prompting us to investigate the role of PKCδ in regulating inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. Under LPS/IFNγ stimulation, the expression of PKCδ significantly increased in both BMDMs and THP-1 cells (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). PKCδ deficiency decreased the protein expression of NLRP3 and mature IL-1β in BMDMs and THP-1 cells treated with LPS/IFNγ (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). LPS combined with nigericin (Nig) is commonly used to trigger pyroptosis via canonical inflammasome activation. Compared with WT BMDMs, PKCδ-/- BMDMs exhibited reduced mRNA expression of Nlrp3 and Caspase 1 (Casp1) upon LPS/Nig stimulation (Fig. 6b), whereas the mRNA levels of NLRP3, CASP1, and GSDMD remained unchanged in PKCδ-knockdown THP-1 human macrophages compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Our data demonstrated that PKCδ deficiency reduced protein expression of NLRP3, mature IL-1β, cleaved GSDMD, and active caspase 1 in both BMDMs and THP-1 cells treated with LPS/Nig (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 4c). Using both computational prediction tools and biochemical analysis, we examined the potential interaction between PKCδ and NLRP3 (Supplementary Fig. 5). AlphaFold Multimer analysis yielded low interface confidence scores (ipTM = 0.17, pTM = 0.33) (Supplementary Fig. 5a), and PEPPI analysis predicted a low likelihood of interaction (log(LR) = −1.328) (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Consistently, co-immunoprecipitation assays failed to detect an association between PKCδ and NLRP3 under either basal or LPS/Nig-stimulated conditions in BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Notably, PKCδ deficiency significantly reduced Nlrp3 mRNA expression after LPS/Nig stimulation (Fig. 6c). Collectively, these data indicate that the reduction of NLRP3 and cleaved caspase 1 in PKCδ-/- BMDMs is more likely mediated by indirect regulatory mechanisms rather than direct interaction or phosphorylation of NLRP3 by PKCδ.

As mentioned above, PKCδ deficiency increases α-KG production, which may enhance mitochondrial fitness by sustaining TCA cycle flux, supporting OXPHOS, maintaining redox balance, and promoting metabolic flexibility26. To assess the role of α-KG-supported mitochondrial fitness in pyroptosis, BMDMs were treated with the cell-permeable α-KG analog DKG. We found that DKG supplementation reduced the expression of pyroptosis-related protein, including NLRP3, active caspase 1, mature IL-1β, and cleaved GSDMD in both WT and PKCδ-/- BMDMs (Fig. 6f). Our data suggest that α-KG functions as a downstream effector of PKCδ, suppressing pyroptosis and linking PKCδ deficiency to reduced inflammasome activation through enhanced mitochondrial function.

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) is a well-established upstream trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation, facilitating ASC oligomerization and subsequent caspase 1 activation25. To determine whether PKCδ deficiency regulates inflammasome activation through modulation of redox homeostasis, we assessed mtROS production in BMDMs. After LPS/IFNγ stimulation, PKCδ-/- BMDMs exhibited significantly reduced mtROS production compared with WT BMDMs. Importantly, exogenous DKG treatment further suppressed mtROS levels under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6). Our findings provide mechanistic support that α-KG suppresses inflammasome activation, in part, by attenuating mtROS-dependent signaling. Reanalysis of RNA-seq data from human carotid artery plaques compared with those from distant, macroscopically intact tissue (GSE43292)16 revealed significantly increased expression of PRKCD, NLRP3, NLRC4, AIM2, IL1β, CASP1, and GSDMD in human atheromas (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Correlation analysis revealed that PRKCD expression was positively correlated with the expression of NLRP3, NLRC4, AIM2, IL1β, CASP1, and GSDMD (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Notably, Pearson’s correlation analysis indicated that NLRP3 expression showed the strongest correlation with PRKCD expression in human atheromas (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Interestingly, plasma PKCδ levels were comparable between patients with ACS and those with stable angina (Supplementary Fig. 8a). To further assess the impacts of PKCδ on pyroptosis in plaque macrophages from HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice, we conducted immunofluorescence staining. PKCδ deficiency reduced the co-localization of CD68 with canonical pyroptosis markers (NLRP3, caspase 1, IL-1β, and GSDMD) in plaques of HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice (Fig. 7a–h). These data suggest that PKCδ regulates the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis in macrophages, contributing to necrotic core formation in atherosclerosis. Discussion

In this study, we examined the role of PKCδ in atherosclerosis, focusing on its impact on macrophage polarization, pyroptosis, and plaque evolution. Elevated PKCδ expression in macrophage-rich areas of ruptured human plaques, together with findings that PKCδ deficiency reduced plaque size, lipid accumulation, and necrotic core areas in Ldlr-/- mice, indicates that PKCδ is critical for plaque evolution. Mechanistically, PKCδ deficiency enhanced OXPHOS and glycolysis in macrophages, modulating metabolic and energy pathways via PIK3AP1–mTORC2 activation to suppress pro-inflammatory responses of macrophages. Moreover, PKCδ deficiency suppressed pyroptosis and attenuated necrotic core formation, potentially through the upregulation of α-KG. These findings establish PKCδ inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for stabilizing vulnerable plaques.

Omics data, including scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq, revealed that PKCδ is highly expressed in macrophages within human atherosclerotic plaques, particularly ruptured plaques. Importantly, we detected a positive correlation between PKCδ and its key associated molecules, such as NLRP3, caspase 1, GSDMD, and IL-1β, which contribute to necrotic core formation. The CANTOS study, which demonstrated reduced major adverse cardiovascular events through IL-1β blockade2, has heightened interest in targeting inflammation in cardiovascular disease. Considering molecules currently under investigation in clinical trials27, PKCδ emerges as a promising therapeutic target for future cardiovascular interventions.

Interestingly, PRKCI expression was significantly downregulated in atheroma plaques compared with adjacent carotid tissues in bulk RNA-seq data. The current evidence regarding the role of PKCι (PRKCI) in atherosclerosis remains limited and somewhat inconsistent. One study reported that saturated fatty acids can activate PKCι and mediate lipid-induced apoptosis in endothelial cells, suggesting a potential role in endothelial injury during atherosclerosis development28. Conversely, another study demonstrated that inflammatory stimuli such as C-reactive protein and endothelin 1 inhibit PKCλ/ι translocation and activity, leading to impaired nitric oxide production and endothelial dysfunction29. Taken together, these findings suggest that the role of PKCι in atherosclerosis may be context-dependent and remains incompletely understood. Additional studies are therefore needed to clarify its precise contribution to vascular inflammation and plaque development.

Macrophages exhibit time-dependent behavior along the inflammatory-reparative spectrum during immune responses. After LPS/IFNγ stimulation, the pro-inflammatory phase predominates for 16–24 hours before transitioning to a reparative phase30. This two-stage metabolic remodeling prevents sustained inflammation by shifting macrophages from pro-inflammatory to M2-like reparative phenotypes that rely on the TCA cycle, fatty acid oxidation, and OXPHOS for energy production31,32. Previous studies have shown that mTORC2 mediates glutaminolysis, which converts glutamine to α-KG to replenish TCA cycle intermediates and maintain mitochondrial function33,34. Our data indicate that PKCδ deficiency maintains mitochondrial fitness by increasing TCA cycle metabolites and OXPHOS activity, resulting in reduced inflammation. These findings suggest that PKCδ deficiency enhances the reparative phase by promoting anti-inflammatory metabolic reprogramming, thereby facilitating the resolution of inflammation.

Accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming is a key determinant of macrophage inflammatory phenotypes. In classically activated M1 macrophages, the TCA cycle is functionally disrupted at critical nodes, particularly IDH and succinate dehydrogenase, leading to the accumulation of pro-inflammatory metabolites. IDH inhibition results in citrate accumulation, which fuels TNFα and prostaglandin production35, whereas succinate dehydrogenase inhibition leads to succinate accumulation and ROS production, both of which stabilize HIF1α and enhance IL-1β production36. Interestingly, TCA intermediates might exert anti-inflammatory effects depending on the metabolic context. Citrate-derived itaconate suppresses IL-1β, IL-6, and HIF1α signaling37, whereas α-KG supports OXPHOS and promotes M2-like polarization through epigenetic regulation, alongside inhibiting LPS/IFNγ-induced NF-κB and NLRP3 expression38,39. Our data suggest that PKCδ deficiency preserves TCA cycle integrity by maintaining key enzyme activity (e.g., IDH), thereby limiting pro-inflammatory metabolite accumulation and sustaining mitochondrial function through mTORC2. This metabolic preservation promotes a shift away from an M1-like state toward a more restrained, metabolically balanced phenotype. Together, our findings support a model in which PKCδ deficiency attenuates macrophage inflammation by maintaining TCA cycle homeostasis and reprogramming immunometabolic signaling.

Our data revealed that PKCδ deficiency decreases necrotic core formation and strengthens the fibrous cap of atherosclerotic plaques in HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice, prompting us to investigate the underlying cellular mechanisms involved. RNA-seq analysis revealed marked suppression of the pyroptosis pathway. Canonical pyroptosis is mediated by inflammasome-dependent activation of caspase 1, which cleaves pro-IL-1β, pro-IL-18, and GSDMD, facilitating cytokine release and inducing pyroptotic cell death21. Our in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that PKCδ deficiency markedly suppressed NLRP3, caspase 1, IL-1β, and GSDMD activation in LPS/Nig-treated BMDMs and reduced their expression in the aortic sinuses of HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice. The mechanism underlying PKCδ-mediated regulation of pyroptosis involves metabolic reprogramming through the mTORC2–α-KG axis. We established that PKCδ deficiency activates mTORC2, leading to increased α-KG production, an effect that is reversed by mTORC2 inhibition, confirming pathway specificity. This metabolic shift toward elevated α-KG levels provides multiple antipyroptotic benefits. Notably, α-KG has been reported to inhibit NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in human ovarian granulosa cells39. Moreover, α-KG enhances mitochondrial quality control by promoting mitophagy and improving mitochondrial structure and function while simultaneously reducing ROS production26. Our results indicate that α-KG attenuates mitochondrial oxidative stress in BMDMs and suggest that PKCδ regulates NLRP3-driven pyroptotic signaling through α-KG-mediated metabolic reprogramming.

Our data suggest that mTORC2 signaling contributes to the antiatherosclerotic effects of PKCδ deficiency. PKCδ deficiency enhances mTORC2-dependent Akt phosphorylation, glycolysis, OXPHOS, and α-KG levels, collectively inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization. Charbonnier et al. reported that loss of mTORC2 activity impairs glycolysis and OXPHOS in regulatory T cells40. Moreover, mTORC2 plays a key role in atherosclerosis by regulating macrophage function and suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways, including inflammasome/IL-1β response. In macrophage-specific mTORC2-deficient mice, increased lesion complexity and cell death further suggest that mTORC2 has a protective role in controlling inflammation and promoting cell survival41. Our study identifies a novel link between PKCδ and PIK3AP1, demonstrating for the first time that PKCδ deficiency upregulates PIK3AP1 protein expression. PIK3AP1 reportedly regulates Akt Ser473 phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting GSK3β and FOXO1 while promoting glycolysis. This metabolic shift enhances the expression of reparative genes such as Arg1 and Klf4, which negatively regulate inflammatory responses24. Furthermore, PIK3AP1 inhibits NLRP3 and NLRC4 inflammasome activation in macrophages42. Accordingly, our findings suggest that PIK3AP1 may function as a key upstream regulator of mTORC2–Akt signaling in macrophages. These findings identify the PIK3AP1–mTORC2 axis as a critical mediator of PKCδ deficiency-associated protection, highlighting its pivotal role in controlling macrophage-driven inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in atherosclerosis.

Our findings indicate that PKCδ deficiency increases fibrous cap thickness, defined as the minimum distance between the necrotic core and the lumen in hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections43. The thickness of the fibrous cap may have bidirectional clinical implications. In advanced lesions, persistent plaque growth or excessive matrix accumulation may lead to luminal narrowing and impaired blood flow. However, fibrous cap thickness is a primary hallmark of plaque stabilization; mechanistically, a thicker cap rich in smooth muscle cells and extracellular matrix provides the structural integrity necessary to attenuate mechanical stress at the plaque surface44. Specifically, thin cap fibroatheromas, defined by a fibrous cap thickness of <65 μm, are strongly associated with plaque vulnerability in humans45,46. We proposed that the increased fibrous cap thickness observed in Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice indicates a more stable plaque phenotype. Moreover, PKCδ-/- BMDMs exhibited increased TGFβ and reduced MMP9 secretion, suggesting a shift toward extracellular matrix preservation. Overall, these findings suggest that PKCδ deficiency promotes plaque stability, at least in part, through modulation of macrophage-mediated matrix remodeling.

We show the distinct effects of PKCδ deficiency between Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice and BMT model mice. Global PKCδ knockout significantly reduced the lesion area, whereas hematopoietic-specific deficiency via BMT did not, suggesting cell type-specific roles in atherosclerosis. This difference is partly attributable to the systemic lipid-lowering effects observed in global knockout mice, indicating that PKCδ regulates lipid metabolism primarily through nonhematopoietic tissues. Consistent with these findings, Bezy et al. reported that hepatic Prkcd overexpression elevates serum cholesterol47. Notably, both models similarly enhanced plaque stability by reducing the necrotic core size and increasing the fibrous cap thickness, highlighting macrophage PKCδ as a key driver of plaque destabilization, consistent with its role in macrophage death-mediated necrotic core formation48. Collectively, these findings emphasize the distinct yet complementary roles of PKCδ in modulating lipid metabolism and plaque vulnerability in atherosclerosis.

In the present study, circulating IL-18, but not other measured inflammatory cytokines, was reduced in hematopoietic PKCδ-deficient BMT mice. Because plaque macrophages primarily produce IL-18 and require caspase 1-dependent maturation, this reduction may reflect decreased macrophage inflammasome activity rather than a generalized suppression of systemic inflammation49,50. Previous studies have shown that IL-18 is enriched in symptomatic and ulcerated human plaques and promotes IFNγ-mediated inflammation and matrix metalloproteinase expression, both linked to plaque destabilization49,50. Importantly, circulating IL-18 independently predicts cardiovascular mortality in patients with coronary artery disease51. Together, these findings suggest that IL-18 serves as a macrophage-specific marker linking hematopoietic PKCδ deficiency to reduced inflammasome-driven inflammation and improved plaque stability.

In our dataset, we found a positive correlation between PRKCD expression and canonical pyroptosis markers (NLRP3, CASP1, and GSDMD) in human atheroma. We further performed complementary functional studies and demonstrated that PKCδ deficiency in macrophages markedly suppressed the canonical NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, as indicated by reduced NLRP3 expression, caspase 1 activation, and GSDMD cleavage. Consistently, Ldlr-/-+PKCδ-/- mice exhibited decreased expression of NLRP3, caspase 1, GSDMD, and IL-1β within atherosclerotic lesions. Our data suggest that PKCδ may modulate inflammasome activation through mtROS production. Together, these findings suggest that PKCδ plays a direct role in inflammasome activation and macrophage pyroptosis in atherosclerotic plaques.

We propose that PKCδ may function predominantly as a local regulator within the atherosclerotic plaque microenvironment rather than as a circulating biomarker. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies indicating that key mechanisms driving plaque destabilization are largely confined to the vessel wall and not necessarily reflected in systemic circulation52,53. These findings suggest that the lack of a detectable plasma difference does not preclude a pathogenic role for PKCδ; instead, it highlights its function as a local regulator of plaque inflammation and cellular dysfunction.

Our study reveals a novel finding that hematopoietic-specific PKCδ deficiency reduces plaque complexity in HCD-fed Ldlr-/- mice, which contrasts with the results of Li et al., who reported increased plaque development in high-fat diet-fed Apoe-/- mice with macrophage-specific PKCδ knockout14. Li et al. used Apoe-/-; PKCδflox/flox; LyzM-Cre+ mice, which eliminate PKCδ in the myeloid lineage from embryonic development, potentially allowing for genetic compensation by the upregulation of redundant genes54. Our BMT approach, however, restricted PKCδ deficiency to adult hematopoietic-derived cells, possibly avoiding such compensatory mechanisms and revealing a more pronounced phenotype. Moreover, Rahman et al. demonstrated that Lyz2Cre expression increases insulin sensitivity while reducing macrophage phagocytic activity, effects that may independently influence atherosclerosis development55. In addition, differences in mouse atherogenic models may contribute to divergent outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that Ldlr-/- mice develop atherosclerosis primarily in response to HCD owing to defective LDL clearance, whereas Apoe-/- mice manifest profound hypercholesterolemia, monocytosis, and rapid atherogenesis even under normal dietary conditions, reflecting broader disruptions in lipid and immune homeostasis56. Regarding the diet, Li et al. used an atherogenic diet (TD.02028; Harland Laboratory) containing 12.5 g/kg cholesterol, whereas our HCD (D02031507) contained only 3 g/kg cholesterol (a 4.2-fold difference). In addition, TD.02028 contains 5.0 g/kg cholic acid, whereas D02031507 contains no bile acids. Cholic acid enhances cholesterol absorption by improving micelle solubilization57, whereas its absence reduces cholesterol absorption and decreases atherosclerosis by 50%58. The greater atherogenic potential of TD.02028 stems from its higher cholesterol content and increased cholesterol bioavailability. These results highlight the importance of developmental timing and model selection in studying the role of PKCδ in atherosclerosis and provide new insights into its context-dependent function in cardiovascular disease.

Our study has several limitations. First, the BMT approach introduces potential confounding from other transplanted immune cells, and murine atherosclerosis models exhibit fundamental differences from human disease in plaque morphology, rupture patterns, and atherothrombotic mechanisms. Second, our mechanistic insights derive primarily from in vitro systems that oversimplify the complex inflammatory microenvironment and dynamic cellular interactions present in human atherosclerotic lesions. Third, our focus on early-to-intermediate lesions may limit generalizability to advanced plaques with necrotic cores, calcification, and intraplaque hemorrhage. Despite these differences, our findings contribute important mechanistic information that can guide future translational research.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that PKCδ plays a critical role in the evolution of atherosclerotic plaques by regulating macrophage inflammation and metabolic reprogramming. Macrophage-specific PKCδ deficiency shrinks the necrotic core, strengthens the fibrous cap, and reduces plaque vulnerability while suppressing inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, likely via the PIK3AP1–mTORC2–α-KG axis. These results highlight the potential of targeting PKCδ as a therapeutic strategy to mitigate the risk of ACS by stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques and reducing inflammatory responses.
