Endovascular biopsy of an isolated sigmoid sinus intravenous lesion with newer-generation stent retriever: illustrative case
Authors: Yasmin Abdelmajid, Florian Roser, Syed I. Hussain, Ahmedyar Hasan, Gabrielle Yeaney, Ayoub Nahal, Seby John
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIntravenous sinus meningiomas are rare tumors that may mimic idiopathic intracranial hypertension when they obstruct venous outflow. Biopsy of such lesions is challenging due to their deep location and the risks associated with open surgical approaches.OBSERVATIONSA 40-year-old woman presented with worsening headaches, papilledema, and elevated intracranial pressure. Imaging revealed a homogeneously enhancing lesion within the right transverse-sigmoid sinus junction, causing venous occlusion. Despite medical therapy, symptoms persisted. An endovascular biopsy was performed using a NeVa NET stent retriever with aspiration. Three passes were made across the lesion, successfully yielding tissue for histopathological analysis. A diagnosis of benign meningioma was confirmed. The patient subsequently underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement with significant clinical improvement.LESSONSEndovascular biopsy using a stent retriever offers a safe, minimally invasive alternative to open surgical biopsy for intrasinus lesions. This technique is especially valuable in anatomically complex or high-risk locations where traditional approaches carry substantial morbidity. The use of newer-generation stent retrievers with integrated mesh structures may enhance tissue acquisition and diagnostic yield, guiding appropriate management in cases of isolated venous sinus lesions. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25456

Intravenous sinus meningiomas are uncommon tumors that can manifest with signs and symptoms mimicking idiopathic intracranial hypertension, particularly when they obstruct CSF outflow. Meningiomas with dural attachments at the region of the transverse-sigmoid sinus junction are relatively uncommon. Of all meningiomas involving the major dural sinuses, only 5% occur at the transverse sinus.1 Establishing a diagnosis of intravenous sinus lesions is challenging. Lesions within the posterior venous system are not readily amendable to an open biopsy and carry considerable risk. There have been remarkable advancements in the endovascular neurosurgical field with expanded indications, techniques, and devices for the treatment of a broad range of cerebrovascular conditions, including those involving the cerebral veins and venous sinuses. An endovascular approach for tissue sampling of intrasinus lesions is attractive given its minimal invasiveness and simplicity. This case highlights a novel application of a newer-generation stent retriever for endovascular biopsy of an isolated intravenous lesion in the sigmoid sinus.

Illustrative Case

A 40-year-old female, with a past medical history significant for chronic migraines, presented with worsening headaches for 2 weeks. She reported daily headaches different in character from her migraine episodes along with retro-orbital pain and dizziness. An ophthalmological evaluation revealed papilledema. Subsequent neurological evaluation with MRI demonstrated an extra-axial lesion within the venous sinus at the right transverse-sigmoid sinus junction with intermediate signal on T1- and T2-weighted sequences (Fig. 1). The lesion did not demonstrate restricted diffusion and showed homogeneous enhancement after gadolinium administration. Additional features of raised intracranial pressure were present, including a partially empty sella, posterior flattening of the globes, relative optic nerve head protrusion, and mild distention of the peri–optic nerve sheath complex bilaterally with vertical tortuosity. An MR venogram with contrast demonstrated co-dominant sinuses and a large occlusive filling defect in the right sigmoid sinus. The right distal sigmoid sinus and jugular vein were seen opacifying normally. The opening pressure on lumbar puncture was 36 cm H2O. Further investigation with MR spectroscopy; CT imaging of the neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis; tumor markers; and ultrasound of the breast was noncontributory. The patient was diagnosed with intracranial hypertension related to venous outflow obstruction from the intrasinus lesion. Differential diagnosis for the lesion included a chronic thrombus, meningioma, or other tumor. She was started on acetazolamide, which was titrated up to 1000 mg/day, beyond which she developed intolerable side effects. Additional medications included topiramate and migraine prophylactics. As a first consideration, the patient was treated for presumed venous sinus thrombus. She was started on therapeutic doses of rivaroxaban. Despite these interventions, there was no improvement in the headache. Follow-up MRI of the brain after 2 weeks was unchanged, and a repeat lumbar puncture again demonstrated an opening pressure of 36 cm H2O.

At this time, the patient was referred to our center, where examination was notable for persistent symptoms and papilledema. Alongside management options of intracranial hypertension, a discussion regarding observation versus biopsy of the lesion was conducted given the possibility of potential malignancy. After extensive multidisciplinary discussion, an endovascular approach for tissue biopsy was offered. Anticoagulation with rivaroxaban was stopped.

The patient was placed under general anesthesia. Diagnostic cerebral angiography was performed using standard technique and devices. This revealed a large filling defect in the proximal right sigmoid sinus with complete occlusion of the sinus (Fig. 2). The proximal margin of the lesion was just at the level of the vein of Labbé with evidence of venous hypertension, as seen by retrograde flow in the right transverse sinus. There was no evidence of flow across the sigmoid sinus lesion. However, there was normal distal reconstitution of the right sigmoid sinus immediately past the lesion via venous collaterals. These findings were confirmed with a 3D rotational angiogram with imaging in the venous phase. No intravenous heparin was administered during the procedure, apart from the heparinized saline infusion lines. Next, access was obtained in the right common femoral vein and an 8-Fr sheath was placed. A 6-Fr CEREBASE long guide sheath catheter (CERENOVUS, Johnson & Johnson) was manipulated into the right sigmoid sinus over a 5-Fr Bernstein diagnostic catheter. A tri-axial system consisting of a RED 72 intermediate catheter (Penumbra) over a Headway 27 microcatheter (Terumo Neuro) over a Synchro2 standard microwire (Stryker) was introduced into the guide sheath. The microcatheter and microwire were carefully manipulated through the intrasinus lesion. There was no difficulty manipulating the microwire through the lesion. Maneuvering the microwire was performed without making a loop of the wire to ensure that the wire has the best chance of passing within the body of the lesion. Subsequently, the microcatheter was advanced over the microwire distally up to the torcula. Venous manometry through the microcatheter in the torcula and right transverse sinus indicated a sinus pressure of 28 mm Hg. Using a technique similar to mechanical thrombectomy for vessel occlusion in acute ischemic stroke, three passes of embolectomy were performed. A NeVa NET 5.5 × 37 stent retriever (Vesalio) was loaded within the microcatheter and deployed across the sinus lesion, with the proximal marker of the stent at the distal margin of the lesion. After the stent was left in place for 60 seconds, the intermediate catheter was advanced to the distal margin of the lesion. The stent retriever was withdrawn into the intermediate catheter up to its first “drop zone,” after which the entire unit was withdrawn out of the guide sheath while maintaining continuous aspiration through both the intermediate and guide catheters. With each pass, the stent retriever was inspected for any tissue, which was carefully extracted and saved in formalin bottles for histopathological examination (Fig. 3). Additional tissue fragments from the aspiration catheter were also collected. A frozen section was requested, and a diagnosis of meningioma was reported. This was later confirmed on formal histopathological examination of the remaining submitted samples. Given the histopathological benign nature of the meningioma, conservative management without resection of the tumor was chosen. The patient subsequently received a ventriculoperitoneal shunt for reduction of the intracranial pressure. Following this, there was significant symptomatic improvement in headache and papilledema, which allowed gradual reduction and discontinuation of acetazolamide.

Discussion

Observations

Open surgery for biopsy of an intravenous sinus lesion, especially in the posterior venous system, is not a minor procedure. In our case, tissue sampling would have required a standard skull base craniotomy with proximal and distal ligation of the sinus, a procedure entailing significant risk of blood loss and low probability of complete resection. An endovascular approach is extremely attractive because it is minimally invasive and uses standard technique and devices that are routinely used in neurointerventional daily practice. In addition, an endovascular approach may afford an option of venous recanalization or reconstruction with stent placement. The latter is especially true in cases of extrinsic venous sinus compression. In our case, we used a newer-generation stent retriever along with an intermediate aspiration catheter to perform the biopsy. The NeVa lineup of stent retrievers is unique in their construction design, with drop zone openings in the basket cell structure. These closed-cell, self-expanding nitinol stents have markers placed at right angles to each other creating the drop zones, which are 360° windows that allow for lateral integration of a clot when used in thrombectomy. The NeVa NET has three such drop zones, as well as a microfiltration mesh at its tip, to prevent distal embolization of clot. Such a design allows for integration of clots of different firmness and consistency, rather than being compressed from the outside.2

To date, there have only been 2 reported cases in the literature, by Khatibi et al.3 and Dincer et al.,4 utilizing an endovascular technique using a stent retriever (Table 1). The case from Khatibi et al. also used a newer-generation stent retriever (EMBOTRAP, CERENOVUS). Dincer et al. reported retrieval of nondiagnostic tissue with aspiration alone using a Zoom 71 catheter (Imperative Care). However, when aspiration was combined with a Solitaire X stent retriever (Medtronic), tissue was identified within the interstices of the stent. With regard to technique, use of the microwire and microcatheter to actually transverse the lesion itself, rather than outside its margins along with the sinus wall, contributes to successfully retrieving tissue sample. Doing this ensures that the stent opens inside the lesion itself and therefore affords a higher chance of extracting a tissue sample. Summary of reported cases utilizing endovascular techniques for intravascular lesion biopsy

To the best of our knowledge, no other reports of an endovascular biopsy were found. Prior to the previously referenced case, Quan et al.5 had published their experience with using percutaneous endovascular biopsy in the diagnosis of venous sinus lesions in 9 patients. The procedure was successful in obtaining biopsies from 6 of 9 patients, using 1.8-mm and 2.3-mm forceps devices.

Meningiomas can produce intracranial hypertension from impaired venous outflow secondary to venous sinus obstruction, commonly caused by meningiomatous invasion or compression. When such lesions involve a dominant or solitary sinus, particularly the transverse or superior sagittal sinus, venous pressure proximal to the obstruction increases, impairing CSF absorption at the arachnoid granulations. In our case, it is unclear why the patient would get acutely symptomatic in the presence of a widely patent contralateral co-dominant sinus. Although intraluminal tumor progression may be clinically silent over time due to slow development and partial compensatory drainage, decompensation can occur abruptly once critical outflow is compromised. It can also be postulated that despite the indolent growth of meningiomas, the cerebral venous system exhibits limited capacity for collateralization due to its low-pressure, passive hemodynamics and the fixed architecture of dural sinuses embedded in the dura mater. Consequently, even gradual occlusion may lead to symptomatic venous hypertension especially in patients lacking sufficient venous redundancy or collateral pathways.

Lessons

Endovascular biopsy using a modern stent retriever is a viable and minimally invasive technique for diagnosing lesions in the dural venous sinuses. This approach offers a practical and safer alternative to open surgery, especially in high-risk or anatomically constrained locations.
