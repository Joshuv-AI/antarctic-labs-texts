A low-dose pemetrexed-cisplatin combination regimen induces significant nephrotoxicity in mice
Authors: Samson A Iwhiwhu, Ravi Kumar, Abdul H Khan, Jeremiah M Afolabi, Jada D Williams, Julia E de la Cruz, Adebowale Adebiyi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPemetrexed is combined with cisplatin to treat cancer. Whether pemetrexed-cisplatin combination chemotherapy exacerbates cisplatin nephrotoxicity is unclear. Here, we investigated kidney injury in mice administered a non-lethal low-dose regimen of pemetrexed or cisplatin alone and compared it with a pemetrexed-cisplatin combination.MethodsMice were randomly divided into four groups and administered intraperitoneally the experimental drugs solubilized in captisol (sulfobutylether β-cyclodextrin). Group 1 received captisol, Group 2 pemetrexed (10 mg/kg), Group 3 cisplatin (1 mg/kg), and Group 4 pemetrexed (10 mg/kg) plus cisplatin (1 mg/kg). The mice were treated every other day for two weeks, three times per week. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was determined on the third day after the last treatment, followed by a necropsy.ResultsWhereas the relative kidney weight was comparable in the control vs. pemetrexed or cisplatin alone group, it was significantly increased in the combination group. Mice treated with cisplatin and pemetrexed-cisplatin combination exhibited reduced GFR. The pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused significant increases in the plasma or urinary levels of kidney injury biomarkers, renal lipid peroxidation, and nitrosative stress compared with pemetrexed or cisplatin alone. Histopathology revealed that pemetrexed or cisplatin alone had minimal effects on the kidneys. By contrast, the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused tubular degeneration, dilatation, and granular casts. Live-cell imaging showed that the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused more severe apoptosis of primary renal epithelial cells than individual concentrations.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that combining pemetrexed and cisplatin causes oxidative kidney damage at individual doses that do not cause significant nephrotoxicity. Hence, the renal function of patients undergoing treatment with the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination needs extensive monitoring.Keywords: Pemetrexed, Cisplatin, Combination chemotherapy, Nephrotoxicity

Introduction

Pemetrexed (LY231514) is an antifolate cytostatic cancer chemotherapeutic that interferes with multiple DNA replication activities involving dihydrofolate reductase, glycinamide ribonucleotide formyl transferase, and thymidylate synthase [1, 2]. Pemetrexed is used to treat solid tumors of the urinogenital system, malignant mesothelioma, and non-small cell lung cancer [1, 2]. The anti-cancer effects of pemetrexed are linked to the competitive blockade of folate binding sites, which results in decreased thymidine and purine nucleotide base synthesis and cell death [1, 2]. Pemetrexed is used as a single agent or in combination with other chemotherapeutic drugs, including platinum-based agents. Pemetrexed-cisplatin combination therapy is used to treat non-small cell lung cancer and pleural mesothelioma [3–5].

Nephrotoxicity is a significant complication associated with anti-cancer medications [6, 7]. Chemotherapeutic agents commonly cause acute kidney injury (AKI), which is often seen within a few days of treatment [8]. AKI is characterized by a sudden decline in kidney function, typically confined to 7 days [9, 10]. AKI, a component of acute kidney disease and disorders (AKD), is a risk factor for future chronic kidney disease (CKD), a condition defined as a progressive deterioration of kidney function over an extended period (> 3 months) [9, 11, 12]. AKD, a relatively new entity that can develop without meeting the criteria for the rapid onset of AKI, is characterized by a duration of ≤ 3 months, and may bridge AKI and CKD [9, 12]. Although AKI is the most delineated kidney disease caused by nephrotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, some patients treated with these medications do not experience significant kidney injury [7]. For example, the incidence of cisplatin-related AKI can vary from 27 to 100% depending on the criteria employed for defining AKI [13]. Out of the 109 patients receiving high-dose cisplatin treatment for head and neck cancer, 12.8% and 25.7% developed AKI and AKD, respectively, using the criteria of Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [14]. Of the 359 patients treated with pemetrexed, 21% had clinically relevant renal insufficiency using the KDIGO guidelines, with 8.1% discontinuing treatment due to nephrotoxicity [15]. Multiple factors that contribute to an increased risk of chemotherapy-induced kidney injury in patients include dosage regimen, duration of treatment, comorbid conditions, and processing of drugs by the kidneys [7, 16]. A combination of different agents may also aggravate nephrotoxicity compared to monotherapy [7, 17].

Common side effects of pemetrexed include thrombocytopenia, nausea/vomiting, neutropenia, mucositis, sensory neuropathy, and cutaneous toxicity, which are associated with disruption of DNA and RNA synthesis [18–20]. Pemetrexed does not undergo significant hepatic metabolism and is mainly eliminated in the kidney, with almost 78% excreted unchanged in the urine [17, 21]. Pemetrexed monotherapy and combination therapy have been reported to cause AKI in cancer patients [15, 17, 22, 23]. The mechanisms that underlie pemetrexed-induced renal toxicity are poorly understood, but many patients exhibited a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [15]. Kidney pathophysiology has also shown various degrees of tubular injury [17, 22]. Cumulative administration of pemetrexed in cancer patients heightens the likelihood of kidney injury, which can continue even after treatment cessation [15, 22].

Cisplatin, an effective and widely used anti-tumor agent, is known to cause nephrotoxicity [10, 24]. Cisplatin-induced kidney injury can manifest as AKI or CKD, depending on the severity and duration of exposure [10, 24]. The risk of kidney injury is also influenced by factors such as the dose of cisplatin, the frequency of administration, and individual patient characteristics [10, 24].

AKI occurs in up to 30% of patients undergoing platinum-pemetrexed combination therapy for advanced non-small cell lung cancer [23, 25, 26]. While hydration with magnesium and mannitol has been reported to mitigate the nephrotoxic effects [26], the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Animal models have been instrumental in uncovering the mechanisms of kidney disease [27]. These models replicate the complexity of human kidney pathophysiology, allowing elucidation of the cellular mechanisms of combination therapies that might be nephrotoxic, leading to a better understanding of their impact on kidney health outcomes.

Since kidney susceptibility to chemotherapeutic agents depends on the dosing regimen and treatment schedule, a low-dose regimen with adjusted frequency may enhance treatment tolerance by reducing side effects, including kidney injury [28–30]. Standard rodent models of cisplatin nephrotoxicity often involve the use of a single high dose (> 20 mg/kg), which frequently results in high fatalities [31, 32]. However, a cumulative low dose of cisplatin in preclinical research mirrors human use better [31, 33]. In this study, we investigated AKI in mice administered a non-lethal, low-dose regimen of pemetrexed or cisplatin alone and compared it with a pemetrexed-cisplatin combination.

Materials and methods

Animals

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center (UTHSC) approved the study protocol. Mice (C57BL/6J, 8–10 weeks old, Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used for the experiments. The mice were housed in UTHSC animal facilities, which provided ad libitum access to food and water. This study was carried out according to the National Institute of Health guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines. Male mice (n = 6, each) were randomly divided into four groups and administered intraperitoneally the experimental drugs solubilized in Captisol (sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin) [34, 35]. Group 1 received Captisol (excipient control), Group 2 pemetrexed (10 mg/kg), Group 3 cisplatin (1 mg/kg), and Group 4 pemetrexed (10 mg/kg) plus cisplatin (1 mg/kg). The mice were treated every other day for two weeks, three times per week (see graphical abstract). Glomerular filtration (GFR) was determined on the third day after the last treatment, followed by a necropsy. The doses of cisplatin and pemetrexed used were not lethal and chosen based on their effectiveness in inhibiting rodent tumor growth [36–38].

GFR determination

GFR was determined using the transdermal FITC-sinistrin clearance method as described [39–41]The optical transdermal GFR device (MediBeacon, Mannheim, Germany) was attached to the mice’s shaved flank region using adhesive patches. After a tail vein injection of FITC-Sinistrin (100 mg/kg), the device was left for 1.5 h to record its clearance. The data were analyzed with MediBeacon Studio 3 software following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Plasma and urine collection

After the experiment, blood was collected from the retroorbital sinus into heparinized tubes, which were then centrifuged to separate the plasma. Urine samples were obtained from individual mice by placing them on a 96-well plate inside a box for approximately 2 h [35, 42, 43]. Urine was collected from the wells, separated from feces, and centrifuged.

Biochemical assays

Urine or plasma blood urea nitrogen (BUN; K024-H1), symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA; MBS2605912), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL; ABCE-EL-M0828), isoprostane (EA85.5), lipid peroxidation (MDA; ab233471) and albumin (E-90AL) were determined using kits purchased from Arbor assays (Ann Arbor, MI), MyBioSource, Inc. (San Diego, CA), Advanced BioChemicals (Lawrenceville, GA), Oxford Biomedical Research (Oxford, MI), Abcam PLC (Cambridge, UK), and Immunology Consultants Laboratory, Inc (Portland, OR), respectively. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was used to determine urine and plasma creatinine concentrations at the UAB/UCSD O’Brien Core Center for AKI Research at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Urinary markers were normalized to urinary creatinine to control for urine flow rate and creatinine clearance variations.

Immunostaining

Post-euthanasia, kidneys were collected and frozen in an OCT medium. As previously described, sections of the kidneys were cut in a cryostat and processed for immunostaining [44]. Images from randomly selected fields were acquired with a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Histopathology

Histopathological processing and analysis were performed blindly by iHisto INC. (Salem, MA, USA). Kidney samples were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin, dehydrated in graded alcohols, and embedded in paraffin. The samples were then sectioned for Hematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) staining. Digital scans were obtained using MoticEasyScan Pro. Electronic slides were evaluated, and images were obtained using the SlideViewer software. Microscopic changes were graded as severity, utilizing a standard grading system whereby 0 = no significant change, 1 = minimal, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = severe. International Harmonization of Nomenclature and Diagnostic (INHAND) Criteria standards are used as the basis of evaluation [45]. All samples were well preserved with minimal to undetected autolytic artifacts, allowing for an adequate and accurate histological evaluation.

Live content imaging of cultured primary renal epithelial cell apoptosis

Mouse primary renal epithelial cells were purchased from Cell Biologics Inc (C57-6034; Chicago, IL). The cells were grown at 37 oC and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator and nurtured in an epithelial cell medium supplemented with insulin-transferrin-sodium-selenite, epithelial growth factor, fetal bovine serum, and antibiotic-antimycotic agent (Cell Biologics Inc). Apoptosis in control and chemotherapeutic agent-treated cells was quantified by seeding the cells sparingly in microplates. Apoptosis was determined using EarlyTox Caspase-3/7 NucView 488 Assay Kits (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) and the ImageXpress Pico Automated Cell Imaging System (Molecular Devices). Caspase-3/7 activation was documented after 24 h. The average number of Caspase-3/7-positive cells was determined by ImageXpress Pico software following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Antibodies and chemicals

Cisplatin was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI), pemetrexed from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ), mouse monoclonal antibody to 3-nitrotyrosine (ab110282) from Abcam, and CF488A-labeled secondary antibody dye from Biotium (Fremont, CA). For immunostaining, the primary antibody was diluted at 1:100, and the secondary antibody was diluted at 1:400.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SE. GraphPad Prism software (Graph Pad, Sacramento, CA) was used for data analysis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine whether a data set follows a normal distribution. If the data met the assumptions of normal distribution, a one-way ANOVA with the Newman-Keuls multiple comparisons test was used to compare the variables between the test groups. When the ANOVA assumptions were unmet, the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test was applied. P values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Cisplatin and pemetrexed-cisplatin combination altered relative kidney weight and GFR

Whereas the kidney-to-body weight ratio was comparable in control vs. pemetrexed or cisplatin alone group, it was significantly increased in the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination group (Fig. 1A). There was a relative decrease in the calculated GFR of mice treated with pemetrexed or cisplatin alone and in the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination (Fig. 1B). However, only mice treated with cisplatin and pemetrexed-cisplatin combination showed a significant reduction (Fig. 1B).

Pemetrexed-cisplatin combination increased the levels of kidney injury biomarkers

The plasma creatinine, BUN, SDMA, urine NGAL, and urine albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) in pemetrexed- or cisplatin-treated mice were not significantly different from the control (Fig. 2A-E). However, the biomarkers’ levels were significantly amplified in the mice treated with pemetrexed-cisplatin combination compared with control or individual doses of pemetrexed or cisplatin (Fig. 2A-E).

Control, captisol; PEME, pemetrexed, CIS, cisplatin. n = 6 each; *P < 0.05 vs. Control; #P < 0.05 vs. PEME and CIS

Pemetrexed-cisplatin combination increased renal oxidative stress

Urine isoprostane levels were comparable in control vs. pemetrexed or cisplatin alone group (Fig. 3A). There were increases in urine malondialdehyde (MDA) in pemetrexed- or cisplatin-treated mice, but the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3B). The intensity of 3-nitrotyrosine staining in the kidneys of pemetrexed- or cisplatin-treated mice was also similar to the control group (Fig. 3C and D). However, isoprostane, MDA, and 3-nitrotyrosine levels in the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination group were significantly higher than the control or individual doses of pemetrexed or cisplatin (Fig. 3A-D).

Pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused morphological kidney damage

Figure 4A and B indicate no significant histopathological changes in the control group. In the pemetrexed alone group, 5 out of 6 mice showed no substantial findings. One pemetrexed-treated mouse showed histologic changes indicative of ascending pyelonephritis, renal-tubular dilation, granular casts, and epithelial cell necrosis. In the cisplatin group, no remarkable findings were observed in 5 out of 6 mice, but one mouse showed minimal tubular-epithelial cell vacuolar degeneration. Renal-tubular dilation, granular casts, and epithelial cell necrosis were absent in all the mice treated with cisplatin. Mice in the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination group showed extensive vacuolar degeneration of the epithelial cells from the cortex (cytoplasmic and micro-and macrovesicular), involving mainly the proximal convoluted tubules and the outer stripe of the outer medulla. Vacuolar degeneration of the epithelial cells ranged from ˂5% to ˃80% of the cortical tubules. Renal-tubular dilation and granular casts were present in 5 out of 6 samples. One sample had minimal epithelial cell necrosis, sloughing, and karyomegaly. The combined injury score shows that morphological kidney damage in the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination group was significantly higher than the control or individual doses of pemetrexed or cisplatin (Fig. 4A and B).

Pemetrexed-cisplatin combination induced renal epithelial cell apoptosis

The fluorogenic caspase 3/7 DNA dye is non-fluorescent in non-apoptotic cells. However, it permeates the cells and binds to nuclear DNA, producing green fluorescence if the cell is apoptotic. Unlike cisplatin, the effects of pemetrexed on cultured renal epithelial cells are unknown. Hence, we used concentrations ≥ 10-fold less than the cytotoxic concentration of cisplatin we have previously reported in a human proximal tubule cell line [34, 35]. Whereas pemetrexed slightly increased caspase 3/7 activation at low concentrations of 0.1 µM, cisplatin did not (Fig. 5B). Treatment of the cells with 1 and 3 µM of both compounds alone caused higher caspase 3/7 activation (Fig. 5C and D). The combination of pemetrexed and cisplatin caused marked apoptosis across all concentrations, significantly more than that of pemetrexed or cisplatin alone (Fig. 5A-D).

Discussion

Cancer treatment approaches involve using a combination of different drugs to target cancer cells from multiple angles or through various mechanisms [46]. This strategy is often employed to achieve better therapeutic outcomes by increasing the effectiveness of treatment, minimizing the likelihood of drug resistance, and enhancing the overall prospects for managing or eliminating cancer [46]. Nephrotoxicity by platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents, especially cisplatin, is well known. Despite clinical studies on the therapeutic efficacy of the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination, the extent of renal injury caused by its use is poorly understood. It is also unknown if pemetrexed monotherapy is more nephrotoxic than its combination with other drugs. We report here that individual non-lethal low doses of pemetrexed and cisplatin had minimal effects on mouse kidney function unless combined. We show that (1) unlike the pemetrexed or cisplatin alone group, mice treated with the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination exhibited increased relative kidney weight; (2) mice treated with cisplatin and pemetrexed-cisplatin combination exhibited reduced GFR; and (3) the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused marked increases in the plasma or urinary levels of kidney injury biomarkers, renal lipid peroxidation, nitrosative stress, and kidney degeneration compared with pemetrexed or cisplatin alone. These in vivo findings were supported by marked apoptosis caused by the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination in cultured primary renal tubular epithelial cells.

Whereas the mechanisms of cisplatin nephrotoxicity have been investigated in hundreds of animal models, the effects of pemetrexed on the kidney are unknown beyond clinical observations. GFR is widely acknowledged as the foremost indicator of kidney function and constitutes a significant index of kidney health [47]. Kidney weight relative to body weight and the score indicating pathological damage are dependable indicators for predicting nephrotoxicity [48]. An increase in the kidney-to-body weight ratio may reflect kidney enlargement due to injury, inflammation, or compensatory mechanisms in response to toxic insults and a decrease in body weight [48]. Characteristic features of AKI, which have many provenances, also include increased blood levels of nitrogenous wastes and blood or urine levels of predictive biomarkers such as NGAL and SDMA [49, 50]. Urinary albumin is an established diagnostic and prognostic marker of CKD progression [51]. Studies have also indicated that albuminuria can detect AKI efficiently [51, 52]. At the individual doses used in vivo in this study, only cisplatin showed a significant decrease in GFR. Increases in kidney injury biomarkers (plasma creatinine, BUN, SDMA, urine NGAL, and urine ACR) were minuscule at individual doses of pemetrexed and cisplatin. However, combining pemetrexed with cisplatin significantly increased relative kidney weight compared with the control and individual doses of the compounds. The pemetrexed-cisplatin combination also increased biomarkers in ways consistent with kidney injury. Tubular damage in mice treated with the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination correlates with increased NGAL, a tubular injury biomarker [53].

Oxidative damage to biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, and DNA, by chemotherapeutic agents contributes to the mechanisms that underlie nephrotoxicity [54, 55]. Oxidative injury occurs when an uneven equilibrium exists between free radical generation and antioxidant protection [56]. The production of free radicals, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), is a major mechanism of AKI and CKD, as they are causal agents of renal cell injury and death [57, 58]. The actions of ROS and RNS on biomolecules generate measurable distinctive compounds that can be used as biomarkers of oxidative damage [59]. For example, oxidative degradation of lipids in which free radicals oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids within cell membranes is described as lipid peroxidation and produces isoprostanes and MDA [59, 60]. Nitrosative stress, which causes mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis, produces nitrotyrosine [61, 62]. Like their effects on kidney injury biomarkers, individual doses of pemetrexed and cisplatin did not significantly increase urinary isoprostanes and MDA and renal 3-nitrotyrosine levels in the mice. By contrast, the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination significantly increased the biomarkers in the mice. These data suggest that the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination caused kidney injury by increasing the production of free radicals in the kidneys. Cisplatin and pemetrexed generate free radicals, which may derange mitochondrial function, causing oxidative damage [63, 64]. The aggravation of nephrotoxicity during a combination of low-dose pemetrexed and cisplatin may intensify kidney damage due to overlapping mechanisms. This phenomenon can arise from synergistic effects, as when drugs with nephrotoxic potentials are combined, their harmful effects on the kidneys, including damage by oxyradicals, may be amplified [65, 66].

Pemetrexed and cisplatin induce apoptosis in cultured primary renal epithelial cells in a concentration-dependent manner. However, cell death caused by the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination was significantly higher than individual concentrations. Pemetrexed and cisplatin are excreted in the kidney [17, 21, 67]. Hence, the more significant kidney injury caused by their combination regimen could be because both drugs act on tubular epithelium to cause cell death.

This study investigated whether combining pemetrexed and cisplatin is more nephrotoxic than monotherapy. We used individual doses of cisplatin and pemetrexed, which have been reported to inhibit rodent tumor growth [36–38]. Perhaps higher doses of pemetrexed are nephrotoxic. Hence, studies involving dose ramp-up regimens and interventions to inhibit specific cellular signaling will provide a better understanding of pemetrexed nephropathy beyond the combination with cisplatin. Studying the nephrotoxic actions of cisplatin and pemetrexed in tumor-bearing animal models may also offer substantial insights into the pathogenesis of the drugs on the kidneys.

Cancer chemotherapy-related nephrotoxicity is a burning issue that critically affects the treatment and management of cancer patients. Pemetrexed and cisplatin are among the most widely used cancer chemotherapeutics. Both agents, particularly their combination, demonstrated robust therapeutic ability . Despite the clinical data on the renal toxicity of pemetrexed, there is a lack of information on the mechanisms of its nephrotoxicity using animal models. Here, we provide novel comparative findings on the nephrotoxic effect of individual low doses of pemetrexed and cisplatin and their combination. Our results indicate that combining pemetrexed and cisplatin promotes considerable kidney damage at individual doses that do not cause significant nephrotoxicity. Therefore, patients undergoing treatment with the pemetrexed-cisplatin combination should be considered a population deserving of extended renal function monitoring, given the potential risk of risk AKI and AKD progressing to CKD.
