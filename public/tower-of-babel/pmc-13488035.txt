Dyadic physiological synchrony and maternal anxiety regulate infant autonomic and neural function
Authors: Annie Aitken, Maggie Zhang, Stephen Braren, Amy Hume, Natalie H Brito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaregiver-infant physiological synchrony has been proposed as a mechanism through which early caregiving interactions shape regulatory systems and facilitate the transmission of emotional signals within dyads. However, it remains unclear how synchrony interacts with maternal emotional context and developmental timing to influence infant neurophysiological development. Specifically, we examined the effects of maternal anxiety and mother–infant physiological synchrony at 3 months postpartum on infant autonomic regulation and neural function. Infant physiological regulation was indexed by respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), and neural function was indexed by aperiodic EEG slope and theta–beta ratio measured at 3 and 9 months. Surrogate control analyses confirmed that physiological synchrony significantly exceeded chance at 3 months. Further, higher maternal anxiety was associated with lower infant RSA and steeper aperiodic slopes across both timepoints. Developmental timing modulated these associations: anxiety effects on RSA and aperiodic slope were stronger at 9 months, whereas the associations of physiological synchrony with RSA and aperiodic slope were age-dependent, with concurrent effects at 3 months that attenuated by 9 months. Finally, physiological synchrony moderated the effect of maternal anxiety on infant RSA in a context-dependent manner – among high-synchrony dyads, greater anxiety predicted lower RSA, while no such association emerged among low-synchrony dyads. Interestingly, there were no main or interacting effects of synchrony or anxiety on theta-beta ratio. Together, these findings highlight the timing- and context-dependent role of physiological synchrony in shaping infant neural and autonomic development, suggesting that synchrony may both support regulation and intensify the transmission of maternal emotional states.Keywords: EEG, RSA, Physiological Synchrony

Background

Infant development unfolds not in isolation, but within a unique social niche shaped by environmental demands and cues. At the center of this niche are interactions with the caregiver, which operate across multiple levels. Behaviorally, caregivers scaffold infants’ emerging emotional regulation, and physiologically, they engage in dynamic cardiac and autonomic attunement (Bornstein and Esposito, 2023; Calkins, 2011; Fogel et al., 1992). During early infancy, when independent regulatory mechanisms are still immature, caregivers support the infant’s physiological stability through a process known as co-regulation. Through repeated experiences of co-regulation, the infant’s autonomic nervous system becomes progressively organized and calibrated to the social environment (Lunkenheimer et al., 2021, Porter et al., 2022). Over time, these processes may contribute to individual differences in emotional reactivity, attentional control, and stress responsivity.

The caregiver–infant relationship is a multidimensional and dynamic process that unfolds through moment-to-moment interactions across development (Abney et al., 2021, Calkins, 2011, Feldman, 2007a, Lunkenheimer et al., 2021; S. Wass et al., 2024). These real-time interactions establish the dyadic bond and are characterized by synchrony across behavioral, emotional, and physiological domains. From a biopsychosocial perspective, synchrony emerges through repeated caregiver–infant exchanges as a mechanism of attunement for the transmission of emotional cues between caregiver and infant (Feldman, 2007b). Physiological synchrony refers explicitly to the coordination between the caregiver and the infant's physiological signals. This synchrony can manifest through the coupling of heart rate, cortisol, or autonomic activity (Braren et al., 2021). While the occurrence of synchrony has been consistently demonstrated, its mechanistic role remains less well established. Theoretical accounts suggest that coordinated autonomic states play a key role in supporting the development of infant biological regulation and shaping the extent to which maternal emotional states are transmitted to the infant across development relative to dyads with weaker linkage.

Another hypothesized evolutionary-adaptive role of caregiver–infant physiological synchrony is the transmission of emotional cues from caregiver to infant. Although postpartum mood and anxiety disorders are known to influence infant neurodevelopment, this association is not deterministic, and considerable variability exists in whether and how infants are negatively affected (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024, Hakanen et al., 2019, O’Donnell et al., 2014, Riva Crugnola et al., 2016). Physiological synchrony represents a likely pathway contributing to this variability by either buffering infants from, or sensitizing them to, the caregiver’s mental state. Prior research has provided evidence for an amplifying role of physiological synchrony, which intensifies the relations between parenting characteristics and their children’s neural and social-emotional outcomes (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024, Lan et al., 2024, Oshri et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2024). Due to the unique emotional and physiological components of arousal regulation and appraisal that characterize anxiety, this emotional state is particularly important to examine in the context of physiological synchrony and the scaffolding of infant regulatory development and neural function. In addition, although the field has largely focused on postpartum depression when considering maternal mental health, maternal anxiety remains a lesser-studied yet equally critical component of postpartum wellbeing that is often overlooked (Lemus et al., 2022, Pérez et al., 2025). Taken together, existing evidence suggests that the dynamic shared physiological and emotional experiences of caregivers and their infants likely play a significant role in shaping infant regulatory development.

Infant reactivity and regulation can be assessed at the biological level early in life by measuring both autonomic and neural activity. The autonomic nervous system is a branch of the peripheral nervous system, whereas the brain is part of the central nervous system. Although these systems serve distinct functional roles, they are tightly interconnected through bidirectional pathways. Both the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the ANS influence neural activity, while the brain simultaneously sends top-down signals to the autonomic system to coordinate regulatory responses to environmental demands (Arnsten, 2009). Parasympathetic regulation is commonly indexed by baseline respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), a well-established marker of vagal-mediated parasympathetic activity (Beauchaine, 2015, Madden-Rusnak et al., 2023; S. W. Porges, 1995). Over time, as autonomic regulation increases across development, it may scaffold neural processes that support emotional, social, and cognitive regulation. In this way, variability in caregiver co-regulation can exert profound effects on infant neurodevelopment. Indeed, higher baseline RSA in infancy has been linked to enhanced cognitive, social, and emotional regulation across development (McQuade and Breaux, 2017, Porges, 2003, Porges, 2014, Xu et al., 2024).

Neural activity can be examined through both aperiodic and periodic features of the electroencephalography (EEG) signal. A key aperiodic parameter is the 1/f aperiodic power spectral density (PSD) slope, which has been proposed to index the balance of neural inhibition and excitation (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2023, Ostlund et al., 2022, Wilkinson et al., 2024). Recent evidence suggests that the 1/f aperiodic slope indexes neural plasticity states. Steeper PSD slopes have been associated with higher concentrations of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) – a well-recognized regulator of synaptic plasticity (Fritschy and Panzanelli, 2014) – and lower concentrations of glutamate (Brake et al., 2024, Sheldon et al., 2026). Greater GABAergic inhibition has been shown to correspond with increased capacity for experience-dependent circuit reorganization, and developmental changes in E/I balance follow patterns of change as inhibitory circuits mature, though these patterns are still being characterized and are not necessarily linear (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2023, Rico-Picó et al., 2023, Schaworonkow and Voytek, 2021, Wilkinson et al., 2024). Research therefore does not suggest that aperiodic slope is globally adaptive or maladaptive; the interpretation likely depends on developmental timing and context. In parallel, periodic EEG activity is often characterized by the theta–beta ratio, a widely used measure of low-to-high frequency EEG power (Anderson and Perone, 2018, Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024; J. Chen et al., 2023; Rodríguez-Martínez et al., 2015). A lower theta-beta ratio has been shown to robustly predict later lower cognitive control abilities as well as risk for ADHD (Begum-Ali et al., 2022) and is considered an indicator of immature thalamocortical connectivity (Wilkinson et al., 2024, Hughes and Crunelli, 2007, Orekhova et al., 1999).

Studying how infant autonomic and neural functioning are shaped by early social regulation is critical, given that the early organization of the ANS and neural systems provides the foundation for basic cognitive and emotional processes, which in turn support later-developing reasoning, emotion regulation, and social functioning (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2019). Differences in autonomic functioning may bias developing neural networks toward more reactive or more reflective patterns of responding, thereby supporting or undermining emerging self-regulation capacities (de Barbaro et al., 2017; S. V. Wass et al., 2016; Wass, Samuel et al., 2020). Accordingly, early experience-dependent changes in physiology and brain function may contribute to individual variability across virtually all domains of development. Understanding both autonomic and neural processes is particularly important because the ANS and the brain are dynamically and bidirectionally linked, yet their development and peak periods of plasticity may unfold on different timescales (Aitken et al., 2025, Dollar et al., 2020, Marshall et al., 2002). Prior research suggests that although infant autonomic and neural development are interrelated, they may not be shaped by the environment to the same extent, or within the same developmental window (Bazhenova et al., 2007).

Prior work from our group has provided preliminary evidence that maternal-infant physiological synchrony moderates pathways linking maternal and infant physiology (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024). Specifically, synchrony moderated the association between maternal resting heart rate variability (HRV) and infant HRV, as well as the indirect pathway from maternal HRV to infant theta–beta ratio via infant HRV. These findings suggest a mechanistic role of physiological synchrony in shaping how maternal stress physiology influences infant stress physiology and neural activity. Despite these initial insights, it is currently unknown the extent to which synchrony moderates the impact of maternal mental health symptoms on infant physiological and neural outcomes. Furthermore, there remains limited evidence on the direct effects of maternal-infant physiological synchrony in predicting infant physiological and neural development across early infancy.

The 3- to 9-month window is theoretically meaningful for examining the joint contributions of maternal anxiety and dyadic physiological synchrony to infant neurophysiological development. This period marks a critical transition in infant self-regulation. At 3 months, infants rely heavily on caregiver-mediated co-regulation and their autonomic and neural systems are highly sensitive to external input (Kiel et al., 2024, Smith et al., 2023). By 9 months, infants exhibit increasingly endogenous regulatory capacities, including improved attentional control, the emergence of goal-directed behavior, and greater capacity to modulate arousal independently (Blair and Raver, 2012). Concurrently, this window encompasses substantial maturation of cortical excitatory–inhibitory dynamics and parasympathetic regulation (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2023, Rico-Picó et al., 2023, Schaworonkow and Voytek, 2021, Wilkinson et al., 2024). Examining this period therefore allows us to capture both the moment when caregiver scaffolding is most salient and a later point at which early caregiving experiences may have begun to consolidate into more enduring regulatory phenotypes.

Current study

The extent to which physiological synchrony is present during physical touch in infancy is not well understood. Therefore, our first analytic goal was to quantify physiological synchrony at 3 months by comparing observed dyads with randomly paired surrogate dyads as a methodological validation. Next, we aimed to fill gaps in the literature by examining direct and time-dependent effects of maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony on infant physiological regulation and neural function at 3- and 9-months of age. Finally, we tested whether physiological synchrony moderated associations between maternal anxiety and infant outcomes.

Based on prior work linking maternal distress to lower infant parasympathetic regulation and altered cortical function and moderation by synchrony (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024), we hypothesized (a) that higher maternal anxiety would be associated with lower infant RSA and steeper aperiodic slopes across infancy, and (b) that higher dyadic physiological synchrony would be associated with higher infant RSA, with the strongest concurrent effects at 3 months, consistent with the developmental salience of caregiver co-regulation at this age (Kiel et al., 2024, Smith et al., 2023). Because physiological and neural systems may differ in their sensitivity to environmental input and in their developmental timing (Bazhenova et al., 2007), we further anticipated that the timing and strength of associations could differ across RSA, aperiodic slope, and theta–beta ratio. In addition, because prior work supports both buffering and amplifying roles for physiological synchrony in moderating parenting effects (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024, Lan et al., 2024, Oshri et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2024), we treated the direction of the synchrony × anxiety interaction as an exploratory question, testing whether synchrony would buffer against or amplify the effects of maternal anxiety on infant physiological and neural outcomes.

The current study leverages a multimodal dataset that includes concurrent recordings of maternal and infant electrocardiography (ECG) and infant electroencephalography (EEG) collected from dyads at 3 months of age. During the 3-month assessment, dyadic ECG was recorded while infants sat on their caregivers’ laps, providing a condition of physical contact without face-to-face engagement. A subset of infants also completed a 9-month laboratory visit, contributing additional ECG and EEG data. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) served as an index of infant physiological regulation, whereas neural function was characterized using both the aperiodic power spectral density (PSD) slope and the periodic theta–beta ratio.

Methods

Participants

Families were recruited through community events, family services, healthcare providers, and flyers distributed at local businesses in the New York metropolitan area. To be eligible for the present study, participants were excluded if their child was born before 36 weeks' gestation, had multiple births, or had a developmental disorder. We recruited 104 participants when infants were 3-months of age (Age M = 3.46, SD = 0.37). Data loss occurred at various stages of data collection and processing, resulting in a usable sample of N = 84 at 3 months and N = 40 at 9 months (See Protocol). Out of the 104 originally recruited, 78 infants provided usable EEG and 84 dyads provided usable ECG. At the 9-month visit, 40 infants provided usable EEG data and 36 dyads provided usable ECG data. Attrition in the study sample is primarily attributed to the onset of the COVID−19 pandemic. Detailed demographic information for the participants is presented in Table 1. The sample was racially and ethnically diverse. Caregivers reported, on average, moderate household income (approximately five times the federal poverty threshold) and relatively high educational attainment, corresponding to some college to college-level education. The infant sample included a slight overrepresentation of male infants See related publications describing the sample: (Brandes-Aitken et al., 2024, Brandes-Aitken et al., 2023, Brito et al., 2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian prior to any assessments or data collection. All research procedures were approved by the New York University Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Protocol

Participants were enrolled in a larger longitudinal study between May 2018 and December 2019, with longitudinal data collection continuing through March 2020, when in-person assessments were halted due to the onset of the COVID−19 pandemic. This timeline accounts for the substantially smaller 9-month sample relative to the 3-month sample. Families visited the lab when infants were approximately 3 and 9 months of age. During each visit, infants were seated on their caregiver’s lap while the dyad viewed a neutral computer stimulus to establish a baseline physiological condition. During this task, ECG was recorded from both the caregiver and infant, and EEG was simultaneously recorded from the infant. Caregivers also completed questionnaires assessing sociodemographic characteristics and mental health. To assess potential attrition-related bias, we conducted independent-samples t-tests examining differences in caregiver anxiety, infant RSA, neural outcomes, and physiological synchrony. These analyses revealed no significant differences between caregivers and infants who completed both the 3- and 9-month laboratory visits and those who participated only at 3 months (p-values >.25).

Measures

EEG data acquisition & processing

Resting EEG data were acquired while infants watched a video of engaging, non-social stimuli (e.g., bubbles, spinning wheel) while seated on their caregivers’ laps. The recording room was dimly lit, and an experimenter was nearby to soothe the infant with bubbles or a toy if the infant became too fussy. Infants provided between 42 and 600 s (M=406, SD=142) of baseline data. EEG was recorded using a 64-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensory Net (Electrical Geodesic, Inc., Eugene, OR) and amplifier (Electrical Geodesic, Inc., Eugene, OR; EB NEURO S.p.A., Firenze, Italy). Electrode impedances were kept below 100 KΩ and the sampling rate was recorded at 1000 Hz.

All EEG files were processed in batch using an electroencephalography automated processing platform (BEAPP) software to ensure standardization in data processing and cleaning across all files (Levin et al., 2018). Continuous resting EEG files were converted from NetStation format to Matlab format. Data preprocessing was carried out using the Harvard Automated Processing Pipeline for EEG (HAPPE V.1), an automated preprocessing pipeline designed for infant EEG data (L. J. Gabard-Durnam et al., 2018). First, a 1 Hz high-pass and 100 Hz low-pass filter was applied to each EEG recording. Second, the data, which was originally sampled at 1000 Hz was resampled with interpolation to 250 Hz, following guidelines for further HAPPE processing. The third step involved artifact removal and included CleanLine’s multitaper approach to removing 60 Hz electrical noise, bad channel rejection, and wavelet-enhanced ICA for artifact rejection with automated component rejection through the Multiple Artifact Rejection Algorithm in EEGLAB (Winkler et al., 2011). A subset of spatially distributed electrodes was selected for analysis with MARA: E2, E3, E5, E9, E10, E11, E12, E13, E14, E18, E20, E24, E25, E28, E30, E31, E35, E39, E40, E42, E44, E48, E50, E52, E57, E58, E59, E60 (NetStation Geodesic 64- Channel Net). Bad channels that were initially rejected were repopulated using spherical interpolation to reduce bias in re-referencing, and the signal was mean detrended. Finally, each EEG file was segmented into 2-second windows, and each segment was subsequently evaluated for residual artifacts. On average, 24 segments were rejected per file (SD=10, range= 0–48). Segment rejection thresholds were determined according to HAPPE’s automated rejection criteria, which uses amplitude thresholding and assessment of segment likelihood using joint probability calculations. EEG power decomposition was accomplished using Fast Fourier transformation using a multitaper windowing (3 windows) to decompose power into 2-second segments for each channel.

Power spectral density (PSD) estimates were averaged across time segments and then across whole-brain electrodes (See Fig. 1). PSD data were subsequently analyzed using a customized implementation of SpecParam (v1.0.08; https://github.com/fooof-tools/fooof) in Python to decompose the power spectrum into periodic and aperiodic components (Donoghue et al., 2020). Modifications were made to the robust_ap_fit function of the original algorithm (adapted from (Wilkinson et al., 2024) to optimize model fitting for infant EEG data.The average model fit using the modified version of SpecParam was R2 = .98 (+/- 0.2 SD), indicating that the model fit the aperiodic data well. The modifications were implemented to accommodate the spectral characteristics of infant EEG, which exhibits a more pronounced spectral knee and steeper aperiodic slopes than adult data. Briefly, modifications included adjustments to the initial aperiodic parameter guesses, modified outlier-trimming thresholds for the second-pass aperiodic fit, and expanded bounds for the knee parameter. The full modified function is provided in OSF page authored by Wilkinson et al. (2024); https://osf.io/u3gp4/overview).

(A) Electrode map showing the green electrodes included in the whole-brain aperiodic and periodic estimations; (B) Experimental setup illustrating that infants were seated on their caregiver’s lap during simultaneous caregiver–infant ECG and infant EEG recording; (C) Example dyad’s respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) time series.

Aperiodic PSD slope was estimated, and periodic power values were derived by subtracting the SpecParam-estimated aperiodic component from the absolute power spectrum. Periodic power within canonical frequency bands was then computed by summing the parametrized spectra between theta (4–6 Hz), low-alpha (6–9 Hz), high-alpha (9–13 Hz), and beta (15–30 Hz). Each frequency band was normalized to the total power of the remaining bands. The theta–beta ratio was computed as the ratio of theta relative to beta power.

ECG data acquisition & RSA processing

Dyadic ECG data were collected while infants were seated on their mother’s laps during infant EEG acquisition using a Physio16 (EGI) device (See Fig. 1). Mothers and infants provided an average of 200 + /- 72 s of resting ECG data (min=150 s, max=385 s). Data were edited and processed using QRSTool (Allen, Chambers, & Towers, 2007) to remove artifacts and identify heartbeats. R-R intervals were extracted from the processed ECG data to receive interbeat interval (IBI) time series data for infants and their mothers. We generated RSA timeseries data from the IBI series based on the approach from (Abney et al., 2021). First, IBI series was first resampled at 5 Hz, following the Porges et al. (1985) methodology. A 51-point band-pass local cubic polynomial filter was then applied to estimate and remove slow periodic and aperiodic trends in the signal. Next, a finite impulse response (FIR) band-pass filter was used to further restrict the IBI variance to the frequency band corresponding to spontaneous respiration: 0.3–1.3 Hz for infants and 0.12–1.0 Hz for adults. Following a high-frequency heart rate variability (HF-HRV) approach to indexing parasympathetic activity, RSA magnitude was estimated using the Porges and Bohrer (1990) method, which involves segmenting the filtered interbeat interval signal into discrete epochs (10–120 s) and computing the natural logarithm of the variance within each epoch. To extend this method for continuous analysis of mother–infant covariation, we implemented a 15-second sliding window, updated every 200 ms, to generate continuous estimates of RSA for both participants (Abney et al., 2021). Infant baseline RSA was derived by calculating the average RSA value across the timeseries.

Dyadic physiological synchrony analysis

The dyadic RSA signals were detrended using the ‘detrend’ function from R’s gsignal package (Feldman et al., 2011, van Boxtel, 2021). Detrending was implemented by fitting a least-squares polynomial to each RSA series and subtracting the fitted trend, yielding residualized RSA signals. This trend-removal approach isolates moment-to-moment fluctuations around slower baseline changes, reducing the influence of low-frequency drift and other non-task-related sources of shared variance (Helm et al., 2018). The detrended signals were submitted to a cross-correlation analysis, where lag−0 cross-correlation was estimated. To evaluate whether observed maternal-infant physiological synchrony was statistically greater than chance, we compared cross-correlation coefficients from the observed dyads to those generated from surrogate pairings of RSA time series. Following a surrogate analysis framework (e.g., Abney et al., 2021), this approach tests the null hypothesis that observed synchrony could arise by chance or spurious correlation. First, we generated surrogate datasets by randomly pairing each infant’s RSA time series with the RSA time series of caregivers from different dyads. For each dataset, we created 1000 non-repeated surrogate pairings and computed lag−0 cross-correlations, producing a surrogate distribution for each participant. Second, we compared the empirical distribution of observed dyadic cross-correlations to the surrogate distributions. Because synchrony can be expressed in both positive and negative directions, we used the value of each coefficient to capture synchrony strength. We then statistically evaluated whether the central tendency of the observed distribution differed from that of the surrogate distribution using a two-sided independent t-test.

Maternal anxiety

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) assesses maternal anxiety. Item responses are scored on a four-point Likert scale with higher scores indicating greater anxiety (Cronbach's alpha range between 0.86–0.95). For the current analysis, we used the STAI state anxiety total score as a primary outcome to index anxiety reflective of a mom’s recent postpartum experience.

Family and household characteristics

Questionnaires collected information on demographic factors such as maternal and infant age, race, and ethnicity. Additionally, caregivers provided details regarding their highest level of education completed and the annual household income. To assess the socioeconomic status of the families, the income-to-needs ratio (ITN) was calculated by dividing the total household income by the federal poverty line that corresponds to the number of adults and children in the household.

Analysis plan

All primary variables were evaluated descriptively for influential data points and normality. Outlier tests indicated one outlier in the 9-month RSA (z-score = −4.5), and two outliers in the 9-month theta-beta ratio (z-score = −4.6 and z-score = −3.7). Given our relatively small sample size, we excluded outlier values to minimize bias from influential data points.

As a methodological validation step, our first aim was to evaluate whether maternal–infant physiological synchrony exceeded chance levels. To do so, we compared the observed cross-correlations between caregiver and infant RSA time series with cross-correlations derived from randomly paired surrogate caregiver–infant RSA combinations. We generated distributions of 1000 non-repeated surrogate caregiver pairings for the infant participant and performed cross-correlation analysis. By establishing a baseline for coupling via surrogate analysis (e.g., Abney et al., 2015), we can compare the properties of observed dyadic physiological coupling with what would be expected by chance or spurious correlation.

For all subsequent analyses, processed EEG and RSA signals were reduced to participant-level estimates. Specifically, infant baseline RSA was computed as the mean of the continuous RSA time series at each visit; aperiodic slope and theta–beta ratio were computed at the participant level from whole-brain-averaged power spectra at each visit. These participant-level estimates served as the analytic variables entered into the longitudinal models described below.

To examine associations between maternal anxiety, physiological synchrony, and infant physiological regulation and neural function across 3 and 9 months, we estimated random-intercept models using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) with full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation to account for the nesting of repeated measurements within infants and to handle missing data without listwise deletion. At Level 1 (within-person), we modeled linear change in infant outcomes over time, treating infant age in months as the primary time-varying predictor. At Level 2 (between-person), we included mean-centered maternal anxiety and dyadic physiological synchrony measured at 3 months as time-invariant predictors. We included gestational age, infant sex, and income-to-needs as participant-level covariates, given prior evidence linking these developmental and sociodemographic factors to infant physiological and neural function (Baysarowich et al., 2025, Mckinnon et al., 2023). Income-to-needs was not significant in any model and was therefore removed from subsequent analyses to improve parsimony. To account for measurement-related variability, we included EEG/ECG recording duration in all models and SpecParam R² as a model-fit covariate in EEG-specific analyses.

Each of Models 0–3 was constructed to address a specific research question. Model 0 tests the direct associations of maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony with each infant outcome (RSA, aperiodic slope, theta–beta ratio) across 3 and 9 months (Research aim 1). Models 1 and 2 introduce cross-level interactions between infant age and each Level−2 predictor — Age × Synchrony and Age × Anxiety — testing whether the strength of each predictor’s association with the outcome is longitudinally moderated by infant age (Research aim 2). Model 3 tests the Level−2 Synchrony × Anxiety interaction, evaluating whether physiological synchrony moderates the association between maternal anxiety and each infant outcome (Research aim 3). All models include the same set of covariates (gestational age, infant sex, and recording duration) with SpecParam mean R² additionally included in EEG-specific models. Covariates are held constant across Models 0–3 so that observed differences between models reflect the addition of the interaction term of interest rather than differences in the covariate structure.

Infant age was modeled continuously in months and grand-mean centered prior to estimation, such that lower-order coefficients for maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony reflect the conditional association at the sample mean age. Interaction probes at 3 and 9 months are reported as interpretive anchors corresponding to the two assessment timepoints. We emphasize that the Age × Predictor coefficients reflect continuous moderation of the association between each predictor and the outcome by infant age, not discrete effects at separate ages.

Results

Evidence of physiological synchrony at 3 months

As a methodological validation step, we compared observed dyadic physiological synchrony at 3 months with the distribution of cross-correlation coefficients derived from 1000 surrogate pairings. A two-sided independent-samples t-test indicated that observed dyadic synchrony (M = 0.05, SD = 0.22) was significantly greater than synchrony derived from surrogate pairings (M = −0.02, SD = 0.20), t = 2.69, p = .001, indicating that observed maternal–infant physiological synchrony at 3 months exceeded what would be expected by chance.

RSA: evidence of maturation, developmental timing, and maternal-context specific effects

To test for maturational effects on infant physiological regulation, we first evaluated direct effects of age on RSA from 3 to 9 months and found a significant positive effect, indicating that RSA increases across infancy.

Second, we measured direct effects of maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony on infant physiological regulation across 3 and 9 months (Table 2; model 0). We found a negative effect of maternal anxiety on infant RSA such that higher reports of anxiety were associated with lower physiological regulation across both time points. There was also a trend-level main effect of infant sex on RSA, suggesting that males had higher resting RSA across 3 and 9 months. There were no significant main effects of physiological synchrony, gestation, or recording duration on RSA.

At 3 months, the conditional association was non-significant (b = −0.00, t = −0.02, p = .99), whereas at 9 months, the conditional association was significantly negative (b = −0.41, t = −2.97, p < .01), reflecting a continuous strengthening of the negative association between maternal anxiety and infant parasympathetic regulation across infancy (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

The current study supports theories of dyadic physiological coordination as both a mechanism that scaffolds infant self-regulation and a channel through which emotional information is communicated between caregivers and infants. Leveraging a longitudinal dataset of maternal–infant physiological synchrony at 3 months and infant RSA and EEG measured at 3 and 9 months, we examined how early synchrony shapes infant physiological and neural function. Results revealed that maternal state anxiety during the postpartum period predicted lower infant RSA and steeper aperiodic EEG slopes, but not theta-beta ratio. These effects were amplified at 9 months, suggesting that maternal anxiety may influence the development of physiological regulation and excitatory/inhibitory neural dynamics. Furthermore, 3-month physiological synchrony was uniquely associated with concurrent RSA and aperiodic EEG slopes; dyads with greater attunement exhibited higher infant physiological regulation and flatter PSD slopes. Finally, synchrony moderated the association between maternal anxiety and infant RSA – though not EEG – indicating that early dyadic coordination shapes the impact of maternal anxiety on infant stress physiology. Taken together, these findings provide some of the first direct evidence that caregiver–infant physiological synchrony not only contributes to infants’ stress regulation but also buffers or sensitizes infants to the effects of maternal anxiety on neural functioning.

Research has established significant caregiver–child RSA synchrony during interactions in infancy and early childhood (Feldman, 2012, Lunkenheimer et al., 2021, Waters et al., 2014); however, the strength and nature of this linkage appear to vary depending on the timing and context of dyadic interactions. In the present study, using surrogate analyses of randomly paired caregivers and infants, we found evidence of dyadic physiological synchrony at 3 months of age while infants were seated on their caregivers' lap. This finding could reflect the early developmental salience of touch-based regulation, which may transition toward face-to-face, socially mediated synchrony as infants become more socially engaged in mid-infancy. At the same time, the significant synchrony observed here may also reflect stimulus-driven alignment, insofar as caregivers and infants were jointly exposed to common, time-locked computerized stimuli during the task, which could induce coordinated physiological responses. These findings empirically support the theoretical work of Feldman, 2007a, Feldman, 2007b and Hasson et al. (2016), suggesting that distinct forms of dyadic synchrony may emerge across infancy both to support co-regulation and to facilitate the development of a shared social world, with each form potentially serving different developmental functions across early milestones.

Across infancy, several well-established neurophysiological developmental changes occur. Physiological stress regulation increases, neural oscillatory activity shifts toward relatively greater higher-frequency power, and the aperiodic component of the EEG power spectrum changes as cortical circuits mature. The current findings broadly replicate these developmental patterns. Consistent with prior work demonstrating increasing parasympathetic regulation across the first year of life, infant RSA increased from 3 to 9 months in the present sample (Bornstein and Suess, 2000; Porges, 2007). Unlike RSA, changes in the aperiodic power spectral density (PSD) slope are thought to be nonlinear across development. In the current study, aperiodic slopes increased from 3 to 9 months, which may reflect ongoing maturation of cortical circuitry during this sensitive developmental window. More broadly, developmental work suggests that aperiodic slopes tend to flatten across childhood and adolescence, but in a nonlinear manner that reflects dynamic changes in cortical excitation–inhibition balance (Rico-Picó et al., 2023, Wilkinson et al., 2024). Aperiodic PSD slope reflects non-oscillatory neuronal spiking and indexes cortical excitatory–inhibitory balance. Steeper slopes, associated with greater GABAergic inhibitory circuitry during sensitive developmental windows, may indicate increasing capacity for cortical reorganization and heightened neural plasticity from 3 to 9 months (Donoghue et al., 2020; R. Gao et al., 2017). In contrast, the theta–beta ratio showed strong time-dependent decreases from 3 to 9 months, suggesting that it indexes a different aspect of neurodevelopmental maturation. Both theta and beta oscillations undergo substantial change across infancy as thalamocortical networks mature and cortical oscillatory organization becomes more differentiated (Hughes and Crunelli, 2007, Orekhova et al., 1999, Wilkinson et al., 2024). Declines in the theta–beta ratio across this period may therefore reflect the increasing prominence of faster cortical rhythms relative to slower activity as neural systems supporting attention and regulatory control begin to emerge.

Our findings revealed that maternal anxiety was associated with lower infant physiological regulation and differences in neural excitatory–inhibitory dynamics across 3 and 9 months of age. Although relatively few studies have examined maternal anxiety specifically during the early postpartum period using infant psychophysiological measures, the present findings are broadly consistent with prior work linking maternal anxiety and stress with differences in infant physiological regulation (Ostlund et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2025) and neural function (Adamson et al., 2018, Valdes et al., 2026). For example, maternal anxiety and emotion dysregulation have been associated with differences in infant autonomic regulation and stress recovery processes, suggesting that maternal affective state may shape the development of early regulatory systems (Ostlund et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2025). Maternal anxiety during the postpartum period may influence infant neurodevelopment through multiple pathways. Heightened vigilance and arousal associated with anxiety may alter caregiving signals and increase infants’ exposure to dysregulated physiological cues, contributing to greater infant arousal and reduced regulatory capacity (Holmberg et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2022; Waters et al., 2014). In the present study, higher maternal anxiety was also associated with steeper aperiodic power spectral density slopes, indicating relatively greater inhibitory compared with excitatory neural activity. Steeper slopes may reflect increased neural malleability, potentially allowing infants to adapt more readily to environmental demands associated with higher-anxiety caregiving contexts (Donoghue et al., 2020; R. Gao et al., 2017). While this finding is consistent with past work linking antenatal maternal distress to infant neural outcomes it also contrasts results from an prior study which failed to show any significant direct effects between maternal anxiety and infant aperiodic slopes (Sacks et al., 2025).

Our interaction findings indicate that maternal anxiety at 3 months postpartum predicted longitudinal changes in infants’ baseline RSA and aperiodic PSD slope from 3 to 9 months. These results suggest that anxiety during the early postpartum period may have developmental consequences for infant physiological and neural processes. Notably, higher maternal anxiety was associated with altered developmental trajectories in infant regulatory functioning. Infants of mothers with higher anxiety showed blunted increases in parasympathetic regulation across infancy, with the strongest differences in RSA emerging at the 9-month timepoint. This pattern is consistent with prior research linking maternal anxiety to longitudinal differences infant parasympathetic regulation pathways (Bazhenova et al., 2007, Downes et al., 2024; B. D. Ostlund et al., 2017; Simons et al., 2019). Parallel effects were observed in the neural domain. Developmental changes in the aperiodic EEG slope also differed as a function of maternal anxiety, such that infants of mothers with higher anxiety exhibited steeper PSD slopes at 9 months. This pattern suggests altered neural developmental trajectories and is consistent with research documenting lasting effects of antenatal maternal anxiety on infant neural development (Valdes et al., 2026). One interpretation of this finding is that elevated maternal anxiety shapes the infant’s neurobiological environment during a sensitive developmental window, shifting the brain toward a heightened “change-readiness” or vigilance state that increases sensitivity to negatively valenced cues. From a developmental cascade perspective, small early neurobiological differences embedded during highly plastic periods may become amplified across development, ultimately manifesting in measurable physiological and neural divergence (Masten and Cicchetti, 2010). Together, these findings underscore the importance of developmental timing when evaluating the influence of maternal mental health on infant neurobiological outcomes.

Interestingly we found that effects of physiological synchrony on infant RSA and aperiodic EEG slope were only present at the 3-month time point. This finding would indicate evidence of a realtime regulatory role for physiological synchrony on infant parasympathetic regulation and neural excitatory/inhibitory dynamics. Caregiver–infant physiological linkage during non-interactive conditions of touch (e.g., infant seated on caregiver's lap) may reflect synchronized rhythms mediated through physical contact (Waters et al., 2017). One possibility may be that synchronized parasympathetic rhythms reflect coordinated bodily signals that are still present and left over from the prenatal synchrony that may elongate into the antenatal period (Van Leeuwen et al., 2009). Moreover, touch is an important facilitator of emotional communication and autonomic regulation (Feldman et al., 2010)). Much like the developing brain, infants’ parasympathetic nervous system also demonstrates plasticity (Cerritelli et al., 2021, Feldman and Eidelman, 2003). At 3 months of age, these systems are functionally immature and sensitive to environmental input (Feldman, 2015). Through repeated experiences, the PNS and brain becomes tuned to function optimally within its caregiving environment. Consequently, infants remain highly dependent on caregiver regulation early in life (Bornstein and Esposito, 2023, Calkins, 2011, Gao et al., 2023). This may explain why concurrent associations between dyadic physiological synchrony and infant RSA are evident at 3 months but not 9 months when infants begin to develop more independent regulation strategies.

While we did not see any significant main effects of physiological synchrony on infant RSA at 3 and 9 months, we did find evidence of an interaction effect of physiological synchrony and maternal anxiety on infant RSA. Specifically, we found that physiological synchrony moderated the effects of anxiety on infant RSA such that for highly attuned dyads, the effect of maternal anxiety on infant physiological regulation was amplified. In other words, in highly attuned dyads, for mothers with higher anxiety, infant RSA was lowest, but for mothers with lower anxiety, RSA was highest. For less attuned dyads, the effects of anxiety were not present on infant RSA. This finding could be evidence for physiological synchrony as a conduit for emotion contagion or emotion transmission, which could be beneficial or detrimental depending on the emotional state of the mother. Conversely, because maternal anxiety involves heightened physiological arousal, it may amplify the signal of synchrony to the infant’s physiological state. This finding is aligned with other research linking maternal anxiety with physiological synchrony (Smith et al., 2022). Taken together, these findings align with a broader body of research suggesting that physiological synchrony amplifies contextual influences on infant outcomes (Lan et al., 2024, Oshri et al., 2023).Therefore, although the steeper slopes observed in high- maternal anxiety, high-synchrony dyads could be interpreted as reflecting a less mature cortical profile, this interpretation is highly contingent on developmental timing and context.

While we hypothesized a similar interaction between maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony regarding infant EEG, this was not observed. Although maternal anxiety showed a significant direct effect and physiological synchrony showed a trend-level direct effect on infant aperiodic slope, the synchrony × anxiety interaction on aperiodic slope was not significant. One possibility is that maternal anxiety influences infant cortical excitatory–inhibitory dynamics through multiple pathways beyond moment-to-moment physiological coupling, such that the robust direct effect of anxiety on aperiodic slope may operate relatively independently of the current dyadic coupling context. Furthermore, anxiety-related differences in synchrony that would moderate this neural pathway might emerge more clearly during socially contingent interactions (e.g., face-to-face engagement) than during the touch-based, non-interactive context used in the present study (Feldman, 2007a, Feldman, 2007b).

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering the timing-specific and maternal context-specific dynamics of physiological synchrony’s influence on infant development (Darling et al., 2022, Feldman, 2007a, Silk, 2019). Consistent with the Age × Anxiety interactions reported above, the direct effects of maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony on infant outcomes were more pronounced at 9 months compared to 3 months. This pattern suggests that the influence of maternal mental health on infant physiological regulation may not be immediately apparent early in infancy but may emerge over time as infants transition from co-regulation to increasingly autonomous self-regulation. This developmental pattern is consistent with evidence that early infancy represents a window of heightened neural plasticity and experience-dependent shaping of cortical dynamics and peripheral physiological stress-regulation system (L. Gabard-Durnam and McLaughlin, 2020). Taken together, the moderated change-score effects observed here capture when maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony are detected on physiological regulation and neural function, whereas the synchrony × maternal anxiety effects illuminate the conditions under which synchrony shapes infant physiological regulation.

Interestingly, we found no significant associations of maternal anxiety or physiological synchrony with the theta–beta ratio. One possible explanation is that aperiodic neural activity may index a more environmentally sensitive window of cortical plasticity. Aperiodic activity is thought to index the excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance in the cortex (Donoghue et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2017), which is shaped in part by the prolonged maturation of fast-spiking parvalbumin-positive (PV) GABAergic interneurons. These PV interneurons play a central role in coordinating cortical rhythms and are particularly plastic and sensitive to environmental input during early developmental periods (Chen et al., 2018; Hensch, 2005; Le Magueresse and Monyer, 2013). The protracted maturational timeline of the inhibitory neural network in infants may lead to increased plasticity. Thus, aperiodic slope may be more responsive to early caregiving and dyadic regulatory processes because it reflects underlying E/I dynamics during a highly malleable neurodevelopmental window. In contrast, theta–beta ratio appears to capture maturational processes more robustly than it reflects sensitivity to early dyadic interactions or maternal anxiety.

Of note, both EEG recording duration and SpecParam model fit (R²) emerged as significant covariates in our aperiodic slope models. The negative association between recording duration and aperiodic slope likely reflects improved spectral estimation with longer recordings, whereas the positive association between SpecParam R² and slope likely reflects better identification of aperiodic structure when the model captures more variance in the power spectrum. These associations highlight the value of accounting for measurement quality in infant EEG analyses. Importantly, the primary effects of maternal anxiety and physiological synchrony remained significant after controlling for these methodological covariates, supporting the robustness of the substantive findings.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contributions to developmental cognitive neuroscience, several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was modest, particularly at later time points due to attrition from the COVID−19 pandemic. Future studies with larger samples and additional assessment waves would allow for more precise modeling of developmental change across infancy. Second, the present design did not experimentally manipulate interaction contexts. Future work that systematically varies conditions such as face-to-face engagement and physical touch would help clarify the causal role of these interaction contexts in shaping caregiver-infant physiological synchrony. Third, although the State–Trait Inventory is a widely used measure of caregiver distress, single time-point questionnaires may not capture the dynamic fluctuations in mental health symptoms across the perinatal period. Repeated or ambulatory assessments would provide a more comprehensive view of caregivers’ emotional experiences during early infancy. Finally, future research should extend these findings by linking neurophysiological measures to behavioral outcomes, examining how physiological synchrony relates longitudinally to infants’ emerging emotional and cognitive regulation.

Conclusion

Findings from this study provide novel evidence that maternal anxiety predicts infant neural and physiological functioning across early infancy, and that these associations are shaped by caregiver–infant physiological synchrony. Specifically, synchrony either buffered or sensitized infants’ physiological functioning to the effects of caregiver anxiety, suggesting that synchrony may operate in concert with maternal mental states as a conduit through which physiological and emotional signals are communicated and potentially shape early stress regulation. We also observed developmental timing–specific effects, including evidence that physiological synchrony at 3 months concurrently modulated both infant physiological and neural functioning, as well as a stronger association between maternal anxiety and infant outcomes at 9 months. Rather than exerting uniform effects, physiological synchrony appears to function in a context- and timing-specific manner. These findings underscore the importance of considering both the developmental timing and emotional context of caregiver–infant interactions when evaluating early neurobiological development. More broadly, advancing a mechanistic understanding of how early caregiving environments shape neurophysiological development will require integrating autonomic, neural, and behavioral measures across development. Such work will help clarify when physiological synchrony supports regulatory scaffolding and when it may amplify the transmission of caregiver emotional states within the caregiving environment.
