A multi-modal deep learning framework for enhanced breast cancer diagnosis using mammograms and clinical data
Authors: Adam M Ibrahim, Jianqiang Li, Faheem Akhtar, Anis Ahmad Chaudhary, Mohamed A M Ali, Haneef Khan, Musa Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fatality due to breast cancer is one of the most universal causes among women worldwide. The limited interpretation is due to tissue overlapping, high breast density and delicate lesion characteristics even though mammography is the standard screening tool. To overcome this universal challenges, we propose a multi-model deep learning framework that integrates mammographic image with structured clinical data (note: synthetic clinical variables were employed based on the known distributions of the risk factors due to the lack of real paired data) to achieve the accuracy in the diagnostics. Architectural developments establish promising directions for future multi-modal tactic with reliable clinical data. The architecture utilize a hierarchical fusion approach that incorporates with cross model attention mechanism and dynamic modality weighting to efficiently integrate heterogeneous features. Moreover, a region of interest care module is integrated to highlight crucial anatomical regions in mammograms. Experimental evaluations on a benchmark dataset validate a classification accuracy of 94.6% (95% CI: 92.8–96.4%) and an AUC of 0.963 (95% CI: 0.951–0.975), expressively overtaking prevailing baseline models (p < 0.01). These results suggested the significant potential of projected architectural framework as a proof-of-concept for steadfast and easily explainable clinical decision supporting tools.Keywords: Breast cancer, Diagnosis, Multi-modal fusion, Mammography, Clinical dataSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer and a major cause of cancer related deaths in women around the world with about 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths reported annually1. Early diagnosis and detection would be critical in improving survival possibilities with early diagnosis of breast cancer, 5-year survival rates can increase upto 90%2. Mammography is the main screening test of breast cancer and in the clinical applications, the most commonly used imaging modalities are the digital mammography (DM) and the digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) in clinical practice3.

Despite its widespread use, the interpretation of mammograms sets a serious challenge for the radiologists. They have to interpret intricate tissue images, detect small lesions, and learn to distinguish normal and cancerous lesions- all of which they have to navigate around problems of overlapping tissue, change in tissue density, and the uneven quality of images4. Such problems are associated with false positives, which causes unnecessary biopsy and patient suffering, and false negatives, which cause late diagnosis and treatment5. The sensitivity of mammography is usually 70-90% (in women with dense breast tissues, it is lower) and specificity decreases between 80 and 95% respectively6.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly Deep learning (DL) have become a potentially promising technology to analyze medical images (such as mammography)7,8. Most recent advances in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers show performance that matches or even increases than the performance of expert radiologists in mammography interpretation tasks9,10. However, most of the existing strategies only consider imaging data and ignore valuable clinical data that may be useful in enhancing accuracy of diagnosis. Clinical data, such as demographics, medical history, hormonal influences, genetic indicators, and risk assessment scores, may be the key information in the diagnosis and risk categorization of breast cancer11. Risk factors have been well established as age, family history, genetic mutations (e.g., BRCA1/BRCA2), exposure to hormones, and breast density are overall risk factors of breast cancer12. We can potentially have better diagnoses since the clinical data will be combined with mammographic results and a better comprehension of the individual patient.

Multi-modes that combine information in different data sources have expressed high promise in several medical applications13,14. Multi-modal models can be more effective than single-modality models because of the opportunities to capitalize on the complementary information that is generated through various modalities. Nonetheless, there are serious difficulties, such as modality alignment, the modeling of interactions between features and missing or noisy data, in integrating heterogeneous models (images and clinical information)15.

The paper discusses architectural solutions to incorporating mammographic images with clinical information. We have to admit a serious drawback: our experiments are based on the artificial clinical variables, not on real patient data. Hence, our work should be interpreted as a study of multi-modal architectures but not a proven diagnostic instrument that could be used in the field of clinical practice. We address the problems of multi-modal integration with a number of key innovations:

1. Hierarchical fusion strategy: This is a combination of both modalities at various levels of abstraction and thus the model has the ability to provide a view of complementary information at various levels of semantic levels.

2. Cross-modal attention mechanism: The dynamic exchange of information between modalities that enables the modalities to concentrate on the appropriate features of the other.

3. Dynamic modality weighting: Each modality is adjusted automatically in terms of the quality and reliability of data, enhancing its resilience to noise or missing data.

4. Region-of-interest (ROI) attention module: Provides automatic identification and emphasis on suspicious areas in mammograms simulating the visual scan of the images by experienced radiologists.

5. Handling of specialized clinical data: Processes different types of patient data (categorical, numerical and temporal).

6. Comprehensive explainability features: Gives explainable information about the diagnostic decisions of the model, and this is a vital feature in developing clinical trust and easier adoption.

The rest of this paper is organized in the following way: Sect. 2 will provide a review of related literature on the use of deep learning in diagnosing breast cancer, multi-modal learning, and explainable AI. A description of our proposed multi-modal approach is given in Sect. 3. Section 4 describes our experimental setting, such as datasets, implementation, and metrics of evaluation. The results of the experiment are discussed and reported in Sect. 5. Lastly, Sect. 6 summarizes the paper and gives future research directions.

Related work

Deep learning for mammogram analysis

Deep learning methods of analysis of mammograms have evolved considerably over the past decade. Early research focused mainly on modifying generic CNN networks (VGG, ResNet, DenseNet, etc.) to mammogram classification16,17. These initial methods considered the mammogram analysis as a simple image classification problem with minor consideration of the peculiarities and intricacies of the mammographic images.

Recent studies have initiated to integrate domain-specific information and architectural advances which can be tailored to the needs of mammogram analysis. For example, Zhao et al.18 developed a multi-view improve the classification accuracy. Wu et al.19 proposed a deep multi-instance learning model that can identify and aggregate the data from various regions of interest in a mammogram. Specifically, McKinney et al.9 developed a set of deep learning models that can perform comparable to expert radiologists in breast cancer screening, which proves that AI can truly change the way clinical practice is performed.

Recently, transformer-based designs have been applied in the analysis of mammograms since they not only have the potential of modeling the complex relationships between image regions, but also, they can model long distance relationships. The transformer architecture projected by Yala et al.20 was adapted to mammography images and positional encoding is tailored to mammographic images. Based on this, Chen et al.21 suggested hybrid CNN-transformer which combines the power of the two models to enhance feature extraction.

Despite these advances, most recent approaches still focus exclusively on imaging data, overlooking valuable clinical information that could enhance diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, many of these methods operate as “black boxes” with limited interpretability, which remains a significant barrier to clinical adoption and trust among healthcare professionals.

Multi-modal learning in mammography

Multi-modal learning has shown promising results in the number of medical applications that involve integrating information from multiple data sources. Specifically, in breast cancer diagnosis, a number of researchers have explored different way to integrate mammographic images with other modalities like ultrasound, MRI or even clinical data.

Luo et al.22 developed a multi-modal deep learning model, which integrates mammography, ultrasound, and MRI in order to enhance breast cancer diagnosis. This strategy applied a late fusion method, where each modality was trained independently and their output combined by a weighted average. Similarly, Wang et al.23 suggested a hierarchical attention network which helps in predicting risks of breast cancer by incorporating clinical risk factors with mammographic image. They relied on the attention mechanisms to detect the relevant features in each of the modalities and then combined them with the help of feature concatenation.

Rodriguez-Ruiz et al.24 investigated how AI and radiologists work together in detecting breast cancer and found out that the two methods had better performance when used together than when used individually. Yala et al.25 in another research designed a deep learning model that considers both clinical risk factors and mammographic images, showing better risk prediction and the importance of the combination of multiple data sources.

Although these methods have shown positive outcomes, they are often based on comparatively simple fusion methods, e.g. late fusion or concatenation of features, which do not necessarily account for the polysemy of modalities. Also, most of those approaches give each modality some fixed weights, which can be inappropriate when the quality of data differs among patients or modalities.

Explainable AI in medical imaging

Explainable AI (XAI) has become a highly important research area in medical systems, where explanations of the decisions of the model are most important in developing clinical trust and facilitating adoption. In the case of mammogram analysis, the researchers have proposed a number of methods to provide interpretable information on model predictions.

Visualization tools like Grad-CAM or integrated gradients, which are gradient-based, have been widely applied to highlight zones in mammograms, with a significant impact on model predictions26,27. The approaches generate heatmaps that visually explain model decisions by revealing which regions of the image were most influential.

Attention mechanisms have also become useful in improving explainability since it intrinsically emphasizes on relevant features or areas of input data28. Using attention maps, clinicians can gain insights into which specific areas of the mammogram the model is focusing on, which may help them in detecting potential suspicious areas of the mammogram, which may require further investigation.

In addition to the approaches of interpretability which are based on visualization, there are also recent studies which aim at providing textual explanations in order to describe model predictions. Selvaraju et al.26 suggested a framework that generates a natural language explanation of what was seen and what feature caused the model to make a decision, in terms of mammogram classification. These textual descriptions when coupled with visual explanation may give a more detailed explanation of the reasoning process of the model.

The feature attribution methods including SHAP (SHapley Additive explanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), have been used to measure the amount of different features to estimate the model prediction27,29. Such methods are especially effective and useful in multi-modal approaches for understanding how clinical features influence the model’s decisions. Although such explainability methods have potential, it is still difficult to successfully incorporate them into multi-modes. The majority of the existing approaches concentrate on the explanation of either image or clinical aspects alone, with little consideration of how the two modalities interrelate with each other- such as is especially necessary in multi-modal diagnostic systems.

Some of the recent works have contributed well to the mammogram analysis and processing of breast masses. Xu et al.49 proposed a multi-scale attention-guided network (MSANet) of mammogram classification, and it uses multi-scale feature extraction to enhance classification. The same group proposed ARF-Net50, an adaptive receptive field network of whole mammograms and ultrasound images segmentation in breast masses. Wang et al.51 explored learning multi-frequency features in convolutional networks to classify mammography, the authors revealed that frequency-domain information can complement the discriminative ability. Zhao et al.52 proposed an adaptive channel and multiscale spatial context network to segment breast mass on full-field mammograms, which demonstrated the significance of the spatial context modeling. Lou et al.53 presented a detailed review of the techniques of measuring breast density on the mammogram that is pertinent to the issue of the difficulties of dense breast tissue. Additionally, Lou et al.54 developed MGBN, which is a multi-level global-guided branch-attention network that is used to classify benign and malignant breast masses automatically. Pi et al.55 proposed FS-UNet, which is an encoder-decoder network that uses feature strengthening in mass segmentation in mammographs and Lou et al.56 developed DCANet which is a dual contextual affinity network of mass segmentation of whole mammograms. Moreover, it is also taught by recent developments in medical image segmentation and analysis, such as attention-based architectures57,58 and hybrid CNN-transformer models59,60 for various clinical applications, reinforcing the potential of our proposed multi-modal approach.

Proposed methodology

In this section, we introduce our innovative multi-modal deep learning approach for the diagnosis of breast cancer, which utilizes clinical data and mammograms. The architecture that has been proposed is intended to effectively incorporate information from both modalities and resolve the challenges of multi-modal learning in medical applications. The proposed approach is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The multi-modal deep learning approach for breast cancer diagnosis that has been proposed has an overall ar- chitecture. The architecture is comprised of four primary components: (1) Diagnostic Decision Module with explainability components, (2) Multi-Modal Fusion Module with cross-modal attention mechanisms, (3) Mammogram Processing Module with region-of-interest attention, and (4) Clinical Data Processing Module for heterogeneous patient information. Mam- mographic images and clinical data are integrated at various abstraction levels, allowing for a comprehensive breast cancer diagnosis that provides interpretable insights.

Overall architecture

Our architecture is composed of a five component architecture including:

1. Mammogram Processing Module: The current module uses an integration of transformer-based architectures and CNN to process mammographic images in various perspectives (CC and MLO) and obtain relevant features. It uses the region-of-interest (ROI) attention model to automatically detect and focus on suspicious areas.

2. Clinical Data Processing Module: This module will deal with different clinical data, including demographic details, medical history, hormonal attributes, genetic markers, as well as clinical risk assessment scores. The approach produces homogenous representations of features and uses dedicated processing methods of different types of data.

3. Multi-Modal Fusion Module: The of module uses the cross-modal attention mechanism and hierarchical fusion strategy to combine the features of the two modalities. It enables the dynamic interchange of information across modalities and information capture of complementary information through various levels of abstractions.

4. Dynamic Modality Weighting Module: The contribution of a given modality is dynamically adjusted by this module depending on the reliability and quality of the information. It produces normalized weights to combine features and uses quality assessment networks on both modes.

5. Diagnostic Decision Module: The module makes the final decision of diagnosis based on the fused multi-modal features. It consists of elaborate explainability elements and task-specific heads to different diagnostic tasks (e.g., classification, segmentation).

The whole architecture is trained in the end-to-end way through a multi-task learning framework with specific loss functions of each task, that are described in detail in the subsection below.

Mammogram processing module

The mammogram processing unit is aimed at extracting the pertinent features of mammographic image and combating the issues of mammogram analysis like high resolution, tissue overlaps and the small abnormalities. The images of both craniocaudal (CC) and mediolateral oblique (MLO) views are processed in the module; these two views present complementary information regarding breast tissue.

1. Mammography-specific architecture: In contrast to general attention mechanisms, our module uses prior knowledge of the mammographic features by special convolutional filters, which are used to bring up edges, textures, and density patterns that are typical of breast lesions.

2. Integration with cross-modal attention: Our ROI attention module is to be integrated with the cross-modal attention mechanism such that clinical data will have an impact on the weights of attention in terms of feedback connections.

Clinical data processing module

Our clinical data processing begins with comprehensive preprocessing:

1. Categorical Variables: We process categorical features (e.g., family history, previous biopsies) using entity embeddings rather than simple one-hot encoding. Each categorical variable c with cardinality |c| is mapped to an embedding space with dimension demb = min(50, 2×|c|). This approach captures semantic relationships between categories and reduces dimensionality.

2. Numerical Variables: For numerical features (e.g., age, BMI), we apply z-score normalization (zero mean, unit variance) based on training set statistics. We also apply nonlinear transformations for skewed variables: log transformation for highly skewed variables (skewness > 1.0) and square root transformation for moderately skewed variables (0.5 < skewness < 1.0).

3. Missing Values: Missing values are handled through a learned imputation approach. Each feature has an additional binary mask indicating missingness, and the imputed values are learned during training rather than pre-specified.

Multi-modal fusion module is the next step, which involves the integration of this representation with imaging features.

The cross-modal attention mechanism is a key component of our multi-modal fusion approach, enabling effective information exchange between the mammogram and clinical data modalities. This mechanism allows each modality to attend to relevant features in the other modality, facilitating the integration of complementary information.

The cross-modal attention makes use of the feature representations of each modality and produces attention-weighted features that combine data of both modalities. This reciprocal direction of attention flow is what enables to make sure that every modality can affect and be affected by the other, resulting in more detailed feature representations.

The attention mechanism is executed with the help of a transformer-based architecture with query, key, and value projections. In the case of any modality, we calculate attention weights according to how similar the query of a modality is to the key of the other modality. These weights are then applied to add the values vectors of the other modality hence resulting in modified feature representations that include cross-modal information. In order to make the attention mechanism more expressive, we use multi-head attention, in which the model is able to attend to the various elements of the cross-modal relationships simultaneously. The results of the various attention heads are pooled together and projected to get the final cross-modal features. The cross-modal attention operation is implemented on various levels on the feature hierarchy which allows integrating the information of different semantic levels. This hierarchical methodology will guarantee that low-level and high-level cross-modal interactions are both considered effectively.

In the case of segmentation, we combine Dice loss and binary cross-entropy loss. To be detected, a mixture of classification loss, regression loss, and objectness loss is used.

Explainability components

In order to give some interpretable information about the diagnostic choices of the model, we include detailed explainability elements which are used to visualize and explain how various attributes and areas contribute to the final choice.

In the case of mammographic features, we construct attention maps that denote areas in the mammogram that played an important role in the prediction. These attention maps are obtained based on the ROI attention mechanism and the cross-modal attention mechanism, which gives a visual description of what the model focuses on. We calculate the scores of feature importance through gradient-based attribution models, like integrated gradients29, to compute clinical features. These scores measure the input of each clinical characteristic to the ultimate forecast that allows clinicians to comprehend what factors informed the decision of the model.

We use the Adam optimizer30 with a learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of 16. We employ a learning rate scheduler that reduces the learning rate by a factor of 0.1 when the validation loss plateaus for 5 consecutive epochs. We train the model for a maximum of 100 epochs and use early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting.

To address the class imbalance problem (as malignant cases are typically less frequent than benign cases), we use a combination of data augmentation, class weighting, and focal loss31. Data augmentation techniques include random flipping, rotation, scaling, and contrast adjustment for mammograms, and synthetic minority over-sampling for clinical data.

Experimental setup

Datasets

We evaluate our approach using four publicly available datasets to ensure robustness and generalizability:

1. CBIS-DDSM32: Contains 2620 digitized film mammography images from 1566 patients with annotations for masses and calcifications. Each case includes CC and MLO views, with pixel-level pathology information.

2. INbreast33: Includes 410 digital mammography images from 115 patients. Each image contains annotations for masses, calcifications, and distortions, along with BI-RADS categories.

3. BCDR-D0134: Provides 1734 images from 348 patients with annotations for lesions and histopathology. All cases include CC and MLO views.

4. CMMD35: A Chinese cohort with 5000 digital mammograms from 1775 patients, labeled with BI-RADS assessments and pathology.

Since some datasets lack complete clinical metadata, we generate synthetic clinical variables based on distributions reported in prior studies of breast cancer risk. These include:

• Demographics: Age, ethnicity, BMI, menopausal status.

• Medical History: Family history of breast cancer, prior biopsy, prior breast cancer, radiation therapy.

• Hormonal Factors: Age at menarche, parity, oral contraceptive use, hormone replacement therapy.

• Genetic Risk: BRCA1/2 mutation status.

Preprocessing

All mammograms are resized to 224 × 224 and normalized using z-score scaling. Augmentation techniques include rotation (± 15°), flipping, and contrast adjustment to improve generalization. Clinical features are one-hot or label encoded as appropriate. Missing values are handled through a learned imputation approach with binary masks indicating missingness, as described in Sect. 3.4.

Train/test splitting

Data are split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test sets using patient-level stratified sampling to avoid data leakage. External validation is performed by training on BCDR-D01 and testing on CMMD.

Implementation details

All models are implemented in PyTorch and trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. We use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and batch size of 16. Early stopping based on validation loss and learning rate decay every 20 epochs are applied.

Case studies

We present detailed case studies for representative examples from the validation set, illustrating how our approach integrates information from both modalities to make diagnostic decisions.

Case 1: True Positive (Correctly Identified Malignancy) A 65-year-old woman with a family history of breast cancer presents with a subtle architectural distortion in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast. The mammographic features alone yield a malignancy probability of 0.78, while the clinical features alone yield a probability of 0.65. Our multi-modal approach assigns a higher weight to the mammographic features (0.7) than the clinical features (0.3) due to the high quality of the mammogram, resulting in a final malignancy probability of 0.82. The architectural distortion is accurately identified by the attention map, and the feature importance analysis identifies age and family history as the most influential clinical factors.

Case 2: True Negative (Correctly Identified Benign Finding) A 42-year-old woman with no family history of breast cancer presents with a well-defined lesion in the lower inner quadrant of the left breast. The probability of malignancy is 0.35 when the mammographic features are considered in isolation, and 0.25 when the clinical features are considered in isolation. This is based on the probability of malignancy at the end that has a value of 0.30, because our multi-modal technique uses proportions of both modalities (0.5 each). The analysis of feature importance finds the age and breast density most influential clinical variables, whereas the focus on the well-circumscribed mass is sufficiently detected on the attention map.

Case 3: False Positive (Incorrect Diagnosis of Malignancy) A 58-year-old woman with the history of the benign biopsy demonstrates with a focal asymmetry of the upper inner quadrant of the left breast. Mammographic features alone result in a probability of malignancy of 0.82 and the clinical features alone resulting in a probability of 0.45. We have estimated that the clinical features have the greatest weight because of the minor problems in image quality (0.6) and the mammographic features have a lower weight (0.4) leading to the ultimate chance of the malignancy which is 0.60. Although this is as predicted by the model, the pathology report does reveal that a benign finding (sclerosing adenosis) was detected. The focal asymmetry is used correctly in the attention map, but there is a misconception in the meaning that the model uses.

Case 4: False Negative (Missed Malignancy) The patient is a 38-year-old woman who has no other major risk factors and presents with a small, ill-defined mass in the retro areolar region of right breast partly covered by dense tissue. The probability of malignancy of mammographic features is 0.40 and the clinical features are 0.30. The weighted average of the results of the above multi-modal approach points to a 0.7 weighting of the mammographic features and a 0.3 weighting of the clinical features to provide the final malignancy probability at 0.37. Although the prediction in the model was successful, the pathology report confirms the invasive ductal carcinoma. The attention map does not sufficiently point out the mass, which is relatively small and could be because of its insignificant appearance and the high density surrounding tissue.

The case studies have shown how effective and deficient our approach is. Although the multi-modal integration and the dynamic weighting typically enhance the level of diagnostic accuracy, difficulties in interpretation of the subtle or atypical results exist especially in the dense breasts tissue or abnormal clinical situation.

Explainability evaluation

Quantitative evaluation of explainability

Although explainability has been assessed qualitatively, we have used quantitative approaches to assess the effectiveness of our explainability elements:

1. Accuracy of Localization: In this research, the ROI attention maps are compared with radiologist-marked regions of interest with Dice coefficient and Intersection over Union (IoU). Our method had a mean Dice accuracy of 0.76 (95% interval: 0.72–0.80) and IoU of 0.68 (95% interval: 0.64–0.72) which were considerably better than general attention mechanisms such as SE-Net (Dice: 0.68) and CBAM (Dice: 0.71).

2. Feature Attribution Fidelity: To assess fidelity of feature importance scores, we calculated area over the perturbation curve (AOPC) which is the deviation of model prediction to removal of important features. Our method realized an AOPC of 0.83, which is a high level of fidelity of feature attributions.

3. Clinical Alignment: Three board-certified radiologists rated the correspondence of model explanations and clinical reasoning on 100 randomly chosen cases. The ratings were out of 82% clinically aligned, 13% partially aligned, and 5% not aligned.

Radiologist feedback on explainability

A user study was carried out with 7 radiologists (3 experienced > 10 years, 4 experience 3–10 years) who estimated the explainability features of our system. The main outcomes from this study are:

1. 85.7% of radiologists rated ROI attention maps as “helpful” or “very helpful” in identification of suspicious areas.

2. The 71.4% of the radiologists rated clinical feature importance visualizations as helpful or very helpful in learning the contribution of clinical factors.

3. The visualizations of cross-modal attention indicating correlations of image parts with clinical variables were considered as somewhat hard to interpret by 42.9% of radiologists; this may be an area to make.

4. 85.7% of radiologists indicated that they felt more confident in the model predictions with the addition of explainability elements than with a black-box model.

These results suggest that our explainability components enhance transparency and trust, though further refinements are needed to improve the interpretability of cross-modal relationships.

Computational overhead of explainability

The explainability components add minimal computational overhead to the model:

1. ROI attention maps: 0.03s additional inference time.

2. Feature importance calculation: 0.05s additional inference time.

3. Cross-modal attention visualization: 0.07s additional inference time.

This represents approximately 15% additional computation time compared to a non-explainable version of the model, which we consider an acceptable trade-off given the benefits for clinical interpretation and trust.

Limitations and ethical considerations

Limitations

There are several significant limitations to our study that must be mentioned:

1. Synthetic Clinical Data: This is the most substantial limitation of the present work because instead of real patient records, synthetic clinical data are used. Although the mammographic images are real, the clinical variables have been artificially created using statistical distributions in literature. This is because the method was required based on the limitation of privacy and the scarcity of paired image-clinical data sets. It is therefore recommended that we should perceive our findings as a demonstration-of-concept of architectural innovations and not a validated clinical instrument. Clinical application can never be discussed until future work on authentic paired data is performed.

2. Dataset Representativeness: The mammographic images that are used in this study belong to certain populations that might not be representative of the global demographic diversity. The model can have different performance in other populations, especially those that are not adequately represented in training data. The growth of performance gaps between racial and ethnic groups is a critical field to be pursued in the future.

3. Scarcity of Prospective validation: We performed our assessment on retrospective analysis of available data. Their applicability should be evaluated in the future in clinical conditions to determine actual performance and to integrate into clinical practice.

4. Computational Requirements: The proposed multi-modal architecture is computationally-intensive, and training (around 24 h on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs) and inference (0.8 s per case on a single GPU) are computationally-intensive. This can restrict its use in resource-constrained environments.

5. Limitations in explainability: Although our method has explainability elements, the provided interpretations are not necessarily aligned with the estimated radiological experience and other aspects of diagnostic reasoning. Attention maps and feature importance scores are supposed to be considered as a supplement to expert interpretation and not as a substitute to it.

Ethical considerations

The development and implementation of AI systems in the diagnosis of breast cancer introduces a number of ethical concerns:

1. Algorithmic Bias: AI systems can recreate or magnify existing biases in healthcare when trained on non-representative data. The performance of our model should be thoroughly assessed under different populations and then implemented in clinical settings.

2. Privacy and Data Protection: In this research, synthetical clinical data was utilized, but in reality, the implementation would be impossible without stringent compliance to privacy laws and safe processing of sensitive patient data.

3. Proper Clinical Integration: AI systems must not take over clinical expertise but supplement it. It is necessary to have clear guidelines on how far AI predictions should be used in clinical decision- making to avoid over-reliance or misuse.

4. Transparency and Accountability: The use of AI in the diagnostic process, capabilities, limitations, and error potential of the system should be made known to the healthcare providers and patients.

5. Regulatory Considerations: Before the clinical implementation of AI systems to diagnose breast cancer, they have to be thoroughly regulated. What we have is a research prototype of what would be considered an extensive amount of validation and regulatory approval to be able to use clinically.

Future directions

To overcome these limitations, the future work should focus on:

1. Making the proposed architecture valid with real paired image-clinical data of different populations.

2. Conducting prospective clinical trials to determine actual performance and effect on clinical outcomes.

3. developing more effective implementations to lower computational requirements.

4. Increasing explainability elements to better align with clinical reasoning processes.

5. Exploring any biases that could be present and coming up with mitigation measures that would make performance among diverse populations.

Discussion

Advantages of multi-modal integration

The incorporation of mammographic and clinical data supports extremely valuable for breast cancer diagnosis based on our results. The multi-modal strategy achieves a consistent higher performance as compared to single-modality strategies across all performance metrics, thus affirming the complementary nature of the mentioned data sources. The improvement in performance is notably high in the instances that are especially difficult including those that might have faint mammographic results or those that have atypical clinical manifestation. The integration of the two modalities in such cases will provide more complete information about the situation of the patient, which will allow making a more accurate diagnosis.

The advantages of multi-modal integration are further improved with the dynamic weighting mechanism which varies the contribution of each modality depending on the quality and reliability of the data. This will ensure that the model is more modality-dependent and of quality that is pertinent and reliable in predicting across a large variety of patient populations and data circumstances.

The significance of cross-modal attention

The most crucial aspect in our methodology is the cross-modal attention mechanism, which has been demonstrated by the ablation study. This process allows that every modality focuses on the relevant aspects of the other modality as a result of facilitating fluid exchange of information between modalities, which leads to the more efficient incorporation of complementary data.

The visualization of the cross-modal attention maps can provide an idea how the model uses the information provided by both modalities. To illustrate this, the model provides significant consideration on subtle mammographic results that might be overlooked on patients with low-risk profile at the condition of high-risk clinical profile. Conversely, the model focuses on clinical variables that can have an influence on the likelihood of malignancy in suspicious mammographic findings, including age, family history or previous biopsies. This two-way communication between modalities is similar to the clinical reasoning process of radiologists who interpret the mammographic findings within the context of clinical history and risk factors of the patient. The multi-modal data combination we achieve through our approach does so in a more comprehensive and clinically relevant fashion due to the formalization of this process by cross-modal attention mechanisms.

Clinical consequences

The increased diagnostic efficacy of our approach has significant clinical implications. The improved sensitivity of about 94.1% could enable earlier detection of breast cancer, Dataly resulting in better outcomes of the treatment and the survival rates. At the same time, the higher specificity of 91.2% can help in eliminating unnecessary biopsies and anxiety of patients, making breast cancer screening programs more specific and cost effective.

The explainability components also enhance the clinical utility of our method since they provide interpretable information relating the model decisions. The attention map visualizations help radiologists to detect suspicious areas that need investigation and the feature im- portance analysis highlights the relevant clinical factors that influence the diagnosis. The natural language explanations of the model convert the reasoning into a format that is understandable to clinicians, and hence it can be incorporated into clinical pathways.

The case studies show the effectiveness of our method that can be used to make diagnostic decisions in clinical practice. Our approach can decrease the variability of the interpretation and improve the accuracy of the diagnosis by providing a more complex assessment that includes clinical and mammographic data, therefore, becoming a useful second opinion of radiologists.

Limitations and future work

There are a few limitations in our approach that will require further research, even though the promising outcomes have been received. First, the use of synthesized clinical data, that is necessary because of the lack of comprehensive clinical data. However, this synthesized data may not completely reflect the complexity and variability found in real-world clinical data. To confirm the effectiveness of present approach in clinical practice, there is a need to test it on the datasets using datasets with actual clinical data in the future study.

Secondly, our recent methodology focus on the most commonly implemented modality, i.e. 2D digital mammography. However, other imaging techniques, such as ultrasound, MRI, and digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) provide complementary data, and could improve the diagnostic accuracy. Future research should focus on the extension of our methodology to incorporate these additional imaging modalities.

Third, our explainability components can be useful in providing valuable insights in understanding the decision of the model, but it is necessary to conduct additional research to ensure that the explanations can be clinically relevant and actionable. It would be improved through the cooperation with radiologists to enhance and test the explainability components and increase their clinical utility.

Fourth, our existing approach can only make predictions on the basis of the breast, without modeling explicitly the bilateral symmetry of left and right breasts as well as the correlation between different views (CC and MLO). These structural relationships, based on graph neural networks or other relational modelling methods, may potentially improve the performance of the model.

Finally, there is prospective clinical validation which is important to evaluate the actual effect of our approach on clinical outcomes, such as cancer detection, biopsy and interval cancer rates. This confirmation would support the use of our approach in clinical practice, and its introduction into clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this research, a novel multi-modal deep learning algorithm is proposed that improves the diagnostics of breast cancer by including structured clinical data and mammographic replicas. The proposed architecture will use three major innovations, including hierarchical fusion, cross-modal attention and region-of-interest localization, to improve the quality of decisions and the quality of features representation. The model demonstrated strong generalization power through extensive testing on a variety of publicly available data, that it recorded good accuracy and AUC values on both the internal and external datasets.

The effectiveness of the critical components especially the dynamic modality weighting and cross-modal attention was confirmed by ablation studies that propelled the model to produce results. Also, Grad-CAM visualization was used to help interpret the results, and it was found that the network pays sufficient attention to clinically significant anatomical structures.

These findings support the clinical relevance of the suggested approach as a radiology decision-support tool. To facilitate the trust and adoption among the medical professionals, the latest research will focus on validation of the model in the real-life clinical contexts, incorporation of histopathological data as well as enhancing interpretability.
