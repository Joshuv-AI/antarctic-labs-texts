Clinical analysis of preoperative and postoperative anxiety and depression in patients with proximal humeral fractures
Authors: Hongfei Qi, Zhong Li, Hua Lin, Shuai Ji, Chengcheng Zhang, Bo Wu, Bing Du, Kun Zhang, Ming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the incidence of anxiety and depressive emotions in patients with proximal humeral fractures rehabilitation plans.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 89 patients with proximal humeral fractures from January 2022 to December 2023. According to the preoperative scores of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), 42 patients with anxiety/depressive symptoms were assigned to the observation group, and 47 patients without such symptoms were included in the control group. The changes of various indicators and prognostic differences between the two groups before and after surgery were compared, and the correlation between preoperative anxiety and depression and surgical prognosis was analyzed.ResultsAmong the 89 patients with proximal humeral fractures, the incidence of preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms was 47.19% (42/89). Both groups showed significant improvements in pain, psychological status, and shoulder function (all P < 0.05). The observation group had higher preoperative and final follow-up VAS and HADS scores, and lower ASES scores (all P < 0.05). The improvement ranges of postoperative VAS, HADS-A and HADS-D scores in the observation group were significantly larger than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that the total preoperative HADS score was significantly negatively correlated with the postoperative ASES score (r=-0.426, P < 0.001), and significantly positively correlated with the postoperative VAS score (r = 0.389, P < 0.001).ConclusionPreoperative anxiety and depression are highly prevalent in patients with proximal humeral fractures and are associated with poorer postoperative pain relief and shoulder function recovery. Surgical treatment effectively improves both physical and psychological conditions, with more pronounced improvements observed in patients with preoperative psychological distress. Routine preoperative psychological screening and targeted intervention are recommended to optimize surgical prognosis.

Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures are a common type of upper extremity fracture in clinical practice, accounting for approximately 4% to 5% of all skeletal fractures. They predominantly affect middle-aged and elderly populations and are mostly caused by low-energy falls or high-energy traumatic injuries [1, 2]. As a key component of the shoulder joint, the proximal humerus, when fractured, easily leads to shoulder pain and limited mobility, which severely impairs patients’ limb function and activities of daily living. Furthermore, factors such as limb dysfunction, pain stimulation and anxiety about prognosis following the fracture are highly likely to induce psychological and emotional disorders in patients [3, 4]. In recent years, the impact of psychological factors on the prognosis of orthopedic surgery has become a research hotspot. A number of studies have confirmed that preoperative negative emotions such as anxiety and depression can significantly reduce postoperative pain tolerance and impair rehabilitation compliance in fracture patients, thereby resulting in poor recovery of physical function [5–7].

The treatment plan for proximal humeral fractures is formulated comprehensively based on fracture classification, patient age and underlying health status. For fractures with obvious displacement, surgical reduction and internal fixation is the mainstream treatment modality, yet the postoperative recovery of shoulder joint function is affected by a variety of factors [8, 9]. At present, numerous studies have investigated the preoperative and postoperative psychological status of patients with fractures of the hip, knee, calcaneus and other sites [10, 11], whereas studies focusing on the correlation between psychological factors and postoperative prognosis of proximal humeral fractures remain relatively insufficient. Relevant studies have shown that due to the direct impact of limb dysfunction on daily life and work, the incidence of psychological and emotional disorders in patients with upper extremity fractures is no lower than that in patients with lower extremity fractures [12].

This study analyzed the status of anxiety and depression in patients with proximal humeral fractures before and after surgical treatment, and explored the impact of preoperative psychological status on postoperative pain relief and shoulder joint function recovery. It aims to provide evidence-based medical evidence for clinicians to attach importance to psychological intervention while treating physical injuries and optimize diagnosis and treatment protocols, so as to further improve the overall therapeutic effect and quality of life of patients.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of patients with proximal humeral fractures who underwent surgical treatment in the Department of Orthopedics of our hospital from January 2022 to December 2023.

Inclusion criteria

① Fresh proximal humeral fractures confirmed by X-ray and computed tomography (CT) examinations, with a Neer classification of type Ⅱ or higher; ② Treatment with open reduction and plate-screw internal fixation; ③ Aged 18 to 80 years with closed epiphyseal plates; ④ Complete follow-up data with a follow-up duration of no less than 12 months; ⑤ Provision of informed consent by patients to participate in this study.

Exclusion criteria

① Multiple fractures or complicated with severe neurovascular injuries; ② A history of shoulder joint diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, scapulohumeral periarthritis and shoulder dislocation; ③ Poorly controlled underlying diseases including hypertension, diabetes mellitus and coronary heart disease that may affect surgical procedures and postoperative rehabilitation; ④ A history of mental illness, cognitive impairment, or inability to cooperate with scale assessments; ⑤ The occurrence of postoperative complications such as infection, internal fixation loosening and fracture nonunion.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital (Ethics Approval No.: 202401053), and informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients.

Grouping method

All patients completed the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) assessment preoperatively. The scale comprises an Anxiety Subscale (HADS-A) and a Depression Subscale (HADS-D), with each subscale containing 7 items scored on a 0 to 3 point scale, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21. A score of ≥ 8 was defined as the diagnostic criterion for the presence of anxiety/depressive symptoms [12]. According to the assessment results, patients with a preoperative HADS-A score of ≥ 8 and/or HADS-D score of ≥ 8 were assigned to the observation group (with anxiety/depressive symptoms), while those with a HADS-A score of < 8 and a HADS-D score of < 8 were enrolled in the control group (without anxiety/depressive symptoms).

Outcome measures and assessment methods

Preoperative assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive assessment within 3 days after admission and before surgery. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was used to evaluate the degree of pain, which was represented by a 100-mm scale with a score of 0 indicating no pain and 100 indicating the worst pain imaginable, and the score was self-reported by the patients [13]. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was adopted to assess the status of anxiety and depression, with the assessment method consistent with that described in "Grouping method" section. Limb function scoring was not performed preoperatively due to restricted shoulder joint mobility caused by the fracture.

Postoperative follow-up and assessment

All patients received regular follow-up evaluations at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months after surgery, with the final follow-up conducted at 12 to 24 months postoperatively. At the final follow-up, the VAS and HADS were re-administered to assess the patients’ pain intensity and psychological status, respectively. The American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score was used to evaluate shoulder joint function, which consists of three components: pain (50 points), function (30 points) and range of motion (20 points), with a total score of 100 points. A higher total score indicated a better recovery of shoulder joint function. The functional recovery was graded as excellent (≥ 85 points), good (70–84 points), fair (50–69 points) and poor (< 50 points). The excellent and good rate was calculated as follows: (number of excellent cases + number of good cases) / total number of cases × 100% [14].

Surgical and rehabilitation protocol

All surgical procedures were performed by the same team of senior orthopedic surgeons in our hospital. Open reduction and plate-screw internal fixation was selected based on the fracture classification, and all operations were conducted under general anesthesia or brachial plexus block anesthesia. The deltopectoral approach was adopted to expose the fracture: the skin, subcutaneous tissue and deep fascia were incised layer by layer, and the surgical field was accessed along the intermuscular space, which revealed significant displacement of the proximal humeral fracture. After careful debridement of blood stasis and clots in the joint cavity and fracture ends, fracture reduction was performed. Once satisfactory reduction was achieved, multiple Kirschner wires were used for temporary fixation, followed by implantation of a proximal humeral PHILONS locking plate for definitive fracture fixation. Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory fracture reduction and optimal positioning of the internal fixation device. The surgical wound was then irrigated extensively with normal saline, one drainage tube was placed in the wound, and the incision was closed by layered suturing finally.

Routine postoperative symptomatic treatments including analgesia, detumescence and infection prophylaxis were administered to all patients. The affected limb was immobilized with a shoulder abduction brace for 2 to 4 weeks postoperatively. Passive shoulder joint functional exercises were initiated at 2 weeks after surgery under the guidance of physiotherapists, based on the fracture healing status, and the training was gradually transitioned to active exercises at 4 weeks postoperatively. Patients were advised to resume normal shoulder joint activities progressively at 3 months after surgery according to the results of follow-up imaging examinations.

The surgical procedures, postoperative medication regimens and rehabilitation guidance protocols were standardized and consistent for both groups to ensure the homogeneity of interventions. Representative cases are illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2.

Statistical analysis

All data were processed using the SPSS 26.0 statistical software package. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s); paired t-tests were used for intragroup comparisons of preoperative and postoperative data, and independent samples t-tests were applied for intergroup comparisons. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented as median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)], and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as n (%) and compared between groups using the chi-square (χ²) test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to investigate the correlations between the total preoperative HADS score and postoperative ASES score, as well as the postoperative VAS score. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were represented by Cohen’s d or the rank correlation coefficient r to evaluate the clinical significance of the observed differences.

Results

Comparison of general demographic data between the two groups

A total of 89 patients with proximal humeral fractures were enrolled in this study, including 36 males and 53 females, with ages ranging from 22 to 78 years and a mean age of 56.38 ± 11.25 years. According to the Neer classification system, there were 41 cases of type Ⅱ, 35 cases of type Ⅲ and 13 cases of type Ⅳ fractures. The follow-up period ranged from 12 to 24 months, with a mean of 16.52 ± 3.17 months. Among the enrolled patients, 42 were assigned to the observation group and 47 to the control group. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, age, fracture classification and follow-up duration (P > 0.05), indicating that the two groups were well matched and comparable (Table 1).

At the final follow-up, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score of the control group was 90.25 ± 6.89 points, which was significantly higher than that of the observation group(77.68 ± 12.35points), with a statistically significant difference (t = 5.264,P < 0.001,d = 1.12,95% CI:0.65∼1.59). The excellent and good rate of the control group was significantly higher than that of the observation group, and the difference was statistically significant (χ2 = 4.826,P = 0.028,OR = 3.05,95% CI:1.12∼8.32) (Table 4).

Comparison of the improvement amplitudes of various indicators between the two groups after surgery

The improvement amplitude of the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score in the observation group was 51.68 ± 9.05 points, which was significantly larger than that in the control group (48.82 ± 6.12 points), with a statistically significant difference (t = 2.015,P = 0.047,d = 0.45,95% CI:0.01∼0.89). The improvement amplitudes of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety (HADS-A) and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Depression (HADS-D) scores in the observation group were 4.00(3.00,5.50) points and 6.00(4.50,7.00) points, respectively, both of which were significantly larger than those in the control group (1.00(0.50,1.50) points and 1.00(0.50,2.00) points). The differences were statistically significant (Z = − 7.215,−6.982,P < 0.001 for both, r = − 0.85,−0.82) (Table 4).

Correlation analysis between preoperative anxiety and depression and postoperative prognosis

Results of Pearson correlation analysis showed that the total preoperative Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) score was significantly negatively correlated with the postoperative ASES score (r = − 0.426,P < 0.001), indicating that the more severe the preoperative anxiety and depression, the poorer the postoperative recovery of shoulder joint function. In addition, the total preoperative HADS score was significantly positively correlated with the postoperative VAS score (r = 0.389,P < 0.001), meaning that the more severe the preoperative anxiety and depression, the higher the postoperative pain intensity (Table 4).

Discussion

Incidence and etiological analysis of preoperative anxiety and depression in patients with proximal humeral fractures

The results of this study indicated that the incidence of preoperative anxiety/depressive symptoms was 47.19% among the 89 patients who underwent surgery for proximal humeral fractures, accounting for nearly half of the study population. This finding is consistent with the incidence of preoperative psychological disorders reported in patients with fractures of the calcaneus, hip and other skeletal sites [10, 15], suggesting that anxiety and depression are common psychological problems in orthopedic patients following fracture. The underlying causes for this phenomenon are multi-factorial and can be summarized as follows: First, pain stimulation—proximal humeral fractures are associated with severe and persistent shoulder pain, which readily induces negative emotions such as irritability and anxiety in patients. Second, limb dysfunction—the shoulder joint is the most mobile joint in the human body, and immobilization of the affected limb after fracture impairs patients’ activities of daily living, exerts a direct impact on their daily life and work, and consequently triggers feelings of self-doubt and depression. Third, anxieties regarding surgery and prognosis—due to the lack of professional medical knowledge, patients tend to experience excessive worry about surgical risks, postoperative complications and the recovery of shoulder joint function, which further exacerbates their anxiety. Fourth, demographic and clinical characteristics of the susceptible population—proximal humeral fractures predominantly occur in middle-aged and elderly patients, a group that is often complicated with underlying medical conditions and poor physical tolerance. In addition, feelings of loneliness and poor psychological resilience in some of these patients render them more vulnerable to emotional disorders [16, 17].

The impact of preoperative anxiety and depression on the surgical prognosis of proximal humeral fractures

The results of this study indicated that preoperative pain intensity was significantly higher in the observation group with anxiety and depressive symptoms than in the control group. At the final follow-up, the observation group also exhibited significantly poorer pain relief, psychological state improvement and shoulder joint function recovery than the control group. Correlation analysis further confirmed that the severity of preoperative anxiety and depression was negatively correlated with postoperative shoulder joint function recovery and positively correlated with postoperative pain intensity. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of previous orthopedic studies [18, 19], suggesting that preoperative anxiety and depression are adverse prognostic factors for surgical treatment of proximal humeral fractures.

The potential mechanisms by which negative emotions affect surgical prognosis may be summarized as follows: First, anxiety and depression can induce increased pain sensitivity and reduced pain tolerance in patients. Even after the repair of physical injuries postoperatively, patients still subjectively perceive more severe pain, which in turn exacerbates negative emotions, forming a vicious cycle [20, 21]. Second, preoperative anxiety and depression may impair patients’ compliance with postoperative rehabilitation. Driven by low mood and lack of confidence in recovery, patients may refuse or passively cooperate with shoulder joint functional exercises, leading to poor recovery of joint range of motion and thus compromising the overall shoulder joint function [11, 22]. Third, psychological factors can modulate the body’s repair capacity through the neuroendocrine-immune network. Anxiety and depression result in elevated secretion of stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline, which inhibit callus formation and soft tissue repair, delay fracture healing, and thereby indirectly impair postoperative functional recovery [23, 24].

The improvement effect of surgical treatment on physical and psychological status in patients with proximal humeral fractures

The results of this study showed that the pain and psychological indicators of both groups were significantly improved at the final postoperative follow-up compared with the preoperative levels, and the improvement amplitudes of VAS, HADS-A and HADS-D scores in the observation group were significantly greater than those in the control group. These findings indicate that surgical treatment can not only effectively repair the physical injuries of the proximal humerus, alleviate pain and restore limb function, but also markedly improve patients’ psychological status, with more pronounced improvements in psychological and pain indicators observed in patients with preoperative anxiety and depression.

The underlying reasons for this phenomenon can be explained as follows: surgical treatment achieves anatomical reduction and rigid internal fixation, which effectively relieves pain stimulation at the fracture site. With the gradual recovery of limb function after surgery, patients’ activities of daily living are improved and their confidence in prognosis is significantly enhanced, thereby alleviating negative emotions [25, 26]. In addition, patients in the observation group had higher baseline levels of psychological and pain indicators before surgery, resulting in a larger room for improvement postoperatively and thus showing more significant improvement amplitudes. This result suggests that active surgical treatment to ameliorate physical symptoms serves as an important foundation for alleviating negative emotions in patients with proximal humeral fractures complicated with preoperative anxiety and depression.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study suggest that psychological factors are important determinants of surgical prognosis in patients with proximal humeral fractures. In clinical diagnosis and treatment, the traditional model of prioritizing physical care over psychological care should be abandoned, and psychological assessment should be incorporated into the routine preoperative examinations for patients with proximal humeral fractures. Standardized scales such as the HADS should be adopted to screen for anxiety and depressive symptoms, and individualized psychological interventions should be promptly implemented for patients with psychological and emotional disorders.

Psychological intervention measures may include the following: First, health education—providing patients with a detailed explanation of fracture classification, surgical protocols, postoperative rehabilitation processes and prognostic outcomes to eliminate information gaps and alleviate their anxiety regarding surgery and prognosis. Second, psychological counseling—conducting regular communication between medical staff or psychologists and patients to listen to their inner needs, alleviate negative emotions and help them build confidence in rehabilitation. Third, pharmacological intervention when necessary—administering anti-anxiety and antidepressant medications under the guidance of psychologists [27]. Additionally, postoperative psychological status should be continuously monitored alongside physical functional rehabilitation, with timely adjustments to intervention protocols. The integration of physical rehabilitation and psychological intervention can further improve the overall therapeutic effect for patients.

Study limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study with a relatively limited sample size, and all study subjects were recruited from our hospital, which may lead to selection bias and thus restrict the generalizability of the study results. Second, the assessment indicators mainly relied on patients’ subjective scale scores, which are susceptible to the influence of factors such as patients’ emotional states and cognitive levels, and lacked corroboration from objective physiological indicators (e.g., cortisol levels, brain functional imaging). Third, the impacts of factors including patients’ educational level, socioeconomic status and family support on their psychological state and postoperative rehabilitation were not fully considered in the analysis. Future research may conduct multicenter, large-sample prospective studies that incorporate more potential influencing factors and adopt a comprehensive assessment combining subjective scales with objective physiological indicators, so as to further verify the improvement effect of preoperative psychological intervention on the surgical prognosis of patients with proximal humeral fractures.

Conclusion

The incidence of preoperative anxiety and depression is relatively high in patients with proximal humeral fractures. Preoperative anxiety and depressive symptoms were significantly associated with more severe preoperative pain, and were significantly correlated with poorer postoperative pain relief and shoulder joint function recovery, which may be related to unfavorable overall surgical prognosis. However, surgical treatment can effectively ameliorate somatic pain symptoms and psychological status in all patients with proximal humeral fractures, and the improvement amplitudes of psychological and pain indicators are more pronounced in patients with preoperative anxiety and depression. A significant negative correlation was found between the severity of preoperative anxiety and depression and postoperative shoulder joint function recovery, while a significant positive correlation was observed between preoperative anxiety and depression severity and postoperative pain intensity.

In clinical practice, emphasis should be placed on preoperative psychological screening and assessment for patients with proximal humeral fractures. Individualized intervention measures such as health education and psychological counseling should be promptly implemented for patients with anxiety and depressive symptoms. The integration of physical treatment and psychological intervention can reduce the adverse impact of negative emotions on surgical prognosis, optimize the efficacy of postoperative pain relief and the recovery of shoulder joint function, and further improve the overall therapeutic effect and quality of life of patients.
