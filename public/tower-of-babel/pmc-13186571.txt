Lactate, a Spearhead of Cancer Aggressiveness: Metabolic Reprogramming, Immune Suppression, and Metastatic Progression
Authors: Yunus A. Luqmani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReprogramming of cellular energy metabolism is a defining feature of malignancy, characterized by sustained glucose conversion to lactate under aerobic conditions. Once viewed as metabolic waste or a consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction, lactate is now recognized as a multifunctional metabolite that actively drives malignant progression. This review advances the concept of lactate as a spearhead of malignancy that coordinates metabolic adaptation with microenvironmental conditioning, immune suppression, epigenetic regulation, and metastatic dissemination. Beyond its role in metabolism, lactate functions through receptor-mediated signaling and histone lactylation, linking metabolic state to transcriptional programs associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition, stemness, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance. Lactate also reshapes the tumor microenvironment by promoting angiogenesis, stromal activation, immune suppression, and invasive behavior, including effects that cannot be explained solely by extracellular acidosis. Together, these observations support lactate as a critical effector of metastatic competence and a strategic target for disrupting multiple cancer hallmarks.

Introduction

From Genetic Determinism to Metabolic Governance of Malignancy

For much of the 20th century, cancer was conceptualized predominantly as a genetic disease driven by somatic mutation, chromosomal instability, oncogene activation, and loss of tumor suppressor function. This paradigm has been instrumental in defining mechanisms of tumor initiation, clonal evolution, and intertumoral heterogeneity [1]. However, as malignancies progress beyond early stages, genetic alterations alone do not fully account for the remarkable biochemical adaptability, phenotypic plasticity, and capacity of tumors to reshape their microenvironment and evade host defenses. Over the past 2 decades, cancer biology has undergone a significant conceptual expansion in which metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a core and actively selected feature of malignancy rather than a passive downstream consequence of oncogenic signaling [2, 3]. Metabolic pathways are now understood to influence not only proliferation but also invasion, immune evasion, therapy resistance, and metastatic competence [4, 5]. Within this broader framework, lactate has emerged as a molecule of exceptional relevance. Previously relegated to the status of metabolic waste, lactate is increasingly recognized as a central integrator of metabolic flux, redox balance, signaling, epigenetic regulation, and intercellular communication [6, 7]. In cancer, these functions are not merely preserved but chronically amplified, highlighting lactate as an important effector of malignant progression.

This review synthesizes evidence across metabolic, immune, stromal, and epigenetic domains to argue that the critical vulnerability lies not simply in glucose availability, but in the production, accumulation, and utilization of lactate itself. Reframing metabolic intervention around lactate-directed pathways may, therefore, provide a more tractable and biologically grounded therapeutic strategy.

Lactate in Normal Physiology: Redox Homeostasis and Metabolic Exchange

In normal mammalian physiology, lactate production is inseparably linked to maintenance of cytosolic redox balance. When glycolytic throughput exceeds mitochondrial oxidative capacity, pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, regenerating NAD+ and permitting continued ATP production via glycolysis [8, 9]. Upon restoration of normoxia, lactate is readily reconverted to pyruvate and oxidized through the tricarboxylic acid cycle [10]. However, lactate is not merely a transient product of anaerobic stress. It is continuously produced and consumed under fully aerobic conditions [8, 9], and the lactate-to-pyruvate ratio provides a sensitive readout of cytosolic redox state [11]. Through well-characterized lactate shuttles [7, 12], lactate supports intercellular and inter-organ metabolic exchange, including muscle-heart coupling, hepatic gluconeogenesis, and astrocyte-neuron cooperation [13, 14]. These roles establish lactate as a multifunctional physiological metabolite before its role in cancer is considered.

Aerobic Glycolysis and the Deliberate Persistence of Lactate in Cancer

Otto Warburg’s observation that cancer cells convert large amounts of glucose to lactate despite adequate oxygen availability remains one of the central findings in cancer metabolism [15]. Although once interpreted as evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction, most tumors retain functional mitochondria and substantial oxidative capacity [16, 17]. Aerobic glycolysis is now recognized as a regulated and selectively advantageous phenotype that enables rapid ATP generation, diversion of intermediates into biosynthetic pathways, and maintenance of cytosolic redox balance under fluctuating nutrient and oxygen conditions [18–20]. Tumors actively sustain high intracellular and extracellular lactate levels through coordinated regulation of lactate metabolism and transport [21–25], indicating that lactate accumulation is not simply an incidental consequence of glycolysis but a selected component of malignant metabolism.

Lactate as a Multifunctional Effector of Tumor Progression

Lactate functions simultaneously as a carbon fuel [26], a redox regulator [27, 28], a signaling mediator [29], and an epigenetic substrate [30]. Through coordinated regulation of production, transport, and utilization [21–23, 31, 32], tumors sustain high lactate flux that reshapes intracellular metabolism and the extracellular milieu. Within the tumor microenvironment, proton-coupled lactate export contributes to acidic niches associated with invasion and metastasis [33, 34], while independently contributing to immune evasion across multiple cell populations [35–41].

Histone lactylation provides a direct link between intracellular lactate levels and gene regulation, offering a mechanism through which sustained metabolic states may be translated into durable transcriptional programs relevant to tumor progression [30, 42–45].

Elevated lactate has also been linked to epithelial-mesenchymal transition, enhanced tumor cell motility, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, immune evasion, and conditioning of pre-metastatic niches [40, 46–49]. Experimental suppression of lactate production reduces invasion-related phenotypes in breast cancer models [50], and lactate-enriched stromal microenvironments promote invasive programs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [51]. Together, these findings support a functional role for lactate in metastatic processes and provide the basis for the mechanistic sections that follow.

Normal Lactate Biology: Physiology and Biochemistry

Lactate occupies a central position in intermediary metabolism as a continuously exchanged intermediate that supports redox balance, energy distribution, and metabolic flexibility across tissues.

The Lactate-Pyruvate Axis and Cytosolic Redox Homeostasis

Glucose metabolism through glycolysis generates pyruvate, ATP, and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate can be transported into mitochondria via the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, where it is oxidatively decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex to generate acetyl-CoA, fueling the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation [8, 52].

Sustained glycolytic flux, however, requires continuous regeneration of oxidized NAD+. A critical mechanistic point is that glycolysis depends on the availability of NAD+ at the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step [53]. When the rate of glycolytic NADH production exceeds the capacity of mitochondrial oxidation, LDH reduces pyruvate to lactate, oxidizing NADH to NAD+ and thereby preserving cytosolic redox balance [8, 9]. This reaction is rapid, reversible, and operates near equilibrium, rendering the lactate-to-pyruvate ratio a sensitive indicator of the cytosolic NADH:NAD+ redox state rather than a direct marker of oxygen deprivation [11, 54].

Importantly, lactate production is not restricted to hypoxic conditions. Even under fully aerobic states, cells continuously generate lactate as part of normal metabolic flux. Lactate should, therefore, be understood as a constitutive redox-balancing metabolite whose intracellular and extracellular levels reflect metabolic demand, enzymatic configuration, and substrate availability rather than a failure of oxidative metabolism. Lactate Dehydrogenase Isoenzymes and Functional Specialization

Lactate dehydrogenase is a tetrameric enzyme composed of LDHA and LDHB subunits, generating isoenzymes with distinct kinetic properties and tissue distributions [55, 56]. LDHA-enriched isoenzymes preferentially support pyruvate reduction in highly glycolytic tissues, whereas LDHB-rich isoenzymes favor lactate oxidation in oxidative tissues. LDH expression is regulated transcriptionally and post-translationally [4, 24, 57–59], linking lactate metabolism to growth, oxygen availability, nutrient status, and redox requirements. Differential LDH expression enables tissues to adopt specialized strategies for lactate production, consumption, or bidirectional exchange.

Monocarboxylate Transporters and Lactate Flux Control

Because lactate exists predominantly as an anion at physiological pH, its transmembrane movement requires monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), which mediate proton-coupled transport of lactate, pyruvate, and related monocarboxylates [21]. MCT1 supports bidirectional transport with relatively high substrate affinity, whereas MCT4 is optimized for lactate efflux from highly glycolytic cells [25, 60]. Functional expression of both depends on accessory chaperones such as basigin and embigin [61, 62]. Coordinated expression of LDH isoenzymes and MCTs regulates lactate gradients, intracellular pH, and metabolic exchange, enabling rapid adaptation to changing workload, oxygenation, and substrate availability.

The Lactate Shuttle and Systemic Metabolic Integration

The lactate shuttle hypothesis established that lactate is continuously exchanged between cells, tissues, and organs under fully aerobic conditions [10, 12, 63]. Rather than being confined to sites of production, lactate acts as a mobile carbon source and redox carrier linking glycolytic and oxidative metabolism across spatial scales. Major physiological examples include exchange between glycolytic and oxidative muscle fibers, transfer from skeletal muscle to heart, hepatic and renal gluconeogenesis, and astrocyte-to-neuron lactate transfer in the brain [13, 14, 63–65]. These pathways provide the conceptual basis for understanding how tumors later exploit lactate exchange to sustain heterogeneous cell populations within the tumor microenvironment.

Lactate, pH Regulation, and Signaling Functions

Although lactate transport is proton-coupled, physiological lactate production does not inherently cause acidosis. Buffering systems, bicarbonate transport, and carbonic anhydrases maintain pH homeostasis, while lactate oxidation and gluconeogenesis consume protons [9, 10, 66].

Lactate also functions as a signaling molecule. Activation of hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1 (HCAR1/GPR81) links extracellular lactate to systemic fuel regulation [67, 68], and lactate can modulate neuronal excitability, vascular tone, and tissue repair through receptor-mediated and metabolism-dependent mechanisms [14, 69]. Together, these observations establish lactate as both a metabolic and signaling intermediate.

Reprogramming of Lactate Metabolism in Cancer

Cancer cells undergo profound metabolic reprogramming to support sustained proliferation, stress tolerance, and plasticity. A defining feature of this reprogramming is persistent lactate production, even under oxygen-replete conditions. Rather than reflecting metabolic inefficiency, this phenotype represents an actively selected metabolic configuration that integrates oncogenic signaling, redox control, spatial heterogeneity, and microenvironmental adaptation.

Aerobic Glycolysis as a Selected and Adaptive Phenotype

The persistence of lactate production in the presence of oxygen, long taken as evidence of defective mitochondrial respiration, has been decisively reinterpreted. Most cancers retain functional mitochondria, intact tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, and substantial oxidative phosphorylation capacity [16, 17, 70]. Aerobic glycolysis, therefore, does not reflect respiratory failure but instead represents a regulated metabolic state selected during malignant progression. Glycolysis confers both kinetic and biosynthetic advantages, enabling rapid ATP generation and diversion of intermediates into anabolic pathways for biomass accumulation and cell division [18–20, 71]. Lactate production should be viewed not as an unavoidable metabolic by-product but as a functional necessity for maintaining the glycolytic–anabolic state characteristic of many malignant cells.

Oncogenic and Pseudo-Hypoxic Regulation of Lactate Production

The glycolytic and lactate-producing phenotype of cancer is tightly coupled to oncogenic signaling networks. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) upregulates glucose transporters, multiple glycolytic enzymes, and LDHA, while also inducing expression of the lactate exporter MCT4, thereby coordinating glucose uptake with lactate production and efflux under hypoxic conditions [58, 72–75]. Importantly, HIF-1α activation in tumors frequently occurs independently of ambient oxygen tension. Mechanistically, oxygen-independent HIF-1α activation may reflect either increased protein stabilization through impaired prolyl hydroxylation and proteasomal degradation, or enhanced transcriptional and translational induction driven by oncogenic and inflammatory signaling pathways. Such pseudo-hypoxic HIF-1α activation can arise through diverse mechanisms, including activation of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, c-Myc overexpression, loss of Von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor (VHL) or p53 function, inflammatory nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling, and accumulation of metabolic intermediates such as lactate [58, 74, 76–79]. This pseudo-hypoxic state enforces a glycolytic, lactate-producing phenotype even in well-oxygenated tumor regions.

Additional oncogenic pathways further reinforce lactate metabolism. c-Myc directly upregulates LDHA and glycolytic gene expression, while PI3K/AKT signaling enhances glucose uptake and glycolytic flux [4, 78]. Oncogenic RAS mutations similarly promote glycolysis and lactate output across multiple tumor types through transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms [80, 81]. Together, these pathways establish sustained lactate production as an intrinsic output of oncogenic transformation rather than a secondary adaptation to hypoxia or nutrient limitation. Once established, however, the lactate-rich metabolic state can further reinforce malignant behavior through redox support, signaling, metabolic symbiosis, and transcriptional reprogramming [4, 58, 74, 78–81].

LDH Isoenzyme Reprogramming and Redox Plasticity

Many cancers exhibit selective up-regulation of LDHA, supporting efficient regeneration of NAD+ and sustained glycolytic flux required for high proliferative demand [24, 82]. Beyond its canonical metabolic role, elevated LDHA expression has been functionally linked to enhanced tolerance to oxidative stress, improved survival under hypoxic conditions, and resistance to metabolic perturbation in defined experimental contexts [82, 83].

At the same time, increasing evidence highlights an important role for LDHB in metabolically heterogeneous tumors. Oxidative tumor subpopulations with high LDHB activity can oxidize lactate to pyruvate and channel it into mitochondrial metabolism, thereby supporting efficient energy production in these compartments [26, 84–86]. This functional specialization is consistent with a division of metabolic labor within tumors, enabling cooperative use of lactate across glycolytic and oxidative cell populations and contributing to overall metabolic robustness. In more strongly glycolytic cancers, however, reduced LDHB expression may reinforce lactate-producing phenotypes; in some contexts this suppression has been linked to promoter methylation, providing an epigenetic mechanism that stabilizes glycolytic reprogramming [24, 86–88]. LDH isoenzyme expression and activity are further shaped by epigenetic regulation, post-translational modification, and, in some settings, non-canonical subcellular localization [24, 87, 89]. Together, these regulatory layers position LDH as a flexible integrator of redox balance and metabolic adaptability, allowing tumor cells to dynamically adjust lactate production and utilization in response to environmental constraints.

Mitochondrial Pyruvate Oxidation, PDH/PDK Control, and Reversal by Dichloroacetate

Tumor-associated lactate accumulation is closely linked to suppression of mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation through the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. In many cancers, this step is restrained by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases (PDKs), which phosphorylate and inhibit PDH, thereby diverting pyruvate away from mitochondrial oxidation and toward lactate production [18–20, 24, 82, 83, 90]. Dichloroacetate (DCA) [70] has attracted sustained interest in this context because it inhibits PDK activity and thereby relieves inhibitory phosphorylation of PDH. By reactivating PDH, DCA can promote pyruvate flux into mitochondrial oxidation, decrease reliance on lactate-generating glycolysis, and in selected preclinical models partially reverse the Warburg phenotype.

This framework also intersects with the reverse Warburg effect, in which glycolytic stromal cells generate lactate that can then be oxidized by adjacent tumor cells [26, 84–86, 90].

Lactate Transport, pH Regulation, and Spatial Organization

High lactate flux necessitates efficient export to preserve intracellular pH and sustain glycolytic activity [89]. Tumors, therefore, up-regulate monocarboxylate transporters in a spatially organized manner. MCT4 predominates in highly glycolytic or hypoxic regions, facilitating lactate efflux, whereas MCT1 is enriched in more oxidative tumor compartments and supports lactate uptake and utilization [23, 25, 61, 75, 91]. Transporter function depends on the chaperone basigin (CD147), which is required for MCT membrane localization and is frequently overexpressed in aggressive cancers, where it correlates with invasive behavior and poor clinical outcome [61, 62, 92]. Through coordinated expression of LDH isoenzymes and MCTs, tumors establish steep lactate gradients that define distinct metabolic compartments within the tumor mass [84, 85].

In addition to contributing to invasion-associated selection pressures, this extracellular proton gradient may also facilitate proton-coupled nutrient transport and thereby support metabolic adaptation in acidic tumor niches [73, 93, 94]. However, while extracellular acidosis contributes to invasion and selective pressure within the tumor microenvironment, it does not fully account for all lactate-associated phenotypes. These observations are consistent with lactate-associated effects that are not fully explained by extracellular acidosis alone [95, 96], as discussed in section entitled Lactate beyond Acidosis; Metabolic and Signaling Effects.

Lactate as a Central Metabolic Currency in Tumor Ecosystems

Within this framework, lactate functions as a central metabolic currency that facilitates redistribution of carbon substrates, preservation of redox balance, and adaptation to spatial gradients in oxygen and nutrient availability, thereby providing the metabolic foundation for its broader roles in stromal activation, immune modulation, angiogenesis, epigenetic reprogramming, and metastatic progression.

Lactate and the Tumor Microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises malignant cells, stromal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune infiltrates, extracellular matrix, and an evolving biochemical milieu. Elevated lactate is a defining metabolic feature of many solid tumors and contributes to microenvironmental organization by influencing metabolic crosstalk, stromal reprogramming, vascular responses, and immune behavior [7, 26, 33, 84, 97, 98].

Spatial Metabolic Heterogeneity and Lactate Gradients

Irregular tumor neovascularization, high metabolic demand, and fluctuating perfusion generate steep gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolites within tumors [99]. These gradients create spatially organized metabolic compartments rather than homogeneous environments [85]. Hypoxic or poorly perfused regions rely predominantly on glycolysis and generate large quantities of lactate, whereas better-oxygenated perivascular regions can import and oxidize lactate, contributing to mitochondrial metabolism in these compartments [26, 84, 85]. Proton-coupled lactate export establishes extracellular lactate gradients that extend beyond highly glycolytic cells and are associated with altered metabolic conditions in neighboring tumor, stromal, and immune populations.

Lactate beyond Acidosis: Metabolic and Signaling Effects

Accumulating evidence indicates that lactate can exert biological effects independent of extracellular acidity, acting as both a metabolic substrate and a signaling molecule in addition to proton-mediated effects [97, 98] (see Fig. 2).

In oxygenated tumor regions, lactate can support ATP production and cellular redox balance [26, 84]. Beyond these metabolic roles, lactate also influences intracellular signaling pathways [29] and receptor-mediated responses, including HCAR1 (GPR81)-dependent signaling that does not require extracellular acidification [67, 100]. Experimental studies further indicate that lactate can enhance motility, invasion, and angiogenic signaling in specific in vitro models, supporting the view that lactate itself can function as an active effector within the tumor microenvironment [101]. Accordingly, although lactate and pH remain tightly coupled in vivo, experimental evidence supports lactate-specific effects that cannot be attributed solely to proton toxicity.

Stromal Fibroblasts, Endothelial Cells, and Metabolic Coupling

Cancer-associated fibroblasts and endothelial cells are central mediators of lactate-driven microenvironmental restructuring. Lactate reprograms CAF metabolism and gene expression, promoting phenotypes that support tumor growth and dissemination. In some settings it enhances fibroblast glycolysis and lactate production [47, 102], whereas in others stromal cells import tumor-derived metabolites and contribute to nutrient partitioning within the tumor microenvironment [86, 103].

This reciprocal metabolic exchange is consistent with the reverse Warburg effect, in which stromal cells metabolically support tumor growth and progression [104]. Beyond metabolic coupling, lactate-conditioned CAFs promote ECM reorganization and invasion-permissive tracks through cytokine, growth factor, and matrix-modifying enzyme secretion [39, 40, 105].

Endothelial cells similarly integrate lactate into angiogenic programs. Lactate uptake via MCT1 activates NF-κB signaling and stabilizes HIF-1α in an oxygen-independent manner, leading to increased expression of VEGF and other pro-angiogenic mediators [79, 101]. Mechanistically, lactate oxidation can increase intracellular pyruvate and interfere with prolyl hydroxylase-dependent HIF-1α degradation under normoxic conditions, thereby contributing to pseudo-hypoxic signaling [79]. Lactate can also serve as an oxidative substrate for endothelial metabolism, supporting the energetic demands of vessel sprouting [106]. The resulting tumor vasculature is frequently disorganized and hyperpermeable, exacerbating metabolic gradients and facilitating tumor cell intravasation [107, 108]. Lactate Gradients and the Spatial Patterning of Immune-Excluded Niches

Lactate levels likely vary across the tumor mass according to vascular topology, oxygen and nutrient delivery, transporter localization, and local metabolic activity, generating gradients that may contribute to spatial patterning of stromal and immune phenotypes within tumors [109].

Imaging studies provide direct support that lactate production and accumulation can be mapped in situ with spatial resolution. Hyperpolarized magnetic resonance imaging and magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRI/MRSI) using [1-13C] pyruvate has demonstrated regional heterogeneity in pyruvate-to-lactate conversion in human prostate cancer and brain tumors [110], while complementary imaging and paired spatial metabolomics have strengthened interpretation of tissue-level lactate heterogeneity [111, 112]. From a microenvironmental perspective, lactate-rich regions frequently align with features associated with immune exclusion and reduced effective immune surveillance, contributing to spatially restricted immune-privileged niches [97, 113].

ECM Re-Modeling, Invasion, and Pre-Metastatic Conditioning

Lactate accumulation reshapes the physical architecture of the tumor microenvironment by promoting ECM remodeling. In defined experimental systems, lactate-conditioned stromal and tumor cells display invasion-supportive programs, including increased matrix metalloproteinase activity, basement membrane degradation, and local tissue infiltration [40, 114].

These structural changes are closely linked to epithelial–mesenchymal transition and invasive plasticity. In specific experimental contexts, lactate has been shown to reinforce epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated transcriptional programs through TGF-β/IGF1- and HIF-1α-dependent signaling pathways, thereby supporting migratory and invasive phenotypes [79, 115].

Lactate’s influence is not confined to the primary tumor. Tumor-derived metabolites, vesicles, and soluble factors contribute to conditioning of distant tissues before tumor cell arrival by promoting vascular permeability, stromal activation, and immune suppression [48, 116]. Within this broader systemic metabolic-signaling axis, lactate is increasingly recognized as a contributory component of permissive pre-metastatic niche formation [98, 117].

Tumor Cell-Intrinsic Actions of Lactate

In addition to its microenvironmental effects, lactate directly regulates tumor cell behavior through mechanisms that support metabolic adaptability, survival under stress, phenotypic plasticity, and resistance to therapy [54, 97, 98, 118].

Lactate as an Oxidative Fuel and Metabolic Buffer

Although cancer cells are often described as glycolytic, many tumors retain substantial oxidative capacity. In oxygenated regions, tumor cells can import lactate via MCT1 and oxidize it to pyruvate through LDHB, fueling the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation [7, 119, 120]. This routing provides ATP, supports selected biosynthetic reactions, and reduces reliance on glucose as a primary carbon source.

Utilization of lactate as an oxidative fuel enhances metabolic resilience and helps tumor cells adapt to fluctuations in nutrient availability and oxygen tension. By allowing preferential glucose use in hypoxic or highly anabolic compartments, lactate oxidation supports cooperative metabolism within heterogeneous tumor populations and can undermine therapies that target glycolysis alone [22].

Lactate-Dependent Signaling and Redox Regulation

Beyond its metabolic functions, lactate also acts as a signaling molecule through receptor-mediated and metabolism-dependent mechanisms. Activation of HCAR1/GPR81 by extracellular lactate has been associated with tumor-cell survival, angiogenic signaling, and resistance to chemotherapy in several experimental models [67, 121]. By sustaining regeneration of cytosolic NAD+, lactate may also influence NAD+-dependent enzymes involved in transcriptional control, chromatin regulation, and DNA repair, thereby coupling metabolic flux to stress tolerance and adaptive responses [24, 113].

Promotion of EMT, Motility, and Invasive Phenotypes

Lactate has been reported to promote EMT-associated features and invasive behavior in several experimental cancer models [46, 122, 123]. Elevated lactate levels stabilize HIF-1α [79, 124], a central regulator of EMT-associated transcriptional programs [125, 126], and have been linked to activation of TGF-β-dependent signaling in defined models [114, 127]. Lactate has also been reported to induce EMT-associated transcription factors such as SNAIL [128], TWIST [46], and ZEB1 through HIF-1α-dependent mechanisms. Beyond classical EMT, lactate can promote alternative modes of tumor cell motility. Lactate sensing through GPR55 has been shown to trigger an amoeboid-like invasive phenotype in prostate cancer cells via RhoA/MLC2 signaling [129], while concurrent induction of matrix metalloproteinases further facilitates invasion through ECM remodeling [130, 131].

Survival under Stress, Autophagy, and Therapy Resistance

Lactate has been reported to contribute to cellular stress tolerance through effects on redox homeostasis, antioxidant capacity, and pro-survival signaling in specific experimental contexts [132, 133]. Under glucose-deprived conditions, lactate has been shown to activate mTOR signaling and upregulate Bcl-2, thereby suppressing apoptosis and promoting survival under metabolic stress [134]. Lactate has also been reported to promote autophagy, enabling recycling of intracellular components to sustain energy production and biosynthesis during stress or therapy exposure [86]. Together, these adaptive responses may contribute to resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted agents in a context-dependent manner [135, 136].

Epigenetic Plasticity, Stemness, and Durable Phenotypic States

Sustained lactate exposure has been associated with stabilization of aggressive phenotypes through epigenetic mechanisms. Histone lactylation links intracellular lactate availability to chromatin modification and transcriptional activation, reinforcing programs associated with EMT, stemness, immune modulation, and survival [42, 43, 87]. Cancer stem-like cells have been reported to occupy lactate-rich niches in several tumor models and may exploit lactate-mediated signaling and epigenetic reinforcement to maintain self-renewal and plasticity [137, 138]. By embedding metabolic conditions into chromatin architecture, lactate may convert transient environmental cues into more durable phenotypic states.

Lactate-Mediated Epigenetic Reprogramming: Histone Lactylation and Beyond

The discovery that lactate directly regulates chromatin structure provides a mechanism by which sustained metabolic state can be translated into transcriptional control. In tumors, where lactate accumulation is chronic rather than transient, this mechanism may help stabilize aggressive phenotypes [31, 42, 43, 45].

Histone Lactylation as a Metabolic-Epigenetic Interface

Histone lactylation was first identified as lysine lactylation on core histones, conceptually analogous to acetylation but derived from intracellular lactate pools [30]. Initial studies demonstrated that lactylation tags correlate with transcriptional activation, establishing lactate as a metabolite capable of reflecting metabolic information directly into chromatin.

In cancer, this physiological mechanism may be co-opted. Persistent lactate accumulation increases the availability of activated lactyl donors, allowing sustained chromatin modification rather than transient signaling [43, 45]. This feature is particularly relevant in tumors, where chronic metabolic re-programming imposes long-lasting selection pressures that favor durable phenotypic adaptation.

Enzymatic Basis and Integration with Canonical Chromatin Regulation

Although the enzymology of histone lactylation remains incompletely resolved, accumulating evidence implicates canonical lysine acetyltransferases, particularly p300/CBP, as candidate enzymes capable of catalyzing lactyl group transfer to histones [30, 45]. This places lactylation within the broader family of metabolite-sensitive histone acylations and suggests competition or cooperation with acetyl-CoA–driven acetylation. Because acetylation and lactylation target overlapping lysine residues, high lactate states may alter chromatin occupancy among competing acyl marks, altering transcriptional output in a context-dependent manner. In addition, lactate-driven changes in NAD+ availability could influence sirtuin activity, CD38 signaling and other NAD+-dependent chromatin regulators such as PARP, indirectly modulating histone deacetylation and DNA repair processes [139]. Thus, lactylation does not function in isolation but integrates with established epigenetic machinery to reshape chromatin landscapes in response to metabolic flux in many cancers [140].

Lactylation-Driven Transcriptional Programs in Cancer and Immune Cells

Across experimental models, histone lactylation is most consistently associated with transcriptional activation. In cancer cells, lactylation-linked genes intersect with transcriptional programs implicated in epithelial–mesenchymal transition, invasion, metabolic adaptation, stemness, and survival [42, 43, 45, 46]. Lactylation was first connected to macrophage polarization, where it promotes expression of genes linked to tissue repair and tumor-supportive macrophage phenotypes [30]. Subsequent studies have extended this paradigm to tumor-associated M2 macrophages [43] within the cancer microenvironment, providing a chromatin-level mechanism linking lactate accumulation to immune suppression [141]. Importantly, lactylation is not restricted to immune cells. Cancer cells themselves display extensive lactylation-associated transcriptional restructuring [142], supporting the view that this mechanism links tumor cell–intrinsic programs with immune conditioning and stromal reprogramming.

Stabilization of EMT, Plasticity, and Stem-like States

Phenotypic plasticity is essential for metastatic progression, enabling tumor cells to transition between epithelial, mesenchymal, and hybrid states in response to environmental demands. Lactate influences EMT phenotypes through signaling pathways [46]; lactylation introduces a chromatin-level mechanism that may contribute to persistence of these transitions over time. Evidence implicates lactylation of EMT transcription factors and invasion-associated programs, effectively reinforcing aggressive phenotypes following sustained lactate exposure [43]. This may help explain the persistence of invasive behavior even when cells migrate into microenvironments with altered oxygen or nutrient availability. Similarly, lactate-driven epigenetic re-modeling supports cancer stem-like phenotypes. Stemness requires transcriptional programs that promote survival, metabolic flexibility, and resistance to differentiation cues. Lactylation may reinforce these programs in lactate-rich niches, operating alongside HIF-1α stabilization and NF-κB/IL-8 and ERK/AKT signaling [40, 79, 101].

Non-Histone Lactylation and Broader Regulatory Consequences

Beyond histones, lysine lactylation has been detected on non-histone proteins, including transcriptional regulators and metabolic enzymes [44, 45, 143]. Although functional consequences remain under investigation, emerging proteomic data suggest that lactylation may influence protein stability, enzymatic activity, and interaction networks relevant to metabolism, transcription, and stress adaptation. These findings raise the possibility that lactate-driven post-translational modification extends epigenetic regulation beyond chromatin into broader regulatory circuits, further expanding the potential for lactate to influence malignant behavior at multiple regulatory levels [144, 145].

Lactate and the Immune Microenvironment

Effective immune surveillance represents one of the most formidable barriers to tumor progression and metastatic dissemination. Elevated lactate concentrations within tumors, generated through sustained aerobic and hypoxic glycolysis, have been shown to alter immune cell metabolism, differentiation, signaling, and spatial organization. These effects are not fully explained by extracellular acidification alone, and involve transporter-mediated uptake, receptor signaling, and epigenetic regulation [35, 37, 113, 118].

Metabolic Suppression of Effector T Lymphocytes

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells require robust glycolytic reprogramming to support proliferation, cytokine production, and target cell killing. Tumor-derived lactate and lactic acid have been shown to impair this metabolic shift, resulting in reduced cytokine production, impaired proliferation, and diminished cytotoxic capacity [35, 37, 146]. Fischer et al. [35] demonstrated that these inhibitory effects cannot be reproduced by extracellular acidification alone, identifying lactate as a major suppressive metabolite rather than a simple consequence of low pH. Through these metabolic constraints lactate contributes to impaired adaptive immune surveillance within the tumor microenvironment.

Natural Killer Cell Dysfunction and Early Immune Escape

Natural killer (NK) cells provide rapid, antigen-independent anti-tumor surveillance and play a critical role in eliminating transformed cells and early metastatic seeds. Lactate-rich environments have been shown to suppress NK cell function, including reductions in proinflammatory IFN-γ, and TNFα and chemokine production, impairing granzyme and perforin release, and compromising cytotoxic capacity [37, 147, 148]. These effects may weaken early immune containment and thereby lower barriers to tumor establishment during dissemination and circulation, removing a key barrier to metastatic spread prior to effective adaptive immune engagement.

Selective Support of Regulatory T Cells and Immune Tolerance

In contrast to cytotoxic lymphocytes, regulatory T cells display metabolic adaptations that allow them to function in low-glucose, high-lactate environments. FOXP3-dependent metabolic reprogramming may facilitate Tregs to maintain suppressive capacity under these conditions, which may support Treg persistence within lactate-rich tumor microenvironments [149]. Elevated lactate promotes FOXP3 expression and enhances secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, reinforcing immune tolerance within the tumor microenvironment [97, 113]. By selectively supporting Treg survival and function while suppressing effector cells, lactate shifts immune balance toward tumor tolerance.

Myeloid Cell Reprogramming and Epigenetic Immune Suppression

High lactate concentrations have been shown to promote polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward M2-like phenotypes characterized by immunosuppression, angiogenesis, tissue repair programs, and support of tumor growth [36]. Mechanistically, lactate stabilizes HIF-1α and has been shown to promote histone lactylation at regulatory regions of M2-associated genes, providing a potential epigenetic basis for sustained immune reprogramming [30, 43].

Lactate also promotes expansion and suppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, enhancing arginase activity, nitric oxide production, and reactive oxygen species generation that inhibit T-cell function [150, 151]. In addition, tumor-derived lactic acid impairs dendritic cell differentiation and antigen-presenting capacity, weakening T-cell priming and adaptive immune activation [152, 153]. Lactate signaling through GPR81 in antigen-presenting cells may further reinforce tolerogenic immune programming within the tumor microenvironment [154].

Construction of Immune-Privileged Tumor Niches and Therapeutic Implications

Across immune compartments, a consistent pattern emerges: effector immune cells are suppressed, suppressive populations are reinforced, antigen presentation is compromised, and immune infiltration is restricted [35, 37, 97]. These observations have direct implications for immunotherapy. Lactate-driven suppression of T- and NK-cell function can undermine checkpoint blockade and adoptive cell therapies [37], while the persistence of regulatory T cells in high-lactate, low-glucose environments reinforces immunosuppression [149]. Accordingly, strategies that limit lactate production, export, signaling, or downstream epigenetic consequences provide a rationale for combination approaches aimed at restoring immune competence within tumors [97, 155, 156].

Lactate and the Metastatic Cascade: The Spearhead Concept

Metastasis is a multistep and lethal process in which dissemination depends not only on genetic alterations but also on metabolic state, microenvironmental conditioning, and immune escape.

Conceptual Framework: Lactate as the Leading Edge of Metastasis

The spearhead concept positions lactate as an enabling factor in metastatic progression. Through its combined metabolic, signaling, microenvironmental, and immunomodulatory effects [3, 33, 54], lactate may help lower physical, metabolic, and immunologic barriers to metastatic spread, promoting invasive phenotypes, weakening host immune defenses, conditioning permissive microenvironments, and sustaining tumor-cell survival at distant sites.

Metabolic Foundations of Metastatic Competence

Metastatic dissemination requires high ATP turnover, biosynthetic capacity, and redox control. Lactate production sustains glycolytic flux through regeneration of cytosolic NAD+ [8, 9, 18–20], while lactate accumulation is associated with invasive and stress-adaptive behavior rather than proliferation alone [54, 98, 157].

Local Invasion, EMT, and Intravasation

Lactate contributes to local invasion by promoting invasion-associated transcriptional reprogramming in tumor cells, including stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α in oxidative tumor compartments [79]. In parallel, lactate-driven conditioning of the tumor microenvironment, particularly through stromal cell-derived lactate, can promote invasive behavior, as demonstrated in models of perineural invasion driven by cancer-associated fibroblasts [51]. These invasion-promoting programs may be further reinforced by histone lactylation, providing a chromatin-level mechanism that may reinforce EMT-associated gene expression.

Circulating tumor cells encounter extreme metabolic and mechanical stress, including shear forces, oxidative damage, and anoikis. Tumor cell clusters display increased metastatic efficiency, immune evasion, and resistance to stress relative to single circulating cells [158]. Lactate-driven redox buffering and anti-apoptotic signaling may contribute to stress tolerance during dissemination; however, these metabolic effects should be considered distinct from the specific biological mechanisms governing tumor cell clustering [53, 83, 159].

Beyond transcriptional reprogramming, lactate can enhance tumor cell motility. In breast cancer models, genetic suppression of LDHA or LDHB reduces lactate production and significantly impairs migratory capacity, supporting a functional link between lactate metabolism and tumor cell movement [50]. Under endocrine-resistant conditions, lactate-associated signaling has been linked to membrane blebbing and amoeboid motility, enabling efficient movement through confined tissue spaces [157].

Lactate also promotes angiogenesis and increases vascular permeability through endothelial activation, generating aberrant vasculature that facilitates tumor cell entry into the circulation [101]. Lactate-associated microenvironmental conditioning may further lower barriers to intravasation, particularly within perivascular and invasive niches; the underlying cellular mechanisms are detailed in section entitled Lactate and the immune microenvironment.

Survival in Circulation and Pre-Metastatic Niche Conditioning

Circulating tumor cells encounter metabolic and mechanical stress, including shear forces, oxidative damage, and anoikis. Lactate can support redox balance, stress-adaptive metabolism, and pro-survival signaling in defined contexts [54, 84, 148, 159]. Beyond the tumor, systemic metabolic changes associated with high glycolytic flux may also contribute to permissive immune and stromal conditions that complement established pre-metastatic niche drivers [148].

Colonization, Outgrowth, and Phenotypic Plasticity

At secondary sites, successful colonization requires metabolic flexibility, immune evasion, and phenotypic plasticity. Lactate oxidation can provide ATP in glucose-limited microenvironments, while lactate-driven epigenetic reprogramming supports adaptive state transitions during colonization and expansion [43, 45]. Through sustained metabolic and epigenetic influence, lactate may support metastatic seeding, outgrowth, and adaptation at distant sites.

Therapeutic Targeting of Lactate Metabolism and Lactate-Mediated Pathways

Recognition of lactate as a multifunctional contributor to malignant progression has intensified interest in targeting lactate metabolism, transport, and signaling. Unlike classical oncogenic drivers, lactate represents a system-level vulnerability linking metabolic adaptation with invasion, immune modulation, angiogenesis, and epigenetic regulation. Tumors exhibit quantitative, spatial, and regulatory dependencies on lactate metabolism that may be selectively exploitable [7, 22, 118]. Major therapeutic strategies directed against lactate production, transport, signaling, buffering, and downstream consequences are summarized in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552146).

Targeting Lactate Dehydrogenase Isoenzymes

Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) has been the principal focus of therapeutic development due to its role in sustaining glycolytic flux and cytosolic NAD+ regeneration in many aggressive tumors [82, 160, 161]. Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of LDHA reduces lactate production, perturbs redox balance, and has been shown to impair biosynthetic capacity and reduce survival under hypoxic or nutrient-limited conditions in multiple but not all experimental models [19]. Preclinical LDHA inhibitors, including oxamate, FX11, and GNE-140, suppress tumor growth and metabolic stress adaptation in a range of preclinical cancer models [9, 24]. However, challenges related to selectivity and therapeutic index have constrained clinical translation, reflecting LDHA function in normal proliferative tissues.

In parallel, LDHB has emerged as a complementary therapeutic target, particularly in metabolically heterogeneous tumors that exploit lactate oxidation to support growth and invasion. By supporting lactate-fueled oxidative metabolism, LDHB may contribute to lactate availability and glucose sparing within tumor populations [26, 84]. Disruption of LDHB impairs invasive behavior in aggressive breast cancer models, highlighting its relevance to dissemination-associated phenotypes rather than proliferation alone [50, 59, 157]. Combined disruption of both LDH isoenzymes may be required to substantially impair lactate-dependent metabolic flexibility and suppress tumor growth, underscoring functional redundancy within the LDH system [162, 163]. A further caveat in targeting LDH, particularly LDH-A, is the dependence of erythrocytes on glycolytic NAD+ regeneration. Because erythrocytes lack mitochondria, conversion of pyruvate to lactate is obligatory for continued glycolytic ATP production and cell survival. Any systemic LDH-directed strategy will, therefore, require careful attention to on-target hematologic toxicity and therapeutic window [164].

Inhibition of Lactate Transport and Metabolic Symbiosis

Monocarboxylate transporters are key mediators of lactate-dependent cooperation between glycolytic and oxidative tumor compartments. MCT4 mediates lactate efflux from highly glycolytic or hypoxic tumor cells, whereas MCT1 supports lactate uptake by more oxidative tumor cells, as well as endothelial and immune cell populations [21, 23]. Pharmacologic inhibition of MCT1 can disrupt lactate uptake and interfere with lactate-associated angiogenic signaling, particularly through effects on endothelial cells. The selective MCT1 inhibitor AZD3965 has demonstrated preclinical antitumor activity and entered early-phase clinical trials, with tolerability and preliminary activity under evaluation in selected tumor types [96, 101, 165]. Selective inhibition of MCT4 remains technically challenging but represents a promising strategy to target hypoxia-adapted tumor compartments by limiting lactate export. Given compensatory transporter expression, combined targeting of MCT1 and MCT4 may be required to disrupt lactate-driven processes and constrain tumor adaptability.

Targeting Lactate Signaling Pathways

Lactate also functions as a signaling ligand through the G-protein-coupled receptor GPR81 (also known as HCAR1). In cancer cells and stromal compartments, GPR81 activation has been implicated in pro-survival signaling and pathways associated with angiogenic, metabolic, and tumor-supportive phenotypes [121, 166]. Genetic suppression of GPR81 has been shown to reduce tumor growth and metastatic phenotypes in preclinical models, supporting a functional role for lactate-dependent receptor signaling in tumor progression. Because GPR81 expression appears more restricted in normal tissues than core metabolic enzymes, receptor-directed inhibition may offer a more selective therapeutic window. However, development of potent and specific GPR81 antagonists remains early, and further work is needed to define downstream signaling networks and context-dependent effects relevant to therapeutic intervention.

Indirect Targeting through pH and Microenvironmental Regulation

Tumor tolerance of high lactate flux depends on coordinated regulation of intracellular and extracellular pH. Sodium-hydrogen exchangers (NHE1), carbonic anhydrases, and vacuolar H+-ATPases maintain intracellular alkalinity while permitting extracellular acidification, supporting continued glycolytic flux and lactate handling in tumor cells [73, 93]. Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of these pH-regulatory systems disrupts cellular pH homeostasis and has been shown to reduce invasion, cancer stem cell survival, and resistance to therapy in multiple experimental models, while indirectly interfering with lactate-associated metabolic phenotypes [167]. Although not lactate-specific, these approaches may synergize with direct metabolic interventions by destabilizing pH conditions required for lactate-dependent tumor adaptation.

Integration with Immunotherapy and Epigenetic Strategies

Lactate-directed interventions are particularly relevant to immunotherapy because lactate suppresses effector T- and NK-cell metabolism while reinforcing immunosuppressive cell populations [37, 54]. In parallel, limiting lactate availability or lactylation may disrupt durable programs linked to EMT, stemness, and metastatic plasticity [30, 43].

Breast Cancer as a Model for Lactate-Driven Aggressiveness

Breast cancer provides a useful model of lactate-driven malignancy because its molecular heterogeneity and metabolic plasticity are well characterized. Across luminal, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative subtypes, sustained glycolytic flux and elevated lactate production are linked to invasive behavior, immune evasion, and therapeutic adaptation, particularly in aggressive disease contexts [50, 118, 168].

Metabolic Architecture and Lactate Accumulation across Subtypes

Lactate has been reported to enhance changes in cytoskeletal structure that can influence cellular motility of tumor cells transiting from a rigid epithelial formation to a more migratory mesenchymal configuration. In endocrine-resistant breast cancer models, membrane blebbing and amoeboid-like motility have been described under altered extracellular pH conditions [157, 169]. Where lactate exposure enhances tumor cell motility, this has been associated with signaling changes consistent with increased ERK1/2 activity and reduced epithelial adhesion, although such effects are context-dependent [50]. These behaviors facilitate movement through dense extracellular matrices and confined tissue spaces.

Breast tumors display aerobic glycolysis and high glucose uptake across subtypes, particularly in estrogen receptor–negative and triple-negative disease [168, 170, 171]. Up-regulation of LDHA and high expression of MCT4 are characteristic of aggressive breast cancers, where PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling help sustain elevated intra-tumoral lactate levels [91, 172–174].

At the same time, oxidative tumor sub-populations can import and oxidize lactate via MCT1 and LDHB, enabling metabolic symbiosis and adaptation to fluctuating nutrient availability [26, 84]. This compartmentalized architecture supports survival and outgrowth across diverse microenvironments, including those encountered during metastatic colonization.

Lactate-Driven EMT, Motility, and Phenotypic Plasticity

Lactate contributes to invasion-associated transcriptional reprogramming in breast cancer, including stabilization of HIF-1α in oxidative tumor cell populations and activation of ERK- and TGF-β-linked pathways associated with motility and invasive behavior. Rather than acting as a sole EMT inducer, lactate is better viewed as a metabolic factor that reinforces EMT-associated programs in permissive signaling contexts. Sustained lactate exposure may also favor hybrid epithelial-mesenchymal phenotypic states that retain both epithelial and mesenchymal features, a configuration increasingly associated with metastatic competence and stress tolerance during dissemination [30, 43]. In endocrine-resistant breast cancer models, altered extracellular pH and lactate-associated signaling have been linked to membrane blebbing and amoeboid-like motility, facilitating movement through dense extracellular matrices and confined tissue spaces [160, 169].

Matrix Re-Modeling, Stromal Coupling, and Local Invasion

Lactate reshapes the breast tumor microenvironment by promoting stromal reprogramming, ECM remodeling, and invasion-permissive tissue architecture [47, 51]. Activated stromal and myeloid cells can contribute to matrix degradation and directional invasion through coordinated structural and paracrine mechanisms [175, 176]. This stromal coupling supports local invasion and intravasation by reducing structural tissue constraints and enhancing access to the vasculature.

Immune Evasion and Therapy Resistance

Consistent with the mechanisms detailed in section Lactate and the immune microenvironment, lactate-enriched breast tumor microenvironments are associated with reduced effective anti-tumor immune activity, particularly in highly glycolytic disease contexts such as triple-negative breast cancer [25, 37, 173]. Sustained lactate production has also been linked to resistance to multiple therapeutic modalities in breast cancer models. In endocrine-responsive models, lactate exposure can induce ERK activation, enhance motility, and promote resistance-associated phenotypes even in the absence of overt epithelial–mesenchymal transition [95]. Together, these observations support considering lactate as an active contributor to immune-evasive and therapy-resistant states in breast cancer.

Metastatic Dissemination, Colonization, and Clinical Implications

Breast cancer cells exhibit metabolic flexibility that supports survival in glucose-limited metastatic microenvironments, including the capacity to oxidize lactate as an alternative fuel source. Tumor-derived lactate may also contribute to systemic conditioning of distant tissues through stromal and immune reprogramming [48, 116]. Clinically, circulating LDH levels, transporter expression, and emerging imaging or lactylation-linked signatures are being explored as biomarkers of aggressive metabolic state and metastatic risk [161, 177].

Lactate-Driven Aggressiveness beyond Breast Cancer: Tumor-Specific Nuances

Lactate-driven aggressiveness is not confined to breast cancer but represents a broader metabolic strategy across malignancies. Different tumor types exploit lactate to couple metabolic conditioning with microenvironmental remodeling, immune modulation, and epigenetic regulation, although the dominant entry point varies by tissue and disease context [3, 23, 44, 54]. Representative cancer contexts in which lactate contributes to aggressive phenotypes across malignancies are summarized in online supplementary Table 2.

Lung Cancer: Metabolic Symbiosis and Immune Modulation

Lung cancers exhibit pronounced metabolic compartmentalization, with hypoxic glycolytic regions juxtaposed against oxygenated vascular niches. This architecture favors lactate-based metabolic symbiosis, whereby oxidative tumor cells import lactate via MCT1 and LDHB to sustain tricarboxylic acid cycle activity while sparing glucose for hypoxic compartments [26, 84, 85]. Lactate uptake supports redox balance and metabolic fitness, thereby promoting tumor cell survival under invasive and dissemination-associated stress. At the same time, lactate-rich microenvironments have been associated with immune-excluded tumor regions, contributing to immune evasion and heterogeneous responses to immune checkpoint blockade [37]. In this context, non–small cell lung cancers with elevated LDHB expression display greater reliance on lactate shuttling and may be particularly sensitive, in preclinical models, to therapeutic disruption of lactate transport or oxidation pathways.

Colorectal Cancer: Stemness, Wnt Signaling, and Organotropism

In colorectal cancer, lactate can reinforce stem-like tumor cell states through direct metabolic–signaling mechanisms that intersect with core oncogenic pathways. Hypoxia-induced β-catenin lactylation has been shown in colorectal cancer cell models to increase β-catenin stability and augment Wnt signaling, thereby promoting proliferation and stemness [178]. Consistent with this, lactate has also been reported to enhance metastatic traits in normoxic colorectal cancer stem-like cells by promoting oxidative phosphorylation through a lactate uptake/oxidation program, linking lactate availability to aggressive dissemination phenotypes [174]. Beyond tumor-intrinsic effects, lactate can weaken antitumor immunity in ways relevant to colorectal tumor progression. Tumor-derived lactic acid impairs dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation, suggesting a mechanism by which lactate-rich microenvironments can compromise priming of effective adaptive immune responses [152].

Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma: Stromal Coupling and Immune Exclusion

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) exemplifies lactate-integrated aggressiveness within a dense, hypovascular, and nutrient-depleted stroma. LDHA has been reported as a predictive biomarker associated with immune cell infiltration [179]. A glycolytic PDAC subtype characterized by high MCT4 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and distinctive metabolic dependencies, supporting the concept that efficient lactate export is a characteristic feature of aggressive PDAC biology [51, 180]. In parallel, stromal fibroblast populations can foster lactate-rich microenvironments that directly promote invasive programs in PDAC, providing a mechanistic basis for lactate-driven stromal coupling [181]. Beyond metabolic cooperation, lactate contributes to immune exclusion in PDAC. Tumor-derived lactate has been shown to enhance the suppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) through HIF-1α–linked mechanisms and to blunt antitumor T-cell responses in PDAC models, connecting lactate accumulation to an inhibitory immune microenvironment and therapy resistance [182]. Lactate also participates in more durable stromal programming: lactate-mediated epigenetic reprogramming has been shown to regulate the formation of pancreatic cancer-associated fibroblasts, providing a mechanism by which sustained lactate flux may contribute to maintenance of tumor-supportive stromal states [183]. Together, these findings position PDAC as a paradigmatic example in which lactate links tumor metabolism, stromal activation, immune suppression, and epigenetic stabilization within an integrated, treatment-resistant malignant context.

Prostate and Melanoma: Hormonal Control and Therapy Resistance

In prostate cancer, androgen receptor (AR) signaling can contribute to broad metabolic reprogramming, including upregulation of glycolytic and anabolic pathways that increase lactate-generating flux and link lactate biology directly to hormonal control of tumor metabolism [184]. In vivo metabolic imaging further supports this connection: AR signaling in castration-resistant prostate cancer alters pyruvate-to-lactate conversion and lactate levels, consistent with hormonally regulated control of lactate-associated metabolism [184]. Within skeletal metastases, prostate tumors engage tightly coupled metabolic interactions with the bone microenvironment; osteoclast activation is a major component of disease progression, creating conditions conducive to progression that may be further reinforced by tumor-associated metabolic acid production [185].

Melanoma provides some of the clearest mechanistic evidence that lactate functions as an immunosuppressive metabolite. LDHA-associated lactic acid production in melanoma has been shown to blunt tumor immunosurveillance by inhibiting T-cell and NK-cell function in vivo, providing strong evidence linking lactate accumulation to immune escape [27]. Lactate also contributes to therapy resistance in BRAF-mutant melanoma: re-accumulated lactate in BRAFi/MEKi-resistant cells can support survival through lactylation-dependent epigenetic reprogramming [186]. Together, these findings highlight tumor-specific “entry points” into lactate biology, hormonal regulation in prostate cancer and immune/epigenetic mechanisms in melanoma, while converging on a shared outcome of aggressive, treatment-refractory disease.

Cross-Cancer Synthesis

Although lactate-dependent mechanisms recur across tumor types, their relative contribution, regulatory entry points, and therapeutic tractability remain shaped by tissue context and disease state. Across these settings, lactate links metabolic flux to malignant progression and helps explain shared patterns of aggressiveness [3, 54].

Future Directions and Research Priorities

Although substantial evidence supports lactate as an important contributor to cancer aggressiveness, important mechanistic, methodological, and translational questions remain unresolved. Addressing these gaps will be essential for transforming the spearhead hypothesis from a conceptual framework toward clinically actionable strategies. The priorities outlined below focus on areas where improved resolution, integration, and experimental rigor are most likely to advance the field. An overall summary is schematically illustrated in Figure 3, which highlights the multiple roles that lactate plays in aiding tumor progression.

Spatial and Temporal Mapping of Lactate Flux in vivo

A major limitation in current understanding of lactate biology is the lack of high-resolution, dynamic mapping of lactate production, utilization, and diffusion within intact tumors. Tumors exhibit extreme metabolic heterogeneity, and lactate flux varies across cellular subpopulations, vascular niches, and stages of progression [26, 84]. Static measurements of lactate concentration are insufficient to capture these dynamics.

Emerging imaging modalities, including hyperpolarized 13C-pyruvate magnetic resonance imaging, lactate-sensitive positron emission tomography probes, and advanced magnetic resonance spectroscopy, offer unprecedented opportunities to visualize lactate flux in vivo and correlate it with invasion, immune exclusion, and metastatic risk [187]. Integration of these approaches with spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics will be essential for identifying lactate “hotspots” that support dissemination and for stratifying patients most likely to benefit from lactate-targeted therapies [113]. Development of methods (such as magnetic resonance) to measure extracellular pH along different regions of the cancer cell membrane would be informative.

Histone Lactylation and Epigenetic Integration

Histone lactylation provides a direct link between metabolic state and gene regulation, but its enzymatic control, kinetics, and functional specificity remain incompletely defined [43, 45]. Clarifying how lactylation intersects with acetylation, methylation, and DNA methylation will be important for understanding durable malignant phenotypic states.

Mechanistic Dissection of Lactate Signaling Pathways

Although GPR81 (HCAR1) is an important mediator of lactate signaling, its downstream pathways and context-specific functions remain incompletely resolved [67, 100]. Defining these mechanisms may reveal new therapeutic vulnerabilities and explain tissue-specific responses to lactate accumulation.

Lactate and Immune Cell Metabolic Programming

While lactate-mediated suppression of CD8+ T cells and NK cells is well established, the precise metabolic checkpoints involved remain incompletely characterized [37, 149]. Understanding why regulatory T cells and suppressive myeloid populations thrive in lactate-rich environments may reveal strategies to reprogram tumor immunity without compromising systemic immune function.

Translational Integration and Therapeutic Stratification

Translating lactate-directed concepts into clinical benefit will likely depend on rational combinations with immunotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents, epigenetic modulators, or targeted signaling inhibitors, together with biomarkers that identify tumors most dependent on lactate metabolism.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Lactate should not be viewed merely as a correlate of aerobic glycolysis, but as a multifunctional metabolite linking oncogenic signaling to metabolic adaptation, stromal remodeling, immune suppression, angiogenesis, epigenetic regulation, and metastatic competence. Future progress will depend on refining biomarkers of lactate dependence, clarifying the relative contributions of lactate signaling versus proton-driven effects, and integrating lactate-directed strategies alongside other targeted therapies.
