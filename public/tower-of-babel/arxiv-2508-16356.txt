An Arithmetic Characterization of 2-Generated Numbers
Bireswar Das, Kavita Samant, Dhara Thakkar

Abstract
A group G G is said to be k k -generated if it has a generating set with k k elements. A positive integer n n is called a 2-generated number if every group of order n n is 2-generated. In this article, we establish an arithmetic characterization of 2-generated numbers expressed in terms of the prime factorization of n n .

Introduction

For a natural number
n
, let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
denote the set of all groups of order
n
. Given a group-theoretic property
P
, we say that
n
is a
P
-number
if every group in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
satisfies property
P
. For example, if
P
denotes the property of being cyclic, then
n
is termed a
cyclic number
if every group of order
n
is cyclic. Various properties
P
such as being abelian, nilpotent, satisfying the converse of Lagrange’s theorem (CLT) have been studied in this context, often yielding elegant arithmetic characterizations of the corresponding
P
-numbers (see
[
2
,
7
,
14
,
18
]
, etc.). In these works, the characterizations are mainly based on the prime factorizations of the integers involved.

For instance, a positive integer
n
is a cyclic number if and only if
\gcd(n,\varphi(n))=1
, where
\varphi(n)
is Euler’s totient function (see
[
7
]
). In
[
14
]
, the authors developed a unified approach to characterize
cyclic numbers
,
abelian numbers
and
nilpotent numbers
. For their characterization, they used the notion of
nilpotent factorization
, defined as follows: let
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
be the prime factorization of
n
, where the
p_{i}
are distinct primes. Then
n
is said to have a
nilpotent factorization
, if for all integers
a,i,j
with
1\leq a\leq e_{i}
, we have
p_{i}^{a}\not\equiv 1\pmod{p_{j}}
.

With this definition, a positive integer
n
is a
nilpotent number
if and only if it has nilpotent factorization. Furthermore,
n
is a cyclic number if and only if it is a square-free number with nilpotent factorization, and
n
is an
abelian number
if and only if it is cube-free and has nilpotent factorization.

In
[
2
]
, an arithmetic characterization is given for CLT-numbers, and in
[
18
]
, the authors further explored special cases, such as abelian and cyclic CLT-groups, which are referred to as ACLT-numbers and CCLT-numbers, respectively.

Motivated by the interplay between group-theoretic properties and arithmetic structure, we study a new class of integers, namely
2-generated numbers
, defined as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

A positive integer
n
is called a
2-generated number
if every group of order
n
can be generated by two elements.

While the structure of 1-generated (i.e., cyclic) groups is well-understood and has been extensively characterized, the study of 2-generated groups is more challenging and less structured. Numerous investigations have addressed conditions in this direction, though the results are scattered in the literature without a comprehensive characterization. A well-known result in this area is that every finite simple group is 2-generated. The proof is mainly based on the Classification of Finite Simple Groups. It begins with Miller’s early work on alternating groups
[
13
]
. In
[
19
]
, Steinberg proved this for simple groups of Lie type. However, the proof was finally completed in 1984, when Aschbacher and Guralnick
[
1
]
showed that every sporadic group is 2-generated.

Apart from simple groups, several other interesting families of groups such as symmetric, dihedral, and dicyclic groups are also 2-generated even though their internal structures are different. The structural differences among these groups show the complexity of 2-generated groups. This makes it important to look more closely at how generation is linked to the arithmetic of the group order. This is one of the main motivations for studying 2-generated numbers.
To better understand the structure of 2-generated groups, Lucchini and Maróti introduced the concept of
generating graphs
in
[
10
,
11
]
. The
generating graph

\Gamma(G)
of a finite group
G
is the graph whose vertex set consists of the elements of
G
, with an edge joining two distinct elements if and only if they generate
G
. These graphs are trivial (i.e., empty) for groups that cannot be generated by two elements.
Determining the values of
n
for which all groups of order
n
are 2-generated yields a sufficient condition for the generating graphs to be non-empty, based solely on the number of vertices.

The main results of this article are stated as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n
be an odd positive integer. Let
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\dots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, where
p_{1}>p_{2}>\dots>p_{k}
are distinct primes and each
e_{i}
is a positive integer. Then
n
is a 2-generated number if and only if
n
is cube-free and for every
i>j
,
p_{j}\nmid(p_{i}-1)
or
e_{i}=1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n
be an even positive integer. Then
n
is a 2-generated number if and only if
n=2^{\alpha}p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\dots p_{k}
, where each
p_{i}
is an odd prime, and
\alpha\leq 2
.

Semidirect Product

Let

\phi:H\rightarrow{\mbox{Aut}}(N)

G:=\{(n,h)\mid n\in N,h\in H\}
with the product operation,
(n_{1},h_{1})(n_{2},h_{2})=(n_{1}\phi_{h_{1}^{-1}}(n_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})

of

The following theorem by Schur–Zassenhaus is one of the fundamental theorems in finite group theory.

Theorem 2.1

(see Theorem 9.1.2,
[

]
)

Let

Metacyclic Groups

A group
G
is called
metacyclic
if it contains a cyclic normal subgroup
N
such that
G/N
is also cyclic. The subgroup
N
is called the kernel of
G
. Metacyclic group has been classified completely in
[
6
]
. It is easy to see that a metacyclic group

can be written as
G=HN
with
H\leq G
,
N\triangleleft G
, and both
H
and
N
are cyclic. Such a product is called
metacyclic factorization
of
G
. If
G
has a metacyclic
factorization

with
H\cap N=\{1\}
, then
G
is a semidirect product of
H
by
N
.
A finite metacyclic group can be presented on two generators and three defining relations as follows (see Theorem 7.21 in
[
20
]
):

G=\langle x,y\,|\,x^{k}=y^{l},y^{m}=1,x^{-1}yx=y^{n}\rangle,

where
k,l,n,m
are positive integers such that
m\mid(n^{k}-1)
and
m\mid l(n-1)
.

It follows from the above result that metacyclic groups are 2-generated. The next result concerns to finite groups with all Sylow subgroups cyclic, known as
Z
-groups
– a special case of
metacyclic groups
.

Theorem 2.2

(Hölder, Burnside, Zassenhaus, see Theorem 10.1.10,
[
16
]
)
.

If
G
is a finite group all of whose Sylow subgroups are cyclic, then
G
has a presentation

G=\langle a,b\,|\,a^{m}=1=b^{n},b^{-1}ab=a^{r}\rangle,

where
r^{n}\equiv 1\mod m
,
m
is odd,
0\leq r<m
, and
m
and
n(r-1)
are coprime.

Let
G
be a finite group. A generating set of

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem 1.3,

Let

If

Suppose

We use the following easy lemma a couple of times in this paper.

3
2
-Generated Number and Groups of Cube-Free Order

Recall that a positive integer
n
is called a 2-generated number if every group in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
can be generated by two elements (
Definition

1.1
). In this section, we first observe that if
n
is a
2
-generated number then
n
must be cube-free (
3.1
). We then determine a necessary and sufficient conditions under which a cube-free number of the form
p^{a}q^{b}
, with
p
and
q
are distinct primes, is
2
-generated.

Proposition 3.1
.

A positive integer
n
is a
2
-generated number, then
n
is cube-free.

Proof.

Suppose, in contrast, that
n
is not cube-free. Let
p
be a prime such that
p^{k}\mid n
and
k\geq 3
. Note that
d({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})=3
. Consider a group
G=({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{n/p^{3}}
. Note that
G
is of order
n
, and
d(G)\geq 3
. Thus
n
is not a
2
-generated number.
∎

Observation 3.2
.

Let
G
be a group of cube-free order. Then
d(G)\leq 3
. In particular, square-free groups are 2-generated.

Proof.

Note that any Sylow
p
-subgroup of a group
G
with cube-free order is cyclic or isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for some prime
p
. By
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, we have
d(G)\leq\max_{p\,\,prime}d(S_{p})+1
, where
S_{p}
denotes a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. Thus, the result follows.
∎

To study when a cube-free positive integer of the form
n=p^{a}q^{b}
, where
p
and
q
are distinct primes, is a 2-generated number, we start by considering the case when
a+b\leq 2
. In this case,
n
is either a prime power or a square-free number. Every group of order
p^{2}
is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, both of which are 2-generated. Also, as noted in Observation
3.2
that groups of square-free order are 2-generated.
Now, in the following discussion, we will consider the cases when
n
satisfies
a+b=3
.

3.1
Groups of Order
pq^{2}
with
p>q

In this section, we prove that every group of order
p^{2}q
, where
p>q
and
(p,q)\neq(2,3)
, is metacyclic. Metacyclic groups have been completely classified in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n=pq^{2}
, where
p>q
and
(p,q)\neq(3,2)
. Then any group of order
n
is metacyclic.

Proof.

Let
G
be a finite group of order
pq^{2}
, where
p>q
are distinct primes and
(p,q)\neq(3,2)
. We aim to show that
G
is metacyclic.

By Sylow’s theorems, the number
n_{p}
of Sylow
p
-subgroups satisfies
n_{p}\equiv 1\mod p
and
n_{p}\mid q^{2}
, so
n_{p}\in\{1,q,q^{2}\}
. Similarly, the number
n_{q}
of Sylow
q
-subgroups satisfies
n_{q}\equiv 1\mod q
and
n_{q}\mid p
, hence
n_{q}\in\{1,p\}
.

We first claim that the Sylow
p
-subgroup is normal. Suppose, for contradiction, that
n_{p}\neq 1
. Then
n_{p}=q
or
n_{p}=q^{2}
. If
n_{p}=q
, then
p\mid(q^{2}-1)=(q-1)(q+1)
. Since
p>q
, this forces
p\mid(q+1)
, which occurs only when
(p,q)=(3,2)
, the exceptional case we have excluded. A similar argument eliminates the possibility
n_{p}=q^{2}
. Therefore, we must have
n_{p}=1
, and the Sylow
p
-subgroup, which is necessarily cyclic of prime order
p
, is normal in
G
. Denote this normal Sylow
p
-subgroup by
S_{p}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.

Next, we consider the structure of a Sylow
q
-subgroup
S_{q}
, which has order
q^{2}
and is isomorphic to either
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{2}}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. Since
S_{p}\trianglelefteq G
, the group
G
is an extension of
S_{p}
by
S_{q}
, i.e.,
G\cong S_{p}\rtimes_{\phi}S_{q}
, where
\phi:S_{q}\to\operatorname{Aut}(S_{p})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p-1}
.

If
S_{q}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{2}}
, then
G\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{2}}
, and such a group is clearly metacyclic, since both
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{2}}
are cyclic. Thus,
G
is metacyclic in this case.

Assume now that
S_{q}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. Since
S_{q}
is not cyclic, any homomorphism
\phi:S_{q}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p-1}
is determined by its values on the standard generators of
S_{q}
, and its image must be cyclic. Because
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p-1}
is cyclic, it has at most one subgroup of order
q
. Therefore, a non-trivial homomorphism
\phi
must have image of order
q
, and the kernel of
\phi
is a subgroup of
S_{q}
of order
q
. In particular,
{\mbox{ker}}(\phi)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
is a subgroup of
S_{q}
on which
\phi
is trivial, and hence its elements centralize
S_{p}
. Since
S_{p}
is normal and abelian, the subgroup
S_{p}\times{\mbox{ker}}(\phi)
is abelian of order
pq
and is contained in
G
.

Now consider the quotient
S_{q}/{\mbox{ker}}(\phi)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
, so we can write

G\cong(S_{p}\times{\mbox{ker}}(\phi))\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{pq}\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{q},

where
{\mathbb{Z}}_{pq}
is cyclic. This is again a metacyclic group, being a semidirect product of two cyclic groups. Since these are the only two possible structures for the Sylow
p
-subgroup, we conclude that every group of order
pq^{2}
, where
p>q
and
(p,q)\neq(3,2)
, is metacyclic.
∎

We now check the groups of order
n=pq^{2}
with
p=3
and
q=2
, i.e.,
n=12
.

Remark 3.4
.

Every group of order
12
is
2
-generated.

Proof.

Every group of order 12 is 2-generated. Note that every group of order 12 is isomorphic to one of the groups
{\mathbb{Z}}_{12}
,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
,

A_{4}
,
D_{6}
, or the nontrivial semidirect product
Z_{3}\rtimes Z_{4}
. Thus there are exactly 5 groups upto isomorphic (see, e.g., Theorem 4.24,
[
17
]
for details). Clearly, they all are 2-generated.
∎

Based on the above discussion, we deduce the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
n=pq^{2}
where
p,q
are primes and
p>q
. Then
n
is a 2-generated number.

Proof.

Since metacyclic groups are 2-generated, the desired result follows directly from Theorem
3.3
and Remark
3.4
.
∎

3.2
Groups of Order
p^{2}q
with
p>q

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
n=p^{2}q
where
p,q
are primes and
p>q
. Then
n
is a 2-generated number if and only if
q\nmid(p-1)
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a group of order
n
. Then the Sylow-
p
subgroup
S_{p}
of
G
is either
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{2}}
, whereas the Sylow-
q
subgroup of
G
is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. Since
p>q
, Sylow’s theorem implies that
S_{p}
is normal and
G=S_{p}\rtimes S_{q}
.

Suppose that
q\nmid(p-1)
. We show that
n
is a
2
-generated number. Let
G
be a group of order
n
. If
S_{p}
is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{2}}
, then
G
is metacyclic and hence
2
-generated. Therefore, we consider the case when
S_{p}={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
By Sylow’s theorem, the number of conjugates
n_{q}
of
S_{q}
can be
1,p
or
p^{2}
. If
n_{q}=1
, then again the semidirect product is trivial, and thus
G
is abelian and
2
-generated. Since
n_{q}\equiv 1\mod q
, the case,
n_{q}=p
can be ruled out as it contradicts with
q\nmid(p-1)
. Therefore,
n_{q}=p^{2}
is the only remaining possibility. In this case, the number of elements of order
p
and
q
are respectively
p^{2}-1
and
p^{2}(q-1)
. Since
(p^{2}-1)+p(q-1)+1=p^{2}q
, we conclude that there are no elements of order
pq
. Hence, if
G
contains a subgroup
H
of order
pq
, then
H
cannot be cyclic. However, applying Sylow’s theorem to
H
, and using the conditions
p>q
and
q\nmid(p-1)
, we find that if such an
H
exists, it must be abelian, and hence cyclic–a contradiction. Therefore,
G
does not contain any subgroup of order
pq
.
Therefore,
S_{q}
is maximal in
G
. Let
x\in G\setminus S_{q}
. Then
\langle S_{q}\cup\{x\}\rangle=G
. Since
S_{q}
is cyclic, we see that
G
is
2
-generated.

We now consider the other direction. Namely, we assume that
q|(p-1)
and show that
n
is not a 2-generated number by constructing a group
G
of order
n
which is not
2
-generated.
Since
q|(p-1)
, there is an element
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{*}
of order
q
. We now define a homomorphism
\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\longrightarrow Aut({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
as follows: Let
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
, the image of
z
under
\phi
, denoted
\phi_{z}
maps
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
to
a^{z}x
. One can easily verify that
\phi_{z}
is an automorphism of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
\phi
is an injective homomorphism.

Let
G=({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\rtimes_{\phi}{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. It is easy to check that an element
(x,0)
has order
p
, where
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, (here
0
denotes the identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
). For an integer
s>0
, we note that
(x,z)^{s}=((1+a^{z}+a^{2z}+\ldots+a^{(s-1)z})x,sz)
. We observe that

(1-a^{z})(1+a^{z}+a^{2z}+\ldots+a^{(s-1)z})=1-a^{sz}.

(1)

If
z\neq 0
, then for
s
to be the order of
(x,z)
we need
s
to be a multiple of
q
. On the other hand, with
s=q
the right hand side of
Equation

1
is
0\mod p
. Also as
(1-a^{z})\not\equiv 0\mod p
,
(1+a^{z}+a^{2z}+\ldots+a^{(s-1)z})\equiv 0\mod p
. Thus,
q
is the order of
(x,z)
if
z\neq 0
. These observations imply that

(a)

there are no element of order
pq
in
G
,

(b)

any subgroup
P
of order
p
in
G
, which has to be cyclic, is normal.

Now we will show that
G
is not 2-generated. Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
. If
o(g_{1})=o(g_{2})=p
, then
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
cannot generate any element of order
q
, and hence cannot generate
G
. If
o(g_{1})=p
and
o(g_{2})=q
, then
\langle g_{1}\rangle\lhd G
, and therefore
|\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle|=|\langle g_{1}\rangle\langle g_{2}\rangle|=pq
. Hence, they cannot generate
G
.

Lastly, we suppose that
o(g_{1})=q
and
o(g_{2})=q
. Here we only need to consider the case when
\langle g_{1}\rangle\neq\langle g_{2}\rangle
. Based on the preceding discussion, we must have
g_{1}=(x_{1},z_{1})
and
g_{2}=(x_{2},z_{2})
, for some
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
r
be such that
rz_{2}=-z_{1}.
So,
r=-z_{1}z_{2}^{-1}
. Such
r
exists as
z_{2}\neq 0
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
and
r\not\equiv 0\mod q
. We note that
\langle g_{2}\rangle=\langle g_{2}^{r}\rangle
. Therefore,
\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle=\langle g_{1},g_{2}^{r}\rangle
. We note that
o(g_{1}g_{2}^{r})=p
. Let
g=g_{1}g_{2}^{r}
. It is clear that
g_{1},g
generate the same group
\langle g_{1},g_{2}^{r}\rangle=\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle
. However, we have already seen that an element of order
p
and an element of order
q
cannot generate
G
.

Therefore,
G
is not
2
-generated. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Main Results

In this section, we give a proof of
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.3
.
See
1.2

Proof.

Suppose that
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\dots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
is an odd positive integer and let
\pi(n)=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}\}
.

For the forward direction, we assume that
n
is a 2-generated number. It is clear that
n
has to be a cube-free number (see Proposition
3.1
). Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there are primes
p,q\in\pi(n)
such that
p>q
and
p^{2}\mid n
and
q\mid(p-1)
. Then we can construct a group
G
of the form
H\times\mathbb{Z}_{n/(p^{2}q)}
, where
|H|=p^{2}q
and
d(H)=3
. The existence of such a group
H
is guaranteed by Theorem
3.6
, since
q\mid(p-1)
. Thus,
d(H\times\mathbb{Z}_{n/p^{2}q})\geq 3
, contradicting the assumption that
n
is 2-generated. This completes the proof of necessity.

To prove the converse, we use induction on
k
. For the base case
k=1
, the statement holds trivially. Now, assume it is true for some
k>1
.
The inductive case is addressed by considering the following two cases:

(i)

e_{1}=2
;

(ii)

e_{1}=1
.

Case (i):
Suppose
n=p_{1}^{2}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\dots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
is cube-free and for every
i>j
,
p_{j}\nmid(p_{i}-1)
or
e_{i}=1
.
Since
n
is an odd and cube-free number, by Corollary
2.8
, the group
G
is of the form
S_{p_{1}}\rtimes_{\phi}H
, where
S_{p_{1}}
is the Sylow
p_{1}
-subgroup of
G
,
H
is a subgroup of order
n/p_{1}^{2}
, and
\phi
is a homomorphism from
H
to
{\mbox{Aut}}(S_{p_{1}})
.

If
\phi
is the trivial homomorphism, then
G\cong S_{p_{1}}\times H
. Note that
\gcd(p_{1},|H|)=1
. Since
H
has
k-1
distinct prime divisors, by the induction hypothesis, we have
d(H)\leq 2
. Hence, we can apply Lemma
2.6
to obtain

d(G)=\max\{d(S_{p_{1}}),d(H)\}\leq 2.

Therefore,
G
is 2-generated.

Now we assume that
\phi
is a non-trivial homomorphism from
H
to
{\mbox{Aut}}(S_{p_{1}})
. We first consider that
S_{p_{1}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{2}}
,
so
{\mbox{Aut}}(\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{2}})\cong U(p_{1}^{2})
, where
U(m)
denotes the group of units of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. Note that
|U(p_{1}^{2})|=p_{1}(p_{1}-1)
. Since the action
\phi
is non-trivial, we have
\{1\}\neq\phi(H)\leq U(p_{1}^{2})
, which implies
|\phi(H)|\mid p_{1}(p_{1}-1)
. However, this contradicts the assumption that
p_{j}\nmid(p_{1}-1)
for all
p_{j}\in\pi(n)\setminus\{p_{1}\}
. Therefore, such a non-trivial homomorphism cannot exist if
S_{p_{1}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}^{2}}
.

Further, we assume that
S_{p_{1}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}
. Suppose there exists a non-trivial homomorphism
\phi:H\rightarrow{\mbox{Aut}}(\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}})\cong GL(2,p_{1})
. Since
H
is of odd order with the condition that for every
p_{j}\in\pi(n)
,
p_{j}\nmid(p_{1}-1)
, from the proof of Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
, we obtain that
\phi(H)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{b}
for some
b\mid(p_{1}+1),

b\mid|H|
and
\phi(H)
is an irreducible subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(2,p_{1})
.

Since
H
acts on
S_{p_{1}}
via the homomorphism
\phi:H\to\operatorname{GL}(2,p_{1})
, and
\phi(H)
acts irreducibly on
S_{p_{1}}
(see
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
), it follows that the action of
H
on
S_{p_{1}}
is irreducible. That is,
S_{p_{1}}
has no proper nontrivial
H
-invariant subgroups. Hence,
S_{p_{1}}
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
.
Note that
|G/S_{p_{1}}|
has fewer than
k
prime divisors, so we can apply the induction hypothesis to the quotient group, which implies that
d(G/S_{p_{1}})\leq 2
. Suppose
d(G/S_{p_{1}})=1
. Since
S_{p_{1}}
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
, by Theorem
2.3
, we have
d(G)\leq 2
. Suppose
d(G/S_{p_{1}})=2
. By Theorem
2.1
, all the complements of
S_{p_{1}}
in
G
are conjugate to
H
. The number of such conjugates is equal to
[G:N_{G}(H)]
, and since
H\leq N_{G}(H)
, it follows that
[G:N_{G}(H)]\leq p_{1}^{2}
. Thus

|S_{p_{1}}|^{d(G/S_{p_{1}})}=p_{1}^{4}>[G:N_{G}(H)],

and applying Theorem
2.5
, we obtain
d(G)=2
. Therefore,
G
is 2-generated.

Case (ii):
Let
e_{1}=1
. Then
S_{p_{1}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}
. By Corollary
2.8
, we can write
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}\rtimes_{\phi}H
, where
H
is a group of cube-free order with
\gcd(p_{1},|H|)=1
.
By applying the induction hypothesis to
H
we obtain
d(G/S_{p_{1}})=d(H)\leq 2
.

First, suppose
\phi
is the trivial homomorphism. Then, as in the earlier case, applying Lemma
2.6
we conclude that
G
is 2-generated.

Now, suppose
\phi:H\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}^{*}
is a non-trivial homomorphism. If
H
is cyclic, then
G
is a metacyclic group, and therefore 2-generated.

Next, we assume that
H
is non-cyclic, so
d(G/S_{p_{1}})=d(H)=2
. Then
{\mbox{ker}}(\phi)\neq\{1\}
, and
S_{p_{1}}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{1}}
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. By Theorem
2.1
, the complements of
S_{p_{1}}
are precisely the conjugates of
H
. Since
H\leq N_{G}(H)
, it follows that
[G:N_{G}(H)]\leq p_{1}
. Thus

|S_{p_{1}}|^{d(G/S_{p_{1}})}=p_{1}^{2}>[G:N_{G}(H)],

and by Theorem
2.5
, we get
d(G)=2
.

Thus, under the given condition on
n
, every group of order
n
is 2-generated. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we address the case where
n
is an even positive integer and examine the conditions under which
n
is a 2-generated number. First we recall Theorem
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
, which state that
n=4p
is a 2-generated number, whereas
n=2p^{2}
is not a 2-generated number, for any odd prime
p
, respectively.

See
1.3

Proof.

Let
n=2^{\alpha}p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}
, where each
p_{i}
is an odd prime and
\alpha,e_{i}
are positive integers.

Suppose first that
n
is a
2
-generated number. Then by Proposition
3.1
,
n
must be
cube-free
and thus
\alpha\leq 2
. We aim to show that each exponent
e_{i}=1
.

Suppose for contradiction, that
e_{i}=2
for some
i
. Then
2p_{i}^{2}\mid n
. Consider a group
H
of order
2p_{i}^{2}
which is not 2-generated (such a group exists by
Theorem

3.6
). Define
G=H\times\mathbb{Z}_{n/(2p_{i}^{2})}
. Since
d(H)=3
, we have
d(G)\geq 3
, contradicting the assumption that
n
is a
2
-generated number. Thus, each
e_{i}=1
. This proves that if
n
is a
2
-generated number, then
n=2^{\alpha}p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
with
\alpha\leq 2
and
p_{i}
’s are distinct odd primes.

For the other direction, suppose
n=2^{\alpha}p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
with
\alpha\leq 2
. Let
G
be a group of order
n
. We now show that
n
is a 2-generated number. If
\alpha=1
, then
n
is square-free. Thus, all
G
is 2-generated.

We now proceed with the remainder of the proof under the assumption that
\alpha=2
, considering the following two cases:

Case 1:

G
is not solvable. By Theorem
2.7
, any non-solvable group of cube-free order has the form

G=\mathrm{PSL}(2,p)\times L,

where
p\geq 5
is a prime, and
L
is a group of odd square-free order. Since
\gcd(|\mathrm{PSL}(2,p)|,|L|)=1
, and both
\mathrm{PSL}(2,p)
and
L
are 2-generated (the former is non abelian simple, the latter square-free), it follows from Lemma
2.6
that
d(G)=\max\{d(\mathrm{PSL}(2,p)),d(L)\}=2
. Thus, in this case
G
is 2-generated.

Case 2:

G
is solvable. We analyze this case based on the structure of the Sylow 2-subgroup
S_{2}
. There are two possibilities.

First, we suppose that
S_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{4}
. Then all Sylow subgroups of
G
are cyclic, since all odd primes appear only to the first power. Thus,
G
is a
Z-group
. By Theorem
2.2
, Z-groups are 2-generated.

Secondly, we assume
S_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In this case, we proceed by
induction on
k
, the number of distinct odd prime divisors of
n
.

Base case
(
k=1
):
Then
n=4p_{1}
, and by Theorem
3.5
, such groups are always 2-generated.

Inductive step (
k>1
):

Assume every group of order
4p_{1}\cdots p_{k-1}
is 2-generated. Let
G
be a group of order
4p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
. Since
G
is solvable, it contains a
non-trivial minimal normal subgroup
N
, which must be elementary abelian.

Suppose
N\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}}
for some odd prime
p_{i}
.
Then
G/N
has order
4p_{1}\cdots p_{i-1}p_{i+1}\cdots p_{k}
, and by the induction hypothesis, it is 2-generated. If
d(G/N)=1
, then by Theorem
2.3
,
G
is 2-generated. If
d(G/N)=2
, then note that
\gcd(|N|,|G/N|)=1
, so by the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem (see
Theorem

2.1
),
N
is complemented in
G
. Let
H
be a complement of
N
. Since all the complements are conjugate, the number of such complements is bounded by
[G:N_{G}(H)]\leq|N|=p_{i}
. By applying Theorem
2.5
, since
|N|^{d(G/N)}=p_{i}^{2}>[G:N_{G}(H)]
, we conclude that
d(G)=2
.

On the other hand, when
N\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and
G
has no minimal normal subgroup corresponding to any odd prime. In this case,
N
must be the
unique
minimal normal subgroup of
G
. The quotient
G/N
is of square-free order, therefore
d(G/N)\leq 2
. Then by Theorem
2.4
, we have

d(G)=\max\{2,d(G/N)\}=2.

This completes the inductive argument, and hence the proof in all cases.
∎
