Utility of Placental Growth Factor for the Prediction of Miscarriage in the First Trimester of Pregnancy: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Meruyet Kuspanova, Andrey Gaiday, Saule Bermagambetova, Andrii Dinets, Zhanna Amirbekova, Gulmira Zhylkaidar, Gulnaz Sarkuchikova, Svetlana Sakhanova, Aruzhan Amantay, Meruyert Aidarkhanova, Akylbek Tussupkaliyev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAbout 26% of all pregnancies result in miscarriage, with up to 10% occurring in clinically diagnosed pregnancies. Additionally, recurrent pregnancy loss affects approximately 5% of couples of childbearing age. Although there are several known causes of pregnancy loss in the first half, including parental chromosomal abnormalities, uterine malformations the possible role of placental growth factor (PLGF) remains unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate the possible predictive role of serum and urinary PLGF for miscarriages in the first trimester of pregnancy.Materials and MethodsCase-control studies were conducted. The study included 347 pregnant women, the group of miscarriages comprised 156 and the control group 191. The study did not include patients with serious health problems or high risk of miscarriage. Miscarriages were identified: confirmed clinical and ultrasound signs of embryo/fetal expulsion, anembryonic pregnancy and missed abortion. Concentrations of serum and urinary PLGF were detected by ELISA.ResultsSerum PLGF concentrations in miscarriage were 19.8 (12.7–31.9) pg/mL, which was significantly lower than serum PLGF concentrations in uncomplicated pregnancy of 45.5 (25.4–60.0) pg/mL p <0.0001. ROC analysis revealed the diagnostic significance of serum PLGF: AUC 0.765 (CI 0.717–0.809) (p <0.0001), Se 84.6% (CI 77.7–90.0) Sp 68.7% (61.7–75.1), with cut-off ≤35.6 pg/mL, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) 12. Odds ratio for miscarriages for serum PLGF concentration <35.6 pg/mL was 10.9 (6.5–18.4). Evaluation of urinary PLGF did not reveal diagnostic and prognostic efficacy of miscarriages.ConclusionThe serum PLGF might be considered as a prediction marker of the miscarriages with a suggested cut off concentration of 35.6 pg/mL. A further investigation should be carried out on the larger cohorts from various geographical areas.

Introduction

The most common complication of the pregnancy is spontaneous abortion, at a term less than 20 weeks of gestation.1 According to some estimates, the total risk of miscarriages is 15.3% (95% CI 12.5–18.7%) of all diagnosed pregnancies2 and recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) is 5% among couples of childbearing age.3,4 Currently, such risk factors are known as very young or older female age (younger than 20 years and older than 35 years), older male age (older than 40 years), very low or very high body-mass index, black ethnicity, previous miscarriages, smoking, alcohol, stress, working night shifts, air pollution, and exposure to pesticides.2 It is worth to mention that approximately 50% of the cases of pregnancy loss in its first half remain unexplained showing no connection to known causes of pregnancy loss in the first half such as recurrent pregnancy loss, including parental chromosomal abnormalities, uterine malformations, endocrinological disorders and immunological abnormalities.5 Previous studies have revealed that the chorion is exposed to oxidative stress during normal pregnancy and rearrangements on the periphery of chorionic villi are similar to changes during miscarriage, which may be a similar process caused by oxidative stress.6 It has also been established that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and placental growth factor (PLGF) are of key importance in stimulating neovascularization, while soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase (sFlt-1) acts as an inhibitory growth factor.7–9 Disorders of placental vascularization in combination with oxidative tissue damage can lead to the development of preeclampsia, fetal growth retardation and miscarriage in the first trimester of pregnancy.10,11 Recently, it has also been noted that pro- and antiangiogenic factors act as significant markers for predicting pregnancy complications associated with placental dysfunction, such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction.8,9,12 Recent data have demonstrated that altered expression of VEGF family members may be a contributing factor to pregnancy loss and recurrent pregnancy loss.13–15 The hypothesis of imbalance of angiogenic factors (sFLT-1/PLGF) indicates that Flt-1 mRNA expression was significantly and PLGF and Endoglin showed a tendency of higher expression levels in the chorion (placenta) of spontaneous abortions than in cases of induced abortion.16

The aim of this study was to evaluate the possible prognostic role of serum and urinary PLGF for miscarriages in the first trimester of pregnancy.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Local Commission on Bioethics of the West Kazakhstan Medical University (protocol no.3/18 25/03/2020). The study was registered at the Science Committee of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education of the Republic of Kazakhstan (AP1486944), and was funded by Ministry of Science and Higher Education of the Republic of Kazakhstan. All patients signed a written informed consent.

Selection Criteria

The sample size was calculated by the Epi Info™ 7.2.0.1 (CDC USA) statistical processing program using the Unmatched case-control study statistical calculator using the Fleiss method, selecting a 95% confidence interval, with a power of 80%, and a frequency of spontaneous abortions according to published series of 15%,2,17 resulting in a representative sample size of n=344, with a 1:1 group distribution with an imbalance not exceeding 10%.

The inclusion criteria were defined as singleton pregnancy up to 14 weeks; age 18–35 years, absence of chromosomal abnormalities in the subject and in the family history. In order to exclude any confounding effect and according to suggestions in the previously published series, we excluded patients diagnosed with chromosomal abnormalities in the patient and/or in close relatives; pregnancy achieved through assisted reproductive technologies; multiple pregnancy; ectopic pregnancy; diagnosed fetal anomalies in a current pregnancy; taking medications not recommended in the 1st trimester of pregnancy as suggested elsewhere;2,17 addicted to alcohol, drugs, or smoking (including electronic cigarettes or devices). The “case” group included patients with clinically confirmed miscarriages who were admitted to the gynecology department. The “control” group was formed by comparing data (age, BMI, number of miscarriages and medical abortions, birth history, and pregnancy duration at the time of the study) corresponding to the patients in the “case” group.

Clinical Investigations

Gestational age of pregnancy was estimated according to the last menstruation and compared with ultrasound fetometry, which was performed using a Samsung Medison HS-40 ultrasound scanner (Samsung Medison, South Korea). In case of more than 5-day discrepancy between gestational age according to the menstruation and ultrasound scans, ultrasound fetometry data were taken to establish gestational age.18 Miscarriage was identified as a vaginal bleeding with embryo/fetal expulsion detected by gynecological examination and/or ultrasound scanning; anembryonic pregnancy or missed abortion.19–21

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

To assess PLGF concentrations, 5 mL of venous blood and a 20 mL of medium portion of urine were collected. Samples were stored for no more than of 8 weeks, maintained at −20°C until ELISA. ELISA was performed using a kit for analyzing serum and urine for human placental growth factor (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). Optical density was measured using a Dialab ELX808IU microplate reader (Dialab, Austria) by photometry at 450 nm. Optical density was compared with the standard curve recommended by the manufacturer (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), and using Sigma plot software (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The reproducibility was: intra-assay <10%; inter-assay <12%. Concentrations of PLGF were measured and presented in pg/mL.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was performed by Statistica 12.0 (Stat Soft Inc., USA) and MedCalc (MedCalc, USA) programs. To assess the statistical significance, nonparametric statistics were used after identifying the abnormal distribution of data using the Shapir-Wilk test. Shapiro–Wilk test. Medians (Me) and 25 and 75 interquartile range (IQR) were estimated for data with non-normal distribution. Statistical significance for independent variables was assessed by the Mann–Whitney U-test, while the Wilcoxon test was applied for dependent variables.

For proportions, 95% confidence intervals (CI) and statistical relationship were assessed using the χ2 criterion with Yates correction. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve test was applied to determine the cutoff levels of PLGF concentration. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity (Se) and specificity (Sp), Youden index (J), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and positive likelihood ratio (PLR) were also evaluated using ROC analysis. Odds ratio (OR) and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were calculated according to previously published protocols.22 The level of statistical significance was considered as p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this case-control study, we aimed to predict miscarriages. We attempted to compare serum and urine PLGF concentrations in patients with miscarriages and uncomplicated pregnancies. Outcomes were assessed according to the protocols of previous studies and clinical guidelines.19,21,23 It should be noted that in our study, we did not find significant differences in clinical findings other than miscarriage symptoms in patients with miscarriages compared to controls, and we did not consider some factors that might be associated with miscarriages. For example, in study by Jayasena et al evaluated such factors as age, BMI, gestational age, smoking and blood pressure24 or in study by Schuster et al evaluated cervical length measurement and detection of urinary tract infection.23

We found that serum PLGF concentrations in cases of miscarriages were 19.8 (12.7–31.9) pg/mL and were statistically lower in uncomplicated pregnancy. In study by Daponte et al, it was found that the concentration of serum PLGF was lower both in ectopic pregnancy (14.6±3.42 pg/mL) and in pregnancy losses (16.25±4.73 pg/mL) compared to uncomplicated pregnancy (21.64±5.68 pg/mL), however, there were shown lower concentrations of serum PLGF, compared to our study.25 Similarly, in study by Toth et al the average concentrations of serum PLGF, in patients with miscarriages and recurrent pregnancy loss, were 16.07 pg/mL compared with controls 24.32 pg/mL, p = 0.0106.26 Jayasena et al in a study examining the relationship between angiogenic factors and late-term miscarriages, found that serum PLGF concentrations were significantly lower in women with impending miscarriage than in patients with prolonged pregnancies.24 However logistic regression modeling showed that serum PLGF concentrations (odds ratio 0.02 95CI 0.01–0.05, p = 0.0001) were associated with the lower risk of miscarriage after adjusting for age, BMI, gestational age, smoking and blood pressure.24

In study by Muttukrishna et al identified, Muttukrishna et al found that PLGF concentrations were significantly lower in pregnant women with symptoms and subsequent miscarriages (44% decrease, p = 0.001) compared to patients with a symptoms of miscarriage but prolonged pregnancy before term birth.24 In our study serum PLGF concentrations were 2.3 times lower and urinary PLGF concentrations were 17.5% lower in cases of miscarriage compared to control group, which is also consistent with study by Muttukrishna et al.27

In a study by Toth et al it was found that in patients with miscarriage in actual pregnancy and having recurrent miscarriages in the anamnesis, serum PLGF concentrations were 16.07 pg/mL, compared to controls 24.32 pg/mL, p = 0.0106. It was also revealed that, between 6 and 13 weeks of gestation, ROC analysis of the results of adjusted maternal serum PLGF concentrations showed AUC 0.644, PLR 1.73 and NLR 0.96, DOR 1.8, which is in line with our results.26

Urinary PLGF concentrations in cases of miscarriage were found to be statistically significantly lower than in the control group. However, ROC analysis failed to identify it as a reliable diagnostic tool due to its low sensitivity and specificity, therefore, it was not included in the further analysis. Most large-scale studies are devoted to the study of urinary placental growth factor concentrations as a predictor of preeclampsia or other late pregnancy complications.28–30 However, we have not found other studies devoted to the assessment of urinary placental growth factor concentration in spontaneous pregnancy losses. Additionally, our study had a limitation regarding the determination of urinary PLGF concentrations: glomerular filtration rate and serum creatinine concentration were not evaluated, lack of longitudinal follow-up and absence of multivariate analysis were also not performed. Urinary PLGF concentrations in cases of miscarriages should be investigated in future studies. According to the results of our study, and taking into account the limitations, we conclude that further research is needed to explore the relationship between urinary PLGF concentrations and spontaneous pregnancy loss in the first half of pregnancy.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that serum PLGF concentrations in the first trimester of pregnancy might be considered as an additional biochemical marker for the prediction of miscarriage. The role of PLGF in urine as a diagnostic marker of pregnancy failure requires further evaluation. Determination of the role of urinary PLGF as a diagnostic marker for miscarriages requires further evaluation.
