Multi-omics analysis reveals that puerarin modulates the gut-liver axis to improve hepatic health in aged laying hens
Authors: Xubin Du, Dong Zhao, Yuchen Zhang, Xinyue Wu, Jiawen He, Fei Liu, Debing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge-related hepatic metabolic dysfunction significantly impairs the health and productivity of laying hens. Puerarin (Pue), a natural isoflavone, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities relevant to liver and gut health. Our previous work demonstrated that dietary supplementation with Pue improved laying performance and egg quality, while reducing the feed conversion ratio in aged laying hens. Building on these findings, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach to investigate whether Pue exerts hepatoprotective effects by restoring gut-liver axis homeostasis, thereby supporting liver function and potentially extending the production performance of aged laying hens.ResultsPue supplementation significantly ameliorated hepatic pathological changes, including vacuolar degeneration, lipid accumulation, fibrosis, and apoptosis. Additionally, Pue reduced malondialdehyde levels and enhanced the activities of total antioxidant capacity, glutathione peroxidase, and catalase in the liver. Transcriptomic and serum metabolomic analyses revealed that Pue upregulated CYP7B1 and downregulated CYP7A1, thereby altering bile acid profiles without affecting total bile acid levels. Molecular dynamics simulations further demonstrated that Pue exhibits a stronger binding affinity to CYP7B1 than to CYP7A1. Although Pue treatment did not significantly alter the α-diversity and β-diversity of the gut microbiota, it significantly increased the abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group and enhanced butyric acid production, which in turn elevated the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins (Claudin-1 and Claudin-3). Multi-omics correlation analysis confirmed the regulatory network between the gut and liver.ConclusionsPue enhances hepatic health in aged laying hens by modulating bile acid metabolism and altering gut microbiota composition, primarily through gut-liver axis regulation as revealed by integrated multi-omics analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12949-8.

Introduction

China is the world’s largest egg producer and consumer, accounting for approximately 40% of global egg production in 2020 [1]. The late laying period, defined as the stage when the laying rate declines below 80% after 48 weeks of age, constitutes nearly half of a hen’s productive lifespan [2]. Extending the productive lifespan of laying hens is therefore essential for reducing feeding costs and improving economic efficiency. Recent studies have demonstrated that natural plant-derived compounds can enhance laying performance by modulating the gut-liver axis [3]. In our previous work, dietary supplementation with puerarin (Pue), a major isoflavone extracted from Pueraria lobata, significantly improved laying performance and egg quality, while reducing the feed conversion ratio [4]. Building on these findings, the present study explored whether and how Pue enhanced hepatic health via gut-liver axis modulation to support prolonged productivity in aged laying hens.

Hepatic lipid accumulation and impaired lipid metabolism are closely linked to the onset of metabolic dysfunction and reduced egg production in aged laying hens [3, 5, 6]. Concurrently, age-associated disruption of the gut microbiota and intestinal barrier function emerges as a parallel driver, impairing nutrient absorption and amplifying systemic inflammation [7]. This dynamic interplay occurs via the gut-liver axis, enabling bidirectional crosstalk: microbial metabolites regulate hepatic functions, while bile acids (BA) as key hepatic secretions modulate the gut microbial composition. Physiologically, a bidirectional circuitous gut-liver exchange is facilitated by portal venous flow and biliary secretion. The system mediates hepatic exposure to gut-derived microbial products, including endotoxins and metabolites [8]. Conversely, BA secreted by the liver reaches the intestine via the bile duct, influencing the composition of gut microbiota [9, 10].

Multi-omics approaches offer powerful tools for resolving complex physiological interactions across tissues. In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of liver transcriptomes, serum non-targeted metabolomes, and gut microbial profiles based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing. This study aimed to determine whether Pue mitigates age-related hepatic steatosis in laying hens through modulation of gut-liver axis homeostasis, thereby contributing to improved systemic health during aging. Our findings offer mechanistic insights into the hepatoprotective effects of Pue and establish a molecular basis for its role in maintaining metabolic integrity in aged avian models.

Materials and methods

Puerarin, animals, diets, experimental design

Puerarin (Pue, purity > 98%) was obtained from a commercial supplier (Huatai Shaanxi Biotechnology Co., Ltd) [4]. A total of 180 Hy-Line Brown laying hens (480 days old) were randomly assigned to four dietary treatment groups. Each treatment comprised three replicates, with each replicate consisting of 15 hens distributed across five cages (three hens per cage; cage dimensions: 50 cm × 45 cm × 42 cm). Before the formal experiment, all hens underwent a two-week acclimatization period to the basal diet [4]. Over the course of the 42-day feeding trial, the hens were distributed among four dietary treatments: three groups supplemented with Pue at 200 mg/kg (L-Pue), 400 mg/kg (M-Pue), or 800 mg/kg (H-Pue), and a control group that received a basic diet (Con). Table 1 lists the detailed composition and nutrient levels of the experimental diets [4]. The hens were housed in a temperature-controlled environment (20–25 °C) with relative humidity maintained at 60–70%. A 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle was provided using white incandescent lamps at an intensity of 30–40 lx. At the end of the experiment, hens were fasted before euthanasia via exsanguination from the jugular vein. Serum, liver tissue, and cecal contents were promptly collected and stored for subsequent analysis at -80 °C.

Histopathological analysis

Liver samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Following deparaffinization, stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), Oil Red O, Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius Red to assess histological features. Stained sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and scanned using an Ocus 40 slide scanner.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TUNEL) staining assay

Apoptotic cell death is characterized by DNA fragmentation, which was identified using the TUNEL assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). This method enzymatically catalyzes the binding of fluorescent-dUTP to 3’-hydroxyl termini of single-stranded DNA breaks generated during endonuclease activation. The stained sections were scanned using an Ocus 40 digital slide scanner (Grundium Ltd., Finland).

Hepatic enzyme activities and biochemical parameters

Liver samples were collected from the middle of the right lobe from 6 hens per treatment in both the Con and M-Pue groups. Hepatic superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), total cholesterol (TC), and total triglyceride (TG) levels were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Angle Gene Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China).

Transcriptome sequencing and enrichment analysis

For both the Con and M-Pue groups, hepatic tissues were consistently sampled from the middle of the right lobe of 3 individual hens per group. Liver tissues were processed for total RNA extraction using an RNA Isolation Kit (Life Technologies, USA). The mRNA sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150 bp mode. To ensure data reliability, raw reads were subjected to rigorous quality control. Clean data were obtained by removing reads with adapter contamination (> 5 bp), low-quality reads (where bases with quality score Q < 15 accounted for > 30% of the read length), and reads containing more than 5% unknown nucleotides (N). The clean reads were then mapped to the chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus, GRCg7b) using HISAT2 (v2.0.4). Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized using the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) method, which eliminates the influence of gene length and sequencing depth disparities. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 (v1.51.7) package in R (v4.4.0). The statistical framework of DESeq2 is based on the negative binomial distribution model to estimate variance and test for differential expression among samples. Lowly expressed genes were inherently filtered by the default independent filtering algorithm of the DESeq2 package before statistical testing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the M-Pue and Con groups were identified strictly based on the thresholds of p-value ≤ 0.05 and |log2Ratio| ≥ 1. Biological function and process enrichment analyses of the DEGs were subsequently performed using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/) [11]. Because the chicken (Gallus gallus) genome is not natively supported by Metascape, the DEGs were analyzed by mapping them to their human (Homo sapiens) orthologs. The analysis was conducted using default annotation settings, including a p-value cutoff < 0.01, a minimum count of 3, and an enrichment factor > 1.5. The gene annotation databases selected for this analysis included KEGG Pathway, GO Biological Processes, Reactome Gene Sets, Canonical Pathways, CORUM, WikiPathways, and PANTHER Pathway. p-values were calculated based on the cumulative hypergeometric distribution, and q-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to account for multiple testing. Enriched terms were hierarchically clustered into a network using Kappa scores (> 0.3) as the similarity metric, and the final interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

For both Con and M-Pue groups, cecal contents were collected from the same 6 hens per treatment that were used for liver sampling. Total genomic DNA from cecal contents was isolated with the PowerSoil® Kit (MO BIO, USA), integrity-confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and quantified via Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). DNA concentrations were normalized to 1 ng/µL before amplifying the 16 S rRNA V3–V4 region using barcoded primers and Phusion® HIFI Master Mix (NEB, USA). Target-size amplicons (400–450 bp) were gel-extracted (QIAquick Kit, Qiagen, Germany), pooled equimolarly, and processed into libraries with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, USA). Library QC (Qubit 3.0; Bioanalyzer 2100, Agilent, USA) preceded 2 × 300 bp paired-end sequencing on Illumina MiSeq.

Paired-end reads obtained from the Illumina MiSeq platform were merged using PEAR (v0.9.10) and subjected to strict quality control according to the QIIME pipeline (v1.9.1). Chimeric sequences were subsequently identified and removed using the UCHIME algorithm against the Gold database to yield effective clean tags. The UPARSE pipeline (v8.1.1861) was employed to cluster the effective tags into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence identity threshold. The most abundant sequence within each OTU was selected as the representative sequence, and taxonomy was assigned using the uclust method against the SILVA database. For subsequent α and β diversity analyses, the OTU abundance table was normalized by subsampling (rarefying) all samples to the minimum sequencing depth to eliminate the influence of uneven library sizes.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) detection

To further evaluate gut health, SCFAs were measured in the cecal contents obtained from the same 6 hens in both the Con and M-Pue groups. Stock solutions of 2-methylbutyric acid (Dr. Ehrenstorfer, Germany), valeric acid (CNW, Germany), isobutyric acid (Dr. Ehrenstorfer, Germany), isovaleric acid (Dr. Ehrenstorfer, Germany), caproic acid (Rhawn, China), butyric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), acetic acid (CNW, Germany), and propionic acid (AN, China) standards were freshly formulated in methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, CNW, Germany) at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. For sample preparation, 20 mg of cecal material was accurately weighed into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 0.5% (v/v) phosphoric acid and a stainless steel bead. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and vortexed for 10 min. It was then subjected to ultrasonic treatment for a further 5 min. The resulting supernatant was collected after centrifugation. An additional 500 µL of MTBE solution containing the internal standard was then added, followed by a further 3 min of vortexing and 5 min of sonication. After a final centrifugation under the same conditions, the upper phase was carefully collected for subsequent GC-MS/MS analysis.

Serum non-targeted metabolomic analysis

Serum samples from six hens per group were thawed on ice and extracted with an acetonitrile: methanol (1:4, v/v) solution containing internal standards. After centrifugation and protein precipitation at -20 °C, the supernatants were analyzed using an LC-MS system equipped with a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column. MS data were acquired in IDA mode on a TripleTOF system (Sciex) covering a mass range of 50–1000 Da. The raw data were processed and normalized via unit variance scaling (UV) to ensure a normal distribution (mean = 0, variance = 1). Multivariate statistical analysis, including Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), was performed using the R package ropls. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were identified based on a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score > 1 and a P-value < 0.05 (Student’s t-test). Finally, pathway enrichment analysis of the DEMs was conducted using MetaboAnalystR (version 3.0) based on the hypergeometric test against the KEGG database.

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (Solarbio, China) and quantified using a BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Equal protein amounts were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE (NCM, China) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). Membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Claudin-1 and Claudin-3 (1:2000, Wanleibio, China), and GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech, China) as a loading control. After TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Proteintech, China) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL reagent (Vazyme, China) and quantified via ImageJ (v1.52a).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from intestinal mucosa using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the RevertAid Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). qPCR was conducted in triplicate on a LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Switzerland) [4]. Gene expression was quantified using the 2− ΔΔCT method [12]. Primer sequences for target genes, including GPX1, SOD2, NQO1, CYP7B1, CYP7A1, HSD3B1, and the reference gene β-actin, together with their GenBank accession numbers, are listed in Table 2.

Molecular docking

CYP7B1 and CYP7A1 amino acid sequences were obtained from the UniProt database, and the AlphaFold database was used to estimate their 3D structures. PyMOL (v2.4) was used to process and show the generated models. Utilizing the PubChem database, the chemical structure of Pue was acquired. The CB-Dock2 web server was used to conduct molecular docking utilizing a blind docking technique [13]. The PLIP web tool was used to profile protein-ligand interactions, and PyMOL was used to view the docking conformations that were produced.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

All-atom MD simulations were independently conducted for each protein–ligand complex obtained from the docking results, using GROMACS software (v2020.6) [14]. Each simulation was run for 50 ns. The AMBER force field was employed for protein parameterization, while the General Amber Force Field (GAFF) was used for ligands [15, 16]. System setup involved hydrogen addition via the pdb2gmx tool, solvation in a cubic TIP3P water box with a 10 Å margin, and ion placement to achieve a physiological Na⁺/Cl⁻ concentration of 0.145 M. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm with standard convergence criteria. The system was subsequently equilibrated through a 100-ps NVT phase (0→310.15 K) followed by a 100-ps NPT phase at 1 bar using standard pressure coupling. Production MD simulations employed a 2-fs timestep with LINCS constraints. Long-range electrostatics were treated with PME (real-space cutoff: 1.2 nm), while short-range interactions utilized appropriate cutoffs. Trajectory analysis included RMSD evaluation and MM/GBSA binding free energy estimation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (v20.0). For hepatic biochemical markers (TG and TC), hepatic antioxidant parameters (T-AOC, SOD, GSH-Px, CAT, and MDA), intestinal TBA levels, and intestinal tight junction protein levels, statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For RT-qPCR results, gut microbiota relative abundance, and DEMs, differences between the Con and M-Pue groups were evaluated using the independent samples t-test. Pearson correlation coefficients between gut microbiota and hepatic antioxidant enzymes, liver function markers, and other physiological parameters were calculated in R (v4.4.0) and visualized using the Corrplot package (v0.95). Raw metabolomic data were pre-processed via total sum normalization, log-transformation, and auto-scaling. DEMs were identified based on VIP > 1 and P < 0.05. Subsequently, a signed weighted co-expression network was constructed using the R package WGCNA after removing outliers via hierarchical clustering. Based on the scale-free topology criterion (R2 > 0.80), a soft-thresholding power of β = 30 was selected to construct a signed adjacency matrix, which was further transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Metabolic modules were identified using average linkage hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree-cut algorithm (minimum module size = 15), with similar modules (correlation > 0.75) being merged. The Module Eigengene (ME) for each module was calculated, and Spearman rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the associations between MEs and the top 10 microbial genera (P < 0.05). All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of Pue on liver pathology and liver function

Liver tissues from aged laying hens were subjected to histopathological examination and functional assessment. H&E staining revealed pronounced structural damage in the Con group, including vacuolar degeneration and infiltration of inflammatory cells. In contrast, Pue supplementation markedly attenuated these pathological features (Fig. 1A-B). Oil Red O staining demonstrated pronounced hepatic lipid accumulation, which was reduced following Pue supplementation (Fig. 1C). Masson’s trichrome and Sirius Red staining further demonstrated decreased liver fibrosis following Pue administration (Fig. 1D-E). TUNEL staining showed a reduction in apoptotic cells in the Pue-treated groups (Fig. 1F). Moreover, biochemical analyses revealed that Pue markedly lowered hepatic levels of TG and TC (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G–H).

Effects of Pue on liver transcriptome profiling

Consistent with our previous study showing that dietary M-Pue supplementation improves laying performance and egg quality in aged hens [4], we further found that M-Pue improves hepatic health. To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, we performed transcriptomic analysis on liver tissues from the Con and M-Pue groups. Liver transcriptional profiling was performed to identify genes implicated in Pue-mediated improvements in hepatic metabolism. The analysis generated 37.26 to 44.28 million clean reads per sample (Table S1), with the vast majority mapping efficiently to the reference genome (overall mapping rate: 93.34%–94.62%; Table S2). RNA sequencing revealed a total of 830 DEGs, including 439 upregulated and 391 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Materials 1). These DEGs were visualized as a volcano plot and protein-protein interaction network (PPI) joint analysis plot (remove three outliers; Fig. 2B) and scatter diagram (Fig. 2C). The genes related to Pue modulating liver antioxidant capacity and BAs metabolism were revealed in the volcano plot and PPI joint network (Fig. 2B).

Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed. The top 20 enriched GO terms were visualized as functional clusters distinguished by color (Fig. S1). Additionally, the DEG-associated pathway interaction network (Fig. 2D) demonstrated that these genes were predominantly involved in a variety of biological processes, such as hemostasis, cellular response to organonitrogen compound, response to hypoxia, carboxylic acid metabolic process, cytoskeleton in muscle cells, response to oxidative stress (OS), actin filament-based process, mitotic cell cycle, cellular homeostasis, response to steroid hormone (Fig. 2D).

Aging can disrupt redox balance in the body, enhancing antioxidant capacity crucial for combating aging. As shown in the heatmap (Fig. 2E), redox-associated genes such as STAT2 and MAPK1 were downregulated in the Pue-treated group. In contrast, multiple genes implicated in OS response—including FOXO1, GSTT1, LHPP, GPX1, GSTK1, GPX4, NQO1, SOD2, and GSTT1L—were significantly upregulated (Fig. 2E). We further evaluated antioxidant enzyme activity and OS biomarkers. Notably, MDA levels were significantly lower, while SOD, T-AOC, and CAT activities were significantly higher (P < 0.05), and GSH-Px levels showed an increasing trend in the Pue group (P > 0.05; Fig. 2F). These findings are in line with our earlier research, which showed that Pue increases the antioxidant capacity [4].

Aging laying hens commonly exhibit hepatic lipid dysregulation, in which BA metabolism plays a critical regulatory role in maintaining lipid homeostasis [17]. The heatmap of BA metabolism-related genes, including CYP7A1, HSD3B1, CYP2W2, SLC51A, ABCB6, PLTP, and CYP7B1, showed a tendency toward up-/downregulation in the Pue group (Fig. 2G). Based on transcriptomic profiling and functional annotation, three DEGs involved in BA metabolism (CYP7B1, CYP7A1, and HSD3B1) and three redox-associated genes (GPX1, SOD2, and NQO1) were chosen for qPCR validation. qPCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data, confirming similar expression patterns across both platforms (Fig. 2H).

Distinct binding profiles of Pue with CYP7B1 and CYP7A1

Using the crystal structures of CYP7B1 and CYP7A1 proteins predicted by AlphaFold3, Pue was selected for molecular docking. The docking results showed that Pue formed an H-bond with residues Met355, Tyr130, Arg255, Tyr111, and Ala101 of CYP7B1, along with a π–π stacking interaction with Phe97, resulting in a binding free energy of -8.6 kcal/mol (Fig. 3A). In CYP7A1, Pue formed H-bond with residues Asp350, Glu428, Lys360, and Lys440, with a binding free energy of -8.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 3B). MD simulations showed that Pue caused no significant fluctuations in the RMSD of the CYP7B1 structure (Fig. 3C), whereas CYP7A1 exhibited notable structural fluctuations (Fig. 3F). Additionally, the MM/GBSA binding free energy of Pue with CYP7B1 was -47 kcal/mol (Table 3), whereas that with CYP7A1 was − 25.19 kcal/mol (Table 4). During the entire 50 ns simulation, the binding positions of Pue with CYP7B1 and CYP7A1 remained relatively stable, with only minor conformational adjustments observed in CYP7B1 (Fig. 3D-E). However, the binding site of Pue in CYP7A1 underwent significant changes (Fig. 3G-H).

Pue modulates microbiota and enhances the gut barrier

Based on previous observations regarding production performance, egg quality [4], and liver histopathology, we selected the Con and M-Pue groups for further analysis of intestinal microbiota. A total of 2,128 distinct OTUs were identified across all samples. A Venn diagram was used to analyse microbial composition similarities between the two groups, revealing 1,086 shared operational taxonomic units (OTUs), with 505 specific to the Con group and 537 specific to the M-Pue group (Fig. 4A). Regarding α-diversity, no significant differences were observed in the Shannon index, Chao1 index, or PD_whole_tree (P > 0.05; Fig. 4B–D). In terms of β-diversity, as shown in Fig. S2, the non-metric multi-dimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis based on OTU levels indicated that the microbial community structure did not differ significantly between the Con and Pue groups (R2 = 0.0914, P = 0.429), with a Stress value of 0.151, suggesting that the NMDS plot provides a reliable representation of the microbial distribution. Pue treatment altered bacterial abundance across different taxonomic levels (Fig.S3A–F, Supplementary Materials 2). Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominated both groups (Fig. S3A). Pue treatment significantly reduced Lactobacillaceae relative abundance while increasing Ruminococcaceae and Rikenellaceae relative abundance (P < 0.05; Fig. S3D). At the genus level, Pue shifted the microbial composition by increasing Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group and depleting Lactobacillus and Parabacteroides populations (P < 0.05; Fig. 4E). Additionally, Pue treatment significantly increased butyric acid content in the cecum (P < 0.05), while levels of 2-methylbutyric acid, valeric acid, isobutyric acid, acetic acid, and propionic acid showed no statistical significance (P > 0.05; Fig. 4F-M). Elevated expression of Claudin-1 and Claudin-3 indicates that Pue exerts a protective effect on intestinal barrier integrity (P < 0.05; Fig. 4N).

Effects of Pue on the serum metabolome

Building upon our previous findings [4], we selected the Con and M-Pue groups for serum metabolomic profiling. LC-MS/MS analysis identified a total of 1,561 metabolites. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear metabolic differentiation between the two groups, suggesting distinct biochemical alterations (Fig. 5A). Relative to the control, a total of 93 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were upregulated, while 80 DEMs were significantly downregulated in the Pue-treated group (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Material 3). The z-score plot identified the top 50 DEMs with the highest variable importance in projection (VIP) values, such as 5-methoxy-L-tryptophan, N-(2-hydroxyethyl) icosanamide, 7alpha-hydroxycholesterol, D-kynurenine, nervonic acid, oleoyltaurine, among others (Fig. 5C, Table S3). The score plots from partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) revealed a clear separation between the Con and M-Pue groups (Fig. 5D-G). To evaluate the possibility of model overfitting, permutation tests were conducted [18]. The data of the R2 values (yellow line) were higher than those of the Q2 values (purple line), indicating a low likelihood of overfitting in the models (Fig. 5H-K).

Effects of Pue on metabolic pathways in the serum

All DEMs were functionally annotated through the KEGG database, and pathway enrichment was carried out based on the hypergeometric test. In the positive ion mode, 58 DEMs enriched in 33 KEGG pathways. Among these, alpha-linolenic acid, linoleic acid, glycerophospholipid, and arachidonic acid metabolism pathways, along with general metabolic pathways, were the most enriched (Fig. 6A). In the negative ion mode, 16 DEMs were associated with 14 KEGG pathways, among which the most enriched were general metabolic pathways, along with those involved in glycerophospholipid and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 6B). A pathway enrichment map analysis using MetaboAnalystR [19] revealed that DEMs between the Con and Pue groups were predominantly involved in three metabolic pathways: tyrosine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and vitamin B6 metabolism (Fig. 6C).

Transcriptomics deciphers gene-level regulation, whereas metabolomics reflects downstream metabolic shifts, together providing a comprehensive understanding of biological function. The integrated analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics enables a more accurate and comprehensive explanation of potential regulatory mechanisms influencing phenotypes. Initially, we identified redox-associated genes and BA metabolism-related genes from the transcriptome and performed correlation analyses with metabolites in the metabolome (Fig. 7B-C). Redox-associated genes identified in the transcriptome (GPX1, GPX4, GSTT1, GSTK1, GSTT1L, NQO1, and MAPK1) were enriched in five key pathways, and showed correlations with key bioactive metabolites, such as 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, 16-hydroxyhexadecanoic acid, gamma-linolenic acid, and chenodeoxycholic acid (Fig. 7B). BA metabolism-related genes identified in the transcriptome (CYP7A1, CYP2W2, HSD3B1, and CYP7B1) were enriched for four key pathways, and were closely related to some important biofunctional metabolites such as glycocholic acid, 7alpha-hydroxycholesterol, chenodeoxycholic acid, 4-oxoretinoic acid, and 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (Fig. 7C). WGCNA was performed to identify metabolite modules linked to specific microbial taxa. First, a soft-thresholding power of β = 30 was selected to ensure the construction of a scale-free topology network (Fig. S4). Based on the module–trait relationships, the abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group exhibited a positive correlation with the MEyellow module. Lactobacillus was positively correlated with the MEred and MEmidnightblue modules, while showing a negative correlation with MEyellow. Furthermore, Faecalibacterium displayed negative correlations with the MEblue and MElightyellow modules. In contrast, Parabacteroides was positively associated with MEred but negatively associated with the MEgreen module (Fig. S5, Supplementary Material 4).

Discussion

The aging process of laying hens is often accompanied by excessive liver fat deposition as well as a decline in intestinal function and gut microbiota dysbiosis, which affect performance and may pose serious health risks [20]. Our previous research has confirmed that Pue enhances the production performance and egg quality of aged laying hens, improves antioxidant capacity, and positively influences serum hormone levels and intestinal morphology [4]. This study aims to clarify how Pue modulates hepatic metabolism via gut-liver axis regulation.

As a multifunctional organ, the liver plays an essential role in systemic metabolism, BA homeostasis, immune surveillance, and the clearance of endogenous and exogenous toxins [21]. Maintaining hepatic function is therefore essential for sustaining optimal egg production and ensuring egg quality in laying hens [6]. In aged laying hens, the liver is prone to excessive lipid deposition and oxidative damage, and such pathological insults can profoundly compromise both metabolic integrity and egg production efficiency. Normal hepatocytes are arranged in a plate-like pattern, radiating radially outward from the central vein [21]. However, the liver exhibited the accumulation of lipid droplets, hepatocellular swelling, and vacuolar degeneration, accompanied by mild hepatic fibrosis in aged laying hens, and Pue effectively ameliorated these pathological changes in our study. Pue has been shown to attenuate liver dysfunction and hyperlipidemia, and to reduce TC, TG, AST, and ALT in the blood of a mouse model of MAFLD [22]. Similarly, in our previous studies, we have demonstrated that Pue can reduce serum TC, TG, and ALT [4]. Our results demonstrated that Pue treatment effectively reduced hepatic levels of TG and TC, contributing to improved hepatic metabolic function in aged laying hens.

Aging disrupts the redox balance in favor of oxidative processes, resulting in excessive ROS generation and subsequent oxidative injury in hepatic and intestinal tissues [5, 7, 23]. To defend against oxidative challenges, organisms rely on a well-coordinated antioxidant system, predominantly involving enzymatic antioxidants such as SOD, GSH-Px, and T-AOC. Conversely, MDA, a lipid peroxidation end-product, is widely recognized as a biomarker of oxidative injury [24, 25]. Supplementation with natural antioxidants, such as lycopene, Flammulina velutipes stem, and quercetin, has been shown to enhance antioxidant enzyme activities and reduce MDA levels [26–29]. Evidence suggests that Pue improves redox homeostasis by boosting endogenous antioxidant activity and reducing lipid peroxidation [30]. Our study found that the expression of FOXO1, GSTT1, LHPP, GPX1, GSTK1, GPX4, NQO1, SOD2, and GSTT1L were upregulated, while STAT2 and MAPK1 were found to be downregulated. FOXO1 modulates numerous targets, among which antioxidant enzymes are important ones [31]. Qi et al. [32] reported that treatment with grape seed proanthocyanidin extract significantly upregulated the expression of Foxo1 and its downstream targets, including SOD1, SOD2, and CAT, thereby mitigating hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell damage induced by ionizing radiation. Similarly, in our experiments, we found that Pue enabled the expression level of FOXO1 mRNA and upregulated SOD2 gene expression, as well as SOD and CAT enzyme activities in the liver. GPX1 utilizes selenocysteine as its active center, primarily functioning to reduce toxic peroxides (e.g., hydrogen peroxide) via glutathione (GSH), thereby maintaining redox balance [33–35]. GPX4 catalyzes the reduction of complex lipid hydroperoxides, thereby protecting cell membranes from oxidative damage. This function is distinct from other glutathione peroxidases (e.g., GPX1), which primarily reduce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and small-molecule hydroperoxides [33, 36]. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the Pue group exhibited significantly higher levels of both GPX1 and GPX4 genes. Although GSH-Px enzyme activity was not significantly increased in the liver, our previous study demonstrated that Pue enhanced serum GSH-Px activity in aged laying hens [4]. GSTT1 [37], GSTK1 [38], and GSTT1L [39] are members of the glutathione S-transferase (GSTs) family, which are thought to be associated with antioxidant activity. In our study, all three of these genes were found to be significantly upregulated, suggesting that Pue can activate the GSTs family enzyme activity and participate in antioxidant activity. In addition, we found that the GSTs family is associated with the synthesis of GCA, CDCA, and 7alpha-Hydroxycholesterol.

BAs are small molecules derived from cholesterol in the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and secreted into the duodenum, where they are subsequently converted into secondary BAs by the gut microbiota [40, 41]. Primary BAs are synthesized through either the classical or alternative pathway in the liver. In these processes, 7α-hydroxylation of cholesterol is catalyzed by CYP7A1 in the classical pathway; in the alternative pathway, cholesterol is first hydroxylated at the 27-position by CYP27A1, and subsequent 7α-hydroxylation of oxysterol intermediates is catalyzed by CYP7B1. The resulting BAs are subsequently secreted into the bile and then enter the intestinal tract [41]. In the high-fat diet model, hyodeoxycholic acid activates liver CYP7B1 and inhibits CYP7A1, thereby shifting the biosynthesis process of BA from the classical pathway to the alternative pathway, while Pue appears to have the same function in aged laying hens [42]. According to Wen et al. [43], a high-cholesterol, low-choline diet in laying hens significantly upregulated hepatic CYP7A1 expression and disrupted serum BA metabolism. In arsenic-exposed chickens, a damaged liver disrupts BA homeostasis by strongly upregulating CYP7A1, leading to excessive accumulation of total BAs in serum [44]. These findings suggest that aberrant activation of the classical BA synthesis pathway may contribute to hepatic lipid accumulation and metabolic dysregulation. The reduction in CYP7A1 expression in the present study further led to a decrease in serum 7alpha-hydroxycholesterol level, which subsequently led to decreased levels of CDCA and GCA, while serum α-MCA levels were found to be increased. Due to the activation of the alternative pathway of BA metabolism, we found no statistically significant difference in levels of total BAs compared to the Con group in the serum, which may be caused by changes in intestinal absorption efficiency or microbial metabolism. Multiple studies have indicated that modulating the BA profile may contribute to delaying the aging process. Dietary interventions, including the Mediterranean diet and calorie restriction, are known to influence gut microbiota composition and BA metabolism, thereby potentially promoting health during aging [45–48]. Both of the above enzymes are closely related to Pue’s ability to improve BA disorders in aged laying hens, as well as to change the BA profile. Pue can bind to these two core proteins, which was further confirmed by molecular docking and MD simulation. Molecular docking showed that Pue could bind to both CYP7A1 and CYP7B1, but MD simulation showed that Pue had a better affinity for the CYP7B1 protein. Together, these findings highlight Pue’s potential to rebalance BA metabolism through selective modulation of hepatic CYP7A1 and CYP7B1, offering a nutritional strategy to mitigate age-related hepatic dysfunction in laying hens.

In the KEGG pathway analysis of serum metabolites, we found that Pue influenced not only the primary BA metabolic pathway but also α-MCA metabolism and arachidonic acid metabolism. These metabolic pathways play crucial roles in host metabolism, immune responses, OS, neurodegeneration, and the regulation of gut microbiota composition [49–53]. Using MetaboAnalystR 3.0, we identified three pathways enriched by DEMs between the Con and Pue groups: pyrimidine metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, and vitamin B6 metabolism. These findings underscore the multifaceted regulatory potential of Pue across diverse metabolic pathways, which may collectively contribute to the restoration and maintenance of physiological homeostasis, thereby significantly enhancing the overall health and longevity of aged laying hens.

Acting as a central regulator of host physiology, the gut microbiota plays a key role in preserving intestinal homeostasis while simultaneously interacting with extraintestinal organs, such as the liver, via microbial metabolites [54]. Reduced intestinal barrier function and microbiota dysbiosis are also commonly associated with reduced performance in laying hens [55]. Aging of the intestinal tract is often accompanied by decreased microbial diversity, with a loss of beneficial symbionts and an increased abundance of opportunistic pathogens, which may contribute to impaired barrier function and systemic inflammation [56, 57]. Parabacteroides, which are generally opportunistic pathogens, have also been extensively studied for their relevance to aging-related diseases such as inflammation [58–60]. Similarly, in this study, Parabacteroides was abundant in aged hens but significantly reduced by Pue supplementation. In addition, it has been shown that Parabacteroides is present in large quantities in the frail elderly, reaffirming the key influence of this bacterium on the aging process [61]. In the Pue group, the abundance of the genus Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was significantly increased, a taxon known to produce SCFAs, particularly butyric acid [62–64]. Consistently, our results also showed elevated butyric acid levels in this group. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a positive association between Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group abundance and butyric acid concentration, suggesting a potential link between this genus and butyric acid production. WGCNA further revealed that Parabacteroides was positively correlated with the MEred module (enriched in several pro-inflammatory LPCs). This suggests that Parabacteroides may drive age-related systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [60, 65]. Conversely, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was positively associated with the MEyellow module, which is characterized by the energy regulator α-ketoglutaric acid and anti-inflammatory prostaglandins [66–68]. Combined with the increased serum butyric acid levels, our findings indicate that Pue promotes a shift from a pro-inflammatory metabolic profile (MEred) to a protective and energy-efficient one (MEyellow). This microbial-metabolic reprogramming effectively mitigates hepatic dysfunction in aged laying hens.

The metabolites SCFAs play a crucial role in intestinal mucosal protection, of which butyric acid, as one of the SCFAs, has been recognized as a potential alternative to antibiotics in livestock production due to its many beneficial effects on poultry health, performance, and egg quality when used in feed rations [69, 70]. The present study demonstrated that butyric acid was the most notable intestinal bacterial metabolite following Pue intervention. A balanced gut microbiota and its metabolic outputs are essential for sustaining liver health, primarily through their roles in immune regulation, BA metabolism, and inflammatory signaling [71, 72]. Existing studies suggest inhibition of diseases such as hepatocellular carcinoma and cholestatic liver injury by promoting gut microbial sources of butyric acid [73, 74]. Butyric acid functions by promoting epithelial tight junctions (e.g., upregulating Claudin-1, Claudin-7, and ZO-1), serving as an energy source for colonocyte ATP production, and modulating immune responses [75]. In our study, elevated Claudin-1 and Claudin-3 protein expression were observed, consistent with our previous findings. These results suggest that Pue enhances intestinal barrier integrity by stimulating butyric acid production, which in turn reinforces tight junction assembly. This mechanism likely facilitates mucosal barrier restoration and mitigates the translocation of deleterious substances through the gut–liver axis, thereby playing a pivotal role in preserving hepatic health in aged laying hens.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive multi-omics characterization of Pue’s regulatory effects on the gut-liver axis in naturally aged laying hens. Through transcriptomic, metabolomic, and microbiota analyses, we demonstrate that Pue modulates hepatic BA metabolism by upregulating CYP7B1 and downregulating CYP7A1, thereby shifting BA synthesis toward the alternative pathway. Concurrently, Pue remodels gut microbial composition, enhances butyrate production, and promotes intestinal barrier integrity via upregulation of Claudin-1 and Claudin-3. These integrated responses contribute to the mitigation of age-associated hepatic metabolic dysfunction. Collectively, our findings uncover a coordinated mechanism linking host metabolism and microbiota in response to phytochemical intervention, offering novel insights into the molecular basis of liver-microbiome interactions in aging animals.
