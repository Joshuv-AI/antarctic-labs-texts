Optical analysis of 3D-printed terahertz waveplates from common thermoplastics
Authors: Gabriel F Freitas, Lucas T Roque, Nicolas M Kawahala, Felix G G Hernandez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the fabrication and characterization of birefringent terahertz (THz) waveplates produced via fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing using three widely available thermoplastic filaments: polylactic acid (PLA), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), and high-impact polystyrene (HIPS). Form-birefringent subwavelength gratings were printed using a 0.2 mm nozzle to tailor the phase retardance. The intrinsic THz optical properties of each polymer were first assessed through terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) on unstructured slabs, revealing material-dependent absorption. Polarization-resolved THz-TDS was then employed to extract the effective birefringence, retardance, and polarization modulation of the fabricated waveplates. HIPS exhibited the lowest absorption and the most stable birefringent response, followed by ABS, while PLA showed strong absorption and limited polarimetric performance. Measured birefringence spectra agreed with effective medium predictions below the diffraction threshold (0.6 THz); beyond this range, retardance saturation enabled continued phase shifting. Full polarimetric analysis using Stokes parameters confirmed consistent modulation of ellipticity and polarization rotation, particularly in HIPS-based devices. These results provide a comparative framework for selecting printable materials and optimizing designs of low-cost, customizable THz polarization components.Keywords: Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS), 3D printing, Birefringent waveplates, Form-birefringence, Polarization control, Fused deposition modeling (FDM)Subject terms: Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Fused-deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing1 is a widely used additive manufacturing technique that has been increasingly employed for the rapid and cost-effective fabrication of optical components in the terahertz (THz) spectral range2. Common polymers compatible with FDM exhibit low absorption and moderate refractive indices below 1 THz3–6, while consumer-grade FDM printers typically offer spatial resolutions on the order of hundreds of microns7. This level of precision enables the production of subwavelength structures essential for manipulating THz waves. These advantages have led to the realization of a wide range of 3D-printed photonic devices—including waveguides8–10, diffraction gratings11–13, prisms14–16, lenses17–19, and waveplates20–22—capable of tailoring THz transmission, polarization, and wavefronts.

Among these, polarization control of THz waves has gained particular attention due to its importance in polarization-sensitive terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS)23–25, imaging26–28, and emerging communication technologies29. While many THz-TDS setups rely on combinations of wire-grid polarizers (WGP) to decompose and analyze the polarization state30–32, the development of broadband THz waveplates remains a technological challenge33,34. Various materials—including quartz35,36, sapphire37, and silicon38,39—and diverse fabrication methods have been explored. However, such approaches often involve costly or time-consuming processes. In this context, FDM 3D printing offers a low-cost and versatile alternative for fabricating form-birefringent THz waveplates, with recent demonstrations achieving both quarter- and half-wave retardance using periodic subwavelength structures20–22. Nonetheless, evaluating the optical performance of waveplates produced from different filament materials remains essential to assess their suitability, printability, and structural fidelity.

While previous demonstrations of 3D-printed THz waveplates have focused on single-material implementations or alternative grating architectures—such as super-wavelength metagratings in polylactic acid (PLA)20, subwavelength cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) structures fabricated near the resolution limit of FDM21, and stacked high-impact polystyrene (HIPS) gratings designed for achromatic performance22—a systematic assessment across multiple common FDM thermoplastics remains unavailable. Our contribution aims at filling this gap by establishing a direct, material-resolved comparison within a uniform subwavelength geometry, clarifying how differences in absorption, birefringence, and fabrication fidelity impact THz waveplate performance. In addition, we employ polarimetry mapping to visualize polarization modulation across frequency and orientation, providing a compact framework to identify operational regimes and deviations from effective-medium behavior.

In this work, we demonstrate the fabrication and characterization of THz waveplates using FDM 3D printing with three commonly available thermoplastic filaments: PLA, HIPS, and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS). By combining subwavelength structural design with polarization-resolved THz-TDS, we evaluate the effective birefringence, retardance, and polarimetric response of each printed waveplate. The results reveal material-dependent performance trends, with HIPS exhibiting consistently low absorption and stable polarization modulation over a broad frequency range, supporting its suitability for THz applications in agreement with prior studies. This comparative study provides a practical framework for selecting and implementing 3D-printed birefringent optics in low-cost, customizable THz devices.

3D printing and materials

To fabricate the waveplates, we employed a consumer-grade FDM 3D printer (Creality Ender 3 S1 Pro), known for its affordability and widespread use in desktop manufacturing. Three thermoplastic filaments were investigated (PLA, ABS, and HIPS) based on their availability, ease of printing, and distinct terahertz optical properties. These materials are summarized as follows:

PLA42–44 is a biodegradable polyester derived from renewable sources such as corn starch or sugarcane. It is widely used in consumer-grade 3D printing due to its ease of use, relatively high thermal capacity, and non-toxic emissions during extrusion. However, it tends to be brittle, has limited heat resistance, and may be unsuitable for applications demanding long-term mechanical or thermal stability.

PLA42–44 is a biodegradable polyester derived from renewable sources such as corn starch or sugarcane. It is widely used in consumer-grade 3D printing due to its ease of use, relatively high thermal capacity, and non-toxic emissions during extrusion. ABS45–47 is a petroleum-based thermoplastic composed of acrylonitrile, butadiene, and styrene. It is popular in FDM for its mechanical strength, impact resistance, and relatively high thermal stability. Nevertheless, it is prone to warping during cooling due to thermal contraction, which can cause dimensional inaccuracies and interlayer delamination. THz polarimetry measurements

Polarization-resolved THz-TDS32 was performed in transmission geometry using photoconductive antennas (PCA) as both emitter and detector. A mode-locked Ti:sapphire laser provides 100 fs pulses at 780 nm and 80 MHz repetition rate to pump the emission and gate the detection of broadband THz pulses spanning range 0.1 THz to 2 THz. The THz beam is routed using off-axis parabolic mirrors, and a set of three wire-grid polarizers (WGP) was employed to resolve the polarization state of the transmitted THz electric field waveform E(t)30. A simplified schematic of the setup is shown in Fig. 1d.

To extract the orthogonal polarization components of the transmitted field, WGP1 fixes the incident THz polarization along the laboratory x-axis, while WGP2 acts as a rotatable analyzer oriented at relative to WGP1. WGP3 is aligned parallel to WGP1 to maintain consistent detection polarization. Two time-domain signals, and , are recorded with WGP2 set to and , respectively. The horizontal and vertical components of the transmitted field are then reconstructed as and 31. These relations arise exactly from the successive projections implemented by WGP2 and WGP332. The sample was mounted on a rotational stage and measurements were taken at azimuthal angles from 0 to 360 in 10 increments. All measurements were acquired using a common time-zero reference, i.e., the temporal origin was not reset between rotation angles , ensuring consistent relative phase between the reconstructed field components. Fourier transformation of these time-domain signals yields the complex spectra and , enabling full characterization of the waveplate’s polarimetric response, including frequency-dependent polarization rotation and ellipticity31.

Results

THz optical characterization of 3D-printed polymers

To evaluate the THz optical response of the materials employed in our waveplate fabrication, we performed THz-TDS measurements on 2 mm-thick, unstructured slabs of PLA, ABS, and HIPS. Although the THz properties of these polymers have been previously reported2–6, refractive index and absorption spectra can vary substantially between filament brands and batches. We therefore characterized the specific filaments used in our fabrication to ensure accurate modeling and enable a direct comparison of their optical performance.

Figure 2 shows the optical coefficients extracted by comparing the THz-TDS response of each slab to a sample-free reference, using standard thick-sample transmission analysis50. Measured waveforms are presented at Supplementary Information51. In this procedure, Fabry–Perot reflections were removed by truncating the time-domain waveform prior to the first internal echo (without apodization), consistent with the windowing routine discussed in Ref.52. The phase was unwrapped using the standard cumulative unwrapping algorithm, and refractive index and absorption spectra were obtained from the truncated sample/reference ratio through the bulk slab approximation50.

These results highlight HIPS as the most favorable material among those tested for THz optical components, combining a relatively low refractive index with minimal absorption over the frequency range of interest. Although PLA and ABS are more commonly used in 3D printing, their significantly higher THz losses limit their applicability in transmissive optics across a broad frequency range. A comparison with literature data further emphasizes the variability in reported optical parameters: for instance, Brodie et al.3 report refractive indices of 1.54 for PLA, 1.47 for ABS, and 1.48 for HIPS, which differ slightly but meaningfully from the values measured here, and further deviate from the data compiled by Sun and Hu in Ref.2. Table S1 in the Supplementary Information51 summarizes these literature values alongside our measurements. Such discrepancies, though seemingly minor, can affect the design and performance of form-birefringent waveplates. Therefore, in-house characterization of the specific filament used is crucial to ensure accurate device modeling and reliable optical performance.

Retardance and effective birefringence of fabricated waveplates

Building on the refractive index data obtained from bulk slabs, we now evaluate the birefringent response of the waveplates fabricated from each material. By performing THz-TDS measurements with the electric field aligned along the principal axes of the subwavelength grating, we extract the frequency-dependent birefringence and resulting retardance. These quantities provide a direct measure of the effectiveness of each waveplate in introducing controlled phase shifts between orthogonal polarization components. The phase spectra used in this analysis were obtained using the same procedure applied to the bulk slabs, namely from time-domain signals truncated before the first Fabry–Pérot echo. The birefringence was computed from the unwrapped phase difference between the principal axes using .

By measuring the transmitted THz electric field at multiple waveplate orientations , we applied the Stokes formalism21,31,53 to extract the output polarization ellipticity and rotation angle spectra. Among the three materials, HIPS exhibits the most regular and symmetric modulation pattern, consistent with a well-defined birefringent response. Alternating red and blue bands appear at fixed intervals across a broad frequency range, indicative of consistent retardance and a stable phase relationship between orthogonal polarization components. ABS shows qualitatively similar ellipticity modulation patterns but with increased distortion at higher frequencies, likely due to absorption-driven reductions in signal-to-noise ratio. In contrast, PLA displays irregular and noisy ellipticity maps, with distorted and less pronounced periodic modulation even at lower frequencies, reflecting its comparatively poorer optical performance in the THz range.

Similar modulation patterns recur above the cutoff frequency, particularly for HIPS and ABS, though with increased distortions and reduced smoothness, reflecting a breakdown of ideal waveplate behavior in this regime.

Discussion

We conducted a comparative study of FDM 3D-printed THz waveplates fabricated from PLA, ABS, and HIPS, using form-birefringent subwavelength gratings and polarization-resolved THz-TDS to assess their birefringence, retardance, and overall polarimetric response. Ellipticity and rotation angle maps, plotted as functions of frequency and waveplate orientation, offered a compact and intuitive visualization of polarization modulation, clearly revealing narrowband regimes of quarter- and half-waveplate behavior within the 0.1 THz to 0.3 THz interval. These operational bands reflect the specific grating heights used in fabrication, which directly govern the phase delay and can be systematically tuned by controlling the printed thickness.

Despite similar refractive indices near 1.5, the three materials exhibited markedly different behavior due to variations in THz absorption, as expected from literature. PLA suffered from strong losses that degraded signal quality and compromised reliable polarization modulation above approximately 0.4 THz. ABS performed better, sustaining effective polarization control up to around 0.8 THz. HIPS showed the most consistent performance, combining low absorption with stable birefringence and well-defined polarization modulation throughout the measured bandwidth from 0.1 THz to 1 THz. Above 0.6 THz, all waveplates deviated from effective medium predictions, and both ABS and HIPS exhibited retardance saturation near , indicating continued phase shifting beyond the design range that may be relevant for extended applications. This deviation is consistent with the known limits of the effective-medium approximation, which requires subwavelength grating period (), moderate index contrast, and negligible diffraction; as leads to approaching unity near 0.6 THz, the accuracy of the approximation is reduced.

We note that the birefringence spectra extracted from the waveplates closely follow the effective-medium predictions, with no additional frequency-dependent terms that would suggest infill-induced form birefringence. Likewise, the optical characterization of the bulk slabs showed no excess loss or spectral irregularities beyond the expected material absorption, indicating that neither the rectilinear infill pattern nor printing-related issues such as warping or layer gaps produced measurable scattering or coherence degradation in the THz range below the designed cutoff.

Alongside performance, fabrication considerations offer valuable insights. While PLA shows poorer THz performance, it remains the easiest to print reliably and can function adequately at the lower end of the frequency band. For instance, Rohrbach et al.21 and Dong et al.20 have demonstrated promising PLA waveplates using both sub- and super-wavelength designs. Although waveplates fabricated from ABS have not been reported to our knowledge, ABS presents better THz performance than PLA despite printing challenges, making it a potential candidate for specific applications. Among the materials tested, HIPS stands out for its low absorption and superior performance, enabling broadband designs as shown by Jäckel et al.22 using larger structures printed with a 0.4 mm nozzle. Building on this, our work demonstrates finer gratings enabled by a 0.2 mm nozzle—despite increased printing difficulties such as extrusion clogging—which extend the usable frequency range.
