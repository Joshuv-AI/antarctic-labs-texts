The predictive value of the hospital frailty risk score for 28-day mortality in older critically ill patients with acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Sen Zhang, Chang Liu, Shoudao Shi, Nan Chen, Liansheng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to validate the predictive value of the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) for 28-day mortality risk in older patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database (MIMIC-IV v3.1), including 1,702 older patients (aged ≥ 65 years) with AIS admitted to ICU. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) and piecewise Cox regression analyses were employed to explore the association between HFRS and 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients. The predictive performance of HFRS was compared with the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Based on the frailty risk stratification assessed by the HFRS, patients were divided into low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk groups, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests were generated to assess mortality trends. Multivariable Cox regression models were utilized to evaluate the independent association between HFRS and 28-day mortality. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess heterogeneity in outcomes by age, sex, and comorbidities.ResultsA nonlinear relationship was observed between HFRS and 28-day mortality in older critically ill patients with AIS, characterized by a threshold effect with an inflection point at 10.5. Below this threshold, each 1-unit increase in HFRS was associated with a 19% higher mortality risk (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] = 1.19, 95% confidence interval [CI]:1.13–1.26). Above the threshold, the incremental risk increase attenuated significantly (adjusted HR = 1.08, 95% CI:1.06–1.10). HFRS demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to other clinical scores, as evidenced by a higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC = 70.6% vs. Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA]: 65.5%, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI]: 60.4%, Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS]: 68.6%). After adjusting for multiple covariates, multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that individuals in the high risk group had an approximately 4.3-fold higher mortality risk compared to those in the low risk group (adjusted HR = 4.28, 95% CI:2.62–7.00; P < 0.001).ConclusionHFRS shows a nonlinear correlation with short-term mortality in older critically ill AIS patients, outperforming SOFA, GCS, and CCI in predictive utility. As a reliable tool for assessing 28-day mortality risk, HFRS highlights the need for tailored intervention strategies based on frailty severity thresholds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07624-2.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), Mortality prediction, Intensive care unit, Aged

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third most prevalent disabling disease worldwide [1]. With the growth of the global population and accelerated aging, both the prevalence and mortality of stroke have increased substantially, as reflected by a 43% rise in absolute mortality. Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounts for approximately 62.4% of all stroke cases and has become a major component of patients in intensive care units (ICUs) [1]. Accurate assessment of clinical outcomes and functional prognosis in patients with AIS holds significant clinical value for guiding personalized treatment, optimizing prognostic prediction models, improving physician–patient communication quality, and mitigating medical dispute risks [2].

In the context of accelerating population aging, frailty in older adults has emerged as a major global public health challenge. Frailty is an age-related clinical syndrome characterized by multisystem physiological decline, increased susceptibility to external stressors, and elevated risks of adverse clinical outcomes [3, 4]. Epidemiological studies reveal a significant comorbid association between stroke and frailty, with the prevalence of pre-stroke frailty and frailty reported to be 49% and 22%, respectively, among stroke patients [5]. Notably, frailty not only significantly increases recurrence risks in mild stroke patients, but also elevates the risks of poor prognosis in those receiving endovascular therapy and in severe stroke cases [6–9]. Therefore, incorporating frailty assessment into clinical management pathways for AIS patients in ICUs is imperative.

The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), developed by Gilber T et al. in 2018, is a weighted scoring system based on International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes (Table S1) that enables rapid identification and risk stratification of frailty in hospitalized patients [10]. Previous studies have validated HFRS's independent predictive value for 3-month functional outcomes in AIS patients receiving endovascular therapy [11, 12], in-hospital mortality among patients undergoing endovascular or surgical revascularization for critical limb ischemia [13], postoperative complications in intracranial tumor cases [14], and 1-year survival rates in critically ill patients [15]. However, the predictive value of HFRS for 28-day mortality in older AIS patients in ICU remains unexplored. To address this knowledge gap, this study utilizes the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database [16] to validate HFRS's predictive capacity for 28-day mortality in older AIS patients admitted to ICUs.

Materials and methods

Database and study population

This observational analysis was conducted using version 3.1 of the MIMIC-IV critical care database, a longitudinal repository documenting 53,897 intensive care unit admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, an accredited comprehensive stroke center, between 2008 and 2022 [16]. Authorization for data utilization was secured through completion of CITI program certification modules (Credential ID: 51,383,956) and ethical approvals from MIT and BIDMC institutional review boards. Implementation of de-identification protocols in accordance with HIPAA standards exempted individual consent requirements.

The study cohort was derived from the MIMIC-IV database by identifying individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of AIS. In this database, the diagnosis listed in the first position is considered the primary reason for hospitalization. Accordingly, this study included only patients with AIS recorded as the first diagnosis, in order to minimize selection bias. Exclusion parameters encompassed: (1) Lack of critical care documentation or recurrent ICU transfers; (2) Intensive care duration below 24 h; (3) Age below geriatric threshold (65 years); (4) Insufficient clinical documentation. Figure S1 delineates the sequential participant selection process with comprehensive inclusion–exclusion implementation.

Data extraction

The investigation employed the PostgreSQL system (v16.0, PostgreSQL Global Development Group, California, USA) for clinical data retrieval. The identification and confirmation of the patient cohort were primarily based on diagnostic codes (ICD-9/ICD-10) derived from electronic health records. The relevant ICD codes are detailed in Table S2. Clinical parameters captured during the initial 24-h critical care period encompassed seven domains: (1) Demographic profiles: ethnicity, gender, age; (2) Pre-existing conditions: cerebrovascular disorders, chronic pulmonary obstruction, hypertensive states, hepatic dysfunction, neoplastic diseases, renal impairment, myocardial ischemic events; (3) Physiological metrics: diastolic/systolic pressures, mean arterial pressure, respiratory rate, cardiac rhythm; (4) Prognostic indices: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA); (5) Biochemical markers: hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit levels, leukocyte counts, platelet indices, serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride), renal function parameters (creatinine, urea nitrogen), hepatic enzymes (ALT, AST), acid–base balance indicators (bicarbonate, anion gap), protein metabolism markers (albumin); (6) Therapeutic interventions: acute stroke treatment (intravenous fibrinolysis and mechanical thrombectomy), statin therapy, anticoagulant regimens, antiplatelet protocols; (7) Critical care documentation: ICU admission chronology, mortality timestamps.

Diagnostic information was extracted via SQL queries using dual-coded ICD-9/10 classifications. Mortality outcomes were derived from hospitalization timelines and post-discharge surveillance records, employing temporal alignment between index stroke diagnosis timestamps (per MIMIC-IV 3.1 documentation) and mortality registry data. All biological parameters represent 24-h mean measurements following ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

Calculation of HFRS

Based on the HFRS model established by Gilbert T et al. (Table S1), all relevant ICD-10 and corresponding ICD-9 diagnostic codes were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. The calculation was performed using all diagnostic codes documented over the full course of hospitalization. Each diagnostic code was assigned its corresponding weighted score as specified in the pre-established HFRS model. Subsequently, the HFRS score for each patient was derived by summing the weighted scores of all applicable ICD codes associated with that patient.

Data analysis

To achieve the study objectives, multiple statistical methods were employed. First, participants were stratified based on frailty risk categories assessed by the HFRS (low risk: < 5 points; intermediate risk: 5–15 points; high risk: > 15 points) and 28-day outcomes to describe baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as median or interquartile range, while categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U test or t-test based on data distribution, with Pearson's chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was applied to examine potential linear associations between HFRS levels and 28-day mortality. A two-segment Cox regression analysis was conducted for threshold effect assessment. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated the predictive performance of HFRS, SOFA, CCI, and GCS for 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients, with sensitivity, specificity, and Area Under the Curve (AUC) calculated. Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests were generated according to HFRS risk categories. Univariate Cox regression identified potential predictors of 28-day mortality. Multivariate Cox regression models determined the independent association between HFRS and mortality, expressed as Hazard Ratio (HR) with 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Model I: unadjusted; Model II: adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity; Model III: additionally adjusted for hospitalization duration, hematocrit, hemoglobin, white blood cells, urea nitrogen, creatinine, albumin, bicarbonate, sodium, potassium, anion gap, chloride; Model IV: further adjusted for mean heart rate, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, respiratory rate, SOFA, GCS, and CCI, endovascular recanalization therapy and medication therapies. Considering potential effect modification by ventilator support, vasoactive agents, and comorbidities, subgroup analyses with interaction tests were performed. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2, with P < 0.05 (two-tailed) considered statistically significant.

Restricted cubic splines analysis

RCS analysis was applied to assess the association between HFRS and 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients. After adjusting for all relevant covariates, we identified a nonlinear relationship (P = 0.015, Fig. 1). The 28-day mortality initially showed a sharp increase with rising HFRS levels, but the upward trend decelerated when HFRS exceeded 10.5 (Fig. 1), suggesting HFRS = 10.5 as the optimal clinical intervention threshold.

ROC curve analysis and threshold analysis

ROC curves were plotted for HFRS, SOFA, CCI, and GCS to predict 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients (Fig. 2). HFRS demonstrated superior predictive performance with an AUC of 70.6% (95% CI: 67.9%−73.3%), surpassing SOFA (65.5% [63.8%−71.0%]), CCI (60.4% [57.4%−63.3%]), and GCS (68.6% [65.6%−71.7%]) (Table S4). The optimal cutoff value for HFRS was 10.5, yielding sensitivity of 85.6% and specificity of 39.5%. Using two-segment Cox regression with HFRS = 10.5 as the threshold (Table 2), each 1-unit increase in HFRS below the threshold was associated with a 19% increase in mortality (adjusted HR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.13—1.26). Above the threshold, each 1-unit HFRS increase corresponded to an 8% mortality rise (adjusted HR = 1.08; 95% CI: 1.06—1.10).

Association between HFRS and 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients

We first employed the Schoenfeld residual test to evaluate whether the HFRS satisfied the proportional hazards assumption. Results indicated no violation of the assumption, as the correlation test between rank and residuals yielded P > 0.05 (Figure S2). Univariate Cox regression analysis for 28-day mortality in older critically ill AIS patients was performed as detailed in Table S5. Variables showing significant associations (P < 0.10) were then entered into multivariate Cox regression models. Table 3 presents the results of the multivariate Cox regression models, the unadjusted model demonstrated that HFRS increased mortality risk (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.09—1.12, P < 0.001), with the most pronounced effect in high risk group (HR = 7.83, 95% CI: 4.99—12.30, P < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity in Model II, HFRS remained significantly associated with mortality (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.08—1.11, P < 0.001). Compared to low risk group, both intermediate risk group (HR = 3.22, 95% CI: 2.06—5.02, P < 0.001) and high risk group (HR = 7.03, 95% CI: 4.47—11.06, P < 0.001) showed elevated risks. Model III, further adjusted for hospitalization duration, hematocrit, hemoglobin, white blood cells, urea nitrogen, creatinine, albumin, bicarbonate, sodium, potassium, anion gap, and chloride, continued to demonstrate heightened mortality impact with increasing HFRS levels (high risk: HR = 7.00, 95% CI: 4.31—11.37, P < 0.001). Model IV incorporated additional adjustments for mean heart rate, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, respiratory rate, endovascular recanalization therapy, SOFA, GCS, CCI, and medications. Results confirmed increased mortality risks in both intermediate risk and high risk groups versus low risk group, with high risk group exhibiting the strongest association (HR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.67—7.00, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational cohort study, we analyzed data from 1,702 patients in the MIMIC-IV database to evaluate the predictive performance of the HFRS for 28-day mortality in older patients with AIS in the ICU. The study found a significant non-linear association between HFRS and 28-day mortality (threshold = 10.5) in older critically ill AIS patients. Its predictive performance (AUC = 70.6%) was significantly superior to traditional scoring tools such as SOFA, CCI, and GCS. Moreover, HFRS remained a strong independent predictor of mortality risk after multivariable adjustment.

Frailty constitutes a leading cause of mortality in the older population and serves as a critical determinant of clinical outcomes in critically ill patients [17, 18]. Frailty diminishes physiological reserve capacity and heightens susceptibility to stressors. Previous studies have demonstrated that frail individuals exhibit elevated stroke risk, with potential mechanisms involving frailty-induced vascular endothelial dysfunction [19], accelerated arterial stiffness [20–22], cerebrovascular pathology development [23, 24], and impaired cerebral blood flow autoregulation [25]. Furthermore, frailty is frequently accompanied by multiple chronic comorbidities and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors [26, 27]. The combination of multisystem functional decline and cumulative risk factors contributes to enhanced stroke vulnerability in frail individuals.

Furthermore, frailty exerts persistent detrimental effects on long-term prognosis in stroke patients. A meta-analysis incorporating 25 studies demonstrated that frailty was significantly associated with poor functional outcomes and mortality in stroke patients, showing a 2.66-fold increased mortality risk in frail versus non-frail stroke patients [28]. Subsequent investigations revealed a dose-dependent relationship between frailty severity and mortality risk in stroke populations. Despite employing heterogeneous frailty assessment instruments, studies consistently demonstrated that severe frailty predicted elevated mortality risk [29–31], aligning with our findings. Diminished physiological reserves in frail critically ill stroke patients impair recovery and compensatory capacities [32, 33], prolonging rehabilitation for motor deficits and dysphagia complications [9, 34, 35], while increasing susceptibility to infections, organ failure, electrolyte imbalances, and acid–base disorders [26, 36]. These complications necessitate complex therapeutic decision-making, particularly regarding anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy indications. The synergistic interaction of these factors perpetuates a vicious cycle of frailty progression and mortality risk escalation.

It is noteworthy that current stroke assessment systems appear to lack integration of frailty as a critical dimension [37]. A study by Andrea Pilotto et al. involving 102 patients with similar baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores, vascular risk factors, and treatment strategies, but differing pre-stroke frailty levels, demonstrated that stroke patients with pre-existing frailty had higher risks of both short-term and long-term poor outcomes [38]. Andrea Pilotto et al. suggested that these findings might explain the substantial number of recanalization therapy failures observed in clinical practice, as well as the wide variability in short-term and long-term outcomes.

Previous studies have reported that the rate of intravenous thrombolysis use among AIS patients aged over 60 years in the United States in 2017 ranged from 11.4% to 12.3% [39], which is consistent with the 12.0% IVT rate observed in our study. However, the same study reported a mechanical thrombectomy (MT) rate of only 2.2%–3.2% in this age group during the same period [39], which is considerably lower than the 10.2% observed in our cohort. We propose that this discrepancy may be attributed to the following factors. First, the inclusion of MT in AIS treatment guidelines occurred relatively late. Since the publication of landmark positive clinical trials in 2015 [40], the utilization of MT has steadily increased, particularly among older adults [39]. Our study included patients diagnosed with AIS between 2008 and 2022, a study period that encompasses the rapid dissemination phase of MT. Consequently, the overall MT rate in our study is higher than that reported for a single year (2017). Second, the study population is distinct. Our analysis focused on older critically ill patients with AIS, who typically present with more severe neurological deficits and are thus more likely to meet the eligibility criteria for MT. This clinical characteristic may have contributed to the higher rate of MT observed. Third, the data source may have influenced the results. The data for this study were obtained from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, a major teaching hospital of Harvard Medical School and a certified tertiary stroke center. Compared to community hospitals or non-teaching institutions, such high-volume tertiary centers generally have more established infrastructure and greater expertise in performing MT, which may lead to higher procedural rates. Finally, as a retrospective study based on electronic health record data, our findings may not fully reflect real-world MT utilization and should be interpreted with caution.

This study observed that non-survivors had a significantly shorter median length of hospital stay compared to survivors (6.1 days vs 8.5 days). Univariate analysis indicated that a shorter LOS was associated with a higher risk of mortality (HR = 0.93). This phenomenon results from the truncating effect of early mortality events on LOS, suggesting that a shorter LOS reflects greater disease severity rather than being a direct cause of death. In the multivariate model, after adjusting for LOS, HFRS retained its significant independent predictive value, confirming that the frailty risk it represents maintains stable prognostic significance even after controlling for hospitalization duration.

To assess patient frailty status, this study employed the HFRS proposed by Gilbert T et al. in 2018. After adjusting for confounding factors, we found that individuals in the high risk group had approximately a 4.3-fold increased mortality risk compared to those in the low risk group (adjusted HR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.67–7.00; P < 0.001).

This study revealed a non-linear relationship between HFRS scores and 28-day mortality in older critically ill patients with AIS. Specifically, while mortality risk generally increased with rising HFRS scores, the risk increment attenuated when HFRS exceeded 10.5, particularly in the more frail population. We speculate this phenomenon may be attributed to several factors: First, as a weighted assessment tool based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes, elevated HFRS directly indicates significant health risks and frailty status, providing clinicians with clear warning signals. Consequently, for high-risk patients (HFRS ≥ 10.5), medical teams typically implement more intensive monitoring, aggressive interventions, and greater resource allocation. These enhanced clinical management measures may partially mitigate the inherent high risk, resulting in relatively reduced mortality risk increment in this subgroup. Conversely, patients with HFRS < 10.5 may present with less apparent clinical manifestations and underrecognized risks, potentially receiving less intensive care, thereby exhibiting more pronounced HR increases. Second, selection bias may influence these results. By including only ICU patients with stays exceeding 24 h while excluding those who died or were transferred within 24 h—typically more critically ill with higher frailty—we may have underestimated the risk in the high HFRS group. Finally, a "risk saturation" effect may be operative, wherein beyond a certain HFRS threshold, mortality risk plateaus despite further score increases. Notably, Gilbert et al. initially reported similar patterns during HFRS development: although adverse outcomes increased with rising scores, the HFRS-mortality association plateaued beyond 15 points [10].

To objectively evaluate the predictive ability of the HFRS for 28-day mortality in older critically ill patients with AIS, this study compared HFRS with three other assessment tools known to predict poor outcomes in AIS—SOFA score, GCS score, and CCI—in terms of sensitivity and specificity. The results showed that HFRS had an AUC of 70.6%, with an optimal cut-off value of 10.5, sensitivity of 85.6%, and specificity of 39.5%. Compared with the other three tools, HFRS demonstrated higher predictive accuracy and reliability. This may be because HFRS comprehensively assesses patients' overall condition across multiple dimensions, including comorbidities, physiological reserve, and mental status, thereby providing a more holistic clinical perspective and overcoming the limitations of relying solely on individual physiological or pathological indicators.

Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed several noteworthy findings. First, the predictive power of HFRS for mortality risk was slightly higher in patients aged < 75 years (HR = 1.12) than in those ≥ 75 years (HR = 1.09). This phenomenon may be explained by the following mechanisms: The competing risk effect likely plays a major role—in very older patients, mortality risk is simultaneously influenced by multiple factors including advanced age and multimorbidity, thereby diminishing the marginal contribution of frailty as a single risk factor. Furthermore, differences in treatment intensity warrant attention, as clinicians may tend to adopt more conservative treatment strategies for older patients, and such bias may interfere with the assessment of the true association between HFRS and mortality. Another subgroup analysis showed a positive association between HFRS and mortality risk (HR = 1.09) in patients requiring ventilation, whereas in the subgroup not requiring ventilation, the HR was 0.93 (P = 0.419), which did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may stem from the following reasons: Previous studies have demonstrated that frailty is significantly associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation duration and weaning failure [41, 42], suggesting that patients requiring ventilatory support inherently exhibit higher frailty levels and poorer baseline prognosis, thus HFRS retains some predictive capacity in this subgroup. Conversely, no clear association was observed between HFRS and mortality in patients not requiring ventilation. Simultaneously, patients receiving ventilation may have received more differentiated treatment regimens due to their distinct clinical status, and the treatment strategies themselves may also influence mortality outcomes. Finally, it should be noted that as a single-center retrospective study based on electronic medical records, this research inevitably contains various potential biases, which may also exert some influence on the results of the aforementioned subgroup analyses.

In summary, this study suggests that the HFRS may offer better predictive performance for prognosis in older critically ill patients with AIS than traditional scoring systems. By objectively reflecting patients' frailty risk, HFRS demonstrates its unique predictive utility, providing novel assessment perspectives and intervention directions for the management of this patient population in the ICU. These findings support the integration of HFRS into risk assessment frameworks for older AIS patients in ICU settings to optimize risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Limitations, strengths and future research directions

This study possesses several notable strengths: First, as an emerging frailty assessment tool, there is currently a scarcity of research examining the relationship between HFRS and short-term outcomes in older patients with AIS admitted to the ICU, and our findings effectively address this knowledge gap. Second, the rigorous methodology employed in developing HFRS, with statistical weights derived from large-scale data, enhances the credibility of our results. Finally, the substantial sample size and complete follow-up data in this study lend greater authenticity and clinical relevance to our conclusions. These strengths provide a potential theoretical foundation for clinicians to develop precise treatment strategies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis relying on electronic medical records, the findings may be influenced by unavoidable selection bias. Second, the absence of key clinical indicators such as the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) in the MIMIC-IV database limited the ability to perform stratified analyses based on neurological deficit severity, which may affect the comprehensiveness of the conclusions. Additionally, the lack of specific exclusion criteria related to acute treatments and standardized etiologic classifications in the database restricted the analysis of treatment selection bias and prognostic variations across different stroke subtypes. Furthermore, the calculation of the HFRS based on administrative ICD codes is contingent upon the completeness and accuracy of coding. Potential omissions or errors in diagnostic coding may introduce information bias, leading to misclassification of patients' true frailty status. It should also be noted that HFRS scores in this study were derived from all diagnostic codes recorded during the entire hospitalization period, which may have inadvertently included hospital-acquired complications, possibly resulting in some degree of overestimation of its predictive performance. Finally, due to constraints in data accessibility, this study was unable to compare the accuracy of HFRS with other established frailty assessment tools, such as the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), thereby limiting further validation of HFRS’s utility in older critically ill patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS).

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, future research could be conducted in the following directions:

First, systematic comparison of the predictive accuracy between the HFRS and other frailty assessment tools in older critically ill patients with AIS is needed. Currently limited by data availability, subsequent multicenter prospective studies should determine whether HFRS outperforms existing assessment tools in both frailty evaluation and prognostic prediction. Second, further validation and optimization of the applicable HFRS threshold in this population are warranted. Although this study proposed 10.5 as a new cut-off value, its predictive efficacy requires verification in large-sample cohorts, and more appropriate classification criteria should be explored. Third, transcending the limitations of single-score prediction by integrating HFRS into multidimensional prognostic assessment models is advisable. Combining HFRS with existing stroke prediction indicators to establish a comprehensive evaluation system incorporating frailty, critical illness, and stroke-specific factors would provide a more holistic reflection of patient status and outcomes. Fourth, expanding HFRS assessment dimensions to enhance its sensitivity in critical care settings is recommended. Building upon the original ICD-10 coding framework, incorporating indicators reflecting critical care pathophysiology—such as duration of mechanical ventilation, sepsis, acute kidney injury, and other complications—could facilitate development of a revised HFRS-Critical Care Adapted Score to improve recognition of frailty status changes in critically ill patients. Fifth, promoting dynamic, automated HFRS assessment and clinical integration is essential. Given the rapid clinical changes in older critically ill patients, multiple HFRS assessments during ICU hospitalization are necessary to capture real-time evolution of frailty status. Specifically, embedding an automated HFRS calculation module within electronic health record (EHR) or hospital information management systems could enable automatic diagnostic code acquisition through real-time interfaces, achieving seamless data extraction. The system should support daily or weekly batch operations, automatically generating individual HFRS scores and corresponding risk stratifications. Assessment results could be integrated into clinical workflow interfaces via dashboard displays, with automated alerts for high-risk patients prompting physicians, nurses, or case managers to conduct timely further assessment and intervention. Future research should refine these application mechanisms, systematically optimizing HFRS implementation strategies in critically ill stroke populations to enhance assessment efficiency and predictive accuracy, ultimately facilitating a transition from static evaluation to dynamic interventional care models in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study revealed that HFRS demonstrated a nonlinear association with short-term mortality in older critically ill AIS patients, exhibiting superior predictive value compared to SOFA, GCS, and CCI. These findings establish HFRS as a reliable prognostic tool for short-term mortality in this population, with its threshold effect suggesting the necessity of tailored intervention strategies across distinct frailty stages.
