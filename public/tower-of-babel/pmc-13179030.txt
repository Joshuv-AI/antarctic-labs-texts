Improving the First-hour Breastfeeding Rate in Cesarean Deliveries: A Quality Improvement Initiative
Authors: Nalina Amuji, Clive Martin Rodrigues, Harshitha M. Swamy, Lakshmi Parvathi Bandlamudi, Tinu Krishna vilasam Regunath, Chandrakala Bada Shekharappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Breastfeeding during the golden hour after birth is crucial for establishing successful long-term breastfeeding. Despite the World Health Organization recommendation, according to the National Family Health Survey-5, the first-hour breastfeeding rate in India following cesarean delivery is only 23.9%. We aimed to improve the first-hour breastfeeding rate in stable neonates born through cesarean delivery from the baseline of 9% to 80% within a period of 4 weeks.Methods:This quality improvement initiative was conducted in Southern India during 8 months (April 2022 to November 2022) by a multidisciplinary team comprising doctors and nurses. The baseline first-hour breastfeeding rate among inborn, stable neonates of 34 weeks’ gestation or greater delivered via cesarean section was assessed during a 10-week period. The Point Of Care Quality Improvement model (POCQI_learner_manual_ver 03_2020.pdf) was used to enhance the breastfeeding rate during 4 weeks. Successful change ideas were adopted, and their sustainability was evaluated during the 22-week sustenance period.Results:Out of the 592 neonates delivered by cesarean section at a gestational age of 34 weeks or older, 418 were eligible. The average gestational age and birth weight were 38 weeks (±1.4) and 2916 g (±489), respectively. The first-hour breastfeeding rate increased from a baseline of 9.6% to 100% at the conclusion of the intervention phase, and this was maintained at greater than 90% throughout the sustainability period.Conclusions:This quality improvement initiative enhances healthcare workers’ knowledge, competence, and confidence in immediate first-hour breastfeeding within the operating theater. Further research is required to assess the potential for generalization across diverse units.

INTRODUCTION

Breastfeeding within the first hour (commonly referred to as the “golden hour”) after birth is essential for the newborn’s survival and the establishment of successful breastfeeding. This practice not only enhances bonding between the mother and her infant but also promotes the transfer of nutritious colostrum to the baby, stimulates milk production, and improves breastfeeding exclusivity and duration. Initiating breastfeeding within an hour of birth safeguards the newborn against acquired infections and decreases neonatal morbidity and mortality.1 Therefore, starting breastfeeding within the first hour is considered one of the “sentinel indicators” of successful breastfeeding.

According to the National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5), the neonatal mortality rate was 6.6 per 1,000 live births among children breastfed within 1 hour of birth, compared with 8.1 per 1,000 live births among those breastfed 1 day after birth.2 A study conducted in India, Ghana, and Tanzania has demonstrated that the risk of neonatal mortality was 41% and 79% higher among children who commenced breastfeeding between 2 and 23 and 24 and 96 hours after birth, respectively, compared with infants who began breastfeeding within the first hour after birth.3 Infants who are breastfed within the first hour are more likely to be exclusively breastfed for up to 6 months and to continue breastfeeding for a longer duration.4 Given these benefits, the World Health Organization and the United Nations Children’s Fund recommend breastfeeding within the first hour of birth.5

Cesarean delivery is recognized as an independent risk factor for suboptimal early breastfeeding practices. It is consistently associated with delayed or reduced initiation of breastfeeding compared with vaginal birth. Contributing factors include postoperative maternal pain, limitations in early positioning, maternal–infant separation during postoperative recovery, and the effects of surgical stress and medications on early milk secretion. In addition, infants born by cesarean section (C-section) may demonstrate delayed feeding cues or reduced initial suckling effectiveness.6,7 Multiple studies have shown that infants delivered via C-section are less likely to receive breastfeeding within the first hour of birth compared with those delivered vaginally.8–10

Although breastfeeding within the first hour of birth is considered the standard of care during the crucial “golden hour” postpartum period, only 39% of neonates in South Asia receive it within this time frame. Globally, less than half (42%) of all newborns are breastfed within the first hour of birth, according to the United Nations Children’s Fund 2022.11 According to NFHS-5, the first-hour breastfeeding rate in cesarean deliveries in India is only 23.9%.12 A study done in India showed that babies born by cesarean are 11% more likely to experience delayed initiation of breastfeeding.13 Therefore, the prompt initiation of breastfeeding remains a pressing need.

Available Knowledge and Rationale

Within our unit, it was observed that the proportion of early initiation of direct breastfeeding following vaginal deliveries exceeded 90%, whereas it was merely 9% in cases of C-sections. The average time to initiate direct breastfeeding after a cesarean was recorded at 155 minutes. This observation motivated us to undertake a quality improvement (QI) project to enhance the rate of early breastfeeding initiation in cesarean deliveries.

Specific Aim

We aimed to increase the rate of direct breastfeeding among stable neonates delivered by C-section, from an initial baseline of 9% to 80% within 4 weeks.

METHODOLOGY

Context

This QI initiative was conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in Southern India during a period of 8 months (April 2022 to November 2022). The hospital reports a total of 2,500 deliveries annually, with approximately 45%–50% being C-sections. These cesarean deliveries are performed across various floors of the facility. Delivery procedures are overseen by a team of trained healthcare professionals, including doctors and nurses who are qualified in the Neonatal Resuscitation Program.

Study Population

Stable inborn neonates with a gestational age of 34 weeks or older, delivered via C-section, were eligible for the study. We included the neonate and the mother dyad, regardless of the type of anesthesia (spinal or general). Neonates requiring immediate neonatal intensive care unit care following birth—such as those who were hemodynamically unstable, experiencing respiratory distress, encephalopathy, or suspected malformations contraindicating breastfeeding—were excluded. Additionally, dyads necessitating intensive care unit support during or after cesarean delivery were excluded. Gestational age was determined by first-trimester ultrasound or by the last menstrual period when ultrasound was unavailable.

Strategies

This initiative was conducted in collaboration with the hospital’s quality and safety team, using the Point Of Care Quality Improvement model.14 It was conducted during a period of 8 months, divided into 3 phases: a baseline period lasting 10 weeks, an intervention period lasting 4 weeks, and a sustenance period spanning 22 weeks.

Baseline Period (March 28, 2022, to June 12, 2022)

During the baseline period, a multidisciplinary team was established, consisting of physicians and nurses from neonatology, obstetrics, and gynecology, as well as a member from the institutional QI team. Baseline data regarding the rate of direct breastfeeding within the first hour in stable neonates delivered via C-section were collected. The process chart (Fig. 1) and Ishikawa diagram (Fig. 2) were used to analyze issues associated with the failure to initiate direct breastfeeding within 1 hour. The team observed that immediately after the cesarean, the baby receives initial care (resuscitation, weighing, administration of vitamin K), is handed over to the family, and the mother is shifted to the recovery room, then to the postnatal ward. Breastfeeding is initiated in the postnatal ward upon the mother’s arrival. This process usually takes more than an hour, resulting in delayed breastfeeding. The average rate of initiation of breastfeeding in stable neonates delivered by cesarean during the baseline period was 9.6%. The most common problems noted were reduced awareness among healthcare personnel about the early initiation of breastfeeding, delays in transferring the mother from the recovery room to the postnatal room, and restrictions on the entry of attendees to the recovery room. Details of PDSA Conducted During the Study Period

To address the delay in transferring the mother from the recovery room to the postnatal ward, the team educated healthcare practitioners (HCPs) on the importance of early initiation of breastfeeding and the benefits of initiating it in the recovery room itself. In the first PDSA, awareness training was conducted in 3 batches using a PowerPoint presentation that outlined the advantages of breastfeeding within the first hour in neonates and encouraged participants to feed the neonate when the mother was shifted to the recovery room. We also instructed doctors to have HCPs sign the date and time of initiation, and to include a direct breastfeeding order on the medication prescription, with HCPs signing the date and time of the first direct breastfeeding. The team studied the feasibility of this change idea for 1 week and found that all mothers were transferred to the recovery room 1 hour after birth. Even though breastfeeding orders were written on the prescription paper, the rate of 1-hour breastfeeding initiation did not increase (8.3%), and the time to direct breastfeeding initiation was delayed. Therefore, we planned to continue educating the newly recruited HCPs, but the idea of initiating breastfeeding in the recovery room was abandoned. In the second PDSA, the team decided to breastfeed the baby in the operating theater (OT) itself while the C-section was proceeding. All doctors, nurses, and anesthesiologists were informed to place the baby horizontally on the mother’s bare chest to provide skin-to-skin contact for at least 10–15 minutes, followed by initiation of breastfeeding (Fig. 4). During this period, the neonate’s respiration, color, and temperature were monitored using the touch method. The practice was discontinued if the condition of either the mother or the neonate worsened and required intensive monitoring. This approach was followed regardless of whether spinal or general anesthesia was used, provided the mother was clinically stable and did not require intensive care monitoring, as determined by the attending anesthetist. The feasibility of this change idea was studied for 1 week, and it was found that breastfeeding on the OT table was feasible irrespective of the type of anesthesia. The rate of 1-hour breastfeeding initiation improved from 8% to 56%. We also observed that the number of mothers requiring general anesthesia at 34 weeks or older was lower. Nurses and doctors felt the need to train on breastfeeding the baby when the mother is on the OT table, as most junior nurses were afraid to breastfeed at the table. In the third PDSA, HCP training (28 nurses and 8 doctors) was conducted using simulation with mannequins. This training session lasted 1 hour and was delivered in 3 batches, focusing mainly on placing the baby horizontally on the mother’s bare chest for skin-to-skin contact for 10–15 minutes, followed by breastfeeding. They were instructed to monitor the neonate’s respiration, color, and temperature using the touch method. All nurses and doctors attended this simulation training. The proportion of eligible neonates breastfed within an hour improved from 55% to 85%. Through this, the PDSA team learned that the change was feasible but difficult to sustain when there were many simultaneous deliveries. In the fourth PDSA, more assistants (nursing students) were provided for cesarean deliveries, and student nurses assisted with breastfeeding under the supervision of doctors and nurses during subsequent deliveries. The proportion of eligible neonates breastfed within an hour improved from 85% to 100%.

Sustenance period (July 11, 2022, to December 5, 2022)

During this phase, doctors and nurses continued to breastfeed the baby in the OT when the C-section was going on. The percentage of eligible neonates breastfed within 1 hour was monitored, and weekly run charts were displayed. Training and education of nurses on 1-hour breastfeeding continued whenever a new nurse/doctor joined the department.

Data Collection and Analysis

During the baseline period, breastfeeding rates were collected in real time by a member of the QI team and a member of the nursing team using a Google proforma that also captured basic demographic details. During the intervention period, the introduction of a written breastfeeding order, along with documenting the date and time of the first breastfeeding on the prescription sheet, facilitated systematic data collection. During both the intervention and sustenance periods, the attending doctor continued to enter data into the Google proforma. Compliance with writing breastfeeding orders and documenting the initiation of breastfeeding exceeded 90%. When documentation was missing, the mother or healthcare providers were interviewed to determine the time and place of breastfeeding initiation.

The outcome indicator was the percentage of stable neonates delivered by C-section who received direct breastfeeding within the first hour, irrespective of the type of anesthesia. We measured the rate of neonates with whom a breastfeeding order was written as a process measure. These data were entered into Microsoft Excel. Weekly run charts were created using Microsoft Excel. The QI Macro free version was used to generate a statistical process control chart.

Ethical Consideration

The institutional ethics committee approved the study, and a waiver of informed consent was granted.

RESULTS

Of the 592 neonates of 34 weeks or older delivered by cesarean delivery during the study period, 418 were eligible (baseline period 99, intervention period 57, sustenance period 260). The mean gestation age was 38 ± 1.4 weeks (mean ± SD), and the mean birth weight was 2916 ± 489 g (mean ± SD). During the baseline period, the 1-hour breastfeeding initiation rate was 9.6%, which increased to 8.3%, 56%, 85%, and 100% after PDSAs 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. This improvement was sustained at more than 90% throughout the sustenance period. DISCUSSION

Globally, more than 1 million newborn infants could be saved each year by initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of delivery.15 Despite the well-documented benefits, the early initiation rate remains suboptimal. According to NFHS-5, only 49% of neonates in Karnataka, a state in southern India, were breastfed within the first hour, and this proportion is even lower among cesarean deliveries. In our unit, the early breastfeeding rate in stable neonates delivered by C-section was 9.6%, which is comparable to reports from other centers, where rates range from 0% to 20%.8,12,16

Following implementation of our project, the rate of breastfeeding initiation within 1 hour improved from 9.6% to more than 80% within 4 weeks among stable neonates delivered by C-section. Moreover, this improvement was successfully sustained during the following 5 months. This outcome is consistent with other studies that implemented similar interventions to enhance early breastfeeding after cesarean delivery.11,12 These findings reaffirm that QI methodologies are effective in addressing such gaps in perinatal care.

First-hour initiation of breastfeeding after cesarean delivery is often delayed due to a combination of maternal, healthcare provider, and health system–related barriers. Some of the obstacles mentioned in various studies include postoperative pain, limited mobility, residual effects of anesthesia, maternal drowsiness, routine separation of mother and newborn for postoperative monitoring, neonatal observation, and lack of an enabling environment to support breastfeeding. Evidence consistently shows that these barriers are largely modifiable and that breastfeeding in the recovery room is both safe and feasible when mothers are clinically stable.7,8,16 Our findings are consistent with these observations and extend the evidence to a low- and middle-income country context, where additional systemic challenges such as limited staffing, lack of awareness among healthcare providers, delays in transferring mothers from the operation theater to the recovery or postnatal wards, restrictions on attendants in recovery areas, and infrastructural barriers often impede early initiation after cesarean deliveries.16,17 The interventions that proved most effective were initiating skin-to-skin contact, followed by breastfeeding on the operating table during the cesarean procedure, and sensitizing healthcare providers to the importance of early initiation.

Similar results were reported by Dudeja et al16 in India, who demonstrated that structured PDSA cycles and multidisciplinary engagement improved the first-hour breastfeeding rate after cesarean delivery from 0% to more than 90% within 3 months. Likewise, Qureshi et al in Pakistan achieved significant improvements in early breastfeeding initiation through staff training and maternal counselling interventions.

Globally, the incidence of breastfeeding within the first hour of birth remains suboptimal. In high-income countries, reported rates vary widely, from 23% to 93%, whereas in Europe they range from 5% to 84%. Although published QI reports focusing specifically on early breastfeeding after cesarean delivery from high-income countries are limited, several studies demonstrate that implementing immediate skin-to-skin contact after cesarean birth markedly increases the likelihood of breastfeeding initiation within the first hour.18–21 These findings from high-income settings mirror those from low- and middle-income countries, underscoring the universal benefit of uninterrupted maternal–infant contact soon after delivery, regardless of resource setting or mode of birth.

Strengths

A significant strength of our project was the rapid achievement of the target within 4 weeks, mainly due to the strong motivation of healthcare staff and the sustained improvement over time. This study is among the few published QI reports addressing early initiation of breastfeeding after cesarean delivery and was conducted in collaboration with the Department of Quality and Safety. Sequential PDSA cycles were implemented using the Point Of Care Quality Improvement framework without requiring additional resources. Significantly, the interventions did not disrupt other aspects of clinical care, and multidisciplinary teams—including anesthesiologists, obstetricians, pediatricians, and nurses—did not report an increased workload. No mothers refused early breastfeeding.

Challenges and Limitations

The main challenges encountered during the intervention period were the rapid turnover of doctors and nurses, which necessitated repeated education and training of healthcare providers. One barrier that could not be addressed was the restriction on allowing attendants into the OT recovery room, as this policy remained unchanged. However, we mitigated its impact by ensuring mothers were transferred to the postnatal ward as soon as they were clinically stable. An additional limitation was that we did not assess maternal stress levels during the intervention.

Balancing Measures

As we initiated skin-to-skin contact, followed by breastfeeding on the operating table during the cesarean procedure, we monitored for any untoward complications during the process of skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding, such as worsening respiratory distress in the newborn, accidental falls from the OT table, or maternal complications as observed by the anesthesiologist. Reassuringly, no such adverse events were reported during the study period.

CONCLUSIONS

Our QI project demonstrates that early initiation of breastfeeding after cesarean delivery can be substantially improved and sustained through simple, structured interventions requiring no additional resources. Sequential PDSA cycles, multidisciplinary collaboration, and consistent staff engagement were key factors in achieving and maintaining success. Replication and adaptation of this model across diverse healthcare settings could further enhance neonatal outcomes globally.
